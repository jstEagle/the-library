## 3. The Night the Stars Went Out

There is a particular kind of boredom that comes from watching stones do nothing, and I had become, by my twenty-third year, something of a connoisseur of it.

The border watch was the worst assignment in the Guild, and Corvane gave it to me every time. Not out of cruelty—I'd learned to read his moods well enough over seventeen years, and cruelty wasn't among them—but because I was the only one left. The Wardens' Guild of Hollowmere had once numbered forty. Then twenty. Then nine. Now it was an old man with bad knees and a young man who talked to lamps, and between us we maintained the illusion that the valley's borders were still guarded by anything more than habit.

The watch post sat on the eastern ridge where the land rose up to meet the first of the border stones—a line of grey monoliths marching off into the dark in both directions like the vertebrae of some enormous sleeping animal. Each stone was supposed to hold a ward. Each ward was supposed to hum with the quiet magic that had kept Hollowmere sealed for sixty years: warm air in winter, cool air in summer, no wind from outside, no travelers either way. That was the story, anyway.

What the wards actually did these days was flicker. Occasionally. When they felt like it.

I'd brought two things with me that night: a thermos of Sela's terrible spiced cider—she made it with too much clove and refused all criticism—and the lamp I'd been repairing, the one with real light inside it. I told myself I'd brought the lamp for practical reasons. The watch post's own lamp had died weeks ago and I hadn't gotten around to replacing its wick. But if I'm honest, which I am trying to be more often lately, I brought it because sitting alone on a cold ridge is better company when you're holding something impossible.

It sat beside me on the bench, glowing softly under its shuttered hood, throwing a coin of gold light across my knees. Warm. Alive. A small rebellion I could fit in my pocket.

"Right," I said to it. "You don't say anything, and neither will I."

* * *

The first hour passed the way hours do on watch: slowly, and then not at all, depending entirely on how much you wanted them to pass.

I drank the cider. It was aggressively mediocre. I thought about Sela making it, standing in her father's mill kitchen with flour on her sleeves, saying, "It's got character," in the tone of someone defending a misshapen child. I thought about how she'd laughed at me that morning when I'd said, again, that I couldn't leave the valley, and how her laughter had that edge in it now, the one that said she didn't believe me anymore either.

I thought about the lamp. About the way true light moved differently than lamplight—not brighter exactly, but *more present*, like the difference between a painting of a fire and a fire. I'd spent three nights trying to make it die and failing, and somewhere in those three nights I'd stopped being frightened of it and started being curious, and curiosity, in Hollowmere, was a dangerous thing to cultivate.

Corvane would have said magic doesn't return just because you want it to. He'd have said wards decay, seals weaken, and a flicker means nothing. He'd have said all of this while looking at me very steadily, the way he looked at me whenever I asked questions he considered structural rather than practical.

But Corvane wasn't here. It was just me, and the stones, and the stars—

And that was when I noticed.

Not all at once. That's the thing people get wrong about catastrophes; they imagine a sound, a flash, some grand announcement. What actually happened was smaller than that. What happened was that I looked up to check the cloud cover—it was a clear night, I remember thinking it was a remarkably clear night, the kind we never got, the whole sky scoured clean—and I noticed there were no clouds at all.

And no stars either.

The sky was simply *not there*. Not black—black is a color, black is something. This was an absence, a smooth and total nothing stretched from ridge to ridge, as if someone had taken the night and turned it inside out. As if the world above the valley had been closed like a book.

I stood up so fast the bench scraped back and the cider went over, soaking into the dirt with a smell of cloves that would forever after make my stomach clench.

"No," I said, to nobody, because there was nobody. "No, that's not—that isn't—"

My mind did what minds do. It offered alternatives. High haze. Some atmospheric trickery. A local fog I somehow hadn't noticed swallowing the entire sky. My mind offered these explanations the way a drowning man offers himself reasons the water isn't real.

Then the border stone nearest me—the old one, the cracked one, the one I'd spent four days last summer re-pointing its mortar with mundane lime because the ward inside it had been dead for years—hummed.

Not flickered. *Hummed.* A low note I felt in my teeth, in the bones of my feet through the ground, a sound like a struck bell heard from inside the bell. And along the ridge, one after another, the other stones took it up, a wave of sound rolling away north and south until the whole border line was ringing together, sixty years of silence breaking open at once, and the light—

Light came out of them. Real light, white and gold, pouring up out of the cracks in the monoliths like water from a breached dam. I fell backward off the bench. The lamp tumbled from my hands and landed on its side and its little flame stood straight up, straining toward the stones like a plant toward sun.

For one moment—one long, singing, unbearable moment—the entire valley border blazed, and I thought, with a wonder so large it hurt: *it still works. All of it, it still works, it was all still there the whole time.*

Then the wards snapped.

That is the only word for it. *Snapped*, like cut ropes, like something holding tension for decades finally released all at once. The hum climbed to a shriek and then stopped—not faded, *stopped*—and the light collapsed inward into each stone and vanished, and the silence that came after was the loudest thing I have ever heard.

And then the cold arrived.

* * *

You have to understand what cold meant in Hollowmere.

The valley sat in its bowl of mountains like soup in a tureen. Sixty years of sealed wards meant sixty years of gentle climate. Winters were gray and damp but never bitter. Snow fell soft and melted in a day or two. The oldest villagers told stories of the Frost Years, before the sealing, when breath froze in beards and livestock died in barns, but those stories had gone the way of all stories—half legend, half lesson, told mostly to make children wear their hats.

Cold was a foreign country. Cold was a rumor from the world outside.

Now it poured down the eastern slopes like a flood finding a breach in a levee. I felt it cross the ridge before I saw any sign of it—a wall of air moving at walking pace, and everything it touched changed. The grass stiffened. My breath came out white for the first time in my life. Frost crawled up the legs of the overturned bench and across the surface of the spilled cider, turning it to slush, and I watched this happen with the detached fascination of a man watching his own house burn: unable to look away, unable to understand, aware on some deep level that everything familiar was ending.

I should have run. I knew that even then, some sensible clerk in the back office of my brain stamping memos: *Report to the Guildmaster. Sound the bell. Do your duty.* The sensible clerk had been filing reports all my life and I had ignored every single one, and I ignored him now too, because I couldn't stop looking at the sky.

Because the stars were coming back.

They returned wrong. They bloomed back into existence in patches, raggedly, like lights stuttering on in a house where someone is moving through pulling cords—and the patches didn't match. Constellations I'd known my whole life, the ones in the almanac, the ones Corvane taught me to navigate by—they were twisted, rearranged, the Plough bent into a shape like a broken hook, stars sitting next to other stars that had no business sharing a sky. And between the constellations, in the gaps, there were stars I had never seen before. Hundreds of them. Thousands. A crowded, glittering, alien heaven pressing down close enough to touch.

I had spent my whole life under a sky I thought I knew. In one hour I learned it had been a ceiling painted to look like the outdoors.

"That's not possible," I informed the universe, conversationally, because my voice was the only thing keeping me anchored to the concept of reality. "Stars don't move. Stars especially don't move *inward*. That's not—" My breath frosted. "That's not how *anything* works."

The universe declined to respond. It had clearly decided it owed me no further explanations.

* * *

Somewhere past midnight—I think; time had gone strange and syrupy—the frost reached the watch post door and I finally remembered I owned a coat, which was hanging in the guildhouse, six miles down the valley. I wrapped myself in the horse blanket from the post's chest and built a fire in the iron stove with hands that shook too much for the task, taking three attempts at the flint. The stove was a mundane thing, wood-burning, installed years ago precisely because the watch post had nothing magical left in it worth trusting. Old Corvane's pragmatism. At the time I'd found it sad. Now I crouched in front of it like it was a shrine.

The lamp had rolled against the wall. Its flame had gone out—or rather, I discovered when I picked it up, its flame had gone somewhere else, drawn thin and strange, guttering toward the east like a compass needle. I held it and watched the light strain against the glass, pointing, always pointing, toward the fallen border stones.

Toward whatever lay beyond them.

I want to record, for the sake of honesty, that my thoughts during those hours were not noble. I was not thinking of the valley, or the Guild, or my sacred duty as last apprentice of the Wardens of Hollowmere. I was thinking, in a loop, like a man tapping a broken fence rail:

*I held real magic in my hands for three nights and told no one.*

*I watched the wards die and I felt—God help me—I felt the wards die and some part of me was relieved.*

Because that was the truth underneath the awe and the dread, the truth I'd been carrying around like a stone in my boot for months, maybe years: I had wanted the walls to come down. I had wanted *out*. And now the walls were down, and outside was rushing in, and it was vast and wrong and beautiful and it was going to eat everything I loved, and I could not untangle the grief from the guilt from the terrible, shameful thrill of it.

Sela would know what to say about that. Sela always knew what to say. The thought of her arrived with a physical jolt—her kitchen, her flour-dusted sleeves, her mill down in the valley where the cold flood was now surely arriving, where frost was creeping over her father's waterwheel and the sheep in the low fields were huddling against a winter that had no business existing in autumn.

The bells. The valley had warning bells for emergencies—flood, rockslide, fire. Nobody had ever imagined needing one for *the sky*.

I killed the stove, shouldered my pack, and started down the ridge at a pace just short of running, the blanket streaming behind me like a cape, the lamp swinging at my side with its flame still straining eastward like a dog on a leash. Behind me the border stones stood dark and dead for the first time in sixty years, and above them the wrong stars wheeled in their unfamiliar patterns, and ahead of me the valley lay silvered with frost under a moon that I realized, with a fresh lurch, was also wrong—fuller than it should have been, hung lower, its craters sharp as knife-cuts in a way no moon I'd ever seen had been.

Halfway down, my foot caught a root and I went sprawling, and I lay there for a moment in the frozen grass, cheek against the ground, laughing—actually laughing, a slightly unhinged sound that startled a night bird out of the hedge.

"All right," I said to the valley, to the stars, to the dead wards, to Corvane asleep in the guildhouse who had no idea yet, who was about to wake up to this same sky and whose reaction I suddenly, desperately needed to see. Because here was the thing I kept circling and couldn't land on: everyone else in Hollowmere was going to look up tonight and feel terror. Grief. Loss.

But Corvane—when I pictured his face, weathered and steady, hearing the news of the fallen wards—I couldn't picture surprise.

And that frightened me more than the empty sky had.

I got up. I brushed the frost off my knees. And I ran the rest of the way down into the valley, toward the guildhouse with its dark windows, carrying a lamp full of impossible light, under a sky full of impossible stars, toward whatever it was that my guildmaster already knew.
