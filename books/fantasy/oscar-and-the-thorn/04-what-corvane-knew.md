## 4. What Corvane Knew

I want to say, in fairness to myself, that I ran the whole way back to the guildhouse. That my boots hammered the frost-hard road and my breath tore out of me in white ribbons and I burst through the door shouting the news like a proper Warden's apprentice, duty-first and brave.

That is not what happened.

What happened is that I walked. Slowly. The way you walk when your mind has come loose from its moorings and your body no longer trusts it to steer. The stars had gone out — all of them, at once, like a hand snuffing a thousand candles — and the border wards had fallen with them, and I had stood on the ridge for what felt like an hour watching cold air pour into the valley through gaps that hadn't existed since before my grandfather was born. Then I had turned around and walked home, because home was the only idea left in my head, and because some stubborn animal part of me believed that Guildmaster Corvane would open the door, hear my report, and make the world sensible again. That was his whole function, as far as I'd ever understood it. Corvane made things sensible. He made the ledgers balance and the lamps stay lit and the apprentices stop asking questions by answering them so thoroughly that the questions died of exhaustion.

The guildhouse was lit when I arrived. Every lamp, which should have been my first warning. We were a dying guild in a forgotten valley; we economized on light the way other households economized on meat.

Corvane was in the cellar.

I knew it before I knew it, if that makes sense — the way you know a thing from the shape of the silence. The trapdoor at the back of the kitchen stood open, and warm air rose out of it smelling of tallow and old paper and something sharper underneath, something acrid that stung my nose. Not lamp oil. I'd spent six years learning the smells of that house down to their bones.

"Emrys." His voice floated up, level, unhurried. "Shut the door behind you. Bolt it."

I bolted it. My hands did it without consulting me, which was fortunate, because the rest of me was still standing in the dark on a ridge watching the sky go blind.

I went down the stairs.

He had cleared the long worktable — the one where we trimmed wicks and soldered lamp-casings, where he'd taught me to read ward-script when I was nine — and covered it now with documents. Real documents. Not the guild's mild records of lamp-oil deliveries and apprentice attendance, but thick folios bound in grey leather, sealed scrolls, sheaves of loose pages weighted down with the brass lamp-weights I'd handled a thousand times without once wondering why there were so many of them, or why they were kept in the cellar instead of the workshop upstairs.

Corvane stood over them with a candle in one hand and an expression I had never seen on him before. It took me a moment to name it, because it didn't belong on his face, and then I understood: it was *efficiency*. Pure, undistracted efficiency. A man doing a task he had rehearsed.

"Guildmaster," I said. My voice came out strange. "The stars. They're gone. All of them. And the wards—"

"I know."

"You—" The word snagged. "You know?"

"The wards fell at moonrise. Forty minutes ago." He set the candle down and picked up the first folio without looking at me. "You watched them fall from the ridge. You'll have felt it in your teeth. You always feel the wards in your teeth; you told me so yourself, years ago, and I noted it because it meant your sensitivity was stronger than mine ever was." He opened the folio, glanced at the first page, and fed it to the candle flame. "Which is why you're here and not out there panicking with the villagers. Sit down, Emrys."

I sat down. On a crate of lamp glass, because there was nowhere else.

He burned documents. That was the thing my mind kept snagging on while the rest of me screamed. He burned them calmly, one after another, holding each page until the flame crept close to his fingers, then laying it in the iron brazier we used for melting sealing-wax. The firelight moved on his face and he might have been doing the accounts.

"Guildmaster, the wards are *down*. The border stones are dead. Cold air is coming into the valley for the first time in sixty years and the stars are *gone* and you're down here burning—"

"Burning paper that must not exist tomorrow." He said it the way he said everything: precisely, without apology. "Yes. I'm aware of the priorities. The wards are beyond either of us tonight. This is not."

"What is it?"

"Paperwork," said Corvane, and for one mad instant I thought I heard something almost like humor in it, gray and exhausted. "The kind that kills people."

He fed another page to the flame. I watched the ink curl and go black. And somewhere under the shock and the cold dread, a small clear part of me — the part Sela called my lamp-tinker's mind, the part that noticed flickers nobody else saw — began, quietly, to take inventory.

He wasn't surprised. That was the first entry in the ledger. The stars going out was the end of the world, and the man who had raised me since I was seven years old received the news like a delivery arriving on schedule.

He had prepared. That was the second. Nobody assembles this many documents into a cellar overnight. These had lived here, hidden, for years — decades, maybe — beneath the workshop where I'd slept and eaten and learned my letters, and I had never known.

And third: he had sent *me* to the border stones tonight. Of all nights. Me, the last apprentice, the boy who felt the wards in his teeth, assigned the night watch on the exact night the wards died.

I looked up at him across the brazier, and the small clear part of me asked a question I very much did not want answered.

*Did you know?*

*Did you know this would happen? Did you send me up there to watch?*

"Read nothing," Corvane said. He had caught my eyes on the table. "Whatever falls out of a folio, whatever scrap you see, you read nothing. Do you understand? Some of these pages are written in cipher, but not all of them, and the ones in plain script are worse. Ignorance is armor, Emrys. I have spent sixty years — well. Thirty-one years, in this valley, but the work is older than me — I have spent my life making sure certain knowledge stayed in very few heads. Tonight the number of heads goes from two to one."

"Whose?" I asked. My mouth was dry. "Yours or mine?"

He paused, candle in hand, and looked at me for a long moment. The firelight made his eyes look younger than they were, and tired in a way I'd never let myself see before, because I'd never had reason to look.

"Mine," he said. "For now. And then, if I don't come back — yours."

*If I don't come back.* The phrase dropped through me like a stone through deep water. I heard it land somewhere far below, in a place I wouldn't feel the impact of until later.

"Where are you going?"

"Out." He resumed burning. "There are people who will come for these documents whether they exist or not, and there are people who will come for the people who might have read them. I intend to be a difficult person to find, and easier to find than you."

"That makes no sense."

"It makes perfect sense. It's just arithmetic." Another page curled into ash. "You are the last apprentice of the Wardens' Guild. Whatever happens to me, the guild continues in you. That is not sentiment, Emrys, that is doctrine — the guild does not die with its master, it dies with its memory, and memory lives in the living. So you will do exactly as I say, and the first thing I say is this:"

He set down the candle. He came around the table, took me by both shoulders — his grip firm, his hands smelling of wax and smoke — and looked at me the way he had looked at me exactly twice before in my life: the day I arrived, and the day I finished my first ward-repair alone.

"You will say nothing," he said. "To anyone. Not to Sela Voss, though she's your friend and you trust her and she'd cut off her own arm for you. Not to the miller, not to the priest, not to the village council when they come knocking tomorrow asking why the sky broke. You saw nothing. You know nothing. The wards failed of old age, the stars are a weather, and Emrys Winterbourne is a lamp-tinker who happened to be awake. Can you say that?"

"They'll know I'm lying."

"Of course they'll know. You lie badly; you always have. But badly is enough, if the listener only needs plausible doubt." He released my shoulders and stepped back, and just like that he was Guildmaster again, brisk and dry. "Inquisitors don't need truth. They need permission to stop looking. Give them a boring answer and they'll take it, because boring answers let everyone go to bed."

"Inquisitors." The word tasted wrong. "What inquisitors? Why would—"

"Because the valley's peace is a document," Corvane said, "and someone has stolen a page of it."

I stared at him.

He turned back to the brazier and fed it another handful of papers, and the flames leapt, and for a moment his shadow climbed the cellar wall huge and hunched, like something out of the stories Sela's grandmother used to tell about the Thorn War. The war we didn't talk about. The war that had ended before any of us were born, that had given the valley its wards and its silence and its sixty years of forgetting, and that Corvane had never once spoken of except to say, in the flat voice he saved for closed subjects, *"We keep the peace. That is the whole of the duty."*

"We keep the peace," I said now. My voice shook. "That's what you always say. Is this the peace? Burning papers in a cellar while the sky goes dark?"

"This is the price of the peace." He said it without turning around. "You've tended dying lamps for six years, boy. You know better than most that light costs fuel. Everything costs fuel. The question is only who pays, and how quietly."

Something cold slid down my spine, and it wasn't the air from the fallen wards.

"How quietly," I repeated.

"Yes." He glanced back at me, and there it was again — that flash of something almost gentle, almost sorry, quickly banked like a lamp turned low. "You're beginning to see. Good. Seeing is painful; I won't pretend otherwise. But you'll forgive me if I don't unwrap the whole of it tonight. There isn't time, and there are ears on the roads already, and some truths, once spoken aloud in a room, can never be stuffed back into the bottle. What I can give you—"

He crossed to the far wall. Behind a stack of empty oil jars — jars I had dusted around, stacked and restacked, a hundred times — there was a seam in the stone I had never seen. He pressed something, and a cavity breathed open, exhaling cold and dust, and from inside it he drew a single envelope.

Thick paper. Grey wax seal, stamped with the Winterbourne crest — the lamp within the circle — pressed deep and clean, the way seals are pressed when the person pressing them has done it many times and wants it done right.

"For if I don't return," he said, and held it out to me.

I didn't take it.

My hands stayed at my sides. Because taking it would make it real — the letter, the *if*, the whole collapsing architecture of the evening — and some last childish part of me wanted to stand in that cellar and refuse the premise entirely. To say: *you're coming back, obviously, you always come back, you're Corvane.* Six years. He'd found me in the low town when I was seven, an orphan with quick fingers and too many questions, and he'd brought me here and made me a Warden, and in six years he had never once lied to me that I could detect, and I had built my entire life on the load-bearing assumption that he never would.

"Take it," he said.

"Tell me what's in it."

"No."

"Then tell me where you're going."

"No."

"Corvane." I used his name, not his title, which I had done perhaps four times in six years, and it landed between us like a dropped tool. "You raised me. You taught me every craft I have. If you walk out that door and I never see you again, and all I have is a sealed letter I'm forbidden to open, then you're not my guildmaster anymore, you're just another man keeping secrets, and I have spent my whole life in a valley full of those."

The words hung in the smoky air. Somewhere above us, the house ticked and settled, ordinary as ever, and outside the bolted door the valley lay under a starless sky, breathing cold air for the first time in sixty years.

Corvane closed his eyes. Just for a breath. When he opened them, he looked old — truly old, older than I'd ever allowed him to be, older than the valley itself somehow, as if he'd carried something heavy so long he'd forgotten it wasn't part of his body.

"You're right," he said quietly. "And I'm sorry. Both of those are true, and neither of them helps you tonight." He stepped closer and pressed the letter into my hands, folding my fingers over it, and his hands were steady and warm and shaking, somehow, both at once. "Open it when I'm gone. Not before. Not because I don't trust you — I trust you more than I have ever trusted anyone, which is precisely why you must not read it while I can be questioned. If you know what I know, and they take you, they will take it out of you however they must. As long as you don't know, you're only a lamp-tinker. The moment you know, you're a threat. Do you understand the arithmetic?"

"I understand you're leaving me arithmetic instead of answers."

"Yes," he said. "That's the trade. I'm sorry. Take it up with the world."

He let go. He gathered a travel cloak from a peg I'd never noticed was a peg, shrugged it on, and took from the table one last item: a short iron rod, dull and unadorned, that hummed faintly when his fingers closed around it. I had never seen it before. Six years in that house, and the cellar alone had held more secrets than the rest of the valley combined.

At the foot of the stairs he stopped. He didn't turn around. His back was straight and his voice was level and it was, I think, the hardest thing he did all night — harder than the burning, harder than the letter.

"There's bread and hard cheese in the blue crock," he said. "Eat something. You didn't eat before your watch; you never do. And Emrys —" a pause, the length of a heartbeat, maybe two — "whatever you find in that letter, whatever you decide it means, remember that I chose you. Out of every possibility, every path, I chose you. Don't let the world tell you different before I get the chance to explain it properly."

Then he went up the stairs, and the trapdoor closed over him, and I heard the kitchen door, and then I heard nothing at all.

* * *

I sat in the cellar for a long time.

The brazier burned itself down to a red eye and then to grey. The smell of ash settled over everything, into my hair, into my clothes, and I thought distantly that I would probably carry the smell of burnt paper for the rest of my life, and that this seemed fitting for reasons I couldn't yet name.

The letter sat in my lap. Grey seal. Lamp within the circle. Heavy as a stone and light as a leaf, depending on the minute.

I should have obeyed him. Open it when I'm gone, he said, and *gone* meant gone-out-the-door, and he was out the door, and I had six years of obedience worn into me like a groove. But I also had the lamp-tinker's mind, the flicker-noticer, the boy who had spent his life being told the valley was simple and had never quite believed it. And that mind kept returning, with horrible patience, to the inventory it had taken while he burned:

He wasn't surprised.
He had prepared.
He sent me to watch.

Three entries. Three facts. And between them, a conclusion trying to be born, kicking against the walls of everything I believed about my own life.

*Did you know?*

I got up. I banked the brazier. I climbed the stairs on legs that felt borrowed, and I ate the bread and the cheese from the blue crock, because he'd told me to and because grief, I was discovering, is mostly appetite's opposite and mostly hunger's twin, and I couldn't tell which one was gnawing me. I sat at the kitchen table where I'd eaten six years of meals, under a sky with no stars, and I turned the letter over and over in my hands.

The seal was unbroken. The wax was cool. My thumbnail rested against the edge of it, and I thought about the weight of it, the way I thought about the weight of everything — how much force, how little force, where the crack would run.

*Open it when I'm gone.*

"He's gone," I said out loud, to the empty kitchen, and my voice sounded young and stupid and afraid.

But I didn't break the seal. Not yet. Because there was one more entry in the ledger, one more fact my mind kept circling, and it was this: he had said *if I don't return*, and he had said it like a man who knew the odds. And men who know the odds, men who burn thirty years of paperwork in an hour and leave bread in the crock and instructions in the walls — such men do not expect to die tonight. Such men expect to run, and to be chased, and to buy time. Which meant the letter wasn't a eulogy.

It was a fuse.

And fuses, my lamp-tinker's mind whispered, are meant to be lit at the right moment. Not before. Lighting it early doesn't make the explosion bigger. It just means you're standing closer when it goes.

So I sat in the dark kitchen with an unbroken seal under my thumb and the smell of ash in my hair, and I waited for morning, and I hated myself for waiting, and I waited anyway.

Somewhere out in the valley, cold air moved through broken borders for the first time in sixty years. Somewhere on the roads, boots were already coming — I didn't know whose yet, royal grey or something worse, but the night felt *occupied*, the way a house feels occupied when a stranger's coat hangs in the hall.

And somewhere past the mill, past the sleeping village, past the dead border stones, a man who had raised me was walking into the dark carrying an iron rod that hummed, and I understood — finally, fully, with the whole of my traitor heart — that the comfortable smallness of my life had never been comfort at all.

It had been a wall. And I had been kept inside it, gently, all this time, by the people who loved me most.

The seal waited under my thumb.

Morning would bring riders. I could feel it the way I felt the wards in my teeth — except the wards were dead now, and what I felt instead was something newer, colder, and infinitely less familiar.

It felt, I realized, like the beginning of the rest of my life.
