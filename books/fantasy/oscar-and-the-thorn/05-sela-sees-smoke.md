## 5. Sela Sees Smoke

I have ground flour for the whole of Hollowmere since I was eleven years old, and in all that time I have learned exactly three things. One: grain is patient and people are not. Two: my father's opinions arrive like weather and leave like weather, meaning eventually, if you wait indoors long enough. Three: when something is about to go terribly wrong in this valley, it announces itself first as a small wrongness — a wheel that squeaks where it never squeaked before, a dog that won't stop looking at the treeline.

That morning, the third thing arrived as a smell.

Smoke, but not hearth-smoke. Hearth-smoke is friendly. It smells of bread and wet ash and my mother's terrible attempts at tallow candles. This was different — sharper, chemical, the smell of paper burning in quantity, of ink and glue and old leather covers giving themselves up all at once. It was the smell of a library dying, and there was only one library in Hollowmere.

I shut off the millrace feed, wiped my hands on my apron out of pure habit — they were no cleaner afterward, they are never cleaner afterward, flour is a permanent condition — and climbed to the loft window that faces up-slope toward the guildhouse.

The guildhouse sat on its shelf of rock above the terraced fields the way it had sat for longer than anyone could remember: grey stone, slate roof, the ward-lamps along its eaves burning their pale, fireless light even at midday, because Guildmaster Corvane believed lamps should be lit whether or not anyone needed them. *A lit lamp costs nothing,* he liked to say. *An unlit one costs everything.* Emrys had repeated that line to me so many times over the years that I'd started saying it in my sleep, which he found hilarious and I found deeply unfair.

The lamps were still burning. The roof was intact. But from the cellar vents below the eastern wall, where the guild kept its records and its stores and whatever else Corvane pretended wasn't down there, thin ribbons of smoke rose into the cold morning air and bent eastward on the wind, steady as threads pulled through an eye of a needle.

Somebody was burning documents. Somebody was burning them carefully, in a controlled way, the way you burn things when you have planned the burning and brought the right kindling and chosen a day with good wind.

You do not accidentally burn a library. You accidentally burn dinner. You burn a library on purpose.

* * *

Here is what you need to know about me, since apparently I'm telling this part of the story: I am not brave. I want that on the record before anyone gets sentimental. Emrys Winterbourne is the sort of person who runs toward trouble with his sleeves rolled up, and I am the sort of person who calculates how much trouble weighs, whether it can be carried, and what the exit looks like. When we were children and someone dared us to touch the border stones, Emrys touched them. I stood at a respectful distance and memorized the way back home.

So when I saw the smoke, my first instinct — my honest, unglamorous instinct — was to mind my own business. The guild's business was the guild's business. Corvane was terrifying in the specific way that quiet old men who know things are terrifying, and the last thing any sensible miller's daughter wanted was to be caught noticing his secrets.

Then I looked at the pass.

The pass road came down from the northern saddle in a long switchback, visible from the loft window for nearly its whole length, and at this hour it should have been empty. Nobody came down from the north. Nobody had come down from the north in sixty years, because the wards sealed the valley and the wards were the whole point of the valley, and everyone knew this the way you know your own name — without ever once thinking about it.

There were riders on the pass road. Six of them, maybe seven, coming down at an easy trot, unhurried, the pace of men who knew exactly where they were going and did not expect to be stopped. Even at that distance I could see they weren't wearing anything like our colors. Hollowmere dressed in browns and undyed wool because dye cost money and nobody had any. These riders wore grey. Not work-grey, not the grey of rain-soaked wool. A tailored grey, uniform grey, with pale trim that caught the morning light like frost.

Royal grey. I'd never seen royal grey. No one in Hollowmere had seen royal grey in living memory. And yet I knew it the moment I saw it, the way you know a face from a story your grandmother told you — some knowledge doesn't travel by roads, it travels by blood and bedtime.

My hands were shaking. I noticed this with a kind of detached irritation, the way you notice a wheel bearing going bad. *Not now,* I told my hands. *Later. Shake later.*

Because here was the arithmetic, and I am good at arithmetic, it comes with the milling: the wards fell two nights ago. Everyone felt it — the cold air pouring into the valley like water finding a crack, the sky gone black and starless, the lamps guttering. Corvane had spent two days moving through the village like a man with a list, checking stones, checking houses, checking people, asking questions in that soft voice of his that made answers feel like confessions. And now, on the third morning, while the valley was still rubbing its eyes and trying to convince itself the stars would come back if it just gave it time — strangers in royal grey were riding down the pass, and the guildmaster was burning his papers.

Those two facts did not sit next to each other comfortably. They fit together like gears, and I did not like what the machine was for.

* * *

I went down the ladder faster than is strictly safe and nearly took my father's head off with the flour scoop.

"Sela," he said, "the race—"

"Feed's cut. Watch the stones for me."

"It's barely past dawn, the queue'll—"

"Father." I put the scoop down and looked at him, and whatever he saw in my face made him stop talking, which in forty-odd years of knowing him I could count on one hand. "Something's wrong. Up at the guild. Riders on the pass. Just — watch the stones, and don't tell anyone about the riders until I say."

He frowned, opened his mouth to deliver one of his opinions, and then — credit where it's due — closed it again and picked up the miller's tally-stick instead. My father is a lot of things, most of them loud, but he has never once been stupid.

I took the shortcut behind the tannery, which is foul and steep and saves a quarter mile, and came out on the lane below the guildhouse just as the first of the grey riders reached the village square. I ducked behind Brannoc's woodpile — original, I know, but it was there and I was in a hurry — and watched.

They were soldiers. That was clear even before they dismounted, in the way they moved, the way their eyes swept the square in overlapping arcs without anyone appearing to give an order. Six of them, hard-faced, dusty, and leading them a seventh man on a finer horse: tall, silvering at the temples, wearing the same grey but better cut, with a chain of office at his collar that said *important* louder than any herald could. He looked around the square the way a man looks around a room he owns but hasn't visited in a while.

An inquisitor. I didn't know the word yet — I'd learn it soon enough, learn it the way you learn a disease, by catching it — but I knew the shape of him. Power doesn't announce itself in Hollowmere; we don't have any. So when it arrives, you feel it like a change in pressure.

"Good people," the man called out, and his voice carried beautifully, warm as fresh bread, and I hated it immediately. "Riders of the Crown, come in peace. There has been a disturbance in the night sky. We've come to help."

Behind the woodpile, I thought: *help.* Sure. And the mill grinds flour out of the goodness of its heart.

And then, above and behind them all, the smoke from the guildhouse cellar vents thinned, faltered, and stopped. Which meant either Corvane had finished burning everything worth burning —

— or he had heard the horses too.

* * *

Finding Emrys was the easy part. Finding Emrys is always the easy part; he is a creature of habit wearing a boy's body. He was at the eastern border stones, third one from the stream, doing something fiddly to the lamp housing with a screwdriver and a look of profound concentration, which is his face for both fixing lamps and avoiding feelings.

"Emrys."

He didn't startle — he never startles, it's infuriating — but he set the screwdriver down with the care of a man buying himself time. "Sela. You're supposed to be at the mill."

"The mill is fine. You're not." I grabbed his sleeve before he could deploy any of his reasonable objections. "Emrys. Listen to me. There are riders on the pass. Seven. Grey cloaks, pale trim, armed, and their leader talks like a festival barker and smiles like a debt collector. They're in the square right now calling themselves helpers."

His face did something complicated. I watched it happen — the flicker of disbelief, then the quick, hungry search of my face for the lie, then the moment he found none and had to live with that. "Grey? From the north?"

"From the north, down the pass, past wards that fell two nights ago, arriving with suspiciously good timing. And there's more." I lowered my voice, though we were alone with nothing but sheep and border stones to hear. "Smoke. From the guildhouse cellars. Paper smoke, Emrys. Lots of it. He's burning the archives."

The color left his face in stages, like a tide going out. "Corvane wouldn't—"

"He already has. I watched it for half an hour. Controlled burn, east vents, good wind for it. Your guildmaster is destroying sixty years of records the same morning the crown rides into town, and if you can make those two facts mean nothing together, you're cleverer than me, and we both know that bar is on the floor."

"He told me—" Emrys stopped. I watched him decide how much to tell me, watched the little internal war between loyalty and honesty that he fights every single day and usually loses slowly. "He gave me a letter last night. For if he doesn't return. He told me to stay inside. To say nothing. To trust him."

"And do you?"

"That's the problem," he said quietly. "Yes. I do. That's why I'm standing here wanting to believe you're wrong, and you're never wrong about trouble, Sela, you're like a weathervane for disaster."

"I'm choosing to take that as a compliment."

Down the slope, faint on the wind, came the sound of the inquisitor's warm, terrible voice addressing a gathering crowd in the square. Words drifted up in pieces: *...disturbance... the Crown's concern for its people... anyone with information...*

*Its people.* Not *your*. I noticed that. I notice things; it's the miller in me. You spend your life separating what's grain and what's chaff, and after enough years you can't stop doing it to sentences.

"Emrys." I turned him by the shoulder so he had to look at me. "Whatever's in that letter, whatever he knows — he's burning it *right now*, before those men reach the guildhouse. Men like that don't ride six days through hostile country to *help*. They ride to collect. And when collectors find an empty cellar, they start collecting people. He's not protecting you by sending you home to wait. He's protecting the secret. There's a difference, and I think you already know it, and I think it's eating you alive."

"You don't understand what he's given me," Emrys said. "What he's *trusted* me with. Forty years, Sela. He taught me everything. He's the closest thing to family I—"

"I know." And I did know, and it hurt to say the next part anyway, so I said it gently, which for me is practically a sacrament. "I know. And that's exactly why he chose you to carry it. Because you'd obey. Because you'd stay inside and say nothing and trust him, right up until trusting him got you buried alongside the truth."

The wind shifted. Below us, the grey riders remounted, and began, unhurried, to ride up the guild road.

Emrys looked at the letter-shaped bulge inside his coat, then at the smoke-thin ghost of where the smoke had been, then at me. His jaw worked. Everything he was — apprentice, orphan, loyal son of a lying old man, keeper of lamps that held no fire — was balanced on the head of a pin, and I could see the pin bending.

"They're coming up the road," I said. "Choose fast."

He closed his eyes for one breath — one full breath, the way Corvane had taught him, the way he does when he's choosing — and when he opened them, he wasn't looking at the guildhouse anymore.

He was looking at the fallen border stones, and the world beyond them.

"Sela," he said, "how fast can you run?"
