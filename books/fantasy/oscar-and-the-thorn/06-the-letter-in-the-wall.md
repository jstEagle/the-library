## 6. The Letter in the Wall

The cellar smelled of tallow, old paper, and the particular dust that gathers on things nobody wants to look at. I'd swept it a thousand times as a boy, back when the Wardens' Guild still had enough apprentices to make sweeping feel like a shared punishment instead of a private chore. Now it was just me, three guttering candles, and Guildmaster Corvane feeding documents into an iron basin like a man paying off a debt he'd hoped to die before owing.

He didn't look up when I came down the stairs. He'd heard me; he always heard me.

"You were told to stay at the gatehouse," he said.

"You told me to say nothing. You didn't say where."

A ghost of something crossed his face—not quite amusement, not quite pain. "That's my fault, then. I taught you too well to find the gaps in instructions." He fed another sheaf into the fire. The flames caught it eagerly, and for a moment I could read half a line of script as it blackened—coordinates, maybe, or a date—and then it curled and was nothing.

"Guildmaster." My voice came out steadier than I felt. "The stars went out. The wards fell. The cold is coming down the pass right now, and you're burning papers in a hole in the ground. Forgive me if I'm having trouble finding the gap in *that* instruction."

He stopped. Set down the stack. Looked at me across the basin with those grey eyes that had watched over my whole life and had, until last night, seemed tired only from age rather than from weight.

"Sit," he said.

"I'll stand."

"You'll want to sit." But he didn't press it, and I didn't sit, and we both pretended that settled something.

"The wards did not fail," he said quietly. "I want you to understand that much, because everything else depends on it. A ward does not fall like a rope snapping under strain. It frays. It dims. It gives you weeks of warning, sometimes years. What happened last night was—" his hand made a small, precise gesture, like a surgeon describing an amputation— "*severance.* Someone cut them. Deliberately. From the anchor side."

My mouth had gone dry somewhere around the word *deliberately*. "Who could—"

"Someone with access to the anchor stones. Someone who knew their true names." He picked the stack back up, but he wasn't reading it anymore. "Which means someone of this guild, Emrys. Or someone who learned what only this guild knew."

"You're saying one of us—"

"I'm saying," and here his voice dropped into something I'd never heard him use before, something old and careful, like a man walking on ice he'd tested many times and never trusted, "that sixty years ago, the founders of Hollowmere built our peace on a foundation they were willing to kill to protect. And I have spent my life making sure that when the foundation finally cracked, there would be as little left standing on it as possible."

The candle nearest me guttered. I remember thinking, absurdly, about the lamp on the border road—the one that still flickered with real light while all its brothers held nothing but glass and wick. All those years I'd hidden that little flame from him. All those years, apparently, he'd been hiding an inferno from me.

"What's in the papers?" I asked.

"Lies," he said. "Careful ones. The guild's true records are elsewhere, and the crown must not be allowed to think otherwise." He resumed burning, faster now, methodical. "Inquisitors are already on the road. Grey riders out of Cindermarch. They will come asking questions, and every question will be a knife with a polite handle. You will answer them exactly as a frightened young warden would answer: honestly, badly, apologetically. You know nothing. You saw nothing. The stars went out and it frightened you. That part will even be true."

"And you?"

"I have an errand."

"At night. In the cold. With the valley open for the first time in sixty years."

"Yes."

"That's madness."

"That," Corvane said mildly, "is the first accurate thing anyone has said in this cellar today." He finished the last sheaf, doused the basin with sand, and then did something strange: he went to the far wall, the one with the old wine racks that hadn't held wine since before I was born, and pressed his palm flat against the third rack from the left.

Stone grated on stone. A section of wall swung inward, no wider than a bread oven, revealing a cavity lined with oilcloth. Inside sat an iron box, small enough to carry one-handed, sealed with wax stamped by a signet I'd seen on every official letter of my childhood: the Winterbourne crest, a mountain over three bars.

Corvane took the box out, held it a moment as if weighing more than its metal, and pressed it into my hands.

"For if I don't return," he said.

The words landed in my stomach like stones dropped down a well. "Return from where? Guildmaster, what is—"

"Don't open it tonight. Don't open it unless I'm gone longer than three days, or unless riders in royal grey come through the pass and start asking about names." His hands closed over mine around the box, and they were trembling. I had never once seen Corvane's hands tremble. Not in storms. Not when the fever took half the village the winter I was twelve. "Emrys. Listen to me as though I am dying, because men who speak as though dying are the only men worth listening to. Whatever is in that box, it will make you angry. It may make you hate me. Let it. Anger keeps a man moving; hatred tells him where. But do not let it make you careless, because there are people coming through that pass who would burn this entire valley for what you're holding, and they would sleep well afterward."

"Why give it to me? Why not take it with you?"

"Because anything I carry, they can capture." He stepped back, and just like that, the trembling was gone, folded away like a tool after use. "And because I have spent twenty years teaching you to tend things that seem dead, boy. Lamps without fire. Wards without power. A valley without memory." Something moved behind his eyes—regret, or its close cousin. "You are better at keeping alive the things everyone else gave up on than anyone I have ever known. So keep this alive."

He climbed the stairs. At the top he paused, silhouetted against the grey pre-dawn light, and said, without turning around:

"If I come back, we'll laugh about how seriously you're taking this. If I don't—" a pause long enough to hold a lifetime— "then know that everything I did, I did because I loved this valley. Even the parts that will look unforgivable. Especially those."

Then he was gone, and I heard his boots on the gravel path, unhurried, almost casual—a man going out to check a fence line—and then the sound faded and there was nothing but the cellar, the candles, and an iron box in my hands growing warm from my own grip.

* * *

I did not open it that night. I want that on the record, whoever's record ends up keeping this. I sat in the guildhouse kitchen with the box on the table and a pot of tea going cold beside it, and I stared at the Winterbourne seal—that mountain over three bars I'd known since before I could read—and I told myself stories.

It's guild accounts. It's correspondence with the other valleys. It's Corvane being dramatic, because Corvane had always been a little dramatic in the way that careful men are dramatic, precisely, in measured doses, so you never quite call it drama.

But the tea went cold, and the fire burned down, and dawn came up thin and colourless through the east window, and none of my stories survived contact with the memory of his hands shaking.

Three days, he'd said. Or grey riders asking about names.

The grey riders arrived before noon on the second day.

* * *

I heard them before I saw them—hooves on the frost-hardened road, too many, moving at the disciplined trot of soldiers rather than the scattered canter of travelers. From the guildhouse steps I counted eight. They wore travelling cloaks the colour of wet slate, and their horses were the tall, deep-chested breed they raised out past Cindermarch, and at their head rode a man whose bearing announced him before his voice did.

He was perhaps fifty, lean rather than gaunt, with silver threading his temples and the kind of face that looks friendly the way a well-lit room looks friendly—which is to say, you notice the warmth and forget to ask why there are no windows. He reined in at the foot of the steps, looked up at me with pale eyes that took inventory—box nowhere visible, hands empty, expression frightened, good, good—and smiled.

"Good morning," he said, and his voice was warm too, warm as a hearth in a house you've been invited into and cannot leave. "Forgive the intrusion. Dain Marrec, Royal Inquisitor, Third Circle. Is this the Warden's Guildhouse?"

"It's what's left of it," I said, which was honest, and which was also, I would learn, exactly the wrong kind of honesty to offer a man like Marrec, because it sounded rehearsed.

"Indeed." He dismounted, handed his reins to a soldier without looking, and climbed two steps so that we stood nearly level. Close up, I could see the fine lines around his eyes and the utter absence of hurry in them. "And you would be?"

"Emrys Winterbourne. Apprentice warden. Last one, actually." A smile I did not feel. "The guild's mostly me and Guildmaster Corvane, and he's away just now."

"Away." Marrec tasted the word like a man checking wine for vinegar. "Away where, if I may?"

"He didn't say. He often goes out to check the border stones. With the wards fallen, there's rather a lot to check."

"Mm. And when did he leave?"

"This morning. Before dawn."

Something flickered across the inquisitor's face—there and gone so fast I might have invented it. Satisfaction, maybe. Or disappointment. With a man like Marrec, I would learn, the two wear identical masks.

"Before dawn," he repeated. "On the morning after the most catastrophic magical event in six decades. Your guildmaster woke before dawn and rode out alone to check fences." He turned slightly, surveying the valley as though it were a document he was skimming. "You'll forgive me, Master Winterbourne, but I've read the reports on Hollowmere. Quiet valley. Careful valley. A valley where nothing happens without the Wardens' Guild knowing, and nothing happens in the Wardens' Guild without your guildmaster knowing twice." His gaze came back to mine, gentle as a hand on the shoulder, heavy as a hand on the throat. "So I find myself curious. When the stars went out—and they did go out, did they not? Every one of them, all at once, like snuffed candles—what did the guild do?"

"We—assessed the damage. Tended the lamps. Waited for dawn."

"No messages sent? No runners to the other valleys?"

"The passes were—"

"Cold. Yes. I imagine they were." He smiled again, and it reached his eyes, which somehow made it worse. "Master Winterbourne, I am going to ask you some questions over the next few hours. Some of them will seem strange. Some of them will seem insulting. I want you to remember, throughout, that I am not your enemy. My task is simply to understand what happened to the wards, and to help the crown restore order to a frightened valley. Anything you tell me helps me help you. Anything you withhold—" the smile adjusted by a single degree, "—helps someone else, and I should very much like to know who."

He walked past me into the guildhouse as though he owned the deed, his soldiers fanning out to the stable and the gatehouse with the smooth efficiency of men who had done this many times, and I stood on the steps with the iron box's hiding place somewhere behind my ribs where no search would find it, and understood, with the clarity that arrives only after it is useless, that I had lied badly, that he had noticed, and that everything Corvane feared had just walked through the pass wearing a friendly face.

* * *

The questions lasted four hours. I will not reproduce them all; my dignity has suffered enough, and besides, most of them blur together now into a single long interrogation shaped like a conversation. But certain ones stayed.

"Describe the night the stars failed."

"They went out. All of them. At once."

"All at once? Not in sequence? Not spreading outward from any point?"

"...All at once. Like a lamp being covered."

His pen paused. "Interesting. A covering, not an extinguishing. You tend ward-lamps, yes? Then you know the difference between a flame put out and a flame hidden."

I did know the difference. I hated that he knew I knew.

"Your guildmaster's chambers. May I see them?"

"They're—being cleaned."

"By whom?"

"Me. I mean, I was going to. It's my turn."

"Your turn," he repeated softly, writing something down. I never learned what.

"Winterbourne," he said at last, setting down his pen entirely. "An old name. One of the founding families, isn't it? The first Wardens of Hollowmere."

"Everyone says so. There aren't records anymore. The archive flooded, decades back."

"Flooded." He let the word sit between us like a coin on a table. "How convenient floods are, Master Winterbourne. They wash away exactly what needs washing, and never a drop more." He rose, buttoned his coat, and became suddenly, effortlessly genial again, the inquisitor vanishing beneath the travelling gentleman. "Thank you for your candor. You've been very helpful."

"I don't feel helpful."

"That," he said, settling his cloak across his shoulders, "is usually a sign that honesty is present. Dishonesty feels wonderful. That's why people use it." At the door he paused. "Oh—one more thing. Your guildmaster. If he returns, or if he sends word, or if you discover he left anything behind—correspondence, seals, records of any kind—the crown would consider it a kindness, and kindnesses to the crown are rarely forgotten." His pale eyes rested on me a moment too long. "Nor are the failures to offer them."

* * *

They quartered themselves in the gatehouse—naturally, the gatehouse, the one building with a view of the road and the door—and I spent the evening being aggressively ordinary in front of them. I ate supper. I washed dishes. I went to bed early, as a frightened apprentice would, and lay awake in the dark listening to the wind come through the open pass for the first time in living memory, carrying smells the valley had forgotten: pine resin, snow-melt, distance.

At some point in the black hours, I got up.

I won't pretend it was courage. It was closer to drowning—the sense that I could not breathe in that house one more minute with the box unopened, that whatever truth Corvane had sealed in iron was pressing outward against the walls of my silence, and that Marrec's polite knives were already inside the valley, already sharpening questions I had no answers for. Three days, Corvane had said. Or grey riders asking about names. The grey riders had come a full day early, and they were asking about names.

The cellar was dark and smelled of sand and burnt paper. I lit one candle, knelt at the false wall, and pressed my palm where his had been. The stone swung inward with the same soft grind, and the cavity breathed out air that had been sealed for years, air that smelled of oilcloth and patience.

The box opened easily. The wax cracked along the seal like thin ice, and the mountain-over-three-bars split neatly in two, and I lifted the lid.

Inside: a folded map, brittle with age. A ring on a chain—heavy gold, set with a stone the colour of dried blood, worked with a thorn motif I didn't recognize and had certainly never seen in the valley. And a letter, written in Corvane's precise, upright hand, dated eleven years ago.

Eleven years. He had written this before I was trusted with the ledgers. Before I was given the border watch. He had carried this knowledge through every quiet morning of my apprenticeship, every lesson, every meal, and had waited for the world to force it loose.

I unfolded the letter with hands that would not stop shaking.

It began with coordinates—numbers and compass marks I copied onto my palm without understanding, points somewhere far beyond the northern moors. Then a name, underlined twice in ink gone brown: **Veyra Thorn**.

And beneath the name and the numbers, a single line, in handwriting that pressed hard enough to tear the page:

*the valley was built on a grave.*

I read it again. And again. The candle flame stood perfectly still, as though it too were waiting.

Built on a grave. Whose grave? Where? And why did the name Thorn—Thorn, like the war, like the war they'd ended sixty years ago, like the word every adult in Hollowmere lowered their voice around—why did that name sit in my guildmaster's most secret letter, addressed to me?

Outside, above the open pass where stars should have been, the sky hung black and starless, and the wind came down off the mountains carrying the smell of snow and pine and distance, and for the first time in my life I understood that the valley I loved was not a home.

It was a lid.

And underneath it, something had begun to move.
