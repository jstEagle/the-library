## 7. Grey Riders at the Gate

There is a particular smell to a valley that has been lied to for a long time. I've never been able to name it precisely—something like bread and river water and old varnish—but I know it when I walk into it, the way a physician knows fever by putting two fingers to a wrist. Hollowmere had it in abundance. The valley smelled of comfort, and comfort, in my experience, is almost always evidence.

I rode down from the pass with six riders behind me and my face arranged into its most disarming configuration: the slight smile of a man who is tired, reasonable, and no threat whatsoever. It's a good face. I've used it on smugglers, ambassadors, and one archduke who later wept into his wine. The trick is not to look clever. Clever men make people guard their tongues. You want to look like someone whose opinion doesn't matter much—someone people talk *over*.

"Marrec," said Corporal Vess beside me, squinting at the village. "It looks like a painting."

"It does," I agreed. "That's what worries me."

Because here is what I knew when my horse set hoof on Hollowmere mud: three days ago, every star over this valley went out at once, and the border wards—wards that had held for sixty years, wards that were supposed to be as permanent as geology—collapsed between one heartbeat and the next. Here is what else I knew: the Wardens' Guild that maintained those wards had reported them sound eleven days before they fell. And here is the third thing, the thing I kept folded up in the back of my mind where I keep all the things that don't fit: guilds do not fail suddenly. Guilds are murdered.

The crown wanted me to find out whether the collapse was accident or attack. The crown did not particularly care which answer I brought home, so long as I brought home only one of them. There are questions the Chancellery asks because it wants answers, and questions it asks because it wants the asking seen to be done. This was the second kind. Which meant my real orders lived in a second letter, sealed in wax the color of dried blood, and that letter said, in essence: *find the truth, then decide how much of it survives the journey back.*

I am very good at deciding how much truth survives a journey. It is, if I'm honest, the only thing I've ever been good at.

* * *

The miller's wife was first, because millers' wives are always first—they see everything that comes up the road and have usually formed three opinions about it before you've dismounted.

"Inquisitor," she said, wiping floury hands on an apron that had clearly been put on fresh for the occasion. "What a blessing. We thought—" she caught herself, smiled wider, "—well, with the stars going out and all, folk have been frightened."

"Frightened," I repeated, warmly. "I can imagine. Tell me, madam—when the stars went out, did you notice anything else? Sounds? Lights? Anything from the direction of the guildhouse?"

"From the—" Her eyes flicked left. Just once, just a fraction, toward the low stone building on the hill with its crooked lamp-tower. Then back to me, smooth as a well-oiled hinge. "Nothing, sir. It was night. We were abed."

"You were abed," I said, "on the night the sky over your home turned into a blank page for the first time in sixty years."

"People sleep badly these days," she said. "But sleep nonetheless."

I complimented her bread—I hadn't eaten any, but her hands were floured and it seemed polite—and moved on. In my notebook, under her name, I wrote: *liar, but a practiced one. Lying for someone.* Villagers lie badly for themselves and beautifully for each other; it's one of the few redeeming features of small communities, and one of the most inconvenient features of my profession.

By midday I'd spoken to the smith, who told me with great sincerity that he'd slept through the end of the world; the ferryman, who'd also slept through it; and a boy of perhaps nine who told me, with the magnificent indifference of children to adult catastrophes, that he'd seen "a green light go up off the stones like a bird made of sparks, and then everything went dark, and then Da pulled me inside and made me drink nettle tea." I wrote down *bird made of sparks* and underlined it twice. Children and drunkards are the only honest witnesses in any jurisdiction, and the boy had the added advantage of having no idea he was being dangerous.

The pattern assembled itself while I ate a lunch of grey cheese in the tavern: everyone in Hollowmere had been asleep. Everyone in Hollowmere had seen nothing. Everyone in Hollowmere looked, when they mentioned the guildhouse, at the guildhouse—and then away, quickly, like people checking that a door is still locked.

So. The valley was lying. The question that interested me was not *whether*, but *for whom*. A whole village does not coordinate a lie overnight. Either they were all protecting the same secret—which meant the secret was old, communal, load-bearing—or they were all afraid of the same person. In my experience those two categories overlap more than anyone would like to admit.

"The guildmaster," I said to the tavern keeper, refilling my cup myself because he kept finding reasons to be elsewhere. "Corvane. What sort of man is he?"

"Good man," he said instantly. Too instantly. "Kept the lamps lit. Taught his apprentice letters. Never raised his voice."

"Apprentice?"

"Aye." Something moved behind the man's eyes—not guilt exactly, but its cousin, the one that lives next door and borrows tools. "Emrys Winterbourne. Quiet lad. Keeps to himself."

"And where is Master Winterbourne now?"

"Asleep, likely. He keeps the night watch." The keeper said it easily enough, but his hand had stopped wiping the same clean tankard, and there is no tell in the world more reliable than a man polishing a glass that is already clean.

Night watch. On the exact night the wards fell. I thanked him, paid too much for the cheese, and went out into the street feeling the particular pleasure I always feel when a shape starts to emerge from fog—the pleasure, I should say, that I have learned to distrust most, because the shapes you *want* to see are the ones most often drawn by someone else's hand.

* * *

I saved the apprentice for last. Not out of any great cunning—though I'll admit the ordering looked like cunning afterward—but because the guildhouse itself came first, and what I found there rearranged my priorities considerably.

The building was cold. That sounds unremarkable until you understand what it meant: a Warden's guildhouse, even a dying one, even one tending lamps that hadn't held true magic in decades, should have held warmth the way a church holds silence. The ward-lamps along the walls were dark, and not merely unlit—*drained*, emptied to the glass, the way a cup is empty after someone has drunk from it rather than after it has simply never been filled. And in the cellar, ash. Fresh ash in the hearth, still warm at the center when I knelt and pressed my fingers into it, and in the ash, the corner of something that had burned incompletely. Wax. Sealing wax, royal grey, the color used only for Chancellery correspondence.

Someone had burned documents in a hurry, and the documents had been ours.

I stood there in the cellar with warm ash on my fingers and felt the case tilt beneath me, the way a floor tilts when you realize the house is built over a hollow. Corvane had reported the wards sound. Corvane's wards had fallen. Corvane had burned Chancellery correspondence the same night. Any one of those could be innocent. Three of them together were a confession wearing a very bad disguise.

And yet the man himself was gone. Vanished into the same night as the stars, leaving behind an apprentice, a cold house, and a story the whole valley seemed to have agreed upon before breakfast.

Which brings me, at last, to Emrys Winterbourne.

* * *

He came down the hill at dusk with a lantern in his hand and the expression of a young man who has been told visitors are coming and has spent the intervening hours failing to think of anything natural to say about it.

I watched him cross the yard, and I will confess something professionally embarrassing: for about four seconds, I underestimated him. He had that quality—openness, call it, or the appearance of it—that makes people want to feed him soup and tell him their troubles. Young, tired-looking, ink stains on his fingers, a face built for honesty. I thought: *this will take ten minutes.*

In fairness to myself, it took eleven.

"Inquisitor Marrec," I said, extending a hand. "Royal Chancellery. I hope we're not intruding."

"You're not—no, sir, welcome, it's—can I offer you tea?" He shook my hand with the grip of someone who has shaken perhaps forty hands in his life and remembers all of them.

"I'd murder for tea, frankly. The cheese in this valley could stand trial itself."

He laughed. It was a real laugh, startled out of him, and I noted it down internally as both a point in his favor and a warning: he laughed before he remembered to be afraid. That meant the fear was new. That meant whatever he was carrying, he'd picked it up recently.

We sat in the guildhouse kitchen, which was aggressively tidy in the way of rooms where someone had recently cleaned in order to have something to do besides think. He made tea with careful, competent movements—mundane craft, no cantrips, though I noticed he arranged the cups with a precision that suggested habits drilled deep—and I let the silence stretch just long enough to become his problem instead of mine.

"Terrible business, the sky," I said at last, conversationally. "You're the night watchman, I understand."

"For the guild, yes. The border stones. Lamps and such." He handed me my cup. His own he held without drinking, which I marked.

"Lamps and such. And on the night the stars went out—you were on watch?"

"Yes."

"What did you see?"

"Nothing." He said it fast, and then, hearing how it sounded, tried to build on it. "That is—I saw the stars go out. Obviously. But nothing before. No warning signs. The lamps were fine, the wards read stable, and then—" his hands made a small helpless gesture, "—they didn't."

"They read stable," I repeated gently. "You checked them yourself?"

"Yes."

"When?"

"That evening. Before full dark."

"And the wards fell when?"

"Sometime past midnight. I couldn't say exactly. Time gets strange, out at the stones, in the dark."

"Mm." I sipped my tea. It was excellent tea, which annoyed me slightly; I'd been prepared to hold the cheese against him, not the tea. "Master Winterbourne, help me with something. Sixty years, these wards have stood. Your guildmaster has tended them, what, twenty years? Thirty?"

"Twenty-six," he said, and then looked briefly horrified at knowing the number so precisely.

"Twenty-six years. And in all that time, has the watch ever once failed to notice a ward failing? Even a flicker? Even a dimming?"

"No," he said quietly.

"So either every warden for sixty years was vigilant, and the wards failed in a single hour, without warning, on the one night the watch happened to be kept by the guild's newest and least experienced member—" I paused, letting the arithmetic sit on the table between us like a third guest, "—or something else happened. Which do you think it was?"

I watched him work through it. Genuinely watched—he had no talent whatsoever for concealing the machinery. Fear first, crossing his face like weather. Then the instinct to defend Corvane, arriving a half-second late and visibly unwelcome. Then—this was the interesting part—a flash of something that wasn't fear or loyalty at all. Doubt. Real doubt, aimed inward, the look of a man who has spent three days doing the same arithmetic I'd just done aloud and hasn't liked his own answers any better.

"I think," he said carefully, "that I checked the lamps, and they were fine, and then they weren't, and I don't know why. And I think if I knew why, I'd tell you."

There it was. The flaw in the glaze. *If I knew why, I'd tell you*—which is a conditional, and conditionals are where liars live. An honest man says *I don't know why*. A liar says *if I knew, I'd tell you*, because the conditional preserves a future in which he might know and might choose otherwise. He'd offered me, quite sincerely, a door he intended to keep locked.

"Of course you would," I said warmly, and smiled, and let him believe the smile meant I believed him.

We talked another quarter hour—about the guild's history, about the valley, about his master, whom he described in terms so carefully neutral they rang like a struck bell. *A good teacher. Strict, but fair. He knows more than anyone about the old wards.* Every sentence accurate and every sentence hollow, a portrait painted entirely in outlines with the interior scraped out. When I asked where Corvane was now, Emrys Winterbourne looked at the table and said, "Traveling," and the word came out of him like a tooth.

"Traveling," I echoed. "Without telling his apprentice."

"He left a note."

"May I see it?"

And here—here is the moment I want recorded, whoever eventually reads this file, because it's the moment I understood I was not dealing with a boy who had stumbled into a conspiracy but with a boy who was *becoming* something, rapidly and against his will. Because he didn't fumble. He didn't say *it's lost* or *it was private*. He looked me directly in the eye—his first successful act of the entire evening—and said:

"It burned. With the rest of the papers. I'm sorry."

A perfect lie. Calm, plausible, unfalsifiable, delivered with steady eyes. Except for one thing, one small thing, the kind of thing I collect the way other men collect coins: he said it *too smoothly*. The stumbles earlier in the conversation had been genuine; this polish was manufactured. People who lie badly and then suddenly lie well are the most dangerous witnesses in existence, because the improvement itself is evidence. Sometime between the stars going out and my arrival, someone had taught this boy to lie—or he'd taught himself, in a cold kitchen, practicing sentences alone.

I finished my tea. I thanked him. I told him I'd be in the village a few days more, asked a scattering of harmless-seeming questions about lamp oil and supply ledgers, and took my leave with every appearance of satisfaction.

At the door, I paused. "One more thing, Master Winterbourne. Purely curiosity. The lamp you're holding—it's lit, yes? All the valley lamps still burn?"

"Yes," he said. "They're ordinary flame now. Since the wards fell."

"Ordinary flame," I repeated. "All of them?"

"All of them," he said, and his voice did not change at all, and his eyes went—for less than half a second, so briefly I might have invented it—to the lantern in his own hand, and then back to me, and he smiled.

I walked down the hill into the dark with the ash of burned Chancellery wax still under my fingernails, and I thought: *there is one lamp in this valley that isn't burning ordinary flame, and this boy just told me where it is without saying a word.*

* * *

Vess found me at the tavern an hour later, working through my notes by candlelight.

"Well?" she said. "The apprentice?"

"The apprentice," I said, "is a catastrophe in progress. He lies like a man reading from a script he memorized an hour ago—which means the script is recent, which means the role is recent, which means three days ago he was exactly what he appears to be: nobody. And nobody, Vess, does not burn royal correspondence in a cellar."

"You think he burned it?"

"I think his hands smell of smoke and his master is missing and his valley is lying in chorus. I think the wards didn't fail, they were killed, and I think the weapon is sitting on that hill drinking tea and apologizing to me." I turned my notebook toward her: the miller's wife, the smith, the boy with the bird made of sparks, and underlined twice, *Winterbourne — if I knew why, I'd tell you.* "I also think he's not very good at it yet. Which is useful. Men get better at lying with practice, and every day we wait is a day of practice."

Vess sat down across from me, lowering her voice. "Orders? Do we take him?"

"Take him and we get a frightened boy who knows nothing worth knowing, and whatever he *does* know goes silent behind a cell door. No." I closed the notebook. "We let him run. Watch the guildhouse. Watch the pass road. If Corvane left him instructions—and Corvane left *something*, men like that always leave something—the boy will move on them soon. Frightened people with secrets always move. They can't help it. A secret is a stone in the shoe; sooner or later they stop walking to deal with it, and that's when you step out of the hedge."

"And if he runs instead?"

"Then he runs *toward* whatever matters most to him," I said, "and I have spent twenty years learning to follow frightened men home."

She nodded and went to arrange the watches. I stayed at the table a while longer, turning my cup slowly, thinking about that half-second glance at the lantern. Thinking about the word *traveling*, spoken like a tooth being pulled. Thinking about a boy who had laughed at my joke before remembering to be afraid.

Here is the trouble with my profession, the thing they never put in the recruitment speeches: you spend so long reading people that you start believing you can't be wrong. And I was not wrong about Emrys Winterbourne. I want that understood. Every mark I made against him was true—the conditional, the polished lie, the glance at the lamp. My error was smaller and far more expensive: I assumed that because he was lying, he was hiding something *from* me.

It did not occur to me—not yet, not that night—that a man might lie to everyone equally. That he might be standing in the middle of a chessboard on which kings and rebels and dead mentors had all placed their pieces, holding the only piece none of them controlled, trying desperately to learn the rules of a game he hadn't agreed to play.

I would learn it. Later. At considerable cost to us both.

For now, I blew out my candle, climbed the stairs to my room, and lay listening to the valley breathe—this soft, complicit, sleeping valley—and somewhere out in that darkness, I'd have sworn I heard a stable door open quietly, and hooves on frost, and a rider taking the pass road north at a pace that had already decided not to come back.

I smiled into the dark. Run, then, Master Winterbourne. Run toward whatever matters most.

I had already sent a rider ahead to watch the border stones.
