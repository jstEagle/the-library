## 8. Over the Border Stone

I had always assumed that if I ever left Hollowmere, it would be with a packed bag, a tearful goodbye at the mill, and Sela shouting something embarrassing after me until I was out of earshot. Instead I left before sunrise, in my nightshirt, carrying a dead man's letter, a cold heel of bread, and a lie I'd told a royal inquisitor with all the grace of a dropped crock.

So. Not the leaving I'd planned. But then, nothing about that week had gone to plan.

The valley was silver-grey when I came down from the guildhouse, mist pooled in the hollows like spilled milk. Somewhere behind me, smoke still rose from Corvane's study — he'd burned everything, right down to the ledgers, and then he'd walked into the dark without so much as a backward glance. *Say nothing,* he'd said. *Touch no one. Wait.* As though waiting were something I'd ever been good at.

I passed the miller's fence and did not look toward the mill, because looking would have meant stopping, and stopping would have meant explaining, and explaining would have meant admitting that I didn't know what I was doing. Which was true, but there's a difference between knowing a thing and saying it out loud where Sela can hear you. She'd have talked me out of it in under a minute. She's very good. It's her worst quality.

The border stones sat on the eastern rise where the valley narrowed to a throat of granite and gorse. Sixty years ago, they said, you could walk through. Then the Wardens' Guild raised the wards, and the world outside became a story grandmothers told: cities with towers of glass, roads paved with light, kings and wars and winters that actually deserved the name. Inside, we had lamps that didn't light, a guild with one apprentice, and weather so mild the farmers had forgotten what frost looked like.

I'd tended those border stones every night for three years. I knew them the way you know your own teeth — which one leaned, which one hummed faintly if you pressed your ear against it, which one had a crack shaped like a river delta running down its face. The wards were supposed to be dying anyway. Corvane had told me that much truth: the old magic was thinning, guttering like a lamp low on oil. My job was to keep the lamps polished and pretend otherwise, because pretending otherwise was apparently a full-time profession in our guild.

But last night, during my watch, the stars had gone out.

All of them. At once. No clouds, no moonrise to explain it — just a sky wiped clean, like a slate before a lesson. And the wards hadn't faded. They'd *fallen*. I'd felt it go through me like a rope snapping somewhere behind my ribs. Cold air had come pouring over the stones for the first time in living memory, and the mist on the eastern rise had turned to actual frost, sharp and glittering, and I had stood there with my lantern shaking in my hand thinking: *this is not decay. Decay doesn't have a direction.*

Then Marrec had come asking his soft questions this morning, and Corvane had gone up in smoke and secrets, and here I was.

I stopped ten paces from the stones and made myself look at them properly, because some part of me still wanted an excuse to turn around.

They were wrong. That's the only word for it. For three years they'd been grey, patient things, humming their thin note. Now the hum was gone — and in its absence the silence had a texture, like the quiet in a house where someone has just died. Frost furred the granite in patterns too regular to be natural: spirals, whorls, little constellations of ice that matched, exactly, the sky I'd memorized on a thousand watches. Except the stars were back now, fading into dawn, and the frost-patterns didn't match anymore. The wards hadn't just fallen. Whatever map they'd been holding up had come loose at the edges.

"You're talking yourself out of it," I said aloud, because nobody was there to hear me be ridiculous. "You've been talking yourself out of it since the guildhouse. If you walk home now, you'll spend the rest of your life polishing lamps and wondering."

That wasn't entirely fair. There were better reasons to go than curiosity. Marrec's questions had been too precise — he'd asked about the guild's founding charter, about Corvane's teachers, about whether any records survived from *before* the wards. Nobody asks those questions unless they're hunting something. And whatever he was hunting, Corvane had handed me a letter and run, which meant either I was bait, or I was cargo. Neither option appealed.

Also — and I want to be honest, because honesty is the one luxury this story has left me — there was the other thing. The thing I'd hidden from Corvane for two years. The seventh lamp on the northern line, the one that flickered with real light whenever I got close. Real light. Gold-white, alive, warm enough to feel on my knuckles in the dead of a magicless valley. Lamps don't do that. Lamps don't choose. Unless, of course, they weren't really lamps.

I'd told myself it was a flaw in the ward-stone beneath it. A leak. A quirk. I was very good at telling myself things.

I took the last step.

Nothing happened. That was the strange part — I'd braced for a wall, a shock, the old stories' warning about flesh meeting ward-edge. But the wards were dead, and the space between the stones was just space, cold morning air smelling of gorse and wet granite, and beyond it a road I had only ever seen in drawings: rutted, muddy, ordinary, running downhill through brown hills toward a horizon with no wards on it anywhere.

I stood on the far side for a long moment, feeling profoundly cheated. Sixty years of sealed borders, a lifetime of longing, and the other side of the miracle was... a cart track.

Then the world noticed me.

It started in my teeth. A high, fine vibration, like biting foil, except it was coming from everywhere — the ground, the air, the frost melting off the stones behind me. Then my ears popped, and the sound arrived properly: a hum, vast and layered, a chord held by a thousand invisible throats. The grass at my feet shivered though there was no wind. Dew rose off the ground in threads of silver and hung there, refusing to fall. Down the hillside, a hawthorn tree bloomed — in autumn, in seconds, white flowers bursting open along its black branches like applause.

And it hurt.

I don't know how else to put it, and believe me, I tried, later, many times, in front of people who understood these things better than I did. It hurt the way bright light hurts eyes kept long in the dark. The valley had been a dim room; I had grown dim to match. Now the whole world was shouting magic at me and every part of me that had spent twenty years learning to be quiet was flinching. My vision swam. The horizon doubled, trebled. I went down on my knees in the mud of the road and was sick, elegantly, into the ditch, while a tree bloomed at me.

"Right," I said, wiping my mouth. "Noted."

Because here's the thing — even kneeling in the dirt, shaking, tears freezing at the corners of my eyes, some stubborn clerk-part of my mind was taking notes. The dew hanging midair: that was magic unbound from any ward, loose in the world like water from a burst dam. The hawthorn: seasons had been held steady inside the valley by the same wards that held out the world, and outside, apparently, magic and season both ran wild. The pain: mine, specifically, or nearly — because Sela had once brushed that same northern lamp and felt nothing but a draft, while I'd felt it sing. Whatever the wards had done to the valley, they'd done something extra to me. Or I was something extra the wards had been doing.

*The valley was built on a grave.* Corvane's handwriting, cramped and urgent. I touched the letter through my shirt and did not take it out. Not yet. Not on an empty stomach beside my own sick. Some revelations deserve better staging.

I got up. The hum settled from a shriek to an ache, the way ears adjust to altitude. I could breathe again, mostly. I picked up my satchel, took stock — bread, flint, the letter, Corvane's spare cloak rolled tight, four coppers and a button I was emotionally attached to — and started down the Road of Broken Bells.

That's what Maren called it later, and the name stuck, because names do when they're earned. But that morning it was just a pilgrim road, worn smooth in the middle by centuries of feet, and it confused me immediately, because pilgrims need somewhere to go, and I'd grown up believing there was nowhere. The road argued otherwise. It ran confidently downhill, past waymark stones carved with symbols I half-recognized from the guild's oldest books — spirals, thorns, a bell with its clapper snapped off. Every few hundred yards stood a little shrine-post, and hanging from each shrine-post, rusted green with age, was a small bronze bell.

Sixty years of silence, and every bell on the road had been cut mute. Somebody had wanted no ringing on the borderlands. Somebody had wanted the approaches to Hollowmere quiet.

The first village came into view near midmorning: a scatter of thatched roofs around a well, smoke rising straight in the windless cold. I was so hungry and so relieved to see chimney smoke that I forgot to be careful, which, if you're keeping notes, is the defining feature of my character. I walked in like I owned the place.

Nobody owned the place. That was obvious within thirty steps.

The well was whispering. I heard it before I saw it — a dry, papery voice rising from the stone throat, murmuring in Old Thorn dialect, and my skin prickled before my mind caught up, because I *knew* that dialect. Not from books. From lullabies. From a woman's voice singing me to sleep when I was too young to remember being sung to, except I did remember, the way you remember warmth rather than words. I'd assumed they were valley songs. Everyone sang them. Everyone, I realized now, standing in a foreign village listening to a haunted well speak my mother's language — everyone in a sixty-year-old sealed valley sang them.

I gripped the well's rim and listened. The voice was fragmentary, looping, a broken record of grief:

*"...the heir, the heir, they took the heir..."*

A door banged. A woman came out of the nearest cottage with a laundry paddle raised like a weapon, saw me — muddy, grey-faced, obviously ill — and lowered it halfway. Behind her, children crowded the window. One of them was looking at me with eyes that glowed faintly gold, like coals banked under ash. He couldn't have been more than six. He waved.

"Where are you from, then?" the woman asked. Her accent was thick, clipped, nothing like the rounded valley speech I'd grown up with. "You look half-dead."

"Hollowmere," I said.

The paddle came back up. So did every shutter on the street, one after another, like eyes closing.

"Hollowmere." She said it the way you'd say the name of a disease. "Warden-born?"

"Apprentice. Last one."

"Last one," she repeated softly, and something in her face shifted — pity, or contempt, or both wearing the same coat. "Come to see what your masters buried?"

"I don't—" I stopped. Swallowed. The well was whispering again, *the heir, the heir*, and the child at the window was still watching me with his banked-fire eyes, and my head was pounding with the world's loud chord, and I suddenly understood that I had crossed a border into a country where my guild's name was a curse and my ignorance was not going to be accepted as an excuse. "I'm trying to find out what they buried. That's why I'm here."

She studied me for a long moment. Laundry paddle still raised, but loosely now. "There's a hedge-witch down the lane past the chapel ruin. Maren. She takes strays." A pause. "She also decides which strays keep walking. Mind how you answer her."

"What happens if I answer badly?"

"Same as anything, love." She turned back to her door. "You keep walking."

* * *

Maren's cottage squatted at the end of the lane like a toad that had decided architecture was beneath it. Herb bundles hung under the eaves; a goat watched me from the garden with the flat hostility of its kind. Before I could knock, the door opened and a small, wiry woman with iron-grey braids looked me over from boots to hairline.

"Warden boy," she said. Not a question. "Smell the guildhouse tallow on you. In."

"It's that obvious?"

"You've got the walk. Shoulders in, eyes down, like you're always apologizing to a doorframe." She stepped aside. "Sit. Drink. Don't touch the jars."

The interior was warm, cluttered, and smelled of rosemary and woodsmoke — the first kind smells I'd encountered since crossing, and my homesickness hit me sideways, sudden and stupid, over dried herbs. I sat on a stool by the fire and drank the tea she pushed at me and let my hands stop shaking, and she watched me with sharp black eyes and said nothing for a long time, which I came to understand was her primary method of interrogation.

Finally: "The stars went out two nights past."

"Yes."

"And your master's wards fell with them."

"How did you—"

"Boy, the wells have been whispering for two days. The children glow. My bees built comb in the shape of a spiral. When the wards on your valley fall, the whole border feels it, like a tooth coming loose." She leaned forward. "What I want to know is why a Warden apprentice is sitting in my kitchen instead of standing guard over his master's precious silence."

I should have lied. I want that on the record: I knew, even then, that lying was the correct move, and I opened my mouth to do it, and what came out instead was the truth, entire and unpolished, because I was tired and hurting and a thousand miles — no, forty miles, but it felt like a thousand — from anyone who'd ever fed me.

"The wards didn't fail," I said. "They were killed. My guildmaster burned his papers and vanished in the night and left me a letter I haven't opened, and a royal inquisitor named Dain Marrec is asking questions in my valley with that particular smile men get when they already know the answers, and I think whatever's in this letter is worth killing for, and I think—" My voice cracked. I took a breath and finished it. "I think it might be worth dying for too. I just don't know whose death buys what yet."

Silence. The fire ticked. The goat bleated once outside, philosophically.

Maren held out her hand. "Letter."

"It's addressed to me."

"I'm not asking to read it, am I? Give it here." She turned it over in her fingers, and I watched her face change as she found the seal — a thorned branch breaking through a circle of seven stars, pressed in dark green wax. Her thumb hovered over it without touching. When she spoke again, her voice had gone quiet and careful, the voice you use in a sickroom.

"Where did he tell you to take this?"

"He didn't say. Just coordinates, folded inside. And a name."

"What name?"

"Veyra Thorn."

Maren closed her eyes. For a moment she looked very old, and very tired, and like a woman hearing a bell ring that she'd spent sixty years believing was broken. Outside, far off down the road, a horse neighed — and then another, and then the particular rhythmic clop of shod hooves moving at an unhurried, official pace.

She opened her eyes and thrust the letter back at me.

"Kitchen's got a back door," she said. "Goats won't mind. Move."
