## 9. The Road of Broken Bells

I had crossed the border stone at dawn, and by midmorning I had learned three things about the world outside Hollowmere.

First: it was loud. Not with sound, exactly—though the pilgrim road carried more carts and voices in an hour than our valley saw in a season—but loud underneath. A hum in my teeth, a pressure behind my eyes, like standing too close to a struck bell that never finished ringing. Magic, I supposed. Sixty years of it, pooled and thickening while our valley sat dry as a drained pond. My body had been raised on emptiness, and emptiness was all it knew how to drink.

Second: the world was beautiful in ways that made me angry. Nobody in Hollowmere had told me the hills turned gold this time of year. Nobody had mentioned that rivers could be the color of hammered tin, or that hedges grew waist-high and wild instead of clipped into obedient squares. Every mile of wonder felt like a small theft. All those years Corvane sent me out to mend lamps along the border, and never once said, *Emrys, walk the other way and look.*

Third—and this is the one that matters—the world outside was hurt.

I didn't understand that yet when I passed the first shrine. It stood where the road bent around a hill, a little stone alcove with a bell hanging under its roof, the kind of thing pilgrims ring for luck or forgiveness or just to prove they passed. The bell was broken. Not cracked—*broken*, torn open like a chestnut shell, and the metal around the tear was fused and blackened, as if something inside it had burned its way out.

There were more shrines. More bells. Every one of them ruined the same way, spaced along the road like a sentence written in a language I almost spoke.

By noon I reached the first village, and that's where I stopped pretending this was a pilgrimage.

* * *

The village was called Bellharrow—I learned that later, from a signpost someone had defaced with a scorch mark shaped like a handprint. I came in hungry, with coin enough for bread and a plan simple enough to fit in a thimble: buy food, ask nothing, keep walking toward the capital, because the letter in my shirt said the answers lived there.

The letter. I should say what I knew of it, since I'd only had it one night and one terrible morning. Coordinates. A name: Veyra Thorn. And one line, in Corvane's cramped hand: *the valley was built on a grave.* I'd read it perhaps forty times between the guildhouse and the pass, hoping the words would rearrange themselves into something survivable. They hadn't.

So: bread. Keep walking. Ask nothing.

But you can't walk through a village and ask nothing, not when the village is like Bellharrow. The well at its center wasn't a well anymore. Oh, it had the shape of one—a stone circle, a roofed frame, a bucket on a rope—but the water below didn't sit still. It moved against the wind. And when I leaned over the lip, the way any traveler checks a strange well, it whispered.

Not words. Not yet. A sound like a crowd heard through a wall, murmuring, rising and falling, and every few seconds a single voice would surface—clear, wet, close to my ear though the water was thirty feet down—and say something half-formed. *Cold.* Or maybe *gold.* Or maybe a name I couldn't catch.

I straightened up so fast I banged my head on the well's roof beam.

"You're staring," said a woman nearby, wringing laundry with hands gone red from cold. She didn't seem alarmed by me. She seemed tired of everything else. "Everyone stares the first week. Then you stop looking at it, and then you stop hearing it, and then it's just the well."

"It talks?"

"It remembers." She snapped a sheet flat. "Wells remember water, is the old saying. This one remembers more than water now." Her eyes flicked to me, took in my valley-made boots, my Warden's Guild cloak with its faded lamp-and-key badge, and something in her face closed like a door. "You're one of theirs."

"I'm—" I started to say I was nothing, which was mostly true, but she'd already turned back to her washing, and I understood I'd been dismissed by someone who had decided what I was before I opened my mouth. That was new too. In Hollowmere everyone knew exactly what I was: the apprentice. Out here I was a category.

I bought bread anyway, from a boy who wouldn't meet my eyes, and as I left I passed the schoolhouse—or what had been the schoolhouse. Its windows were painted over from the inside. Whitewash, thick coats of it, and beneath the newest layer I could see older layers, and beneath those, faintly, shapes. Handprints. Small ones.

A man was nailing a board across the door. He worked steadily, without hurry, the way you do a job you've done many times.

"Is the school closed?" I asked.

He didn't stop hammering. "School's fine. School's empty." Hammer. Hammer. "Children learn at home now."

"Where are the children?"

Hammer. Hammer. Then he did look at me, and his face was the face of a man carrying something heavy down a long stair, careful not to spill it. "You're from the sealed valley." He said it flatly, no question in it. "Lucky you. Whatever your masters kept out, they also kept in. Out here, sixty years of it came home all at once, and it came home *hungry*." He set down the hammer. "Go look at the Merrick farm if you want to know where the children are. Or don't. Most people choose don't."

I chose don't. I'm not proud of it, but I chose don't, and I walked out of Bellharrow with my bread untouched and my heart going like a rabbit's, and behind me I swear the well whispered louder, as if it had noticed me noticing.

* * *

The second village had no name left on its signpost, only burn marks, and I nearly walked past it entirely because the road there was lined with bell-shrines that hadn't just broken—they'd *answered*. Each shattered bell had a scorch trail leading away from it, across fields, converging on the village like iron filings around a magnet. Whatever had come out of those bells on the night the stars went out, it had been called somewhere, and the somewhere was here.

I should have walked past. Every instinct Corvane ever beat into me said: *strange things on a road are other people's problems; your problem is the border and the lamps and the letter.* But the light was failing, my legs had gone watery, and the village had one building with smoke rising from it—a low house at the far end, stone-walled, herbs hanging in the eaves, a garden that grew in rows too neat for accident.

She was waiting on the step before I knocked. Old, small, wrapped in a shawl the color of rust, with eyes pale as skimmed milk and sharp as awls.

"You've been followed by three villages' worth of bad news," she said, "and you're wearing a dead guild's badge, and you're carrying paper you keep touching through your shirt like a man touches a wound. Come in before the road decides it wants you back."

"I haven't told you anything about myself."

"You told me plenty. You flinch at the hum. Your shadow doesn't sit right—you've spent your life inside a ward-line and it shows, like a tan from a different sun. And you looked at my chimney smoke the way starving men look at bread." She stepped aside. "Maren. Hedge-witch, retired, insufficiently retired. Well? Are you coming in or do you enjoy suffering?"

I came in. Of course I came in.

Her house smelled of rosemary and woodsmoke and something older underneath, like the memory of a smell. One room, mostly—kitchen, hearth, bed behind a curtain, shelves crowded with jars whose contents I decided firmly not to speculate about. A cat regarded me from a chair with the expression of a landlord assessing a tenant.

"Sit. Eat. Don't touch the blue jar." She put a bowl of barley stew in front of me without asking whether I wanted it, which was correct, because I would have said no out of pride and then wept into it. "Now. You'll tell me who you are, eventually, but I'll start us off. On the night the stars went out, every old working within forty miles woke up. Bells that had been blessed and bound for generations tore themselves apart. Wells started remembering. Hedges walked—don't make that face, boy, the hedge by the mill walks two feet every night and we've agreed to ignore each other. Sixty years of quiet magic, all of it coiled tight under the wards, and when the wards dropped, it all sprang at once." She stirred her own stew without eating. "Your valley's wards didn't fail, did they. Something happened to them."

"How do you know about the wards?"

"Because the night they fell, I felt it. Everyone with a drop of the gift felt it—a great intake of breath, like the world sucking air through a wound. And then the pressure came *out*. Toward the borders. Toward the places that had been held shut longest." Her milk-pale eyes fixed on me. "And two days later, a boy walks up my road wearing the badge of the guild that held the shutters, flinching at magic like it bites, carrying a letter he's afraid of. So. What happened to the wards?"

I looked at my stew. At the cat. At the fire, which burned with a steadiness I found suddenly, desperately comforting—one thing in the world still doing what it was supposed to do.

"A man I loved more than my own father burned a cellar full of documents and disappeared into the night," I said. "He left me a letter with coordinates and a dead woman's name and one sentence. And a royal inquisitor is somewhere behind me asking questions I lied badly about. That's what happened to the wards."

Maren was quiet a long moment. Outside, the wind moved through the hedges, and I could have sworn the hedges moved *back*.

"Veyra Thorn," she said at last.

"You know the name?"

"Everyone past fifty knows the name. Girl from the Thorn lands, the ones the crown broke at the end of the war. There was a peace treaty, there were reparations, there was a sealed valley in the mountains that nobody asked too many questions about." She tapped the table once, hard. "And there was a lie. There's always a lie under a peace that clean. I've said so for sixty years and been called a mad old hedge-witch for sixty years, which is the standard wage for saying so." She leaned forward. "Show me the letter."

Every instinct said no. Corvane's voice in my head said no. But Corvane had burned his own history and run, and Marrec's grey riders were somewhere behind me, and this old woman had fed me stew and told me the truth about hedges, and I was so tired of carrying the thing alone.

I gave her the letter.

She read it the way I imagine scholars read scripture—slowly, twice, lips moving on the second pass. Then she set it down, smoothed it flat with both hands, and looked at me with an expression I couldn't read, and didn't like, because it looked like pity.

"Coordinates first," she said. "That's a place in the Thorn lands. Cindermarch way. I know the road." Her finger stopped on the name. "Veyra Thorn. If that woman lives, she'll be fifty-odd now, and she will have spent every one of those years waiting for proof. Do you understand what you're carrying? This isn't a letter, boy. It's a spark, and every dry field in three kingdoms is waiting for it."

"The inquisitor wants it buried. I figured someone else would want it used."

"And which do you want?"

"I want to know what's true," I said, and heard how young it sounded. "Someone built my whole life on top of something, and I want to know what's underneath before everyone starts fighting over the digging rights."

Maren studied me for a long time. The cat jumped down, stretched, and left the room, having apparently concluded I was tolerable.

"You might be the first person to carry that letter for honest reasons," she said finally. "Which means you're the most dangerous kind of courier there is. Men who want it buried can be bribed. Men who want it weaponized can be aimed. But a man who wants the truth—" she shook her head, "—there's no handling him. He has to be protected from himself." She stood, joints cracking like green kindling, and pulled a folded map from a shelf. "Sleep tonight behind a warded door, which mine is, barely. Tomorrow I take you as far as the crossroads at Tarnbeck myself. After that you're on the pilgrim road proper, and the pilgrim road goes to Cindermarch, and Cindermarch goes to whatever's waiting for you."

"Why help me?"

"Because sixty years ago I watched soldiers burn a village two valleys over, and I hid in a root cellar and swore that if the lie that made that burning ever crawled into daylight, I'd spit on it on its way past." She banked the fire. "Also because you eat like a stray dog and it pains me. Sleep."

I slept. Badly, but I slept, and dreamed of a well whispering a name I almost caught, and woke before dawn with Maren already packing bread and cheese into a cloth like the world's sternest mother.

At the door, she pressed the letter back into my hands and held them shut around it a moment longer than necessary.

"One more thing," she said. "On the road tomorrow, you'll pass the Merrick farm. You chose not to look in Bellharrow. Look this time. Whatever you decide to do with that paper—decide knowing what it costs when lies like this one come undone. Not for the guilty. For everyone standing near them when the truth arrives."

* * *

We walked together as far as the shrine at the Tarnbeck crossroads, where the pilgrim road split—west to the river towns, east toward Cindermarch and the Thorn lands. Maren pointed east with her chin.

"That way. Three days if the weather holds, five if it doesn't, forever if you lose your nerve." She squinted at me. "You won't lose your nerve. That's what worries me."

Then she left me, a small rust-colored figure going back down the road the way we'd come, and I stood a moment in the ringing silence of the crossroads before turning east.

The Merrick farm was a mile on. I saw the smoke first—not hearth smoke, but a thin grey thread rising from a field, steady as an offering. Then I saw the field itself, and understood why the man in Bellharrow had nailed his schoolhouse shut.

They had burned it. The whole crop, whatever it had been—standing stalks fused black and glassy at the roots, a circle a hundred yards wide where the soil had cooked to brick. And at the center, planted in rows with terrible care, wooden markers. Dozens of them. Small ones.

I stood at the fence a long time. The wind came off the burnt field and it did not smell like ash. It smelled like the inside of the well at Bellharrow had sounded—old, and crowded, and awake.

Whatever Corvane had helped bury in our valley, it hadn't stayed buried in spirit. It had soaked outward for sixty years, through the wards, into the water and the bells and the children, and when the wards fell, all of it surfaced at once, everywhere, hungry and unquiet. The peace of Hollowmere hadn't just been a lie. It had been a dam, and I was standing downstream of the break.

I thought of Maren's face when she'd read the name Veyra Thorn. Pity. I'd thought it was pity for me. Walking away from the Merrick farm, I began to suspect it had been pity for whoever met me next—because a boy carrying a spark through three kingdoms of dry grass draws company, and I felt it before I saw it, halfway up the next hill: that pressure behind my teeth changing pitch, sharpening, the hum of the world bending toward me like a crowd turning its head.

Behind me, on the road, riders. Grey cloaks. And ahead, at the crossroads shrine where I'd stood minutes before, a figure in ash-grey rags stepping out of the hedgerow as if the hedge had grown her there—tall, weathered, fifty-some years old, watching me with eyes that had clearly spent decades learning exactly what to wait for.

"You're the one from the sealed valley," she called, and her voice carried like a bell that hadn't broken. "I've been following your bad news for two days. I'm Veyra Thorn. And you and I need to talk about that letter before the grey cloaks take it off your corpse."

The hum in my teeth sang one clear note.

Somewhere behind us, the riders spurred forward.
