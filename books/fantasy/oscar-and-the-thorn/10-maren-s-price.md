## 10. Maren's Price

I have kept bees for forty-one years, and I will tell you what I know about secrets: a hive keeps them better than any kingdom. Every bee knows where the good clover is, and not one of them has ever told me. They just go and come back heavy with it, and the whole colony thrives on a treasure nobody speaks of.

That was the old world in miniature, before the wards fell. Magic everywhere, humming under everything like honey under wax, and everyone agreeing—without ever saying it aloud—not to mention it.

Then the sky went dark over Hollowmere way, the cold came down off the mountains like an uninvited relative, and my bees started acting strange. Circling the hives at midnight. Humming in a register that made my teeth ache. And every candle in my cottage burning green at the edges, no matter how many times I trimmed the wicks.

So when the knock came at my door three days after the stars went out—a thin, exhausted knock, the kind a person makes when they've been walking since dawn and crying since noon—I was already in a mood to be suspicious.

"Go away," I called pleasantly.

"Please," said a voice through the wood. Young. Cracked. "The hedge-witch. Maren. Someone said—" A pause, the sound of a boy gathering courage like scattered coins. "Someone said you help people."

"Someone said wrong. I keep bees and hold grudges. Those are my trades."

Silence. Then, so quietly I almost missed it: "Please. I don't know where else to go."

I stood there with my hand on the latch and gave myself the same lecture I'd given myself a thousand times: *Maren, girl, you're seventy years old, you've buried two husbands and one very stupid son, and every single time you've opened your door for a stranger it has cost you something.* 

I opened the door.

He was soaked to the ribs, hollow-cheeked, and carrying his fear around him like a second cloak. Valley-born—you could tell by the pallor, the squint, the way he flinched from the open sky as if it might fall on him. Which, I suppose, in a manner of speaking, it had.

"You'll be wanting soup," I said, "and you'll be wanting to tell me nothing until you've eaten it. Sit."

* * *

Here is what you must understand about Bellhollow, the village I've lived in since I was younger than this boy and stupider than anyone has a right to be: we sit on the pilgrim road, six days' walk from the Hollowmere pass, and for sixty years we have been the last place travelers stopped before they reached a valley they could never enter, or the first place valley-folk stopped after they decided never to leave. We were a doormat village. A threshold. People passed over us without looking down.

We got used to it. There's a dignity in being overlooked, if you can find it. The crown forgot us, the guilds forgot us, and in exchange we were left alone to practice our small heresies—the hedge-crafts, the herb-lore, the little magics that survived in the cracks of a land where magic had supposedly been sealed away. Nobody sealed anything, of course. You can't seal magic. You can only teach it manners, and manners fade in the third generation.

When the wards fell, magic didn't return to Bellhollow gently. It returned like water finding a cracked cellar—everywhere at once, and all of it going somewhere it shouldn't. The miller's youngest daughter woke up with eyes like lantern-glass. Old Tam's well began whispering the names of people who hadn't been born yet. My bees, as I mentioned, lost their minds entirely.

And then, four days after the sky went out, the riders came through. Grey cloaks. Royal colors. Asking questions in that soft voice officials use when the answer doesn't matter because they've already decided what happened.

They asked about a boy traveling alone. They asked whether anyone had sheltered strangers. They left a silver coin on the innkeeper's table, which in Bellhollow is roughly equivalent to leaving a lit fuse.

So when I sat across from this drenched, shivering young man and watched him eat my soup like he'd never seen food before—and maybe he hadn't, valley rations being what they were—I made my calculation quickly. He was running from the grey riders. He'd come to my door because someone in the last village had pointed him here. That someone would be questioned too. Which meant the thread leading to my doorstep was already strung taut, and somewhere behind this boy came men with knives and royal warrants, walking along it hand over hand.

"Maren." He set down the spoon. His hands were still shaking. "Thank you. I only need passage east. Across the moor. There are people—" He stopped himself. Good instinct, stopping himself. Bad execution; his whole face finished the sentence for him. *There are people who want what I'm carrying.*

"Eat," I said again. Then, because I'm old and tired and have run out of patience for the slow approach: "Show me the letter."

He went white. Actually white, like candle-tallow. His hand went to his breast pocket, then froze there.

"How did you—"

"Boy. I've been a hedge-witch on this road for fifty years. Everyone who knocks on my door at night, alone, terrified, and heading east with their hand guarding their chest is carrying a letter. It's practically the village coat of arms." I held out my palm. "Show me."

"I can't show anyone. I promised—"

"You promised whom? A dead man?"

That landed harder than I intended. He flinched as though I'd struck him, and I watched him wrestle with the question, and I knew the answer before he spoke it.

"He might be," Emrys said. "Alive. Or not. He told me not to let anyone read it."

"And did he tell you why?"

"...No."

"No," I agreed. "Because the men who write letters like that always think the secret matters more than the carrier. Sit still." I rose, went to the mantel, and took down the thing I'd sworn I'd never need again: a small lens of horn and river-glass, older than the valley itself, older than the war, handed down through nine generations of women who'd each been told *keep it, but pray you never use it.*

I sat back down across from him and turned the lens in my fingers.

"Now," I said, "you're going to make a choice, and I'm going to make it easy for you. Either you show me that letter, or you walk out my door and take your chances with the grey riders camped at the Crossed Bells inn, asking after exactly one boy traveling alone. Because yes—they're here. They arrived yesterday. Charming men. One of them complimented my bees."

* * *

I want to be fair to him. Most people, faced with that choice, either break immediately or bluster. This boy did neither. He looked at me for a long moment—really looked, weighing me the way you'd weigh a rope before trusting it with your weight—and said:

"If I show you, will you swear to tell me true what you see? Even if it's bad? Especially then?"

Well. That wasn't the answer of a courier. That was the answer of a boy who had already suspected the letter was a wound and wanted someone else to confirm the bleeding.

"I swear it on the hive," I said, which among my kind is a heavier oath than any crown's. Bees don't lie. It's their one great virtue and the reason I prefer them to most people.

His hands trembled as he worked the packet from inside his coat—waxcloth, oilskin, tied with cord, and beneath it all a fold of heavy paper bearing a seal pressed deep in green wax.

I knew that seal.

You have to understand—I knew that seal the way you know a scar on your own child. The interlocked W and T, the wardens' knot, the Winterbourne mark. My grandmother had served the last true Warden of the vale before the Guild closed its doors and became a lamp-lighting society for a border nobody crossed. She used to trace the shape in spilled flour while she talked about the Thorn War she was too young to remember and too honest to pretend she'd forgotten.

"They told us it ended," she'd say. "Nobody tells you how wars end, child. Only that somebody signs something, and everybody stops asking questions, and sixty years later nobody remembers what the questions even were."

I broke the seal. I know—he'd shown me, not handed it over, and breaking another's seal is a trespass. But I am seventy years old and I have learned that some doors, once shown to you, must be opened all the way, because a half-opened truth is more dangerous than a whole one.

I read it once. Then I read it again, slower.

Coordinates. A name: *Veyra Thorn.* And one line, in a cramped, hurried hand that had clearly been written by lamplight by a man expecting not to survive the week:

*The valley was built on a grave.*

"Well," I said. My voice came out steadier than I felt. "There it is."

"There what is?" He leaned forward. "Maren, please—what does it mean? I've read it forty times. I know what the words say. I don't know what they *mean.*"

And here is where I should tell you the truth about myself, since the boy was paying me in honesty and debts should be honored. I could have lied to him. I could have said, *It means nothing, child, an old man's poetry,* and sent him on his way lighter and safer and ignorant. Some part of me—that part which had survived two husbands and one stupid son by knowing when not to look—wanted to say exactly that.

But my grandmother traced that seal in flour. And the name on the page was *Thorn*, and my grandmother's mother had come out of the Thorn lands as a refugee with nothing but a recipe for bread and a hatred she passed down like an heirloom until my generation, in our wisdom, put it in a box and called it history.

"It means," I said slowly, "that the peace we've all been living in—the sixty quiet years, the sealed valley, the wards, the whole arrangement—it isn't a wall. It's a lid. And somebody, somewhere, has been sitting on that lid for sixty years, and now the lid is coming off, and everything underneath it is going to come up at once."

"What's underneath?"

"A grave," I quoted. "And probably more than one. Boy—Emrys, was it?" He nodded. "Emrys. Do you understand what you're carrying? Not what the paper says. What the paper *is.*"

He shook his head.

"That letter is a weapon. Not the kind that cuts—the worse kind. The kind that makes other people cut each other. There are kingdoms on both sides of that moor that have spent sixty years pretending to be at peace, and both of them are sitting on half a truth apiece, and that piece of paper is the other halves. Whoever holds it gets to decide which lie dies first." I pushed the letter back across the table. "And right now, that's you. A wet, starving, nineteen-year-old boy with no sword, no horse, and no idea."

He took the letter back with both hands, careful as a man handling a live coal, and something moved across his face—not fear exactly. Something older than fear. Recognition, maybe. The look of a person realizing that the weight they've been carrying has a name.

"The grey riders," he said quietly. "They're not just looking for me, are they. They're looking for *this.*"

"Now you're learning."

"But why does the crown care? If it's about the valley—if it's old crimes—why send riders now, tonight, into a village full of farmers—"

"Because old crimes are only safe while they stay old," I said. "The moment someone starts digging them up, every crown on the continent has to choose: confess, or bury the digger. Guess which one is cheaper."

* * *

We heard the horses at moonrise.

Not many—three, maybe four, moving at a walk, which is somehow worse than a gallop. Galloping men are in a hurry; walking men are confident. These men were confident.

Emrys was asleep in the chair by the fire, finally, the letter tucked against his chest like a second heart. I was awake, because I am always awake now; the bees won't settle, and when the bees won't settle, neither do I. I watched him sleep for a moment and felt that old, treacherous softness rise in me, and I crushed it flat, because softness gets people killed on nights like this.

"Wake up," I said, and shook his shoulder. "Riders on the road. Coming here, I'd wager, or near enough."

He came awake the way valley folk apparently do—all at once, no grogginess, eyes sharp before his feet hit the floor. Corvane trained him well, whoever Corvane was. I'd gotten the shape of the story out of him over supper: the guildmaster, the burned papers, the letter through the wall, the inquisitor with the smooth voice. Dain Marrec, he'd said the villagers called him. I'd heard the name before, in the bad old stories. Inquisitors with reputations are like storms with names. You remember them precisely because of what they destroyed.

"Back door," I said. "Through the apiary. Follow the fence line east to the sheep track—it climbs past the whispering well, and no rider follows a path they can't see the bottom of. Can you ride?"

"Not well."

"Wonderful. Take the mule, then. She bites, but only people she doesn't respect, so you should get on fine." I was already moving as I talked, pulling my cloak, stuffing bread and cheese and a jar of winter honey into a satchel. "There's a ferryman at Coldford, two days east. Tell him Maren sent you and he'll grumble and take you anyway. Beyond that—" I hesitated. Here was the fork in my own road, and I'd known it was coming since I broke that seal.

Beyond that lay the moorlands. And on the moorlands, these days, moved the Ashcloaks—the descendants of the Thorn survivors, the ones the histories pretended had all died at Cindermarch, the ones who'd been nursing their grief in the high country for sixty years like a fire banked under ash. Every hedge-wife on this road had heard the rumors since the stars went out: the Ashcloaks were moving again. Looking for something. Looking for *someone*, some said.

Looking for proof, said the darkest rumor, the one whispered over fences. Proof that the peace was a theft. Proof worth killing for. Proof worth crowning a king over.

"Maren." He'd caught my hesitation, damn his quick eyes. "What aren't you telling me?"

"Nothing you wouldn't learn in two days anyway." I shoved the satchel into his arms. "The people you're seeking—the name in that letter. Veyra Thorn. Her people watch the moors. If you cross them, be polite, and whatever they offer you, remember this: people who have been robbed for sixty years do not want justice. They want the robbery reversed. It is not the same thing, and the difference will cost you dearly."

The knocking started. Not at my door—at the gate, and then boots on gravel, unhurried, several pairs.

Emrys went gray. "You should let them find me. You shouldn't be involved—"

"Boy, I broke a sixty-year seal tonight and read the family curse aloud in my own kitchen. I'm involved. The question is only how much." I pulled open the loose stone by the hearth—every old house on this road has one; we built them that way during the wars, and we never quite stopped needing them—and gestured him in. "In. Quietly. And Emrys Winterbourne—" He paused at the threshold of the hiding place, looking back at me, and I saw the question forming. I answered it before he could ask. "Yes. I knew the seal. My grandmother served the Wardens. That's my price, boy, and I'm collecting it now: you carry that letter east, and you carry my grandmother's name with it. Sefa. From the Thorn lands. When you find whoever needs to hear the truth—say it was Sefa's granddaughter who broke the seal. Somebody should remember us. Somebody should remember all of us who remembered."

Something flickered in his face—not understanding, not yet, but the promise of it.

"Sefa's granddaughter," he repeated solemnly. "I swear it. On the hive."

"On the hive," I agreed, and shut the stone over him, and turned to answer my door with my heart hammering like a apprentice smith.

* * *

The man at my door was not Marrec himself. I'd know him from the descriptions—tall, courteous, with a voice like warm milk and something cold underneath. This was a lesser instrument: a compact, neat man with a clerk's hands and a soldier's stillness, flanked by two others who stood with the patience of furniture.

"Good evening, mistress," he said, and inclined his head. "Forgive the hour. We're searching for a runaway—a ward of the crown, confused, possibly ill. He may have sought shelter. May we look?"

"Runaways," I said, leaning on the doorframe with all the creakiness I could muster. "In my experience, young men run *from* things, and the crown is usually one of them. Why would one run to a bee-keeper?"

"We don't presume to know his reasoning. Only his direction." His eyes drifted past my shoulder, into my kitchen, and lingered—on the two bowls, on the damp footprints by the hearth that I had, in my hurry, failed to sweep. His gaze rested there perhaps two seconds longer than courtesy allowed.

Then he smiled, and it was a lovely smile, and it frightened me more than a scowl would have.

"You've had a visitor tonight," he said.

"My son's boy came for honey. He's gone home."

"Mm." He produced, from inside his cloak, a small object, and held it up between two fingers: a scrap of green wax, broken, bearing the impression of an interlocked W and T. Found, no doubt, somewhere along the boy's road. "Do you know this seal, mistress?"

Every year of my age settled onto my shoulders at once. Behind the hearthstone, a boy was holding his breath hard enough to crack granite.

"I'm seventy years old," I said. "I know every seal on this road, and most of the people who forged them, and half their sins. That's the Winterbourne mark. The Wardens' Guild. Dead sixty years, give or take. Where did you find it?"

"Where indeed," he said softly, and for a long, long moment we simply regarded one another—two professionals, each estimating the other's price.

Then, from the darkness beyond my yard, came a sound I will hear until I die: my bees. All forty thousand of them, rising from their hives at midnight in a single column, a black rope of fury climbing into the moonlight and hanging there above my garden like a drawn bow.

The clerk's composure flickered—one heartbeat, two. His men stepped back, hands drifting toward weapons that would have been useless and they likely knew it.

"Hedge-craft," he said, and there was something new in his voice—not fear, exactly. Recalculation.

"Old women and their hobbies," I said. "You were leaving, I believe."

He inclined his head, that lovely smile reassembled. "Another time, mistress. Give my regards to your grandson." And they withdrew down the lane, unhurried, confident—men who had learned what they needed and would report it to a man with a warmer smile and colder hands.

I waited until the hoofbeats faded. Then I opened the hearthstone, and Emrys climbed out pale and shaking, staring through my window at the swarm still hanging in the moonlight.

"They protected you," he whispered. "How—"

"Sixty years I've fed them charm and sung to them in the old dialect," I said shortly, because my hands were trembling now that it was over and I hated for anyone to see. "Magic's coming back, boy, and it remembers kindness better than people do. Now listen. They'll be back by dawn with more men and less courtesy. You take the mule and the sheep track and you ride east like the sky itself is chasing you—which, if the astronomers are right, it may be. And when you reach the moorlands—"

"I know. Find Veyra Thorn. Say Sefa's granddaughter broke the seal." He gripped my hands, sudden and fierce. "Maren. Thank you. Whatever happens—thank you."

"Don't thank me yet." I looked east, toward the dark line of the moors, where somewhere in that vast black silence a woman named Thorn was gathering an army around a grief sixty years old. "Just live long enough to keep your promises. Both of them."

He swung onto the mule—who bit him, as predicted, and respected him thereafter—and was gone up the track into the dark, a boy carrying a grave on his back and a grandmother's name in his mouth.

I stood in my doorway a long while, watching the stars come back wrong overhead, and wondered which of us I'd just doomed: him, or everyone.

By morning, the grey riders would know a hedge-witch in Bellhollow had broken the Winterbourne seal. By evening, so would Dain Marrec.

And by week's end, I suspected, so would the whole wounded world—but that, as my grandmother used to say, is another chapter, and none of us writes our own.
