## 11. The Rebel Order

I want to say I fought. That's the story I told later, when it mattered, and by then I'd repeated it enough times that I almost believed it myself: how I put my back to a tree, how three of them were needed to take me down, how I bit someone.

The truth is that I was asleep, and they put a sack over my head while a woman held a knife against my throat and said, very politely, "Please don't."

So much for the last apprentice of the Wardens' Guild.

* * *

They marched me through the moorlands for two days with the sack still on my head, which I decided was either a security measure or a mercy, because the world beyond Hollowmere had not stopped humming since I crossed the border stone, and I wasn't sure I could bear to look at it directly for that long. The magic in the air pressed against my skin like damp wool. Every so often we'd pass something—a leaning waystone, an abandoned shrine—and the hum would spike, and my teeth would ache, and whoever was walking behind me would mutter "he's doing it again" as though I were sneezing on purpose.

"Doing what?" I asked, once.

"Shut up," said the knife-woman, whose name I eventually learned was Brakka, and who turned out to be one of the gentler members of our company.

At night they fed me stew through the bottom edge of the sack, which is a method of feeding I do not recommend, and asked questions in rotation, so many voices and none of them repeating themselves, as though they'd rehearsed it. Where did you get the letter. Who sealed it. Do you know what you're carrying. Did the old man send you or did he run.

"I don't answer questions I can't see the face of," I told them.

"You can see the face of this one." A new voice—dry, amused, older. "Answer or don't. We'll know soon anyway."

That voice belonged, I would learn, to a man called Odo Fenwick, who had been a quartermaster in a war that ended before I was born and who still spoke like he was tallying inventory. He was the one who finally pulled the sack off my head, on the morning of the third day, at the top of a ridge where the wind came straight off the high fells with nothing between us and the sky.

I looked out over a valley that was not my valley.

It took me a moment to understand what I was seeing, because my whole life I had understood valleys as places where people lived inside the weather, sheltered from it, the way Hollowmere sat inside its wards like a hand cupped around a candle flame. This valley had no such courtesy. The wind moved through it visibly, bending the bracken in long silver waves, and there were ruins down there—I saw them before anything else—the bones of buildings too large and too fine to have ever been farmhouses. A tower with its top sheared off clean, as though something enormous had taken offense. Walls that had been white once. And scattered among them, small and dark, figures moving with purpose. Tents. Smoke. A camp.

"The Ashcloaks," Odo said, beside me. "Or what's left of them. Welcome to Cindermarch, apprentice."

"That's not—" I started, and stopped, because the geography books Corvane had made me memorize said Cindermarch was a burned province, uninhabited since the Thorn War, a cautionary tale with borders drawn around it. "People live here?"

"People died here," Brakka corrected. "We just didn't leave."

* * *

The camp resolved itself as we descended, the way things do when you stop looking at the shape of them and start looking at the parts. A cookfire with a cauldron the size of a bathtub. Laundry lines strung between broken columns. Children—one, two, three of them, actual children—chasing each other around the base of the sheared tower, shrieking in a dialect I half-recognized and couldn't place until it hit me sideways: it was Old Thorn. The lullaby language. My mother's language, if Corvane was to be believed about my mother, which at that point I believed about forty percent of the time and rising.

One of the children stopped chasing and stared at me. Not at my face—at my chest, where the letter rode under my shirt, wrapped in oilcloth against my heartbeat. She stared the way villagers stare at a lamp that flickers wrong.

"She does that," Brakka said, following my gaze. "Kids feel things. Don't worry about it."

I worried about it.

They brought me to the largest intact building, a hall whose roof had been patched with canvas and slate and, in one section, what looked suspiciously like a wagon bed flipped upside down, and inside it the hall was warm and crowded and smelled of tallow and ink, and every person in it turned to look at me at once, which is a sensation I recommend only to people training to become saints.

And then the crowd parted—not dramatically, not like a play, but the way water parts around a keel—and she came through.

I knew her name before she introduced herself. Of course I did. Veyra Thorn was a story I'd grown up on, the way children in Hollowmere grow up on warnings: *don't go past the stones, the Ashcloaks will take you, Veyra Thorn drinks blood.* She was supposed to be old. She was supposed to be terrible. She was supposed to have teeth like a pike and eyes like coals and a voice that curdled milk.

She was perhaps thirty-five, with grey already threading her black hair at the temples, and she wore the ash-grey cloak of her order like it was simply clothing, nothing ceremonial, and she looked tired in the specific way of someone who has been carrying something heavy for a very long time and has gotten good at hiding it. Her eyes found mine and stayed there, and I felt seen the way you feel seen when a physician looks at you—assessed, diagnosed, catalogued.

"So," she said. "The Wardens' Guild sent a boy."

"The Wardens' Guild didn't send anyone," I said. "The Wardens' Guild is one old man and me, and the old man ran."

Something flickered across her face—not surprise. Recognition, maybe. "And yet here you are, outside the valley, carrying a letter with the Winterbourne seal, on the same week the stars went out and sixty years of wards fell like wet paper." She stepped closer. Up close I could see the fine lines at her eyes, the ink stain on her right middle finger. "You'll forgive me if I don't believe in coincidence, Emrys Winterbourne."

"How do you know my name?"

"Odo read your letter while you slept."

The floor tilted slightly. "You *what?*"

"We read his letter," Odo confirmed placidly, from somewhere near the stew. "He was unconscious. It seemed efficient."

"It seemed—" I looked around the hall for an ally and found only interested faces. "That letter is addressed to me. It says *for Emrys.*"

"It does," Veyra agreed. "And it names coordinates in the old war-country, and a dead woman, and a line I have spent my entire life waiting for someone outside these walls to write down honestly." Her voice didn't rise. That was the unnerving thing about it—it never rose, never strained; it simply went deeper into certainty. "Say the line, boy. You've read it. Say what the valley was built on."

I should have lied. I want to be clear about that. Some part of me, some cold surviving part, knew even then that the truth was currency and I was standing in a room full of people who had been bankrupted by its absence for sixty years. But I was twenty years old, exhausted, magic-sick, three days from home, and the woman looking at me had her grandmother's face in a way I couldn't explain and couldn't shake.

"A grave," I said. "The valley was built on a grave."

The sound the hall made was not a cheer. It was quieter and worse than a cheer—a long collective exhale, sixty years of held breath leaving sixty throats at once, and somewhere in it a woman began to cry without any noise at all, tears just running down her face while she kept working at whatever was in her hands.

Veyra closed her eyes for one breath. When she opened them, they were wet, and she let them stay that way, and I understood that this too was deliberate—that she had learned long ago that her grief was a weapon and she wielded it openly, honestly, and with terrible skill.

"My great-grandmother is under the wards of Hollowmere," she said. "Did your letter tell you that? No. Your letter gave you a name and a line. Let me give you the rest." She gestured, and the crowd settled onto benches, onto crates, onto the floor itself, and I realized with a lurch that I was not being interrogated. I was being *taught.*

"They call it the Thorn War in your histories, if your histories bother to call it anything. Two kingdoms, thirty years, and at the end of it a peace signed by men who were tired of burying their sons. Only the peace was rotten from the first day, because the winning side hadn't won—they'd *bought* it. They bought it with a theft. They took something from House Thorn, hid it behind wards, and built a valley on top of the evidence, and they called it mercy. They called it *stability.*" She was pacing now, slow, and every eye followed her, including mine, especially mine. "Sixty years. Two generations born inside the lie. And the people who did it grew old and respectable and wrote the histories, and the people it was done to grew old in exile and were written out of them entirely."

"And you?" I managed. "What was done to you?"

She stopped. Looked at me for a long moment with an expression I couldn't read, and I would spend months learning to read it, and never fully succeed.

"What was done to me," she said quietly, "is that I am named for a graveyard. What was done to all of us—" she swept a hand around the hall, at the patched roof, the mended cloaks, the children outside shrieking in a dead queen's language, "—is that we survived the crime and inherited the silence. The crown calls us rebels. Bandits. We are archivists, Emrys. We are the only archive left."

"And the letter?" I asked, and hated how small my voice sounded. "What do you want with it?"

For the first time, Veyra Thorn smiled. It transformed her face entirely—warmth blooming across it like sunrise, and I felt the pull of it in my chest, the awful gravitational tug of a person who believes something completely and wants you to believe it too.

"The letter is proof," she said. "Signed, sealed, in the hand of the last Warden of Hollowmere himself. Proof that cannot be dismissed as rebel invention or survivor bitterness. Proof that comes out of the mouth of the lie itself." She reached out—not grabbing, offering her open hand, palm up, and I stared at it like a snake might stare at a charmer. "Give it to us, and we will make sure the whole world reads it. Every court, every conclave, every village square. The truth, shouted from the rooftops, until the kingdoms that built their peace on a stolen child have to stand in the ashes of their own signature and *answer for it.*"

"And if I don't?"

"Then keep it." She shrugged, easy, unbothered. "Carry it until it gets you killed in some ditch, and the truth dies with you, and another generation learns to live inside the silence. That's the other option. There are only ever those two, in my experience. Truth spoken, or truth buried. Everything else is just the speed of the shovel."

The hall waited. Sixty faces, and not one of them hostile, which was somehow more frightening than hostility—they looked at me the way starving people look at bread, and I understood suddenly why the little girl had stared at my chest. To them I wasn't a person. I was a delivery.

"There's a third option," I said. "You could give it back. Let me decide."

Veyra laughed—a real laugh, surprised out of her—and some of the tension in the hall broke with it. "Oh, I like him," she told the room. "Did you hear that? Give it back. As if it were his." She turned back to me, and the warmth stayed but something steel came up underneath it, the blade under the silk. "It isn't yours, Emrys. It was never meant to be yours. Corvane wrote your name on it because he needed a courier too frightened and too loyal to ask questions. You're not the keeper of that letter. You're the *pack mule.*"

The words landed harder than they should have, because some traitorous part of me had been thinking exactly the same thing since the cellar, since the smoke, since the sack over my head. Pack mule. Useful fool. The last apprentice of a dying guild, carrying other people's secrets to other people's wars.

But.

But I had watched Corvane burn papers with hands that shook. I had heard him say *the valley was built on a grave* in a voice like a man confessing to murder. And whatever I was—mule, fool, courier—I was the one holding it now, and everyone else who'd touched it had wanted either to burn it or to aim it like a weapon, and neither of those felt like justice to me, and I said so.

"Not tonight," I told her.

Her eyebrows rose.

"I'm tired, I'm half-sick from whatever's in your air, and I'm not handing anything to anyone while I'm too stupid with exhaustion to think. Tomorrow. I'll talk tomorrow." I lifted my chin, aware of how young I must have sounded and unable to do anything about it. "Feed me first. That's my price."

Another laugh, warmer, and this time it spread around the hall like the fire's heat, and people started moving again, life resuming around me, and Veyra clapped me on the shoulder with a hand heavier than it looked.

"Feed him!" she called, delighted. "Our honored guest negotiates! Odo—stew. Brakka—find him a blanket that doesn't smell like the goats."

As they steered me toward the fire, she leaned close, and her voice dropped to something only I could hear, soft and certain and terrifying in its gentleness:

"You said 'not tonight,' which means you're already deciding. Good. Deciding slowly is wiser than most manage." A pause, and her thumb pressed briefly against my shoulder, right over the hidden letter. "But understand something, Emrys Winterbourne. I have waited my whole life for proof. I will wait one more night. But there are others coming for that letter—men in royal grey, with charming smiles and long knives—and they will not negotiate over stew."

She straightened, smiled at the hall, and raised her voice to ordinary warmth again, asking after the state of the roof canvas.

I sat by the fire with my stew and my blanket that smelled faintly of goats, surrounded by people who wanted me to be their trumpet, hunted by people who wanted me to be their silence.

And far off across the darkening moor, so faint I might have imagined it, I heard—or dreamed I heard—the creak of harness, and the ring of iron shoes on old stone.

Riders. Grey ones.

Coming at a walk, patient as winter.
