## 12. Veyra's Version

I had planned to hate him.

It's worth saying that up front, because everyone who tells this story later will want to paint it as destiny—the heir and the avenger, drawn together by fate, etcetera, etcetera. Fate had nothing to do with it. I took one look at the boy the Ashcloaks dragged into my tent—muddy, terrified, clutching a letter like it was his mother's hand—and I thought: *this is going to be the hardest work of my life.*

Not because he was stupid. He wasn't. Because he was good. And goodness, in my experience, is the most stubborn material there is to shape.

His name was Emrys Winterbourne, which meant nothing to me yet, or rather it meant everything, but I'll come to that. What I saw first was a valley boy who had never been more than a day's walk from home, blinking at my camp as if someone had dropped him into a story he hadn't consented to be in. Which, to be fair, is how most of us end up in stories.

"You're Veyra Thorn," he said, when they brought him to me. Not a question. Everyone knew my name; that was rather the point of having spent eleven years building it.

"And you're the boy who carries dead men's letters," I said. "Sit down before you fall down."

He sat. He looked at the map on my table—the whole Thorn Marches inked in my own hand, every burned village marked with a small black thorn—and I watched him do the arithmetic of what those marks meant. He was quick. That was the first thing that gave me hope. The second thing, unfortunately, was what he said next.

"These are graves," he said quietly. "Aren't they."

Not *what happened here*. Not *who did this*. Graves. He'd skipped straight past the question to the answer, because some part of him already knew. Valley children were raised on the Peace the way other children were raised on porridge: fed it daily until they couldn't imagine any other meal. But even porridge-fed children know, somewhere under the fullness, that they are being fed.

"Yes," I said. "Graves. Come walk them with me tomorrow, if your legs will carry you. Words are cheap. I'd rather show you where I keep mine."

* * *

I want to be honest about something, since honesty is the currency I'm always demanding from others and rarely paying myself: I chose the route carefully.

There were three ways from our camp to the ruins at Karavel's Ford. The short way passed through farmland that had healed—green fields, fat sheep, farmers who waved at my outriders because my outriders protected their harvests better than any crown garrison ever had. I did not take him that way. The long way passed through the ash country, where nothing had grown in sixty years because the burning had been thorough and the forgiveness had been nonexistent.

We went the long way. Of course we went the long way.

He noticed. I could see him noticing—the way the ground changed underfoot, the way the trees thinned into blackened skeletons that still stood after six decades because nothing living had the strength to fell them. He didn't say anything. Valley manners, probably. Or fear. Both, likely. Fear and manners make excellent traveling companions; they keep each other quiet.

"Tell me what you learned about the Thorn War," I said, as we walked. "In Hollowmere. What does the Guild teach?"

He recited it like a lesson, because it was one. "The war ended sixty years ago. The kingdoms of Cindermarch and the Thorn Marches made peace. The border wards were raised so that no army could cross again, and the valley of Hollowmere was founded as a neutral sanctuary, dedicated to keeping the peace." A pause. "That magic was sealed away for everyone's protection."

"Everyone's," I repeated. "Whose everyone?"

"I—" He frowned. "Everyone. All people."

"Mm." I let the silence stretch. Silence is a tool I've sharpened over many years, and I am not ashamed of using it on a boy half my age. "When you were a child, did you ever ask why the sanctuary needed walls? Why the wards pointed inward?"

"They don't point inward," he said automatically. Then stopped. I watched him actually think about it, watch the thought move through him like a fish through clear water. "Do they?"

"The ward-stones on your southern border face south," I said. "The ones on your northern border face north. Every stone faces out, Emrys. Except the four great anchors. Those face in." I turned to look at him. "Ask yourself why a sanctuary needs to keep its own people from leaving."

"That's not—" He shook his head. "Nobody leaves because nobody wants to leave. It's peaceful. It's *good* there."

"Yes," I said softly. "It is. That's the cruelest part. They built you a beautiful cage and called it mercy, and you loved it, and loving it was the lock." I gestured ahead, to where the land rose into a ridge of black hills. "Come. The lesson gets worse from here."

* * *

Karavel's Ford had been a town of three thousand people, once. A river crossing, a market, a shrine to some river god whose name even I have forgotten. In the last winter of the Thorn War, the Cindermarch army retreated across the ford and, finding themselves pursued, made a decision that military historians have spent sixty years describing in language designed to avoid the word *atrocity*. They fired the town with the people inside it. All of them. The official histories call it "the Karavel Containment." Containment of what, exactly, has never been specified. You cannot contain three thousand corpses. They contained witnesses.

The ruins were exactly as I wanted him to find them: unhealed, unmourned, unmarked by any royal monument. No plaque. No cairn. Just the foundations of houses rising out of the ash-grass like teeth, and the shrine wall still standing in the middle of it all, scorched black, with the river god's face worn smooth by weather and neglect.

Emrys walked into the ruin the way people walk into cold water—one careful step, then another, then all at once.

"There should be bones," he said finally. His voice had gone strange and flat.

"There were." I stood at the edge, letting him go in alone. This part had to be alone. "The crown collected them. Officially, for 'respectful interment.' Unofficially, for a reason you'll see in a moment. Follow the road east, past the shrine. There's a hollow. Go look."

He went. I followed at a distance, the way you follow someone walking toward a thing they cannot yet imagine.

The hollow was a mass grave—not the crown's tidy excavation, but the older one beneath it. When the Cindermarch soldiers burned the town, they had not managed to burn everyone. Some two hundred souls had sheltered in the cellars beneath the shrine, and when the fires reached them, they had not died quickly or cleanly, and afterward the survivors of the surrounding villages—who were forbidden by martial law from burying the dead, because the dead were evidence of a crime the crown had already decided never happened—had dug this pit in secret, at night, in defiance of orders, and laid their neighbors in it. Then they had been arrested for it. Most of them died in camps. The pit remained, unmarked, illegal, an act of grief that had cost everyone who performed it.

Sixty years later, the grass grew over it thin and grey, and the earth above it sank in a long shallow trough, like a bed someone had recently risen from.

Emrys stood at the edge of it for a long time. I watched his shoulders. That's where grief shows first, in people who haven't learned to hide it—in the shoulders, which curl forward as if to shield something carried against the chest.

"My guildmaster told me the wards protect the valley," he said, without turning around. His voice was steady in the way that ice is steady. "He told me the sealing keeps magic from hurting anyone."

"He wasn't lying," I said. "That's the genius of it. Corvane Winterbourne—I know your guildmaster's name, boy, I've known it for years—is not a liar. He's a believer. He believes the lie so completely that he can tell it with a straight face and a kind heart. That's what makes him dangerous. Liars can be caught. Believers have to be *grieved*."

"You know him?" Now he turned. There was something new in his face—not anger yet, but the raw material of it, ore waiting for a forge. "You know Corvane?"

"I know what he guards." I walked down into the hollow beside him, because some things you shouldn't make a person stand in alone, even when you brought them there. "Your valley isn't a sanctuary, Emrys. It's a vault. And the wards aren't there to keep armies out. They're there to keep the world from remembering what's buried inside it—and to keep one particular thing inside from ever being found."

"What thing?"

I looked at him for a long moment. Here is where I should tell you I hesitated. Here is where a kinder woman would have weighed her words.

I didn't hesitate. Kinder women don't lead rebellions; they die in them, early, mourned briefly.

"A grave," I said. "The grave. The one the entire Peace was built on top of, the way you build a house on a foundation. Pull it out and the house comes down. Your guildmaster knows it. The crown knows it. The only person who doesn't know it is standing in front of me asking questions, and that, Emrys Winterbourne, is the single greatest crime of the last sixty years—not the burning, not the burial, but the raising of a child inside a lie so complete he thanks his jailers for it every morning."

* * *

We walked back through the ash country in silence, and I used the silence, because silence teaches too.

Let me describe what I was doing, honestly, because I promised myself I would be honest in this account even where it makes me look bad. Especially there.

I was breaking him open. Deliberately. Methodically. I had spent eleven years collecting survivors' testimony—every burned village, every disappeared family, every forged deed and stolen inheritance—and I had learned that testimony alone changes nothing. People hear atrocities the way they hear weather reports: terrible, distant, unrelated to breakfast. But take a person to the grave itself, put their boots in the ash, let them breathe the air that still smells faintly of smoke after sixty years—that transforms them. Grief is contagious. Outrage is contagious. And a man who has wept at a graveside belongs to the graveyard forever after.

I needed him to belong to it. I needed what he carried—whatever that letter held, whatever secret the Wardens' Guild had spent generations suffocating—to become *his* cause and not merely his cargo. A courier can be robbed. A believer has to be killed.

So yes. I showed him the worst things I owned, arranged in the order most likely to wound, and I told myself it was necessary, and mostly it was, and the part that wasn't necessary I have decided not to examine too closely. Every leader carries a ledger like that. Mine is simply longer than most.

But here is the thing I did not expect, the thing that unsettled me then and unsettles me now as I write it: somewhere between the ford and the hollow, between his first silence and his second, I stopped performing.

It was his question at the shrine. He put his palm flat against the scorched wall, against the worn-smooth face of that forgotten river god, and he asked—not rhetorically, not cleverly, with the simple devastating directness of a man who has just discovered the floor is missing—"Why does nobody know this? Sixty years. Three thousand people. Why does the world get to forget?"

And I heard myself answer, "Because forgetting was the price of peace," and I heard how much it cost me to say it, and I realized I had never once said it aloud to anyone who could feel the cost with me. My Ashcloaks know the history—they were raised on it, nursed on it, weaned on ashes. To them my accounts of Karavel are scripture, and scripture doesn't require the teller to bleed. But this valley boy, this stranger, this enemy's apprentice—he flinched when I spoke. He *heard* it. And for one moment on that ruined road I was not Veyra Thorn, leader of the Ashcloak Order, architect of the reckoning. I was just a woman showing a young man the place where her grandmother's sisters had died, and discovering that the telling hurt, and that no one had ever told her it would keep hurting.

I hated him a little for that. I've decided to count that as evidence of his character.

* * *

Back at camp, the mood had changed while we were gone, and not in a direction I liked.

Doran, my lieutenant, met us at the picket line with a face like a closed door. Doran is a good soldier and a bad waiter, and he had spent the day of my absence doing what my officers do whenever I leave them alone with a prisoner: planning. I could see it in the way the camp had rearranged itself—sentries doubled, the council fire built larger than necessary, the Ashcloaks who gathered around it falling quiet in that particular way soldiers fall quiet when a decision has been made above their pay grade.

"We should talk," Doran said.

"We will." I put a hand on Emrys's shoulder—possession, in front of the men, deliberate—and steered him toward my tent. "Alone. After I've seen our guest fed."

"He's not a guest, Veyra. He's a Warden's apprentice carrying a sealed letter from the Guild that murdered our families, and half the order thinks we should take it off him and the other half thinks we should take it off him *gently*."

"And what do you think?"

Doran glanced at Emrys, who was watching us both with the expression of a man realizing he has been rescued by wolves. "I think you went out this morning with a courier," Doran said, low, "and you came back looking at him like he was a weapon. And I've followed you through eleven years of ash, Veyra, so I'll follow this too. But weapons misfire. Remember whose hands got burned last time we picked one up."

He left. Emrys stood in the firelight holding a bowl of stew he hadn't touched.

"They want the letter," he said.

"Of course they do. So do I." I sat down across from him and, because honesty is my best performance, gave him the truth straight. "Listen to me, Emrys Winterbourne, because I won't pretend otherwise and you deserve better than pretense. I am not a gentle woman. I have done ruthless things in service of what's buried under that ash, and I will do more, and some of them may involve you in ways you won't like. If you run, my people will catch you. If you lie to me, I will know. If you give that letter to the crown, I will hunt you to the edge of the map." I leaned forward. "But I will also tell you the truth, always, even when it costs me—which is more than your guildmaster ever did, and more than the crown will ever do, and if you weigh us all fairly, weighing is what you seem built for, I think you'll find that counts for something."

He looked at me for a long moment. Firelight, exhaustion, grief, and underneath all of it—a mind working. I had shown him graves and given him threats, and instead of breaking, he was *weighing*. God help me, he was actually weighing.

"Why show me?" he asked. "The ruins. You could have just taken the letter. Why waste a day on me?"

And there it was—the question I'd been dreading, asked in the flat, tired voice of a boy who had learned in a single day that his whole life was furniture arranged around a hidden door.

"Because the letter is paper," I said. "Paper burns. Paper can be stolen, forged, argued with, dismissed. But what you saw today can't be any of those things. I don't need your letter, Emrys. I need to know whether what you saw today lives in you the way it lives in me." I stood, and took the untouched stew from his hands, and pretended not to notice that they were shaking. "Sleep. Tomorrow we talk about what your guildmaster is guarding, and you're going to tell me everything, and then—"

Then the sentry shouted from the north ridge, and the whole camp came up out of its blankets like a kicked anthill, and I forgot the rest of the sentence, because through the dark beyond the fires, down out of the hills where nothing should have been moving, came riders. Grey riders. Royal grey.

And at their head, unhurried, almost polite, a tall man in an inquisitor's coat who did not stop at the picket line but called out, in a voice pitched to carry across the whole camp:

"Lady Thorn! A word, if your conscience permits!"

Dain Marrec had found us. And the letter was still folded against Emrys's heart, and I had not yet asked him the only question that mattered:

What, exactly, had Corvane Winterbourne written?
