## 13. Marrec Closes In

There are three ways to find a man in the moorlands, and I have used all of them in my career. The first is to follow his trail, which is honest work and slow as a funeral. The second is to ask the locals, which is faster but requires you to smile at people who have every reason to hate you. The third is to let someone else do both and simply pay for the results.

I had been using the third method for four days, and it was costing me a small fortune and most of my self-respect.

The traitor's name was Pell, though he'd offered me three others in the first hour of our acquaintance, each one presumably belonging to some poor soul who had wronged him at market. He was a tanner by trade, an Ashcloak sympathizer by conviction, and a coward by nature — which is the most reliable combination in the world if you're buying information. Conviction gets men into camps. Cowardice gets them out again, usually carrying something valuable.

"He keeps to the eastern tents," Pell told me, hunched beside my fire on the ridge above the valley called Hollowmere's ugly cousin — a bowl of grey grass and standing stones where the Ashcloaks had made their camp. "The tall one. Winterbourne. He doesn't sleep well. Keeps looking south, like the valley's calling him home."

"Does he now," I said, and fed another stick to the fire because Pell talked better when his hands were warm and worse when he thought about what he was doing. "And the woman? Thorn?"

Pell flinched at the name. Good. Everyone flinched at that name; it was practically a national pastime outside the valley. "She's the problem. She posts watches in pairs, and she changes the rotation every night. You can't buy your way through Veyra Thorn's camp, inquisitor. People believe in her." He said it with the particular bitterness of a man who had believed once himself and resented the expense.

"People believe in lots of things," I said. "The sun. The crown. That tanners are honest."

He didn't laugh. Few of them did. I've found that humor is a currency that only appreciates among people who aren't afraid, and everyone I'd spoken to in the last week was afraid — the villagers along the pilgrim road, the hedge-witch whose house I'd searched while she smiled at me with too many teeth, and now Pell, sweating in the cold with thirty silver crowns' worth of treason folded into his coat.

"The letter," I said. "Where is it?"

"On him. Or near him. They don't leave it anywhere — Thorn sleeps with it under her head some nights, or so the story goes, and other nights the boy carries it against his chest like a relic." Pell wiped his nose. "It's just paper, isn't it? That's what I keep telling myself. All this fuss over paper."

"All wars are fuss over paper," I said. "Paper says who owns what. Everything after that is just arithmetic done in blood."

I'm told I have a gift for making people feel worse without raising my voice. It has served me well. Pell went pale and told me the watch changed at moonrise, and that the eastern picket had a gap in it — a stretch of dead ground behind the old cairn where no one liked to stand because the wind came off the stones like a knife.

* * *

You should understand something about my orders before you judge what I did next, though I suspect you'll judge anyway. People always do. It's one of the few free pleasures left in this world.

My commission came from the Office of Inquisition directly — not from any provincial governor, not from any general, but from the sealed hand of Chancellor Aldery herself. I had read it perhaps forty times since leaving the capital, and every reading it seemed to shrink, the way a comfortable room shrinks when you learn what happened in it.

*Recover the document known as the Winterbourne letter intact. Determine whether copies exist. If copies exist, recover them also.*

That was the first paragraph, and it was almost reasonable. A man could do that paragraph with a clear conscience. Find the paper, bring the paper home, file a report, drink bad wine in a good mood.

Then there was the second paragraph.

*Determine who has read the document. Any person who has read or heard its contents in full is compromised. Compromised persons are to be silenced. This includes, without exception, members of the Wardens' Guild, associates of the rebel order styling itself the Ashcloaks, and any civilian who has been exposed to the document's contents.*

*Silenced.* Not arrested. Not detained for questioning. The word had been chosen carefully, and whoever had chosen it had chosen it knowing I would read between the lines, because that was the point. Plausible deniability flowed downhill in the Office like water finds the sea. If I succeeded, the chancellor had given me discretion. If I failed, I had acted alone, exceeded my mandate, and would be disavowed with genuine-looking sorrow.

And then the third paragraph, which I will set down here exactly as written, because I have earned the right to be tired of carrying it alone:

*The boy Emrys Winterbourne is to be recovered alive if practicable. His identity is a matter of state security. If recovery is not practicable, his death must precede any public revelation of his lineage. The peace of two kingdoms outweighs one life. You know this. Act accordingly.*

I had sat with that paragraph on the first night out from the capital, in a tavern full of farmers arguing about grain prices, and I had felt nothing at all, which frightened me more than feeling something would have. *The peace of two kingdoms outweighs one life.* It was true. That was the obscene part. Every sensible person in both kingdoms would sign their name under that sentence without hesitation, and the boy would die, and the sky would go on being whatever the sky pretended to be, and in sixty years no one would remember there had ever been a question.

I am a sensible person. I want that understood. Whatever you think of me by the end of this, understand that I never once believed I was the villain of the piece. No one does. That's the trick of it — history is written by sensible people doing arithmetic.

* * *

The camp was easier to reach than Pell had promised, which should have made me suspicious and did, briefly, before ambition got the better of caution. I went in alone, which my sergeant Tam thought was madness and said so, twice, in the flat way he had of disagreeing with me that managed to sound like agreeing.

"You're the only one they can't replace," he'd said, checking the action on his crossbow by the light of our hidden cookfire. "I mean that professionally. Also personally. If you die out there I have to write the report, and I'm terrible at reports."

"You're terrible at everything except shooting people at distance," I said. "It's why we get along."

"I'll hold the road," he said. "Two miles south, the ford. If you come out running, whistle."

"If I come out running," I said, "it means something has gone so badly wrong that whistling won't help either of us."

The dead ground behind the cairn was exactly as advertised — wind like a blade, stones humming faintly with old magic the way everything hummed now that the wards were down. That was the thing nobody in the capital had properly grasped yet, I think. The wards hadn't just kept magic *out* of Hollowmere. They had kept it *quiet* everywhere nearby, the way a great bell muffled goes unheard even when struck. With them fallen, the whole border country rang like a struck bell, low and constant, and I could feel it in my teeth. Magic had come back to the world the way floodwater comes back to a dry riverbed — finding every crack, filling every hollow, indifferent to what it drowned.

I counted sentries. Four visible, two probable. Pell's gap was real. I waited in the dark behind the cairn for the better part of an hour, long enough for my hands to go numb inside my gloves, and then the tall figure I'd been promised came down to the eastern tents alone, moving like a man who hadn't slept.

Emrys Winterbourne looked younger than his file photograph. Files always make people look older, I've noticed — ink adds years, or maybe guilt does. He stood at the edge of the firelight talking to a woman with red hair cropped short, and even at that distance I could see the argument in their posture: her leaning forward, hungry; him leaning back, holding something — the letter, I'd have wagered my pension — against his chest the way Pell had described.

I could have taken him. That's the honest accounting. Two of my crossbowmen were positioned on the ridge with clean sightlines, Tam was at the ford, and the boy was tired and grieving and surrounded by rebels who trusted him less than they trusted his usefulness. One signal flare, ninety seconds of chaos, and I'd have had him trussed and on the road south before dawn.

I didn't give the signal.

I've asked myself why, in the weeks since, and I still don't have an answer I like. Part of it was the letter itself — dead paper tells you nothing about copies, and a dead boy tells you even less. Part of it was Veyra Thorn, whose reputation preceded her the way weather precedes a storm: if the raid went loud, she'd scatter the camp and the letter both into the moorlands, and I'd spend a winter chasing fragments. And part of it — the part I dislike admitting — was that I wanted to hear him talk first. Interrogation is a craft, and I am a craftsman, and there is a difference between breaking a man who knows he's caught and learning what a man believes while he still believes it. The first gives you facts. The second gives you the shape of the hole the facts will leave.

So I watched instead. I watched the boy shake his head at whatever Thorn was saying, and I watched her smile — a smile with no warmth in it, all edges, the kind of smile that makes promises it intends to collect on later — and I watched him walk away from her toward the tents with his shoulders up around his ears like a man walking into weather.

Then I withdrew, quiet as I'd come, and went back down the hill to where Pell was waiting with his hand out.

* * *

"You didn't take him." Pell sounded almost offended. "Thirty crowns and you just — looked at him?"

"Looking is underrated," I said. "Kings have lost thrones over skipping it."

We were back at my fire on the ridge, and I was paying him, counting coins into his palm one at a time because slow payment looks like more payment. He watched each coin land like a man watching his own funeral procession.

"There's more," he said, when the last coin was gone. "If there's more coin."

Of course there was more. There is always more. Treason is a ladder, not a door; you climb one rung and discover the next one waiting.

"Talk."

"They're moving." He glanced over his shoulder at the dark, though the camp was two miles off and invisible. "Thorn. Day after tomorrow, maybe sooner. South and east, toward Cindermarch."

Cindermarch. The winter solstice conclave. Every crown, every order, every ambitious fool with a banner and a grievance, gathered in one spire to argue about the falling stars — and Veyra Thorn marching toward it with a stolen heir and a letter that could set the whole assembly on fire.

"She means to use it there," I said. "At the conclave."

"She means to *shove it down their throats* there," Pell corrected, with a flicker of the old believer showing through the coward, and then the coward reasserted itself. "Which is madness. The delegations will tear each other apart. There'll be war inside a month. Maybe sooner. My family lives outside Cindermarch, inquisitor. I have sisters."

Everyone had someone. That was the lever I'd been pulling all week, and I pulled it again, gently. "Then help me stop it before it starts. The letter, Pell. Exact location. Who holds it, when, how it's guarded. Do that, and this ends quietly, and your sisters never learn there was anything to fear."

"And the boy?" Pell asked. And then, because he was not entirely stupid: "What happens to the boy?"

I looked at the fire for a moment. It's a good trick, looking away when you answer questions like that — it reads as reluctance, as conscience, when really it's just giving yourself a second to decide which lie fits best.

"The boy comes home," I said. "That's all this is. A lost lamb and a lot of noise about wool."

Pell nodded, satisfied, and gave me what I'd paid for: the letter traveled in an oilskin wallet on Thorn's own belt during marches and moved to a locked writing case in her command tent at night; the case was guarded by a woman named Brakka who had once held a bridge alone against nine men and was not, Pell emphasized, to be bought at any price I possessed.

I thanked him, told him where to leave his next report, and watched him scurry down the hill toward the treeline with his silver riding against his ribs.

Tam materialized out of the dark the way he did, silent until he chose not to be. "Well?"

"Well, what?"

"You didn't take him." He crouched by the fire and held his hands to it. "I had the flare ready. I could see the whole camp from the rise. Ninety seconds, you said."

"Ninety seconds," I agreed. "And then a hundred years of war, because Thorn would've run, and the letter would've run with her, and every copy she'd already made would've ended up nailed to church doors from here to the coast. You don't catch smoke with a net, Tam. You wait for it to drift somewhere with walls."

He grunted. Tam communicates primarily in grunts; it saves wear on both of us. Then: "The chancellor's timetable. She wanted the letter in the capital by month's end. We're burning days sitting on this hill watching."

"We are," I said. "And the sky is falling, literally, and two armies are sharpening, and the man who wrote my orders is a hundred leagues away sleeping soundly. The timetable can grieve."

He grunted again, differently this time — the grunt that meant *you're lying to yourself, boss*, which Tam deployed perhaps twice a year and always accurately.

Because here is the truth I wasn't saying, the one that had been sitting in my chest since the ridge, since I watched a tired boy refuse a charismatic woman and walk away with a dead man's letter held against his heart:

I had looked at Emrys Winterbourne through a spyglass in the dark, and what I had seen was not a threat. It was not a weapon, or a symbol, or a casus belli wearing a coat. It was a homesick apprentice who missed a valley he couldn't go back to, being dragged toward a conclave by people who needed him more than they knew him.

And my orders said that if I couldn't deliver him safely to silence, I was to deliver him to a grave, and call it peace.

I fed the fire another stick and watched the sparks climb toward a sky with holes in it. Somewhere overhead, the missing stars were supposed to be returning — the astronomers in the capital had sworn it, misaligned and wrong, the heavens themselves coming unstitched — but from this ridge the dark looked simply dark, patient and total, the way it looks before something happens that everyone will afterward claim they foresaw.

Day after tomorrow, Veyra Thorn broke camp. Which meant I had one day to decide what kind of man was going to follow her south.

I had hoped, honestly, that I'd have longer to choose.
