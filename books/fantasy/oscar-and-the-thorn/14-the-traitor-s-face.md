## 14. The Traitor's Face

I want to say I saw it coming. That some part of me — the part that had spent three years mending ward-lamps and pretending not to notice when they flickered — felt the shape of the betrayal before it arrived, the way you feel weather in an old scar.

I didn't. What I was doing that morning was learning to cook oatmeal over a rebel fire without burning it, which tells you everything about how well my instincts were working.

"Stir it," Veyra said, without looking up from the map she'd spread across a flat stone. "You stir it like it's insulted you."

"It has," I said. "Oatmeal is just punishment porridge. Somewhere in the valley there's a miller laughing at us."

"Sela would know." Veyra said it lightly. Too lightly. I'd learned by then that Veyra did nothing lightly; every casual sentence was a stone dropped down a well, and she was listening for the splash.

"She'd tell me the miller's whole family history first," I said. "And then charge him for the telling."

The Ashcloak camp sat in a fold of the moorlands where two hills leaned together like conspirators — forty tents, maybe fifty people, all of them moving with the particular quiet of folk who'd spent their lives being hunted. They called themselves descendants of Thorn survivors, and watching them, I believed it. There was a patience in how they worked, a grimness, like people who had inherited grief along with the land.

For six days I'd been their guest. Their *prize*, if I was honest, though nobody said the word where I could hear it. Veyra wanted the letter. The letter named names, dates, coordinates — the architecture of the crime that built Hollowmere. And Veyra wanted me too, though what exactly she wanted me *for* stayed unspoken, hovering around her like smoke.

I kept the letter sewn into my coat lining, because I was raised by Corvane, and Corvane taught me one lesson above all others: paper outlives promises.

"You keep touching your left side," Veyra said now, still studying the map. "Every time someone walks past."

"Do I?"

"Like a man checking his purse." She looked up at last, and her grey eyes did the thing they did — went through you rather than at you. "Emrys. When this is over, whatever this becomes, I need you to trust someone eventually. It doesn't have to be me."

"That's good," I said. "Because it won't be."

Something crossed her face — hurt, quickly filed away behind the commander's mask. I almost apologized. I'm glad, in the long accounting, that I didn't. Some things are easier to survive if you never pretend to be kinder than you are.

The oatmeal burned anyway.

* * *

The attack came at dusk, which is when attacks come, because dusk is when sentries blink and cooks burn porridge and everyone's eyes play tricks with shadows.

I heard it before I understood it: the camp dogs going silent all at once, which is wrong — dogs bark at danger or they don't, but they don't *agree* to stop. Then a whistle from the eastern picket, cut short in the middle like a door closing on a room.

Then the arrows.

They came out of the failing light in a hiss, and men I'd eaten beside two hours earlier started folding up like laundry. I didn't think. Thinking was for later. My legs took me low and fast between tents, toward the treeline Veyra had pointed out on our first day as the escape route — *if anything ever goes wrong* — and I remember being absurdly grateful to past-Veyra for planning, even as present-Veyra's voice cracked across the camp:

"To the trees! Form on the trees! Leave the tents!"

A hand caught my collar and hauled me behind a supply cart. Dallen, the big quiet man who'd taught me to whittle on the second night, pressed a knife into my palm.

"They're after *you*, boy," he said. "Not us. Go."

"I can't just—"

"You can. You will. That's an order from a man who's known you six days, so imagine how much weight it'll carry coming from her." He grinned — actually grinned, blood already on his teeth — and shoved me back into the open.

I ran. Behind me the camp came apart: screams, the ring of steel, a tent collapsing in flame. Royal grey moved through the smoke in disciplined pairs, the way Marrec's riders had moved through Hollowmere, efficient as water finding cracks. And above it all, cutting through the chaos, a voice calling out in a clear carrying tone:

"Hold! Hold your lines! The letter is the objective — no reprisals against those who stand down!"

Marrec. Of course. Even his raids sounded reasonable.

I reached the treeline. I should have been in the trees, gone, safe. Instead I stopped — because I saw her.

Sela stood at the edge of the clearing, beside a royal officer's horse, wearing a travelling cloak I didn't recognize, and she was pointing.

Pointing at the tent I'd just left.

* * *

Here is the thing about heartbreak that nobody warns you: it doesn't feel like breaking. It feels like arithmetic.

I stood behind a pine trunk with my lungs full of smoke and did sums. Sela was in Hollowmere. Sela was always in Hollowmere; she had a family there, a mother with bad lungs and two younger brothers and a mill that fed them. Sela could not have followed me over the border unless she'd chosen to. Sela could not have found the Ashcloaks' camp unless someone had told her where to look. Sela was standing next to a crown officer's horse, pointing at my tent, and the officer was nodding at her the way you nod at a colleague.

Conclusion: Sela had made a bargain. Conclusion: the bargain was me.

There was a version of me — the boy who'd tended lamps, who'd thought the worst thing in the world was a cold winter — who would have called her name right then. Who would have walked into the open shouting *why*, demanding the story make sense, because stories were supposed to make sense and she was my oldest friend and friends don't point.

That boy died behind the pine tree, mostly. What was left of him did the harder thing: he watched.

The officer — young, tired-eyed, a captain by the stitching — dismounted and spoke to her. I couldn't hear the words over the burning camp, but I saw her hands. Her hands were shaking. She wrapped them around each other like she was holding something alive that might get away.

She wasn't pointing because she wanted to. She was pointing because she'd traded the only thing she had that they wanted — knowledge of me — for something she needed more.

Her family. It was always going to be her family. I knew it the way you know a lullaby, without deciding to.

And the worst part — the part I've never told anyone until now, writing this — is that I understood. Standing there in the smoke with the letter hard against my ribs, I understood her completely, and understanding her didn't help at all. Understanding is not forgiveness. Understanding is just pain with better vocabulary.

Then a hand closed over my mouth from behind and the world went dark.

* * *

"Don't scream. If you scream we both die, and I've grown fond of you, so that would be wasteful."

Veyra. She pulled me deeper into the trees, away from the fires, moving with a hunter's silence while I moved like a sack of turnips. We went perhaps half a mile in the dark, downhill, following a stream because she said riders lose sound near running water, and only when we reached a hollow under a rock shelf did she let us both stop breathing hard.

"The camp?" I managed.

"Gone or scattered. Dallen got the wounded moving north. Marrec got what he came for." She said it flatly, but I heard the cost underneath. Fifty people. People who'd whittled and cooked and trusted her. "He got what he came for," she repeated, and turned to look at me, and her face changed. "Unless he didn't. Emrys. The letter."

My hand went to my left side.

The coat was there. The coat was intact. The lining—

The lining was lighter than it should have been. My fingers found the seam I'd sewn myself with borrowed thread six days ago, neat stitches, and beneath them a neat slit, and beneath *that* — nothing. Cold cloth. Empty air.

"No," I said, which is the most eloquent thing I've ever managed under pressure.

I tore the lining open properly, as though the letter might have simply wandered off and needed encouragement. Nothing. Just the faint ghost of wax, a smear of red where the seal had been, and a small hole cut with a blade finer than mine.

"When," Veyra demanded. Not *how*. She'd skipped straight past how. "When did you last check it?"

"This morning. After breakfast. It was—" My voice did something humiliating. I pushed through it. "It was there after breakfast. I checked. You told me once I touched my side like a man checking his purse, and I checked, and it was *there*, Veyra, I—"

"After breakfast." She was very still. "Who was near you?"

Everyone. Everyone was near me. That was the trap of camps — you lived in each other's pockets. But my mind, doing its terrible new arithmetic again, kept circling back to the clearing, to the shaking hands, to the pointing finger.

"She followed me," I said. "From Hollowmere. She must have. She made a deal with them — Marrec's people — information for her family's safety, that has to be—"

"Emrys." Veyra crouched to my level. In the dark her eyes were just glints, but her voice had gone gentle, which frightened me more than any blade. "Your friend followed you over a border that had been sealed for sixty years, tracked a hidden rebel camp through moorland, and got close enough to you — sleeping ten feet from you — to cut a letter out of your coat without waking you. Do you understand what kind of skill that takes? What kind of *training*?"

"What are you saying?"

"I'm saying I don't know yet. I'm saying neither does anyone else, and that frightens me more than Marrec does." She stood. "But I know this: Marrec's riders rode out of the camp twenty minutes ago, heading east, fast. Toward Cindermarch. Whatever he took, he's taking it to his superiors, and once it reaches them, every option we have dies quietly in a filing cabinet."

I sat down on the cold stone. Somewhere above us, the sky was filling with stars — real stars, returned stars, stars that had come back wrong and misaligned and getting worse, though I didn't know that yet. All I knew was that I'd lost the proof. The single object that mattered, the reason for every mile I'd walked and every person who'd bled for it, and I'd lost it to a girl whose porridge jokes I'd been repeating in my head all week like a talisman.

"She saved my life once," I said to nobody. "When we were nine. Fell through the ice on the mere. She went in after me. Both of us nearly drowned, and her mother beat her black and blue for it, and Sela said—" My throat closed. I made myself finish it. "Sela said, *you're stuck with me now, Emrys Winterbourne, till death does us part.*"

Veyra said nothing for a long moment. Then, quietly: "Maybe she considers this keeping the vow. Maybe she thinks she's saving your life again, in the only way she can reach."

"That's generous."

"I'm rarely accused of that."

"It's what makes it convincing," I said, and something that was almost a laugh passed between us, brief and bitter, the way soldiers' laughter is.

* * *

We walked most of the night, west and south, away from the road, and near dawn Veyra stopped so abruptly I walked into her back.

Ahead of us, on the ridge line against the greying sky, stood a lone figure. Small. Cloaked. Waiting, the way you wait when you've calculated exactly where someone will be.

I knew the set of those shoulders. I knew the way she shifted her weight from foot to foot when she was terrified and trying not to show it — I'd known it since we were children stealing apples from the guildhouse tree.

Veyra's knife was out before I finished inhaling. "Wait here."

"No." I stepped past her. "She came to find *me*."

"She led soldiers to my camp."

"And she's standing alone on a hill at dawn, which means whatever she wants, it isn't safety." I walked forward. Behind me I heard Veyra swear softly and follow, because Veyra followed me now — that was the arrangement, however strange it felt — and the distance closed with every step like a wound closing, painfully, over something still infected inside.

Up close Sela looked terrible. Hollow-eyed, mud to the knees, a bruise darkening along her jaw. She'd run a long way over hard country, and she'd done it alone, and suddenly I couldn't bear how young she looked. We were both so young. That was the joke of it all — crowns and wars and stolen heirs, and at the bottom of it, two kids who'd fallen through ice together.

"You lost my letter," I said. No hello. I couldn't afford a hello; I'd cry, and crying comes later, crying can always come later.

"Yes."

"You pointed them to my tent."

"Yes." Her voice cracked on it. She didn't look away, though. She owed me that much and she paid it, holding my gaze while it wrecked her. "They have my family, Emrys. They came to the mill the night after you left — Marrec himself, sitting at our table, drinking our tea, asking my mother questions with his eyes on the boys. He didn't threaten. He *thanked* her. For raising such a fine daughter in such a loyal valley. And then he said he'd want to visit again soon, and my mother's hands shook for three days, and I—" She stopped. Breathed. "So I followed you. I made a deal. Your location for their protection. And I told myself it would be fine, because you'd be long gone before they ever traced you, because you're clever, because you're *you* —"

"I slept ten feet from you."

"I know." A tear finally fell, and she let it. "I know. I'm sorry. I'm so sorry, Emrys. I'd do it again tomorrow. That's the worst part. They have my brothers, and I'd do it again, and I can't apologize for that, I can only apologize for hurting *you* doing it."

The wind moved through the moor grass, and somewhere far behind us a bird started the day as though none of this had happened, and I stood there holding the truth I'd been walking toward since the border stones: loyalty isn't a thing you have. It's a thing you spend, and she'd spent hers on the people who couldn't defend themselves, and who was I to call it treason? Who was I, heir to a lie, keeper of a stolen name?

But knowing all that didn't unbreak anything. Some things stay broken and you carry them broken, and that's just what love costs in a world like this one.

"Did they pay you in anything besides threats?" I asked. "Information. Did Marrec tell you anything?"

Sela blinked. "Why—"

"Because if I'm going to forgive you someday — and I'm not promising, Sela, hear me clearly, I am *not* promising — then you're going to earn it. Starting now. So: did he say anything?"

She wiped her face with the heel of her hand, and when her voice came, it was steadier. Sharper. My friend was still in there, under the ruin, and she'd brought something back from enemy country after all.

"He talked in his sleep, the second night. At the mill. His men thought I was asleep by the fire." She glanced at Veyra, then back to me. "He said the letter was only half the prize. He said the other half was making sure it never reached the Silent Choir."

Veyra went rigid beside me. "The monastery," she breathed. "The archive. He knows where the records are kept."

"He said," Sela continued, and her eyes found mine, and there — buried under guilt and exhaustion — was the fierce stubborn girl who'd jumped into freezing water for me, "*if the choir reads it aloud, the war starts in spring.*"

The sun cleared the ridge behind her, sudden and blinding, and I thought: sixty years of peace built on a grave, and now the grave itself is ammunition, and the only people who might still stop the firing are a traitor, a rebel, and a boy with no name worth keeping.

"Right," I said. "Then we go to the choir first."

Veyra stared at me. "Emrys, we have no proof. No letter. No—"

"We have something better." I turned east, toward Cindermarch, toward the monastery where patient monks kept the world's memory, and began to walk. "We have witnesses. And I intend to become one."
