## 15. Chasing Paper

*Emrys*

The letter was a day ahead of us and moving fast.

That was the arithmetic I kept doing in my head as the Ashcloaks' horses tore across the moorland, hooves throwing up black peat and cold rain. Somewhere east of us, Dain Marrec's column was carrying a satchel that contained every secret my life had been built on top of, and he was carrying it at the pace of soldiers who knew exactly where they were going, while we were chasing them at the pace of people who only knew which direction to scream in.

"You're going to be sick again," Veyra said, riding beside me without looking at me.

"I'm fine."

"You went grey at the last ford."

"That was the ford's fault. The magic out here sits on the water like oil." It did, too. Ever since I'd crossed the border stone, the world had hummed at me — a low, constant pressure behind my eyes, like a headache with ambitions. In Hollowmere I had spent twenty years tending lamps that held no fire, and I'd thought the silence was the valley being empty. Now I understood it had been the valley holding its breath, and outside it, everything exhaled at once.

Veyra made a sound that might have been sympathy in a language I didn't speak. She rode like she'd been poured onto the horse and set there permanently, grey cloak snapping behind her, her ash-marked face turned into the wind. The Ashcloaks called it the Thornbrand — a burn scarred deliberately over the jaw, the mark of families that had survived what the crowns called the end of the Thorn War. Every one of her riders wore one. Every one of them had earned it in a way none of them talked about.

"Tell me again why we're chasing paper," I shouted over the wind.

"Because paper is power." She said it the way other people said good morning. "Marrec has your letter. Your letter proves what the crown did. With it, he can bury the crime forever — or he can leak it when leaking suits him, which is worse, because then *they* choose the moment and the audience, and the story arrives wearing their clothes."

"And if we catch him?"

"Then I burn it."

I nearly lost my stirrup. "Burn it? We rode a day and a night so you could *burn* it?"

"So nobody else dies for it." Her eyes cut sideways at me, quick and hard. "Emrys. Look around you. That village back there with the whispering well. The children with light in their eyes. Magic came back to this land sixty years too early and it came back wrong, because the wards were built on a lie, and lies rot whatever they're anchored to. That letter is the match. Marrec wants it. I want it. Half the kingdoms want it. And every single one of them wants it for the same reason — to make fire with it. Burn the match, and maybe everyone goes home."

"Or maybe the truth just dies quietly and the valley stays a grave forever."

"Maybe." She faced forward again. "But I've buried enough people to prefer a quiet injustice over a loud war."

I didn't answer. Mostly because she was right in a way that frightened me, and also completely wrong in a way that frightened me more, and I couldn't work out how to say either.

The truth — my truth, the one I hadn't told even Veyra — was smaller and more selfish than justice. I wanted to read the letter. All of it, every line, every seal and signature. Corvane had given me one page; Marrec's men had taken the rest off me at the raid on the camp, along with my cloak, my knife, and most of my dignity. Whatever was written in full, it concerned a stolen infant heir of House Thorn. It concerned my guildmaster's whole life of quiet guilt. And some stubborn, terrified part of me believed — needed to believe — that before anyone burned anything or broadcast anything or built an army on it, someone should simply *read it*. Like a person. Like a son.

The rain thickened. Ahead, the column of Ashcloaks bent eastward along a ridgeline, following a tracker named Osk who read ground the way Sela read weather. Behind us, the moor unspooled grey to the horizon, empty except for crows, which I had decided not to take as an omen because I couldn't afford any more omens that week.

"There," Osk called, pointing. "Smoke. Thin. They've stopped to eat."

Veyra smiled, and it was not a comforting smile.

* * *

*Veyra*

I had taken the Winterbourne boy into my camp for three reasons, and I was honest enough with myself to count them.

One: his guildmaster had kept records, and records were worth more than soldiers. Two: the boy himself was evidence — a Warden's apprentice raised inside the sealed valley, alive at the exact hour the wards fell, which no honest explanation survived contact with. Three — and this one I kept folded small, in the pocket of myself where I kept the things I wasn't proud of — the old hedge-witch Maren had looked at him and gone white, and Maren did not go white at nothing.

A living heir of House Thorn would do more for my cause than ten thousand dead ones. Dead heirs made martyrs. Living heirs made *kingdoms*.

So far, though, the living heir was mostly making trouble.

"He'll slow us down," Osk muttered beside me as we dismounted behind the ridge, a half-mile from the crown camp's cookfires. Rain dripped off the bracken. "He rides like a sack of turnips and he argues like a scholar."

"He argues because he thinks," I said. "It's rare. Don't shoot it on principle."

Osk grunted. He had carried his Thornbrand since he was nine years old, when the crown's reclamation companies burned the second valley, the one that isn't in any history book. He did not have pockets full of things he wasn't proud of. He just had anger, worn smooth as riverstone from handling.

Below us, the enemy camp sat in its hollow like a fist in a glove. Forty riders, maybe fifty. Marrec kept a disciplined camp — pickets posted properly, horses watered in rotation, fires small. I had fought against royal officers my whole adult life and I will say this for Dain Marrec, whom I otherwise intended to kill or ruin: he did not waste men, and he did not mistreat prisoners, and both of those things made him infinitely more dangerous than the bastards who did.

"The satchel stays with the command tent," Osk said. "We've seen it. A courier sleeps with it. Marrec doesn't trust his own shadows."

"Good. Then we don't take the camp." I traced the plan in the mud between us. "We take the road ahead of them. There's a defile at Harrow Beck — single file, wet stone, a drop on the left. If they ride through it tomorrow morning, we can bring down the scree and take the satchel out of the middle of their column without fighting fifty men. We fight six confused ones and a courier."

"And the boy?"

"What about him?"

"He'll want to talk to them. Reason with them. He's got that look."

I looked over at Emrys, who was standing apart from the others, staring down at the enemy camp with an expression I recognized because I'd worn it once, years ago, before I'd learned what looking did to you. Homesick and furious about it. Guilty and innocent about it.

"He's not wrong that I'd rather this ended without corpses," I said. "He's just wrong that talking is a kind of weapon anyone down there respects. Let him come. He can carry rope."

* * *

*Emrys*

Carrying rope, it turned out, was my entire military value.

We moved before dawn, forty Ashcloaks walking their horses soft along the high ground above Harrow Beck, and I walked among them trying not to think about the fact that I had spent last week repairing lamps and this week helping stage an ambush on the king's own soldiers. My hands were steady. That surprised me. Inside, I felt like the flickering lamp in the guildhouse storeroom — the one real flame hiding among fakes, waiting for someone to notice and put it out.

The defile was exactly what Veyra had promised: a throat of wet stone with the beck roaring below and scree slopes stacked above like a rockslide having second thoughts. We split. Veyra took twelve riders to block the far mouth. Osk took the rest up onto the slope with ropes and iron bars, ready to bring the hillside down on cue. I stood in the lee of a boulder with a coil of rope and the growing, sick certainty that I was about to watch people die for a piece of paper, and that some part of me — the part that wanted to read it first — was complicit in all of it.

"They're coming," a scout whispered.

Hoofbeats. Then the column, threading into the defile in twos, torches hissing in the drizzle. I picked out Marrec by his seat — upright, unhurried, a man riding through a hostile country the way a teacher walks through a rowdy classroom. The courier rode near the center with the satchel strapped crosswise over his chest, and my heart did something humiliating and hopeful at the sight of it. *There. That's everything. That's my mother's lullaby and my birthmark and Corvane's whole silent life, right there in leather and wax.*

The column reached the narrows.

Veyra gave the signal — a single note from a bone whistle, high enough to hurt — and the mountain answered.

I will hear that sound for the rest of my life. The scree let go all at once, tons of stone pouring down the slope, and the defile filled with screaming horses and cracking timber and dust that ate the torchlight. Osk's crew slid down on ropes toward the wrecked middle of the column. Veyra's riders charged the far mouth. It was brutal and fast and exactly as planned, and it still went wrong in under a minute, because plans are just stories you tell stones.

Because Marrec didn't panic.

Even half-buried in chaos, he got his vanguard turned and formed — I watched it happen, watched royal greys close ranks in the smoke like ink dropping into water — and suddenly it wasn't an ambush anymore, it was a battle in a corridor, and battles in corridors don't care whose paper it is.

I saw the courier, though. Alive, unhorsed, scrambling up the near slope with the satchel bouncing against his chest, heading for open moor. Nobody else had seen him. Everyone was busy killing each other over the principle of him.

I went after him.

I'm not brave. I want that recorded somewhere, by someone neutral. What I am is stubborn, and grief makes me stupid, and somewhere in the smoke I had decided that I had already lost my home, my guildmaster, my name, and possibly my entire identity, and I was not losing the one object in the world that explained why.

I caught him at the top of the rise. He was young — younger than me — and he swung a short sword at me with the flat, mechanical competence of a man trained to deal with bandits. I had exactly one advantage: I had spent twenty years hauling, climbing, and fixing things on bad terrain, and he had spent his career sitting a horse. I took the sword stroke on my rope-wrapped forearm, which hurt like a broken promise, shoved him, and we both went down in the wet grass wrestling over a leather bag like children fighting over bread.

He was stronger. He was winning. He had his knee on my chest and his hand reaching for his dagger when a shadow came over us both and Veyra's boot connected with his temple, gently, in the way of people who have done it often.

She hauled me up by the collar. "You absolute idiot."

"It worked."

"You're bleeding."

"Rope burns. Mostly his fault." I got my fingers on the satchel strap and pulled it free, and the weight of it — just paper, just wax seals and folded pages, light as anything — nearly buckled my knees. Around us the battle was collapsing back into rain and moaning horses. The Ashcloaks were breaking off, as drilled, melting into the bracken with six dead of ours left behind and more of Marrec's. Down in the defile, through the settling dust, I saw Dain Marrec on his feet, sword drawn, blood on his cheek, scanning the ridge — and I swear on every lamp in Hollowmere that he found me instantly, across two hundred yards of murk, and simply *looked* at me. Not angry. Taking notes.

"Move," Veyra said. "Now. He'll regroup within the hour and he knows this country better than we pretend to."

* * *

*Emrys*

We didn't stop until noon, and when we finally halted in a fold of moorland with sentries out and horses blowing, I knelt in the peat and opened the satchel with hands that shook for reasons that had nothing to do with the fight.

Veyra stood over me. "Burn it now," she said, "while we still can. While it cost what it cost and buys nothing worse."

I looked up at her. Rain, exhaustion, six dead riders who'd followed her banner, and she was offering to throw away the prize for all of it. There are people who are ruthless because they enjoy it, and people who are ruthless because they're trying to save the world from themselves, and I was beginning to understand which kind I'd thrown in with.

"One hour," I said. "Give me one hour to read all of it. If it says what you think it says, burn it, broadcast it, nail it to a church door — I don't care. But I'm not letting anyone decide the fate of my family's crimes without reading them first. Including you. *Especially* you."

Something crossed her face — annoyance, respect, and underneath both, briefly, hunger. She wanted to know what was in those pages too. She'd never admit it. She didn't have to.

"One hour," she said. "Then I decide."

I broke the first seal. It was Corvane's hand — cramped, careful, the handwriting of a man who had spent sixty years writing things he hoped no one would ever read. And the opening lines were not about politics at all.

They were about a baby. About a night in a burning house. About a choice.

And halfway down the first page, in ink gone brown with age, was a name I knew better than my own — because it *was* my own.

I heard Veyra's breath change beside me. She was reading over my shoulder.

She already knew what it said. From the way she'd gone absolutely still, I understood that some part of her had known all along.

"Emrys," she said quietly, "there's something you should—"

Down the slope, a sentry whistled. Marrec's riders, cresting the moor, a full column, coming straight for us.

My hour was up.
