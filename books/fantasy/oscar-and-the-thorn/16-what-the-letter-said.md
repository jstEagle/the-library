## 16. What the Letter Said

The letter came back to us the way most things do on the moors: badly, and at speed.

We'd chased Marrec's column for two days—Veyra riding like a woman who had personally offended her horse, me clinging to my saddle with the desperate piety of a man who has recently discovered that his stomach is a separate entity with its own political opinions. The Ashcloaks' outriders found the supply wagon abandoned at a burned waystation, and there, wedged under a loose floorboard with the casualness of someone who'd hidden it in a hurry, was a courier's satchel. And in the satchel, wrapped in oilcloth against the damp, was Corvane's letter.

Or what remained of it.

Marrec's people had opened it—I could tell by the wax, cracked cleanly rather than broken by thumb—and I braced myself to find it gutted, pages torn out or burned. But it was all there. Every page. Which meant Marrec had read it too, and had chosen, for reasons of his own, not to destroy it. I filed that away. It seemed like the sort of thing that would matter later, the way a distant thunderclap matters before you understand it's coming for you.

"He wanted us to have it," Veyra said, turning the pages over with gloved fingers. "Or he wanted us to *think* we took it."

"Does it matter?"

She looked at me then, and there was something almost gentle in her face, which frightened me more than any of her ruthlessness had. "Everything matters, Emrys. That's the curse of being right."

* * *

We made camp that night in the shell of an old watchtower, one of the war's leftovers, half-swallowed by gorse and standing at an angle like a drunk leaning on a friend. Veyra posted sentries and then did something she rarely did: left me alone with the letter.

"You should read it without me watching," she said. "Whatever's in it, you deserve to meet it privately first."

I remember thinking that was decent of her. I remember also thinking, with a small cold part of my mind, that she'd already read everything twice.

The tower had no roof, so I sat where the stars could see me—the new ones, the wrong ones, sliding slowly back into their places like guests returning to a house they'd been evicted from. I broke Corvane's seal, which felt like breaking something else entirely, and I began to read.

* * *

My guildmaster wrote the way he taught: plainly, precisely, and without a single word more than the truth required. He knew I'd know his hand. He knew I'd know when he was telling me something he hated telling me. So he told it straight.

*Emrys—if you are reading this, then I am gone, and the wards are down, and you have done exactly what I trained you never to do, which is to leave the valley. Good.*

*You will want to hate me for what follows. Save some of it. You'll need the rest for other people.*

*Hollowmere was not built as a refuge. It was built as a vault.*

*At the end of the Thorn War, the fighting had gone on long enough that both kingdoms were drowning in their own dead. House Thorn's heir—an infant girl, born in the last winter of the war—was the last living claimant to the Thorn succession. As long as she lived, the war had a reason to continue. Her existence was a banner, and banners get people killed.*

*So the founding Warden of Hollowmere—a man named Aldous Winterbourne, my own grandsire's grandsire—took her.*

*Not rescued. Not sheltered. Took. There is no kind way to write this, so I won't pretend to find one. He stole her from her cradle in the burning of Thornholt, carried her through the pass, and sealed the valley behind wards anchored to her blood. Then he gave her a new name, a new history, and eventually, when she was old enough to be lied to convincingly, a husband from among the wardens themselves. The peace treaty was signed six weeks later. Both sides believed the heir dead. Without her, the Thorn cause dissolved, and the war ended—not because anyone made peace, but because someone made the reason for war disappear.*

*The wards needed an anchor of royal blood to hold. Aldous used hers. And when she grew and bore children of her own, the anchor passed down the line—the line he started, the lie braided into blood itself. Every Winterbourne since has been both jailer and prisoner: keeper of the wards, keeper of the secret, and keeper of the child who was never supposed to exist.*

*I am the last warden who knew the whole of it. My father told me at nineteen, as his father told him. We were told it was mercy. A life saved. But I have read Aldous's private ledger, Emrys, and mercy does not keep accounts of what it costs other people.*

*The name she was born with was Veyra Thorn.*

I stopped reading.

I want to be honest about what happened next, because I've spent years learning to describe things accurately—it's the trade I was raised in, tending lamps, recording what the wards held back—and accuracy demanded that I sit very still for a while with the wind coming through a roofless tower, holding three pages of my guildmaster's handwriting, feeling something in my chest come apart with a sound I couldn't hear but could feel, the way you feel a tooth crack before you taste the blood.

Veyra Thorn.

The rebel leader who'd pulled me out of Maren's village, fed me, armed me, called me *herald*—her name wasn't a coincidence, couldn't be, she'd said she descended from survivors, collateral branches, the scattered family—but the infant in the letter, the stolen heir—

*A girl,* the letter said. *Born in the last winter of the war.*

And the arithmetic started whether I wanted it to or not. It started the way sums start when you've been avoiding them: quietly, then all at once.

Sixty years since the wards rose. Sixty years since the valley sealed. I was twenty-three. My mother—my mother whose lullabies were in Old Thorn dialect, whose songs none of the other wardens' families sang, whose grave I'd tended every autumn with a devotion I'd never examined—she had died when I was seven. Sixteen years before the wards fell. She had lived her whole life inside Hollowmere, and if the letter was true, then she was either the stolen heir herself grown old in hiding, or—

No. Do the arithmetic properly, Emrys. You were trained for precision.

The stolen heir, born the last winter of the war, would be sixty-one now, if she lived. My mother died at thirty-four. So she was not the heir. She was the heir's *daughter*. Which made the heir my grandmother, which made—

Which made the anchor of the wards. The blood that sixty years of Hollowmere's peace hung upon. The royal blood of House Thorn, thinned across two generations of deliberate obscurity, diluted and hidden and guarded by men sworn to forget why they were guarding it—

Was mine.

*You will have done the arithmetic by now,* Corvane wrote, further down the page, in a hand that had begun to shake slightly, as though even ink could feel the weight of it. *I'm sorry. I considered sparing you the sum. But you'll be safer knowing than guessing, and besides—you deserved the truth more than anyone alive, because you have paid for it your entire life without ever seeing the bill.*

*You asked me once, when you were twelve, why the lamps flickered near you and no one else. I told you it was your talent. It was. Your talent is that you are the lock. The wards were anchored to your grandmother's blood, and then to your mother's, and then to yours. When the stars went out, it was not sabotage alone. The anchor was failing because you were leaving—or because the saboteur knew that you would. Either way, Emrys, understand this clearly: the sky did not break on its own. It broke around you.*

*The valley's peace was purchased with a stolen child. The interest has compounded for sixty years. And the debt collector is coming, wearing whatever face it wears in your time. All I could give you was the truth and a head start. I'm sorry it isn't more. I'm sorry it isn't less.*

*Burn this if you must. Keep it if you can. But whatever you decide, decide it as yourself—not as the heir, not as the apprentice, not as the weapon anyone wants to make of you. Decide it as Emrys.*

*That name was given to you by your mother, who chose it from a book of old Thorn saints. Remember that someone loved you before any of this. Remember it when the rest of us have earned nothing but your anger.*

*—C.*

* * *

I don't know how long I sat there. Long enough that the gorse stopped scratching my legs, which means I'd stopped noticing it, which means I'd gone somewhere else entirely.

Here is what I remember of that hour, in fragments:

The wind through the broken tower, making a low sound in the stones like a choir humming the note before the song starts.

My own hands, holding pages, perfectly steady, which struck me as obscene. Some part of me should have been shaking. Instead my hands behaved like a good apprentice's hands—careful, competent, keeping the paper safe—as though the boy they belonged to still existed.

The thought, arriving with terrible clarity: *the lamp.* The lamp in the border shrine that had flickered with true light when I touched it, that I'd hidden from Corvane for months, ashamed of it, proud of it, treasuring it as the one proof I had any magic at all. It hadn't been proof of talent. It had been proof of proximity. The wards hadn't been failing near me—they'd been *recognizing* me. Sixty years of Hollowmere's magic, straining toward its anchor like a dog toward its master, and the master had been a boy mending lamp-glass with beeswax and twine, thinking himself ordinary.

Every strange thing about my life reassembled itself around the new center of gravity. The way the ward-stones hummed when I walked past. Why Corvane had assigned me the night watches himself, always, every year, though there were older wardens. Why my mother sang songs in a language no one else spoke, and went quiet whenever travelers asked about it. Why Corvane looked at me sometimes—those long looks I'd catalogued as affection, or worry, or the particular melancholy of a man growing old with only one student—why he looked at me the way a man looks at a debt he can never repay and cannot escape.

He hadn't been my guildmaster who happened to love me. He had been the last keeper of a prison, and I was the prison, and he had loved me anyway, and I could not yet tell which of those facts hurt worst.

* * *

Veyra found me there eventually. Of course she did. I heard her boots on the stone, unhurried, giving me warning, giving me the courtesy of composure—which was itself a kind of calculation, I understood now, everything with her was—and she sat down beside me on the fallen lintel without asking permission.

"Well?" she said softly.

I held out the letter instead of answering. She didn't take it. She'd read it already; we both knew the choreography.

"The heir was a girl," I said. "Born the last winter of the war."

"Yes."

"She'd be sixty-one now."

"Yes."

"And she had a daughter." My voice came out flat, recitation-flat, the voice I used for ward-inscriptions. "Who had a son."

Veyra was quiet for a moment. When she spoke, her voice had the careful warmth of someone handling something that might detonate. "Say it, Emrys. Whatever it is. Say it out loud, because it doesn't become real until it's said, and I've watched men carry unspoken things until the carrying killed them."

I laughed. It came out wrong—too short, too sharp, a laugh with no humor in it anywhere, just the shape of one. "You want me to say it? Fine. The founding warden of Hollowmere stole the heir of House Thorn to end a war, hid her in a valley behind wards keyed to her own blood, and the Winterbourne line—the line that raised me, the line whose name I wear, the only family I have ever had—that line has been guarding the theft ever since." I turned to look at her, and the stars wheeled slowly overhead, misaligned, patient, waiting. "And the arithmetic says the blood ran down through two generations into me. The wards weren't failing, Veyra. They were calling. To me. They've been calling to me my whole life, and I thought it was *talent*."

"There it is," she said quietly.

"There it is," I agreed. "I'm the heir. Or close enough that the difference won't matter to anyone with an army. I'm the stolen child's grandchild, the anchor of the wards, the reason the stars went out, and the best casus belli two kingdoms have seen in sixty years." I folded the letter along its creases, carefully, precisely, because my hands insisted on doing their job even if the rest of me had resigned. "Corvane knew. He's known since I was a baby. He raised the heir in a lamplit cellar and taught him to trim wicks."

"Are you angry?"

"I'm—" I stopped. Searched honestly. Found the answer and disliked it. "I'm grieving someone I never met. The boy I thought I was. He seems like he was decent. Quiet. Good with his hands. I'd have liked him." I stood, and my knees objected, and I ignored them. "And yes. Underneath that. Angry enough to burn something down. Which is exactly why I'm not going to trust the feeling."

Veyra smiled then, and it was the smile I'd learned to distrust—the one that meant a door had opened somewhere and she intended to walk through it. "Then you understand better than most what that truth is worth."

"No," I said, pocketing the letter. "What I understand is that everyone who learns what I am immediately starts calculating how to use it. Marrec wants to bury it. You want to crown it. Somewhere out there, Corvane—" I stopped, because the thought arrived mid-sentence and knocked the breath out of it: somewhere out there, Corvane was running, wounded maybe, hunted certainly, and every hour I spent being the prize in other people's games was an hour he was spending being the consequence. "Somewhere out there, the man who actually loved me is paying for this. So here's my price, Veyra, and it's non-negotiable: I'm not a herald, and I'm not a banner. The next person who tells me what I am gets to hear my opinion of it first."

She inclined her head, gracious as a queen, unreadable as weather. "Fair terms."

They weren't fair terms. They were the terms of a scared young man trying to hold onto one small true thing—his own name—in a world that had just informed him his name was the least true thing about him.

Above us, the returned stars slid another degree out of true, and neither of us mentioned it, and both of us noticed.

And far off across the dark moor, so faint I might have imagined it, a bell began to ring—the pilgrim road's warning bell, the one Maren's village hung for strangers. Someone was traveling by night. Someone was following our fire-light home.

Veyra's head came up like a hawk's. "Company," she said, and reached for her sword, and I put my hand over Corvane's letter in my pocket and thought, with a strange calm: *he read it and let it live. What does a man like that want with me now?*
