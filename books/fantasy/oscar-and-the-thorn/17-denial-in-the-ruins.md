## 17. Denial in the Ruins

I did not sleep. I don't mean that in the dramatic way people say it, the way they mean *I lay awake fretting about taxes*. I mean that when dawn should have come to the ruins of Harrow Fen, the sky stayed the color of a bruise and my mind stayed the color of a slammed door, and somewhere behind that door a single sentence kept knocking.

*The Winterbourne line has guarded the lie.*

And beneath it, smaller, worse: *the stolen infant heir of House Thorn.*

I had read the letter four times by candlelight and once more by the grey false morning, and each reading was like pressing on a tooth. You know it will hurt. You do it anyway, because some part of you needs to confirm that the hurt is real and not a trick of the tongue.

The arithmetic was simple. That was the obscene thing about it. Sixty years since the Thorn War ended. The valley founded the same season. A warden named Aldric Winterbourne arriving with an infant no one questioned, because who questions a baby? Babies come with women. The woman had died—of fever, said the guild records, though I now understood that every record in Hollowmere was a sentence someone had chosen to write. And I had been born twenty-one years ago. Twenty-one. Not sixty. But bloodlines are not calendars; they are chains, and chains get passed hand to hand. Corvane had no children. Corvane had never married. Corvane had, now that I thought of it with my stomach slowly rotating, always looked at me the way a man looks at something he is carrying for someone else.

So the arithmetic went like this: if the heir of House Thorn had been smuggled into Hollowmere as an infant, and if the Wardens' Guild's true purpose was not warding but hiding, then the heir would have been raised as a Warden's foundling, and then passed to the next warden when he came of age or when the last one died, and—

No. Stop. That logic only worked if the heir was still alive, still hidden, still *someone*, and the letter didn't say that. It said the line guarded the lie. It didn't say the line *was* the lie.

Except it also didn't have to say it, did it? Because why else would Corvane give the letter to me? Why else would he look at me the way he looked at me? Why else would the wards—the wards I had tended all my life, the lamps I had trimmed like a man trimming candles before a shrine—why else would the whole sky have gone out the moment—

The moment what? I hadn't done anything. I'd stood on a border stone and watched stars vanish like snuffed wicks. I hadn't claimed anything. I hadn't even known there was anything to claim.

But magic, apparently, does not require your signature. Magic, it seemed, only required your existence.

* * *

Veyra found me at midmorning sitting in the shell of what had once been a chapel, judging by the arches. Everything at Harrow Fen was a shell of something. The war had come through here at the end and broken it the way a child breaks a thing to see what's inside, and nobody had ever come back to reassemble the pieces. Thorn banners hung from the broken tower, faded to the color of old tea. Ashcloak sentries nodded at Veyra as she picked her way across the fallen masonry toward me, and I noticed—they were watching me too. Not her. Me. With a kind of hungry attention I told myself I was imagining.

"You look terrible," she said.

"Thank you. I've been doing arithmetic."

She lowered herself onto a fallen pillar across from me, folding her hands in her lap with the patience of someone who has decided to wait out a storm. "And?"

"And nothing. It's just numbers. Numbers can be made to say anything. You taught me that yourself, remember? In your version of the war."

"My version," she said quietly, "had mass graves in it. Yours had lamp oil." She let that sit between us, then gentled. "Emrys. What does the letter actually say?"

"It says a founding warden stole a baby." My voice came out flat, which surprised me. I had expected it to shake. "It doesn't name the baby. It doesn't say what happened to him. For all I know, the child died on the road and the whole conspiracy has been guarding an empty cradle for sixty years, and the wards fell because—" I gestured vaguely at the bruised sky. "Because magic is having a very bad decade."

"Do you believe that?"

"I want to."

"That's not what I asked."

"No," I said. "It isn't."

She studied me for a long moment. Veyra Thorn had a face built for conviction—sharp planes, dark eyes that held you like a hand around the wrist—and I watched her decide, deliberately, to soften it. It was like watching someone sheathe a blade so you'll talk longer. "Tell me about your mother," she said.

The question caught me sideways. "What?"

"The woman who raised you. Before Corvane. You must have had one."

"She died when I was small. Fever." I heard myself repeat the guild-record sentence and flinched. "That's what I was told. Sela's mother helped wash the body. There was a funeral. There's a marker behind the guildhouse—a plain stone, no name carved, which I always thought was strange, but Hollowmere is strange about graves, we don't put names on them, we say names call things back and—"

I stopped.

Because I had heard my mother sing. That was the thing. That was the splinter working its way to the surface of everything. She used to sing when she thought I was asleep, songs I'd never heard from anyone else in the valley, in words that weren't quite our words—older, rounder, a language with moss growing in it. When I was eight I asked her what language it was and she stopped singing for a month.

Old Thorn dialect. I knew that now. Every Ashcloak prayer I'd heard in this camp, every curse Veyra's soldiers muttered over their dice, carried that same mossy roundness. The language of a kingdom that no longer existed, sung over a foundling boy in a valley that existed specifically to make sure no one remembered it.

"Emrys?" Veyra said.

"There were lullabies," I said, and hated how small my voice went. "In Old Thorn. Nobody sings those anymore except you people. Except—" I couldn't finish it.

Veyra leaned forward. She didn't touch me. I was grateful; I think if anyone had touched me just then I'd have shattered like a dropped lamp. "Go on."

"A birthmark." I laughed, and it came out wrong, jagged. "This is ridiculous. Everyone has birthmarks. Mine is on my left shoulder, a thorn-shaped—"

I stopped again. Because I could see her face changing. Could see the careful softness crack into something rawer, older, and I realized with a sick lurch that I had just handed her the last piece of a puzzle she had been assembling for years.

"Say it," she whispered. "Say the rest."

"A thorn-shaped mark. Red. My mother called it my rose-thorn kiss and said it meant I'd been claimed by something that loved me too hard to let go." I pressed the heels of my hands against my eyes. "Which is exactly the sort of thing mothers say, Veyra. Mothers invent meanings. That's most of what a mother is."

"Yes," she said. "And mine told me the heir of House Thorn bore the mark of the first thorn, on the left shoulder, red as spilled wine. It's in the family records. It's in three separate survivor accounts from the war. Emrys—it's in the letter you're carrying, if you read past the grief."

I had read past the grief. That was the problem. I had read every word, and every word fit, and fit was the one thing I could not forgive any of it for.

* * *

I walked out of the camp alone after that. Nobody stopped me; I think Veyra gave the sentries a look. I wandered east through the ruins until the tents and cookfires were behind me, until I reached the place the Ashcloaks used for their dead—a field of markers, thousands of them, little cairns of black stone stretching to the treeline like a fossilized forest. Names scratched deep, in Old Thorn characters, weathered but stubborn.

I found the newest section, the ones for soldiers lost in the last few years of skirmishing, and then I found, off to the side, apart, a cluster of much older stones. These had been tended. Fresh flowers. Someone came here often.

The central marker was tall, and the carving on it was crisp, renewed by loving hands:

**LIANNE THORN. LAST QUEEN OF THE THORN. MURDERED WITH HER HOUSE ON THE NIGHT OF ASHES. SURVIVED IN EXILE BY ONE CHILD.**

Survived in exile by one child.

I stood there while the bruised sky pressed down and the wind moved through the cairn-forest making a sound like a congregation whispering, and I tried to feel like a prince. I tried to summon even a flicker of recognition, some royal shiver, some sense of homecoming. Anything.

What I felt instead was a memory, absurdly specific, unbearably ordinary: Corvane teaching me to trim a lamp-wick when I was six, his huge scarred hand wrapped around my small one, saying *gentle, lad, gentle, the flame is a living thing, you don't command it, you negotiate.* The smell of lamp oil and beeswax in the guildhouse cellar. Sela's laughter echoing off the millrace. Rain on the border stones. My whole life, laid out end to end, and every inch of it suddenly suspect—not because any of it was fake, but because I could no longer tell which parts were real and which parts were assignments.

Had my mother's lullabies been love, or protocol? Had Corvane's tenderness been affection, or maintenance? Had the guild raised a boy, or curated an artifact?

You cannot grieve properly when you don't know what you've lost. If my childhood had been a cage, I could rage against it. If it had been a kindness, I could mourn it. But if it had been both—if the bars had been wrapped in wool, if every act of care had been also an act of concealment—then there was nothing to hold. Grief needs a shape. Mine kept sliding through my fingers like water.

I sat down among the dead queens and dead soldiers and did the other arithmetic. The one that mattered less and hurt more.

If I was the heir, then my mother—the woman with the mossy songs, whoever she truly was—had either been a kidnapper's accomplice or a stranger paid to play a role, or she had genuinely loved a child placed in her arms and died without ever telling him the truth, and I would never know which, because she was under a nameless stone behind a guildhouse in a valley built on a grave.

And if I was not the heir—if this was all coincidence, if the lullabies and the birthmark and Corvane's long looks were just the random debris of an ordinary life—then I was a lamp-tender from nowhere having a spectacular breakdown in a graveyard, and Veyra Thorn was going to build a rebellion out of my corpse anyway, because she wanted it too much to check whether it was true.

There was no version where I got to just be Emrys again. That was the trap. That was the elegant, vicious design of it. The letter had taken the one self I owned and set it on fire in front of me, and now every path forward led through the ashes.

I must have sat there for hours. The light never changed—the stars were still gone, and whatever pale imitation of day the world was running on, it didn't have the decency to move.

When I finally looked up, Veyra was standing at the edge of the cairn-field. She hadn't approached. She'd just waited, which I was learning was its own kind of tactic with her.

"You're wondering," she said, "whether I'd want you even if you were nobody."

"The thought crossed the ruins."

"I took you in before I finished reading the letter," she said. "You were bleeding, half-frozen, and lying about your name badly. Whatever you are on paper, I decided what you were on the road." A pause. "But I won't insult you by pretending the paper doesn't matter. It matters enormously. To my people, to the crowns, to every grave in this field. And to you, whether you like it or not."

"That's what frightens me," I said. "It shouldn't matter. A man is what he does, not what was done to him before he could walk."

"Then you believe that," she said. "Prove it. Live it. Don't run from the claim and don't kneel to it—decide it." Her voice dropped, and for once there was no general in it, only a woman standing among her dead. "But decide soon, Emrys. Because Marrec is on the moor with a column and a traitor's map, and the sky is coming apart over our heads, and the world is not going to wait for you to finish grieving a boy who may or may not exist."

I looked at the queen's marker one last time. *Survived in exile by one child.*

"One more thing," I said. "The night the wards fell. The moment the stars went out. Did anything happen at that exact time? Here, in the camp? Anything strange?"

Veyra frowned. "Why?"

"Because I need to know if the sky broke when I *learned* the truth," I said, "or whether it broke hours earlier—which would mean it wasn't the letter that did it. It would mean something else anchored those wards. Something that changed. Something that—" I swallowed. "Something that died."

Veyra went very still. Then she said, quietly, "Come with me," and started walking toward the camp at a pace that made it clear the grieving would have to wait.
