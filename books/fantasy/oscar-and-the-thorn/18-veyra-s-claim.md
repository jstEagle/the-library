## 18. Veyra's Claim

I have lied to kings, priests, and my own reflection, and I have never once lost sleep over it. Lying to Emrys Winterbourne was different, and I resented that difference before I even understood it. It is one thing to deceive a man. It is another to deceive someone who looks at you the way he did in those first days at the Ashcloak camp—like a drowning man looking at a rope, entirely certain the rope could not possibly want anything from him.

He was wrong about the rope. Ropes always want something. That is what makes them ropes and not gifts.

* * *

It happened on the fourth night, in the longhouse at Thornhold, after the others had gone to their beds and the fire had burned down to that red, honest light that makes confession feel like conversation.

He was mending a strap on his satchel with the same patient, fumbling competence he brought to everything—the lamp-warden's hands, hands made for small repairs—and I sat across from him with a cup of nettle wine I had no intention of drinking, because I needed my head clear for what came next.

"You should know," I said, "why I really took you off that road."

His needle stopped. Not dramatically. It just stopped, the way a bird stops singing when it hears a cat's bell. Small things first. Always the small things tell you first.

"I assumed," he said carefully, "it was because Marrec's riders were twenty minutes behind me."

"That was convenient. Convenience is rarely motive." I turned the cup in my hands. "Do you know how old I was when the war ended?"

"Older than me."

"Funny boy. I was six." I let that sit. Six years old, carried out of Cindermarch on a dead woman's back, through fields where the crown's soldiers were doing arithmetic with bayonets—counting every soul with Thorn blood and subtracting it from the future. "Six years old, and I watched men burn a granary with forty families locked inside it, and they called it peacekeeping. The peace you grew up inside, Emrys—the one your valley kept so beautifully, all those gentle lamps and quiet nights—it has a floor. And the floor is made of us."

"I know. The letter—"

"The letter is paper." I set it down between us. I had let him keep it; that much mercy I'd allowed myself. "Paper burns, gets stolen, gets disputed by scholars until everyone's tired of arguing. You know what doesn't get disputed? A face."

He frowned. He was slow to anger, this boy—I'll grant him that—but he wasn't slow. People mistake him for slow because he pauses. His pausing is a warden's habit: check the lamp twice before you swear it's lit.

"What face?" he said.

So I told him. About the portraits in the vaults beneath Cindermarch, the ones my grandmother's people had smuggled out piece by piece over sixty years, hidden in book bindings and coffin linings. Portraits of House Thorn. And about an old woman named Cassia who had nursed the last trueborn daughter of that house, and who remembered—not a portrait, but a sound—the particular way a Thorn infant cried, high and furious, like the world owed it an apology.

"When the wards went up around Hollowmere," I said, "sixty years ago, one child went in who never came out on any census. A foundling. Taken in by a guildmaster with no family and too many secrets. And every few years, Cassia's granddaughter—my aunt—would send someone to look at that foundling. Just to look. To compare a jawline against a dead man's portrait."

The fire popped. He didn't move.

"They reported back, year after year: no. Too tall for a Thorn. Wrong coloring. Wrong everything. And then—" I smiled, and I hated my own smile in that moment, because I knew exactly what it looked like, I had practiced it in broken mirrors since I was fourteen and learned it could stop a room, "—and then three years ago the reports changed. The foundling had grown into his grandfather's brow. His mother's mouth. And there was a birthmark behind his left ear shaped, if you squinted and wanted very badly to see it, like a thorn."

His hand went up to his ear. I watched him do it—watched him touch the mark he must have thought was nothing, an accident of skin—and something in his face closed like a door.

"You're lying," he said.

"Yes," I agreed pleasantly. "That's the question, isn't it? Whether I'm lying. I've asked it of myself every night since you arrived." I leaned forward. "Here is what I can give you honestly, Emrys Winterbourne, or whatever your name is. When my scouts brought me word three years ago that the foundling at Hollowmere might be the heir, I wept. Actual tears, alone in a tent, like a fool. Because I had spent thirty years building a rebellion out of grief and grudge, and grief and grudge are terrible mortar. They hold until winter. But a living heir—that's stone. That's a cause a farmer can believe in. 'Come fight for my grievances' recruits the angry. 'Come restore the rightful king' recruits everyone."

"So that's what I am." His voice had gone flat. Warden-flat, level as a lamp-glass. "A recruitment poster."

"You are the difference between a riot and a restoration."

"That's not an answer to the question."

"It's the only honest answer I have. I didn't know. I hoped. There's a canyon between those two things and I have been living at the bottom of it for three years." I stood, because sitting still while a man decides whether to hate you has never been among my talents. "When I saw you on that road—this gangly lamp-boy with ash on his sleeves clutching a letter like it was a holy relic—I didn't think, 'there walks my king.' I thought, 'there walks my hope, and I am going to catch it before Marrec does.'"

"And if I hadn't been the heir?" He was standing now too. "If some archivist had done the measurements and I was just a foundling with a birthmark and good cheekbones—what then? What was I to you?"

"A courier," I said. "A brave one. I'd have gotten the letter out and mourned you properly."

He laughed. It was not a kind laugh, and it did something strange to my chest, hearing it—not guilt exactly, guilt is a luxury I budgeted away decades ago, but something adjacent. Something in the same poor neighborhood.

"Twice," he said. "Corvane used me to guard his lie for ten years. And now you—you didn't take me in, you took delivery. Like cargo." He picked up the letter, and I noticed his hands were shaking, and I noticed that I noticed, which was new and unwelcome. "Did you ever once, in these weeks, ask me what I wanted? Not what the heir wants. Me. The boy who fixed lamps."

And here is the truth I will only admit here, in the privacy of my own telling: I opened my mouth to say yes, of course, of course I saw *you*, and I discovered that the machinery of charm, which has never failed me, produced nothing. Because the answer was no. I had looked at him for three weeks and seen a throne wearing a young man's coat. I had planned his speeches, imagined his coronation, rehearsed the moment I would kneel—and I had never once asked him what he wanted for breakfast.

"No," I said. "I didn't."

Something went out of his shoulders. Not relief. Surrender, maybe, or the opposite of surrender—I couldn't read it, and I realized with genuine alarm that I had lost track of what he was feeling, and that this mattered to me, and that *that* was the most dangerous development of my entire career.

"Veyra," he said quietly, "do you know what I did when the stars went out? Before I knew anything about heirs and wars? I checked the lamps. All eleven border stones. Because that was my job, and jobs matter, and someone has to tend the small fires or nobody sees anything at all." He tucked the letter inside his coat. "You don't want a herald. You want a flag. Flags don't get asked questions. Flags just hang there while other people march under them."

"Emrys—"

"Don't." He held up a hand, and for one absurd moment he sounded exactly like Corvane—like every exhausted teacher who ever caught their best student cheating. "I need you to understand that I'm not leaving. Not tonight. Marrec is out there, and the letter is real, and whatever I am or aren't, people buried in those graves deserve better than both of us using them." He looked at me, and his eyes were wet and furious and steady, all three at once, which should not be possible in a human face. "But I'm not yours. Whatever I decide to do next, I decide. And Veyra—if you lie to me again, even a small one, even a kind one—we're finished. Do you hear me? Finished. I've had enough of being managed by people who love me."

He walked out into the cold. I stayed by the fire with my untouched wine and understood, with the clarity that only arrives after you've already lost the thing, that I had just heard the first honest sentence anyone had spoken to me in twenty years—and that I had earned it by lying.

* * *

My lieutenant Brann found me an hour later, still sitting there, still holding the cup.

"He's sleeping in the map room," Brann said. "Alone. Told the sentries to keep everyone out." He hesitated, which Brann never does. "You told him, then. About the portraits."

"I told him."

"And?"

"And he's angrier at being *hoped at* than Corvane was for lying to him outright. Isn't that remarkable? Corvane stole his whole life and the boy grieves him. I gave him a destiny and he won't look at me." I finally drank the wine. It had gone bitter. Everything does, left sitting too long. "You know what Corvane understood, Brann? The thing I'm only learning now, from a lamp-warden half my age? People can survive being deceived. What they can't survive is being *useful*. Being loved for what they can do for you." I set down the cup. "Every tyrant in history made that mistake. I nearly built a kingdom on it."

Brann shifted his weight. "There's word from the south," he said. "Marrec's column moved at dusk. Heading west, toward the old monastery road."

"The Silent Choir." Of course. Marrec was clever enough to guess where a hunted letter would go—to the one place in the world sworn to keep everything and judge nothing. "Then we ride at first light."

"And the boy?"

I looked at the door Emrys had gone through, at the darkness beyond it, and I made myself say the true thing, because apparently I'd caught his honesty like a fever.

"We follow him," I said. "Not the other way around. And pray to whatever's listening that when this ends, there's still a version of him willing to speak to me."

Brann nodded slowly and left. I sat alone with the dying fire and the bitter wine and the growing, unfamiliar weight in my chest, and far to the west, past the hills, I imagined I could see it—a monastery full of silent monks, holding every truth the world had ever tried to bury, waiting for a lamp-warden's boy to come knocking.

And riding hard behind him, patient as winter, Dain Marrec—who wanted that archive burned, and who had orders, I now suspected, far worse than burning.

We had perhaps three days. Perhaps less. And for the first time since I was six years old, I was afraid not of losing the cause—

—but of what the cause was going to cost the one person in the world I had, without meaning to, started to care about.
