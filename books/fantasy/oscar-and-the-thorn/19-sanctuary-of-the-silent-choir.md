## 19. Sanctuary of the Silent Choir

The monastery appeared out of the fog like something remembered rather than seen.

We had been walking for two days since leaving Veyra's ruined chapel, and I will not pretend those days were gentle. Veyra spoke perhaps forty words total, most of them directions, all of them clipped, and I spent them replaying our argument in my head until it wore a groove. *A living heir legitimizes the cause.* She'd said it as though she were confessing to a minor accounting error. As though telling me she'd hoped I was a stolen prince was the same as telling me she'd hoped it would rain.

So when Brother Anselm—the monk who found us half-frozen on the switchback road—asked no questions and offered bread and a roof, I nearly wept into the crust.

"Welcome to Cael Scriptor," he said, pushing open a door that must have weighed more than Sela's mill. "You'll find we ask nothing of guests except that they leave their quarrels outside."

"And if they can't?" Veyra asked.

"Then they may leave their quarrels in the guesthouse," Anselm said mildly, "and collect them on the way out. We are not particular about where people keep their wars. Only that they don't bring them into the library."

I looked at her. She looked at me. For once we agreed on something without speaking: neither of us was ready to let go of ours yet, but both of us were tired enough to set them down for a night.

* * *

The Silent Choir does not take vows of silence, which surprised me. I had imagined monks who communicated by chalkboard and meaningful glances. Instead, Anselm explained over supper—a soup so plain it was almost a philosophical statement—that their vow ran the other way entirely.

"We swear to speak only what has been witnessed or recorded," he said. "Never opinion. Never judgment. If you ask a brother whether the Thorn War was just, he will tell you what both sides claimed, what the grain ledgers show, what the survivors testified—and nothing else."

"That sounds impossible," I said.

"It is extraordinarily difficult," Anselm corrected. "Which is why there are only thirty-one of us. Judgment is the easiest thing in the world. Any fool can have an opinion; I have several myself, which is precisely why I took the vow." He smiled for the first time, and it transformed his face from merely kind to genuinely mischievous. "I keep them locked in a box under my bed. On my dying day I intend to open it and say everything at once. The brothers have a betting pool on how long the resulting scandal takes to reach the capital."

Veyra snorted—the first sound resembling laughter I'd heard from her in two days—and then looked annoyed at herself for it.

I should describe the place properly, because I have never been anywhere like it, and because I think it mattered later, though none of us knew it then. Cael Scriptor stood on a shoulder of the Greywether mountains where three kingdoms met and none bothered to claim it, being poor land with bad weather. The buildings were old grey granite, squat and windowed high, arranged around a central cloister where snow fell into a courtyard garden that someone tended anyway—rosemary stubbornly green against the stone. Inside, everything smelled of ink, tallow, and cold paper. The scriptorium occupied the whole eastern wing: long tables, a hundred copying desks, and shelves—shelves beyond counting, shelves like cliff faces—holding the accumulated testimony of everything anyone had thought worth writing down.

"The war records are in the west stacks," Anselm said, catching me staring. "If you've come looking for something."

"I haven't said I'm looking for anything."

"No," he agreed placidly. "But your friend keeps touching the letter-case inside her coat the way other people touch amulets, and you flinched when I mentioned the word 'records.' Guests always think monks are blind. We're merely sworn not to comment." He refilled my bowl without asking. "Eat. Questions keep until morning. So do answers, unfortunately, but that's the nature of questions."

* * *

I slept in a cell barely wider than my shoulders, on a straw pallet, under a blanket that smelled of cedar, and for the first time since the stars went out I did not dream of falling wards or burning documents. I dreamed of Hollowmere instead—of the guildhouse kitchen, of Corvane humming off-key while he stirred porridge—and woke with my face wet and no memory of crying, which is somehow worse than the alternative.

At dawn I found the Abbot in the cloister, sweeping snow off the rosemary.

He was older than Anselm, older than any man I'd ever seen, with a beard gone the color of ash and hands that shook slightly until they touched the broom, at which point they went steady as a mason's. He didn't look up when I approached.

"You're the one carrying the letter," he said.

My hand moved toward my coat before I could stop it. Old habit. Corvane had trained the reflex into me: guard the proof, trust no one. But the letter was gone—lost at the Ashcloak raid, recovered mid-flight, read, and finally... where was it? In Veyra's keeping. Or mine. It seemed to migrate between us depending on who trusted whom less that day.

"It's not here," I said. "And it's not mine to give."

"Everything written belongs eventually to whoever will carry it honestly," the Abbot said. He finished his row of rosemary and straightened with a small grunt. "Brother Anselm says you're running from something. I suspect he's right. I also suspect you're running *toward* something and haven't admitted it yet. Which is it?"

"I don't know. I thought I did." I sat down on the cloister wall, and the honesty arrived before I could vet it—which happens to me around certain kinds of people, monks and dying men especially. "Everyone who learns what's in that letter wants to use it. The inquisitor wants it buried. Veyra wants it shouted from towers until it becomes an army. And now—" I stopped.

"Now you've learned you may be part of what's in it," the Abbot said, not unkindly.

The snow ticked down through the still air. Somewhere in the scriptorium a pen scratched, the only sound in the world.

"Yes," I said.

"My condolences. Being a fact in someone else's argument is a wretched way to live." He leaned the broom against the wall and regarded me with eyes pale as winter water. "May I offer an observation? You may decline it; I've taken no vow about observations, only about judgments."

"Please."

"You have two choices in front of you, as you see them. Give the truth to the crown, which will bury it. Give it to the rebellion, which will spend it like coin, buying soldiers with dead men's grievances. Both paths end with the truth owned by power. And power," he said, "has never once in recorded history—which we would know, we wrote most of it—used a truth gently when it could use it loudly."

"So what's the third path?" My voice came out smaller than I intended. "There isn't one. That's what everyone keeps telling me. Pick a side or get trampled."

"There is always a third path. It's simply the one nobody guards, because it has no army on it." He gestured toward the east wing, the shelves beyond the windows, the hundred quiet desks. "Give it to the archive."

"To *shelves*."

"To memory. To record. Not a weapon and not a secret—a *fact*. Read aloud, copied a thousand times, held by no one and therefore unstealable. Do you know what happens to a truth when it enters an archive properly? Nothing. That's the miracle of it. Nothing happens immediately. No kingdom falls, no army marches. It sits on a shelf, patient as water, and waits for the world to be ready. And the world is always eventually ready, because the world cannot help becoming ready—it's the only thing the world does." His shaking hands folded together and went still. "Crowns move fast and die fast. Archives move slowly and never die. Choose your eternity, young man."

"But it doesn't *fix* anything," I said, and heard the hunger in my own voice, the wish for some act that would make the graves unburied and the sky stop bleeding wrongness. "People died. People were stolen. Me, possibly. Saying it into a book doesn't avenge anyone."

"No," the Abbot agreed. "It doesn't. Justice is not vengeance wearing better clothes, though most people mistake one for the other daily. What you want—what Veyra wants, what Marrec wants, what every soul in this story wants—is for the past to have meant something. And the past means something only if it's *known*. Everything else is theater." He picked up the broom again. "Think on it. We serve breakfast at prime. The porridge is terrible, which the brothers consider a discipline."

* * *

I thought on it. That was the trouble with monasteries—they gave you nothing to do but think, and I was dangerously full of thinking.

Veyra spent the day in the west stacks. I watched her through the archway, pulling war ledgers with hands that trembled—not with fear, I realized, but with recognition. These were her people's deaths, catalogued in a clerk's indifferent hand. She read the way starving people eat. Once she caught me watching and her expression dared me to pity her. I looked away first. Cowardice, maybe. Or courtesy. With Veyra the two had started to look alike.

In the afternoon I sat in the scriptorium with Brother Anselm and learned that my warden's training was good for something after all. Copying, it turned out, was warding-adjacent: precision, patience, reverence for small marks that carried large meanings. My letters came out clean. Anselm pronounced me "tolerably mediocre, which is high praise after a week."

"Why do you do it?" I asked him, dipping the pen. "Copy things no one reads?"

"Oh, people read everything eventually. We've had scholars dig out grain tallies from four centuries back to settle a boundary dispute. A queen once requested a recipe." He blew ink dry. "But that's not why. We copy because writing something down is an act of faith that the future exists. Every scribe is saying to someone unborn: *this happened, it mattered, you should know.* Can you imagine a better prayer?"

I couldn't. I said so.

"Then you understand the third path better than you admit," Anselm said, and passed me the next page.

It was near vespers, sitting at that desk with my wrist aching and the light going amber through the high windows, that the idea finally settled into me—not as a plan but as a weight finding its balance. The truth didn't need me to be its herald or its corpse. It needed me to be its *witness*. There was a difference, and the difference was everything. Veyra wanted to wield the truth. Marrec wanted to erase it. But wielded or erased, it stayed theirs. Given freely to the record—to everyone and no one—it became something no faction could own, no army could capture, no lie could starve.

I didn't tell anyone yet. Some decisions need to sit overnight like bread dough, and I had learned, at last, to let things rise.

* * *

The hook came the next morning, as hooks do—instantly and from an unwelcome direction.

I was carrying two cups of the disciplinary porridge toward the west stacks to make peace overtures to Veyra when I heard voices below the archive stair—one of them unmistakably hers, sharp with alarm, the other low and unfamiliar. I pressed myself against the cold stone and listened.

"—entered the valley registry last month under a merchant's seal. Someone has been asking after Winterbourne correspondence." A pause. "Sister, the man asking is royal grey. He signed the guest ledger at Thornford as *Dain Marrec*, and he is nine miles away as the crow flies, and crows fly faster than you'd like."

The porridge went cold in my hands. Nine miles.

Above me, faint through the high windows, the bells of Cael Scriptor began to ring for morning office—thirty-one voices' worth of silence beneath them, ten thousand truths asleep on their shelves, and somewhere down the mountain road, unhurried as winter, the man who knew exactly what I was coming to collect.
