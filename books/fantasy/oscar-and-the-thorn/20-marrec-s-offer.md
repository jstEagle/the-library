## 20. Marrec's Offer

I had been a royal inquisitor for nineteen years, and in that time I had learned three truths that kept me alive: never trust a quiet village, never trust a cheerful monk, and never—under any circumstances—trust a man who says he wants nothing.

The Abbot of the Silent Choir said he wanted nothing. He said it while feeding me honeyed bread in a scriptorium that smelled of ink and tallow and the particular patience of men who copy other people's words for a living. He said it with the serene confidence of someone whose order had survived six wars by the simple expedient of refusing to have opinions.

"You are welcome here, Inquisitor," he told me. "All guests are welcome."

"I'm not a guest. I'm a hunter."

"Yes," he agreed pleasantly. "You are welcome too."

I did not like him at all. I liked him slightly more than I liked my own superiors, which is not a compliment, but it is a distinction.

* * *

The Silent Choir sat in a fold of hills like an egg in a napkin—a squat monastery of grey stone, ringed by orchards gone wild, its bell tower capped not with a cross or a crown but with an open hand carved from weathered oak. The monks took no vows of poverty or chastity. They took a vow of record. They wrote down everything they witnessed and judged none of it, which made them either the holiest men in the kingdom or the most dangerous, depending on how much you feared the truth.

My superiors feared it very much indeed.

Which was why, three days after Emrys Winterbourne and Veyra Thorn slipped into that fold of hills, I followed them in—and why, instead of the forty soldiers I would have preferred, I came with two men who owed me money and a wagon of wine bought on the crown's account.

"Couldn't you just burn the place down?" asked Corporal Hesk, my larger shadow, as we walked the last mile. He was a simple soul, Hesk. I valued him for it.

"The archive is fireproofed, warded by people better at wards than we are, and full of documents the crown spent four hundred years pretending don't exist. Burning it would be like trying to drown a fish."

"We could shoot the fish," Hesk offered.

"That's the spirit. Wait outside."

The truth was I didn't want soldiers. Soldiers were for battles, and this wasn't going to be a battle. This was going to be a conversation—the kind where you lay every card face-up on the table and let the other man realize he's been losing since before he sat down.

Or so I told myself, climbing the monastery steps, past the open-handed tower, through doors older than the kingdom I served.

* * *

They knew I was coming before I reached the refectory. Of course they did. The Choir's whole business was knowing things; their hospitality was simply the courtesy extended to facts. A young brother met me at the door, looked at my warrant, looked at my face, and said, "Brother Aldous will see you refreshed. The others are waiting in the east cloister. They asked me to tell you that no one will attempt to leave."

"They asked you to tell me that?"

"The tall one with the scarred hands," the brother confirmed. "He thought it might save us all some shouting."

Veyra Thorn, inviting me in. That alone should have frightened me more than it did. But I was tired—I'd been riding moorland roads for eleven days, eating hard cheese and softer lies—and there is a particular exhaustion that comes from chasing people across a country that is quietly coming apart at the seams. Wells that whisper. Children born with light behind their eyes. Farmers in three provinces who had stopped paying taxes because, they said, the sky had stopped being trustworthy, and if the sky couldn't be trusted then surely the crown's claim on their grain was void.

So I accepted the bread, and the watered wine, and the escort to the east cloister, where the afternoon light fell gold and forgiving across stones that had heard every kind of confession except, perhaps, mine.

Emrys Winterbourne stood up when I entered. That was the first surprise. He stood up the way a boy stands when his schoolmaster enters—guiltily, reflexively—and then caught himself and made it into something harder. The scarred woman beside him did not stand at all. She watched me the way a cat watches a dog that has learned where she sleeps.

"Inquisitor Marrec," Emrys said. His voice had changed since the guildhouse. There was iron under it now, badly forged, still cooling. "You found us."

"You left a trail a blind man could follow. You paid a ferryman in crown silver. You argued loudly with your companion about whether moorland or river valley was safer—which, incidentally, it was neither, you should have taken the coast road. And you stopped to help a family whose cart axle broke, which was noble and stupid and cost you half a day." I spread my hands. "I've hunted smugglers with better tradecraft. Your guild trained you to keep lamps lit, Master Winterbourne, not to run."

"He's right," the scarred woman said. It took me a moment to place her—Sela, the miller's daughter from Hollowmere, the traitor whose betrayal had cost the Ashcloaks their camp and cost Winterbourne, I suspected, considerably more than that. Interesting company he was keeping now. Grief makes strange bedfellows, and guilt makes stranger ones. "It was stupid. All of it."

"Thank you, Sela," Emrys said, in a voice with a great deal of history packed tight inside it.

"And the Lady Thorn?" I asked. "Hiding?"

"She's praying," Emrys said.

"In a monastery."

"She says she wants to know what the monks have about the war. She says—" he hesitated, and I watched him decide whether to lie to me, and watched him choose not to, which was either honesty or strategy and with that boy it had become genuinely difficult to tell— "she says an archive that records without judging might be useful to her. If she can get her hands on what it holds."

"And what do you want, Master Winterbourne?"

He didn't answer right away. Outside the cloister arches, a monk was teaching two novices to grind pigments, and the soft rhythmic scrape of stone on stone filled the silence like breathing.

"I want to read all of it," he finally said. "Every page. Before anyone burns anything or broadcasts anything or bleeds anything. I want to know what's true. Not what's useful. What's true."

Ah. There it was. The thing about Emrys Winterbourne—the thing that had unsettled me from our first interview in Hollowmere, when he lied to me so badly I could have taught a seminar on it—was that underneath the fear and the flailing and the catastrophic lack of fieldcraft, he actually meant things. He meant wanting the truth. He probably even believed he could survive having it.

I had been sent to disabuse him of that belief.

* * *

Here is what my orders said, in the elegant cipher of the Chancery's second desk: *Recovery of the document designated WINTERBORN-1 is no longer the primary objective. The individual Emrys Winterbourne, formerly of Hollowmere, is designated a threat to the continuity of the realm. Termination authorized. Discretion paramount.*

Termination. Discretion. They dressed murder in clerical language because plain words might embarrass someone at dinner.

I had carried those orders for six days. I had read them perhaps forty times. And each reading, something in me had shifted a degree, the way a foundation shifts—not enough to crack the house, just enough that the doors no longer quite close.

Because here was the arithmetic I could not stop doing. When the stars went out, sixty years of peace went with them—or so the court believed. The court believed the valley's wards had anchored something, and that the anchor had failed, and that if the wrong people learned *why* it had failed—if they learned what House Winterbourne's founding warden had done, whom he had stolen, what grave the whole gentle valley of Hollowmere had been built over—then two kingdoms that had spent three generations perfecting the art of not killing each other would remember, all at once, exactly how good they'd been at it.

The court's answer was a body in a shallow hole and a secret kept one funeral longer.

And I had spent nineteen years keeping secrets for that court, and I could count on one hand the ones that had ever purchased peace, and I could not count at all the ones that had merely postponed the bill. Lies are credit, gentlemen. Someone always collects.

But I am getting ahead of myself. In the cloister that afternoon, I hadn't yet decided anything. I only knew I wasn't going to do it the way the second desk wanted it done. So I sent Hesk and the other one to water the horses, and I sat down across from Emrys Winterbourne and his traitor, and I did something no one at court would have understood.

I told the truth.

* * *

"Do you know what happens if I report your location?" I asked.

"Soldiers come," Emrys said.

"Hundreds. Within the week. The Ashcloak remnant dies in the orchard, the monks die defending their books, and you—" I leaned forward. "You don't die, Master Winterbourne. Not quickly, and not publicly. You disappear into a cellar in the capital and stay there until the court decides what shape the story takes. Maybe you're a rebel martyr. Maybe you never existed. Maybe you're blamed for the stars. The court is very flexible about dead men."

Sela's hand had gone to the knife at her belt. I ignored it. A knife against me in a choir's cloister would buy her a shallow grave beside her companions, and I think she knew it, and I think that's why her hand shook.

"But," I continued, "that's what happens if I report. Here's what happens if I don't." I took a folded paper from my breast pocket and set it on the table between us. Emrys looked at it without touching it, which showed more sense than he'd shown on the ferry.

"What is it?"

"A writ of amnesty. Signed by the Lord Chancellor's own clerk, sealed with the second desk's stamp. It names Emrys Winterbourne and one companion"—I nodded at Sela, whose eyes went wide and wet in the same instant, which told me rather a lot about what she'd traded and what she feared she'd lost— "as witnesses under crown protection. It expunges all charges. It grants passage home."

"To Hollowmere?" Emrys whispered.

"To Hollowmere. To the guildhouse. To your lamps, boy. To the life you had before a dying old man put a letter in your hands and ruined you."

"It's not that simple," Emrys said. "The letter—"

"Give me the letter."

Silence. Somewhere in the orchard a bird made an obscene comment about the state of the world.

"The original is gone," Emrys said carefully.

"I know. Veyra Thorn's people copied it before the raid—I recovered a partial transcription from a dead courier. I also know the Silent Choir holds documents concerning the Thorn War that make your letter look like a market list. So let us not insult each other with amateur theater." I folded my hands. "Here is my offer, and I will say it once, plainly, because you've had enough lies to fill a cathedral.

"Come back with me. Both of you. The crown buries everything—the theft, the graves, the letter, the name Thorn. The valley stays sealed, or it doesn't; that part I can negotiate. The Ashcloaks scatter with pardons. The Choir keeps its archive because nobody remembers to look for it. And in exchange, you go back to being nobody. An orphan apprentice of a dying guild in a valley that smells of lamp oil. No inheritance. No war. No casus belli. Two kingdoms sleep on, ignorant and therefore peaceful, and you grow old mending things that hold small amounts of nothing."

I sat back. "That's it. That's the whole offer. Amnesty for silence."

* * *

I expected anger. I expected Veyra's rhetoric to come sweeping back into the room like weather, all banners and blood debt. Instead Emrys Winterbourne just looked at me for a long moment with an expression I recognized, because I wore it myself, privately, roughly once a week.

He looked tempted.

"Why?" he said. "Why would you offer this? Your orders are to kill me. Don't bother looking surprised—Veyra intercepted a courier two days ago. We know what the second desk decided. So why is Dain Marrec sitting here offering me a pardon instead of putting a knife in me?"

"Perhaps I'm bad at my job."

"Perhaps. Or perhaps you believe it. Really believe it—that peace needs the lie. That people can't survive the truth." He tilted his head. "Do you? Believe it?"

And there it was, laid out on the table next to the amnesty writ: the question I had been avoiding for six days and nineteen years.

"I grew up in Cindermarch," I heard myself say. "River district. I was nine when I saw what the last border skirmish left of the town of Aldenford. I stepped over the dead for a mile. Some of them were boys my age. One of them was holding a flute." I looked at my hands—clean hands, well-kept, hands that had signed death warrants written in other men's ink. "Sixty years of quiet, Winterbourne. Sixty years of children who learned farming instead of pike drills. You want to call that peace a lie? Fine. It's a lie. It's also the longest stretch of not-burying-children this land has known, and it was bought with exactly one stolen infant and one sealed valley, and I have searched my conscience thoroughly and found that I cannot summon the outrage to un-buy it. Truth is a luxury of people who aren't standing on a fault line. Somebody has to hold the lid down. I volunteered. That's the whole of my philosophy. It isn't noble. It's load-bearing."

Sela made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled at birth.

"And you'd ask me to be part of the lid," Emrys said quietly.

"I'm asking you to be a man, not a symbol. Symbols get people killed, and the people they get killed are rarely the symbols." I tapped the writ once. "Veyra Thorn needs you to be the heir. The court needs you to be a corpse. I'm offering you the only thing anyone in this entire miserable affair has offered you: permission to be a person. Take it."

For a moment—one long, golden, cloister-haunted moment—he considered it. I saw it happen. I saw him look inward at some image of the guildhouse, the smell of lamp oil, the ordinary blessed smallness of a life where your largest decision is which wick to trim. I had built my career on reading faces and I am telling you, whatever you think of me afterward, that in that moment Emrys Winterbourne wanted to say yes with his whole heart.

Then his gaze drifted to the cloister arches, where the monks ground their pigments and recorded their unjudged histories, and something in his face closed like a door.

"My mother sang lullabies in Old Thorn dialect," he said. "In a valley where that language supposedly died out sixty years ago. Corvane hummed along. Every night, for fifteen years, the two people raising me were singing a dead language over my cradle, and I'm supposed to sign a paper that makes that mean nothing?"

"The alternative means a war."

"Maybe." He picked up the writ—actually picked it up, examined the seal, turned it over in his scarred lamp-tender's hands. "Or maybe the war comes anyway, because lies rot, Marrec. Maybe you haven't been holding the lid down. Maybe you've been sitting on a kettle." He set the writ down gently, almost kindly, which was worse than throwing it back. "I need to read everything. All of it. Then I'll decide what I owe the dead—and I notice you didn't offer anything to them."

"The dead," I said, "have never once accepted my offers."

"No," he agreed. "Mine either."

* * *

I rose to leave, because staying longer would have been surrender, and because dusk was coming and dusk in a place like that does something to a man's certainties. At the cloister arch I paused.

"For what it's worth," I said, not turning around, "the offer stands. I'm the only man in this kingdom for whom it stands. When the second desk loses patience with me—and they will, I'm slower every year—you'll wish you'd taken it."

"Marrec." His voice stopped me. "Why did you really come yourself? With two men and a wagon of wine. You could have brought the hundreds."

I considered several answers. The true one arrived before the diplomatic ones, as true answers are rude that way.

"Because I wanted to look at you," I said. "Before. Whatever happens. I've spent my life carrying out decisions made by men who never look at anything. I wanted to look at what we're burying this time." I glanced back at him—at the thin frame, the tired eyes, the terrible weight settling onto shoulders built for lamp oil. "You look like him, you know. The portraits of the old Thorns. Around the eyes. Whoever painted them flattered everyone else in the room and got him honest by accident."

I walked out into the falling dark, and behind me I heard Sela say, very softly, "He's right, you know. About the eyes."

* * *

I did not leave that night. I claimed the guest cell the Choir offered all comers—even hunters, apparently, especially hunters, the monks' theology being that a man asleep is at least temporarily harmless—and I lay awake listening to the monastery breathe.

Toward midnight I heard footsteps in the cloister and, out of habit older than my conscience, I went to the window.

Two figures stood in the moonlight below. One was broad and bent and moved like a man carrying something heavy in his chest. Grey traveling cloak, hood back, white hair cropped short. I knew that walk. I had seen it crossing a courtyard in the capital twenty years ago, younger and straighter, when the Wardens' Guildmaster Corvane had come to court to swear the annual oath of the valley's sealing and had looked at the Chancellor the way a man looks at a debt he intends to collect.

The other figure was Emrys Winterbourne.

Corvane was saying something. I was too far off to hear it—but I saw Emrys reach out and grip the old man's arm, saw the old man sway as if the touch weighed more than armor, saw the two of them stand together under the open-handed tower while the returned-and-wrong stars wheeled overhead like a ceiling coming loose.

Corvane was supposed to be dead. My reports said dead. My orders assumed dead. The court had already begun writing his eulogy into the official record of the affair.

And he had walked into the one monastery in the world holding the full account of his crime, straight to the boy whose life he had stolen and saved and spent, and whatever he was saying to Emrys in the dark, the boy was listening the way you listen when the truth has finally caught up with you and there is nowhere left to run.

I stayed at the window a long time. Then, very quietly, I began doing sums again—the inquisitor's oldest prayer—and for the first time in nineteen years, I did not like any of the totals.

By morning, I suspected, everything would depend on what Corvane Winterbourne had come to confess.

And on whether I reported it.
