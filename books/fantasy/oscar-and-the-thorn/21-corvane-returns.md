## 21. Corvane Returns

The monastery of the Silent Choir kept no clocks, which I decided was either profound wisdom or a very convenient way to avoid accountability. Either way, I'd been awake since before dawn, sitting in the scriptorium with a candle I wasn't supposed to need, doing arithmetic I didn't want to do.

Heirs did not, as a rule, grow up mending ward-lamps with beeswax and stubbornness. Heirs did not learn to gut a fish or lose at cards to a miller's daughter. Somewhere in sixty years of careful lies, someone had made me ordinary on purpose, and the cruelest part was how well it had worked. I had believed in my own smallness the way you believe in weather. It was simply there. You dressed for it. You built your life under it.

"You're doing the face again," Sela said from the doorway.

"What face?"

"The one where you look like you're trying to solve yourself like a cipher and the cipher is winning."

"I'm fine."

"You're never fine. You're either quietly fine or loudly not, and that face is neither." She came in and sat across from me, stealing my candle's warmth without apology. In the grey light she looked tired in a way sleep wouldn't fix. She'd told me most of it by now—the bargain with Marrec's agents, the letters home she'd written under their eyes, the way fear had worn her down grain by grain until betrayal seemed like just another chore. I hadn't forgiven her. I don't think I'd even started. But she was here, and some mornings that had to be enough, because the alternative was being alone with the arithmetic.

"The Abbot says the Choir will copy the letter," she said. "Three copies. One stays here, one goes to the tribunal elders when they convene, and one—" she hesitated, "—one Veyra wants."

"Veyra wants everything. That's rather her whole personality."

"Emrys."

"I know." I rubbed my eyes. "I know. She believes in what she's doing. That's what makes it dangerous. A cynic you can buy. A believer you can only survive."

Outside, the monastery bells rang the hour—not for time, exactly, but for attention. The Silent Choir rang them whenever they wanted the world to pause and be noticed. I'd learned that much in three days. The monks wrote history without judging it, which sounded simple until you realized it meant they wrote *everything*: the crown's massacres and the rebels' reprisals, side by side, in the same careful hand, with no verdict rendered anywhere. The Abbot had shown me a shelf of volumes on the Thorn War. Forty of them. Not one line of moralizing in forty volumes. Just names, dates, deeds. It was somehow more damning than any sermon could have been.

We were still sitting there when the bells changed.

Not the hour-chime. Something faster, arrhythmic, urgent—a pattern I hadn't heard before but understood anyway, the way you understand a scream. The monks in the cloister stopped copying. Heads lifted. And then Brother Anselm was in the doorway, breathless in a way that men who take vows of measured speech should never be.

"There's a man at the gate," he said. "He's asking for Emrys Winterbourne. He says—" Anselm paused, and something in his face made my stomach drop through the floor. "He says he's dying, and he hasn't much time, and he'll say his piece to no one else."

I was moving before I finished understanding. Down the stairs, across the courtyard, past the herb garden where a monk was calmly weeding as though the world weren't tilting. Sela fell into step beside me, saying nothing, which was its own kind of loyalty.

And there, propped against the gatepost with two monks supporting him, was Guildmaster Corvane.

* * *

He looked terrible.

I want to be precise about this, because precision is all I have left. Corvane had always seemed carved rather than born—grey stone given a beard and a bad temper and an unreasonable talent for lamplight. Even old, even after the wards fell, he'd had the density of a thing meant to last. Now he looked hollowed. His coat was stiff with dried blood that wasn't all his own. There was a wound in his side wrapped in field dressings gone brown and wrong, and another along his arm, and his skin had the grey-yellow pallor of a lamp running out of oil. His hands, those hands that had taught me every knot and seal and prayer of the Wardens' Guild, trembled against the gatepost.

But his eyes were the same. Sharp, dark, and unbearably tired.

"Guildmaster," I said, and my voice cracked on the second syllable, which was embarrassing, so I covered it with anger, which was easier. "You're supposed to be dead. Or gone. Or—something. Anything other than bleeding on a monastery gate."

"Good morning to you too." His voice was gravel dragged over gravel. "Still talking back. Good. Means you haven't changed." He tried to smile and got about halfway there. "Help me sit down properly. If I'm going to die today I refuse to do it hanging off a fence like a scarecrow."

The monks moved him to a bench inside the gatehouse, and I knelt in front of him and did what I could with the dressings, which was not much. The wound in his side was deep. I'd seen enough blood in the last weeks to know what deep meant. Behind me, Sela had gone white, and the Abbot stood at a respectful distance with his hands folded, watching with those patient archival eyes.

"How did you find us?" I asked.

"I didn't. I found Marrec's trail, and Marrec found yours, and I've been following both of you for nine days like a dog after a cart." He coughed, wet and long, and I hated every second of it. "Your friend the inquisitor is half a day behind me. Maybe less. He's stopped pretending to chase. Now he's herding."

"Then why come at all? Why not just die quietly somewhere and spare everyone the drama?"

"Because I owe you a deathbed confession, boy, and I'm old-fashioned enough to believe debts get paid." He caught my wrist. His grip was still strong; whatever else was failing him, that wasn't. "And because I needed to see your face when I tell you the truth, so I'll know whether I wasted forty years."

* * *

They gave us the guest cell, because Corvane refused the infirmary—"hospitals are where people go to be managed, and I'm done being managed"—and because the Abbot, with his infuriating gentleness, understood that some conversations require walls. Sela brought water and clean linen and then, at Corvane's flat request, left us alone. I think she was glad to go. Some griefs you can't watch without feeling complicit.

Corvane lay on the narrow bed looking smaller than I'd ever seen him, and I sat on the floor beside him like an apprentice at a lesson, because apparently even now my body remembered its place.

"Start with the fire," I said. "The night the stars went out. You burned papers. You weren't surprised. Tell me why."

He was quiet a while. When he spoke, his voice had changed—lower, slower, the voice of a man setting down something he'd carried so long it had grown into his spine.

"You've read the letter. You know about the theft. The heir of House Thorn, taken in the last winter of the war, carried into Hollowmere, and the valley sealed around him like a wound closed over a splinter." He looked at the ceiling. "What the letter doesn't say—what I burned—is what happened after. The founding Warden didn't just steal a child. He built a guild to guard the lie. Sixty years of Wardens, each one taught the truth and sworn to it, each one passing the burden down. My master. His master before him. All the way back."

"And you were the last one carrying it."

"I was the last one willing to carry it, which is different." His mouth twisted. "The others wanted out. Wanted to tell the crowns, or sell the secret, or just stop. I held the line. Because I'd seen what happens when that truth gets loose, Emrys. I fought in the border skirmishes before the sealing. I saw villages burn over less than what's in that letter. The valley's peace wasn't built on the lie—it was *maintained* by it. Every quiet morning in Hollowmere, every festival, every child who grew up never knowing war—that was bought with one stolen life. And I judged the price fair."

"That's monstrous."

"Yes." No flinch. No excuse. "It is. I've had sixty years to make peace with being monstrous, and I've failed at it every single day. That's why I taught you lamps, boy. Why I taught you knots and patience and how to mend things instead of break them. Because I couldn't undo what was done, but I thought—I *hoped*—if I raised the stolen heir myself, quietly, kindly, as an ordinary boy who knew how to fix broken things..." His voice frayed. "Maybe he'd grow up to find an answer better than mine. Better than the crown's. Better than the rebels'. Someone who'd learned smallness first might carry weight differently than someone who'd learned thrones."

I stared at him. The candle guttered. Somewhere outside, a bell rang, patient and nonjudgmental.

"You chose me," I said. "Not just—you didn't just hide me. You *chose* me. To raise."

"I asked for the assignment when you were two years old. My master thought it was sentiment. It wasn't. It was the only plan I had left." He turned his head to look at me, and his eyes were wet, which I had never once seen in twenty years, and which undid me more thoroughly than any blade could have. "I couldn't give you back your name. Names start wars. So I gave you a trade instead. A craft. A self you built with your own hands. And I prayed—me, who doesn't pray—that it would be enough when the sky finally came apart."

"The wards," I whispered. "They were anchored to me. That's why they fell when—"

"When you crossed the border stone? No. Before that. They were failing for years, because an anchor that grows up and starts *wanting things* is a poor anchor. Your restlessness, boy. Your staring at the pass. Every night you spent wishing at the horizon, the wards thinned a little more. I watched it happen and I let it, because part of me wanted you to break free, and part of me knew that if you ever did, everything would follow." He laughed, which cost him visibly. "So I sabotaged nothing and delayed everything and lied to you daily, and called it duty, and here we are."

"Why tell me now?"

"Because you're about to make a choice, and I won't have you make it thinking I was your jailer." He gripped my hand again, hard. "I was your defender. Poorly. Late. But that's what I was. And whatever you decide—give the truth to Veyra's army, or Marrec's crown, or the Choir's archive—I need you to hear this: there is no version of this where you come out clean. There's only versions where you come out *true*. Pick true. It costs more and it's worth it."

* * *

He faded after that, in and out, and I sat with him through the worst of it while the monks did what monks do—quietly, competently, without hope of changing anything. Toward evening he surfaced one more time and beckoned me close.

"Marrec," he rasped. "Half a day. Less. He has orders you don't know about. I took them off a courier I killed." His hand found mine. "They're not retrieval orders anymore, boy. They're erasure orders. The crown's decided a living heir is a lit fuse. Understand?" His fingers tightened. "Whatever else happens—don't trust the offer he makes you. He may even believe it himself. Men like Marrec always believe their own lies right up until the moment they're ordered to break them."

"He offered me amnesty already."

"And you believed him?"

"I wanted to."

"That's worse." He almost smiled. "Listen. The Choir. The archive. The Abbot showed you the shelves?" I nodded. "That's the answer I hoped you'd find. Truth that belongs to no army. Give it to them. Let every faction read the same page at the same moment, and let none of them hold the pen." His breath rattled. "Promise me."

"I promise."

"Good lad." His eyes drifted toward the window, toward the west, toward a valley he'd defended for sixty years and would never see again. "Tell Sela... tell her the miller's daughter deserved better teachers than the world gave her. And tell her—" he paused, gathering strength for it, "—tell her I'm sorry I never learned her name properly. I knew it the whole time. I just... liked pretending the guild was smaller than it was."

I bent over him. "Rest," I said. "I'll be here."

"No, you won't." His voice was fading now, soft as lamplight burning down. "You'll be doing something brave and stupid and necessary. That's what I raised you for." His hand went slack in mine. "Emrys. Oscar."

My breath stopped.

"Your mother used to call you that," he murmured. "In the Old Thorn tongue. *Oscar.* Little bear-cub. I heard her sing it to you the night we carried you over the stones. First lullaby I ever heard in that dialect, and I never forgot it, and I never told you, because telling you would have been the end of everything." His eyes were closing. "I kept it sixty years. Like a lamp. Waiting for someone to light it."

And then, between one breath and the next, the lamp went out.

* * *

The monks buried him at dawn in their archive-yard, beneath a plain stone with no epitaph, because the Silent Choir holds that a life fully recorded needs no summary. The Abbot let me choose the spot—under the old rowan, where the roots had cracked the flagstones and moss grew green over everything. I dug with my own hands. It seemed important. He'd taught me that work was a form of prayer for people who couldn't manage the real thing.

Sela stood beside me the whole time, holding the shroud's edge, crying silently in the way of people who learned young that tears were a luxury. When the earth was filled, she touched the mound once, lightly, like a benediction.

"He knew my name," she said.

"He knew everything," I said. "That was his whole tragedy."

Veyra appeared at the yard's edge during the burial and, to her credit, waited until it was done before speaking. She wore mourning grey, which I suspected was tactical, but her face when she looked at the grave was genuine, and complicated, and human.

"He was the last Warden," she said. "The man who guarded the prison of my house for sixty years. I should hate him." She paused. "I find I can't. Is that weakness?"

"It's honesty," I said. "Try it. You might like it."

She gave me a look that promised we'd fight about that later, and then her expression sharpened into something urgent. "There's a rider on the moor road. Grey cloak. Alone. He's been sitting his horse at the tree line for an hour, just watching the monastery. Not approaching. Waiting."

My blood went cold. Half a day, Corvane had said. The courier's orders. Erasure.

"That'll be Marrec," I said.

"Shall I set the Ashcloaks on him?"

"No." I wiped the grave-dirt from my hands and stood, looking out past the wall to where a lone figure sat motionless against the brightening sky. "No armies. Not yet. He came alone, and he's waiting alone, and Corvane said—" I stopped. Swallowed. "Corvane said his orders had changed. That he's not here to retrieve me anymore."

"Then what is he here for?"

I picked up my cloak. Somewhere overhead, the misaligned stars were fading into a dawn that felt borrowed, provisional, like the whole sky was holding its breath.

"I suppose," I said, "we're about to find out."
