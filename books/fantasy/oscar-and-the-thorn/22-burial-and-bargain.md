## 22. Burial and Bargain

The ground on the moor was too hard to dig with a spade, so I dug it with magic, which felt like a betrayal of everything Corvane had ever taught me.

He'd spent sixty years—well, he'd spent *my* lifetime, which amounted to the same thing from where I stood—teaching me that power was a lamp you kept trimmed and covered. That the measure of a warden was what he declined to do. And here I was at dawn, standing over a grave I'd torn out of frozen earth with a word, my hands shaking, my breath steaming, feeling like a man who'd picked his mentor's lock to bury him properly.

He'd have understood. That was the worst part. He'd have understood completely, and he'd have said something dry about it, like "Efficient," in that tone he used when he meant "disappointing but forgivable."

Veyra Thorn stood ten paces off with her arms crossed, watching me work, and she had not said one word in an hour. For Veyra this was roughly equivalent to a religious observance.

I'd wrapped him in his own cloak—the grey one, the one he wore when he wanted villagers to think he was nobody—and I'd put the letter's ashes in his hands before I closed it. Not the letter itself; Marrec had that now, or thought he did, since what we'd recovered mid-flight had been the real one and what Marrec carried away from the burning camp was a decoy Maren had prepared with more cunning than any hedge-witch has a right to. But there had been other papers on Corvane's body. A guild ledger. A page of ward-diagrams in his own cramped hand. Things that were his, that should go into the ground with him rather than into anyone's archive or anyone's argument.

"You're doing it wrong," Veyra said finally.

"I'm doing it the way wardens do it." I tamped the earth down with the flat of the shovel. "North-south alignment. Lamp-stone at the head. He taught me the rite when I was nine and I got the words wrong and he made me do it eleven times."

"Eleven times."

"He said twelve was unlucky and ten was lazy."

Something moved across her face—not softness exactly, but the recognition of softness, the way you flinch from a light you've lived without for years. She came closer and looked down at the mound of turned earth, at the lamp-stone I'd set at its head, unlit because I didn't have a lit lamp to give him and wouldn't take one from her people even if they'd offered.

"He burned your valley's documents," she said. "He built your prison. He stole—" She stopped herself, which was new for her.

"He raised me," I said. "Both things are true. I've stopped trying to make them cancel out."

"That's a coward's arithmetic."

"It's an honest one. You should try it sometime."

She let that stand. With Veyra, letting something stand was its own kind of concession, and I was learning to read her the way you learn to read weather—by what doesn't happen.

We buried him as the sun came up over the moorland, red and low, and I spoke the warden's rite with all the words in the right order this time, and if my voice cracked on the third line, well, the wind was up, and no one who mattered was going to mention it.

* * *

The Ashcloaks made camp a mile east, in the hollow of a dead riverbed, and by midmorning I was sitting across a folding table from Veyra Thorn while her lieutenants arranged themselves behind her like a jury that had already voted.

I want to say something about that table first. It was a campaign table, scarred and wine-ringed, and someone had drawn a map of the borderlands on it in charcoal and then half-erased it, so that the whole war we were all pretending hadn't started yet existed only as smudges. I found that fitting.

"You want something from me," I said.

"I want justice for House Thorn."

"You want a casus belli. Justice is what you're calling it so your soldiers can sleep."

One of the lieutenants—a big man with a burn-scarred jaw whose name I'd learned was Hask—shifted forward, and Veyra lifted two fingers without looking at him, and he settled back. I was beginning to understand that those two fingers were the most powerful object in the rebel order.

"I watched you bury your keeper this morning," she said. "You gave him a warden's rite. The man who helped forge the peace that erased my house."

"Yes."

"Why?"

"Because he died covering my escape, and because grief isn't a reward you give people for being good. It's just what happens when they stop."

Hask made a sound like a boot in mud. Veyra ignored him.

"You've read the letter," she went on. "You know what you are. The last heir of Thorn. The living proof that the crown's peace is a theft wearing a festival costume. Every village on this road will open its doors to you. Every survivor of the war will kneel. You could end a sixty-year lie with your face."

"And start a sixty-year war with the same face. I've done the arithmetic you don't like."

"The crown murdered the wards, Emrys. They'll murder again. Better we choose the battlefield."

"Better nobody chooses a battlefield." I leaned forward. "Here's what I'll give you, and then I'll tell you what I won't, and then we can all stop performing."

Her eyes narrowed. Veyra Thorn did not like being managed, which was ironic, given how much of her life she'd spent managing everyone else.

"I'll stand as heir," I said. "Publicly. Once. In front of witnesses. I'll say what the letter says, that the peace was forged on a stolen child, that House Thorn was robbed, that the valley was a grave with a fence around it. I'll say it plainly and I won't retract it."

Hask smiled. It was not a nice smile.

"But," I continued, "not before your army. Not on a stage you build, with banners you've sewn, surrounded by men with burn scars who've been rehearsing their outrage for a decade. If I'm going to be the proof, then the proof gets heard by everyone at once—including the people it indicts. A council of survivors. From every side. War-widows, deserters, border-folk, guild remnants, crown subjects, Thorn loyalists. Everyone who lost something. They hear it together or I don't say it at all."

Silence. The kind of silence that has weight and temperature.

"You want a tribunal," Veyra said slowly.

"I want the truth to be *expensive for everybody*. That's the only way it survives. If it costs only the crown, the crown buries it. If it costs only you, you spend it on banners. It has to cost all of us, or it's just ammunition."

"And if the council refuses? If they decide the lie is load-bearing?" She gestured at the sky through the tent flap—at the wrong-colored stars still faintly visible in the daylight, misaligned, trembling. "If they decide you're easier dead than declared?"

"Then I'll have learned something important about institutions," I said. "And I'll try something else. But it'll be *my* something else. That's the part you keep missing, Veyra. Everyone keeps missing it. Corvane chose me hoping I'd find a better answer than either side offered. I'm not going to hand his hope to your quartermaster."

She studied me for a long time. Behind her, Hask whispered to another lieutenant, and I caught the word "asset," and decided not to be flattered.

"You understand," Veyra said at last, "that once you stand up publicly, you can never sit back down. There's no version of this where you go back to tending lamps."

"I know."

"They'll come for you. The crown, the orders, the opportunists, the true believers. Your face becomes a flag whether you consent or not."

"I know that too."

"And me? What am I, in your arrangement?"

I looked at her—really looked, past the conviction and the charisma, at the tired woman underneath who'd grown up on stories of a stolen kingdom and had organized her entire self around getting it back.

"You're the reason it has to be a council," I said. "Because you'd win otherwise. And winning like that would kill everything worth winning."

That, finally, got a laugh out of her—short, sharp, unwilling. "Corvane trained you to negotiate?"

"Corvane trained me to repair lamps. Same skill set. Everything's broken in a slightly different way than people admit, and the fix always costs more than promised."

She put out her hand across the scarred table. I took it. Her grip was calloused and strong and entirely unsentimental, and I understood that I had just made a bargain with someone who would honor it exactly as long as it suited her, and that this was still better than most of the alternatives on offer.

"A council of survivors," she said. "You'll get your tribunal. And when they refuse you—because they will, Emrys, people always protect the lie that fed them—you'll remember I offered you the army."

"Noted," I said. "Filed under things I'll regret."

* * *

Marrec was watching from the ridge.

I didn't see him, of course. I found out later—from Sela, who had her own ways of knowing things now, ways I was still not ready to ask about—that the royal inquisitor had been lying prone in the heather above our camp since before dawn, close enough to count the buttons on Hask's coat, and that he had watched the entire burial and heard enough of the negotiation through a listening-charm to reconstruct the shape of it, if not the details.

What he saw was this: the last heir of House Thorn burying his jailer with full honors, then making terms with the rebel order—terms that included a public declaration, but on neutral ground, before a council, outside anyone's control.

What he concluded, I would learn much later, was this: the boy wasn't a weapon anymore. He was a variable. And variables were worse.

Sela told me he stayed on the ridge until full dark, motionless, and then he took out paper and wrote three lines, sealed them with grey wax, and gave them to a rider who left at a gallop toward the capital.

Three lines. We would spend the next eight chapters—I mean, the next several weeks—wondering what they said. Knowing Marrec, they were probably polite. Knowing the crown, polite was worse.

* * *

Sela found me at the graveside that evening, after the camp had gone quiet.

She sat down on the cold earth beside me without asking, the way she used to sit next to me on the guildhouse roof back when our biggest problem was whether Corvane would notice we'd skipped evening drills. For a while neither of us said anything. The misaligned stars crawled overhead, wrong constellations grinding against each other like millstones.

"You gave him a good rite," she said finally.

"I got the words right this time."

"He'd have complained anyway."

"He'd have said 'efficient.'"

She laughed, and it broke halfway through, and then we were both quiet again, and I realized I still didn't know how to be angry at her in a way that lasted. She'd betrayed the camp, yes. She'd made her bargain. But she'd also followed me over a fallen border into a world that hurt to breathe in, and she was sitting on frozen ground in a rebel camp at night because there was nowhere else her loyalty could live.

"The council," she said. "When it happens. You'll need someone inside it who knew you before you were a symbol."

"Sela—"

"I'm not asking for forgiveness. I'm telling you my use." She stood, brushed frost off her skirts, and looked down at me with the same expression she'd worn over the mill smoke: evidence, forcing a choice. "That's all I've got left to offer. Usefulness. Take it or don't."

I watched her walk back toward the fires, small and straight-backed against the wind, and I thought about Corvane in the cellar, feeding documents to flames, buying time with secrets. About Veyra at the table, offering me an army I refused. About Marrec on the ridge, writing three lines to masters who had already decided what peace was worth.

Everyone bargaining. Everyone spending. And somewhere in the middle of all of it, a stolen heir with dirt under his fingernails, trying to buy justice with the only coin he had left: the truth, spent once, in front of everyone, at a price none of them would control.

The stars shuddered overhead. Wrong, and getting wronger.

Three days later, a courier reached us with news from the south: the council of survivors had gathered at Cindermarch ahead of schedule—and the crown had sent delegates of its own.

They had sent Dain Marrec.

I looked at the message, then at Sela, then at the fresh-turned earth of Corvane's grave, and I said, mostly to myself:

"Well. He always did like to watch."
