## 23. The Road to Cindermarch

Emrys

The road out of Maren's country was called the Pilgrim's Way on old maps and the Bone Road by everyone who actually walked it.

I learned the second name from a carter whose cart had lost a wheel, an axle, and most of its optimism somewhere behind us. He let me ride on the tailboard in exchange for my help with the wheel, which took an hour, during which he talked without stopping, the way frightened men do when they've decided silence is more dangerous than speech.

"Everyone's going somewhere," he said, spitting over the wheel he'd just remounted as if it had personally wronged him. "That's what I don't like about it. Roads are supposed to go *somewhere*, not *everywhere*. Look at them."

I looked.

The road ahead of us crawled. Families with everything they owned strapped to their backs. A woman leading three goats and a child who was too old to be carried and too young to understand why his father wasn't with them. Two soldiers out of uniform — you could always tell, the way they walked like the ground owed them money — moving south with their boots tied together over their shoulders because walking barefoot meant no one could tell which army they'd deserted.

"Which way is everyone running?" I asked.

The carter gave me a look usually reserved for children and idiots, and I'd gotten that look often enough lately to recognize it as a compliment of sorts. "Both ways, lad. That's how you know it's coming."

He wouldn't say what "it" was. Nobody would say it out loud. They said *the sky*, and *the stars*, and *you heard about the wells*, and then they lowered their voices as though the war were a fire that could be started by a stray spark of conversation.

Veyra rode ahead of us, hood up, and I watched her watch the refugees with an expression I couldn't read. That was becoming a habit of mine, watching Veyra Thorn and pretending I understood her. It was either that or admit I'd thrown in with someone I understood about as well as I understood the weather.

We passed a shrine at midday — one of those little roadside alcoves where travelers used to leave offerings before the wards fell and the gods presumably got busy. The shrine had been stripped. Not robbed; *stripped*, carefully, every offering taken and every candle stub collected, and nailed to the stone above it was a strip of parchment bearing the Ashcloak seal: a thorn branch, black on white.

Below the seal, in Veyra's own hand, because of course she'd been through here before us: *THE TRUTH WILL WALK TO CINDERMARCH ON ITS OWN FEET. STAND ASIDE AND LET IT PASS.*

"They're reading it," she said, reining in beside the cart. She nodded at the parchment. At the road. At the people. "Every one of them. Word travels faster than horses now. The valley opened, the stars went dark, and half the world thinks the sky itself is accusing somebody." She smiled, and there was nothing warm in it. "Good."

"The other half?" I asked.

"The other half thinks whoever gets blamed first wins." She spurred on. "So we'd better be loud."

* * *

Veyra

I have marched through contested country twice before in my life. The first time I was nine years old, and my mother carried me across the Sorrow Ford on her back while Crown cavalry burned the far bank, and I remember thinking, with the terrible clarity of a child, that the smoke smelled like bread.

The second time is now.

Now I am the one carrying people, in a manner of speaking — carrying a story, carrying a name, carrying a boy who does not yet believe he is a banner. And I will confess what I confessed to no one: this time, the smoke smells like bread again, and I hate myself for remembering why I know that.

We made the ferry at Duskwater two days later. Or rather, we found where the ferry used to be. The ferryman's boat was burned down to the waterline, and the ferryman himself sat on the bank mending a net that had no future, watching the river with the patience of a man who has decided the river is the only honest thing left.

"No crossing," he said, before Veyra even asked. "Crown pickets on the bridge at Harrowgate. Ashcloak outriders on the north road. And me? I'm not carrying anybody anywhere, because last month I carried a man who turned out to be carrying letters, and both sides decided that made me carrying something else entirely." He held up his hands, palms out. Ten fingers. "See? All still attached. I intend to keep it that way."

"We're not soldiers," Veyra said.

The ferryman looked at her hood, then at me, then at the sword I still didn't know how to wear properly, and laughed — a short, ugly sound. "Nobody's a soldier until they need to be. That's how wars start."

In the end it was Emrys who solved it, which should not have surprised me and did anyway. He crouched by the ruined boat, asked the ferryman three quiet questions about the current, and then pointed upstream to where the river bent around a gravel bar and went shallow and slow over stones.

"There," he said. "You could ford that with goats."

"I could," the ferryman agreed, "and lose the goats. But you're welcome to try."

We tried. The water came up past my waist and the cold of it was a living thing, and Emrys slipped on the slick stones halfway across and I caught his arm and for one moment we stood there in the middle of the Duskwater, the two of us, heir and avenger, clinging to each other like children in a stream, and I thought: *this is what they'll never put in the songs.* Songs want fords crossed at a charge. Nobody sings about the part where your boots fill up.

On the far bank, wringing out my cloak, I watched him do the same and said, "You could still turn back. Marrec's offer stands. Be nobody again."

It was a test. I'll admit that. I wanted to see what he'd say now, after the ruins, after the graves, after everything.

Emrys wrung out his sleeve and considered the question with the same seriousness he gave everything, which is either his finest quality or his most exhausting one.

"If being nobody means the truth dies with me in some cellar," he said, "then I'd rather be somebody badly."

I laughed. Out loud, in front of him. I hadn't done that since the war took the last person I laughed in front of.

I'm beginning to suspect that was the exact moment my plans started coming apart. Not at the conclave. Here. In wet socks.

* * *

Marrec

There is a particular misery to following someone across a country that is actively trying to forget it has roads.

My column — eleven men, reduced from nineteen, two to desertion and six to a fever camp outside Thornhollow that I will not describe in this account or any other — moved south along the pilgrim road, and everywhere the road told the story before we arrived. Burned ferries. Stripped shrines. Black thorn seals nailed up like the countryside had broken out in a rash of rebellion.

Captain's log, day nine. We passed a village where every door had been painted with a white star. Day ten, a village where every door had been defaced, the stars smeared to grey ghosts. The two villages were four miles apart and I genuinely could not have told you which one was loyalist and which was rebel, or whether either knew anymore.

That is the thing about a casus belli. Everyone wants one. Nobody wants to be the one it happens to.

I found Winterbourne's trail easily enough, because Winterbourne traveled like a man writing a confession in mud: kind to everyone, remembered by everyone, paying for things with coins he clearly couldn't spare. My informants described him variously as a pilgrim, a scholar, a wounded soldier, and once — this from a miller's daughter with romantic eyes — "a gentleman with sad hands." Sad hands. Eleven years in the King's service and I have been outflanked, ambushed, and shot at, but I had never before been defeated by sad hands.

The Thorn woman was harder to trace because the Thorn woman left nothing but fear, and fear is common currency these days.

What troubled me was not the trail. Trails end. What troubled me was the orders.

They'd come by courier hawk at Harrowgate, sealed with the privy signet, and I read them standing in the rain because the courier wouldn't dismount and I suppose I felt that if I stayed uncomfortable enough the words might rearrange themselves into something survivable.

They did not rearrange.

*Recover the letter if practicable. If recovery risks exposure, remove the bearer. The peace admits no witnesses.*

Remove the bearer. I had read those words in dispatches before, always about other people — rebels, spies, men whose names were filed under threats rather than persons. I had signed orders containing those words. I had never been the bearer.

I burned the orders, which is treason, and kept a copy, which is worse, and told my lieutenant the route had changed, which is the smallest lie I told that week.

Sergeant Ollivan noticed. Ollivan notices everything; it's why I keep him and why he terrifies me. He fell into step beside me on the ninth evening and said, conversationally, "We're gaining on them, sir."

"Yes."

"And when we find them?"

I looked down the road, where the dusk was coming down blue and cold, and somewhere ahead of us a boy with sad hands was walking toward Cindermarch carrying the end of the world in a leather tube.

"When we find them," I said, "we'll see."

Ollivan looked at me a moment longer than a sergeant should look at a captain, and then he said, "Aye, sir," in a voice that suggested he intended to hold me to it.

Emrys

We picked up followers the way a wound picks up flies.

Not soldiers — not yet. Just people. An old stonemason named Brek who'd walked out of his contract when his lord started drilling levies. A pair of sisters who'd fled a border town where, according to them, the well had started whispering names, and one of the names belonged to a grandmother forty years dead. A boy of maybe fifteen called Pip who attached himself to our fire the first night and refused, with the immovable logic of the young, to attach himself to anything else.

"Why do you all walk toward Cindermarch?" I asked Brek on the third morning. Behind us, the road ran empty in the direction everyone sensible was fleeing. Ahead, it climbed toward the pass and the conclave and whatever waited there. "If there's a war coming, this is the road into its mouth."

Brek chewed on that, and on his breakfast, which appeared to be a leather strap he'd softened in broth.

"Because," he said finally, "when the sky starts falling, you want to be standing where they decide why." He tapped his temple. "Men fight over stories, lad. Always have. Whoever's holding the true one when the shouting starts — that's who the future belongs to." He squinted at me. "You've got a story, haven't you? I can see it hanging off you like a pack."

I didn't answer. But that night, when Veyra stood at the edge of our little campfire circle and told the refugees the shape of it — not the whole truth, not yet, but its silhouette — I watched their faces and understood what Brek meant. They weren't hearing a story. They were deciding whether to carry one.

And I realized, with a lurch, that I was doing arithmetic I'd been avoiding: every mile east, our band grew, and every new face was another person who would know, another person who could be made to talk, another name on a list that a royal inquisitor was almost certainly already drafting.

"You're doing it again," Veyra murmured, sitting down beside me at the fire's edge.

"Doing what?"

"Counting exits." She fed a stick to the flames. "Stop it. You can't save them by being less worth following. Believe me. I've tried."

"Pip," I said. "He's fifteen. If Marrec catches us—"

"Then Pip chose his road, same as you did." Her voice was gentle, which from Veyra was more alarming than cruelty. "You want to carry everyone. I know. I used to want that too." She looked into the fire. "Then I learned the difference between wanting to carry people and being able to. It's the distance between here and Cindermarch, roughly."

She rose, said goodnight, and left me alone with the fire and the arithmetic, which refused, as always, to come out even.

* * *

Marrec

Day twelve. The pass at Crowsfoot.

We caught the tail of Winterbourne's party at dawn — or rather, we caught what they'd left of it: a cold hearth, a mended cart-wheel gifted to an old woman, and a boy of fifteen asleep in the bracken who woke with a knife in his fist and terror in his face and, I noted professionally, absolutely no idea how to use either.

Pip. That was his name. He gave it up along with everything else inside a minute, because fifteen-year-olds are transparent as glass and this one had been crying before I even asked questions.

I crouched in front of him. My men fanned out through the trees, finding nothing, because there was nothing to find — Winterbourne had gone to ground, or gone ahead, or gone wherever sad hands go.

"Where are they?" I asked.

"I don't—" He swallowed. "I won't say."

"That's very brave," I said. "Do you know what happens to brave boys on this road, Pip? Nothing good. Do you know what happens to boys who tell me where three travelers went? Also frequently nothing good, but with better odds." I held up the hawk-carried dispatch, still creased from my pocket, still unread by anyone whose job title matched its contents. "Here is what I know. There is a war assembling itself out there like a storm over flat water, and the only thing standing between that storm and the coast is a piece of paper and a boy who doesn't want it. Now. Where. Are. They."

His chin came up. To my lasting surprise and mild annoyance, it stayed up.

"They went over Crowsfoot," he said, "and I hope the mountain eats you."

Well. I have been cursed by professionals. That was the worst curse I'd ever received and the only one I believed.

I stood, sheathed my knife, and looked at the pass — the grey shoulder of it, the thread of road climbing into cloud, the place where, in a few hours, I was going to have to decide what the words *remove the bearer* meant when the bearer had sad hands and a habit of fixing strangers' cart wheels.

"Sergeant," I said. "Bind the boy's wrists in front, leave him food, and point him south toward the fever camp road. There's a healer's tent at the crossroads. He'll be safe there."

Ollivan raised an eyebrow. "Sir, standard procedure is—"

"Standard procedure," I said, "is for bearers. This one's baggage." I turned my horse toward the pass. "Move."

Behind me I heard Ollivan murmur something to Pip — I caught the words *run fast, lad* — and I pretended not to, because a captain hears nothing convenient, and because my hands were shaking, and because somewhere above us, past the clouds, the misaligned stars were wheeling slowly back into a sky that had begun, very quietly, to come apart.

Three days to Cindermarch. Three days for me to become either the man who saved the peace or the man who murdered it.

I spurred uphill and tried not to notice which one I was hoping for.
