## 24. Ambush at the Ford

The river at Cael's Ford was the color of old pewter and about as welcoming, which is to say it looked like it had opinions about us and none of them were kind.

"Three days of moorland," Veyra said, crouching at the bank and studying the water like it owed her money. "And this is where they'll wait. Fords are where columns die."

"You're a fount of comfort," I said. "Truly. When this is over, you should write devotional poetry."

"I have written devotional poetry."

"Was anyone hurt?"

She gave me a look that suggested the poetry had been the weapon. I was beginning to understand that everything Veyra did was a weapon—her silences, her smiles, her habit of standing slightly too close when she wanted an answer. It was exhausting in the way that a well-forged sword is exhausting: beautiful, and designed for someone else's ribs.

We were eleven now. After Cindermarch's roads had filled with refugees and rumor, after the tribunal elders had turned us away with faces like slammed doors, our little company had shed people the way a wet dog sheds water. Some had gone home. Some had gone to ground. Two had simply vanished between one campfire and the next, and I tried not to think too hard about which direction they'd walked.

Eleven of us, one stolen letter's worth of truth folded into my coat, and a river between us and the survivor council at Thornhold, who—at least on paper—had agreed to hear us out.

On paper being the operative phrase. Paper burns easily. So do people waiting at fords.

I knelt and put my hand in the water. Cold enough to bite. The magic in it hummed against my skin like a trapped fly—the world beyond Hollowmere still hadn't learned to be gentle with me, or I with it. Sixty years of wards had left me a man allergic to the very air I'd been born into, if Corvane's letter was to be believed. Which I did believe, mostly, in the way you believe a diagnosis: because the alternative is worse.

"Emrys." Veyra touched my shoulder. "The reeds on the far bank. See how they're bent?"

I looked. They were bent. Reeds bend; it's their whole personality.

"Bent *toward* us," she said quietly. "Something crossed there recently. Something heavy. Horses, maybe. Or men who don't care what tracks they leave because they don't expect witnesses."

I felt the cold move up my arm and settle somewhere behind my sternum, where it had taken up semi-permanent residence since the night the stars went out.

"So we go around," I said.

"The ford is the only crossing inside two days' march, and we don't have two days. Marrec's column will reach Thornhold before us if we detour, and whatever window the council left open will close." She stood, brushing mud from her knees with the brisk efficiency of a woman who had never once in her life dawdled. "We cross fast, spread out, weapons loose. If it's nothing, we've lost an hour. If it's something—"

"If it's something, we're eleven people in open water."

"Yes." She smiled, and it didn't reach anywhere near her eyes. "Welcome to rebellion, Winterbourne. The benefits package is terrible."

* * *

In hindsight—and hindsight is the only sight I've ever truly excelled at—we should have known.

You learn things growing up in a valley built on a lie. You learn to read the small dishonesties of a place. You learn that when the miller says the grain stores are fine, they aren't. You learn that when your guildmaster tells you not to worry, worry has already unpacked its bags and signed a lease. A valley that keeps one secret learns to keep them all, and the people in it learn to hear the silence underneath the words.

So when the far bank stayed quiet—too quiet, no birdsong, no splash of fish, nothing but wind moving through reeds that had already told us their story—I should have said it out loud.

Instead I was three steps into the river, water at my knees, thinking about Sela.

That was the trouble with rivers. They give you nothing to do but think, and thinking, lately, meant her. Sela at the mill, flour on her cheekbones, laughing at me because I'd named every lamp in Hollowmere and couldn't name a single star. Sela pressing bread into my hands the morning I left, saying *come back and tell me it was all nonsense*. Sela's face in the firelight of the Ashcloak camp, the night the raid came, the night I understood that the person I trusted most in the world had sold my location to save her family—and that I could not even hate her properly, because her family was the only family I had ever had besides Corvane, and love makes terrible accountants of us all.

Marrec had the letter now. Had had it for weeks. We'd clawed it back across half the moorland, and every mile of that chase had been paid for in someone's blood, and some part of me kept doing the arithmetic: if Sela had never made her bargain, would the trail have gone cold? Would Corvane—

No. I stopped that sum every time. It never balanced. Grief doesn't do arithmetic; it just spends.

I was mid-river when the first crossbow bolt hit the water a hand's width from my hip.

The sound is strange—a flat *thock*, like a door closing on a room you were just about to enter. Then the world remembered it had edges.

"Ambush!" Veyra's voice cracked across the water like a whip, and then everything happened at once, the way disasters do, politely declining to take turns.

Men rose out of the reeds on the far bank—six, eight, ten, grey cloaks muddied to near-invisibility, the royal grey worn the way wolves wear winter. Crossbows. Two more rose from a copse upstream where they'd clearly been waiting since dawn with the patience of professionals. And downstream, half-hidden by a stone outcrop, the glint of a brazier and a signal mirror catching the sun: someone was telling someone else exactly where we were.

Crown loyalists. Not Marrec himself—he wouldn't waste his best asset on a riverbank—but his work all the same. His orders had changed, Sela's intelligence had whispered before she vanished again into her web of bargains: not retrieval anymore. Erasure. The heir wasn't to come home. The heir was to stop existing.

A second bolt skipped off the water beside me, and I did the bravest thing available to a man whose combat training consisted of lamp repair and mild sarcasm.

I went under.

The river took me like it had been waiting all day for the opportunity. Cold closed over my head and the world became roar and murk and the distant, muffled thunder of violence happening above me without my participation—which, frankly, sounded ideal. I let the current take me, tumbling, coat dragging at my shoulders with its precious cargo of paper and proof, and I thought with odd clarity: *if I drown here, the sky finishes unraveling, two kingdoms go to war over my corpse, and Corvane dies twice.* 

Then a hand closed on my collar and hauled.

I came up gasping behind the downstream outcrop, the same stone that sheltered the enemy's signal post, except the signal post was currently unattended because its operator lay face-down in the shallows with an Ashcloak knife hilt-deep between his shoulders, and the person holding my collar was kneeling over him with another knife already drawn.

Sela.

Flour-cheekbones gone hollow. Hair hacked short and practical. A bruise yellowing along her jaw and a look in her eyes I'd never seen in the mill, in the valley, in any of the years I'd known her: the look of someone who has done things she can't put down yet.

"Emrys." Her voice came out scraped raw. "Don't—don't look at me like that yet. Please. First let me keep you alive. Then you can look at me however you want for as long as you want."

Bolts thudded into the stone above our heads. Somewhere upstream, one of ours screamed. Veyra's voice cut through the chaos, ordering, steady, terrible.

"There are six on the bank," Sela said, fast, low, all business, because business was the only language either of us could bear just then. "Two reloading, they're slow, their sergeant is pushing them to rush. The two upstream are flanking along the shallows—they'll reach your people in a minute. And there's a rider coming down the far slope with a banner. An officer. He'll call it off or press it, depending on how it looks in thirty seconds."

"How do you know all this?"

"Because I've been following you for four days," she said, "and counting their fires for three."

I stared at her. Water ran off my chin. Above us the battle chipped away at the stone, patient as erosion.

"You've been—" My voice did something humiliating. "Four days."

"I couldn't approach. Your Ashcloaks shoot strangers. And you—" She faltered, the first crack in the competence. "You look at me like I'm the reason Corvane is dead. And maybe I am. I don't know anymore. I've done the sums so many times and they never balance, Emrys, they never—"

"Sela." Bolts again. Shouting. The officer's horse was close enough now that I could hear its breathing. "Later. All of it, later. Right now: the flanking pair, upstream. Where exactly?"

Her jaw set. She wiped her eyes with the back of her knife-hand, quick and furious, and pointed. "There. Along the shingle. They think the outcrop blocks their line."

"It does. From them." I looked at the dead signalman's crossbow, lying in the shallows with its string still dry under his body. "Can you shoot?"

"I'm a miller's daughter. I can throw a hammer through a rat at twenty paces."

"That'll do."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about fighting: it is mostly logistics. Who is where, who is tired, who has reloads, who is willing to die for the thing versus merely willing to wound for it. The loyalists were willing to wound. We were willing to die—or at least Veyra was, and her willingness leaked into the rest of us like dye into water.

Sela and I came around the outcrop's blind side through waist-deep water, and I discovered that terror sharpens certain senses while dissolving others entirely. I could not have told you the color of the sky. I could tell you the exact weight of the dead man's crossbow, the precise angle of the shingle under my boots, and the fact that Sela breathed in through her nose and out through her mouth, slow, the way she used to when the millstone jammed and her father shouted.

The first flanker heard us too late. Sela's thrown knife took him in the thigh—not lethal, but loud, and loud is often better—and as he folded, I did the single most violent act of my life to that point, which was to swing a crossbow like a club into the second man's helmet. He sat down in the river with a look of profound philosophical surprise and did not get up quickly.

"Emrys Winterbourne," Sela said, retrieving her knife, "lamp-keeper of Hollowmere, terror of the Cael Ford."

"Shut up and reload."

Upstream, the ambush was breaking. Veyra had done something with fire—one of her soldiers carried a hedge-witch's charm, illegal and ugly and effective—and the reeds burned in a black-smoking line that turned the loyalists' cover into a liability. The sergeant shouted retreat. The officer on the slope hesitated, banner limp, weighing thirty seconds of bad optics against a career, and chose career. Grey cloaks melted back into the moor like frost giving up on a spring morning.

It was over in less time than it takes to tell. Most terrible things are.

We gathered our wounded—two, both walking, one swearing creatively—and our dead, because we had one, a young Ashcloak named Perrin who'd joined us outside Cindermarch and whose whole biography fit on a scrap of paper: farm boy, brother, believed in Veyra completely. Veyra knelt by him in the shallows and closed his eyes, and for a moment her mask slipped and I saw underneath it not a rebel queen but a woman keeping a ledger of names, and the ledger was winning.

Then I turned, and Sela was standing in the river in front of me, soaked to the bone, knife sheathed at last, hands empty and hanging at her sides like she'd forgotten what hands were for.

"Well," she said. "Now you can look at me."

And I did. And the looking was the hardest thing I'd done since the stars went out.

* * *

We buried Perrin above the ford, under a rowan that had no business growing that close to the water but had decided to anyway, which felt right. Veyra spoke the Ashcloak words over him, short and hard, no comfort in them because the Ashcloaks had never been offered comfort and saw no reason to start a tradition. I added the Warden's blessing afterward, the one from Hollowmere, the one for lamps going out. Old habits. Old guild. Old lies, some of them—but the blessing itself had always been honest, and honesty was the only currency I had left that anyone respected.

Sela stood apart during the burial. Nobody spoke to her. The Ashcloaks knew what she was; word had traveled. Traitor, spy, crown's creature—labels stick faster than scars.

Afterward, while the others broke camp with the grim speed of people who knew the officer would report our position within the hour, she came and stood beside me at the rowan tree.

"I wrote you a speech," she said. "Forty versions. In my head, on scraps, once on the back of a wanted poster with your face on it, which felt appropriate. Every version started with *I'm sorry* and ended with *you have to understand*, and every version was garbage, because there isn't a version where I explain it and it stops being true. I did it, Emrys. I sold your road to Marrec's people."

"Why?" It came out quieter than I intended. "Your family. I know. But Sela—your family was never in danger until you made them part of it. That's what bargains like that do. They don't protect. They *enroll*."

"I know that now." Her voice cracked down the middle. "I didn't know it then. Marrec came to the mill and he was—he was kind, Emrys. That's the worst part. He sat at our table and he ate my mother's bread and he said, all reasonable, all gentle: the valley's peace is ending whether you help or not, and the only question is whether your family stands inside the storm or outside it. And I looked at my little brothers and I thought, what's one friend against three lives?" She laughed, and it was an awful sound. "One friend. As if you were a weight on a scale. As if any of this could be weighed."

"And then?"

"And then I watched what they did with what I gave them." She looked at the fresh-turned earth under the rowan. "The raid. Perrin. The chase across the moor. And I understood that Marrec hadn't lied to me, exactly—he'd just shown me a map with one road on it, and I'd been so grateful for a road that I didn't ask where the other ones went." She turned to face me fully. "So I made new bargains. Worse ones, probably, from his perspective. I've spent four days counting his fires and reading his couriers, and everything I've learned, I'm giving to you. Free. No price. Because the price was already paid, and it was paid by people who trusted me, and I will spend the rest of my life trying to make that arithmetic come out even if it kills me, and it might, and I've made peace with that."

I wanted to say *it's forgiven*. The words were right there, sitting in my chest like a lamp waiting for oil. Sixteen years of friendship. Bread pressed into my hands at dawn. The only person from home who had followed me into a world that made me physically ill, just to stand in a cold river and throw knives at men with crossbows.

But forgiveness, I was learning, is not a lamp. You cannot light it by wanting to. It needs something else—time, maybe, or truth, or the slow work of watching someone spend themselves paying back a debt—and I didn't have it yet. Maybe I would. Maybe the person I'd been in Hollowmere would have handed it over instantly, because that boy hated conflict more than he hated betrayal.

That boy had also believed the wards were failing naturally. That boy had a lot of stupid, comfortable ideas.

"I can't," I said. "Not yet. I'm sorry—that's a garbage answer too, apparently it runs in the friendship."

Sela nodded slowly. Something in her shoulders eased anyway, which confused me until I understood: she hadn't come for absolution. She'd come for permission to keep trying, and I had just given it.

"Then here's what I can offer instead," I said. "Partnership. Grudging, suspicious, badly-lit partnership. You know Marrec's movements. You know his methods, his couriers, his tells. And I—" I gestured vaguely at myself, dripping, exhausted, heir to a stolen kingdom I didn't want. "I'm the reason the sky is falling. Between us we're nearly one useful person."

Despite everything, her mouth twitched. "Nearly one useful person. High praise, from the terror of Cael Ford."

"Don't let it go to your head."

She held out her hand—knife-callused, river-cold, the same hand that had hauled me out of the current. I took it. It wasn't forgiveness. It was a down payment on a possibility, which is sometimes all anyone can afford.

"Thornhold by tomorrow night," Veyra called, already marching. "If the council hasn't locked their gates. Move!"

We fell in at the column's tail, side by side, an arm's length of cold air between us that neither of us closed and neither of us widened. Ahead, the road climbed toward the hills where the survivor elders waited with their slammed-door faces, and beyond them, somewhere, Marrec moved with his changed orders and his human face, hunting a boy he'd once shared bread with.

Sela walked in silence for a long while. Then, quietly, not looking at me:

"There's one more thing I learned from the couriers. Something Marrec's superiors sent him after the ford scouts reported. It's why I crossed the river last night instead of waiting."

My stomach dropped. "Sela."

"They're not sending soldiers anymore," she said. "They've hired someone else. Someone the crown keeps for problems that can't be traced back to it." She finally looked at me, and her eyes were the same eyes that had laughed at me over a millstone for sixteen years, and they were afraid. "They call him the Unwritten. And Emrys—nobody he's been sent for has ever been seen alive again. Not even a body to bury under a rowan tree."

Behind us, the ford ran silver and indifferent, carrying the day's blood away toward the sea.
