## 26. Marrec's Orders Change

There is a particular kind of letter that arrives when your career is about to end. I've received two in my life. The first was a promotion. The second came by hawk on a grey morning at the edge of the moorlands, sealed with the Chancellor's private signet rather than the Inquisition's, which is how I knew before I broke the wax that it wasn't going to be good news.

The Inquisition seals its letters with the eye-and-key. Bureaucracy watches; bureaucracy locks doors. The Chancellor's signet is a crown crushing a rose, and it is only ever used for orders that would embarrass the eye-and-key if they were written down where anyone might audit them.

I read it twice, standing in the doorway of the waystation while my sergeant argued with the innkeep about the price of oats.

*Recover nothing,* it said. *Retrieve no one. The bearer of the Winterbourne correspondence is to be ended quietly and completely. No body returned. No record kept. Report completion by cipher, then burn this.*

Then, because chancellors are not entirely monsters, or because whoever drafted it wanted to feel like one of the decent ones: *You have served the realm faithfully for nineteen years. This service will be remembered.*

That last line is what told me everything. You don't write "this service will be remembered" to a man you expect to see again. You write it to a man you're burying in advance, the way you bring flowers to a deathbed instead of a get-well card.

I folded the letter along its original creases, held it over the waystation's stove, and watched it curl into black lace. Then I stood there a moment longer than I needed to, listening to the sergeant lose his argument about oats, and thought: *Well. Here we are.*

* * *

Let me be honest about something, since honesty appears to be the theme of my current professional crisis: I am very good at my job, and my job has never been clean.

People imagine the Inquisition as hooded figures and iron cells. Mostly it's ledgers. Mostly it's reading a miller's tax records until you find the year he paid for a roof he couldn't afford, and following that thread until it leads you to the smuggler who paid him, and following *that* thread until it ends in a name someone powerful doesn't want spoken. And sometimes—three times, in nineteen years—the thread ends in a person, and the person has to stop existing, and I'm the one who makes them stop.

Three times. I can give you their names if you want. That's not a boast. It's the opposite. I keep the names because the alternative is letting them blur into "work," and the day my work becomes an abstraction is the day I become the kind of man who receives a letter like this one and feels nothing but the pleasant weight of trust from above.

So: three names. A counterfeiter in Vell who had seen the face of a duke's bastard son. A customs clerk who'd sold shipping manifests to the Ashcloaks. A woman named Ilse Ferrin, who had done nothing worse than transcribe the wrong ledger for the wrong scholar, and whose death I have never once managed to justify to myself, though I've had nineteen years and considerable practice.

I tell you this so you understand the shape of my hesitation. It was not the hesitation of a soft man discovering cruelty. I have never been soft. It was the hesitation of a careful man noticing, for the first time, that the arithmetic had changed—that the sum on the other side of the equation was no longer a threat to the realm, but a boy.

* * *

Here is what I knew about Emrys Winterbourne as of that grey morning:

He was twenty-two years old, give or take, raised in a valley that hadn't seen magic in sixty years, trained as a warden of lamps that held no light. He had lied to me badly in his own kitchen—I remember thinking, *whoever taught this boy to lie should be tried alongside him*—and then he had run, which was at least competent. He had fallen in with the Ashcloaks, which was predictable, and with Veyra Thorn specifically, which was alarming, and he had lost the letter somewhere on the moorlands, which meant the document itself was now in a saddlebag heading toward my column, and the boy was now merely a witness.

Witnesses are my trade. I know exactly how much a witness weighs.

And here is what the letter told me, in its elegant, rose-crushing way: the weight no longer mattered. The crown did not want the letter recovered anymore. Read between the lines and you could see why. If the heir of House Thorn was alive—if the theft at the founding of Hollowmere was true, and Corvane's papers confirmed it was true—then the peace of sixty years rested on a crime, and crimes, once proven, demand payment. Two kingdoms were already sharpening themselves against each other over rumors. A living heir was not evidence anymore. He was a fuse.

And you do not recover a fuse. You cut it out and you burn it and you report completion by cipher.

*Peace now means erasure.* That was the doctrine, stated nowhere and understood everywhere. I had operated under it for nineteen years without naming it. Naming it, that morning, with oat-dust in the air and the sergeant still grumbling, was the first time it tasted like anything.

* * *

"You're making the face again," Sergeant Brakka said, dropping onto the bench across from me with two cups of the waystation's coffee, which is a strong word for what it was.

"What face?"

"The one where you've read something and you're deciding whether to obey it." She pushed a cup at me. "You get it maybe twice a year. It looks like indigestion."

Brakka has served with me eleven years. She is the only person in the Inquisition who talks to me like a human being, which I suspect is because she could kill me and knows it, and I know she knows it, and we find the symmetry restful. She is also, though she'd deny it under torture, the closest thing I have to a conscience with a crossbow.

"New orders," I said.

"Good ones?"

"They're clear ones."

She drank her terrible coffee and watched me over the rim. "Clear," she repeated. "In my experience, sir, when a chancellor sends clear orders, it's because he doesn't want them understood too closely. Clear orders are for burning. Ambiguous orders are for surviving."

"I taught you that."

"You did. I'm returning it, slightly used."

I looked out the window. The moorlands went on being the moorlands—brown and silver and indifferent, the kind of country that makes a man feel briefly honest because it refuses to lie to him even a little. Somewhere out there, twenty miles east, a boy who had never left his valley until last month was learning that the world runs on lies the way mills run on water. Somewhere behind us, the capital was drafting celebratory histories in advance, the way they always do.

"Nineteen years," I said.

"That's a lot of years."

"It's a lot of bodies, is what it is. Three that count. Maybe forty that don't, depending on how you count, and I've decided not to decide." I turned the cup in my hands. "And now they want a fourth. Except this one isn't a clerk or a counterfeiter. This one is—" I stopped, because finishing the sentence honestly felt like stepping off a ledge.

"A boy who trusts you," Brakka said, when I didn't finish it. "That's the problem, isn't it? Not the killing. You've never had trouble with the killing. It's that he shook your hand."

"He asked me if the stars would come back." I heard my own voice go flat, which is what it does when I'm saying something true. "First night I met him. Standing in his guildhouse doorway, terrified, trying to be brave about it, and he asked me if the stars would come back. And I told him yes. I told him the crown would restore the wards and put the sky right, because that's what I believed. That's what I was *for*. I was the man who made the frightening things orderly."

"And now?"

"Now the order says the frightening thing isn't the falling sky. The frightening thing is the boy. And the tidy solution is to make him stop."

Brakka set down her cup. For a long moment she said nothing, which from Brakka is a sermon.

"You know what I think?" she said finally. "I think you already know you're not going to do it. I think you read that letter twice because you were checking whether it was really what it was, and then you burned it fast because you didn't want to look at it a third time. And I think the real question isn't whether you'll disobey. It's what you'll be afterward. Because there's no coming back from this one, sir. Disobeying this order isn't insubordination. It's defection with extra steps."

"I'm aware."

"Are you? Because you're sitting here drinking coffee like a man with options, and I want you to actually sit with the fact that you don't have any. Either you kill him—and I'll help, because I follow you into rooms, that's the arrangement—or you don't, and every door in the realm closes except the one he's standing in. There's no third door. Chancellors make sure of that. It's how they keep men like you useful for nineteen years."

"The coffee's terrible," I said.

"It really is."

* * *

I did what I always do with an impossible decision: I went for a walk and let my feet argue while my head pretended to be neutral.

The moorland near the waystation is crossed with old pilgrim paths, and the pilgrims, bless their stubborn hearts, built shrines at the crossings—little stone niches with candles, most of them long dead, some of them, since the wards fell, inexplicably lit. Magic coming back into the world like water into a dry cellar, finding every low place. I passed one shrine where the candle flame burned blue and bent toward me as I walked past, as if reading me.

"Mind your own business," I told it.

It flickered. I choose to believe that was agreement.

Here is the argument my feet settled on, somewhere around the third mile:

For nineteen years I had believed I was holding a line. That was the whole self-image, the load-bearing wall of Dain Marrec: the realm is a house, and the house stands on foundations nobody wants to look at, and my job is to make sure nobody looks, because if everyone looks at once the house comes down and people die in the collapse. Ugly work. Necessary work. Someone has to be the man who keeps the cellar door shut, and better a man who counts the cost than a man who doesn't.

But a foundation is supposed to hold the house up. What if the foundation was the thing pulling it down?

Because that was the truth I'd been circling since Hollowmere, the truth Corvane's burning papers had hinted at and the Chancellor's letter now confirmed in its bloodless way: the peace wasn't threatened by the truth getting out. The peace *was* the lie, and the lie was rotting, and you cannot preserve a rotting beam by killing everyone who notices the smell. The sky had gone dark over a valley of farmers. The stars were gone. Whatever anchor had held the wards—whatever the founders had done in that grave the valley was built on—it was failing on its own, and no amount of quiet murder was going to re-thread it.

Killing Emrys Winterbourne wouldn't save the peace. It would just mean the peace died unattended, in the dark, with no witnesses—which, I suppose, is how the crown prefers its deaths generally.

I stopped at the shrine. The blue flame leaned toward me again, patient, like a creditor.

"All right," I said to it. "Yes. I see it."

* * *

There's a version of this story where I ride back to the waystation, announce my defection in ringing tones, and gallop off into legend. I want it noted that I did no such thing. Defection, done properly, looks like paperwork.

I spent the afternoon writing my completion report in cipher. *Target located. Approach planned. Execution anticipated within the week.* Every word of it false, every word of it plausible, timed to arrive at the capital four days after I intended to be somewhere else entirely. Four days of grace. It wasn't mercy for the boy; it was logistics for me. A man needs a head start, and a lie with a delivery date is the best head start ever invented.

Brakka watched me seal it. "Four days," she said.

"Five, if the weather holds against the hawks."

"And after the five days?"

"After the five days," I said, "we're Ashcloaks, I suppose. Or something adjacent. I haven't picked a name for it yet. 'Unemployed' has a certain ring."

"You hate the Ashcloaks."

"I hate their methods. Their grievance happens to be legitimate, which is the most dangerous combination in the world." I tucked the report into the courier satchel myself, because I trusted no one else with it. "Besides, I'm not joining them. I'm following a lead. Nineteen years of habit; let me have it."

"What lead?"

"The same one I've been following since Hollowmere. Who gave the order to sabotage the wards. Because here's what occurred to me on my walk, Brakka, and it's the reason I'm not simply riding south and forgetting all of this: that letter didn't come from nowhere. Somebody in the capital knew what the heir was before I did. Somebody wrote 'recover nothing' with suspicious speed, which means somebody had already decided erasure was the policy, which means somebody has been managing this secret for a long time—possibly longer than Corvane, possibly since the founding. And I have spent nineteen years believing I worked for the house." I looked out at the moor, going purple with evening. "I'd like to meet the architect. Before I decide which side of the demolition I'm standing on."

Brakka grinned, which on her is a rare and unsettling event, like an eclipse. "You're going to get us both killed."

"Probably. But consider the alternative: we succeed, and we live long lives knowing exactly what we did to buy them." I shouldered my pack. "I've carried three names for nineteen years. I found I'm unwilling to carry a fourth who asked me about the stars."

* * *

We rode east through the dusk, toward the contested lands, toward the boy I'd been sent to erase.

Around midnight, on a ridge above the river road, I saw it and reined in so hard Brakka laughed at me.

The stars were coming back.

They'd been gone six weeks—every star, everywhere, a hole punched in the night that had driven priests mad and astronomers suicidal and given every army in the world an excuse to sharpen whatever it owned. And now, over the eastern horizon, they were returning. Any sensible man would have felt relief.

What I felt was the cold finger of the wrongness, because they were coming back *misaligned*. I know the sky. A man in my trade learns the sky the way a locksmith learns keys. And the constellations assembling themselves over Cindermarch were not the constellations I had navigated by for nineteen years. The Thorn-Crown sat where the Reaper's Scythe should be. Stars that had been fixed since the first maps were drifting, sliding, rearranging themselves like furniture in a house being shaken apart from underneath.

"Sir," Brakka said quietly. "Tell me that's normal."

"It is the opposite of normal."

Magic was anchored to something, and that something was moving—or unraveling—or waking up. And I thought of a boy in a lamplighter's coat asking whether the stars would come back, and I thought of the Chancellor's neat little doctrine of erasure, and I understood, with the clarity that only arrives at inconvenient hours, that killing Emrys Winterbourne might not steady the sky at all.

It might be the very thing that pulls it down.

"Change of plan," I said, and turned my horse north, toward the ruins where the rebels camped, toward the boy, toward whatever came next. "We're not following him anymore, Brakka."

"No?"

"We're going to have to warn him. The sky's coming down, and I have a horrible feeling everyone in the capital is about to blame it on him—and I need to reach him before they do."
