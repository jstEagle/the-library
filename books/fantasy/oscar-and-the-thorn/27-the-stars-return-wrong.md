## 27. The Stars Return Wrong

The Silent Choir kept their observatory in the bell tower, which struck me as either a profound theological statement or a very practical use of real estate. Probably the latter. Monks who swear to record history without judging it don't strike me as a crowd given to theology for its own sake.

Brother Aldous was the astronomer. He was also, at seventy-odd years, nearly blind in one eye and entirely deaf in one ear, which meant he conducted conversations by turning his good ear toward you like a sundial seeking a reluctant sun. He had spent forty years charting the sky over Cindermarch from this tower, and he greeted the news that the stars had vanished and returned by not greeting it at all — he simply handed me his ledger and went back to grinding a lens.

"Read me the last three entries," he said. "The ones in red."

I read them aloud. The tower was cold enough that my breath fogged the page, and my voice sounded strange against all that stone, like a guest who'd arrived too early to a funeral.

*"Ninth night of return. The Wanderer rises four minutes late. Position error accumulating."*

*"Twelfth night. The Sisters of the Loom have drifted a full degree from their recorded places. Correction impossible. The sky does not agree with itself."*

*"Fifteenth night. I have checked my instruments against Brother Fen's instruments against the archive's oldest charts. The error is not in our glass. The stars are coming back wrong."*

I lowered the ledger. "Wrong how?"

Aldous turned his good ear at me, then apparently decided he'd heard enough, because he answered without waiting for repetition. "Imagine," he said, "that you left your house for sixty years, and when you came back, every piece of furniture had been moved two inches to the left. Nothing broken. Nothing missing. Just — everything slightly off. You'd spend the rest of your life bruising your hip on the doorframe."

"That's a strange thing for furniture to do."

"That's a stranger thing for *stars* to do." He finally looked up at me with his one sharp eye. "Stars don't move, boy. That's rather the point of them. Kings rise and fall, wars come and go, and the Wanderer crosses the sky exactly as it did when the first drunk shepherd looked up and named it after his mother-in-law. The sky is the one honest thing in creation. And now it's lying to us by degrees."

* * *

I want to say I understood immediately what it meant. I didn't. For most of that day I filed it away as one more catastrophe in a season that had been generous with them — wards murdered, valley opened, letter stolen, inheritance revealed, rebellion fractured, sky gone dark and then un-dark in the wrong way. My capacity for dread had developed a sort of callus. One more wrong thing could stand in line.

It was Veyra who connected it, that evening, in the scriptorium where she'd been arguing with the Abbot about access to the war archives. She came down the tower stairs two at a time, found me mending a lamp — old habits; the Choir's lamps were mundane and grateful for it — and stood there breathing hard like a woman who'd run from good news.

"The wards," she said. "Your guildmaster's wards. How were they anchored?"

I blinked at her. "Stone. The border stones. Old craft, older than the Guild. Corvane said—" I stopped. Replayed it. Corvane, in the cellar, feeding documents to the fire, saying: *the wards aren't failing, boy, they're holding, they've always held, that was never the problem.*

"They weren't anchored to stone," I said slowly. "Or not only. A ward that strong, that quiet, that long-lived — you'd need something with its own... persistence. Something alive." My hands had stopped working on the lamp. "Something with blood."

Veyra nodded once, sharply, the way she did when someone else arrived at her conclusion and she was gracious enough not to gloat. Which, for Veyra, was a form of heroism.

"A living anchor," she said. "A ward woven around a person. Around an heir of Thorn, hidden in plain sight in the middle of nowhere, wrapped in sixty years of silence. The wards didn't fail when the stars went out, Emrys. They were *cut loose*. And now everything they were tied to is drifting back into place — but the knot was cut, so it comes back crooked." She took a breath. "You're not just the heir. You're the keystone. The whole peace of the valley, the whole seal on the magic, was hung on *you*, and now that you're out from under it, the sky doesn't know where to put itself back."

I set down the lamp. Very carefully, because my hands had begun doing something independent of my intentions.

"So when I renounced the inheritance," I said, "at the council — when I refused the claim—"

"You loosened it further. Every day you walk around refusing to be what you are, the sky drifts another degree. You're not unraveling the war, Emrys. You're unraveling the *stars*."

There's a particular quality to a realization that arrives too large for the room it lands in. It doesn't hit you all at once. It hits you in installments. First: the stars. Then: the stars, because of me. Then: the stars, because of me, specifically because I keep insisting on being a lamp-mender from Hollowmere instead of a stolen prince, and the universe has taken this personally.

"I need to sit down," I said, and sat down on the floor, because chairs had become administratively complex.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about being told you are cosmically significant: it feels almost identical to being told you are cosmically insignificant. Both arrive as a cold hand between the shoulder blades. Both make the world go quiet and slightly tilted. The difference is only in the paperwork afterward.

I sat on the scriptorium floor while Veyra paced and laid out implications like a general laying out cavalry positions. If the wards were anchored to me, then whoever controlled me controlled the sky's recovery. The crown would want me dead or contained — a misaligned heaven was a bad omen, and bad omens start wars faster than grievances do. The Ashcloaks' hardliners would want me crowned and paraded — a living heir who could steady the heavens by accepting his throne was a miracle with a banner attached. Either way, I stopped being a person and became a hinge.

"And if I just..." I gestured vaguely. "Kept walking? Kept refusing?"

"Then the sky keeps slipping." Veyra crouched in front of me, and for once there was no strategy in her face, only the raw arithmetic underneath it. "Emrys. I've spent my whole life wanting to burn the lie down. I never imagined the lie was load-bearing."

That was the sentence that got under my skin and stayed there. *Load-bearing.* All those years in Hollowmere, tending lamps that held no fire, and the whole valley had been a roof held up by a single child who didn't know he was a pillar. Corvane hadn't been hiding me from the world. He'd been holding the world up with me, the way you hold a wound closed with your own hands, knowing you can never let go, knowing your arms will give out someday, praying someone finds a better way before they do.

*I chose you hoping you'd find a better answer than either side offered.* His dying words, weeks ago, in the mud outside the ruins. At the time they'd felt like absolution. Now they felt like a bill.

* * *

Brother Aldous confirmed it the next night, and confirmation, I learned, is its own special cruelty. Suspicion leaves room for mercy. Proof doesn't.

He let us into the tower after dark — Veyra, me, Sela, who had drifted back to my side somewhere along the moorlands and whom I still hadn't decided how to forgive, and Marrec, who had arrived two days prior wearing his royal grey like a man wearing a debt, and whom nobody had yet worked up the courage to either arrest or trust. The five of us crowded around a brass instrument that looked like a weapon designed by a mathematician.

"Watch the Wanderer," Aldous said. "And watch this." He tapped a chart pinned beside the eyepiece — his own hand, forty years of nightly observations, each star's position marked in ink fine as spider silk. "Sixty years ago, the night the wards rose, the sky over this valley went dark. Everyone remembers that. What no one recorded — because no one was looking — is that the sky everywhere else kept perfect time. Because it wasn't the sky that changed. It was the *seal*. The wards didn't hide the valley from the stars, boy. They hid the stars from the valley, through you. You were the lens. And now the lens is cracked."

I looked. I won't pretend I understood the instrument, but I understood Aldous's face, and Aldous's face was the face of a man watching forty years of certainty file quietly out of his life.

"It's accelerating," he said. "Three weeks ago the error was minutes of arc. Tonight it's degrees. Whatever's pulling the sky back into place is losing its grip on it." He turned his good ear toward me, though we both knew he'd heard perfectly well. "Tell me again about these wards of yours. Tell me exactly how they were made."

So I told him what I knew — secondhand, half-remembered, fragments of Corvane's cellar confession stitched together with guild lore I'd memorized as a boy without understanding any of it. The founding warden, six decades ago. The infant taken across the border in the last winter of the Thorn War. The bargain struck in secret: the heir hidden, the wards raised, the war ended not by victory but by theft. Peace as a kidnapping.

"And the anchor," Aldous said, when I finished. "The living part. What did the founding warden actually *do*?"

I opened my mouth to say I didn't know.

And then I remembered.

Not a document. Not a confession. A lullaby. My mother's — no, *not* my mother's, the woman who raised me, Sela's aunt, the miller's household — a song in Old Thorn dialect that no one in Hollowmere should have known, sung over my cradle, and the way the room always seemed warmer afterward, and the way Corvane used to visit on those nights and stand in the doorway with an expression I'd mistaken for sentimentality and now recognized as a man checking a load-bearing wall for cracks.

"There was a naming," I heard myself say. "An old rite. The warden gave the child a name that wasn't his, and bound the wards to the false name, and the false name to the true blood. As long as the child lived unknowing — as long as the name stayed false and the blood stayed hidden — the wards held. The moment either truth came out..."

"The structure fails," Aldous said softly. "Like a bridge learning it was built on a river."

Nobody spoke. Somewhere below us, the Choir's bells rang the hour, indifferent and beautiful, and I thought: even the bells are keeping time better than the sky now.

* * *

We stood at the tower's open arch afterward, the four of us, looking up. The stars were back — that was the horror of it, that they were *back*, beautiful and familiar-seeming, and only an old man with one eye and forty years of ink could see that they were home from the war and standing in the wrong rooms.

"The Wanderer," Aldous said behind us, "will rise in the east tomorrow, four degrees south of where it has risen every night since before your grandmother's grandmother. Farmers will notice within a month. Sailors sooner. Astronomers in every kingdom are already writing letters, and letters become councils, and councils become panic. Panic, gentlemen and ladies, becomes war faster than any stolen heir ever could." He paused. "Unless the sky can be set right. And the only mechanism anyone has found for setting it right is currently standing at my window feeling sorry for himself."

"I'm not feeling sorry for myself," I said. "I'm feeling sorry for the sky. It's having a worse week than I am."

"No," Aldous said, with terrible mildness, "it isn't."

Marrec laughed. One short syllable, surprised out of him, and then he looked almost apologetic about it. Inquisitors aren't built for laughter; it leaks.

"He's not wrong, though," Marrec said. He'd positioned himself, as always, where he could see both the door and everyone in the room — old habits dying harder than inquisitors. "I've read the crown's diviners' reports. They've reached the same conclusion Aldous has, through worse methods. They know the sky is anchored to a living heir. Their recommendation—" he paused, and I watched a man weigh honesty against habit and, for once, choose the heavier one, "—their recommendation was that the anchor be *removed*. Quietly. A death, they wrote, would release the binding cleanly. A coronation might re-anchor it, but a coronation puts a Thorn heir on a throne, which the current regime likes even less than a broken sky. So: assassination. They dressed it up in diviner's language. *Liberation of the celestial bond.* But it's a knife, Emrys. They sent me here to be the knife, before my orders changed to retrieval, before they changed again to—" he stopped.

"To whatever they are now," Veyra said flatly. "Which is why you're standing in a monastery instead of reporting back."

"Which is why," Marrec agreed, "I'm standing in a monastery committing treason by omission." He looked at me, and there was something in his face I hadn't seen before — not the smooth interrogator's mask, not the weary soldier. Something more like a man reading his own handwriting on orders he no longer recognizes. "Do you understand what you are now? Not symbolically. Practically. You are the only object in the world that three armies are already marching toward, and none of them are marching to ask you questions."

"The council elders wouldn't listen," I said. "At the conclave. When I tried to give them the middle path." Bitterly: "They said truth would reignite the war. They were right about the truth. They just had the wrong truth. It's not the crime that reignites wars. It's the *sky*."

Sela had been silent through all of it, standing near the arch with her arms crossed against the cold, and when she finally spoke her voice was careful, the way voices get when they're carrying something fragile across a long distance.

"Emrys. There's something I learned. In Marrec's camp, when I—" she stopped, restarted. "When I was spying. Before the ford. There was a dispatch I wasn't supposed to see."

I turned to look at her properly. The stars wheeled, slightly wrong, above her head.

"What dispatch?" I asked.

She glanced at Marrec — a quick, guilty, complicated glance, the glance of someone about to burn down the last bridge between herself and her old life — and said:

"The one that explains why the crown changed your orders, Dain. Why they stopped wanting you retrieved and started wanting Emrys erased. It wasn't just the sky." Her jaw tightened. "They've found a way to blame the falling stars on the Ashcloaks. Sabotage. Heresy. They're calling it *the Thorn Curse* — the rebels poisoning heaven itself. The mobilization orders are already written. They launch the moment the first kingdom publicly blames the other." She looked back at me, and her eyes were wet and steady at once. "Emrys — we have until the solstice. Maybe less. And the only proof that it's a lie is standing right here, being the sky."

Below us, the bells rang the quarter hour. Above us, the Wanderer climbed toward a horizon it no longer quite trusted.

And I stood in the cold with my stolen name and my borrowed life, doing the new arithmetic: not bloodlines now, but days. Counting what remained of the time in which a lamp-mender from Hollowmere could still become anything at all — before the sky finished slipping, before the knives arrived, before the lie I was born into finished devouring the world it was built to protect.

"We need a plan," Veyra said.

"We need a miracle," Marrec said.

"We need both," Sela said, "and we need them by winter."

I looked up one last time at the misaligned stars, at the honest sky telling its new lie, and I thought of Corvane in his cellar, feeding the past to the flames, buying me time with everything he had.

"All right," I said. "Then we'd better start building."
