## 28. Sela's Confession

I had rehearsed the confession forty times on the road to the ford, and every version ended with Emrys forgiving me, because that is the trouble with rehearsing anything—you only rehearse the ending you want.

The real version began with me standing at the edge of his firelight like a stray dog that had once bitten the hand that fed it, which, to be fair, was roughly accurate.

"You're supposed to be dead," Emrys said. He didn't get up. That was worse than shouting. Emrys shouting was Emrys caring; Emrys sitting very still with his hands around a tin cup was Emrys doing arithmetic, and I had never once in our lives come out well in his arithmetic.

"So are you, according to the crown," I said. "We match."

Veyra's lieutenant had a knife at my back and Emrys's eyes in my front, and between those two options I chose honesty, mostly because the knife left less room for improvisation.

"I'm not here for you," I said. "I mean—I am, but not the way you think. I need to tell you what I learned. All of it. And then you can decide what I am."

"And what are you?"

I looked at the fire. "Tired. Mostly tired."

* * *

You should understand what the bargain was, because Emrys only ever saw the end of it—the raid, the letter gone, me standing among grey riders with my face doing whatever my face does when it lies, which is nothing, which is the whole problem with me.

It started with the mill. It always starts with the mill, in my stories, because the mill is where my family's whole world turns, literally, on a wheel, and when the wheel stops everyone starves and everyone blames me, the miller's daughter, who was supposed to marry the cooper's son and die in the same valley she was born in, contentedly, like a candle in a jar.

The night the stars went out, my father stood in the mill yard and looked up at a sky with nothing in it and said, "That's an omen," and my mother said, "That's weather," and my father said, "Since when is weather the absence of everything," and for once in their married life they agreed on something, which was that the world had broken.

Then the grey riders came through the pass, and a man with a soft voice and a ledger came to the mill and bought flour he didn't need and asked questions he did, and when he was done he looked at me the way buyers look at grain—assessing weight, assessing moisture, assessing whether I'd keep.

His name was Quill. Of course it was. Men like him are always named after the instrument that does the damage.

He didn't threaten my family. That's the thing I need Emrys to understand, and the thing I can't make him understand, because it's more frightening than a threat. Quill sat at our table, ate our bread, complimented my mother's preserves, and said, "The crown is very interested in the welfare of families in this valley. Interested families have nothing to worry about. Uninterested families—" and he spread his hands, and his hands said *the crown forgets people*, and in a valley that had just lost its stars and its wards and its guildmaster in a single night, being forgotten by the crown sounded exactly like dying.

He didn't say *spy for us or we burn the mill*. He said, "Your friend Emrys is in danger. The people he's fallen in with are dangerous. If someone who knew him were close to him, someone could keep him safe." And then he paid for the flour with a coin that was worth ten loaves, and left, and I told myself for three days that I hadn't been recruited, I'd just been *consulted*, and on the fourth day I followed Emrys's trail over the border stone because Quill had shown me the trail and because I told myself I was going to protect him.

I have a talent for telling myself things. It's the same talent as lying, just pointed inward.

* * *

The fire cracked. Emrys hadn't spoken in a while, and Veyra had drifted close enough to listen without pretending not to, and Marrec—gods, Marrec—sat across the fire with his wounded shoulder and his inquisitor's face, watching me like a man auditing a ledger he'd already balanced.

"Go on," Emrys said quietly.

So I went on.

"I found you because they let me. I want that on the record. Quill had your trail before I did; I was the leash, not the hound. And when I caught up to the Ashcloak camp, I told myself every night: *tomorrow I'll warn him, tomorrow I'll tell him what Quill is*. And every night Veyra would say something true and terrible about the graves at Cael Harrow, and every morning Quill's coin would feel lighter in my pocket than my father's fear felt, and tomorrow kept becoming today."

"You sold the letter," Emrys said. Flat. Not a question.

"I sold the *location*. Quill already knew about the letter. He knew about it before I did, Emrys. That's what I learned, in the end, the thing I came here to tell you: I was never giving them information. I was giving them confirmation. They had the shape of everything already. What they needed was a witness inside the camp who could tell them when Veyra would move, where she'd sleep, which tent held the paper." I wrapped my arms around my knees. "And I told them. And then I stood in the trees during the raid and watched them take it, and I told myself *at least nobody died*, and then I found out later that two Ashcloak sentries didn't come back, and I stopped being able to tell myself anything at all."

The fire popped. Somewhere out in the dark, the ford murmured over its stones.

"You could have told me," Emrys said. "Any night. There were forty nights."

"Yes." I said it plainly because he deserved plain. "I could have. I want to tell you that I didn't because I was afraid, and that's true, but it isn't the whole truth. The whole truth is that Quill had my family's name in a ledger, and I had watched what happened to names in that ledger that got crossed out. And the whole truth is also that some small rotten part of me wanted to matter. I'd spent my whole life being the miller's daughter in a valley where nothing mattered, and then suddenly the most important secret in the world had a shape, and the shape was my best friend, and men with soft voices were asking me about it, and I—" My voice did something embarrassing. I let it. "I liked being asked."

Emrys looked at me for a long time. He has his guildmaster's habit of silence, Corvane's way of letting a silence do the work of a sermon. I sat in it.

"You're telling me this now," he said finally, "because?"

"Because of the sky."

* * *

Here is what I learned in the weeks after the raid, when Quill stopped needing me for the camp and started using me for the roads, because a miller's daughter can go anywhere with a flour cart and nobody looks twice at flour.

I learned that the crown's astronomers had stopped publishing. I learned that the observatory at Cindermarch had been closed to all visitors in the third week after the stars went out, and that the closure was signed by someone two ranks above anyone with the authority to close an observatory. I learned that Quill's superiors had a word they used for the falling sky, and the word was not *crisis*. The word was *opportunity*.

I learned it because Quill got drunk. Not falling-down drunk—Quill was never falling-down anything—but the loose, generous drunk of a man who has just been told his work has been noticed, and who wants someone to notice it with. I was convenient. I was also, he thought, safe, because what would a miller's daughter do with state secrets, bake them?

"They'll blame the Ashcloaks," he said. He was looking at the fire in the way men look at fires when the fire is not what they're seeing. "The rebels. It's elegant, really. The sky starts failing, the realm panics, and who do we point at? The order that's been howling for sixty years that the peace was a lie. *They* broke the wards. *They* killed the stars. Every peasant who ever lost a cow to a hedge-witch's curse will believe it before his porridge cools." He laughed, a small dry sound. "And a frightened realm forgives its crown anything. Even a preemptive war. *Especially* a preemptive war."

I kept my face still. It's the one thing I'm good at.

"And the heir?" I asked, because I knew about the heir by then, everyone in that machinery knew about the heir, the word went up and down the supply lines like salt. "The Winterbourne boy?"

Quill's face did something I had never seen it do. It went careful.

"The boy is a problem that's being solved," he said.

I have thought about that sentence every day since. *Being solved.* Not *solved*. Present progressive. Ongoing. A problem with a knife moving toward it, not a knife already in it.

I left the next morning with a flour cart and a plan that had one step in it, which was: find Emrys before the solving finishes.

* * *

When I finished, the camp was quiet enough that I could hear the ford, and Veyra's breathing, and Marrec not saying anything, which from Marrec was a whole speech.

Emrys stood up. He walked to the edge of the firelight and stood with his back to me, and I watched his shoulders and waited for them to do something, the way you watch a sky that has already fallen once.

"You understand," he said, without turning, "that I can't just—" He stopped. Started again. "You were my best friend, Sela. You were the only person in the valley who knew I wanted out and didn't make me feel stupid for it. And you sold me for a ledger entry."

"I sold your *location*," I said, and immediately hated myself, because it was the kind of distinction a liar makes. "No. I'm not going to shave the blade. I sold you. I told myself it was to keep you safe and maybe some part of that was even true, but Emrys, I sat across from Quill and I chose my family over you, and I'd choose them again, and that's the thing you actually can't forgive, isn't it? Not that I was weak. That I'd do it again."

He turned around. His eyes were wet, which he'd have denied under torture, and I'd have testified to it under torture, out of spite.

"No," he said. "That's not the thing I can't forgive."

I blinked.

"The thing I can't forgive," he said, "is that you were right to be afraid. Quill did have your family in a ledger. The crown really does solve problems. I've been running from it for months and I know it's true. You weren't a coward, Sela. You were *accurate*. And I don't know what to do with that, because I wanted you to be a coward. Cowards I can be angry at. Accurate people I just have to live with."

I laughed. It came out broken, the way laughs do when they've been waiting too long, but it came out.

"Veyra wants to hang me," I offered. "Just so you know where everyone stands."

"I want to *question* her," Veyra said, from the dark, in a tone that made it clear the questioning would be conducted at length and possibly with instruments.

"You'll do neither," Marrec said, and we all looked at him, because Marrec rarely spent words on the obvious. He was leaning forward, and his inquisitor's face had done the thing it did when a case turned in his hands—when the shape he'd been holding all along suddenly showed him a different shape inside it. "Say the last part again," he said to me. "The astronomers. The closed observatory."

I said it again. The closure, the signature two ranks too high, the word *opportunity*.

Marrec was quiet for a moment, and then he said, in the voice of a man laying a card face-up, "I was told my mission was retrieval. Recover the letter, recover the boy, quietly. Two weeks ago my orders changed. I told you that." He looked at Emrys. "What I didn't tell you is what the new orders were built on. A report from Cindermarch. The wards weren't just anchored to the valley, they were anchored to *you*, and with you loose and the anchor straining, the crown's diviners project the sky fails fully within a season. And a realm that believes it's dying doesn't negotiate. It *strikes*. They need a war before the sky finishes falling, because a war gives them someone to blame for it."

"The Ashcloaks," I said. "Quill told me. They're going to plant the evidence themselves. Sabotage dressed as rebellion. I heard him toasting it—*to the rebels, who finally did something useful.*"

"And the heir," Emrys said slowly, "is the loose thread. If I'm dead, the theft dies with me. No claim, no proof, no inconvenient living reminder that the peace was bought with a stolen child. Just a sky that failed, and rebels to blame it on, and a war to burn the evidence in."

Nobody said anything. The ford kept murmuring. Somewhere in the dark a nightbird made a sound like a hinge.

"So," I said, because someone had to, and being the person who says the unbearable thing was apparently my role now, my penance, my terrible little gift. "You're all sitting around a fire with the two people the crown most wants dead, and one crown inquisitor who's already broken his orders once, and a rebel queen who wants a war of her own, and me, who's lied to every single one of you. And the only thing standing between this valley and a war is a plan that gets the truth out faster than the crown can bury it and louder than Veyra can weaponize it."

"Thank you, Sela," Emrys said dryly, "for that summary of our hopeless situation."

"I'm a miller's daughter. Summarizing grain is what I do."

He almost smiled. It was a small thing, a crack of light under a door, and I took it the way I'd once taken Quill's coin—gratefully, and knowing it wasn't enough, and hoping it was a beginning.

But then Emrys went still, the particular stillness he got when the arithmetic finished, and he looked up at the sky—the wounded, misaligned, slowly unraveling sky—and said, "The conclave. The winter solstice. Every crown, every order, all in one hall, and if the sky is failing on a schedule, then so is the war. We don't need to outrun the crown's lie." His voice had that thing in it it had the night he crossed the border stone, terror and wonder in the same breath. "We need to arrive before it does."
