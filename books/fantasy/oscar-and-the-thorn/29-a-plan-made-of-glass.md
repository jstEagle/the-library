## 29. A Plan Made of Glass

The cellar of the Silent Choir smelled of dust, tallow, and the particular patience of men who had spent four hundred years copying things down without once being asked their opinion. It was, I decided, the only room in the world where a fugitive heir, a rebel queen-in-waiting, a traitor-turned-ally, and a royal inquisitor could sit at the same table without anyone immediately reaching for a weapon.

Though I'll be honest: Marrec kept his hands where everyone could see them, and Veyra kept hers where nobody could.

We'd come down after midnight, by ones and twos, through the scriptorium above, past monks who copied our faces into the margins of their ledgers with the serene indifference of men recording weather. Abbot Ossian had given us the key and one instruction: "Whatever you decide in this room, write it down. Future readers deserve to know how close you came to foolishness."

Veyra had laughed at that. Marrec hadn't. I hadn't either, mostly because I was too busy being terrified of my own idea now that it had to survive contact with three people smarter and more dangerous than me.

"So," Sela said, breaking the silence the way she always did—like cracking an egg against a bowl's edge, briskly, before anyone could get sentimental about the shell. "Emrys wants to walk into the biggest room in Cindermarch full of every crown that wants him dead, and read them a bedtime story about murder and stolen babies. Have I got that right?"

"Not a bedtime story," I said. "An archive."

"A very long bedtime story."

"The *full* record," I said. "Everything the Silent Choir holds. The theft of the Thorn heir. The forged peace. Corvane's confession. The wards anchored to a living child. The graves under Hollowmere. All of it, read aloud, in the conclave hall, at the solstice, when every delegation is already gathered."

"And the part where we all die," Sela added helpfully, "is where?"

* * *

I should explain how we'd gotten here, because getting here had taken four days, two forged passes, and one genuinely alarming amount of bribery paid in hedge-witch remedies that Maren swore would cure anything except honesty.

After my arithmetic finished on the monastery wall—after I understood that the sky was coming apart because *I* was the anchor and the anchor was supposed to have been buried with the lie—the plan had seemed simple. That's the trap of plans. In your head they're clean lines. Then you put them on a table in front of other people and they develop edges.

Marrec had been the first problem. He sat across from me now in his grey traveling coat, out of uniform for the first time since I'd known him, which somehow made him look both less and more like what he was. His orders had changed—I knew that from Sela's spying. Retrieve the heir had become erase the heir. And yet here he was, in a rebel cellar, drinking monastery beer and looking like a man doing long division he didn't like the answer to.

"You understand," he said, "what you're proposing isn't a plan. It's a detonation."

"Good," Veyra said. "Detonations are honest."

"Detonations are *uncontrolled*. There's a difference." He turned his cup slowly on the table, a habit I'd noticed—he fidgeted with objects when he was thinking, as if his hands needed something small to conquer while his mind worked on something large. "You want to hand every faction in the valley war the same evidence at the same moment. Do you know why crowns keep secrets, Winterbourne? It's not shame. Shame is cheap. They keep secrets because secrets are *leverage*. A crime known only to you is a knife. A crime known to everyone is just weather. You're proposing to make the crown's greatest crime into weather."

"Yes," I said.

He looked up. "That's it? Yes?"

"That's rather the point, isn't it? Weather doesn't take sides. If the truth belongs to everyone at once, no one can spend it. No one can trade it. No one can hold it back and release it at the moment of maximum advantage—which is exactly what the crown planned to do, blame the Ashcloaks for the falling sky, and march." I spread my hands. "I'm not trying to control the truth, Marrec. Every attempt to control it is how we got here. Sixty years ago someone decided the truth was safer in a box, and the box became a valley, and the valley became a grave, and the grave became *me*. I'm done with boxes."

The cellar went quiet. Somewhere overhead a monk turned a page; it sounded enormous.

Veyra was watching me with an expression I'd learned to distrust slightly less than her others—not warmth, exactly, but the look of a commander reassessing terrain she'd thought she knew. "And if they don't listen?" she said. "If the delegations hear the reading and reach for their swords anyway? Truth doesn't disarm men, Emrys. Sometimes it aims them."

"Then they aim at each other over a table instead of across a border," I said. "With witnesses. With the Choir's record standing there in front of them, in front of *history*, so that whatever they do next, they do it knowing. That's all justice ever is, I think. Doing the next thing while knowing." I heard myself and winced internally—that was Abbot Ossian's phrasing, borrowed, but it had lodged in me like a splinter and I couldn't get it out. "It's not much. But it's more than any of us has managed so far."

"It's not enough," Veyra said flatly.

"No," I agreed. "But it's the only shape that fits through the door."

* * *

Here was the shape, as I'd worked it out in the sleepless dark of the guest cell, talking to a ceiling that had heard better arguments:

The winter solstice conclave convened in the Spire of Cindermarch—a tower built, in the old boastful way, to be visible from every kingdom in the contested lands. Every crown sent delegations. Every order. The Ashcloaks weren't invited, which Veyra considered a professional insult, and the Silent Choir attended only as recorders, sworn neutral, permitted to stand in the gallery and write down whatever the powerful chose to say to each other.

Sworn neutral. Permitted to record. Standing in the gallery with the largest archive of suppressed history on the continent.

"Do you see it?" I'd asked Ossian, and the old abbot had regarded me over his spectacles with the mild patience of a man who had seen everything twice. "The Choir has spent four centuries being furniture. Furniture gets carried anywhere."

The plan, then: the Choir's annual reading. Every conclave opened with it—a formality, an hour of the monks reciting the year's recorded events in flat voices while delegates dozed or schemed. Nobody listened. Nobody had listened in generations. Which meant nobody would object when the Choir stood to read, because the Choir always read.

Except this year, the reading would run longer than an hour. This year it would run through the theft, the wards, the graves, the heir. This year the furniture would speak.

"We can't force them to stay," I told the cellar. "We can't force them to believe it. All we can do is make sure that at the moment the sky finishes tearing itself apart—and the astronomers give us until roughly midwinter before the misalignment becomes visible to anyone with eyes—they all hear the same words at the same time. After that, it's theirs. All of it. Even the parts that damn my family. Even the parts that damn the Ashcloaks' founders, because Veyra, your order burned villages too, and it's in the record, and it will be read."

Veyra's jaw tightened. "Those were reprisals—"

"They'll be read," I repeated. "All of it, or none of it. If we curate the truth, we've just built a smaller, angrier box, and someone in this room will eventually want the key." I looked around the table. "That includes me. Especially me. I'm apparently the most valuable box on the continent."

Sela snorted. "Modest, too."

"I'm learning humility from professionals."

* * *

The second problem was Veyra herself, and she solved it for me by refusing, magnificently, for two hours.

"You're asking me to bring nothing," she said. She'd risen from her chair somewhere in the first hour and now prowled the cellar's edge, past shelves of ledgers going back further than any kingdom she wanted to burn. "No soldiers at the door. No declaration. No claim. You're asking me to stand in that hall and let the record speak for a house I've bled ten years to restore—and trust that words will do what armies couldn't."

"I'm asking you to trust that words plus witnesses will do what armies couldn't," I corrected. "Your army outside those doors is exactly the pretext the crown needs. 'Rebel forces infiltrate the sacred conclave.' One rumor, one planted dagger, one convenient corpse, and everything we read becomes ash. You know they'll try it. Sela heard them planning it."

"I heard them planning worse," Sela said quietly, and the room turned to her.

She'd been sitting near the stairs, arms wrapped around her knees, wearing a borrowed cloak two sizes too big because everything she owned was still in Hollowmere or confiscated along the road. When she spoke, she spoke carefully, the way she did when she was ashamed and refusing to show it. "The garrison commanders at Coldford. Their orders are dated for the week after solstice. If the Ashcloaks appear at the conclave—or are *made* to appear—the mobilization goes forward within days. Preemptive war. Blame the falling sky on Veyra's people, march before the other kingdoms finish arguing." She lifted her chin. "So no, Veyra. Your soldiers can't be within fifty miles of that spire. Not because Emrys says so. Because I read the dispatches myself, and I'm the reason you have them, and I'd rather be useful than forgiven."

Nobody said anything for a moment. Veyra studied her the way she studied fortifications.

"You betrayed the camp," Veyra said at last. "You sold our position to save your family. And now you're here."

"I sold a map," Sela said. "I didn't sell the people on it. I made sure the raid came at dawn, when the watch changed, when the escape route was open. I've done the arithmetic of what I am about a thousand times since, and I keep getting the same answer: I'm a coward who tried to buy everyone's life with the cheapest coin available, and the coin wasn't cheap enough, and I'll spend the rest of mine making up the difference. Judge me however helps you sleep." A pause. "But judge me accurately."

Something moved behind Veyra's eyes—recognition, maybe, of a fellow creature who had also done arithmetic with lives. "Accurately judged, then," she said. "Useful. Watched."

"I'd expect nothing else."

* * *

Which left Marrec, and Marrec was the problem I couldn't solve, because Marrec was the problem solving itself in directions I couldn't predict.

His superiors had ordered my death. I knew it; he knew I knew it; the knowledge sat between us at the table like a fourth cup nobody drank from. And yet he'd come to the cellar when Sela passed him the word—had come alone, unarmed, through a city crawling with his own colleagues, to sit among enemies and argue tactics for a plan whose entire purpose was to destroy the peace he'd spent his career defending.

"Why?" I finally asked him. Directly. In front of everyone. It was rude and I did it anyway. "You believe in the lie. You told me so—you told me peace requires it, that the valley's silence bought sixty years of children growing up without war. Everything I'm proposing tears that open. So why are you here?"

Marrec took his time answering. He was a man who treated questions like locked doors—he never jimmied them, he always found the actual key, even when the key cost him something.

"Because I went home," he said.

"Home?"

"After the ford. After your loyalists nearly killed you and I found myself standing over your body with my sword out wondering which side of it I was on." He smiled briefly, without humor. "I went back to the capital. I filed my reports. I sat in the chamber where my superiors gave me the new orders—your death, signed, sealed, very tidy—and I looked at the map on the wall. The one with Hollowmere on it. The valley I'd visited eleven times in twenty years, checking the wards, drinking bad wine with old Corvane, telling myself I was maintaining the peace." His voice stayed level, which somehow made it worse. "Do you know what I saw, looking at that map? I saw that I had been guarding a grave for twenty years and calling it a garden. And I realized the superiors giving the orders had never once asked what was *in* the valley. Only that it stay closed. Men who defend a lie they've never examined aren't defenders, Winterbourne. They're just the lock, rusting."

He set down his cup. "I don't believe your plan will work. I think it has perhaps one chance in five, and the failure modes are catastrophic. But I've seen the alternative now—the preemptive war, the scapegoats, the sky coming down over battlefields—and one chance in five beats certainty of ruin. So. Here I am. Reluctant, as advertised. Ask me for anything except confidence."

"Will they come for us at the conclave?" Sela asked. "Your colleagues?"

"Oh, certainly. I'm presumed rogue as of four days ago; there's a warrant with my face on it, badly drawn, which stings more than the treason honestly." He rubbed his jaw. "They'll have men inside the spire. They'll expect infiltration, sabotage, abduction of the heir. What they won't expect—" and here the ghost of the charming, menacing man from the guildhouse gate surfaced, the interrogator's smile, repurposed— "is that the infiltration will consist of monks reading aloud. Bureaucrats prepare for knives. Give them paperwork and they freeze. It's the one advantage we have, and it's thin, so we must not waste it."

* * *

We argued until the candles drowned in their own wax. We argued about the reading order—Ossian insisted the crime come first, unadorned, before any context could soften it; Veyra wanted the graves described in detail; Marrec, horribly, usefully, kept playing the delegations, voicing each crown's likely objection in turn so we could hear where the plan would bleed.

"The northern king will demand proof of the wards' anchoring," he said. "The southern regent will move to arrest the Choir the moment her own crimes surface—she'll claim forgery. The Ashcloak hardliners—" a glance at Veyra— "will want the hall seized the instant the theft is confirmed. And the Cindermarch host will simply want everyone quiet, because quiet is how they've survived forty years between larger neighbors."

"Then we let them want things," I said. "Out loud. In front of each other. That's the whole design, Marrec. For sixty years every faction has held its piece of the truth privately and spent it in the dark. We put the whole truth on the table at once and let them see each other holding nothing." I leaned forward. "A lie survives on privacy. That's its whole diet. Feed it witnesses and it starves."

"And if it doesn't starve?" Veyra asked. "If they draw steel in the hall?"

"Then the Choir keeps reading," I said. "That's the non-negotiable. Whatever happens—blades, arrests, fire—the reading does not stop. Ossian has sworn his monks to it. Four hundred years of neutrality buys them one thing: even men with drawn swords hesitate to strike a recorder mid-sentence. We fight, if we must, *around* the voice. Never at it."

Veyra stared at me for a long moment. "You've built a plan out of glass," she said finally. "One blow and it shatters."

"Yes," I said. "But glass has one property stone doesn't." I picked up the candle and held it so the light came through the empty tumbler beside it, throwing a crooked bright web across the ledger-stacked wall. "Light gets through. Stone keeps the light out and the lie warm. Glass lets everyone see everything—including the cracks. Including us. Especially us." I set the candle down. "I'm done building with stone."

* * *

Near dawn, when the arguments had worn themselves down to terms and contingencies, Sela fell asleep in her chair mid-sentence, chin dropping to her chest with the total surrender of the exhausted, and Veyra draped her own cloak over the girl without comment and dared anyone present to remark on it. Marrec sketched the spire's interior from memory on the back of a Choir ledger page—galleries, doors, the recorder's platform, the sightlines—his pen moving with the calm of a man drawing the floor plan of his own execution.

And I sat with the letter. Corvane's letter, soft at the folds now from handling, the coordinates, the name, the single line that had started all of this: *the valley was built on a grave.*

"He knew," I said quietly, to no one in particular. "Corvane. At the end. He said he chose me hoping I'd find a better answer than either side offered. This—" I tapped the letter— "this is what he was hoping for. Not a king. Not a rebellion. A reading."

"Then let's hope you're a better student than he was a liar," Veyra said, but there was no edge in it. She looked tired in a way she allowed no one to see, and younger, and for a moment I understood why ten years of war hadn't cured her of conviction: underneath the ruthlessness was a girl from a burned village who had never once been allowed to stop fighting, and some part of her was watching this glass plan the way a drowning woman watches a rope—desperate for it to hold, furious at needing it to.

"One condition," she said. "When the reading reaches the founding of the Ashcloaks—when it names what my order rose from and what we did rising—I stand in the hall and I don't flinch. And neither do you. Whatever it says about House Thorn. Whatever it says about *you*."

"Agreed," I said, and found, to my surprise, that I meant it. That was the strange alchemy of the last weeks: grief had burned off vanity like frost off a window. Whoever I'd been—Winterbourne, Oscar, heir, anchor—I mostly wanted, now, to be the man who told the truth in a room full of armed strangers and didn't stop talking when the swords came out.

Marrec rolled up his map. "Midwinter is nineteen days away," he said. "The Choir's copies need to be collated, the readers rehearsed, the routes in mapped, and the three of us need faces that aren't on warrants. I suggest we sleep. Or failing that, lie down and pretend, which is the diplomatic version."

We climbed back up through the scriptorium as the bells rang the hour before matins. At the top of the stairs, Ossian was waiting with tea, having evidently slept the way archives sleep—continuously, and on standby. He looked at the four of us emerging from his cellar, rumpled and wrung out and bound together by nothing sturdier than a shared refusal, and asked only:

"Well? Is it decided?"

"It's decided," I said. "At the solstice, the Choir reads everything. To everyone. At once."

The abbot nodded slowly, and there was something in his face that might have been hope, or might have been the expression of a man watching four people carry a full cup of water across a frozen lake.

"Nineteen days," he said. "Then we had best begin rehearsing. And Master Winterbourne—" he paused, teapot hovering, mild as morning— "you should know that yesterday's post brought a visitor to the gate. A royal courier, asking after a monk named Marrec. He left a letter. It's sealed with the privy signet, and it is addressed—" the old man's eyebrows rose a fraction— "to *all of you*."

Marrec went very still. Veyra's hand found her knife.

"From whom?" I asked.

Ossian set the letter on the table between us, and the wax seal caught the lamplight, red as an open wound.

"That," he said, "is the interesting part. It bears the seal of the Crown Regent of the South." A beat. "And it is signed, in her own hand, *I already know what you're planning.*"
