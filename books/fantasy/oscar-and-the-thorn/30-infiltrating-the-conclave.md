## 30. Infiltrating the Conclave

The trouble with disguises, Sela had told me once back in Hollowmere when we were fourteen and she talked me into sneaking into the miller's cellar, is that they only have to fail once.

I thought about that a great deal on the morning we walked through Cindermarch's gates dressed as archivists of the Silent Choir, because she was right then and she was right now, and the whole plan was built out of things that only needed to fail once to kill us all.

* * *

Cindermarch in winter was a city pretending it wasn't afraid. You could see it everywhere if you knew how to look—the way the bakeries had doubled their output and tripled their prices, the way every third window had a man behind it who never blinked, the way the conclave spire rose over everything like a needle someone had driven into the city's spine to keep it upright. Soldiers drilled in the squares. Heralds rode out at dawn and came back at dusk with mud on their boots and news nobody said aloud. The kingdoms were gathering for the solstice conclave the way clouds gather before weather, and everyone in the streets could feel the pressure drop.

We came in with the supply carts, four of us in grey wool robes cut plain and hooded deep, carrying archive cases that weighed exactly what real archive cases weigh—Maren's idea, delivered via a letter I still don't fully understand how she got to us. "Guards check what you carry," she'd written. "They never check what you've carried all day. Fill the cases with genuine paper and genuine inkstones and let them ache in your shoulders. Aching shoulders are the best credentials an archivist has."

So my shoulders ached beautifully.

Veyra walked beside me with her head bowed and her hands folded like a woman who had spent thirty years copying other people's arguments instead of starting wars. It was, I will admit, a convincing performance. It was also the strangest thing about the whole enterprise: Veyra Thorn, who could make a room full of armed men stand up just by entering it, playing the humble servant. She hated every minute of it. I could feel her hating it, the way you feel a fire burning in the next room.

Sela walked behind us, and Marrec—

Marrec was the plan. Or rather, Marrec was the flaw in the plan that we had decided to turn into its spine. Every disguise fails eventually; ours would fail the moment anyone looked closely at our faces, because three of us were wanted in two kingdoms and the fourth was me. But the Silent Choir had been granted safe passage to the conclave by ancient treaty, and the Choir traveled with a sworn escort drawn from any house that owed them a debt. And Dain Marrec, royal inquisitor, currently missing, presumed disgraced, owed the Silent Choir a debt from a night in the moorlands he didn't talk about and I didn't ask about.

He walked at our flank in his old officer's coat, cleaned and mended, wearing the face of a man doing an unpleasant duty competently. Which, to be fair, was simply his face.

"Remember," he'd said the night before, in the cellar where we'd rehearsed this until midnight, "if we're stopped, you say nothing. Not because you can't lie—I've heard you try—but because your lies are honest ones. You look guilty when you're innocent. It's a gift."

"Thank you," I said.

"It wasn't a compliment."

"I know. I'm choosing to take it as one."

Something that might have been the ghost of amusement crossed his face and died there. He'd been dying by inches since the orders changed—since the letter from his superiors that ordered him to erase me rather than retrieve me, and that he had shown us instead of obeying. He hadn't explained why. I suspected he couldn't explain it even to himself. Some men break loudly. Marrec seemed to be breaking very quietly, in a direction none of us had predicted, holding himself together mostly out of spite toward the people who had made him.

The gate guards stopped us anyway.

Of course they did. The gods of bad luck are diligent.

* * *

There were two of them, young, cold-reddened, bored in the way soldiers are bored right up until the moment they are extremely not. One held out a hand for our papers. Marrec produced them—a writ of passage under Choir seal, names listed not as ours but as the archivists they officially belonged to, Brother Aldous and company, traveling under escort.

The guard read it twice. Then he looked up. At me.

I did what I'd practiced. I looked at nothing, hunched into the robe, adjusted the case strap digging into my shoulder, and became the most tedious person in Cindermarch. Inside my chest my heart was conducting some kind of percussion rehearsal, but outside I was gray wool and mildew and tedium.

"These seals," the guard said slowly. "They're old-style. My sergeant said—"

"My sergeant says many things," Marrec said.

It wasn't rude. That was the artistry of it. It was the tone of a tired professional correcting a junior in front of strangers—not enough to humiliate him, just enough to make him want the conversation to end. The guard's ears went pink. His partner suddenly found the cart queue fascinating.

"The Choir's writ predates your sergeant," Marrec went on, mildly, folding the paper away. "Predates this garrison. Predates, if memory serves, the wall you're standing against. If the crown wishes to revise the treaty terms, it may do so after the solstice, in writing, with the Abbot's countersignature. Until then—" he gestured, courteous, exhausted, "—we have a reading to prepare for, and the Archivist Elect does not forgive lateness. She once made a duke copy out the genealogy of a horse. By hand. For a fortnight."

I nearly broke cover right there. I had to bite the inside of my cheek and think about Corvane's ledger handwriting, which could cure insomnia at ten paces.

The guard stepped aside. We passed under the gate, into the shadow of the wall, out the other side into Cindermarch proper, and only when we'd gone fifty paces did Sela exhale beside me, a long controlled breath through her nose.

"The horse story," she whispered. "Was that true?"

"No," Marrec said.

"Then why did he believe it?"

"Because it sounded true, and because he wanted to believe anything that let him stop talking to me." He glanced sideways at her. "That's most of statecraft, if you're wondering. The rest is stamps."

Veyra said nothing at all. But when Marrec turned forward again, she looked at the back of his head for a long moment with an expression I couldn't name—something between appraisal and grief, as if she were watching a bridge burn in a direction she hadn't expected the fire to travel.

* * *

The Silent Choir's guesthouse stood in the shadow of the spire itself, a low stone building that had clearly once been a granary and now smelled of lamp oil, dust, and the particular silence of people who have taken vows about interrupting each other. The brothers and sisters received us without questions—which was the entire point of the Choir. They had sworn, centuries ago, to record history without judging it, and their vows apparently extended to not judging four obvious fugitives smuggled in among them either. A sister named Osanna showed us to a cell with four pallets, set down a tray of bread and dried apples, and left without having spoken a word.

"She likes us," Sela said.

"You can tell?"

"She gave us the good apples. The mealy ones are for people she doesn't like. I watched her sort them."

That evening the plan assembled itself around the table in our cell, lit by one candle, because more light invited eyes, and eyes were the thing Cindermarch had too many of.

The conclave spire was not one building but five, grown together over centuries like the rings of a tree: the public hall at the base where delegations argued in front of anyone who cared to listen; above it, the council ring where the actual decisions happened behind doors that opened only for rank; above that, the archive levels—our destination—where the Choir kept its master records, including, thanks to the Abbot, a sealed case containing the complete Winterbourne correspondence, the Thorn War casualty rolls, the founding charters of Hollowmere, and every document proving what my valley was built on and who I was.

Above that, the reading chamber. And above everything, the lantern at the top of the spire, which the Choir lit on the winter solstice and kept lit while they read the year's record aloud to whoever would listen—a tradition so old that no kingdom had ever dared forbid it, because forbidding the Choir's reading would be admitting there was something in it to fear.

"At sundown on the solstice," Marrec said, tapping the map, "the reading begins. By tradition it opens with the oldest record in the collection. This year—" a pause, the smallest hesitation, the kind of tell I'd learned to catch in him, "—the oldest record in the collection is a land grant from the Thorn War era. Filed last month by the Abbot. With the full annexes."

Veyra smiled. It was not a warm smile. "He filed it early."

"He filed it where no one could suppress it without breaking a rule older than any crown in that hall." Marrec tapped the archive level. "Our task is simpler and worse. Between now and sundown tomorrow, the case must be moved from the archive to the reading chamber, verified, and entered into the order of reading. Normally six brothers do this over three days. We have one evening, because tomorrow morning the delegations arrive and the spire fills with people who know each other's faces—and ours."

"And the verification," I said. "The rite. I still think it's mad that the security on the truth is a poem."

"It's not a poem," Marrec said. "It's a memory test. The verifying reader must recite the record's opening lines from memory, in Old Thorn dialect, without error, in front of two witnesses. No paper allowed. The Choir reasons—and I've come to agree—that a forger can copy anything, but only someone who has truly lived with a record can carry it in their head." He looked at me across the candle. "You learned the dialect from lullabies. You've had the opening lines for a week. You'll do."

"I'll do," I agreed, "and then vomit in a corner somewhere discreet."

"There's a courtyard," Sela offered helpfully. "I found it earlier. Very discreet. Excellent drainage."

* * *

We moved at the change of the night bell, when the spire's own guards rotated and the corridors belonged to the Choir and to anyone bold or stupid enough to walk like they belonged there.

Boldness, it turns out, is mostly posture.

The archive stair wound up through the second ring of the spire, past offices where tired clerks copied by lamplight, past a landing where two sisters were arguing in whispers about whether a disputed treaty clause from 211 should be cross-indexed under "borders" or "broken promises"—a debate, I gathered, of several decades' standing. Nobody challenged us. Our robes were right, our cases were heavy, our shoulders drooped with authentic exhaustion, and the deepest magic of any disguise is that people see what the setting tells them to see. In a spire of archivists, gray robes are invisible.

Until the third landing.

A man was waiting there. Not a guard—an usher of the conclave, wearing the silver chain of the delegation staff, with the polished, patient face of someone whose entire career was noticing things. He stepped into the stairwell ahead of us and raised one hand, pleasantly.

"Brothers. A moment. The archive levels are closed tonight—preparation for the reading. All transfers go through the Office of Ceremonies."

Marrec stopped. So did we, in a well-practiced stagger, cases shifting, robes rustling.

"Closed by whose order?" Marrec asked.

"The Lord Chamberlain's."

"Ah." Marrec produced the writ again, unhurried. "By treaty, the Choir answers to the Abbot within these walls, and the Abbot alone. The Chamberlain may request. He may not close." He held the paper out. "You're welcome to read it. Article nine. There's also a footnote about penalties, but it's in Latin Thorne and frankly it's depressing."

The usher read. His eyes moved down the page. Somewhere below us, the night bell finished its changes and the tower settled into that enormous quiet that old buildings keep, like breath held.

"This seal," the usher said. "This is a military countersignature."

"It is."

"Whose?"

"Mine," said Marrec, "when I held rank. The writ is old. The debt it certifies is older. Ask the Abbot—he'll confirm it, though I'd advise against making him climb stairs to do it. He's seventy-four and vindictive about being interrupted."

And here was the moment I will remember longer than almost anything else from those years: the usher looked up from the paper, looked at Marrec properly, and I saw the flicker—the tiny, fatal flicker of recognition. Because Dain Marrec had sat in war councils and court chambers for twenty years. His face had been on proclamations. And the proclamations currently posted in Cindermarch described him as dismissed, possibly coerced, to be detained on sight.

The usher's mouth opened.

"Ser Marrec," he said quietly. Not accusingly. Almost reverently, the way you speak to a famous man you're about to ruin by naming. "They said you were—"

"They said a great many things," Marrec said. "Most of them were written by people who have never read article nine." He leaned in, and dropped his voice to something private and weary and absolutely certain. "Inquisitor Ferren of the Crown delegation will be asking for you tomorrow. He'll ask what you saw on the archive stairs tonight. When he does—you saw four Choir archivists, escorted, unremarkable, and a senior officer executing an old writ. That is what happened. That is all that happened. And if you find, tomorrow, that telling it straight costs you something—" he straightened, "—then consider what it costs the rest of us to stand here telling it at all."

Silence. The candle on the landing guttered in a draft from the stairwell.

The usher folded his hands. Stepped aside.

"Third floor," he said. "The case you want is in alcove twelve. The verifying witnesses will be Brother Caedmon and Sister Osanna—they've been told to expect you." And then, softer, as we passed him, almost to himself: "My grandmother came from the marches near Hollowmere. Before the wards. She used to say the stars over the valley were wrong."

I stopped walking. I couldn't help it—one step, half a heartbeat, my body betraying me the way bodies do.

"They were," I said, without turning around. "Someone fixed them recently. Mostly."

I felt him staring at my back. I kept walking.

* * *

Alcove twelve was exactly what Maren's letters had promised: a recessed shelf behind a curtain of oiled canvas, holding a long case of black wood bound in iron, stamped with the Choir's open-book sigil and the Abbot's personal mark. Beside it lay the order-of-reading scroll, fresh-inked, and there it was in the formal hand of the Choir scribes:

*Item the First: Grant of Lands, Year of Ash 212, with Annexes A through K, per the Abbot's certification.*

Annexes A through K. Eleven documents. The theft. The forged peace. The graves. Me.

Sela put her hand flat on the case lid, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal you weren't sure was sleeping. "It's heavier than it looks," she said.

"That's the iron."

"That's not the iron."

She was right. We both knew she was right. Some weights don't register on scales.

Veyra stood apart, arms folded, looking at the case with an expression I had learned to fear slightly more than her anger: calculation. I watched her watch the box that contained her family's stolen heir and her rebellion's best weapon, and I saw her doing arithmetic. Eleven annexes. One reading chamber. Ten thousand listeners, maybe more—delegations, soldiers, spies, the crowd in the square below. Once Item the First was read aloud, there was no version of events afterward in which House Thorn's grievance stayed buried. Whatever else happened, her cause would walk out of that chamber alive.

Whether I did was a separate column in her ledger.

"We move it at dawn bell," Marrec said, checking the corridor. "Two of us carry, one leads, one watches the rear. Same rules as tonight. And Emrys—" he turned, and in the lamplight his face was hollowed and serious, "—tonight, learn the annexes' order. Tomorrow, once the reading starts, you won't get a second chance to correct it."

I sat down on the floor of the alcove with the scroll in my lap and did what I had done every night for a week: recited, in the dialect my mother had sung to a stolen child who didn't know he was stolen, the opening lines of the oldest lie in the valley I had called home.

*Here is given ground to the wardens of the peace, that the thorn be tended and the root kept dark—*

Sela listened. Veyra listened. Even Marrec, who pretended he didn't care about anything except procedure, listened.

And far above us, through five rings of stone, I could feel—or imagined I could feel—the lantern waiting at the top of the spire, unlit, empty, patient. Tomorrow at sundown they would carry flame up those stairs, and the flame would meet the record, and the record would meet the world.

Below us, faint through the walls, the bells began marking the middle of the night.

And underneath the bells—so faint I might have dreamed it, so faint that I told no one until much later—I heard something else. Footsteps on the archive stair below. Many of them. Moving fast, in step, in armor.

Coming up.
