## 31. The Reading Begins

### Emrys

There is a particular silence that falls over a hall full of powerful people when they realize they are about to hear something they cannot unhear. I had imagined shouting. I had prepared myself for shouting—Veyra had drilled us on contingencies for shouting, for guards, for someone recognizing Marrec's face and calling for his head before the first page was turned.

What I got instead was quiet. Four hundred delegates from nine crowns, three orders, and one very nervous conclave of neutral guilds, all leaning forward at once, like a field of wheat bending under the same wind.

The Silent Choir read standing. That was their whole liturgy, Abbot Oren had told us on the road: *we stand because history deserves witnesses on their feet.* There were seven of them, robed in undyed grey, faces bare, voices trained over decades to carry without strain. The eldest—a woman named Sorrel with hands like folded paper—held the first folio as if it were a sleeping bird.

"The record of the Thorn War's ending," she began, "as kept by the Choir of Cindermarch, who judge nothing and omit nothing."

I stood at the back beside Sela, disguised in archivist blue that itched like a moral failing. Beside me, Veyra wore the same disguise, which fooled no one who had ever seen her speak, but so far no one had looked past the front of the room. Everyone was looking at Sorrel.

"Entry the first. Winter of the war's last year. A delegation of House Thorn, including the infant heir, travels under truce banner to the border vale later called Hollowmere. The truce is violated by parties unknown. All in the delegation are killed."

A rustle went through the eastern benches. The Thorn loyalists knew this part. It was their founding grief, polished and carried for sixty years.

Sorrel turned the page. She did not hurry. The Choir never hurried; that was the weapon of them, if you could call it a weapon. Patience, read aloud.

"Entry the second. The infant heir is not among the dead."

The rustle stopped. You could feel the hall recalculating. Somewhere to my left a man began breathing through his mouth, audibly, like a bellows with a hole in it.

"Entry the third. The warden-corps of the vale, acting under seal of the allied crowns, takes charge of the child. Entry the fourth. The allied crowns, exhausted by eleven years of war and unable to produce the heir alive without reopening claims, agree that the child will be raised nameless. Entry the fifth. The peace is signed. The valley is warded. The wards are anchored—" here Sorrel paused, and I felt her find me in the back of the room, though her eyes never left the page, "—anchored to a living descendant of House Thorn."

*To me,* she did not say. She didn't have to. The arithmetic was doing itself in four hundred heads at once, and I could hear it: the creak and grind of old assumptions being forced through new sums.

An ambassador from the Crown of Aldery rose. "This document is a forgery," he announced, with the confidence of a man who had decided that before entering the building. "The Choir has no authority—"

"The Choir has no authority," Sorrel agreed mildly. "That is rather the point of us. We have records. Shall I continue?"

He sat down. It was either that or shout, and shouting would have meant admitting the reading mattered.

She continued. Entry after entry. The forged casualty lists. The payments routed through guild treasuries to keep wardens silent—my guild, my lamp-oil money, sixty years of my life funded out of a grave. The order, dated the winter of my birth year, instructing the Warden of Hollowmere that "the vessel of the anchor is to be kept ignorant of its nature." *The vessel of the anchor.* That was me. That was the word they'd used for a baby. For me.

Sela's hand found mine in the dark behind the bench. Her fingers were freezing. Hers always were, in moments that mattered; I'd known her twenty years and her courage ran cold-blooded, like a lizard's.

Then came the graves.

Sorrel described them by number and coordinate, because grief reduced to coordinates is somehow worse than grief described—the delegation of forty-one, buried in the vale's north hollow beneath what became an orchard. Children in Hollowmere had picked apples off those graves for generations. I had climbed that orchard wall at nine years old and stolen fruit with Sela while Corvane pretended not to see.

I had stolen apples off a mass grave. I put my hand flat against the bench until the floor stopped tilting.

And then Sorrel said the thing we had argued about for three nights whether to include, the line Marrec had insisted on and Veyra had fought against and the Abbot had settled with a single sentence: "The record names the signatories of the concealment. All nine crowns now seated in this hall appear upon it."

Silence. The real kind, the kind with weight.

Then everything happened at once.

### Veyra

I have waited thirty years for a room to break open, and I will tell you what no one believes until they see it: rooms do not break open like thunderclaps. They break open like ice on a spring river—one crack, then another, then the whole surface groaning and heaving and going under, and everyone standing on it suddenly swimming.

The first crack was Lord Aldery's chancellor, on his feet demanding the document be surrendered for authentication—which was a demand that it be taken away from everyone else, and every other crown understood it instantly, and three more ambassadors rose to object before he finished speaking.

The second crack was Queen Isolde of Merrow's envoy, who said, very quietly and therefore very loudly, into the sudden hush: "If the record is false, why did our own treasury disburse these sums? The ledger numbers match ours exactly."

I had planned for this moment the way farmers plan for rain—not whether it would come but whether I'd be ready when it did. I had speeches drafted. I had Emrys positioned, the heir revealed at the proper dramatic instant, the room turning to him like flowers to sun.

I rose anyway. Old habit. And I watched, from the corner of my eye, Emrys stay seated.

That should have warned me. Everything I needed to know about how this day would end was in the fact that I rose and he didn't. But conviction is a kind of blindness, and mine has always been total. I stood in my borrowed archivist blue, threw back the hood, and let four hundred people recognize the face they'd been taught to fear.

"I am Veyra Thorn," I said, "last daughter of the house whose graves were planted with orchards. Every word that woman reads is true, and I can prove the parts you'll claim are forged."

What I wanted, in that moment, was simple and enormous. I wanted the hall to turn to me the way it turns to power. I wanted the chaos to resolve into a shape with my face on it, because I had spent my whole life believing that chaos is only order that hasn't found its owner yet.

For about ten seconds, it worked. Delegates craned toward me. Two of my people, planted in the galleries, began the low rhythmic chant we'd rehearsed—*Thorn remembers, Thorn returns*—and I felt the room's fear begin to organize itself around a name. My name.

Then the western doors opened, and a contingent of Crown marshals entered in formation, and the chant died, and I understood that someone else had also planned for this day, and had planned better.

### Dain Marrec

I want it noted—I want it recorded somewhere, by someone, since apparently I've become a man who cares about the historical record—that I did not let the marshals in. I had vouched for this operation with my commission, my freedom, and quite possibly my neck, and I had done it because a dying warden once told me that a lie held up for sixty years isn't peace, it's just a debt accruing interest. I believed the truth should be heard. I still do.

But my superiors in the Crown Inquisition had their own reading of events, and their reading was: control the room or end the session. They sent twelve marshals with sealed writs authorizing "disruption of seditious assembly." I recognized the phrasing. I had drafted similar phrasing myself, in happier years, for smaller lies.

So there I stood, in archivist blue that fit me badly, watching the marshals fan out along the walls, and I made a decision in about a second and a half, which is roughly my career average.

I stepped out of the row, walked to the nearest marshal captain—a young man named Pell whom I'd trained myself—and said, pleasantly, "Those writs are superseded. Conclave ground is neutral by treaty. Check the precedence clauses."

Pell hesitated. Good boy. He checked.

While he checked, Sorrel kept reading. That's the thing about the Choir that no strategist ever accounts for: you cannot rush them and you cannot frighten them, because they have already accepted death as a working condition of the job. She was on the entries describing the cover-up's maintenance—the wardens' oaths rewritten, the letters burned, the annual "audit" that was actually a bribe—and every delegate from every crown was hearing the machinery of their own government described in a stranger's calm voice.

"Entry the ninth," Sorrel read. "The warden Corvane, last of his line of office, petitions the Choir to hold this record against the day it can be safely spoken. His petition is granted."

My chest did something complicated. I had hunted that man across two provinces. I had burned his guildhouse—no. His people had. I had ordered the fire that flushed him into the night where he took the wounds that killed him, wounds taken buying time for the letter, for the boy, for all of this. And now his name was going into the permanent record as what he actually was, and there was no version of my orders that had allowed for that outcome, and I found I was glad.

Then the far doors opened too, and the Ashcloak hardliners came in the way hardliners always come in—fast, armed, certain—and I realized the room now contained three kinds of certainty and one old woman reading, and only one of those things was holding.

### Emrys

Here is what it sounded like, from the back, when the hall broke:

Shouting, yes, finally—but shouting in nine accents, overlapping, none of it coordinated, each crown convinced the others had engineered the whole revelation for advantage. The Aldery delegation accused the Merrow delegation of forging the treasury evidence; Merrow pointed out, reasonably, that the evidence incriminated Merrow too, which nobody found reassuring. Someone threw a goblet. An actual goblet, arcing through the light of the conclave spire's great lamps, and hitting a delegate from the Glasswrights' Guild, who had come neutral and would leave radicalized, though I didn't know that yet.

Through it all, impossibly, Sorrel read.

Her voice wasn't loud. It didn't need to be. The Choir had chosen this hall—or rather, the hall had been built around the Choir's needs centuries ago, acoustics designed so that a single unhurried voice reaches every seat. The founders of Cindermarch had built the room for exactly this day, six hundred years before anyone knew it was coming, and I thought: *someone always knows. Someone is always keeping the record against the day.*

The hardliners reached the floor. Veyra's soldiers—her real ones, the ones who'd stopped believing in her plan and started believing in their own—moved to seize the dais where the senior delegations sat. Marshals moved to stop them. Marrec moved between them, hands up, saying something I couldn't hear but could lip-read well enough: *"Stand down, both of you, you're proving her right—"*

Sela grabbed my sleeve. "It's going now or never. The gallery broadcast—the Choir's relays—we need the Abbot's key and we need it before someone cuts the lines."

The plan, such as it was, had a second layer. The reading wasn't only for the hall. Oren and his monks had rigged the spire's signal-bells—the same relay system that carried conclave decrees to every major city—to transmit the reading live, verbatim, to anyone listening. By sundown, every capital within range would have heard the whole record whether or not the crowns suppressed the transcript. Truth as weather: you couldn't arrest it, only get wet.

We pushed through the panicking gallery toward the bell-chamber stair. Behind us I heard Veyra's voice rise above the din, and I knew without looking that she was claiming the moment—declaring something, pointing at the dais or at me—and I felt the plan's fragile glass beginning to sing under pressure.

At the stair door, a marshal barred the way, then squinted at my face, then at Marrec's over my shoulder, and his eyes went wide with recognition twice over.

"You," he said. "Both of you are—"

Marrec sighed the sigh of a man whose disguises never survive contact with subordinates. "Pell. We discussed the writs."

"The writs were countermanded an hour ago, sir. From above." Pell's hand moved to his sword, and his expression was genuinely sorry. "New orders came down while you were in there. Sir—they're not retrieval orders anymore."

The bells overhead began to toll the reading forward to the next city, and Pell said the words that ended the world I'd been standing in:

"They're silence orders. And you're on the list."

* * *

Behind us, Sorrel's voice went on and on, patient as water, reading the dead back into the world while the living tore the room apart trying to decide what the truth was worth—and to whom.
