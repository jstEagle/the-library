## 34. Blades and Bells

### Emrys

I have been in exactly three fights in my life, and the first two were over a card game with Sela's cousin Tam, who cheats at everything including honesty. So when Veyra's lieutenant drew steel on the conclave floor and someone screamed "seize them," my contribution to the defense of truth and justice was to fall over a bench.

I'd like it noted that I fell *toward* the archive table.

The reading had reached its eleventh hour. Brother Callum of the Silent Choir stood at the center of the conclave spire with his voice like a plumb line dropped into deep water—steady, unhurried, indifferent to the storm gathering around him—and he was reading the testimony of a woman dead two generations: *"...and the child was carried north by the founding Warden, and the grave at Hollowmere was dug for the mother, who did not survive her."*

That was when Veyra moved.

I saw it happen the way you see weather change—no single moment, just a pressure shift. Her hardliners, the ones she'd stationed at the doors as "honor guard," turned inward. Her hand came up. And her voice, that voice that could make a starving camp believe in morning, rang out over the hall:

"The Kingdom of Thorn is restored! The heir stands among you!"

Every eye in the spire found me. I was still on the floor next to a bench, holding a candlestick I had grabbed for no defensible reason.

"No," I said. It came out smaller than I wanted. I got up. "No. That's not what this is."

"Emrys—" Veyra's face was radiant, certain, terrible. She believed it down to the marrow; that was always the horror of her. "This is your moment. Take it."

"I'm not a moment," I said. "I'm a person. There's a difference, and it matters."

Lieutenant Hask—the scarred one, the one who'd looked at me all through the reading like a man watching kindling arrange itself—drew his sword. Not at me. At the nearest crown delegation, the Cindermarch envoys, who were already on their feet shouting about treason.

And then the whole floor went up like a lamp knocked off a shelf.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about a riot in a room built for ceremony: it is loud, and then it is very quiet in patches, and the patches are worse. Between the screams there were these small pockets of silence where individual things happened—a chair going over, a man saying "please" in a way that meant he knew it wouldn't help—and each one arrived in my ears with horrible clarity, like the world had decided I needed to hear every detail separately.

Veyra's soldiers were moving on the delegations. The delegations' guards were drawing. Somewhere above us the great solstice bell began ringing—not the measured toll of ceremony but fast, panicked strokes, some clerk with more sense than anyone pulling the alarm rope.

And through all of it, Brother Callum kept reading.

I don't know how to explain what that was like. He didn't raise his voice. He didn't stop. A sword fight broke out eight feet from him and he turned the page. His voice threaded through the chaos like a bell rope through a fire—*"...and the peace was signed upon a theft, and sealed upon a grave..."*—and men who were mid-swing would falter, just slightly, because you cannot entirely unhear a thing like that.

The archive. The records were on the central table, and if the table burned or the pages scattered, sixty years of buried truth went back to being rumor. Sela had already seen it. Of course she had. While I was falling over furniture, she was already moving.

"Sela!" I shouted, but she was gone, cutting across the floor toward the table with a fire iron from the sconce held like she'd been waiting her whole life for an excuse.

I went after her, which is how I ended up in my third fight ever, except this one was with an actual soldier, and he did not cheat at cards, he just hit people.

### Marrec

I have broken oaths before. It is not a habit, but it is not unprecedented either. What I had never done until that night was draw my blade against my own column while wearing their colors, and I will tell you honestly: the hardest part wasn't the fighting. The hardest part was Corporal Bren's face when he realized who was standing between him and the archivist's table.

"Inquisitor?" he said. Actually said it, mid-advance, like a man checking whether he'd walked into the wrong room.

"Bren." I kept my point low. Give them a door and most of them will take it; that's the first lesson of ending fights instead of winning them. "Walk away. Go home. Whatever they're paying you, I'll see it doubled out of my own purse."

"They told us the heir was being taken—"

"They told you a story. I've read the same story, Bren. I wrote part of it." I smiled, because a smile disarms faster than steel and costs less. "Go home."

He might have. I think he might have. But Hask was behind him, and Hask had been promised a kingdom instead of a wage, and kingdoms make men stupid in ways money never does.

"Hold the line, corporal," Hask said, and it stopped being a conversation.

Three of them. I'd trained two of them personally, which meant I knew Bren favored his left knee since the moorland march and that young Pell telegraphed every cut like a town crier announcing it. Fighting your own students is either easy or unbearable depending on how much you let yourself feel during it. I chose later. Later is where I keep most things.

We went around the archive table in a slow, grinding circle, and above us the bell kept hammering, and under everything, steady as bedrock, the Choir brother's voice:

*"...and the Warden wept as he rode, for he knew what peace cost..."*

Pell broke off first—broke *off*, literally, his blade skidding along mine and burying itself in the table's edge an inch from the open record. I put him down gently, which is to say I hit him with the pommel rather than the edge, and he folded up like laundry. Bren took a cut on the forearm and lost his taste for it. Hask did not lose his taste for anything; Hask fought the way avalanches travel, committed past the point of sense.

"You believe this rubbish?" he snarled at me. "A stolen heir? Old graves? You're an *inquisitor*. We bury things. That's the job."

"It was the job," I said. "I've resigned. Rather recently. Mid-fight, technically."

He came at me then with everything, no craft, all fury, and I gave ground step by step because the table was behind me and behind the table was a boy from a hidden valley holding a candlestick like a scepter, and somewhere in the last month—God help me—I had started thinking of Emrys Winterbourne as a thing worth standing in front of.

Hask's blade got past me once. Just once. I'll carry the scar on the shoulder and be grateful, because where it landed was nowhere that mattered, and because the sound Emrys made when he brought that candlestick down on Hask's skull was the least dignified noise I have ever heard a hero produce.

Hask went down. The bell tolled. The reading went on.

*"...and the child grew, and knew nothing, and the knowing was taken from him too..."*

"Nice of you to join," I said to Emrys.

"I've had the candlestick the whole time," he said, shaking. "In case you're keeping score."

"I am. It's not flattering."

### Sela

The archive door was oak, old oak, older than any kingdom represented in that room, and it weighed about as much as sin. It opened onto the stairwell where the Silent Choir kept their master copies, and I got to it four seconds before four of Veyra's hardliners did, which in a knife fight is roughly a lifetime.

I put my back against it and my fire iron up and thought, with a strange calm, *Mam is going to kill me if I die here.*

Not the crown. Not Veyra. Mam.

"Move aside," the first one said. He wasn't cruel about it. That was the worst part of Veyra's people—they weren't cruel, they were *certain*, and certainty doesn't negotiate.

"This door stays shut," I said.

"There's a war happening."

"Not through this door there isn't."

They came at me the way water comes at a dam—patient first, then not. I'll say this for mill work: hauling sacks of grain up stairs builds shoulders, and dodging Tam's cheating cousins builds instincts. I caught the first blade on the iron and felt the shock go all the way to my teeth. The second man grabbed my arm. I bit him. I'm not proud. I'm also not sorry.

What saved me wasn't strength. It was the bell overhead and the voice underneath it, both refusing to stop, and the way it made even Veyra's soldiers hesitate between blows—half a heartbeat of listening, every time, like the words were pulling at something they'd buried in themselves. Half a heartbeat is enough to keep a door shut, if you spend them right.

Then hands pulled the soldiers off me from behind, and it was monks. Monks! Grey-robed, soft-handed, sworn-to-silence monks, hitting people with candlestands, and the look on their faces was the politest violence I have ever witnessed. One of them nodded to me—an apology, I think, for the breach of decorum—and stepped into my place at the door.

I should have run. Every sensible instinct said run, find Emrys, get out. But through the closing gap I could see the reading table, and Callum turning another page, and I understood suddenly what Emrys had understood weeks ago on a cold road with a letter in his coat: some things are only true if somebody stays to say them out loud.

So I stayed. I picked up my fire iron and I held the door, and above me the bell rang, and behind me the stairwell full of master copies sat safe in the dark, and in front of me the voice went on:

*"...and let it be written that the valley called Hollowmere was raised over a mother's grave, and called peace..."*

My arms were shaking. My eyes were stinging. I held the door.

### Emrys

It ended the way storms end—not with resolution but with exhaustion. The crown guards rallied around their delegations. Veyra's hardliners, finding the hall not rising to join them but staring at them in horror, fell back to the western wall in a bristling knot around their lieutenant. And Veyra herself stood in the middle of the floor, alone, in the exact spot where she'd declared her kingdom, and the kingdom declined to materialize.

Because Callum was still reading. He had reached the final testimony—the founding Warden's own hand, the confession Corvane's family had guarded for six decades—and his voice filled the ruined hall like light fills a cracked window:

*"...and I stole the child not for crown nor guild, but because I could not bear the war, and I told myself the lie was mercy. Let whoever finds this judge me truly. Mercy that requires a grave is only theft with better manners."*

Silence. Real silence, the whole spire of it, broken only by the bell slowing above us—toll... toll... toll—like a heart coming down from panic.

Veyra looked at me across the wreckage. There was blood on her cheek that wasn't hers, and her eyes were wet and furious, and for one unguarded moment she looked less like a queen than like a girl at a mass grave who had promised the dead she'd fix everything.

"You could have ended it," she said quietly. "Tonight. With a word."

"A word isn't an ending," I said. "It's just a louder beginning. Ask the war."

Her jaw tightened. Behind her, Hask—conscious again, furious again, held up by two soldiers—was saying something low and poisonous to the men around him, and I watched Veyra hear it without wanting to, watched the certainty reassemble itself on her face brick by brick.

She looked at the archive table. At the records. At Callum, serene amid the ruin.

"If the truth won't crown a king," she said, so softly only our little group could hear it, "then it will burn one in."

Marrec heard it too. I felt him go still beside me.

"Veyra," I started—

But she was already turning, already giving orders in that low carrying voice, and her hardliners were already moving—not at the delegations now, not at us, but at the walls, the braziers, the old dry hangings of a spire built from centuries of ceremonial timber.

Sela saw it from the door. I saw her see it. And the bell above us tolled once more, wrong somehow, arrhythmic, and Brother Callum turned the last page and began, in that calm drowned-water voice, to read the names.

All of them. Every name in the graves. Starting with a woman called Thorn, who died in a valley that forgot her, whose son I might have been.

The first brazier went over.

"Emrys," Marrec said, drawing steel again, "I hope you've grown fond of that candlestick."

I gripped it tighter. Above us the sky shuddered—somewhere far off, the misaligned stars were grinding against their wrongness—and below us the fire began to spread, and between the two, one old monk's voice went on naming the dead, and I ran toward the flames, because somebody had to keep the reading alive.

Somebody always has to stay and say it out loud.
