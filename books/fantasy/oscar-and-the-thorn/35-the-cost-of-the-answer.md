## 35. The Cost of the Answer

I have read that the moment before a battle is the quietest moment there is. Whoever wrote that never stood in a conclave hall while a voice recited sixty years of buried crimes and a woman with a crown-shaped hunger decided to stop listening.

The reading had reached its middle chapters—the ones about the forged peace, the graves at Cael's Ford—when Veyra rose from her seat among the Ashcloak delegation and said, in a voice pitched to carry to every corner of the spire: "You have heard the truth. Now hear who has the right to answer for it."

I knew what was coming before she finished the sentence. I think half the hall did. What none of us knew was how fast it would come, or how much of it I would have to carry out of this room on my conscience.

"Veyra." I stood too. My voice came out smaller than hers. "Don't."

She looked at me then, and here is the thing I will spend the rest of my life trying to explain to myself: she didn't look like a tyrant. She looked like a woman standing at last in the place where all her griefs pointed, holding out her hand as if the whole ruined century might finally take it.

"They stole you," she said gently. "They stole your name, your mother, your dead. And now you want to hand the answer back to *them*—to a room full of thieves arguing over the terms?"

"I want to hand it to everyone," I said. "That was the point."

"That was the *risk*," she said, and drew the old Thorn banner from beneath her cloak—not a weapon, just cloth, but cloth that meant war—and the hardliners along the eastern wall rose with her, and the delegations began shouting at once, and somewhere above us a bell started ringing because even bells know when a thing is breaking.

* * *

What happened next happened in pieces, and I have assembled them since from other people's memories, because mine only kept the parts where I was failing.

Piece one: Sela, at the archive door, planting herself in front of it with her arms spread wide and shouting "The reading does not stop!" at men three times her size, and being right so loudly that they hesitated. The Choir monk inside never faltered. His voice went on through the smoke and the screaming, level as water: *"...and the founding warden did seal the child away, and did forge the border wards against his return, and did name the lie 'peace,' and peace it was, and peace it cost..."*

Piece two: Marrec moving—not toward me, exactly, more *through* me, the way weather moves—putting himself between me and the crossbow bolt that one of Veyra's lieutenants loosed into the chaos without orders or, possibly, with exactly the kind of orders men give themselves when history is happening and they want a piece of it. I heard the impact. It sounded like an axe going into wet wood, which is a sound I will not describe further, because I have decided some things stay outside the story.

He caught me as we both went down. That was the strange part. He'd taken a bolt through the shoulder and he caught me anyway, the way he'd been catching things all year—my lies, my escapes, my terrible plans—with the weary competence of a man whose job had always been holding up things that should have fallen.

"You're supposed to be arresting me," I said stupidly.

"Resigned," he grunted. "Didn't you hear? Terrible pension." Blood came through his fingers where he pressed his shoulder. "Get behind the lectern. Stay small."

Piece three: Veyra's own lieutenant—Corren, her shadow for twenty years, the man who'd carried her wounded off two fields—stepping into her path at the head of the eastern stair, sword drawn, face grey with something worse than doubt.

"Stand aside, Corren," Veyra said. "We take the hall. We take the *story*. They'll kneel to a living heir before midwinter—"

"No," Corren said. "Not this way. You told us we fought for the dead. This is fighting *for* you, and the dead can't consent to it."

And Veyra—Veyra looked at him the way you look at a door closing, knowing you built the door yourself—and I saw her decide. Not to yield. Never that. But to go forward into whatever came, alone if necessary, because she had spent her whole life being alone in exactly this way, and the tragedy of Veyra Thorn was never that she wanted too much. It was that no one could ever show her that wanting it *this way* would leave her holding ash.

They say Corren gave her the chance to stand down first. I believe it. He loved her; everyone in the Ashcloaks loved her; that was the whole engine of her rebellion. But love doesn't hold a blade still when the person it loves is drawing steel, and the fight lasted four heartbeats, and I watched all four of them from behind a lectern with Marrec's blood soaking into my sleeve, and I did nothing, and doing nothing is also a thing you carry.

When it was over, Veyra lay on the steps of a conclave she had summoned to crown her, and the reading went on overhead, and Corren knelt beside her making a sound I hope never to hear again from any throat, and her soldiers—who had risen to seize a kingdom—stood very still, like men waking from a shared dream and finding their hands empty.

* * *

Here is what stopped the battle. Not the guards, though they came pouring in eventually. Not Marrec bleeding heroically across the floor, though he was doing his part. The reading stopped it.

Because the monk's voice never changed. Through swords drawn and a woman dying on the steps, through bolts and banners and the delegations' guards clashing at the doors, the Silent Choir read on: *"...and the crown of Aldermere accepted the stolen child as the price of quiet... and the crown of Thorn sold its vengeance for the same coin..."*

Every faction heard its own name in the ledger. That was the trap I had hoped for and the truth I had dreaded: there was no clean side. There never had been. The valley's peace was bought by everyone and paid for by the dead, and now the bill was being read aloud in a room where all the debtors were armed.

By the time the monk reached the final entry—the wards anchored to a living child, the sky itself hitched to one boy's existence—the fighting had guttered out, not from courage exhausted but from something rarer: shame, arriving simultaneously in several hundred people.

An Aldermeran envoy stood first. Old, immaculate, hands shaking slightly. "There is no pretext left," she said, to the hall, to the air. "None of us can call this *theirs*." She gestured at everything—at the ledger, at Veyra's body, at all of it.

A Thorn survivor answered, voice cracked: "Then who answers?"

And I got up from behind the lectern, leaving Marrec in Sela's hands, and walked out onto the floor where a few hours ago I had planned to renounce a crown, and found the words had changed shape in my mouth overnight.

"Not a king," I said. "Not a rebellion. A tribunal—of survivors. Of the people who were robbed, and the people who robbed, and the people who simply endured the lie. No crown chairs it. No army enforces it. It hears everything, and what it decides, you will all have agreed to be bound by, because the alternative—" I looked at Veyra's body, and made myself keep looking— "is that we do this again, forever, until there's no one left who remembers why."

Silence. The bad kind, then the thinking kind, then—slowly, unevenly, like ice deciding to become water—agreement.

It took until dawn. There were amendments, objections, two walkouts, one attempted bribe I won't dignify by naming. But when the sun came up through Cindermarch's high windows and fell across the ledger still open on its stand, the accord was signed by every delegation present, and the first tribunal in history convened under no flag whatsoever.

Victory, I thought, looking at it. And then I looked at the rest of the room, and understood the word differently than I ever had before.

* * *

Sela sat with Marrec while the healers worked, and I sat with both of them, and for a long while nobody said anything, because we had all learned this year that words are the most dangerous material there is.

"He'll live," the healer said finally. "The bolt missed everything that matters. Barely. He'll carry it in cold weather."

"I've carried worse in cold weather," Marrec said, grey-faced and insufferable. Then, quieter, to me: "You did it. Whatever it cost—you did it."

"It cost Veyra," I said.

"Yes," he said, because Marrec had never once lied to me to make a thing easier, and I hated him a little for it, and loved him a little more.

They carried her out at noon, wrapped in the Thorn banner she'd never gotten to raise, and her soldiers lined the way in silence, and Corren walked behind the bier bareheaded, and no one spoke, and it was somehow the most honest funeral I have ever attended—no lies, no glory, just a woman dead of her own conviction in a hall where the truth had finally been spoken and hadn't been enough to save anyone.

I stood at the window of the infirmary afterward, watching the winter light come down on Cindermarch, and felt the strangest sensation: the sky. Steady. Aligned. Since my renunciation on the conclave floor, since the anchor released, the stars had held their places—and astronomers were already writing frantic papers about it, and I was already refusing to think about what it meant that the heavens had needed my surrender to stand straight.

Sela found me there. She didn't touch me; she'd learned that much about my silences.

"The tribunal convenes in spring," she said. "First testimony: the opening of Hollowmere. Your valley. Your home."

"I know."

"Are you ready for that?"

I watched the light. Somewhere south, past the moorlands and the broken-bell road, past the ward-stones that would never hum again, the lamps of Hollowmere were burning for the first time in sixty years with real fire in them, tended by no one, waiting.

"No," I said. "But I'm going anyway."

Behind us, in the infirmary, Marrec slept the heavy sleep of the gravely mended, and on the table between us lay a letter bearing the seal of the Wardens' Guild—a letter I had not opened, addressed in a hand I knew better than my own.

Corvane's handwriting. From a dead man. Dated the night he burned the archives.

Sela looked at it. Looked at me.

"When?" she asked.

"Tonight," I said. "Before I sleep. Some debts don't wait for spring."
