## 36. Tribunal of Survivors

### Emrys

They held the tribunal in the granary at Cindermarch, because it was the only building in the city large enough for three hundred people and honest enough not to pretend otherwise. No throne room, no cathedral, no conclave spire with its bells and its bloodstained marble. A granary, with the smell of last year's wheat still in the rafters and straw scattered on the floor to keep the cold out of everyone's boots.

Veyra would have hated it. That was part of why I loved it.

The Silent Choir had insisted. Abbot Orenin had stood up on the first morning—before the delegations, before the scribes, before the crowd of survivors who'd walked days through the snow to be there—and said, in that mild voice that made kings lean forward without knowing why: "Justice conducted in palaces belongs to palaces. If this truth is to belong to everyone, let it be heard where everyone may stand."

So they built benches. Rows and rows of them, rough pine, no carving, no gilding. And at the front, instead of a judge's seat, a long table where nine elders of the survivor clans sat with the Choir's records open between them—not as rulers, but as readers. Their only authority was that they had lost the most, and everyone in the valley lands knew the arithmetic of grief better than any crown did.

I was neither witness nor judge. I was, formally, "the party renounced," which was a phrase the Choir's lawyers had invented with visible relish. I sat in the third row like anyone else, and every morning I climbed over strangers' knees to get to my seat, and every morning someone recognized me anyway, and every morning I learned again what it cost to have no name worth hiding behind.

The first testimony took four hours. It was an old woman from Thornmere, Maren's cousin as it turned out—the hedge-witch's whole family seemed to be made of iron—describing the winter the wards went up, when the crown's wardens sealed her village's wells and told them the water had soured. It hadn't soured. The wards needed anchoring points, and living water made the best anchor, so sixty villages went dry so that one valley could sleep sweetly.

"The crown called it mercy," she said, her voice dry as seed husks. "They gave us a map to Hollowmere and told us we could go there if we liked. Go to the valley. Go inside the lie. Drink the sweet water bought with ours." She looked at the elders, then past them, at the delegations seated along the east wall—Crown grey, Ashcloak black, even two envoys from the eastern kingdoms whose grandfathers had signed the armistice. "I am not here to forgive. I am here so that forgiving, if it ever comes, will know exactly what it is forgiving."

The scribes wrote it all down. That was the thing about the Choir—they wrote everything down, including the silences. When the old woman finished and nobody spoke, one of the scribes recorded: *Silence, forty seconds. Unanimous.*

I sat in my row and thought about Corvane, who had burned documents in a cellar to protect a peace that dried sixty wells, and who would have sat through those forty seconds with his jaw tight and his hands flat on his knees, and I still didn't know if he'd have been ashamed or merely tired. Probably both. He'd been good at holding two things at once. It was nearly all he ever did.

### Sela

My turn came on the ninth day, and I'll tell you honestly—I hadn't slept properly since the ford, and I'd rehearsed what I meant to say so many times that by the morning it had worn smooth as river stone and meant nothing at all.

So I threw away the rehearsal.

"My name is Sela Marsh, of Hollowmere mill," I said, "and I betrayed my best friend to a royal inquisitor."

The hall went quiet in the particular way halls do when they expected a grievance and got a confession instead. Somewhere behind me, Emrys didn't move. He'd promised me nothing except that he wouldn't look away, and he kept it.

"I traded information to Dain Marrec's agents for my family's safety. My father's debts, my brother's conscription notice—it was all arranged, all of it, in an afternoon, by men in grey who were very kind to me." I heard my own voice go flat and found I couldn't help it. Kindness from power is worse than cruelty. At least cruelty doesn't make you grateful. "I told them where the Ashcloak camp was. People died. Veyra Thorn died, later, of things that began that night, though I've stopped trying to draw the line exactly, because the line goes through everything now."

The eldest of the nine, a stonemason named Halric with hands like tree roots, leaned forward. "What do you ask of this tribunal, Sela Marsh?"

"Nothing," I said. "That's the point. I'm not asking to be punished or pardoned. I'm asking to be *counted*. Write down what I did. Put it in the archive next to what the crown did and what the rebels did and what the founders did. If my name gets to be in the story of what happened, then let the whole story be there."

Halric looked at me for a long moment. Then at the other eight. One of the women—Ysolde, a fisherwoman from the coast who'd lost two sons to the Thorn War's last skirmish—said quietly, "She's asking for the only thing this table can actually give. Accuracy."

They entered my testimony under a heading the scribes chose themselves: *Betrayers, small and otherwise.* Someone laughed when they read it aloud—a wet, surprised laugh—and then half the hall was laughing, and I was laughing too, ugly and relieved, because it was true, wasn't it? The war had been built out of small betrayals stacked on a big one. Count them all or count none.

Afterward, in the granary yard, Emrys found me by the woodpile. We stood there not talking for a while. Sixty years of sealed valley between us, one winter of broken trust, and a woodpile.

"You could have asked them for leniency," he said finally. "For your family. They'd have granted it."

"I know."

"That was the hardest thing you could have done instead."

"I know that too." I picked up a log and put it down again. "Emrys. Your mother used to sing in Old Thorn. Did you ever wonder why I never asked her what the words meant?"

He went very still. "You knew the dialect?"

"Everyone in Hollowmere knew it and nobody said so. That was the whole valley, really." I shrugged. "A town full of people agreeing not to notice things. I noticed. I just never thought it mattered until it mattered too much."

He looked at the sky for a while—the sky, which had been coming back wrong and was slowly, grudgingly, coming back right—and I watched him decide something. Not forgiveness. I wasn't owed that yet, and he was too honest to hand out counterfeit. But something adjacent. A door left unlatched.

"Come to Hollowmere," he said, "when this is done. Help me rebuild the guild. We're going to need someone who notices things and says so."

"I'll think about it."

"You won't. You'll come."

I threw a piece of bark at him. It felt almost like before.

### Dain Marrec

I attended the tribunal in the capacity of "witness, subject to summons," which was the Choir's way of keeping a leash on me without insulting the crown outright. Insulting the crown was, in fact, expressly forbidden; Orenin had rules about it. You could indict a kingdom's founding crime but you must not sneer while doing so. I confess I found the discipline admirable and nearly impossible.

My own testimony was scheduled for the twelfth day, and I had spent eleven nights deciding how much truth a man like me could afford. The answer, I kept finding, was more than comfortable and less than complete—which is to say, the exact recipe for a bad conscience.

When my day came, I stood at the reader's table with my commission papers in my hand, and I laid them out flat.

"On the third of Frostmonth, I received revised orders from the Crown Inquisition," I said. "The original orders were to retrieve a document and a person. The revision ordered the person's death and the destruction of the document. I did not carry out the revision. I wish the record to show precisely why."

"Why?" asked Halric.

"Because I had spent six weeks believing that peace required the lie. I had built a career on that belief. I had buried people for it." I looked down the rows of faces—survivors, clerks, two ambassadors pretending not to listen. "And then a twenty-year-old lamp-keeper stood in front of me at a ford with a stolen letter and refused to run, and asked me—not argued, *asked*—whether I thought a peace that needed murdering children to maintain was peace, or just a longer way of losing the same war. And I discovered I didn't have an answer I'd be willing to say aloud. So I resigned in the field, which is a coward's resignation, and I'm telling you that too, because accuracy was apparently the theme of these proceedings."

Silence. Then Ysolde, the fisherwoman: "Did you kill anyone carrying out the original orders?"

"No. I wounded three of my own soldiers defending the reading at the conclave. Two recovered. One—" I paused. "One did not. His name was Tam Breccan. Twenty-three. He believed what I taught him. I want his name in the record beside mine."

The scribe wrote it. *Tam Breccan, twenty-three.* Four syllables on a page, and I felt them land in my chest like coins into a well.

The tribunal ruled on my case the following week. They did not pardon me—that word was conspicuously absent from their vocabulary, another thing I admired—and they did not condemn me to any crown's justice, which would have meant handing the tribunal's findings back into the lie's custody. Instead they assigned me reparations service: three years, supervised by the Choir, rebuilding what the Inquisition's methods had broken. Wells, mostly. There were so many wells.

I signed the paper with the pen they handed me, a cheap stub of goose feather, and thought: thirty years of state ink, and this is the first document I've signed that I expect to still mean something in a hundred years.

### Emrys

Reparations were the tribunal's great invention, and I don't say that lightly, because most of what tribunals invent is procedure wearing ceremony's coat. But the nine elders kept asking one question of every party that stood before them: *what can you give back that cannot be taken back?*

Money could be taken back. Apologies could be retracted. Land could be reconquered. So they asked for the awkward things. The crown gave up its claim to the valley's ward-stones—all twelve of them, carted down from the border and laid in the granary yard like felled trees, where anyone could walk up and touch the place where the lie had been anchored. The Ashcloaks dissolved their order publicly, arms surrendered to the Choir's keeping, on Veyra's posthumous instruction, which she had written two days before her death in handwriting that shook and did not hesitate. Her lieutenant who killed her was tried by the tribunal itself, not by her martyred cause, and sentenced not to the rope but to ten years of tending the memorial gardens at Thornmere—planting, weeding, answering visitors' questions about what he'd done. Several elders argued even that was too merciful. Ysolde said the garden was harder than hanging, and she was right, and everyone knew it.

The eastern kingdoms paid coin—of course they did, coin was the only language they'd ever learned—but they paid it into a fund controlled by the survivor clans directly, bypassing both crowns entirely, because the Choir's records proved their grandfathers had financed the original sealing and shame travels poorly across generations but debt travels fine.

And the valley itself was opened. That was the ruling that undid me. On the first day of Thawmonth, the elders declared Hollowmere a free holding under survivor stewardship, its borders marked but unsealed, its history posted at the gate on a plank of oak where the old warning sign had hung. I helped carve that plank myself. It took me three tries to get the letters even, because my hands weren't steady, because the chisel kept catching, because some sentences are heavier than timber.

*Here lies a peace that was purchased with theft. Read what was done. Do it differently.*

Not eloquent. True. I've since learned that the second is rarer and more durable.

People came. That was the strange part, the part I hadn't predicted. Pilgrims, scholars, the simply curious, families with children who pressed their palms against the ward-stones in the granary yard and asked why the stones hummed faintly now, and were told the truth: that magic had been starved here for sixty years and was remembering how to grow, and that growing things are always a little loud at first.

Maren came, and stayed a month, arguing cheerfully with our nascent guild about proper hedge-craft pedagogy. She read the carved plank, snorted, and said, "You could've gotten a poet for cheaper."

"It had to be a lamp-keeper," I said. "Poets polish. Lamp-keepers just try to make sure you can see the road."

Sela arrived a week after Maren, with her family's debts discharged by the reparations fund and her brother's conscription annulled by tribunal decree, and she stood at the gate reading the plank for a long time before she came in. She didn't apologize again. She'd already put herself in the record, and we both understood that the record was where apologies lived now—not in private corners where they could be whispered and withheld and weaponized.

"Show me the lamps," was all she said.

So I did.

* * *

### Sela

The tribunal ended in the fourth month, not with a proclamation but with a ledger. Halric stood at the granary table, closed the Choir's records, and announced that the tribunal would reconvene annually—"because justice that ends is a monument, and justice that continues is a practice"—and then he sat down and started eating bread with the delegates like a man finishing a day's work rather than closing a wound sixty years old.

That, more than anything, convinced me it might hold. Kings end things with trumpets. This ended with bread.

I watched Emrys during the final session. He sat in his third-row seat, the renounced party, the boy with no inheritance, and when the elders pronounced the valley's new charter he didn't stand or speak or weep. He just exhaled, long and slow, the way you breathe out when you've been holding your chest against a weight so long you'd forgotten the weight had a name.

Later, walking the mill road in the thaw-mud, he said, "Do you think it's enough? Any of it?"

"No," I said, because lying to him was the one betrayal I intended never to repeat. "It's slow, it's partial, half the crowns are furious, and the eastern envoys will spend a decade trying to lawyer their way around the debt fund. But it's real, and it's ours, and nobody can take it back without standing up in daylight and explaining why."

He smiled at the mud. "Corvane would have called that fragile."

"Corvane called everything fragile. That was how he justified locking it in boxes." I bumped his shoulder. "Some things grow because they're fragile. Ask any lamp-flame."

We crested the hill and Hollowmere lay below us in the evening light, chimney smoke rising straight, the guildhouse windows already lit—my father's doing, he'd taken to lighting them early, "so folk coming home see where the light is"—and beyond the valley the first stars were coming out, aligned, patient, ordinary stars doing ordinary star-work.

Emrys stopped on the hilltop and looked at them for a long time.

"One more thing to settle," he said quietly. "Before the school opens. There's someone who hasn't given testimony yet."

I followed his gaze down the darkening road toward the pass, where, small against the last grey of the sky, a lone figure was walking up from the south with a travel-worn stride I knew, and Emrys knew, and neither of us had dared hope to see again.

Marrec had come to Hollowmere—and he was not alone.
