## 37. Marrec's Choice

I have been stabbed, shot at, cursed by hedge-witches, and once—memorably—bitten by a tax collector in Cindermarch who objected to my audit of his ledgers. None of it prepared me for the indignity of being nursed back to health by monks.

The Silent Choir did not believe in painkillers beyond what grew in their garden, and what grew in their garden believed primarily in making you contemplate your suffering rather than escape it. For three weeks I lay in a cell with whitewashed walls and a window precisely the width of a prayer book, watching the light move across the ceiling like something patient reading over my shoulder. My shoulder, incidentally, was where the blade had gone in. Veyra's lieutenant had been aiming for my heart and missed by the width of a conviction. I chose to find that funny. The alternative was to lie there thinking about how close it had been, and I had done enough of that during the fever.

Emrys came every few days. He never asked how I was. He would sit on the stool by the door—the monks had provided one stool per cell, apparently on the theory that visitors should not get comfortable—and he would talk about nothing. The weather. The state of the road to Cindermarch. A dispute between two brothers in the scriptorium over whether a marginal note constituted commentary or vandalism. He talked the way you'd talk to a man you weren't sure wanted company but were fairly certain shouldn't be alone.

I understood the strategy. It was, after all, mine.

"You're waiting for me to say something," I told him on the ninth day, when the fever had broken and my voice had come back sounding like gravel being politely asked to leave.

"I'm waiting for you to want to," he said.

"That's worse. That's exactly what I do."

"Then you can appreciate the craft."

He left shortly after, and I lay there annoyed at him in a way I recognized as respect. The boy had learned interrogation from the man sent to bury him. There was a lesson in that somewhere, probably about irony, probably one I'd have found funnier if my ribs didn't ache.

* * *

On the seventeenth day, they let me walk to the refectory, which meant they let me walk to the refectory holding the arm of a novice half my age who pretended not to notice me gripping him like a man gripping a railing on a ship in bad weather. The monastery sat on its hill above the moorlands, and from the cloister walk you could see the whole grey sweep of them, patched with snow now, the winter deepening toward solstice. Somewhere out there, delegations were arriving for the tribunal. Somewhere out there, the world I had spent twenty years keeping orderly was being taken apart and reassembled by people with no experience of order at all.

Abbot Oren found me at the rail. He was a wide, slow-moving man with ink permanently staining three fingers, and he had the particular calm of someone whose profession consisted of writing down everything terrible that happened and refusing to editorialize about it. I had decided early on that I liked him, mostly because he never once asked me whether I felt guilty, which everyone else asked constantly, in the tone of people hoping you'd say yes so they could stop wondering.

"The crown sent a rider yesterday," he said. "Third one this month."

"I imagine they're asking after my recovery."

"They're asking when you'll return to duty."

"And what did you tell them?"

"I told them the Choir does not carry messages concerning matters of state." He smiled faintly. "I also told them you were still delirious. It seemed kinder."

"It was accurate until Tuesday."

He looked out at the moors with me for a while. Then he said, without any particular weight, "You know, we have records here going back nine hundred years. Do you know what fills most of them?"

"Grain prices."

"Grain prices." He nodded approvingly. "Men like you assume history is made of decisions. It isn't. It's made of harvests, and births, and the price of nails. Decisions are just the weather that passes over all that. The storm feels enormous while it lasts." He tapped the rail with one stained finger. "But the field remembers the rain longer than the lightning."

I thought about that for longer than I intended to. I had spent my career believing I was the lightning.

* * *

Emrys came on the twentieth day, and this time he brought a bottle.

It was Hollowmere cider, from the look of it—dark glass, wax seal, the kind of rough country pressing that never traveled more than ten miles from the tree it fell under. Which meant he'd carried it across the border himself, through contested territory, past three checkpoints, in winter, for me. I looked at it for a moment before I said anything, because there are gifts that are really arguments, and you should know which argument you're agreeing to before you accept them.

"My mother made this," he said. "Before everything. Sela kept a crate in her cellar through the whole war scare. She sent it along with the last supply cart."

"Your mother," I said carefully, "was not your mother."

"No. But she made it, whoever she was. And she made it for this house, I think—for drinking with someone at the end of a hard day." He set it on the table between us. "So. End of a hard day."

We drank it out of two mismatched cups the novices scrounged up, sitting in the guest cell because the refectory had rules about spirits and Emrys had already developed, I noticed, a very Corvane-like talent for deciding which rules were load-bearing. The cider was sharp and brown and tasted of apples and cold cellars and a valley I had spent two decades pretending was a problem to be managed rather than a place where people lived.

"To Corvane," I said, because it seemed right, and because I had owed the old man a toast for thirty years and had never once been in a position to give it.

"To Corvane," Emrys said. "Who lied to both of us. In opposite directions."

"He lied to me by telling the truth, near the end. That was the cruelest thing anyone's ever done to me, and I include the stabbing."

I told him then, because the cider had loosened something and because he had earned it and because I was tired of carrying it: what Corvane had said to me, the night before the stars went out. I'd ridden to the guildhouse on routine inspection. Corvane met me at the door with wine and small talk, and then, somewhere past the second cup, he'd looked at me with those flat grey eyes and said: *Marrec. When it breaks—and it will break soon—you'll be given orders. I'm asking you, before they're given, to decide who you serve. Not the crown. Not the peace. Decide.* And I had reported the conversation verbatim, flagged it as instability in a key asset, and recommended increased surveillance.

Emrys listened without interrupting. When I finished he said, "And do you regret it?"

"Every day since the letter burned. Every day since I read what was in it." I turned the cup in my hands. "Here is what I believe, Winterbourne, and you may as well have it plainly, because everyone else in this story has gotten my professional face. I believed the lie was load-bearing. I believed sixty years of children growing up without war was worth one stolen infant, one buried grave, one valley that forgot. I believed it the way you believe in a bridge—not because you've inspected every rivet, but because you walked across it this morning and it held."

"And now?"

"Now I've seen what the bridge was standing on." I looked at him. "You. A living boy, anchored to wards, holding up the sky. I keep doing the arithmetic. Sixty years of peace, purchased with one childhood. And I can't make the sum come out clean anymore. Maybe I never could. Maybe I just never checked."

He was quiet for a while. Then he said, "When Marrec the inquisitor cornered us at the ford—before the ambush, before Sela—you offered me amnesty. Come home, be nobody, and the crown buries everything. Was that an offer or a trap?"

"It was both. Most sincere things are." I met his eyes. "It was the best thing I could give you inside the law, and I knew even then it wasn't enough. You knew it too. That's why you refused. That's why—" I gestured vaguely at myself, the bandaged shoulder, the monk's cell, the whole absurd tableau. "That's why I'm here instead of in Cindermarch collecting a commendation."

"Why *are* you here, Marrec? Truly."

Because my superiors ordered me to kill you, and I sat outside your window for six hours with a loaded crossbow and couldn't do it, and the reason I couldn't do it was that you looked, through that window, exactly like every young clerk I'd ever trained—tired, frightened, holding a document he barely understood and trying to do right by it. Because somewhere between the ford and the conclave floor I stopped being an instrument and started being a man again, and men have to live with what instruments don't.

What I said was: "I'm deciding who I serve. Corvane asked me to do it thirty years early. I'm a slow student."

* * *

My resignation took four days to process, which amused me enormously, given that accepting it required the Office of Inquiry to admit in writing that Dain Marrec, Knight-Inquisitor of the Second Circle, hero of the Border Audit, was leaving the service voluntarily rather than being dismissed for consorting with rebels. They drafted three versions. In two of them, I had suffered a nervous breakdown. In the third, I had experienced "a crisis of conscience consistent with prolonged exposure to morally complex operations," which was bureaucratic language for *he read the letter and it broke him*, and which was, for once, entirely accurate. I signed that one. Let the archive hold something true about me; I'd spent enough years filing things false about everyone else.

The rider who brought the final papers also brought a private note from my former superior, Lord-Commander Hale. It said, in full: *You were the best of us, Marrec. That is precisely why you had to go.* I read it twice, decided it was either an insult or the most honest compliment of my career, and burned it in the Abbot's study fire. Oren watched me do it and wrote down, I am nearly certain, *visitor burns correspondence, 4th of Deepwinter, cause unclear.*

Good. Let the record show what it shows.

* * *

They opened the valley in the first week of thaw.

I stood at the border stone with perhaps four hundred others—survivors, descendants, delegates, the simply curious—and watched the tribunal's marshals take down the last of the ward-stones' wreckage. The stones themselves had shattered when the sky broke, months ago, but the rubble had been left standing out of some superstition no one could name. Now a crew of masons and Ashcloak laborers worked side by side, loading the fragments onto carts, and no one gave orders about it, and no one saluted, and it was the least martial thing I had ever witnessed, which is saying something, considering my line of work.

Emrys stood nearby, at the front, with Sela. He wore plain clothes. No house colors, no sigil—he had renounced the inheritance on the conclave floor and, as far as I could tell, had meant it down to the marrow. The crowd knew who he was anyway. Crowds always know. They parted around him the way water parts around a stone, respectfully, without being asked, and he bore it the way he seemed to bear everything now: steadily, and with a kind of tired courtesy that was more Corvane than he would ever admit.

The marshals pulled the final stone free at midday. Nothing dramatic happened. No light, no thunder, no sign from the returned-and-misaligned stars. The air simply moved—a wind coming down off the pass into the valley, unhindered, the first such wind in sixty years—and the crowd made a sound I will remember until I die. Not cheering. Something lower than that, and older. The sound a house makes when a window long painted shut finally opens.

People began walking through. Slowly at first. An old woman went first—I learned later she'd been born in Hollowmere and carried out as an infant during the Thorn War, the mirror image of the theft itself—and she stopped just past the stone and stood there with her face turned up like a plant finding sun. Then others. Then many.

Emrys stayed where he was. He let them go first. All of them.

I came and stood beside him, because someone should, and because I had promised myself one thing on the long ride north and this was it.

"You're not going in?" I asked.

"In a minute. I want to watch it be theirs for a minute first." He glanced at me. "Thank you for coming."

"You asked me to."

"I asked you if you wanted to witness it. You're the only person from the other side of all this who did."

"Not the only one," I said. "Half the Office would have come, if it hadn't meant admitting the valley existed. The other half would have come to burn it. I've always been the half that reads the documents first."

He huffed something that was almost a laugh. Then he stepped over the threshold of the border, into the valley, into the place that had been built on his grave, and I watched him do it without ceremony, without speech, without looking back—which is, I have come to believe, the only way to enter a place that hurt you, if you mean to stay.

I followed. The wind came down the pass and met us both.

* * *

That evening, in the rebuilt guildhouse—it had been gutted in the fire Corvane set, and the tribunal had voted to restore it as a school rather than a garrison, which Corvane would have found either hilarious or perfect—we shared the second bottle of his mother's cider.

The main hall smelled of new timber and old smoke. Someone had hung lamps—real ones, mundane ones, burning oil, no magic in them at all—and their light moved on the walls the way lamplight has always moved, which is to say honestly. Emrys had been working on the building for weeks, I gathered, alongside volunteers from the valley and a surprising number of former Ashcloaks who had discovered that reconstruction suited them better than revolution.

"To the Office of Inquiry," I said, raising my cup. "May its archives rot slowly and be read often."

"To Guildmaster Corvane," he answered. "May whatever comes next be the answer he was hoping for."

We drank. Outside, the valley lay dark except for farmhouses and the new school windows, and above it the stars stood in their wrong-but-mending constellations, and somewhere a nightingale was attempting something ambitious in a hedgerow, having apparently decided the world was safe for song again.

"Ask me," I said, "whatever you've been not asking me for five months."

He considered. "Why did you defend me at the conclave? Against your own soldiers. You bled for it."

"Because you were right." I said it simply, because it deserved simplicity. "Not about everything. Your friend Veyra was wrong about plenty, God rest her, and you were naive about several important things, and Sela was a traitor, and the tribunal is going to be a mess for a decade. But on the one question that mattered—whether the truth could survive daylight—you were right, and I was wrong, and I had staked twenty years on being right. A man who won't bleed to correct his own arithmetic isn't a man. He's a ledger."

"And now? What does a former inquisitor do with himself?"

"Well." I turned my cup. "There's a position open. Keeper of records, teacher of memory, keeper of lamps—something of that nature. The pay is apples and gratitude. The previous management died badly, but the founding principles were sounder than anyone knew." I looked at him. "I'm told the school will need someone who knows how crowns think. How inquiries are built. Where bodies get buried, professionally speaking, so the students can learn to dig them up."

Emrys stared at me. "Are you asking to join the guild?"

"I'm asking to sweep its floors, if that's what's on offer. I'm fifty-three years old, Winterbourne, I have one good shoulder, and I have spent my entire life serving a peace that was a crime. I'd like to spend what's left serving one that isn't. Even if it's smaller. Especially if it's smaller."

For a long moment he said nothing, and I braced for refusal—expected it, almost. He had every reason. I had hunted him, cornered him, offered to erase him, and only changed sides when changing sides became survivable. Grace is cheap when it costs nothing to grant, and mine would cost him something: a permanent reminder, in his own house, of everything done to him.

Then he got up, went to the mantel, and took down a lamp. An old one, brass, dented—one of the ward-lamps, I realized, one of the ones he'd tended as a boy. He filled it with oil from a jug by the hearth, trimmed the wick with a small knife he carried, and lit it with a taper off the fire.

"There's a room at the end of the east corridor," he said. "Small. Bad view. The novices used it for storage. I've been saving it." He set the lamp on the table between us. "It needs a keeper. Someone who understands that a lamp's job isn't to decide what the light falls on. Only to keep it burning."

I looked at the little flame, and at the man across from it, and understood that I had just been given the only absolution available in this whole sorry affair: not forgiveness, which I hadn't earned, but usefulness, which I had.

"I accept," I said, "on one condition."

"Name it."

"When you teach them—the students, the children, whoever comes—when you teach them about the Thorn War, and the valley, and the lie—put me in the record as what I was. Not the redeemed ally. The inquisitor. The man who enforced it. Let them learn from the whole of me."

Emrys smiled, and it was the first smile I'd seen on him that wasn't performing patience. "Oren says the same thing. 'Write it all down,' he keeps saying. 'Even the parts that reflect badly on the archivists.'" He refilled my cup. "*Especially* those parts."

"So it's agreed. I'll be the cautionary tale with keys to the building."

"The most dangerous kind."

Outside, the nightingale gave up on ambition and settled into something simple and true, and the lamp burned between us, ordinary and steady, and I thought: Corvane, you impossible old liar, look what your apprentice built. Look who's sweeping the floors.

Emrys leaned forward, elbows on the table, and the lamplight caught his eyes—grey, like his father's, whoever that had been; grey like the stones; grey like the dawn we'd both crossed into.

"There's one more thing," he said. "A letter came today. From the tribunal. They've finished the reparations schedule for the valley, and they want... well. They want the founder's descendant to sign the transfer of the guildhouse lands. Officially. On paper. Into the school's name." He paused. "Except I renounced the inheritance. There is no founder's descendant anymore. Legally speaking, the land belongs to no one."

I set down my cup. "And who does the tribunal send a question like that to?"

"That's the interesting part," Emrys said. "They didn't send it to me."
