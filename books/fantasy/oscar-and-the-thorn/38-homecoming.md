## 38. Homecoming

The road into Hollowmere used to be the safest road in the world.

That was the whole point of it, of course. Sixty years of wards had made the valley a locked room with a very pleasant view, and the road down from the pass was the thread that tied the lock to the door. I'd walked it a thousand times without once thinking about it — the way you don't think about the walls of your own house until they come down.

Now the walls were down, and I thought about everything.

Sela walked beside me with her pack slung over one shoulder and her chin up, the way she did when she was pretending not to be nervous, which is to say constantly. She hadn't spoken much since the ford. Since she'd put an arrow through a loyalist's throat and then stood there in the shallows shaking, asking me with her eyes for something I wasn't ready to hand over.

"I can hear you not talking," she said finally.

"That's a skill. Most people need silence for that."

"Emrys."

"I know," I said. "I'm working on it."

She nodded and let it go, which was more generous than I deserved, and we came down past the third border stone — the one I'd tended every night for six years, polishing its lamp housing, checking its wick, performing my small devotions to a god that turned out to be a lie with excellent maintenance standards. The stone stood bare now. No lamp. No ward-hum. Just granite and lichen and, growing right up against its base where nothing had ever grown before, a spray of small white flowers that I didn't know the name of.

Magic, entering gently. That was how Maren had described it, back on the pilgrim road: *like weather, not like war.* She'd been mostly right. There were places in the world where it had come like war. But here, in the valley built on a grave, it came the way spring comes — slowly, sideways, in things blooming out of season and lamps that burned without oil if you asked them nicely.

I touched the border stone as I passed. Old habit. It was cool and ordinary under my palm, and I felt absurdly like apologizing to it.

"You're doing the thing again," Sela said.

"What thing?"

"The thing where you say goodbye to rocks."

"It's called closure, Sela."

"It's called being strange." But she bumped her shoulder against mine as she said it, and that small ordinary violence did more to settle my stomach than the entire tribunal's forty-page reparations schedule had.

The village appeared around the bend the way it always did — smoke from the bakery chimney first, then the mill wheel, then the crooked line of rooftops following the stream down like ducks in a row. My chest did something complicated at the sight of it. Six months away. Six months of moorland and ruins and rebel camps and a conclave spire full of screaming delegates, and here was the mill wheel turning at exactly the speed it had always turned, and here was Widow Hart's laundry line sagging under the weight of too many sheets, and here was home, which I had learned — expensively, publicly, at the cost of nearly everything — was also a crime scene.

"They'll have heard," Sela said quietly. "About the reading. About you."

"Everyone's heard about me. That was rather the point."

"Yes, but there's a difference between hearing about someone and having them walk up your road." She glanced at me. "The last time people saw you, you were the guild's apprentice who talked to lamps. Now you're the lost heir of House Thorn who dissolved a kingdom in front of four delegations."

"I didn't dissolve a kingdom. I dissolved a claim."

"You dissolved a claim in front of Veyra Thorn's soldiers while her lieutenant was drawing steel on you. From where I was standing — holding a door, thank you for asking — that looked a great deal like dissolving a kingdom."

We came down into the village proper, and I discovered she was right about the difference between hearing and seeing, because the street went quiet the way water goes quiet when a stone drops in.

* * *

They didn't gawk. That was the first surprise. Hollowmere people don't gawk; we're a valley of practical farmers and stubborn craftsfolk, and our response to anything unusual is to look at it sidelong while continuing to work. So Bram the smith kept hammering, though the hammering slowed. Widow Hart kept pinning sheets, though her eyes followed us the whole length of the line. And old Tam, who has been fishing the same stretch of the stream since before I was born, lifted his pipe in a small salute and said, "Back, then," as if I'd been gone a week to buy nails.

"Back," I said.

"Heard you were dead twice," Tam said. "Once by rebels, once by the crown. Which was it?"

"Neither. Third time's the charm, apparently."

"Hm." He sucked his pipe. "Supper's at Hart's. Whole village turns out. You'll come."

It wasn't a question. It was never a question with Tam. I said yes anyway.

The walk from the bridge to the guildhouse took perhaps three hundred paces and lasted approximately a year. Word moved ahead of us faster than we did — I could see it moving, door to door, the little flutters of curtains, children sent running on urgent errands that consisted entirely of telling their mothers *he's here*. By the time we reached the guildhouse steps, half the village had found reasons to be sweeping their doorsteps or splitting wood within eyeshot.

And the guildhouse itself —

I stopped on the path.

Someone had cleaned it. Not just cleaned it — *tended* it. The windows, which had been dark since the night Corvane burned the archives, were washed and glowing with lamplight. The ivy that had been straggling over the east wall had been trimmed. The garden Corvane had kept — herbs, mostly, and those strange silver-leafed plants he'd never let anyone touch — was weeded, watered, alive. And on the door, where the Warden's Guild sigil had hung for sixty years, there was now nothing. Just fresh-planed wood, pale as a scar, where the sigil had been pried off.

"They've been waiting for someone to give them permission," Sela said.

"Permission for what?"

"For any of it." She gestured at the whole valley — the trimmed ivy, the flowers at the border stone, the lamplight. "Sixty years of pretending the world outside didn't exist. Then the sky goes out, the truth gets read aloud in Cindermarch, and suddenly they're allowed to feel everything they weren't allowed to feel. But nobody wants to be the first one to feel it out loud. So they swept the guildhouse steps instead."

The door opened before I could knock.

It was Marta Ostler, who runs the bakery and who once chased me half a mile with a wooden spoon for stealing a heel of bread when I was nine. She looked at me for a long moment, flour on her hands and something fierce in her face, and then she grabbed me by the collar the way she had when I was nine and kissed me on both cheeks and said, "You're too thin, and you will tell me everything, and if you say 'it's complicated' I will hit you with this spoon, I brought it for that purpose."

From somewhere behind her, she did indeed produce a spoon.

"Marta—"

"Inside. Both of you. There's soup."

* * *

Here is what I learned over soup, in the guildhouse kitchen where Corvane had taught me ward-craft over a table that still bore the burn mark from the night he demonstrated why you never anchor a lamp to your own heartbeat:

The valley had spent the winter grieving, and it had done it badly, and it had done it honestly, and those two things turned out to be the same thing.

Marta told it plainly, in the way of people who have been waiting months to tell it. When word of the reading reached Hollowmere — carried first by traders, then confirmed by a tribunal rider with a formal proclamation — the village had gone mad in the slow, quiet way villages do. Some people refused to believe it. Old Tam threw the proclamation in the stream, fished it out himself an hour later, dried it by his fire, and read it twice. Some people raged. The Hedley brothers wanted to tear down the founder's statue in the square, and were stopped not by persuasion but by the simple fact that no one would help them lift it off its plinth. And some people mourned in ways nobody had expected — because the founders' crime, when you laid it out plain (the stolen heir, the forged peace, the grave under the valley), didn't just indict the dead. It indicted everyone who had ever said the blessing over the harvest, sung the founding hymn, told their children the story of the wise Wardens who kept the darkness out.

"We weren't lied to so much as we lied along," Marta said, staring into her soup. "That's the part that sits wrong. All those years, all those festivals — we weren't victims of the secret. We were its wallpaper."

"You were children of it," I said. "Same as me. None of us chose it."

"No. But we enjoyed the peace it bought, and we didn't ask what it cost." She looked up. "That's not the same as guilt. But it's not the same as clean, either."

There was a silence, and Sela reached over and refilled my bowl without asking, which is her love language, and possibly her only language.

"And the festival?" I asked. Sela had mentioned it on the road — rumors of preparations, garlands going up, the whole village buzzing.

Marta's mouth did something complicated. "That's tomorrow. And that's — hm. That's partly why you should know before you stumble into it." She set down her spoon. "We're not celebrating the founding. We voted on it. First honest vote anyone in this valley has ever cast, some of the elders said, and maybe that's true. We voted, and what we decided—" she paused, and for the first time the formidable Marta Ostler looked uncertain— "what we decided was that the festival would be a funeral. For the founders. For the lie. For sixty years of beautiful, rotten quiet. And after the funeral — well. After the funeral, we start living in the truth. Whatever that looks like."

"A funeral with garlands?" Sela asked.

"A funeral with garlands, and music, and the good beer, because grief that doesn't eat is just sickness." Marta stood and gathered bowls. "The founders get buried properly — the real ones, not the marble ones. And the grave under the valley gets a stone above ground where people can find it. The tribunal's stonemason arrives tomorrow morning. He asked—" and here she looked directly at me, and I understood, with a lurch, why the previous chapter of my life had ended the way it did— "he asked whether the heir would speak the words. Except there is no heir. So we thought maybe the apprentice would do. If the apprentice is willing."

* * *

I didn't sleep much that night, which surprised me, because I'd slept badly everywhere for six months and had somehow expected my own bed to cure it.

The guildhouse was different with other people's touches on it. Someone had put flowers on Corvane's empty chair. Someone else had left the cellar door open — the cellar where he'd burned the archives, where I'd found him feeding sixty years of secrets to the fire with the calm of a man doing arithmetic — and now it was just a clean, swept cellar smelling of lime wash. The letter had been read by the whole tribunal by now. Corvane's last defense of me, dissected by lawyers and scholars. I wondered what he'd have made of that. I wondered if he'd have minded. I decided, lying in the dark, that he'd have found it funny — the man who spent his life hiding documents watching them get argued over by committees.

Near dawn I gave up and went out to the workshop, to the lamp.

My lamp. The one that had flickered with true light back when every other lamp in the valley was dead glass — the small disobedience I'd hidden from Corvane, the first crack in the dam, though I hadn't known it then. It sat on the workbench where I'd left it, dusty, and when I cupped my hands around it and said nothing in particular, it lit.

Not the old ward-light, that flat amber sameness. True light. It moved. It breathed. It was, I realized with a jolt, a slightly different color than it had been six months ago — warmer, deeper, as if it had been practicing while I was away.

"Show-off," I told it.

The door creaked. Sela, wrapped in a blanket, hair like a startled hedge. "Couldn't sleep either?"

"Too many feelings. It's very inconvenient."

"You? Never." She came and stood beside me, looking at the lamp. For a while neither of us spoke. Outside, the sky was doing its new slow brightening, the stars fading one by one into a dawn that belonged to everyone.

"When I made my bargain," she said abruptly, "with Marrec's people. I told myself it was for my family. And it was. But that wasn't all of it. I was angry at you, Emrys. You got to leave. You got to matter. And I got to stay behind and be the friend who stayed behind." Her jaw worked. "So when they offered me a way to matter — even a filthy way — I took it. That's the confession. Not just fear. Envy."

"I know," I said.

"You know?"

"Sela. I grew up as the last apprentice of a dying guild, talking to lamps, in a valley whose entire history was a locked box I was standing on. Of course I know about envy. Why do you think I ran toward every dangerous thing that offered me a purpose?" I turned the lamp in my hands. "The difference is mine nearly got you killed, and yours actually saved my life at the ford. So I'd say we're roughly even, except I'm the one who owes you."

She was quiet a long moment. "You couldn't forgive me before."

"No. Because I was still furious, and forgiveness while you're furious is just lying politely." I looked at her. "I'm not furious anymore. I'm tired, and I'm home, and you're here, and I think — I think I forgive you. Ask me again in a year and see if it holds."

"That," she said, "is the most Emrys answer possible, and I accept it." She bumped my shoulder. "Funeral today. Try to cry like a normal person."

* * *

They buried the founders in the afternoon, in the square, under a sky that had spent the whole morning being extravagantly blue.

The whole village came. They came in their good clothes and their work clothes and, in Tam's case, in whatever he'd fallen asleep in, and they stood in the cold air — real cold now, the first honest winter breaths the valley had felt in sixty years — and they watched the masons lower a marker-stone into place above the vault beneath the valley floor. Not a grand monument. A plain slab of local granite with the names cut deep: the founding warden, Aldric Winterbourne. His wife. The child who was never theirs, whose name the records gave only as *the boy*, and whose real name the Silent Choir's archive restored at last: Osric Thorn. Oscar, as his mother had called him, in the lullaby dialect, in the songs I'd grown up humming without knowing why they fit my mouth so well.

I spoke the words. Not as the heir — I'd renounced that in front of four delegations and a spire full of witnesses, and the renunciation held, and the sky had steadied overhead the moment I made it, which even the astronomers agreed was a decent sign. I spoke them as the apprentice. Last of the Wardens' Guild, keeper of the lamps, the boy who'd polished the lie for six years without knowing it was one.

"We inherited a peace," I told them. "We didn't ask what it was made of. Today we bury what it was made of, and we keep the peace — the real kind, the kind you build in daylight, where everyone can see the seams. It won't be as smooth. It'll hold better."

Old Tam spat ceremonially on the grave, which is apparently an ancient rite of contempt, and then immediately apologized to the grave for mixing his signals. Several people laughed, wetly, and the crying that had been held back all day broke loose all across the square, and nobody tried to stop it, and that was the funeral.

And then — because grief that doesn't eat is just sickness — Marta's ovens had been going since dawn, and the trestle tables came out, and the good beer was broached, and someone produced a fiddle, and by the time the sun went down the funeral had become, without anyone deciding it, a festival. The first honest one. People danced badly and told stories about the founders that were true instead of flattering, and Tam recounted the tale of throwing the proclamation in the stream to general delight, and Widow Hart cornered Sela and demanded the story of the door-holding at the conclave, and Sela told it with such embellishment that I barely recognized my own near-death.

I stood at the edge of the light with a mug I wasn't drinking and watched them, and felt the thing I'd been afraid to feel since the pass: belonging. Not the old belonging — the sealed-valley kind, the belonging that required not asking questions. This was the other kind, the fragile kind, the kind that survives being tested. These people knew what I was now. They knew what their grandparents had been. They knew the valley was built on a grave, and they'd put a stone over the grave and danced next to it anyway, and that, I thought, was the bravest thing I'd seen in a year that had contained several actual wars.

"You're doing the face," Sela said, materializing with two mugs.

"What face?"

"The face where you're happy and annoyed about it."

"I contain multitudes."

"You contain soup. Marta's watching you. Drink it." She handed me the second mug, then nodded past me. "Also, someone's been standing at the edge of the square for the last ten minutes, working up courage. Thought you should know before they faint."

I turned.

At the border of the torchlight, half in shadow, stood a figure in travel-stained grey — not royal grey, just the grey of a long road — and even before the torch caught his face I knew the set of those shoulders, the careful stillness of a man who had spent a career making himself hard to notice and had recently, painfully, stopped trying.

Dain Marrec.

He'd resigned his commission — the tribunal papers had said so, and I'd read that line three times to make sure. He'd asked only to witness the valley opened. But papers are paper, and men are men, and the last time I'd seen him he'd been bleeding out on the conclave floor because he'd chosen to stand between me and his own soldiers.

He raised one hand, awkwardly, the greeting of a man who has rehearsed a speech for two hundred miles and abandoned it in the last fifty paces.

"Apprentice," he said.

"Inquisitor," I said.

And then, from behind him, stepping out of the dark where she'd clearly been letting him sweat the entire time, came Maren the hedge-witch, leaning on her stick, grinning like a woman arriving precisely on schedule.

"Don't mind him," she said. "He talks too much when he's nervous. Me, I brought news — and you'll want to sit down for it, lad, because the stars have started coming back wrong, and there's a man at my cottage who says he knows why, and he claims—" she fixed me with one bright, terrible eye— "he claims he taught you everything you know."

The mug went very heavy in my hand.

Because I had buried Corvane on a hillside in the moorlands with my own hands, and I had piled the stones myself, and I had counted every one.
