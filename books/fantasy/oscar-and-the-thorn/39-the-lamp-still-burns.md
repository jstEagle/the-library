## 39. The Lamp Still Burns

The guildhouse roof took three months to mend, mostly because I insisted on doing it wrong first.

I want that on the record. Somewhere in the new archive, between the testimony of the Thorn survivors and Marrec's deposition—filed under seal, though he pretends not to know I kept a copy—there should be an entry reading: *Emrys Winterbourne, last apprentice of the Hollowmere Guild, fell through his own roof twice and blamed the ladder both times.* History deserves its full accounting. That was rather the point of everything.

The valley had changed in ways that were easier to see from up there, slate under my boots, hammer in hand. The wards were gone—had been gone since the night I stood on the conclave floor and unmade myself as heir—and magic had come back the way water comes back to a field after a long drought: slowly at first, in patches, then all at once, everywhere, indifferent to whether anyone was ready for it. The millrace glowed faintly green on warm nights. Old Marta's goats had opinions now, actual opinions, expressed aloud, and she'd stopped being surprised about it around midsummer. Children born after the solstice arrived with eyes like struck flint, and nobody whispered about it anymore, because there was no guild to whisper *to*.

That was the part I'd built, or was building. The guild had been a vault. What rose from its bones—the same stones, honestly; I wasn't so proud as to demolish good masonry—was meant to be a school. A school of memory-keeping, we called it, because *archive* sounded too much like a tomb and *academy* sounded like something that required funding we did not have.

The principle was simple enough to carve over the door, and Sela had carved it, because her letters were straighter than mine:

**WE KEEP WHAT HAPPENED. ALL OF IT. ESPECIALLY THE PARTS THAT HURT US.**

The second line had been my addition. Sela had argued for it, actually, standing in the doorway with her chisel still warm, saying, "If you only keep the parts that hurt other people, you're just a very slow way of starting another war." She was right. She usually was, which was insufferable, which is presumably why she was my best friend.

* * *

On the morning the lamp was finished, it rained, because of course it did. Hollowmere weather had developed a sense of occasion and chose to express it exclusively through inconvenience.

The lamp itself sat where it had always sat, on the iron bracket above the guildhouse door—the one lamp in the valley that had flickered with true light while every other ward-lamp went dark, the one secret I'd kept even from Corvane, back when secrets were the currency we all traded in without knowing we were broke. Sixty years of dead glass and cold brass, and this one stubborn flame that had never quite gone out.

I'd taken it down in autumn. Cleaned it. Re-cut the wick. And then, because the whole point of the school was that we didn't hide things anymore, I'd left the door open while I worked, so that anyone passing could watch me fumble with a lamp fitting and swear at it in two languages.

"You know," Sela said, appearing beside me with two cups of tea and the expression of someone who has been waiting all morning to say what she's about to say, "most schools open with a speech."

"Most schools don't have a founder who fell through his own roof twice."

"Twice," she agreed solemnly. "We should put it on the plaque." She handed me a cup. "You've been polishing that thing for four hours. It's a lamp, Emrys. It's not going to write your memoirs."

"It's the first thing I ever lied about," I said. "Well. The first thing I lied about *well*. There was an incident involving Corvane's jam when I was nine, but we agreed never to speak of it."

"We did not agree. You begged."

"The point—" I turned the lamp in my hands. Rain ticked against the eaves. The glass was old, older than me, older than the lie, and inside it the flame sat small and steady, burning on nothing I could name. Not oil. Not ward-work. Just... itself. "The point is that everything else got renamed. The guild became a school. The border stones became just stones. My name—" I shrugged. "My name became whatever I decided it was. But this lamp has been telling the truth the entire time. It burned when it wasn't supposed to. It waited sixty years for everyone else to catch up. It deserves one honest morning before I hang it back up and let children point at it."

Sela looked at me for a moment the way she used to look at the river in flood—assessing, fond, slightly alarmed.

"And after the honest morning?"

"After that, it's just a lamp again. Which is rather the miracle, isn't it? That things get to be ordinary again. That's what we bought. All of it. The tribunal, the graves opened, the names read out, Marrec drinking tea in my kitchen looking like a man who's forgotten how chairs work—it was all so that a lamp could be a lamp and a boy could be a boy and neither of them would have to be a symbol."

"Marrec doesn't look uncomfortable in chairs," Sela said. "He looks uncomfortable being *liked*. It's different."

"He'll adjust."

"He asked if he could audit the first class."

"I said yes."

"You said yes to the royal inquisitor who hunted you across two kingdoms auditing your memory-keeping class."

"He resigned his commission, Sela. He carried me half a mile with a spear through him. And he takes notes in a fair hand." I sipped my tea. "Also he brings bread. Have you had his bread?"

"...It is very good bread," she admitted, with the reluctance of a woman conceding territory.

* * *

The honest morning came the next day, because rain has no respect for ceremony.

I hung the lamp at dawn, alone, which was deliberate. There are moments that belong to crowds and moments that belong to one person standing under their own history with wet boots. This was the second kind.

The flame caught the grey light and threw it gold, and for a heartbeat I was seventeen again, crouched in the dark on the border road with my heart going like a drum, watching this impossible little fire burn in a valley where fire wasn't allowed. I remembered thinking: *nobody can know.* I remembered how safe the secret felt, and how the safety was the trap, and how Corvane had known it all along and loved me anyway, which might be the most complicated sentence I will ever have cause to write.

"I kept it lit," I told him, because the graveyard was up the hill and grief travels downhill if you give it a reason. "Not the flame. The question. Why does one lamp burn when everything else goes dark?" I laughed a little. "Took me years and a war nearly starting to answer it. You could have just told me."

But he couldn't have. That was the whole tragedy of Corvane—that he'd inherited a silence so heavy he'd mistaken it for a duty, and had spent his life deciding which lies were load-bearing. He'd chosen me hoping I'd find a better answer than either side offered. I hadn't found a better answer, exactly. I'd found a worse-sounding one that worked: tell everyone, trust no institution, let the survivors hold the scales. It was uglier than a secret. It was also alive.

Below me, the valley was waking. Smoke from chimneys that didn't need to hide their fires anymore. The mill turning. Somewhere, Marta's goats negotiating loudly about pasture rights. And along the road from the pass—a road that had been sealed by fear for sixty years and was now merely a road, muddy and rutted and gloriously ordinary—three figures walking toward the school with bundles and bedrolls and the particular gait of people arriving somewhere they intend to stay.

Students. Our first ones. A hedge-witch's grandniece from the Road of Broken Bells who could hear wells; a former Ashcloak deserter who wanted to learn to record history instead of avenge it; and a quiet young man from Cindermarch whose family had held royal commissions for six generations and who had walked away from all of it to study, of all things, how to write things down honestly.

Maren had sent the girl with a note that read, in its entirety: *She asks better questions than you did. Don't ruin her.* I'd pinned it above my desk as both compliment and warning.

* * *

The first lesson, I gave in the old cellar—the same cellar where Corvane had burned documents while I watched and understood nothing. We'd cleared out the ash and the scorch marks, or rather we'd kept one scorch mark, ringed in brass, labeled: *WHERE THE GUILDMASTER BURNED THE TRUTH, AND WHERE HIS APPRENTICE STOOD AND DID NOT ASK QUESTIONS.* Sela had thought the label morbid. I'd thought it necessary. Morbid won, which tells you most of what you need to know about our curriculum.

Three students sat on benches that still smelled of fresh pine. Marrec sat at the back, uninvited and welcome, taking notes in his fair hand, wearing civilian grey that fit him like an apology.

"So," I said. "Memory-keeping. Rule one."

"Don't burn anything," said the Ashcloak deserter, and Marrec made a sound into his notes that I chose charitably to interpret as a cough.

"Rule one," I said, "is that you are not the story. You are the vessel. The moment you start editing to protect yourself—to protect your guild, your order, your crown, your name—you've stopped keeping memory and started manufacturing it, and manufactured memory is just a slower kind of violence." I picked up the lamp from the table between us, its flame steady in the cellar dark. "Sixty years ago, some very tired people decided that a lie was cheaper than a war. They weren't monsters. Most of them believed they were saving lives. And the lie worked—for a while—at the cost of a stolen child, a murdered truth, and a sky that eventually noticed." I set the lamp down. "Your job is to be the sky. To notice. Even when noticing costs you everything comfortable."

The well-hearing girl raised her hand. "What if the truth hurts innocent people?"

"Then you tell it carefully, slowly, with witnesses, and you help carry the weight of it afterward. You don't decide it's too heavy and bury it. Buried truths don't die. They wait." I glanced at Marrec. "There are men in this room who can testify to how long they wait."

"Decades," Marrec said mildly, not looking up. "In my professional experience."

* * *

Afterward, Sela found me on the doorstep, watching the lamp burn against the coming dusk.

"They're asking about you," she said. "The students. About the heir. Whether you'll ever use the Thorn name."

"No."

"That's it? No?"

"It's a complete sentence." I turned the cup in my hands. "Veyra wanted the name because a name is a weapon—you point it at people. The crown feared the name because a name is a claim—you build armies on it. The council holds it in trust now, for the descendants, for whoever wants to visit it in the archive. It's not mine. It never really was. It was a coat someone dressed a baby in before the baby could say no."

"And Winterbourne?"

I smiled. "Winterbourne was a lie too, once. A cover story. A stolen child given a false name to hide him." I looked up at the lamp, at the small stubborn gold of it. "But here's the thing Sela—I *chose* it. After I knew. After I knew everything, I stood in the ruins and I could have taken Thorn, and I picked the lie instead. Not because it was true. Because it was mine. Every year of it—the lamps, the watches, Corvane's terrible tea, you stealing my boots—the whole shape of it belongs to me in a way blood never will. A name you inherit is a debt. A name you choose is a home." I shrugged. "So I'm keeping the lie. Refurbished. Honest about its origins. Like the rest of us."

Sela was quiet a moment. Then: "Corvane would have hated that reasoning."

"Corvane would have pretended to hate it while writing it down somewhere in code for posterity."

"That's true. He absolutely would." She bumped her shoulder against mine. "First class went well, by the way. The well-girl cried a little at rule one."

"Good crying or bad crying?"

"The kind where someone finally says the thing everyone knows."

"Best kind," I said. "That's the whole trade, really. Saying the thing everyone knows, out loud, first, in a room with witnesses."

* * *

That night I climbed to the border stone—the first one, the one I'd crossed sixty years and one lifetime ago, though it had been less than two years, though some mornings it felt like yesterday and others like a story I'd heard from someone else. The wards were long gone. The stone was just a stone now, mossy and lopsided, with a bronze plaque the tribunal had mounted: *HERE THE VALLEY OF HOLLOWMERE WAS SEALED. HERE IT WAS OPENED. REMEMBER BOTH.*

Beyond it, the moorlands rolled away under a sky thick with stars—real stars, aligned right again, steady as stitches. Astronomers still came to check, every season, and every season they found the heavens holding, and every season I pretended not to be relieved.

I sat with my back against the stone and let the cold come in, the real cold, the honest cold that had flooded the valley the night everything fell apart. Somewhere behind me the lamp burned over the schoolhouse door, one small gold point in all that dark, and I thought about the boy I'd been on this exact spot, terrified, carrying a letter and a lie, believing the world ended at the wards.

The world hadn't ended. It had *opened*, which is scarier and better.

A voice came up the path behind me—light footsteps, familiar ones, and a voice pitched low the way you speak when you've learned that the dark listens: "You're supposed to be resting. Marrec says you still favor the left side."

"I do not favor the left side."

"You favor it enormously. You walk like a ship listing." Sela dropped down beside me, wrapped in her shawl, and followed my gaze up to the returned stars. For a while neither of us spoke. Then she said, quietly, "They're asking in the village when you'll tell the children the story. The whole one. The little ones keep hearing pieces—wards, stars, the war that didn't happen—and piecing it together wrong."

"What are they piecing together?"

"That you're a king in exile who chose poverty out of nobility."

I groaned. "Wonderful."

"There's a version where you fought a dragon."

"There's always a version where someone fights a dragon." I pulled my coat tighter and watched the lamp-glow far below, small and certain against the night. "Tell them I'll tell it. Soon. When the school's settled. The true version—all of it, the thorn and the theft and the boy with the nickname his mother gave him." I paused. "Though maybe not all at once. Truth lands better in chapters."

Sela snorted. "Everything with you is chapters."

"Everything worth keeping is chapters." I stood, and favored the left side, and didn't care. Below us the valley lay quiet under its honest cold and its honest stars, and above the schoolhouse door the lamp that had refused, for sixty years, to stop telling the truth went on burning—patient, ordinary, and bright as a promise kept.

"Come on," I said. "Bread's getting cold. Marrec brought loaves."

"Of course he did."

And we went down the hill together, toward the light.
