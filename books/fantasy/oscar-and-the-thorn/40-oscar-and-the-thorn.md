## 40. Oscar and the Thorn

The stars came back crooked, and we learned to love them that way.

That's the first thing I teach the children now, on the first warm night of every spring: look up. Don't memorize the old charts—there are no old charts anymore, not ones that tell the truth—but look up anyway. The sky over Hollowmere is a patched quilt these days, constellations stitched back together by astronomers who argue in three languages about where things *should* be versus where they actually are. The Shepherd's Crook is missing its hook. The River of Night bends the wrong way around the Miller's Wheel. Somewhere above the eastern ridge there's a bright new star that no one can name, which the children have decided, unanimously and without my permission, belongs to a giant cat.

I let them have it. The world has had enough of people telling it what it must be.

It's been nine years since the conclave at Cindermarch. Nine years since I stood on a floor slick with spilled wine and worse, renounced an inheritance I never asked for, and watched the sky stop shaking. Nine years since Veyra Thorn died believing she was right, and Dain Marrec bled out half his life proving he wasn't, and Sela held a door against soldiers with nothing but her body and a stubbornness I've come to think of as the truest magic either of our kingdoms ever produced.

Nine years. Long enough for scars to fade from angry red to pale silver. Not quite long enough to stop checking the border stones when I walk past them, though they're just stones now. We left them standing, all forty-one of them, as a lesson rather than a wall. Children climb on them. There's a boy—I'll get to him in a moment—who carved his initials into the one that used to anchor the north ward, and I confess I didn't even scold him. Let the stones learn what it's like to be useful for something kinder.

Tonight is one of my teaching nights. The guildhouse still stands—it was burned once, in the early days, by people who thought we were hiding more secrets, and rebuilt twice, once by volunteers and once, grudgingly, by royal decree after the tribunal made them pay for it. But it isn't a guild anymore. Corvane's last wish, or near enough to it, was that the Wardens become something else, and so we did. We're keepers of memory now. Archivists of the valley. Teachers. When folk from beyond the pass want to know what happened here—the whole of it, unvarnished—they come to us, and we tell them everything, including the parts that shame us. *Especially* those parts.

I've got eleven children sitting on the hillside behind the guildhouse tonight, wrapped in blankets because spring in Hollowmere still doesn't trust itself, and they're waiting for the story. They always wait for the story. I could teach them soil rotation or the proper tending of lamp-oil or the seventeen uses of yarrow, and they'd listen politely, but the moment I say *once upon a time*, they go still as herons.

"Master Emrys," says Pip, who is seven and has no sense whatsoever of pacing, "is it the thorn story?"

"It's always the thorn story," says her brother, with the weary air of a boy of ten who has heard it many times and will hear it many more.

"Then it's the thorn story," I say, and settle down among them, and begin the way I always begin.

* * *

"Once upon a time," I tell them, "in a valley that had forgotten how to be cold, there lived a boy called Oscar."

"Oscar!" they chorus, delighted, because they know—he thinks he's fooling nobody—that Oscar is me. That's the joke of it, and it's been the joke for years, and I've stopped minding. His mother gave him the name. Not his mother of blood—his mother of milk and lullabies, the woman whose face he couldn't remember but whose songs lived in his mouth like birds in a rafters. She used to sing to him in Old Thorn dialect, words he didn't understand then, and she called him *Oscar*, which means—in that language, in the old tongue of a kingdom that was erased—"the little one who asks too many questions."

Which, if you know me at all, you'll agree was prophecy.

"So," I say. "Oscar grew up in this valley where magic was sealed away, and his job—when he was grown—was to tend lamps that held no fire."

"No fire?" Pip wrinkles her nose. "What's the point of a lamp with no fire?"

"That," I tell her, "is an excellent question, and it took Oscar twenty-odd years to ask it properly. You see, everyone in the valley believed the lamps were *resting*. That the magic would come back someday, when the wards were strong again. And Oscar believed it too, because believing what everyone believes is very comfortable, like a coat that fits so well you forget you're wearing it."

"But he stopped believing?"

"He saw something he shouldn't have. One lamp, deep in the hills, still flickered with real light. Real magic. And instead of telling anyone, he hid it, because he'd already learned the first rule of living inside a lie: some truths are dangerous, and it's easier to keep them small than to find out why."

The children shift. They don't like that part. Good. They shouldn't. I didn't like living it.

"And then one night," I continue, dropping my voice the way the story demands, "every star in the sky went out at once."

Pip looks up reflexively, as she does every time, checking that the stars are still there. They are. The cat-star burns bright over the eastern ridge. She relaxes.

"The wards fell," I tell them. "The cold came into the valley for the first time in sixty years. And Oscar's master—a man named Corvane, who loved him, remember that part, *remember that he loved him*—started burning papers in the cellar and told him to keep quiet. Because the peace of the valley wasn't peace at all. It was a grave with a roof built over it. A stolen child. An unavenged crime. A war that never really ended, just went to sleep under a blanket of forgetting."

"And the thorn?" asks the older boy, Tam, who likes to skip ahead. He always skips ahead. He'll make a fine archivist; he already reads the ends of books first.

"The thorn," I say, "grew through the lie."

I hold up my hand, and in the starlight they can see it: the thin white line across my palm, the birthmark shaped like a barbed crescent that every child of House Thorn carried, the mark that told me who I was before any letter ever did. I show it to them the way I show it every year, and every year they lean in as if it might have changed.

"A thorn," I tell them, "is a strange thing. It grows on roses, which are beautiful, and it exists to protect them, which is good, but it also catches your sleeve and draws blood, which hurts. And a lie is like that too. The lie that built this valley protected something—peace, real peace, people alive who would have died in a war—and it also cut everyone who touched it. It cut the survivors of the war, who were never allowed to mourn. It cut the crown, which had to keep sending men like Marrec to bury the truth deeper. It cut Corvane, who spent his whole life guarding a crime he hadn't committed but couldn't undo. And it cut a boy called Oscar, who found out one day that his whole life was a story someone else had written, and that his name, his home, his family—all of it—was built on top of a grave."

"Was he angry?" Pip asks.

"Furious," I admit. "He was angry at Corvane, and at the crown, and at a woman named Veyra who wanted to use him as a flag for her rebellion, and at the sky, and eventually—at the end of a very long night on a monastery floor—at himself. Anger is easy. What's hard is what comes after. Because here's the thing about thorns, children: you can't burn them out without burning the rose. You can't pretend they aren't there, because they'll catch you sooner or later. The only thing you can do—the only thing anyone can do—is take hold of it carefully, knowing it will hurt, and pull."

* * *

We sit in silence for a while after that, which is part of the teaching. Silence is a tool. Corvane taught me that, though he taught it to me as a weapon first and a comfort second, and I've spent nine years turning it around.

Tam breaks it, because Tam always does. "Did it hurt? Pulling it out?"

"Terribly," I say. "He lost his name—well, he kept it, but only after giving away the one he was born with. He lost his home—or rather, he learned his home had never really been his, and then had to decide whether to love it anyway. He lost his master, buried him under a cairn on the moor with a Warden's lamp for a headstone. He nearly lost his best friend, and did lose her for two terrible years, until she came back and saved his life at a river ford and he learned that forgiveness isn't a door that opens, it's a garden you grow." I glance at Sela's window across the lane, lit warm and yellow. She's grinding grain; I can hear the mill even from here, the old patient heartbeat of the valley. "And he lost the story of himself. Which is the worst one. But here's what he gained."

"What?"

"He gained the truth. All of it, ugly and magnificent. He gained a tribunal where survivors—not kings, not generals, *survivors*—got to decide what justice looked like. Slow, imperfect, arguing-about-grain-tariffs-for-three-years justice, but real. He gained a valley that could finally be cold in winter and honest in summer. He gained a school where children are taught to ask too many questions." I tap Pip on the nose. "Like certain pupils I could mention."

"And the sky?" asks little Wren, who hasn't spoken yet, being four and mostly interested in whether the cat-star is a real cat. "Did the sky get better?"

"The sky steadied," I tell her, "the moment Oscar let go. That's the secret ending most people miss. All that time, the wards were anchored to *him*—to the heir, to the stolen child grown into a man without knowing it. As long as he held the claim, the sky shook. The moment he set it down—publicly, on the conclave floor, in front of every crown and order that wanted to use it—the anchor released. The heavens settled. Not back to how they were. Forward, to something new. Something truer, maybe, or at least more honest about being crooked."

Wren considers this with the gravity of a philosopher deciding lunch. Then: "Was the cat-star there before?"

"No one knows," I say solemnly. "But it's ours now."

* * *

Later, when the children have been marched home by various parents and grandparents, I stand alone on the hillside with a lamp in my hand.

Not a ward-lamp. Those are gone forever, and good riddance. This is just a lamp—an ordinary thing of glass and oil and wick, the kind I spent my childhood pretending held magic and my adulthood learning held something better: attention. Care. The willingness to sit with a small flame through a long dark night and call that enough.

The first lamp that flickered with true light, all those years ago, sits on my desk inside the guildhouse. It burns every night. Not because it holds magic—I've tested it, exhaustively, and it doesn't—but because I lit it the day the tribunal finished its work, and I have never put it out. Some flames you keep for what they mean, not what they do.

Corvane would understand that. I think, in the end, he understood almost everything, except how to believe that a lie could be set down gently rather than torn away bleeding. He chose me hoping I'd find a better answer than either side offered. I hope I have. I hope the school is the answer. I hope the tribunal is the answer. I hope the children on the hillside, asking their too-many questions under a crooked sky, are the answer—or will be, when they're grown, and it's their turn to hold the lamp.

Sela comes up the path while I'm standing there, flour on her sleeves, breath steaming in the cold spring air. She doesn't say anything for a while. We're long past needing to.

"You told it wrong again," she says finally.

"I did not."

"You always leave out the part where you cried."

"I absolutely do not cry in the story."

"You cried at the ford. You cried at the cairn. You cried when the Abbot read Corvane's letter aloud and got to the part about your mother's lullabies." She leans on the fence beside me, shoulder warm against mine. "You cried so hard the monks brought you tea."

"That was grief," I say with dignity. "Grief is different from crying."

"Grief is crying with better vocabulary." She smiles at the sky, at the cat-star, at the crooked River of Night. "Tell it right next year. They should know the hero wept. It's the best part."

I consider arguing. I decide, as I usually do with Sela, that she's right and I'll admit it in about five years.

Below us, the valley lies quiet. Lamps in windows—real lamps, ordinary lamps, burning honestly. The border stones silvered with moonlight, climbed on by children, carved with initials, holding nothing in and keeping nothing out. Somewhere beyond the pass, the world goes on being large and wounded and healing, kingdom by kingdom, grave by opened grave, testimony by testimony. The tribunal meets again next month. There's a dispute about reparations in the Cindermarch lowlands that will take years, and I find, these days, that I love it for taking years. Slow justice. Imperfect justice. Justice with muddy boots and a ledger full of complaints. It's not the peace the lie promised. It's better. It's *ours*.

"Come inside," Sela says. "It's freezing, and you're sentimental, and the tea's getting cold."

"In a moment."

She goes. I stay.

Above me, the stars burn crooked and bright, misaligned and magnificent, a sky that was broken on purpose and healed by accident and belongs to no crown at all. Somewhere up there, I like to think, a woman who sang lullabies in a dead language is watching a boy who asks too many questions teach other children to do the same.

Once upon a time, in a valley that had forgotten how to be cold, there lived a boy called Oscar—and the thorn that grew through the lie stayed in his hand long after he pulled it, not as a wound, but as a reminder: that truth costs, that peace is a garden and not a wall, and that the smallest light, tended honestly, is enough to see by.

The stars came back crooked, and we learned to love them that way.
