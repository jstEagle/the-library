## 1. The Ledger of Impossible Things

The candle had burned down to its last finger's width when Theo Iversen found the discrepancy.

He sat in the counting room at the top of the guild tower, where the windows were narrow as arrow slits and the wind outside made a sound like a man trying to remember a song. Three ledgers lay open before him — the black for goods received, the red for goods sold, the grey for everything that could not be honestly called either — and his pen hovered above the grey page, where the numbers refused to sit still.

That was not unusual. Numbers in the grey ledger were like cats brought in from the cold: they would settle eventually, but only on their own terms. A bottled storm's weight changed with its mood. Borrowed years shrank a little in storage, the way bread did. The grief of the widow Hessa, sold to them by the ounce and decanted into a stoppered jar of smoked glass, had been logged at four pounds two ounces on the ninth of Frostmonth, and tonight it weighed four pounds one and three-quarters.

Theo frowned at it. Grief was supposed to be stable. That was why the Guild dealt in it — grief kept, grief aged, grief held its value better than love or hope or any of the wetter commodities. Someone had opened the jar.

He rose, took the ring of keys from his belt — forty-one keys, each filed to a different impossibility — and went down the spiral stair to the vaults.

* * *

The vaults of the Veiled Guild ran deeper than the tower was tall, which was the first impossible thing about them, though hardly the most interesting. Theo had counted the steps once, out of an apprentice's boredom, and arrived at a number that did not exist: nine hundred and ninety-nine, then the floor. He had never counted again. Some arithmetic you learned to leave alone, the way you left alone the third door on the left in the long corridor, which breathed.

Lamps burned down there without oil. They had been burning, so far as anyone knew, since before the Guild had a name, and no one had ever refilled them, and no one asked. Asking was the one sin the Guild punished faster than theft.

Theo walked the rows. Shelves of dark oak rose out of the lamplight, and on them the inventory of the impossible slept in its careful arrangements. Bottled storms on the west wall, their glass sweating even in the cold, lightning coiled inside like a sleeping eel. Jars of last words. A crate stamped with the seal of a kingdom that had drowned two hundred years ago, containing — according to the manifest — forty pounds of unspent courage, salvaged from the wreck. On the high shelves, wrapped in oilcloth and chained twice, the years. Borrowed years, sold years, years pried out of dying men who'd wanted one more season and been talked into taking less.

And there, on the shelf marked with Hessa's name in Theo's own small, exact hand: the jar of grief, its wax seal broken, its stopper resting beside it like a fallen tooth.

He stood very still and listened.

The vaults were never silent. Impossible things murmured in their sleep — storms grumbled, years ticked faintly backward, the courage creaked against its crate like a ship dreaming of battle. But grief, properly sealed, made no sound at all. It only *waited*, and its waiting had a texture, a pressure against the ears, like the hush before snowfall.

This jar was silent too. But the silence around it was wrong — thinner, the way air is thinner after something has passed through it quickly.

Theo picked up the jar. It was lighter than it should have been, by exactly the missing quarter ounce. He turned it in the lamplight and saw, on the inner curve of the glass, a smudge — the print of a fingertip, pressed from the *inside*.

Grief did not leave fingerprints. Grief did not press itself against the walls of its container and look out.

He set the jar down, reseated the stopper, poured new wax, and pressed his signet into it before the wax could think better of cooling. Then he climbed back up the nine hundred and ninety-nine steps that were somehow also twelve, sat down at his desk, and wrote in the margin of the grey ledger, in letters smaller than the rules required:

*Inventory stable. Seal renewed. Note: vessel disturbed from within. Cause unknown.*

He looked at what he had written for a long moment. Then he added:

*Not reported.*

His pen trembled slightly on the final stroke. In twenty years of keeping the Veiled Guild's accounts, Theo Iversen had never once failed to report an anomaly. He had reported the night the bottled storms all faced the same direction. He had reported the year that escaped and had to be chased through the market in a net. He had reported his own master's embezzlement, back when he was nineteen and believed that accuracy was a form of love.

But this — a jar of a dead woman's sorrow pressing its face to the glass, wanting out, wanting *in*, wanting something — this felt less like an anomaly and more like a letter addressed to him personally, and he was not ready to read it aloud.

Outside the arrow-slit window, the first snow of the season came down without wind, straight as plumb lines, as if the sky were being very careful.

* * *

In the morning the market woke below the tower, and Theo went down among it because the masters required a tally-taker at the frost-gate, and because — though he would have denied it under torture — he liked the noise.

The Veiled Guild's market did not appear on any map. It occupied the space between a fishmonger's alley and a tanner's yard in the city of Vhalen's Reach, and you entered it by walking past a certain leaning wall and forgetting, briefly, what you had come out to do. Most people forgot permanently. Those who remembered — the desperate, the curious, the already-compromised — found themselves in a square roofed with grey silk, lit by lamps that gave warmth but no light, surrounded by stalls that sold everything the daylight world had agreed not to want.

Theo moved through the crowd with his ledger under his arm and his collar up, nodding to the stall-keepers who nodded back with varying degrees of warmth. Old Semyon, who traded in names — not the people, just the names, which could be removed like coats and worn by others; he was wearing someone else's today, a younger man's, and it fit badly at the jaw. The twins Ilse and Ilka, who sold directions to places that did not exist yet, guaranteed accurate within a decade. The quiet woman at the corner stall whose goods were always covered in cloth and whose prices were always paid in things the buyer would not miss until later.

"Master Iversen." Semyon caught his sleeve as he passed. "A word."

"A tally," Theo said. "You're three weeks behind on your stall-fee, and the fee is due in honest coin, which you don't deal in, which is why we take it in memories at the standard rate, which you've been avoiding."

"I had nothing worth remembering until recently." Semyon smiled with borrowed teeth. "I've acquired something. A memory of the sea, from a sailor who drowned. Very fresh. The salt still stings."

"The standard rate applies."

"The standard rate assumes a *living* donor. This one has no further use for it. Surely that's worth a discount."

Theo wrote it down. He always wrote it down; the pen was quicker than his patience. "Ten percent off for posthumous sourcing. Not a copper more. And Semyon — if the memory starts whispering at night, that's not my department. Take it to the vaults yourself, don't leave it in your stall again. Last time it kept the whole row awake reciting coordinates."

"It was singing."

"It was *navigating*. There's a difference, and the difference is that navigation attracts things." Theo closed the ledger. "Frostmonth the twentieth. Pay by then or I log the arrears in red, and you know what red means."

Red meant the retrievers. Every market-goer knew what red meant.

Semyon's smile flickered — genuinely, for half a second, the borrowed face slipping like a mask in wind — and Theo felt the small, familiar ache of the job. He was good at this. He was fair, which in the Veiled Guild was rarer than honesty and worth more. People paid their debts to him who would cheat anyone else, because Theo Iversen wrote down every promise in both parties' own words and never added a cruelty of his own.

They paid him because he was fair. They did not invite him anywhere. Twenty years, and he could count his invitations to supper on one hand, and two of those had been attempts to bribe him.

He was walking past the fountain — dry for sixty years, filled instead with a slow drift of falling feathers that never landed — when he heard the child crying.

* * *

It was nothing remarkable, at first. Children cried in the market often enough; they cried because their parents had sold something that shouldn't have been for sale, or because they had seen the thing under the cloth at the corner stall, or simply because the market pressed on young minds like deep water. The Guild's rule was to ignore them. Tears were currency here too, somewhere, and sentiment was bad bookkeeping.

But this child was crying in a language Theo did not know, and he understood every word.

He stopped between one step and the next, so abruptly that a man carrying a cage of reversed birds — flying backward, always toward their own tails — swore and jostled him. The words went through Theo like cold water through cloth. He could not have said how he knew them. They were not Vhaleni, nor old court speech, nor the trader-pidgin of the coast, nor anything from the forbidden folios he had secretly copied as a young man. They were older than the shape of his mouth, and yet his mouth knew them, the way a door knows its key.

*It's almost time,* the child was saying, over and over, to no one, to everyone, to the feathers that fell and never landed. *It's almost time and the doors are shut and I can't find the door, I can't find the door—*

She stood near the frost-gate, the great iron-bound arch at the market's north end that led nowhere — that had been sealed since before Theo's grandfather's grandfather, its lock a slab of black metal with no keyhole, its surface always cold enough to burn. She was small, six years old perhaps, seven, dressed in summer clothes stiff with frost, barefoot on the ice-slick stones, and she had no shadow.

The lamps gave warmth but no light, so shadows in the market were soft things anyway, suggestions rather than facts. But everyone had one. Even the reversed birds cast shadows that flew ahead of them. Even the memories in Semyon's jars, if you tilted the light right.

The girl cast nothing. The flagstones beneath her showed her feet and then the stone itself, clean as unwritten paper.

Around her, the market had gone quiet in the particular way markets go quiet — not all at once, but in a spreading ring, stall-keepers finding reasons to examine their wares, buyers remembering urgent business elsewhere. The quiet animals make when something walks through the herd that the herd does not have a name for.

Theo should have fetched the wardens. That was procedure. Unregistered persons appearing inside the Veil were to be reported to the wardens, who reported to the under-masters, who reported to Corvane, and Corvane weighed everything that came through the gate and set its price. That was the whole architecture of the Guild, the reason it had stood five hundred years while kingdoms rotted around it: nothing entered unweighed, nothing left unpaid, nothing was ever, ever free.

The girl lifted her head and looked at him.

Her eyes were the grey of the ledger pages, and they were full of a knowledge that had no business in a face that small, and when she saw him she stopped crying — stopped as if a hand had closed gently over the sound — and said, in that language older than kingdoms, in a voice like a bell heard through snow:

*"Keeper. You kept the count. You kept it true."*

Theo's heart was doing something irregular. He knelt, slowly, the way one kneels to frightened animals and unstable cargo, and heard himself answer in the same impossible tongue, fluently, without accent, as if he had spoken it all his life and merely forgotten:

"You're freezing. Where is your mother?"

The question seemed to strike her strangely. She considered it with grave attention, the way she might consider a locked box.

*"Mothers are doors too,"* she said. *"Mine closed."*

Somewhere above them, in the tower, a bell began to ring — not the tally-bell, not the market-bell, but the third bell, the one that rang only on the eve of the solstice, the one Theo had heard every winter of his life and never once thought to ask about. Its voice rolled down through the grey silk roof, and every lamp in the market guttered at once, and the feathers in the dry fountain all fell an inch closer to the ground without landing.

The girl turned her face toward the sound. Then she turned it back to Theo, and reached out, and took hold of his coat with a fist that gripped like the drowning.

*"You understand me,"* she whispered, and now the tears came again, hot and fast, steaming in the cold. *"Nobody understands me. You understand me. Don't let them weigh me."*

Behind him, at the mouth of the market, boots rang on stone — the wardens' measured tread, coming to investigate the silence, the shadowless child, the third bell. Procedure. Report to the wardens, the wardens to the under-masters, the under-masters to Corvane, and Corvane would weigh her, and whatever she was, whatever door she had lost, whatever *almost time* meant — it would be entered in the red ledger, priced, and sold.

Theo Iversen had followed every rule for twenty years. He had never lied in a ledger, never skimmed a scale, never asked where things came from and never failed to report what they did.

He put his hand on the girl's head, feeling the strange cold of her hair, hair that had never known sun, and heard himself say, quietly, in the language under kingdoms:

"Come with me. Now."

And the bells rang on above them, counting down to the longest night.
