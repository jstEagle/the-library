## 2. A Knock Before Dawn

The knock came at the hour when even the candles forget they are lit.

Theo Iversen heard it through three floors of stone and did not move at first. He was at his desk in the counting room of the Veiled Guild's tower, quill suspended over a column of figures that refused to balance — six vials of bottled thunder against four, which meant someone had been drinking the weather again — and for a long moment he told himself the sound was only the tower settling. Old buildings knock. Old buildings groan and shift and speak to themselves the way old men do.

But the tower beneath him had been silent for twenty years, because the gate at its root had been sealed for twenty years, and sealed things do not knock.

He set down the quill. He listened. The wind worried at the shutters with its usual patience, and somewhere above, the frost on the skylight cracked and whispered as it grew. Nothing else. The tower held its breath, and so did he.

Then it came again: two small knocks, spaced carefully apart, like a child asking permission rather than demanding entry. Not at the tower door. Not at any door a person could reasonably use.

At the *gate*.

Theo rose from his chair, and his knees announced his fifty-one years as they always did on cold nights, and he took the lamp down from its hook because some habits are older than sense. He did not light it. There were no windows between here and the bottom of the stairwell, and a man who has spent his life keeping accounts learns to keep his light the same way — unspent until he knows what it will cost.

The stairs wound down through the tower's spine, past the dormitories where the younger guildsmen slept the sleep of the well-paid, past the archive where the forbidden folios slept less peacefully, down and down into the cold that lived at the tower's roots like a tenant who paid no rent. His breath began to show before the third landing. By the last, it streamed from him like a signal fire.

The gate room was a round chamber hewn from the mountain's hip, older than the Guild, older than the kingdom whose coin the Guild counted. And there, filling the southern wall like a wound that had learned manners, stood the gate itself: a slab of grey stone thirty feet high, carved over every inch with script that predated the alphabet Theo used to keep his ledgers. No seam. No handle. No keyhole. Twenty years ago, when Theo had been young enough to be trusted with nothing, the last Keeper of the Gate had walked through a door that should not have opened, and the stone had closed behind her like water closing over a drowned man, and it had not opened since.

It was against this stone that something knocked a third time.

Small. Patient. Twice more, spaced apart.

Theo stood at the edge of the lamplight spilling down the final stair and felt his heart conduct an inventory of everything he had ever believed about the world, and find most of the entries missing.

"Who's there?" he said, and hated how his voice cracked on the second word.

Silence. Then — and this was the part he would spend the rest of his life failing to explain — an answer. Not words he recognized. A language like riverwater over stones, like frost forming in fast motion, syllables that seemed to arrive already old. It should have been noise to him. Every scholar in the realm would have called it noise, or worse, called it nothing, because no living tongue recorded it and no dead one had left a grammar behind.

But Theo understood it. The way you understand your own name spoken in the dark. The way you understand weeping without translation.

*Cold,* the voice said. *The road was long and the cold came into me and would not leave.*

His hand found the wall. "There's no way in," he said stupidly, to a sealed gate, to whatever stood on the other side of twenty years of silence. "The gate is sealed. It doesn't open."

*I know,* the voice said, and there was something in it that might have been a smile, if smiles could be tired. *I am not coming through it. I am coming around it. Everything comes around it eventually. That is why it is here.*

And then the knocking stopped, and the silence changed quality — thickened, the way air does before snow — and Theo watched, lamp trembling in his grip, as the frost on the gate's carved surface bloomed outward from a single point at the base of the stone. A handprint's worth of white. Then a small shape pressed against the stone from the *outside*, visible through the rock as though the granite had turned briefly to smoked glass: a head, shoulders, the outline of a child no higher than his hip.

The stone did not open. The stone simply stopped being entirely between one place and another, the way a rule stops mattering to someone who has never heard of it, and a girl stepped out of the wall of the world into the gate room, and the frost closed behind her seamless as ever.

She stood swaying in the cold, small hands bare, hair white-blond and crusted with rime, wearing a coat that had been fine once and was now only determined. She looked up at Theo with eyes the colour of deep ice under pressure — not blue, exactly; blue was too warm a word — and she said, in that impossible language that his bones understood before his mind caught up:

*You're the one who keeps the numbers. I can hear them. They tick in you like a clock.*

"I—" Theo's mouth, which had negotiated prices for bottled storms and sold-back griefs without a tremor, failed him entirely. "I don't—"

*You do,* she said. *You count everything. That's good. Counting keeps things in their shapes.* She swayed again, and this time her knees went, and Theo moved without deciding to, crossing the frozen floor in four strides to catch her before she struck the stone.

She weighed almost nothing. That was the first wrong thing, after the gate, after the language. Children are dense with life, warm as coals, heavy with the future. This girl weighed like a bundle of dried stalks, like an empty coat, and she was *cold* — not fever-cold, not dying-cold, but cold the way the space between stars is described in books Theo wasn't supposed to have read. Cold that had come from very far away and settled in for a long stay.

He gathered her up anyway. What else does a body do? The mind can stand in the doorway of a mystery all night; the arms decide much faster.

* * *

By lamplight, in the gate room, with the girl wrapped in his own cloak atop the ledger-desk he'd carried down from the counting room, Theo conducted the closest thing to an appraisal his conscience would permit, which was to say he looked at her and let himself be afraid.

She was perhaps seven years old. Her skin was pale as paper but not bloodless — there was color in her cheeks, faintly, the way there is color in dawn if you catch it early enough. Her breathing was slow and even. Frost melted off her hair and darkened the desk in little rings, and where each drop fell, Theo noticed — and made himself look twice, and looked a third time — the wood did not stain. It *brightened*. As though the desk remembered being a tree and was briefly glad.

But it was the lamp that taught him the truest thing, and the worst one.

He held the flame near her face, gently, the way you check whether a sleeper still breathes. And the girl's shadow — which every living thing casts, which kings and beggars and rats and roaches cast alike, which is the one debt every creature owes the light — did not appear on the wall behind her.

The lamplight fell on her and simply continued, unbroken, to the stones beyond. She sat inside the light like a hole cut in the world, and where a shadow should have been there was only wall, clean and ordinary and utterly wrong.

Theo lowered the lamp. His hands were shaking now, and he watched them shake with the detached interest of a man watching figures refuse to sum.

"No shadow," he murmured. "No shadow at all."

The girl's eyes opened. He hadn't seen them close.

*Shadows are debts,* she said, in the riverwater language, and he understood, God help him, he understood every word. *I paid mine ahead. Or it was taken. I don't remember which. There's a great deal I don't remember, and some of it is remembering itself right now, walking toward us across the winter.*

"What," Theo said slowly, "is walking toward us?"

She considered him for a long moment. Up close, in the lamplight, her strange eyes had depth to them — not the depth of a child's gaze but the depth of a well, of a door left open onto a stair going down further than houses go.

*The thing that wears the crown,* she said. *It is patient. It has been patient for a hundred years, which to it is the length of one held breath. But the Veil is thinning, keeper-of-numbers, and thin things tear, and when it tears it will come through wearing the king's face, and it will be hungry in the way that hunger was invented to imitate.*

"The Veil." The word arrived in Theo's mouth from somewhere older than his memory of receiving it. Every guildsman knew the term; it was practically the Guild's coat of arms. The Veil between the world and the *other* market, the deep market, the one where the Guild's stranger stock originated — bottled storms, borrowed years, grief by the ounce. Everyone knew the Veil the way everyone knows the sea: something you trade across and never think about too hard.

Except the girl said the word the way priests say the name of God, and Theo heard, underneath her pronunciation, the echo of the script carved over every inch of the sealed gate. The same word. The gate was carved with the Veil's own name, over and over, like a warning, like a prayer, like a lock.

"You're speaking the gate's language," he said.

*I am the gate's language,* she said, and then her eyes drifted closed again, genuinely this time, and her breathing slowed toward sleep, and Theo was left alone in the freezing round room with a lamp, a ledger-desk, and a sleeping impossibility.

* * *

He did not sleep. He sat across from her and did what he had always done when the world refused to make sense: he wrote it down.

*Solstice eve. One (1) item recovered at the sealed gate, south face. Item: female child, approx. 7 years. Condition: hypothermic, weight anomalous (low). Speaks unrecorded language — comprehension involuntary on my part, total. Shadow: absent. Effect on local matter: restorative (?). Claims concerning "the Veil," "the crowned thing," pending verification.*

He stared at the entry. Somewhere in the tower above him, the Guild slept — Corvane in the master's quarters with his rings and his ledgers of ledgers, the appraisers with their scales that weighed things like regret, forty souls who had built a fortune on the honest principle of never asking where anything came from. Forty souls who would wake in a few hours and begin the day's weighing.

And Theo understood, sitting in the cold with his pen hovering, that he was holding the day's strangest stock in his own two hands, and that the Guild had exactly one procedure for unregistered goods.

Appraisal. Holding. Sale.

He thought of the widow, years ago, who had sold her grief by the ounce because she couldn't afford bread and couldn't bear the weight of both. He had entered the transaction in his neat hand and never asked where the grief went, and one day he had seen a courtier at the capital wearing a brooch that wept, quietly, at dinner, and he had known, and he had said nothing, because saying nothing was the job, was the whole architecture of his life, load-bearing silence.

The girl stirred. In her sleep she said something soft in the old language, and Theo leaned close to catch it, and it was a single word, repeated, the way children repeat the thing that frightens them most until the fright wears smooth:

*Aldric. Aldric. Aldric.*

The sleeping king's name. The king who had not been seen in public for three years, whose proclamations came from behind palace doors in handwriting that had changed, whose winter — Theo had read the grain reports, it was his job to read the grain reports — had begun to behave like a winter that intended to stay.

The lamp guttered. Outside, the solstice night pressed against the tower like a palm against a window.

And Theo Iversen, who had never broken a rule in twenty years of service, who balanced every column and asked no questions and kept the silence that kept the Guild alive — Theo looked at the sleeping child with no shadow, and heard in his head the tick of his own endless counting, and understood suddenly, with the clarity of a ledger line drawn straight, that some things must never be entered in any book the Guild keeps. Because anything entered can be appraised. Anything appraised can be priced. And anything priced—

Anything priced gets sold.

He tore the page from his notebook, folded it small, and fed it to the lamp. The ink curled and went up in a breath of smoke that smelled, faintly and impossibly, of spring.

Then he lifted the girl — she weighed almost nothing, she weighed like a promise — and climbed the stairs away from the gate, away from the morning's masters, looking for a place in the tower where a child could be hidden from the very people he had served all his life, knowing even as he climbed that there was no such place, that Corvane missed nothing, that the arithmetic of this night could not balance.

He hid her anyway.
