## 3. What the Masters Weigh

Theo Iversen had lied exactly once in twenty years of ledgers, and it took him less time than pouring tea.

But the morning came first — a gray, iron-colored morning on which frost had climbed the inside of the tower windows in feathered patterns that looked, if he squinted, like script. He had learned long ago not to read frost. Frost was only water remembering how to be still. He made the tea anyway, two cups out of habit before correcting to three, and carried them up the narrow stair to the room above the counting floor where the girl had slept.

She was awake. She was always awake when he arrived, as though she heard his footsteps through stone, through timber, through whatever it was she was made of instead of ordinary childhood. She sat cross-legged on the cot with her hands folded and her eyes on the window, watching the frost-feathers the way scholars watched rare manuscripts.

"Good morning," Theo said.

She turned. Her mouth shaped words in that language older than kingdoms, older than the Guild, older — he was beginning to suspect — than the idea of kingdoms at all. And again, as it had every time since the night she knocked at his sealed gate, the meaning arrived in his head fully formed, like coins counted into his palm by an unseen hand.

*The cold is speaking,* she said. *It is frightened.*

"Frost isn't frightened," Theo said mildly, setting down her cup. "Frost is just frost."

She looked at him with an expression of vast, patient pity — the expression of someone who has seen the ocean explain itself to a fish. Then she drank her tea in small careful sips, holding the cup in both hands, and Theo sat across from her and did not tell her what today was.

Today was the solstice audit.

Every year on the longest night's eve, Guildmaster Corvane descended from his offices above the Salt Gallery and walked the ledger-keeper's tower from cellar to weathervane, checking the accounts against the stock. Bottled storms were uncorked a breath and recorked. Borrowed years were sampled against their receipts. Everything that could be weighed was weighed, and everything that couldn't was burned, sold, or buried beneath the counting floor where the Guild kept its deepest silences.

And this year there was a girl in the tower who had no entry anywhere in any ledger. A girl who had appeared at a gate that had been sealed for four hundred years. A girl with no shadow.

Theo had spent the night before with his pen hovering over the great book, trying to write her in. He had tried *foundling, one (1)*. He had tried *inventory, unclassified*. He had even, in a moment of desperation, tried *weather, anomalous*. The ink would not hold. It pooled on the page and slid off, beading like rain on oiled leather, until the paper showed no mark at all. He had blotted it, sanded it, tried a different nib, a different ink, a different page. Nothing.

The ledger did not know what she was. And if the ledger did not know, then Corvane would want to find out, and Corvane finding things out was a process Theo had watched ruin better men than himself.

"You should stay behind the door," he told her now, gesturing at the little closet off her room where cleaning supplies stood in ranks. "When the masters come. Just for an hour. Can you do that?"

She considered him. Then she shook her head once, slowly, and said something in her old tongue that translated itself into his mind with terrible gentleness:

*Liar.*

"I'm not—" He stopped. He set down his own cup. "I am trying to protect you."

*I know,* she said. *That is why you are bad at it. You have never done it before.*

He laughed despite himself — a short, startled sound — and the girl almost smiled, which was rarer in her than snow in July. Then her face went still again, and she pointed one small finger toward the window, toward the north, and spoke a word he had heard her repeat before. A word like a name worn smooth by centuries of saying. He wrote it later in the margin of his private notebook, in pencil, because ink refused her too.

Then the bells rang, and boots began to climb his stairs, and there was no more time for anything.

* * *

Corvane came with six appraisers and a clerk carrying a portable scale.

Guildmaster Corvane was not a large man. He had the build of a well-kept blade — narrow, balanced, nothing wasted — and he dressed entirely in charcoal so that, in certain lights, he seemed to be an absence walking through the room. His voice was soft. Everyone in the Guild knew to be afraid of the soft voice; the loud ones were merely angry, but the soft ones were working.

"Ledger-keeper." He nodded to Theo, and to the scale-bearer: "Begin at the cellars. I'll take the upper floors myself."

"The storm-jars are all accounted," Theo said, falling into step beside him. "I reconciled against the autumn manifest last week. The widow's grief—"

"In a moment." Corvane paused on the landing. His nostrils flared slightly, the way they did near strong inventory. "Something up here smells like the space under bridges."

Theo's stomach performed a slow, deliberate revolution. "There's been damp."

"There is never damp in your tower, Iversen. You'd sooner let the roof fall than admit weather to your accounts." But he continued upward, and Theo followed, and each stair was a rung on a ladder descending somewhere much worse than a cellar.

At the top, Corvane stopped in the doorway of the girl's room.

She was sitting on the cot, hands folded, looking at him with the calm of deep water. Behind her, the closet door stood open a hand's width. She had not hidden. Of course she hadn't. She had simply left the door open enough to make the point that hiding was a thing she permitted others.

Corvane looked at her for a long moment. Then he looked at the wall beside her, where her shadow should have fallen in the lamplight, and Theo watched the Guildmaster arrive at the same conclusion the lamplight already had.

"Well," Corvane said softly. "Unregistered stock."

"She's a foundling," Theo said. "Came to my gate during the storm two nights ago. I was going to file her this morning."

"At your gate." Corvane did not look away from the girl. "Your sealed gate."

"The storm must have—"

"Iversen." Still softly. Still without turning. "Do you imagine I don't know the weight of that door? Do you imagine anyone imagines anything about that door? It has not opened since the Founding. It does not open for storms. It opens for keepers, or it opens for the end of the world, and you are standing here, alive, so we may assume it was not the latter." Now he turned, and his eyes were pale and interested, which was the most dangerous color they came in. "So. Either you opened it — which would be treason against four hundred years of Guild law — or it opened itself for *this*, which is considerably worse, and either way, ledger-keeper, you are going to stand very still while I decide what you are worth."

The appraisers filed in behind him. They were quiet men and women in gray, and they moved around the girl with the unhurried economy of people who had measured everything — grief by the ounce, years by the dram, love by the length of its shadow — and found the world wanting.

One of them held a lens of smoked crystal to his eye and leaned close. Whatever he saw made him step back sharply.

"Master." His voice had gone thin. "It's not wearing a shadow. It's *wearing the absence*. There's structure in the dark around her. Like a mold. Like a cast." He swallowed. "Like something was poured into the world and the world shaped itself around her."

"Value?" Corvane asked.

"To whom, Master?"

"Don't insult us both."

The appraiser lowered the lens. "To the buyer in the capital? Beyond appraisal. This isn't stock. This is *provenance*." He wet his lips. "If the stories about the Veil are true — if any fragment of the First Binding survives in living form — then whoever holds her holds the hinge. Every trade the Guild has ever made went through the Veil. She's not an item. She's the *road*."

The room was silent except for the wind testing the shutters.

And Theo Iversen, who had spent twenty years being the man whose numbers never wavered, whose word was collateral, whose signature closed more bargains than any master's seal — Theo felt something in his chest go very quiet and very cold, and in that cold he discovered a fact about himself that he had never had occasion to learn: he would burn the whole ledger before he let them weigh her.

"File her for appraisal," Corvane said to the clerk. "High security. The Salt Gallery vault, not the general holdings. Ledger-keeper — a full report on my desk by dusk. How she came, what she said, what she carries." He paused at the door, and glanced back with something that might, in a kinder architecture of a face, have been regret. "You've served the Guild faithfully, Iversen. Don't make me learn what else you can serve."

They took her down the stairs between them, a small gray procession, and the girl did not struggle. At the landing she turned her head and found Theo's eyes, and said nothing aloud, but the meaning arrived anyway, patient as tide:

*Now you begin.*

* * *

Interlude — the offices of the Guildmaster, that same hour

Corvane poured himself a measure of plum spirit aged in a cask that had once held thirty years of a condemned man's patience, and considered the report forming in his head like frost.

Unregistered stock. No — worse. *Pre-registered.* Stock that predated registration itself. In nine hundred years of Guild accounting, the books recorded three objects that refused ink, and all three had ended dynasties. Two had ended well. One had ended the Second Kingdom, and the Guild's founding charter existed largely as an apology for it.

And the gate. The sealed gate beneath Iversen's tower, opening after four hundred years, for *that*. Corvane did not believe in coincidence; coincidence was a word clerks used when the arithmetic exceeded their courage. The gate had opened because something on the other side had finished waiting. Or because something on this side had finally become worth opening for.

He thought of the buyer in the capital. The correspondence from the court had grown strange of late — letters written in Aldric's own hand, in Aldric's own cipher, requesting items the king had never wanted before: mirrors that showed yesterday, keys to doors that didn't exist yet, quantities of silence sufficient to fill a throne room. Payment offered in sums that made no sense, from a treasury that could not be audited. Corvane had assumed ambition, or madness, or some new fashion among the powerful. He had not assumed hunger. Hunger wore better masks than madness ever had.

If the crowned thing in the capital wanted the girl — and the appraiser's trembling had said it plainly — then the price would be beyond reckoning. A guildmaster's whole life bought and paid for. Perhaps the crown's favor entire. Perhaps, if he negotiated carefully, the Veil's remaining secrets laid out like a merchant's cloth.

He drafted the letter to the capital in his own hand, in cipher, sealing it with the Guild's broken-ring sigil. Then he sat a while longer, turning his cup, thinking about Theo Iversen's face on the landing — the precise moment it had changed. Not fear. Fear he knew in every variety and discount.

It had been the face of a man doing arithmetic and arriving at an answer he intended to act on.

Corvane smiled thinly at the dark window, where the first flakes of the solstice snow were beginning to fall, and made a small note in his private book: *Watch the ledger-keeper. Counters lie best when they count for something new.*

* * *

By dusk Theo had written the report. It lay on Corvane's desk, complete, meticulous, signed — and false in every particular that mattered.

He had described a foundling, mute, of perhaps ten years, discovered wandering the market district in the storm. He had invented a grandmother in the Tannery Rows who had died in autumn. He had invented a fever that explained the missing shadow — no, he had not mentioned the shadow at all; the omission would be noted, but omission was survivable where fabrication was fatal. He had reported her speech as gibberish, the random vocalizations of a traumatized child. He had requested — this was the stroke he was proudest of, and ashamed of in equal measure — permission to continue housing her personally, citing the tower's existing security and his own experience with delicate inventory. Better she under his key than in the Salt Gallery vault, surrounded by Corvane's gray men.

It was the first lie in twenty years of ledgers, and it had taken him four hours, three drafts, and the destruction of two pages he could not risk leaving in his own waste-basket. When it was done he sat back and looked at his hands, which had not shaken once, and understood that they never would again. That was the thing fear did, eventually, if you fed it properly: it hardened into something with edges. Something you could carry. Something you could use.

Below the tower, the market was lighting its solstice lamps, and the snow was thickening, and somewhere north a false king sat at a lit window moving half a beat behind his own body — though Theo did not know that yet. What Theo knew was smaller and closer: the girl had called his report a lie before he wrote it. Had named him *liar* at breakfast with gentle contempt, and then, at the landing, with the appraisers' hands nearly on her shoulders, she had looked back at him with something that was not reproach.

Trust, maybe. Or instruction.

He took out his private notebook and turned to the page where he had copied, in pencil, the word she repeated like a name. Around it, over the past day, other fragments had accumulated — sounds she made at the frost, at the lamps, at the sealed door downstairs — and he had begun, in the scholar's corner of his mind that never slept, to sort them. Some were grammar. He was nearly sure some were grammar.

And one phrase, recurring whenever her eyes went to the north wall, had begun to resolve itself into something that was not a name at all, but a warning, or a plea, or a map:

*Before the weighing ends. Before they sell the road. Find the ferry-woman who owes you. She owes you more than you remember.*

Theo blew out the lamp, pocketed the notebook, and went down through the sleeping tower toward the frozen market, to call in a debt he could not recall ever being owed.

The ice on the canal had begun, very quietly, to sing.
