## 4. The Language Under Kingdoms

The forbidden folios lived in the third drawer of Theo's desk, beneath a false bottom of seasoned oak that had fooled two generations of guild auditors. He had inherited the drawer from his predecessor, old Master Fenwick, along with the tower, the ledgers, and the particular loneliness of a man who kept accounts for things that should not exist. Fenwick had left no note explaining the books. He had simply died at his desk one autumn morning, pen still wet, and Theo — twenty-three years old and terrified of doing anything wrong — had opened the false bottom within the hour, because grief makes thieves of careful men.

Now, with the girl asleep in the cot behind the ledger-room's iron stove, her breath making small clouds in the cold, Theo laid the folios out on his desk like instruments of surgery.

There were seven of them. Seven books the Guild officially did not possess, written in hands dead before the first Aldric took a throne. He knew them the way a priest knows scripture he is not permitted to read aloud: *The Concordance of Sealed Tongues*, its vellum soft as cloth from centuries of turning; *A Grammar of the Gates*, half-eaten by mold but whole where it mattered; three volumes of gate-keeper's marginalia bound together by someone who had run out of patience with order; and two folios so old their titles had flaked away entirely, leaving only the ghost of ink where words used to be.

He had never dared study them properly. That was the truth he kept folded away beneath the false bottom alongside them. He had catalogued them, yes — Theo Iversen catalogued everything; it was how he loved things — but reading was different from cataloguing. Reading was an act of appetite. And the Guild's first law, the one carved into the lintel of every door in the under-tower, was simple: *Nothing passes unweighed.* Not goods. Not years. Not knowledge.

But the girl had spoken to him last night, in the dark before dawn, in a language older than any kingdom, and he had understood her. Understood her without effort, the way one understands hunger or fear or the meaning of a hand held out in snow. And when she had pressed her palm flat against the sealed gate — the gate he had tended for twenty years without once hearing it make a sound — the iron had *hummed*.

Theo Iversen had never broken a rule. But rules, he was beginning to suspect, were like the Veil itself: they held only so long as no one asked what they were holding back.

He opened the *Concordance* first.

* * *

The work went slowly, and it went wonderfully.

That was the thing no one warned you about forbidden knowledge: it was not a door kicked open but a slow thaw, ice giving way inch by inch, and every inch revealed something more beautiful than the last. The Concordance organized tongues the way his ledgers organized debts — by lineage, by collateral, by what each language could be traded *for*. Most of its entries were dead ends. Merchant-cants. Priest-codes. The whistled trade-speech of drowned coastal towns. But near the end of the volume, past a page whose margins someone had burned carefully, as if cauterizing a wound, he found it:

*Tongue of the Under-Kingdoms. Spoken by none. Understood by four. Do not transcribe. Do not answer. If heard, report to the Masters.*

Understood by four. Theo sat back, and the chair creaked in the silent tower, and outside the wind worried at the shutters like a dog at a bone. Four keepers. Four gates. He had known the number all his life the way he knew his own name — it was in the Guild's founding charter, recited at every induction, drained of meaning by ritual: *the fourfold seal, the fourfold watch.* He had assumed the other three gates were metaphor. Or lost. Or both.

He looked through the doorway into the little room where the girl slept. She had no shadow. In the stove-light her small body cast nothing at all against the wall, and the wall seemed to lean toward her slightly, the way flowers lean toward a window.

*If heard, report to the Masters.*

He turned back to the folio.

The grammar, when he began to assemble it from fragments — the Concordance's refusal to transcribe, the mold-eaten *Grammar of the Gates*, the desperate marginalia of long-dead keepers — was unlike any tongue built by living mouths. Human languages named things. This one *un-named* them. Its verbs had no tense because its speakers had stood outside time. Its word for *door* and its word for *wound* were the same word, differentiated only by a breath-mark that meant, roughly, *chosen*. A door was a wound you agreed to. A wound was a door forced upon you.

Theo wrote until his candle guttered, filling a fresh ledger — a private one, unnumbered, hidden now inside the false bottom where the folios lived — with transliterations and guesses. His handwriting, usually so precise the Masters joked it could be used to calibrate scales, grew ragged with excitement. He caught himself smiling at three in the morning, alone, hunched over stolen grammar like a man drinking in a cellar, and the smile frightened him more than the knowledge did. Because this was what Corvane would see, if Corvane ever saw it: not scholarship. Appetite.

Near dawn the girl woke. He heard the cot creak, heard her bare feet on stone, and then she was standing beside him, looking down at the pages upside-down, which children can do effortlessly and scholars cannot at all.

She pointed at one word. The word he had marked with three asterisks, because it appeared in every fragment, in every keeper's hand, across four hundred years of marginalia. It was always underlined. Sometimes it was underlined so hard the stylus had torn the vellum.

She said it aloud.

It sounded like water finding a crack in stone. It sounded like a bell rung underwater. It was two syllables and it rearranged something in the room — the stove-fire leaned, the shadows of the tools on the wall shifted though nothing had moved — and Theo understood it instantly, completely, the way he had understood everything she said, and the understanding arrived with its price attached, the way all true understanding does:

It was a name.

*Aldric.*

Not the name of the living king in his capital. An older shape of it, a root-word, the way a river's source looks nothing like its mouth. The sleeping king. The king beneath the fourth gate. The king whose crown the Veil existed to hold shut like a lid on a well.

"How do you know that name?" Theo whispered, in guild-cant, and then, feeling foolish and certain at once, said it again in hers — the two syllables of the name itself, which was also the question, because in her language a name spoken was always a question: *is this still true?*

The girl looked at him with eyes the color of deep ice over deep water, and she answered with the most terrible grammar he had yet encountered. She used the construction the marginalia called *the standing tense* — the tense reserved, according to a keeper dead eight centuries, for things that had not ended and therefore could not be spoken of in the past.

She said: *he waits.*

Then she touched the page, touched the torn vellum where four hundred years of keepers had underlined the name, and said something else, gently, the way one tells a child the truth about death. Theo worked through it twice, three times, refusing his own translation, and then refused no longer.

*I am what holds him. When I am spent, he wakes.*

* * *

He did not sleep. He made tea he did not drink and sat at his desk while the tower filled with grey light, and he did the thing he was best at in all the world: he counted.

The Guild's official histories said the Veil was a boundary, a treaty, a wall between the world of bargains and the world of men. The folios said otherwise. The folios said the Veil was a *person* — or had been, once, four persons, four keepers who had woven themselves into the seal rather than trust it to mere magic, because magic could be bought and people, supposedly, could not. Three of the four lines had ended. The marginalia recorded the endings with a brevity that Theo now recognized as grief: *the ferry-keeper drowned, 611. The archive-keeper burned with her archive, 703. The market-keeper sold himself, year unknown.*

Sold himself. Theo read that phrase eleven times. What did it mean? Sold himself *to whom*? For what price? And the fourth line, the gate-keepers, his own line — the marginalia simply stopped mentioning them, somewhere in the reign of the second Aldric, as if the writers had looked away from something they could not bear to watch.

And now a child had walked out of the winter dark to his sealed gate, carrying no shadow, speaking the standing tense, saying *I am what holds him* as calmly as other children said *I am hungry.*

Every impossible thing Theo had ever entered in the Guild's ledgers — bottled storms, borrowed years, the widow's grief sold by the ounce — suddenly reorganized itself in his mind into a single horrifying column. All of it had passed *through* the Veil. All of it had been skimmed off the thin places, the way a corrupt toll-keeper skims every cart on the road. The Guild had not been guarding the border between worlds. The Guild had been *farming* it. Keeping it thin enough to trade through, thick enough to hold a king — and never once, in six hundred years of meticulous accounts, recording the cost.

Because there was a cost. There was always a cost; Theo had built his life on that axiom. And the cost of six centuries of skimming was sitting at his stove with no shadow, being spent, being *used up*, a candle burning from both ends by an institution that had forgotten it was a candle at all and thought itself a lamp.

When I am spent, he wakes.

He got up and checked the lock on the gate. He did not know why. The lock was fine; the lock was always fine; he had oiled it yesterday. He checked it anyway, and then he climbed the tower stairs and looked out at the frozen city, at the smoke rising thin and straight from a thousand hearths, at the market square where the Guild's stalls were already opening, already weighing, already trading in the skimmed edges of a seal that was failing — and he felt the thrill of the night's scholarship curdle, slowly and completely, into responsibility.

This was the difference, he understood now, between collecting knowledge and owing it. Last night he had been a scholar, delighted, drunk on grammar. This morning he was a debtor. The girl had spoken her name-language to him and he had *answered*, and answering, in her tongue, constituted agreement. He had looked up the construction at dawn with shaking hands. To understand someone in the tongue of the Veil was to accept their testimony. To reply was to countersign.

Somewhere in the false bottom of his desk, in a ledger with no number, Theo Iversen had put his signature to a debt six hundred years old.

* * *

Corvane came at midday, which meant Corvane had heard something.

The Guildmaster never climbed to the ledger-tower unannounced; announcements were how men like Corvane proved the stairs belonged to them. So when the knock came — three measured raps, patient as interest accruing — Theo closed the false bottom, slid the new ledger beneath it, and arranged his face into the expression of a man with nothing in his drawers but accounts.

"Master Iversen." Corvane filled the doorway the way cold fills a room. He was not a large man, but he had the gift of seeming larger than whatever space contained him, and his eyes went around the chamber in a slow circuit — desk, stove, cot, window — with the unhurried thoroughness of an appraiser. "Reports of unusual activity. Lights burning past the second bell. You, I'm told, singing."

"I don't sing, Guildmaster."

"No," Corvane agreed pleasantly. "You don't. Which is why the report interested me." He stepped inside without invitation and stood over the desk, and his gaze rested, for one terrible moment, on the false bottom — on the hairline seam of oak that had fooled two generations of auditors. "Where is the child?"

"Asleep." Theo's voice came out level. Twenty years of ledgers had taught him that level was a choice, made anew each second, like keeping a column of figures from drifting. "She spoke to me in the night. Old speech. I was consulting reference folios."

Something moved behind Corvane's eyes — not surprise; appraisal. "Old speech."

"Tongue of the Under-Kingdoms. It appears in the Concordance. Restricted entry."

"And she speaks it."

"She doesn't speak anything else."

Corvane was quiet for a moment. Outside, the wind had stopped, and the silence was worse. "You understand," he said at last, softly, "what the Masters will conclude. Unregistered stock, appearing at a sealed gate, speaking the sealed tongue. Either she is a fraud sent to test us—" he smiled, and the smile did not involve the rest of his face, "—or she is exactly what the old charters say such a child would be. And either way, Master Iversen, she is worth a great deal more than this tower."

"The charter says the keepers shelter such a child."

"The charter says many things." Corvane turned toward the door, then paused, and delivered the sentence the way he delivered all his sentences, lightly, letting the weight land afterward: "The appraisers arrive within the week. Have your accounts ready. All of them."

*All of them.* Theo stood at his window and watched Corvane cross the frozen courtyard below, small and deliberate as a spider crossing a sheet of ice, and he understood that the man had seen the seam in the drawer, or would claim later that he had, and that the week Theo had just been granted was not a courtesy but a countdown.

Behind him, from the cot, the girl watched him with her colorless eyes. She did not speak. She didn't need to. He was learning her grammar, and silence, in her language, was the standing tense too.

He crossed the room and knelt beside the cot, and — breaking another rule, one no charter had ever written, the rule that said a keeper keeps distance from the stock — he took her small cold hand in his.

"They will come to weigh you," he said in guild-cant, and then, because he had countersigned himself to her in her own tongue and there was no honest way back, he said it again in hers, the syllables strange and right in his mouth.

The girl studied him for a long moment. Then she answered — one word, the same word she had spoken at the gate before dawn, the word the marginalia translated as *stay* and *remain* and *do not let go*, a word with no clean equivalent in any human language because no human language had ever needed it enough.

And far beneath the tower, deeper than the foundations, deeper than the Guild's oldest cellar, something that had slept for six hundred years turned over in its sleep — and Theo, kneeling in the cold with a shadowless child's hand in his, felt the gate hum through the soles of his boots, faint and patient, like a heart remembering how to beat.
