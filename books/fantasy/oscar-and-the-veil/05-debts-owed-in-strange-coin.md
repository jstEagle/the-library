## 5. Debts Owed in Strange Coin

Theo had never called in a debt before.

In twenty years of keeping the Veiled Guild's ledgers he had recorded thousands of them — favors entered in columns, priced and dated, some written in currencies that made the ink crawl: three years of a man's laughter, a mother's memory of her daughter's face, the last words of a drowned sailor sold twice because no one checked. Theo had balanced them all with a steady hand and an untroubled heart, because that was the work. The ledger did not ask where things came from. Neither did he.

But now, at the bottom of his own private book — the slim black volume he kept locked beneath the floorboards of his room, the one Corvane didn't know existed — there were only four entries. Four debts owed to *him*, accumulated across two decades like coins dropped into a well he'd forgotten he owned.

He sat on the edge of his bed with the girl asleep behind the false wall of his linen closet, her breath so faint he had to watch the candle flame bend toward her to be sure she lived, and he read the four entries by the light of that same guttering candle.

*Odile Ferren, ferrywoman of the Undermarket. Owes: passage without price, whenever asked. Given: the return of her brother's name, which she had sold to pay a winter's coal.*

*Marnick Hale, locksmith of the Brass Stair. Owes: one death, postponed. Given: twenty years ago, when I signed the ledger line that let him walk out of the Guild alive instead of into the Appraisal Room.*

*Sister Veyra of the Candlehouse. Owes: one question answered truly. Given: I have not yet asked it.*

*And one entry with no name, only a date thirty years old and a single word in his father's handwriting: "owed."*

He closed the book. His hands were not steady. He noticed this the way he noticed everything about himself lately — from a strange distance, as if he were an entry in someone else's ledger being audited.

The girl stirred behind the wall. He heard her turn, heard the small sound she made in sleep — not a word, exactly, but shaped like one, the old language moving through her even now, like water through a channel cut long before she was born.

Tomorrow, Corvane would come back with his appraisers. Theo had perhaps a day. Maybe less.

He dressed, took the black book, and went down into the market.

* * *

The Undermarket did not open so much as exhale.

It lived beneath the Guild tower in caverns older than the city above, and it kept its own hours — which is to say it opened when enough hungry people had gathered to justify the candles. Theo came down the Brass Stair at the hour the lamplighters called the blind hour, past stalls shuttered with chains and wards, past the fountain where a bottled storm turned slowly on its silver tether, throwing green lightning against the wet stone walls.

At this hour the market was nearly empty. That was why he had chosen it. Debts, like confessions, were best transacted without witnesses.

Odile Ferren was already awake. She was always already awake; the ferrymen of the Undermarket policed the black canal that circled the caverns, the waterway that flowed with no source and no mouth, and the water kept stranger hours than the merchants did. Theo found her at her mooring beneath the Ropewalk arch, mending a net by lantern-light, her gray braid coiled on top of her head like a rope itself.

She looked up when his boots rang on the dock planks, and something moved through her weathered face — surprise first, then a quick, fierce gladness she tried to smother under a scowl.

"Ledger-keeper." She spat over the side, an old habit of respect among ferrymen. "Twenty years you've walked my dock and never once asked for your fare. Now you come at the blind hour with your collar buttoned wrong and your eyes like a man who's seen the bottom of his own well. Sit down before you fall down."

"I need passage," Theo said. "For three. Out of the market, out of the city if it comes to it. Without going through any gate the Guild watches."

Odile set down her net. For a long moment she just looked at him, and Theo realized, with the peculiar vertigo of a man reading a page he thought he knew, that she was afraid — not of him, but *for* him.

"You know what you're asking," she said quietly. "There are two ways off the water unwatched. One is the Drowned Stair, and it takes payment in memories, and it takes them whether you can spare them or not. The other is me, and I take nothing tonight, because—" She stopped. Her jaw worked. "Because you gave me back my brother's name, Theo Iversen, and I have been waiting twenty years for you to need something, and I am ashamed of how glad I am that the day has finally come."

Theo sat down on the dock beside her, quite suddenly, as if his knees had filed a complaint his pride refused to countersign.

"You're glad," he repeated.

"Of course I'm glad." She said it roughly, the way people say tender things when tenderness embarrasses them. "Do you think we don't talk about you? The whole Undermarket talks about you. The ledger-keeper who never rounds against the poor. Who once wrote off a widow's debt because the grief she was selling had gone bad in the jar and he couldn't bear to weigh it. You think that goes unnoticed?" She shook her head. "You've been walking around all these years thinking you're just the man who counts. Child, you're the reason half this market still believes there's such a thing as a fair trade."

Theo looked at the black water. It reflected the lantern and nothing else — no stars, no faces, though he had the sudden irrational feeling that something below was listening.

"The girl," he said. "The one they brought to the gate."

"I know." Odile's voice went flat and hard. "Word moves faster than ferries. Corvane wants her appraised. And everyone with ears knows who buys what Corvane appraises." She stood, and began untying her mooring lines with sharp, economical movements. "Get your girl. Get the locksmith too, if you're fool enough to trust Marnick Hale, which I would not be. We leave within the hour or not at all."

"Odile." He stopped her with the word, and she turned, and he found he did not know how to say what was in him — that he had spent twenty years believing himself alone inside the machine of the Guild, a gear among gears, and that in one night the machine had shown its teeth and the people had shown their faces, and he did not know which discovery frightened him more. What he said instead was: "I'll pay the Drowned Stair's price myself. Whatever memories it asks. Not yours. Not hers."

Odile smiled then, and it transformed her whole face, ten years falling away like a dropped cloak.

"That," she said, "is the first favor you've ever done yourself. Now go. The water won't wait, and neither will Corvane."

* * *

Marnick Hale owed Theo a death postponed, and he had never once pretended otherwise.

The locksmith kept a shop on the fourth landing of the Brass Stair, a space barely wider than his workbench, hung with keys — thousands of them, brass and iron and bone and one, in a velvet case he never opened, carved from a tooth of something that had died before mountains learned their names. When Theo knocked, Marnick opened the door already pale.

"I heard the bells change," he said. "Guild bells don't ring at the blind hour unless someone's being weighed. Come in, come in, and tell me fast, because I've been expecting this knock for twenty years and I still haven't decided what I'll do."

"You'll open a lock," Theo said, stepping inside. "The old gate. Beneath my tower. The one sealed since before either of us was born."

Marnick laughed — one short, unhappy bark. "That's not a lock, that's a wound. My master's master helped seal it. There are wards on it written in a hand nobody living can read, and the last man who touched it lost his shadow, and the shadow walks the lower galleries to this day asking people questions in his own voice." He wiped his hands on his apron, though they were clean. "Why?"

"Because the thing it holds is waking anyway," Theo said. "And the Guild has decided to sell the key."

The shop went very quiet. Outside, far below, the bottled storm threw its slow green light against the ceiling of the caverns, and for a moment every key hanging from every hook seemed to tremble on its ring, a thousand small voices saying *turn me, turn me, turn me.*

Marnick sat down heavily on his stool. "A death postponed," he said. "That's what I owe you. Twenty years ago you signed the line that let me live. I've thought about that night every day since. Do you know what I remember most? Not fear. Shame. Because you were young then, younger than me, and you looked at me across that desk and you said — I've never forgotten it — you said, 'The ledger says you're worth more breathing. I'm inclined to agree.' Like it was arithmetic. Like saving my life was just good accounting." He looked up, and his eyes were wet and furious. "You absolute fool. It wasn't arithmetic. It was the single decent act I ever witnessed in this whole rotten market, and I have spent twenty years waiting to repay it and dreading the day you'd ask, because I always knew the day you asked would be the day the asking would cost you everything."

"It may," Theo admitted.

"Then we'd best make it cost them more." Marnick rose, and began pulling keys from the wall with sudden purpose, filling a leather roll with quick, sure hands. "The gate has three locks. One is mine to open — the physical mechanism, iron and craft, and I can beat it, given time and privacy. The second is a ward-lock, and for that you'll want Sister Veyra, because candle-wax and true answers are the only things that unlock it. And the third—"

He stopped. His hands went still over the leather roll.

"The third isn't a lock at all," he said quietly. "My master told me once, drunk, crying, the way men tell truths. He said the third seal opens only for a keeper who chooses to break his own order. Not forced. Not tricked. *Chosen.* The gate tests the hand that turns it." He met Theo's eyes. "What's in that gate, Theo? What have you got down there that needs a broken man to free it?"

Theo thought of the girl's hand in his. Of the word she spoke that meant *stay* and *remain* and *do not let go*. Of the hum beneath the stones, patient as a heartbeat.

"A king," he said. "Sleeping six hundred years. And a crown that doesn't want him to wake."

Marnick closed his eyes briefly, the way men do before jumping.

"Then God help us," he said, "and mind the third step on the way down. It's been loose since the reign of the last honest guildmaster, and I never fixed it because—" he almost smiled, "—because it felt like the only honest flaw left in this place."

* * *

They gathered in the linen closet behind the false wall, all four of them — Theo, the girl, Odile smelling of black water and lamp oil, Marnick sweating over his leather roll of keys — while somewhere above them the Guild bells began to ring again, and this time it was not the blind-hour toll but the appraisal bell, three strokes, the sound Corvane used when he wanted the whole market to know that something valuable was about to be measured.

The girl stood very still in the candlelight. Then she reached up and took Theo's hand, and turned it palm-up, and traced something in it with one small finger — the old script, the letters that predated kingdoms, drawn slow and deliberate so he would feel each stroke.

He knew the folios well enough now to read it. It was not a warning. It was not the word for *stay*.

It was a name. *Her* name — or rather, the shape of a name, offered the way one offers a coin: freely, and only once.

"She's giving it to you," Odile whispered, and her voice shook. "Saints preserve us. In the old stories, that's how the keepers were bound to the Veil. Not by contract. By gift."

Above them, the appraisal bell finished its third stroke, and boots began to descend the tower stairs — many boots, unhurried, certain, the tread of men who had never once been told no.

Theo closed his fingers around the name, and answered the knock at his door.
