## 6. The Appraisal

Odile had poled her ferry through the drowned dark beneath the market for thirty-one years, and in all that time she had learned one law above every other law the Guild pretended to keep: *if you want to know what a thing is worth, watch what the rich men do when they think no one is looking.*

So she was in the rafters.

Not the honest rafters — there were no honest rafters — but the crawl-space above the Appraisal Hall, where the great beams of black river-oak crossed like the ribs of some leviathan the Guild had once, no doubt, sold by the pound. She lay flat on her belly between two of them, wrapped in her ferrying cloak that smelled of tar and cold water, and she breathed the way she breathed when drifting past the guild-sentinels' skiffs: slow, shallow, patient.

Below her, the Hall of Weights had been transformed. Candles of rendered moonfat burned in iron sconces along the walls, throwing light the color of old teeth. The long weighing-tables had been cleared and draped in white linen, and upon them the appraisers had laid out their instruments: calipers of grey glass, a balance whose pans were cut from a single mirror split down the middle, tuning forks made from the wing-bones of something that had once sung over the northern ice. Odile recognized none of it by name and all of it by instinct. These were tools for pricing souls, or the parts of souls that could be pried loose without killing the body outright. The Guild called that *salvage*.

And in the center of the floor, on a chair too large for her, sat the girl.

They had washed her. They had dressed her in a smock of undyed wool, the kind used for cataloguing stock before valuation, and they had combed the tangles out of her hair with a bone comb that left faint silver threads in the strands — residue of the comb's previous work, which had been untangling a storm from a shipwreck. The girl sat very still. Her hands were folded in her lap like a small woman at her own funeral. And she was not afraid.

That was what frightened Odile.

She had watched children come through this hall before. They wept, or they went silent as stones, or they laughed the terrible laughter of children who have already learned that the world is a market and they are goods. This child did none of these things. She watched the appraisers the way Odile watched the river: with the calm of someone who has seen deeper water.

There was one other thing. Odile had to squint, even now, to be sure of it — but she was sure. The candlelight fell on the girl and pooled around her and did not quite touch her, the way lamplight refuses to sink into deep water. As if the light itself were uncertain whether she was entirely present.

*No shadow,* Theo had told her, three nights ago, his voice low and cracked in the doorway of her ferry-house. *She casts no shadow, Odile. I've checked with three different lights.*

*Iversen,* Odile had said, *you checked? You? The man who checks nothing because checking is asking and asking is dying?*

He had looked at her then with an expression she had never seen on him in twenty years of trading favors across the frozen market — an expression like a man standing at the edge of a hole in the ice and hearing something sing from underneath.

*The rules stopped making sense,* he'd said. *The moment she knocked.*

Now, from her nest in the rafters, Odile watched Guildmaster Corvane enter the Hall of Weights.

* * *

Corvane wore grey, as always — grey wool, grey rings, a grey silk cravat pinned with a brooch shaped like a set of scales, because subtlety was for people who couldn't afford honesty. He was not a tall man, but he moved through rooms the way frost moves through a window: gradually, totally, claiming everything he touched. The appraisers rose when he entered. One of them, a narrow woman named Hess with ink-stained fingers and eyes like ledger-lines, bowed so deeply her spectacles nearly fell.

"Master Corvane. The subject is prepared."

"The subject." Corvane smiled. He had a good smile; Odile would grant him that. It was the smile of a man who had never once been surprised by a price. "Let us see what the winter brought us."

Hess lifted a grey-glass caliper and approached the girl. The child did not flinch. Hess measured the distance between her pupils, the span of her hand, the length of her foot against a brass rule. At each measurement she murmured numbers to a scribe who scratched them into a folio, and at each number the scribe's pen hesitated a fraction longer than it should have, as though the figures were arriving in a language the ink distrusted.

Then Hess took up the mirrored balance.

Odile leaned forward along the beam. She knew this instrument by reputation only. The Mirror Balance did not weigh flesh. It weighed *presence* — the density of a soul's footprint in the world, if you liked poetry; the strength of a thing's claim on reality, if you preferred accounting. They used it on relics. They used it on bottled storms. Odile had heard, once, drunk, from a retired appraiser who wept easily, that they had used it exactly twice on living people, and both times the results had been sealed by order of the Masters, and both times the people measured had been gone within the week.

Hess placed a single hair of the girl's — plucked, Odile noted, with a pair of silver tweezers, and the girl had not even blinked — into the left pan.

The right pan held nothing at all.

The balance tipped anyway. Slowly, majestically, the empty pan sank, and the pan holding the hair rose, and rose, until the hair floated free of the mirror-silver and hung in the air above it, gleaming, and the whole instrument began to sing — a high, thin note like a wet finger circling the rim of a glass, and the candles guttered, and every appraiser in the room stepped back except Hess, who stood transfixed, her lips moving as she read figures off the swinging pointer that Odile could not see but could, from the look on Hess's face, tell were obscene.

"Master Corvane," Hess said, and her voice had gone dry as August. "The reading exceeds our scale."

"Then fetch a larger scale."

"We don't *have* a larger scale. The last time—" Hess stopped herself. Looked at the scribe. Looked at the door. "The last time a reading exceeded scale was the night the Veil was first threaded through the market gates. Two hundred years ago. The record is in the sealed folios."

Corvane's smile did not waver, but Odile saw his hand rise and adjust the scale-brooch at his throat, a small compulsive gesture she had seen him make perhaps four times in thirty years, always before signing something monstrous.

"Then we will value her," he said softly, "by what she is *for*."

* * *

It was at this point that Odile saw Theo.

He came in through the side door with a folio under his arm — the tall, stooped figure of him, all elbows and ink-stains, wearing the expression of a man delivering accounts, which was the only expression that got anyone past Corvane's door unannounced. Odile's heart did something complicated. Part of her wanted to hiss at him like a cat: *go away, you fool, you beautiful rule-keeping fool.* Part of her had known, since the night he'd stood dripping in her doorway with a shadowless child hidden under his own cloak, that he would come. That he could not *not* come. That was the whole trouble with Iversen. He was the only honest man in the Guild, and honesty, given enough pressure, becomes the most dangerous substance in the world.

Theo set the folio on the corner of the nearest table. "Quarterly reconciliations, Master Corvane. For your signature."

"After." Corvane didn't even look at him. "Stay, though, Iversen. Stay and observe. You keep our ledgers; you should know what goes into them." He turned back to the girl, and his voice took on the warm, lecturing tone he used for new apprentices and for victims. "Do you know what the Veiled Guild actually sells, Iversen? Not storms. Not years. Not grief by the ounce, whatever the widow-trade grosses us. Those are *trinkets*. Pocket money. Do you know where the fortune comes from? The real fortune?"

Theo said nothing. Odile, flat against her beam, found she was holding her breath.

"The Veil itself." Corvane spread his hands toward the walls, the hall, the market above and beyond it, the whole frozen labyrinth of impossible commerce. "Every gate in this guildhouse sits on a seam of it. The old keepers wove it tight as a drumhead to hold *him* down — the king under the hill, the crown that must not wake. But a drumhead stretched that tight, Iversen, hums. And everything that hums can be tapped. Every trade we broker passes *through* the Veil, and every passage thins it by a hair, and every hair we thin, we charge for. Bottled storms are just water drawn off the flood. The flood itself—" he smiled again, wider— "the flood is ours. We have spent two centuries keeping the Veil thin enough to trade through and thick enough to hold him. A knife's edge. And we have grown fat walking it."

"That's not possible," Theo said quietly. "The founding charter says—"

"The founding charter says the Veil must be kept *whole*." Corvane waved a hand. "Language, Iversen. Whole means intact. Intact means functioning. A veil that functions lets things pass. We have simply been... facilitating function. At margin. For margin." He glanced at Hess. "Which brings us to the buyer."

Odile saw Theo go very still. She saw it the way you see a mooring line go taut just before the cleat screams.

"Buyer?" Theo said.

"Our client in the capital." Hess had recovered her voice, and she spoke in the brisk tone of a professional relieved to be on solid ground. "The commission arrived at midwinter, sealed with the privy signet. The court requires certain... materials. Rare materials. The buyer specified a vessel of the Veil, living, intact, female, pre-pubescent." She consulted her folio. "Payment has been advanced. Half in gold. Half in—" she paused, and Odile watched the ink-stained fingers tighten on the page— "half in *years*. Forty thousand man-years, drawn against the kingdom's general population, delivery deferred."

"Forty thousand years," Theo repeated. His voice was perfectly level, which is how Odile knew it was taking everything he had. "You're selling them forty thousand years of human life."

"We're *buying* a vessel, Iversen, and the client pays in the only currency it has." Corvane walked slowly around the girl's chair, studying her the way he'd study a shipment of dragon-ivory. "You've noticed the winter, of course. Everyone has. The roads won't clear. The hearths won't take. The Veil is thinning faster than we can tax the traffic — because our client has been drawing on it directly, feeding, growing careless. When the vessel is delivered, the client will bind itself properly, stabilize the seams, and spring will return to the realm. Everyone profits. The realm survives. The girl—" he shrugged, a small elegant motion— "the girl is a door. Doors exist to be walked through."

The girl looked up at him then.

She looked up at Corvane with those eyes the color of the sky just before snow, and she opened her mouth, and she spoke — one word, in that language older than kingdoms, the language Theo had been deciphering in stolen hours from forbidden folios, the word Odile had heard him whisper to himself like a prayer he was ashamed of:

"*Aldric.*"

The name of the sleeping king. The name of the man whose face the crowned thing wore.

Every candle in the Hall of Weights went out at once.

* * *

Darkness. Total, ringing darkness, and in it Odile heard the appraisers stumble and curse, heard Hess drop the calipers, heard Corvane's voice crack out sharp with something that was not fear, exactly, but was fear's well-dressed cousin: *"Relight! Relight, you idiots—"*

And in the blackness, the girl began to speak.

Not the single word this time. Sentences. Whole rivers of it, pouring out of her in the dark, that ancient tongue rolling through the Hall of Weights like water through a breached hull, and Odile pressed her hand against her mouth because she understood none of it and yet she understood *all* of it — the way you understand a lullaby, the way you understand grief. The voice was a child's voice and it was also not; underneath it ran a second voice, vast and slow and sorrowful, the way a bell contains the sound of its own striking.

Flint scraped. A candle caught. Then another.

In the returning light, Odile saw the appraisers standing pale as their own linens. She saw Hess clutching her folio to her chest like a shield. She saw Corvane, two steps back from the girl's chair, his hand pressed flat over the scale-brooch at his throat, breathing carefully through his nose.

And she saw Theo.

Theo had not moved from the table's corner. He stood in the half-dark with the quarterly folio still under his arm, and he was looking at the girl with an expression Odile had no name for — an expression like a man reading, at last, the fine print of a contract he signed twenty years ago without reading. His lips were moving slightly. Repeating the word. *Aldric. Aldric.* Filing it, God help him, filing it even now, the meticulous fool, in the great ledger behind his eyes.

"Remarkable," Corvane said, and his voice had regained its polish, though it cost him; Odile could hear the joints of it creaking. "The asset responds to historical nomenclature. Note it, Hess. It may affect the delivery terms." He straightened his cuffs. "Iversen. Your signature is required on the transfer manifest. The client's couriers arrive within the week."

Theo looked at the folio under his arm. Then at the girl. Then at Corvane.

"You'll have my signature," Theo said, "when the accounts are reconciled."

He bowed — precisely, correctly, the bow of twenty unbroken years — and walked out through the side door with his spine straight as a ledger-line.

Odile lay in the dark of the rafters and felt something ignite in her chest, slow and hot as a coal dropped in straw. Thirty-one years she had ferried the Guild's filth and taken its coin and told herself the water was not her doing. Thirty-one years she had watched the rich men weigh what could not be weighed and had kept her mouth shut and her pole moving.

Forty thousand years. Drawn against the kingdom's general population. Against fishermen. Against children. Against everyone who had ever ridden her ferry and paid her in memories because it was all they had.

She waited until the Hall emptied, until the candles were snuffed and the doors barred and the girl led away between two sentinels whose shadows swallowed hers whole. Then she rolled off the beam, dropped through the gap she'd scouted weeks ago, and slipped out into the frozen dark of the market galleries, moving fast, moving angry, moving toward the one place in the world Theo Iversen would go tonight.

Because she had watched the rich men when they thought no one was looking.

And now she knew exactly what the girl was worth, and to whom — and she knew, with the certainty of a woman who has read a river's currents all her life, that Theo Iversen had already decided to steal her back.

The only question was how much of the market they'd have to burn to do it.
