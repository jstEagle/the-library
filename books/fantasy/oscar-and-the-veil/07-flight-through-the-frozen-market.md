## 7. Flight Through the Frozen Market

The diversion began, as all of Odile's work did, with a lie told beautifully.

Theo heard it from the gallery above the market floor: her voice rising over the stalls like a struck bell, carrying that particular grief-ferryman's cadence she used when she wanted every ear in a room to lean toward her. *"A bottled storm has cracked! The one from the Vessel Row — it's leaking weather!"*

And then the market believed her.

He felt it before he saw it — a pressure change, a taste of ozone and old rain threading through the frozen air. Somewhere down among the stalls, glass was singing a hairline note, and three hundred merchants who traded in impossible things did what impossible merchants always do when catastrophe walks in wearing another man's coat: they ran toward it, to save their stock, to steal their neighbor's, or simply to watch.

Theo pressed himself flat against the ice-rimed balustrade of the upper gallery, the girl tucked against his chest inside Odile's borrowed ferry-cloak, and watched chaos bloom below like frost on a windowpane.

The Veiled Market had been built inside a mountain's abandoned throat, and its architects had never intended warmth. Stalactites hung from the vaulted dark like the teeth of something patient. Lanterns burned blue-white with captured lightning. And between the levels — between the galleries where the Guild kept its ledgers and the floor where the Guild kept its sins — stretched the ice-bridges: spans of frozen waterfall, centuries thick, glazed over until they shone like the blades of enormous knives.

He had crossed them perhaps forty times in twenty years of service. He had never once run across them.

"Listen to me," he whispered into the girl's hair. She smelled of cold stone and, faintly, of summer — an impossibility he had stopped questioning sometime around dawn. "When I lift you, you hold my collar with both hands. You do not look down. Can you do that?"

She looked up at him instead — which was not obedience, exactly, but was very much her — and pressed one small hand flat against his chest, right over his heart, as though confirming it still worked. Then she nodded once, solemn as a judge.

Below, the diversion achieved its second act. Odile had not merely claimed a storm had cracked; she had *produced* evidence. Theo caught a glimpse of her through the crowd — broad-shouldered, silver-stubbled, her ferryman's coat flapping — holding aloft a cracked bottle from which actual wind screamed. Where she had gotten it he could not imagine and did not dare ask. It didn't matter. Half the market was now convinced the weather was escaping, and the other half was trying to corner the market on escaped weather before anyone else thought to.

Somewhere in that roiling mass, Corvane's retrievers were being swept along like stones in a flood. Two of them — he'd marked them earlier by their gray hoods and the way they walked as if the ground owed them money — had been stationed beneath the appraisal chamber. He watched them now, shouldered aside by panicking spice-traders and a man dragging a cage of singing moths, their composure cracking for the first time.

*"Now,"* he breathed, though no one could hear him, though the word was more prayer than signal.

Odile looked up anyway. Across the whole width of the roaring market floor, through smoke and screaming wind and the glitter of falling frost, she found him in the gallery as easily as if he'd been lit by his own small sun. She grinned — a terrible, wonderful grin — tipped an imaginary hat, and hurled the bottle into the center of the crowd.

* * *

What happened next, Theo would remember for the rest of his life in pieces, the way a man remembers a shipwreck: not as a sequence but as a series of terrible gifts.

The bottle shattered. The storm inside it — a real one, young and furious and apparently holding a grudge — erupted outward in a column of rain, hail, and shrieking wind that scattered lanterns like dandelion seeds. In the sudden strobing half-dark, with hail ringing off stalactites and a hundred merchants bellowing about their inventory, Theo stood up, lifted the girl onto his back, and ran for the first ice-bridge.

His boots hit frozen waterfall and the world went glassy and treacherous and *fast*. The bridge sloped upward through the dark, its surface carved with old hand-holds worn smooth by generations of guildsmen, and Theo took them without thinking — left hand here, right foot there, twenty years of ledger-keeper's discipline converting itself, without his permission, into the mechanics of flight. The girl clung to his collar with both hands, exactly as instructed, her breath quick and warm against the back of his neck.

Behind and below, someone shouted. Not the general shouting of the storm-panic — this was specific, targeted, aimed. A name.

*Iversen.*

They knew. Of course they knew. Corvane would have checked the appraisal chamber within minutes of the diversion starting, because Corvane trusted nothing, least of all luck. Theo didn't look back. Looking back cost time, and time, he understood with a clarity that felt almost holy, was the only currency he had ever truly dealt in — and tonight he was spending every coin he owned.

The bridge trembled.

It was not a large trembling. It was the sort of trembling that a smaller, wiser man might have attributed to the storm below, or to his own pounding heart. But Theo had spent two decades cataloguing impossible things, and one of the first lessons the ledger teaches you is that nothing in the Veiled Market trembles without cause. The bridges were anchored at both ends into living rock, and they were old, older than the Guild's charter, and they had been groaning under the weight of three hundred panicking bodies for ten full minutes.

Then a second bridge — the one parallel to his, thirty feet away across the black gulf of the market's central shaft — cracked like a rifle shot.

Screams. The merchants streaming across it froze in a knot, and the knot swayed, and the ice sang a long descending note that Theo felt in his molars. He flattened himself against the span beneath him and pulled the girl around to his chest, wrapping her entirely in the cloak.

"Don't look," he said again, uselessly, and she didn't — she buried her face against his throat and held on, and he felt her heartbeat against his own, quick and steady, quicker than his but steadier, as though fear moved through her differently than it moved through men.

Across the shaft, the cracked bridge gave up its argument with gravity.

It went all at once — not crumbling but *releasing*, the whole hundred-foot span peeling free of the rock and dropping into the dark with its cargo of screaming merchants. Theo heard it fall for four seconds. Four seconds is a long time to listen to people fall. Then the sound ended in a boom of ice-shatter far below, and the market's noise swallowed everything after, and Theo lay on his belly on a frozen waterfall with a child in his arms and discovered that his hands were shaking so badly he could barely grip the handholds.

*Get up.* The thought arrived in Odile's voice, which was absurd, since Odile was somewhere below causing further crimes. *Get up, ledger-man. Grief is for people who aren't being chased.*

He got up.

* * *

The upper bridges were worse and better — worse because they were narrower, better because the crowd had thinned to nothing. Who ran across the high spans? No one. The masters took the stairs; the stairs were for people with rank. The bridges were for goods. For things being moved quietly between the vaults and the auction floors, unregistered stock, items whose existence the ledgers politely declined to record.

For twenty years, Theo had been the man who recorded what the ledgers declined to hold. Tonight he finally understood what that made him.

He ran.

The girl's weight on his back was nothing — she weighed less than a folio of vellum, less than a bottled year, as though whatever she was had decided flesh was optional and had only put on enough of it to be polite. But her grip was iron, and her breath steamed past his ear in little urgent puffs, and twice she made a sound — not words, not quite — a low tonal hum that seemed to come from slightly to the left of where she stood, and each time, the ice ahead of them held.

He noticed it the second time. He was a ledger-keeper; he noticed patterns the way other men noticed pretty faces.

"What are you doing?" he gasped, skidding around a support pillar of black stone furred with frost. "What was that?"

She leaned close to his ear and hummed again — a different note this time, lower, resonant — and thirty feet ahead, where the bridge narrowed to a spine of ice barely a boot wide, a hairline fracture he hadn't seen quietly knitted itself closed, the way water heals behind a swimmer.

Theo laughed.

It burst out of him ugly and startled, halfway to a sob, entirely inappropriate, echoing off the vaulted dark. Because he understood — not fully, not yet, the full understanding would come later in a ferry-house smelling of river-mud and lamp oil — but he understood *enough*. She wasn't just cargo he was stealing. She was holding the road together under his feet. Whatever the Guild's appraisers had measured and weighed and priced, they had priced it wrong, they had priced it *insultingly* wrong, and every step he ran deeper into defiance was a step she was making possible.

"You're keeping me alive," he said wonderingly, into the wind. "You're keeping me alive and I don't even know your name."

She pressed her cheek against his shoulder blade. Whether that was yes or no or simply the closest thing to an answer her stolen mouth could give, he couldn't tell. He ran.

* * *

They nearly died at the third span, and it was his fault.

The route he'd chosen — half-remembered from a delivery he'd made six years ago, escorting a crate of hushed voices from Vault Nine to the auction floor — required a transfer at the Weeping Gallery, where a waterfall had been persuaded, by methods Theo had never asked about, to freeze mid-fall and remain frozen, permanently, as a kind of monument to the Guild's power over natural law. The transfer point was a ledge. The ledge was narrow. And when Theo swung himself onto it with the girl clinging to his back, he found it occupied.

A retriever. Gray-hooded, alone — separated from his fellows by Odile's storm, and clever enough to guess the bridge route when everyone else ran for the stairs. He was younger than Theo expected, with a soft jaw and eyes like coins, and he had a contract-scroll already half-unfurled in one gloved hand.

"Theo Iversen," the retriever said. His voice was pleasant. That was the worst part; they trained them to be pleasant. "Ledger-keeper, first class. Twenty years of service, no infractions." He smiled. "Your file makes for beautiful reading. I'd hate to have to add a page."

"Then don't," Theo said. His own voice sounded strange to him — level, cold, unfamiliar. "Step aside."

"The girl comes back with me. You go home with a warning. Everyone's ledgers stay clean." The retriever took a step forward along the ledge, unhurried, a man doing arithmetic he'd already finished. "That's the generous version, Iversen. There isn't a second one."

There was silence then, except for the wind moving through the frozen waterfall — a low, hollow fluting, as though the ice itself were breathing. The girl shifted on Theo's back. He felt her lift her head.

And the retriever's smile faltered.

Later, Theo would try to describe what happened and fail, because it happened partly outside language. The girl hummed — one note, deep, vast, a note with architecture in it — and the frozen waterfall *answered*. Every surface of ice on the Weeping Gallery rang like the string of some inconceivable instrument, and the sound was not loud so much as *total*, filling the skull, pressing behind the eyes, and in it Theo heard — or thought he heard, or remembered hearing, which amounts to the same thing — the sound of a word. One word. Old. Enormous. Patient as geology.

The retriever heard it too. Whatever contracts he carried, whatever years and names and loves he was licensed to collect, none of his paperwork covered this. His face went the color of tallow. He stepped back. His heel found empty air where he was certain ledge remained, and he pitched backward off the Weeping Gallery with a short, terribly ordinary cry, and the dark below took him, and the frozen waterfall went on humming long after he was gone, gently, like something soothing a child.

Theo stood shaking on the ledge, the girl warm against his back, and felt the moment settle over him like snowfall.

He had served the Guild for twenty years. He had never broken a rule — not the small ones, not the unwritten ones, not even the rule about not asking where things came from, which was really the only rule that mattered. He had told one lie, yesterday, to Corvane's face, and it had cost him more sleep than any theft.

Now a man was dead — or fallen, fallen at least, fallen *because of him* — and Theo searched himself for horror and found, underneath the horror, unmistakable and appalling, a thin bright thread of something else.

Freedom feels like falling. That's why most people never try it.

"Come on," he said hoarsely. "We're not done running."

* * *

The last bridge rose out of the Weeping Gallery like a drawn sword — the Long Span, the old-timers called it, though Theo had never learned whether the name referred to its length or to how long it took to cross when you were afraid. It climbed steeply toward the market's highest vent, a ragged mouth of gray daylight where the mountain exhaled its lantern-smoke into winter. Beyond that vent: the scree slopes, the tree line, the ferry-road down to the river. Odile's territory. Odile's escape.

Halfway up, the market announced its opinion of the evening's business.

It came as a sound first — a groan from the roots of the mountain, deep enough that Theo felt it in his sternum rather than heard it — and then as motion, a slow ripple passing through the frost on the bridge's surface like a shiver through a horse's hide. The storm below, the panic, the falling bridge, the dead retriever, the night's whole expenditure of violence: the market had been keeping count. And now, with a creak like the world's largest door deciding, it presented the bill.

Cracks bloomed across the Long Span ahead of them, white fern-patterns spreading faster than frost has any right to spread.

Theo ran harder.

The girl's arms locked around his neck. Below and behind, he heard shouting — more retrievers on the lower spans, too late and too heavy, the ice refusing their weight with cracks of warning — and above, growing, the gray light of the vent, wide as a cathedral door now, spilling cold clean air that tasted of pine and distance. Twenty feet. Ten. The bridge was coming apart beneath him in slow motion, slabs tilting, handholds tearing loose, and Theo stopped thinking like a ledger-keeper altogether and thought like a man on fire, which is to say he didn't think at all, he simply *gave* everything his legs had left—

—and the ice let go.

The world tilted. The vent swung away. For one endless instant Theo was airborne with a child in his arms inside a collapsing mountain, and he had time to notice, absurdly, that the girl was not screaming — she was watching the falling dark with wide, calm, ancient eyes, as though she had seen things fall before, whole kingdoms of them—

—then his fingers hooked the lip of the vent, and stone scraped his forearms to the elbow, and his shoulders screamed, and he climbed, and the girl scrambled up his back and over his head with that impossible featherweight grace, and her small hand closed around his wrist and *pulled* — pulled with strength no child should own, hauling him up and out of the mountain's throat as the last of the Long Span thundered down into the dark behind them like the lid of a coffin closing on the life he'd had before tonight.

* * *

Snow. Wind. A slope of scree silvered with moonlight. The vent exhaled behind them, a plume of smoke and lantern-light and distant furious bells, and Theo lay on his back in the drifts with his lungs heaving and his blood singing and the winter sky wheeling overhead, vast and indifferent and *open* — open in a way no ceiling of his tower had ever been, open all the way up, all the way out, all the way to the cold bright edge of everything.

He started laughing again, and this time he didn't stop it.

The girl knelt beside him in the snow, studying his face with grave attention, the way she studied everything — as though cataloguing him for some archive of her own. When his laughter finally wound down into shivering, she did something new. She reached out and brushed the snow from his hair, carefully, stroke by stroke, the way he had seen old women smooth the cloth on graves.

"I've never defied anything in my life," he told her. His voice was wrecked, and it didn't matter. "Twenty years. I balanced every account. I never once asked if the accounts were *right*." He sat up, and the scree crunched beneath him, and somewhere below, faint on the wind, he could hear the market's alarms giving way to the market's arguments — merchants assessing losses, masters assigning blame, Corvane, surely, already composing the retrieval orders. "Tonight I stole the most valuable thing the Guild ever appraised, broke three bridges, and got a man killed. And I have never—" His voice caught. He laughed at it catching. "I have never felt so much like myself."

The girl tilted her head. Then, slowly, she raised one mittened hand and pointed — not at him, not at the mountain, but north, past the black teeth of the tree line, toward the horizon where the stars sat low and hard and cold over the capital road. Her finger was steady. Her eyes, in the moonlight, held the same patient vastness he had heard in the frozen waterfall's single note.

North. Toward the thing wearing the king's face. Toward the buyer Corvane had promised her to.

"Yes," Theo said. "I know. That's where we're going."

Down at the foot of the scree, where the ferry-road met the frozen river, a single lantern bobbed in the darkness, approaching fast, attached — he would have bet every debt owed to him in every strange currency — to a broad-shouldered woman walking with a grin already prepared.

But as Theo got to his feet and lifted the girl onto his hip and turned toward the lantern-light, he saw what she saw, what her pointing finger had been telling him all along, and the exhilaration in his chest cooled by one careful degree:

There, in the snow along the northern road, printed sharp and fresh in the moonlight, ran a line of tracks. Human, booted, recent — heading north ahead of them.

Corvane had not waited for retrievers.

Corvane had gone to sell her himself.
