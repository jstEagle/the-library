## 9. Hunters of the Guild

Odile had ferried souls across the black water for forty years, and she had learned to read weather in ways that had nothing to do with clouds.

The river told her things. It told her when a widow upstream had stopped eating. It told her when a merchant downstream was about to default on a debt paid in his daughter's laughter — Odile could hear it going thin in the water, like ice forming on the reeds. The river beneath her ferry-house was fed by a hundred hidden springs, and every one of them ran through someplace where someone had bargained with the Veiled Guild.

So when she woke on the third night after their arrival and heard the water go silent — not still, not frozen, but *listening* — she knew before she opened her eyes that they had been found.

She lay in her loft above the boathouse and did not move. Below her, in the room where Theo slept on a pallet of old nets, the girl breathed in the slow rhythm of true sleep, and Odile focused on that sound the way a drowning sailor focuses on a light. *Breathe, little door. Breathe.* As long as the child slept, whatever had come for them had not yet touched her.

The silence spread. She felt it climb the pilings like frost.

Odile rose without a sound. Forty years of ferrying taught you how to move on creaking boards; you learned which plank sang and which one only whispered, and you learned to step on the whispers. She took down the boat-hook from its pegs — ash wood, iron-shod, older than her — and went down the ladder into the dark.

The main room of the ferry-house smelled of pitch and lamp oil and the particular cold that came off deep water. The hearth had burned to embers. Through the window above the mooring bench she could see the river, black and flat as poured ink, and standing at the end of her dock —

— a figure.

It stood the way debt stands: patiently, with perfect entitlement. It wore a grey traveling cloak and a wide-brimmed hat pulled low, and it might have been any collector who ever called at a market stall to repossess a man's courage or a woman's second-best memories. But no lantern burned at its belt, and collectors always carried lanterns, because contracts were read aloud by lamplight even at midnight, especially at midnight, and the reading was part of the ritual.

This one needed no light. It already knew what it had come to collect.

Odile set her palm flat against the cold window glass. Behind her, upstairs, the girl's breathing continued, soft as tide.

"Well," Odile said quietly, to herself, to the river, to the forty years she had spent learning exactly this kind of arithmetic. "So it's come to counting after all."

* * *

They had arrived three nights ago, half-drowned and hunted, and Odile had taken them in without a moment's hesitation — which surprised her, because she had never thought of herself as brave. Careful, yes. Shrewd. A woman who kept her debts small and her favors large, so that when the ledger finally turned against her there would be something in reserve. But not brave.

Theo had stood in her doorway with snow in his hair and blood on his knuckles — he'd broken two of Corvane's locks getting out of the Guild quarter, real locks, guild-forged, the kind that cost a locksmith his reputation to fail — and he had said, "I know what I'm asking, Odile. I'm asking you to burn your ledger."

And Odile had looked past him at the girl.

The child had been shivering so hard her teeth clicked, and she had no shadow — Odile saw it immediately, the way the lamplight fell wrong around her feet, pooling where nothing blocked it. And the child had looked up at Odile with eyes the color of deep water under ice, and she had said one word in that language that predated kingdoms, the language Theo was teaching himself from forbidden folios by candlelight.

Odile didn't understand the word. But she understood the tone. It was the tone of someone asking permission to exist.

"Get inside before the frost takes your ears off, both of you," she had said. "I'll put water on."

That was all. That was everything.

Now she descended the ladder and crossed the whispering boards toward the door, and the figure on the dock did not move, and she was grateful for that, because moving things were harder to negotiate with than patient ones. She had bargained with patient things her whole life.

She unbarred the door and stepped out onto the dock. The cold hit her like a hand. Her breath hung in front of her face and did not drift away; the air itself had gone attentive.

"Evening," Odile said.

The figure tipped its hat back. Underneath there was a face, and that was somehow worse than no face at all — a plain, middle-aged face, forgettable, the kind of face you'd trust with a parcel. Its eyes were the color of contract paper.

"Odile Marsh, ferrywoman," it said. Its voice was mild. "Keeper of the crossing at Graywater Bend. Licensed by the Guild under bond of passage, renewed annually, fees current." It smiled. "You're a valued member of our community."

"I pay my tolls."

"You do. You're one of the few." It produced a folded paper from within its cloak, though its hands had been empty a moment before, and Odile noted that the way she noted everything: the paper had not been carried. It had been *kept*, somewhere, the way a spider keeps silk. "Which is why I'm authorized to offer you an amicable settlement."

"Amicable." She tasted the word. "That's Guild talk for cheap."

"It's Guild talk for *painless*." The retriever unfolded the document. Even unread, it seemed to drink the moonlight. "You are harboring unregistered stock. One item, female-presenting, approximately eight years of age, classified by appraisal as Veil-borne and therefore property of the Veiled Guild, consigned for sale to a buyer in the capital. Harborage is a Class One violation. Standard remedy is forfeit of harborer's entire account — years, name, and loves, collected in that order." It glanced up at her with those paper-colored eyes, almost kindly. "But Master Corvane values efficiency over ceremony. He's authorized me to offer terms. Surrender the stock tonight, unharmed, and your penalty reduces to a fine. Payable in memory. Say, the last ten years of your life, recalled and remitted. You keep your name. You keep your loves. You keep your future. You simply won't remember the last decade."

The wind moved along the river, and Odile thought about the last ten years.

Ten years ago she had been fifty-one, and she had been alone, and she would have said the deal was nothing. Ten years held a bad winter, a leaky roof repaired twice, the slow death of a dog named Bracken, the ferry routes she'd memorized, the faces. Ten years held the widow upstream whose grief she'd helped carry instead of letting the Guild buy it. Ten years held — she counted backward, appalled at herself — ten years held almost nothing she'd have chosen to keep, if keeping were the point.

Except.

Except the last three nights. Except the sound of a child breathing in her loft, sleeping without shadows, safe for one more hour because Odile stood between her and the paper-eyed thing on the dock.

Three nights. They wanted to charge her ten years for three nights of doing the only right thing she'd done in a decade, and that, she thought, was the whole Guild in a single invoice.

"Here's my counteroffer," Odile said. "You can swim home."

The retriever sighed — genuinely, wearily, like a clerk facing paperwork — and that was when Odile struck first.

She had not survived forty years on a haunted river by waiting for other people's timing. The boat-hook came off her shoulder and the iron shoe took the creature across the knee, and it went down hard on the planks, hat spinning away across the ice-slicked wood, and Odile was already turning, already shouting up at the dark window:

"THEO! THE GIRL! THE BACK WAY — NOW!"

* * *

What happened next, Odile would remember in fragments, because the middle of a fight is a country no one visits twice.

She remembered the retriever rising wrong — joints unfolding like a folding rule, the plain face splitting into something patient and enormous behind it, patience given teeth. She remembered driving the boat-hook into its chest and feeling the iron sink in like it was sinking into wet ledger-paper, page after page after page, no body to find bottom.

She remembered the creature catching the shaft and speaking, calmly, while holding her weapon and her hands with it: "Forfeit of goods in transit. Clause nine." And she remembered feeling the words land on her like weights — *goods in transit* — because clause nine meant anything she carried for another person could be lawfully taken, and she was carrying, God help her, she was carrying a child's safety in her two old hands—

She remembered Theo coming through the door with the girl bundled in his coat and a folio under one arm, taking in the scene in one glance, and — this was the part she'd treasure later, in whatever years remained to her — Theo Iversen, twenty years of ledgers, twenty years of never asking where things came from, planted himself between the retriever and the child and said, in the language of the old folios, one syllable.

He said it badly. He said it like a man pronouncing a foreign name from a book, all the vowels wrong.

But the river heard it.

The black water rose. Not dramatically — Odile's river never did anything dramatically; it did everything *finally*. It rose the way a bill comes due. It climbed the dock pilings in a single sheet and took the retriever around the legs, and the creature made its first sound of the night, a dry rustle like pages turning very fast, and Odile wrenched her boat-hook free and drove the creature backward off the dock into the water.

It did not splash. It sank like a signed contract sinks — accepted, filed, gone.

But Odile had seen enough collectors to know that drowning one body doesn't close the account.

"Theo," she said, spitting river-water, hauling herself upright. "How many do they send?"

He was white-faced, holding the girl, who had woken and was looking at the risen river with an expression Odive couldn't read — recognition? Grief? "Retrievers work in threes," Theo said. "One to find, one to fix, one to carry. If that was the finder—"

"Then the other two are coming, and they won't be polite." Odile looked at the child, at Theo, at her own ferry-house with its warm hearth and its forty years of her life soaked into every board. Then she looked at the river.

There are moments when a life divides cleanly into the part before and the part after, and the dividing line is usually invisible until you've crossed it. Odile stood on hers.

"Boathouse," she said. "There's a lockbox under the mooring bench. In it there's a coin — black, heavy, older than the Guild. Don't ask me what it buys; I never asked where it came from either. Take it. Take my ferry at the north slip, the one with the grey sail. It runs on water nobody else can cross." She was already moving, pulling tarps, opening sluices, her hands doing the work while her mouth did the rest. "You're going north. Both of you. To the capital."

"The capital is where—" Theo started.

"Where the buyer is. Yes. Where the crown sits on the wrong head. I can do sums, gatekeeper." She shoved a satchel of food into his arms. "You can't outrun the Guild forever, so stop running *from* and start running *at*. Go take your accounts to the debtor himself."

"And you?" His voice cracked on it. She had known Theo Iversen for eleven years and had never once heard his voice crack.

"I'm going to settle my bill," Odile said.

* * *

The second retriever came out of the treeline as Theo got the girl into the grey-sailed ferry, and it was worse than the first, because it walked slowly, unhurried, carrying a lantern after all.

Contracts read by lamplight. The reading was part of the ritual.

Odile stepped onto the dock to meet it, and she heard the third one behind her, at the far end, blocking the road south. Find, fix, carry. She was the fixed point now.

"Hold," she said, and to her surprise the lantern-thing held. "Before you read anything, I invoke my bond. Passage-license, Graywater Bend, forty years' renewal. I have rights under my own contract, and one of them is arbitration."

The retriever tilted its head. "Arbitration is granted," it said, "to members in good standing. State your dispute."

"My dispute is this." She kept her voice level. Behind her she could hear Theo pushing the ferry off the slip, could hear the grey sail filling with wind that came from nowhere, could hear the girl saying something soft and urgent in the old language — and she made herself not turn around, because turning around would break her. "I harbor no stock. I harbor a guest. There's a difference, and the difference is consent. Your appraisal says the child is property. My bond says anyone who steps onto my crossing consents to my protection while on my water. Those two clauses conflict, and conflicting clauses go to arbitration, and arbitration costs the Guild more than it costs me. Check your own ledgers. You know I'm right."

The retriever was silent for a long moment. Somewhere in its paper depths, she imagined, clerks were being consulted.

"Your reasoning is noted," it said at last, "and declined. Arbitration resolves conflicts between clauses. This is a conflict between a clause and a person. Persons are not parties to arbitration. Persons are assets." It raised the lantern. "Remedy proceeds. Forfeit of account. Years, then name, then loves. Unless the stock is surrendered."

Odile closed her eyes.

She had spent forty years keeping her debts small and her favors large, and here was the truth she'd been saving, the one she'd never said out loud even to herself: she had always assumed that when the final accounting came, she'd pay it alone, in the dark, for nothing much. A life trickled away to cover some petty shortfall. That had been the shape of her fear.

It had never once occurred to her that she might get to spend herself on something worth the price.

"Then here's my settlement," she said. "Take the years."

"Odile—" Theo's voice, raw, from the water.

"Ferry leaves, gatekeeper!" she shouted without turning. "That's an order from the keeper of this crossing! You want to honor me? *Go!*"

She heard the sail crack open. She heard the keel bite into water no ordinary hull could touch. She heard the girl cry out — one high note in the tongue older than kingdoms — and then the sound of the north channel swallowed them, and Odile Marsh stood alone on her dock between two retrievers and offered them her open hands.

"Ten years," she said. "Recalled and remitted. Per your own generous terms. Do the reading."

The lantern swung up. The words began.

* * *

Pain was not the right word. Pain was for bodies. What the collection took was larger than her body — it reached back through her life like a hand through a drawer, and it gathered.

Bracken the dog, dying in her arms with his head on her boot: *taken*. The winter she'd nearly frozen and the stranger who'd carried her to her own fire and vanished by morning: *taken*. Eleven years of Theo Iversen buying passage at her slip, always exact change, always a nod, always the same joke about her tea being strong enough to row upstream — *taken, taken, taken* — and she felt him going and she grabbed at him with everything she had and the contract peeled her grip away finger by finger, gently, professionally, the way you'd peel a label off a jar.

No. Wait. Please. I need those. I need those, they're the proof, they're the receipt, they're how I'll know why I did this—

The years left her in order, oldest first, and she fought for each one and lost each one, and near the end, with only the last three nights remaining, she found she was kneeling on the dock in the snow and the retrievers were watching with their paper eyes, and she spent her final strength not on fighting but on *memorizing*.

The child's breathing through the floorboards. Theo's cracked voice. The word the girl had spoken at her door, asking permission to exist. Whatever else the contract took, Odile pressed those three things down into the deepest part of her, below memory, into the place where the river ran — because the river ran through her too, forty years of crossings, and maybe the water would hold what her head could not.

The lantern closed. The reading ended.

"Account settled," said the retriever, mildly. "You retain your name, your loves, and your future. The Guild thanks you for your cooperation, Odile Marsh, ferrywoman."

She looked up at it from her knees. Her joints ached in a way they hadn't an hour ago. Her hands looked older. When she tried to think about the last ten years she found a smooth blank stretch, like ice over deep water, and somewhere under that ice something was knocking.

"Get off my dock," she said hoarsely. "Crossing's closed. Fees current. Get out."

And — this was the strange thing, the thing she would puzzle over for years — they went. Contracts honored to the letter. They folded back into the dark like receipts into a drawer, and Odile knelt alone on her dock under the indifferent moon, sixty-one years old and suddenly feeling every one of them, reaching for a decade that was gone.

Under the ice of the missing years, faint as a fish passing, something knocked again.

A child's breathing. A cracked voice. A single word in an old tongue.

*Hold on,* she thought at them, at him, wherever the grey sail had carried them. *I don't remember why yet. But I paid for you once, and I'll know why again.*

Far to the north, she did not yet know, three travelers on impossible water were heading toward a capital where a king's eyes moved a half-second behind his body — and toward a reckoning that would need every debt she'd ever bought, and every year she had left.

But the knocking under the ice continued. And Odile Marsh had never once ignored the river.
