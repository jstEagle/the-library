## 10. The Road to the Capital

They left Odile's ferry-house before the third bell of morning, when the river was still smoking with cold and the reeds along the bank had turned to glass. Theo carried the girl on his shoulders for the first mile because the frost had made the towpath treacherous, and she rode with her small hands laced in his hair and her breath puffing warm against his ear — a little bellows of heat in a world that was forgetting how to be warm.

Odile walked ahead with her ferryman's pole repurposed as a walking staff, reading the road the way she read water. She did not say much. She had not said much since the retriever took ten years from her; grief, Theo had learned, does not always weep. Sometimes it just walks faster.

The girl spoke once, softly, in that language under kingdoms, and pointed north.

"I know," Theo said. "I know. The capital."

But she shook her head against his crown and pointed again, more urgently, past the capital, past the horizon, at something only she could see. Then she said the word he had come to dread — not the king's name this time, but another one, older, and though he could not yet translate it whole, he understood its shape the way you understand the shape of a door by the draft coming under it.

*Hunger,* it meant. Or something like hunger. Something hunger was only a child's drawing of.

"Save your strength," he told her, and felt her go quiet, obedient in the way of children who have learned that adults cannot bear too much truth at once. That obedience frightened him more than anything Corvane had ever done.

* * *

The first village was called Harrow's End, though by the look of it nothing had ended there so much as stopped — stopped the way a clock stops, mid-tick, everything still in motion's shape but none of the motion left.

The wheat stood in the fields frozen mid-harvest. Not flattened by snow, not rotted — *frozen*, each stalk upright, each head heavy with grain rimed in ice so fine it looked like sugar. A sickle lay where someone had dropped it, half-sunk in ground hard as iron. And above it all hung the smell of a harvest interrupted: dust and dry stalks and, beneath that, faintly, sweetly, the ghost of ripening that would never finish.

Theo walked out into the field because he could not help himself. He was a ledger-keeper; unfinished entries offended something deep in him. He touched one wheat head and the ice cracked away from it like an eggshell, and inside, impossibly, impossibly, the grain was still soft. Still alive. Held.

"It hasn't died," he called back to Odile. "It's just — waiting."

"Everything up here is waiting," Odile said. Her voice was flat, but Theo heard what was under it. She had grown up in country like this, before the river took her. "That's the trouble."

In the village square, a woman sat on a bench outside a house whose door stood open to the cold, as if the person who'd closed it last had simply forgotten doors mattered. She was mending a net with hands that moved slowly, mechanically, the way hands move when they have done a thing ten thousand times and no longer need the mind for it.

"You'll want to turn back," she said without looking up. "Road's worse ahead."

"We're going to the capital," Theo said. "We need provisions. We can pay."

That got her attention. She looked up, and Theo saw that her eyes were red-rimmed and dry — eyes that had cried themselves out some time ago and were now operating on something harder than sorrow. She looked at the girl on his shoulders a long moment.

"Hearths won't light," she said. "You've seen? No? Well. Three weeks now. Dry wood, good flint, oil — nothing. My husband built fires his whole life. He'd get them down to coals and then—" she made a small gesture, a fist closing, "—out. Like the cold reached in and pinched them. He went to ask the priest. Priest says the gods are owed something. Priest always says that." She snorted, but there was no humor in it. "My mother used to tell a different story. Said there was a veil over the world, long ago, and things traded through it, and if the veil wore thin, the warmth leaked out the holes." She finally set down the net. "Fairy stories. But my bread won't rise either, and fairy stories don't explain that."

Theo's mouth had gone dry. "Your mother — where did she hear that?"

"Where does anyone hear anything? From her mother." The woman squinted at him. "Why do you look like that?"

Because I keep the ledger of impossible things, Theo thought. Because I have sold bottled storms and borrowed years and ounces of widow's grief, and never once asked where any of it came from, because asking was not my office. Because I am beginning to understand that every transaction I ever recorded was a hole punched in something, and the something is bleeding.

"We'll take bread if you have it," he said instead. "And whatever firewood you can spare."

"Won't do you any good."

"It might," said the girl.

She said it in her own tongue, but the woman heard it anyway — Theo watched her hear it, watched her face change, watched her stand up slowly from the bench as if the ground had shifted under her. Because that was the thing about the girl's language, the thing Theo was only now fully grasping: it wasn't a language you learned. It was a language you remembered. It lived somewhere below words, in the part of people that had existed before kingdoms, before writing, before the first bargain was ever struck across the dark.

The woman pressed a sack of black bread and two wax candles into Theo's hands and refused his coin. "For the little one," she said. Then, quieter, gripping his wrist: "Whatever she is. Whatever you're carrying her toward. Hurry."

* * *

They made twelve miles that day, and the world got stranger with every one.

A millpond frozen in perfect concentric rings, like a record of every day it had failed to thaw. A flock of birds — starlings, thousands of them — hanging motionless in the grey air above a copse, wings out, holding position like notes on a stave, and when Theo stopped to stare, one of them slowly turned its head and looked at him with an eye like a chip of black ice, and he walked faster after that.

A shrine at a crossroads, old ones, pre-Guild, the kind with the face worn blank by centuries of weather — and around its base, fresh offerings. Bread. Buttons. A child's shoe. And scratched into the stone in a dozen different hands, some recent enough to still hold chalk-dust: the same word, over and over, in the alphabet of the border-folk and then, cruder, phonetically, in letters Theo recognized from guild folios.

The girl slid down from his shoulders and stood before the shrine and read it aloud.

"What does it say?" Odile asked.

"*Come home,*" the girl said. "Everyone is writing *come home.*"

"To whom?"

The girl didn't answer, but she laid her palm flat against the stone, and for one heartbeat — Theo would swear this to the end of his life — the frost on the shrine melted in a hand-shaped bloom, green showing through, and then the cold rushed back in like water closing over a dropped coin.

"They're praying to her," Odile said quietly. It wasn't a question. She had been watching the girl longer than Theo had. "All these people. They don't know it, but they're praying to her."

"And she can't answer," Theo said. "Not yet. Whatever she is — a vessel, a door, a keeper of something — she's stretched too thin herself. Look at her." And he did look, really looked, for the first time in days: at the way the girl had gone translucent at the edges when the light hit her wrong, at how shadows passed through her a half-second late, at how she ate less and less and slept more and more, curled up against his back on the road like something trying to fold itself small enough to survive.

The Veil was thinning everywhere. He could feel it now, the way you feel weather in an old wound. Every village they passed was a page torn out of the world's book, and the girl was the binding, and the binding was failing, and somewhere north a crowned thing was pulling the thread with both hands.

* * *

They sheltered that night in a shepherd's bothy off the high road, and Theo built a fire because he refused — refused — to accept that he couldn't.

He stacked the wood the way his father had taught him, log cabin and kindling tower, struck the flint until his knuckles bled. Nothing. Again. A curl of smoke, a sullen orange glow, and then the cold reaching in — he *felt* it reach, felt it like fingers — and pinch the flame dead.

"Let me try," Odile said, and tried, and failed, and swore in three languages including the river-argot of smugglers, which has curses in it that would blister paint.

The girl crawled to the dead fire and put both hands around it, cupping it like a wounded bird.

"Don't," Theo said. "You'll spend yourself. You're already—"

She looked up at him. And in the dark of the bothy her eyes were not a child's eyes; they were old, old, old, older than the shrine, older than the kingdom, and behind them he saw — just for a moment — vast slow shapes moving, the way you see shapes behind frosted glass.

Then she breathed on the coals.

The fire caught. Not gradually — instantly, all at once, roaring up cheerful and gold, throwing heat across the bothy like a thrown blanket, and Odile laughed out loud in sheer startled joy, and Theo sat down hard on the packed earth floor.

"How much did that cost you?" he whispered.

The girl shrugged, a child's shrug, and held her hands out to the warmth she had made, and for a while they were just three travelers by a fire, and Theo let himself pretend.

But later, when she slept between them with her head on Odile's rolled cloak, he took out his ledger — the real one, the private one he'd begun keeping since the night of the knock — and wrote by candlelight:

*Day four north. Winter is not falling. Winter is being pulled. Villages hold but barely; the girl spends herself to warm us and grows thinner than her shadow. The folk pray to her without knowing her name. I know her name. I carry it next to my heart like contraband.*

*Corvane will have discovered her gone by now. The retrievers will come again, and next time Odile will not have another decade to give. I must find a way to pay our debts myself, in whatever coin I have left, and I begin to suspect the coin is me.*

*One more thing. Tonight, watching her sleep, I understood the word she keeps saying. Not the king's name. The other one. It means: soon.*

He closed the book. Outside, the stars burned with a hard, cold clarity he had never seen in forty years of nights, as if the sky itself were thinning, as if heaven were a veil too.

And far off to the north — so far it might have been imagination, except that the girl stirred in her sleep and whimpered at exactly that moment — something on the horizon glowed faintly gold. Not sunrise. Sunrise was hours off, and this light came from the wrong quarter entirely, low and steady and patient.

The lights of the capital. Burning, Odile had said, brighter than any city had a right to burn, in a winter when no other fire in the realm would catch.

Warmth, hoarded. Warmth, stolen. Warmth, gathered into one place by a thing that wore a king's face and fed while the world froze.

Theo banked the fire, tucked the ledger away, and lay down between the sleeping girl and the sleeping ferryman, and did not sleep himself.

*Soon,* the word said, in the language under kingdoms, in the voice of everything he loved.

He intended to arrive first.
