## 11. An Audience Refused

The capital announced itself three days before we saw it.

It was the birds first. For leagues the road north had been silent — no crows on the frozen furrows, no sparrows in the eaves of shuttered waystations — but as we crested the last ridge above the river valley, the sky over the city of Carreth was black with them, tens of thousands of wings wheeling above the spires like smoke that had learned to think. The girl stopped in the road and stared at them, and her small hand found mine and gripped it, and she said a word in the old tongue that I had learned by then to translate without thinking.

*Wrong,* she said. *The birds say wrong.*

"They're just crows," Odile said, though she said it the way people say things they don't believe, and she pulled her patched coat tighter against a cold that had stopped being weather and started being a presence. "Cities have birds. Birds like cities."

But the girl did not let go of my hand, and I did not ask her to.

We came down into Carreth with the last of the light, through the southern gate where the guards barely looked at us — they were looking at nothing, all of them, their gazes fixed at a point six inches above every traveler's head, as if the city had trained them to see through people rather than at them. The gate tax was two coppers. I paid four, and the guard made change without counting it, and his hands moved with the smoothness of a thing that had done this ten thousand times and would do it ten thousand more, and there was nothing behind the smoothness at all. No irritation. No boredom. Nothing.

I had spent twenty years in the Veiled Guild, and I knew what it looked like when a person became a function. I had written the ledgers that made it happen. But I had never seen it worn so openly, on a face, in the open air, in the capital of a living kingdom.

* * *

We took a room above a chandler's shop in the Tanners' Quarter, three coppers a night from a landlady who counted our coins twice and still looked at us like we might be carrying something. The room was cold in the ordinary way, which by then felt like a luxury. The girl — who still had no name I could give her, though she had begun, in the last week, to make sounds at me that I suspected were attempts at mine, *Thay-o*, soft and careful, like a word she was carrying without spilling it — the girl stood at the window for a long time after dark, watching the palace district on its hill across the river, where a thousand windows burned with a light that did not flicker.

I noticed that first. Firelight flickers. It is the nature of fire to flicker; it is the oldest truth of flame, older than language, older than the Veil. Every hearth in Carreth guttered and danced. But the palace windows burned steady as painted glass, and the longer I looked, the more I understood that it wasn't one strange thing. It was the whole hill. The banners hung without moving. The smoke from the chimneys rose in straight, unwavering columns, like threads being drawn upward by a patient hand.

"Odile," I said.

"I see it." She had stopped unpacking. "I've been seeing it since the gate. Theo, the whole city's half-asleep, but that hill—" She shook her head. "That hill is *awake*."

The girl turned from the window and looked at me, and in the dark her eyes caught the light from the street in a way eyes shouldn't, and she said, in her wordless, ancient way, the thing she had been saying since the frozen market, the thing I had learned to hear as clearly as speech:

*It knows we are coming.*

* * *

The window procession was announced by criers the next morning, and the whole city turned out for it, because the whole city had been ordered to and because, I think, some part of each of them still wanted to. *His Majesty King Aldric the Second, beloved of the realm, shall show himself at the Winter Window of the Great Hall at noon, to receive the petitions of his people.* Odile read the notice nailed to the chandler's door and laughed, a short, hard sound with no humor in it.

"Petitions," she said. "Half the realm can't get its fields to thaw and the other half can't get its hearths to light, and they're going to stand at a window and *receive petitions*."

"Then we'll be in the crowd," I said. "We need to see him. We need to see his face."

I said it as if it were strategy. It was not strategy. It was need — the need I had been carrying since the night the girl knocked at my gate, the need to know what we were walking toward, to put a shape to the thing that had already put a shape to us. Corvane had sold her, or meant to. The Guild's retrievers had taken ten years of Odile's life on the road and would have taken more if the ice had not broken under their feet at the exact moment the girl had raised her hand. Everything that had happened since the solstice had been flight, and flight had a way of making the enemy abstract. I was done with abstract. I wanted to look at the thing wearing the king.

The crowd on the Processional was enormous and silent in a way crowds are never silent. That was the second thing I noticed, standing with Odile's hand on my shoulder and the girl between us, wrapped in a borrowed cloak with her hood drawn low: ten thousand people packed shoulder to shoulder, and the sound of them was nothing. No shuffling. No coughing. No murmured gossip or children whining or vendors crying their wares. Ten thousand people breathing white breath into the frozen air, and the only sound was the breathing, in and out, in and out, ten thousand lungs keeping a time that no one had set.

"They're not afraid," Odile whispered. "That's what frightens me. Look at their faces. They're *happy*."

They were. That was the third thing. Every face in that crowd wore the same mild, settled contentment, the look of people at a festival who have eaten well and expect to eat well tomorrow. It sat on their faces like frost sits on glass — uniformly, perfectly, as if it had been applied rather than grown. And beneath it, if you looked long enough, if you had spent twenty years reading ledgers and knew what it meant when a number balanced too cleanly, there was the other thing, the thing under the frost: the faint, unnameable wrongness of a face that has forgotten it is supposed to be doing anything at all.

The bells rang noon.

The great window of the hall opened — it did not swing, it *opened*, the way an eye opens, the shutters folding back in silence — and King Aldric the Second came to the glass.

I had seen his likeness a hundred times, on coins and proclamations and the Guild's own records, for the Guild kept accounts with every crown and had kept them with this one for thirty years. He had been a broad man, Aldric, square-jawed, with his father's heavy brow and his mother's quick dark eyes, a man who in his portraits looked like he was about to laugh at something and had decided to let you wait. The thing at the window had his face. It had his face the way a glove has a hand.

He stood in the light, and the crowd made its sound at last — a single exhalation, ten thousand voices becoming one voice, *the King, the King, the King* — and I watched him raise his hand in blessing, and I saw it.

His eyes moved a half-second behind his body.

It is a small thing to describe and an enormous thing to see. His arm rose smoothly, graciously, the wave of a beloved king to his beloved people. But his eyes arrived late. They slid to the left after his head had already turned left, slid to the right after the right, tracked the crowd the way a lantern tracks across a wall — carried, not looking. And when his gaze passed over our section of the crowd, over the ten thousand upturned faces, it did not pause on any of them, because pausing would have required wanting to see, and there was no wanting in it at all. It was the gaze of a thing that had looked at so much for so long that looking had become a formality, like the wave, like the smile, like the crown.

And then — I will swear this until the day I die, and I have died a little several times since — then his eyes stopped.

On us.

The wave continued. The smile continued. The crowd cheered. But the eyes came back, and they settled on the small hooded figure between Odile and me, and they *stayed*, and in that half-second delay between the eyes and everything else I felt the whole of the distance between the Processional and the palace window close to nothing, and something looked out through the king's borrowed face at the girl, and it was patient. That is the only word I have for it, and I have spent a year looking for a better one. It was not hungry the way a wolf is hungry, sudden and hot. It was hungry the way winter is hungry — total, unhurried, certain that everything warm is already its property and merely awaiting collection. It looked at her the way a creditor looks at a debtor who has wandered, briefly, out of reach.

The girl's hand closed on mine hard enough to hurt. She did not flinch. She did not hide. She lifted her chin, and beneath the hood her eyes met the king's eyes across a hundred yards and ten thousand people, and she said one word, not aloud, in the tongue under kingdoms, in the voice that was not a voice, and I heard it as clearly as I have ever heard anything:

*No.*

The eyes left us. The wave finished. The window folded shut like a lid, and the crowd exhaled its contentment and began, at last, to murmur and shuffle and be people again, and I stood in the middle of them shaking, because I understood, all at once and completely, what we were walking toward — not a villain, not a monster in a story, but something older and worse: a *creditor*, with the whole world entered in its ledger, and winter for an enforcer.

"Theo." Odile's voice was very quiet. "His eyes. Did you—"

"I saw."

"And the courtiers—" She nodded toward the palace hill, where the procession had begun, the king's household crossing the courtyard below the window in their furs and their silks. "Look at their veils."

I had seen them already and not understood. Every courtier wore a veil against the cold — a strip of dark silk across the lower face, as the fashion of the northern court had been for a generation, or so the criers said. But the wind was moving that day. It moved the banners, moved the crowd's cloaks and hoods, moved the breath-smoke of ten thousand people. It did not move the veils. They hung across the courtiers' faces smooth and straight as if painted there, as if silk had decided, in the capital, to forget how to flutter, and I remembered what the girl had shown me in Odile's ferry-house, the vision that had woken me screaming: the crowned thing feeding, and around it the shapes of those who served it, their faces wrapped so that it would not have to look at what it was unmaking.

"They're not veils against the cold," I said. "They're veils against *it*. Or—" and the truth of it settled into me with a cold that had nothing to do with the weather, "—they're veils so *we* won't see what's left underneath."

The girl tugged my sleeve. She was looking up at me with an expression I had not seen on her before, and it took me a moment to name it, because I had never been looked at that way by anyone: pity. This small mute child, this last vessel of the binding of a dead king, pitied me — pitied me for having seen, for having crossed the line of seeing from which there is no return to the comfortable arithmetic of ledgers and rules and never asking where things come from.

She took my hand again, and turned it over, and with one cold finger she drew on my palm, slowly, deliberately, the way a teacher writes for a slow student. I knew the shapes by then. I had spent weeks with the forbidden folios, tracing the tongue under kingdoms by candlelight while the girl slept. She drew four strokes, and a circle, and a stroke that broke the circle, and I read it in the old way, and my blood went cold and hot at once.

*Four gates,* she wrote on my skin. *One is under his house.*

I looked up at the palace hill, at the thousand steady windows, at the banners that did not move and the veils that did not flutter and the great hall where a thing wearing a king had looked at us with a creditor's patience. Four gates, the archives would later confirm, though I did not know it yet — four doors that held the binding, and one of them beneath the palace itself, under the feet of the thing that most wanted the binding broken, and the girl was telling me that we had walked all this way to find that the last key was kept in the lock of our enemy's own house.

Odile followed my gaze up the hill, and then my face, and then the girl's, and she said, in the flat voice of a ferrywoman who has already decided:

"Tell me we're not going into the palace."

The girl looked at me. The crowd flowed around us, murmuring, content, frost-faces under a winter that would not end, and somewhere above it all the thing in the king's face was waiting, patient as winter, certain of its collections. I closed my hand around the words the girl had written on my palm, as if I could keep them there, warm.

"We're going into the palace," I said.
