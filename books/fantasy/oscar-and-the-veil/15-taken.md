## 15. Taken

The festival of lights came to the capital on the longest night of the year, which Theo had always thought was either a cruel joke or a very old act of defiance. Ten thousand lanterns hung from the palace eaves and the merchant rows and the frozen branches of the linden trees along the Processional, each one a small yellow sun against the dark, and the people of the city — the ones who could still afford candles, the ones who had not yet sold their warmth by the hour — turned out in their thousands to pretend that winter was only winter, that the cold was ordinary, that the king who would appear at the high window in an hour's time was a king and not something else entirely.

Theo stood in the crowd with the girl on his shoulders.

She had insisted. She had tugged his collar and pointed up, up, toward the lanterns, and made the sound she made when she wanted something — not her Veil-language, which she kept close now like a secret hoarded in a fist, but the small urgent chirp of any child who has decided that tonight is a night for seeing things. He had argued with himself about it for most of the afternoon. Maren had argued with him too, in her flat soldier's way: *crowds are cover for us and cover for them, and you cannot tell one kind of cover from the other until it is too late.* But Odile had come down from the ferry-house with a sack of roasted chestnuts and a grin like thaw water, and Odile had said, "Let her see the lights. She has spent two lifetimes being a door. Let her be a girl for one night," and Theo had found he could refuse Odile nothing, not after what she had paid.

So they went into the crowd. The girl rode his shoulders in a cloak of Odile's mending, grey wool stitched with threads of real silver that caught the lantern-light and threw it back in sparks, and she laughed — actually laughed, a child's laugh, unguarded, the first sound out of her that had nothing of the Veil in it at all — as the lanterns went up one by one, street by street, until the whole city seemed to be floating on a sea of small fires.

Theo watched her laugh and felt something he had no ledger-entry for.

He had spent twenty years recording the movement of impossible things between hands, and he knew every shape desire took when it was written down: the widow's grief weighed in ounces, the borrowed years amortized against a harvest, the bottled storm with its price per gust. But this — a mute child laughing at paper lanterns because they were pretty — this was wealth of a kind the Guild had never once appraised, because it could not be owned, only witnessed. It was the first thing in his long careful life that he did not want to keep. He only wanted it to continue.

He should have known better. He had read enough ledgers to know that the things which cannot be owned are precisely the things the market will find a way to take.

* * *

They had planned the evening carefully, because everything Theo did now was planned, because planning was the last discipline left to a man who had broken every rule that mattered. Maren walked ten paces ahead in plain clothes, her captain's bearing folded down into something that read as a bored court functionary strolling with her family. Odile drifted along the edges of the crowd with the chestnut brazier, close enough to reach them in three strides, far enough away that no pattern-eye watching from a rooftop would connect them. And Theo carried the girl and said almost nothing, because saying things in crowds was how you got noticed, and being noticed was how the retrievers found you.

The retrievers. He had seen two of them already tonight, though he doubted they had seen him. They stood at the mouth of the Processional where the lanterns thinned, dressed in festival colors so fine they looked like minor nobility, and they were doing the thing retrievers do, which is stand perfectly still while everyone else moves. One of them held a small brass instrument to his eye like a spyglass, sweeping the crowd in slow arcs, and Theo knew without looking twice what the instrument was: a finder. Guild-make, old make, the kind that read debt-signature the way a lamp reads oil — and somewhere under the girl's silver-stitched cloak, under the laughter, under the name she had given him, there was still a signature. There would always be a signature. Corvane had sold it weeks ago, formally, with seals and witnesses, in a transaction Theo had watched happen and been powerless to stop: *one vessel of the Veil, location disclosed upon payment, buyer a court functionary acting for interests unnamed.* The Guild had sold her coordinates the way it sold everything, cleanly, legally, without ever once touching her with its own hands.

That was the part that still made him sick when he let himself think about it. Not the theft. The paperwork. Somewhere in the Guild's vaults there was a bill of sale with her on it, described in the trade-tongue as *unregistered stock, female, approx. seven years, shadowless, high residual value*, and some clerk had entered it in a fair hand, and some auditor had countersigned it, and no one — not one person in that whole chain of immaculate procedure — had ever needed to look at her face to know exactly what they were selling.

Theo had looked at her face. That was the whole difference between him and them, and tonight, with the lanterns burning and the crowd singing the old light-song in voices gone thin from a winter that would not end, he clung to that difference like a man clinging to a rail in a flood.

*Look at the lights,* he told her, tilting his head back so she could see past his hood. *Not the men at the corner. The lights.*

She looked at the lights. And then, because she was the Veil and the Veil saw everything whether it wished to or not, she also looked at the men at the corner, and her small hands went still on top of his head, and her laughter stopped.

Theo felt it stop. Felt it the way you feel a room go quiet — not a sound ending but a presence withdrawing, a warmth pulling back behind glass. Her grip tightened in his hair, once, twice: the signal they had agreed on, the one that meant *run.*

He did not run. Not yet. Running confirmed; running drew the eye; running through a festival crowd was how a shadowless child got separated from the one person carrying her. Instead he did the thing twenty years of ledgers had trained into him: he slowed down. He turned, gently, so that his back — and hers — faced the corner and the brass instrument, and he began to drift, unhurried, toward the lane Odile had marked that morning as their exit: the tanner's alley, narrow, unlit, leading down to the ice-locked canal where the ferry-house waited.

Odile saw them turn. Odile always saw. She began closing the distance with the brazier, angling across the crowd's flow like a fish reading a current, and for perhaps forty heartbeats everything worked. The crowd thickened. The lanterns multiplied. The girl's hands loosened in his hair and her laughter threatened to come back, and Theo allowed himself, for those forty heartbeats, to believe that the night might simply pass — that they would slip down the tanner's alley and cross the black ice of the canal and be home before the king appeared at his window with his eyes moving half a second behind his body, and the festival would end, and the longest night would end, and they would all still be together.

Forty heartbeats. He counted them. He would count them for the rest of his life.

* * *

It happened at the mouth of the tanner's alley, where the crowd narrowed from a river to a throat.

A man stepped out of the press ahead of them — not one of the two at the corner, but a third, planted, patient, wearing a festival mask of gilded paper shaped like a fox's face. Behind him, two more, masks of hare and magpie, closing from the flanks with the smooth converging walk of professionals who have done this a hundred times in a hundred markets. And above, on the awning of the chandler's shop, a fourth figure Theo never clearly saw, only felt — a weight settling onto the canvas overhead like a hawk coming to perch.

Fox-mask spoke first, and what he said was not threat but courtesy, which was worse. "Your charge is recalled," he said, in the flat courteous trade-tongue of the Guild's contracts, "under bond of sale. The buyer requests delivery. You will be compensated for your trouble."

Compensated. As if she were a crate. As if the whole night — the chestnuts, the lanterns, the laugh — were line items to be settled.

Theo's mind went white. Twenty years of meticulous procedure, and here at the end of it all he had nothing procedural left. He had Maren ten paces back in the crowd, but the crowd had compressed and Maren was cut off behind a knot of drunks singing off-key. He had Odile three strides away, but Odile was downwind of the brazier and the smoke marked her. He had his own body, his own wits, and a child on his shoulders who weighed less than a ledger but mattered more than every entry in every book he had ever balanced.

So he did the only thing left. He ran.

Down the tanner's alley, into the throat, into the dark. The crowd screamed behind them — festival screams at first, then real ones, as fox-mask and hare-mask and magpie-mask shed their courtesies and gave chase, shoving through the press with masked faces and gloved hands. Theo heard Odile behind him, roaring, tipping her brazier sideways into the alley-mouth so that a sheet of flame and chestnut-smoke bloomed across the pursuers' path, buying seconds, only seconds, her voice shouting words he couldn't hear over the panic-horns the city watch began sounding from the bell towers.

Seconds were what he had. Seconds were what he spent.

The alley dropped toward the canal in a series of worn stone steps, and the steps were iced, and Theo ran down them carrying a child, and the ice did what ice does. His right foot went out from under him on the third landing. He did not fall clean. He fell forward, deliberately, folding himself around the girl the way he had once seen a ferryman fold around a cargo of living storm-glass in a capsizing boat — taking the stones on his forearms, his ribs, his hip, keeping her clear, keeping her held, rolling with her into the lee of the canal wall where the ice was thickest and the lanterns overhead made a stained-glass shimmer on the frozen water.

He was bleeding. He could feel it soaking warm into his cloak before the cold found it and stiffened it — blood from his forearm where the stone had opened it, blood from somewhere near his temple that ran into his eyes and turned the lanterns into halos. The girl was unhurt. He checked first, always her first, the way he had learned to check in the flight from the market: breath, warmth, the small pulse at her throat. Unhurt, terrified, gripping his collar with both hands now, and speaking — for the first time since the solstice eve, speaking aloud in the old language, urgently, a stream of it, rapid and rising, as if she were trying to tell him something before something arrived to stop her telling it.

He understood fragments. He understood enough. What she was saying, in the tongue under kingdoms, was a warning: *they are not men. Do not fight them. Run to the ice. Run to the ice.*

And then the fourth figure came down off the awning.

He had felt it land; now he saw it move, dropping from the chandler's roof to the canal wall in two falls no human should have survived, unfolding as it fell like a puppet whose strings had been cut and rehung wrong. It wore a retriever's coat and a retriever's badge and a retriever's contract-number stamped in brass at the collar, and beneath the coat its proportions were wrong — arms too long, joints that bent backward, a head that turned independently of its body the way an owl's does. It did not run after them. It *arrived* beside them, the way debt arrives, the way winter arrives, without crossing the space in between.

What happened next happened fast, and Theo would spend the rest of his life unable to make it slow.

The thing reached for the girl. Its hands were gloved, contractual, correct — retrieval gloves, Guild-issue, the kind used for handling items rather than persons, because in the eyes of the contract she was an item. Theo put his body between the gloves and the child, and the thing did not push past him, did not hurt him, did not even seem to register him as an obstacle. It simply *took*. It lifted her off his shoulders the way a crane lifts a crate off a dock — smoothly, vertically, without effort, without violence, without touching him at all — and Theo, reaching up with both bleeding arms to hold on, holding on with everything, discovered that holding on was not among the things the contract recognized.

There was a moment — half a second, less — when she was in both their grips. His arms around her legs, her hands locked in his collar, her weight shared between his failing strength and the thing's effortless leverage. In that half-second she did the only thing left to do, the thing she had been trying to tell him on the steps, the thing she had been saving:

She said her name.

Not to the retriever. To him. Down into his face, into the haloed lantern-light, into the blood and the noise and the falling dark — she said it slowly, deliberately, syllable by syllable, in the language under kingdoms, pressing each word home with the terrible clarity of someone handing over the last valuable thing in the world. And then she did something stranger. She pressed her palm against his forehead, hard, and held it there, and whatever passed between her skin and his in that moment — warmth, weight, a pressure like a word being written inside him — Theo would carry afterward in his bones the way you carry a scar: not remembered but *present*.

Then the contract closed. The thing pulled, and the pull was absolute, and his grip broke like old rope, and the girl went up into the dark above the lanterns — up, and then laterally, impossibly, across the frozen canal toward the far bank where a covered sledge waited with its runners greased and its lamps hooded — and Theo fell flat on the ice with his arms still raised, holding the shape of her, holding nothing.

* * *

The ice was very cold. He lay on it and did not get up, and for a while he could not have told anyone why, except that getting up required a reason and he had just lost the only one he had.

Maren found him there. She came through the alley-mouth like a blade through cloth — the crowd parted for her the way it never parted for him, because grief moves people aside faster than fear — and she knelt on the frozen canal and turned him over and looked at the blood and the empty air above his raised arms and said nothing for a long time. Soldiers know that silence. It is the silence you keep when there is nothing that can be said that isn't a lie.

Odile found him too, eventually, when the flames died and the horns went quiet and the crowd drained back to the lanterns. She sat down on the ice beside him, cross-legged, in her patched ferry-coat, and she did not comfort him and did not speak, and the two of them and Maren stayed like that — three adults kneeling on a frozen canal under ten thousand festival lights, guarding absolutely nothing — until the lanterns began to go out, one by one, street by street, the way they always do at the end of a festival, and the longest night resumed being merely long.

Somewhere across the canal, the sledge moved north. Theo could not see it. He did not need to. He knew the roads out of the capital the way he knew ledger-columns, and there was only one road the buyer's people would take, and it led to the palace, and the palace at midnight on the longest night was lit like a second sun, and somewhere inside that light the crowned thing was waiting for its delivery with the patience of a creditor and the appetite of a winter.

He tried to stand, twice, and failed, and the third time Maren and Odile hauled him up between them and he stood swaying on the ice like a fence post in a flood, bleeding, hollow, forty years old feeling ninety.

And then, in the dead quiet after the festival, with the lanterns gone and the stars hard and close above the frozen city, he did the thing he had been unable to do on the ice. He reached up and touched his own forehead, where her palm had been, and the pressure was still there — fainter, but there, the way a brand stays warm — and he spoke aloud, alone, badly, stumbling over sounds no living tongue recorded, the name she had given him.

He did not know yet what the name meant. He did not know yet that names, in the economy of the Veil, were the one currency that could not be minted, appraised, sold, or stolen — that she had given him freely the one thing in all the world the contracts could not touch, and that in giving it she had done something to him that no retriever's glove could reach and no bill of sale could follow. He knew only that it was in him now, written somewhere deeper than memory, and that it was his to carry, and that carrying it was not grief.

Or not only grief.

Because grief was what he felt, overwhelmingly, standing in the snow with blood freezing in his hair: grief, and guilt, and the specific despair of a meticulous man confronting the one account he could not balance. Every other loss in his life had been a line item. This one had no column. The ledgers had taught him that everything could be priced, and the night had taught him the corollary he had spent twenty years avoiding: that the things worth anything are exactly the things that cannot be.

But under the grief, under the guilt, in the place where resolve had been hardening in him since the solstice eve, something else had begun to move. He had spent his whole life keeping accounts for other people's trades. Tonight, for the first time, he had a trade of his own to settle — a debt owed not to the Guild, not to Corvane, not to any contract, but to a child who had given him her name in the half-second before the world took her away. And debts like that, he understood suddenly, viscerally, in the marrow the ledgers had never reached, were not recorded.

They were *paid.*

"We're going to the palace," he said, to Maren, to Odile, to the frozen canal, to the empty air where she had been. His voice came out cracked and small and steadier than he had any right to expect. "Tonight. Now. Before the sledge reaches the gates. Whatever the contract says she is, she is mine to answer for — not by law, not by sale, but by name. And I am going to say her name in front of the thing that bought her, and we are going to find out what happens when a debt is called that cannot be collected."

Maren looked at him and saw a bleeding clerk proposing to walk into a fortress. Odile looked at him and saw something else — the same thing, maybe, that the girl had seen in the half-second before she chose him: a man who had never broken a rule learning, at the worst hour of his life, to break the one that mattered.

Odile spat on the ice, the old ferryman's oath, and said, "Then we go now, and I know a way in that isn't a gate."

The sledge-lamps were already out of sight beyond the far bank. The palace rose against the northern sky, lit gold, humming with festival music, its windows full of courtiers in veils that did not flutter.

And somewhere inside it, in a throne room Theo had never entered and could not yet imagine, the girl was being carried up a stair toward the thing that wore King Aldric's face — and the thing was patient, and the night was long, and the name was in Theo's blood now, and the blood was still warm.

Three people stood on a frozen canal in the dead of the longest night, and began, without torches or trumpets or any promise of survival, to walk toward the light.
