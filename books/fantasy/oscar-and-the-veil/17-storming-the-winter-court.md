## 17. Storming the Winter Court

*Theo*

The longest night began at dusk, and by full dark the palace had stopped pretending to be a palace.

Theo crouched in the shadow of the colonnade above the Winter Court's great hall, and what he saw below was not celebration. It was digestion. The festival of lights had been repurposed — every lantern, every candle, every floating globe of witchfire hung from the vaulted ceiling like fruit on a tree made of gold, and beneath that tree sat rows of courtiers who did not eat, did not drink, did not speak to one another. They simply faced the throne.

The girl was bound before it.

She stood upright in a cage of woven light — no, not light, Theo corrected himself, squinting until his eyes watered. Woven *absence*. Threads of nothing pulled taut between pillars of ice, and inside them the child hung like a moth pinned mid-flight, her small hands open, her mouth moving without sound. With each movement of her lips, one of the threads thinned.

They were unweaving her. Slowly. Ceremonially. As an appraiser unwraps something valuable.

Beside him, Maren Voss breathed once, long and controlled, the breath of a soldier preparing to do the thing she had sworn never to do: disobey. Her armor was plain steel tonight, stripped of the king's colors. "Three hundred courtiers," she said quietly. "Two hundred guards. One crowned thing."

"One at a time," Theo said. He touched the folded scrap of paper in his breast pocket — the girl's name, written in his own hand because he dared not trust his memory alone. It had grown warm against his chest since sundown, warm as a held hand. She knew they were coming. Somehow, through all that unraveling, she knew.

Odile swung down from the rafters' scaffolding above them, landing soft as snowfall. The decade the retriever had taken from her showed now in ways it hadn't before — a fine webbing of silver at her temples, a new carefulness in her left knee when she landed. She noticed him noticing and grinned. "Don't apologize yet. Apologize after we live."

"Is there a way in that isn't the doors?" Maren asked.

"There is always a way in," Odile said. "The palace was built by people who expected to be betrayed by their descendants. There's a servant's gallery behind the east wall — I used it to steal the crown jewels twice when I was nineteen. Nobody guards the walls of power. Power assumes the walls are loyal."

"And the lights?" Theo asked.

Odile's grin sharpened into something older and less kind. "A market trick. Every flame in this hall draws on the same vein of witchfire — the courtiers bought it cheap through the Guild, years ago, thinking themselves clever. Cheap coin buys cheap fire." She opened her hand. In her palm lay a sliver of black glass, a shard of bottled storm Theo had sold her a lifetime ago and she had never used. "You break the source, you break the supply. And then, my ledger-keeper, we see what a room full of borrowed things does when the debt comes due."

Below them, the crowned thing on the throne lifted its head.

It wore King Aldric's face, but it had stopped bothering to wear it well. The eyes moved a full second behind the body now, and sometimes not at all. When its gaze swept the hall, frost bloomed across the courtiers' shoulders like lichen. The girl in her cage of absence turned her head — impossibly, precisely — and looked up through three stories of stone and shadow directly into Theo's eyes.

Her mouth formed a word. No sound crossed the distance, but he heard it anyway, in the language under kingdoms, in the voice she'd used to name herself for him:

*Soon.*

* * *

*Maren*

Maren Voss had served the crown for twenty-two years, and in all that time she had learned exactly one law deeper than any statute: a shield does not abandon the person behind it. For months she had told herself the person behind the crown was still Aldric — sick, ensorcelled, recoverable. Walking these halls with Theo and the smuggler woman, blade drawn in the dark of the longest night, she finally admitted the truth she had buried beneath loyalty like a corpse beneath floorboards.

There was no one behind the crown anymore. There was only the crown.

Which meant her oath had come loose from its mooring, and what remained was choice. She found, examining it honestly, that she did not feel lost. She felt *lighter*. Treason, done for the right reason, weighed less than obedience done against conscience. Someone should have told her years ago. Someone probably had, and she hadn't listened.

"The gallery ends above the dais," Odile whispered, leading them along a passage so narrow Maren's pauldrons scraped both walls. "From there it's a fifteen-foot drop to the platform behind the throne. The crowned thing never turns around. Kings never do — that's the whole point of having backs to guard."

"I'll go first," Maren said.

"You don't know what's back there."

"That's why I go first."

Odile looked at her, and something passed between them — the smuggler recognizing, perhaps, another kind of keeper. "Fifteen feet onto marble. Roll when you land or your knees will end your career."

"My career ended three days ago," Maren said, "when I opened the archive door for a thief." She dropped through the gap before fear could finish forming.

The fall lasted a heartbeat. The marble came up hard; she rolled as instructed, came up in a crouch with her sword already clear of its scabbard — and froze.

Behind the throne, inches from where her boots rested, the wall was *wrong*.

It wasn't stone. Up close, in the witchless dark, she could see the truth of it: the entire back wall of the throne room was a membrane, thin as drumskin, and beyond it pressed something vast. Shapes moved there without light — slow, tidal movements, like leviathans seen through fogged glass. And threaded through the membrane ran filaments, hundreds of them, hair-fine, all converging forward, all feeding through the throne, all ending in the cage of absence where the girl hung.

She was not being unwoven, Maren understood suddenly. She was being *reeled in*. Thread by thread, the Veil itself was being hauled out of the world and into whatever waited behind that wall.

Above, through the grate of the gallery, Theo's face appeared, pale as paper. He had seen it too.

Maren set her jaw, took position beside the throne — the place she had stood ten thousand times in ceremony, guarding a king who had never once needed her — and drew a line in her heart between the man she had sworn to protect and the thing that had eaten him.

When Theo gave the signal, she would hold this ground against anything that crawled out of that wall.

Anything.

* * *

*Theo*

The plan had three parts, which Theo privately considered two parts too many for a plan involving a smuggler, a disgraced knight, and a ledger-keeper whose greatest previous act of violence was slamming a folio shut.

Part one belonged to Odile. From the gallery rail, thirty feet above the hall, she raised the shard of black glass over her head — and Theo watched her hesitate, just briefly, the way a person hesitates before burning a letter from someone dead. Then she hurled it down at the great chandelier-heart where all the festival's flames converged.

The storm inside the glass remembered itself.

Light did not shatter so much as *scream*. Every flame in the hall went black at once, and in the same instant the cold came off the courtiers in waves — the borrowed warmth they had been sitting on all evening, the heat they'd purchased through the Veil and hoarded beneath silk, released all at once like a flock of birds from a broken coop. Three hundred voices rose in confusion and terror as the temperature plummeted and the dark pressed in and the candles refused, absolutely refused, to relight.

In the chaos, part two: Maren dropped from the gallery's hidden mouth, rolled, rose behind the throne with her blade bare. Theo followed the rope she'd fixed, hand over hand, his palms burning, his fifty-two years screaming at him, down past the dais into the shadow behind the throne — where he stopped dead at the sight of the membrane wall, the filaments, the vast blind pressure of the thing beyond.

He had read about the Veil all his life. He had kept its gate sealed for twenty years. Nothing in the forbidden folios had prepared him for how *hungry* it looked.

"Third part," he whispered. His breath frosted and did not disperse; it drifted toward the wall, toward the filaments, like smoke toward an open window. "The cage."

The girl hung six feet away at the edge of the dais, wrapped in woven absence. Up close he could see the damage — whole strands of her gone translucent, and worse, gone *elsewhere*, frayed off into the dark behind the wall. They were eating her a thread at a time, and the threads were the binding that held the long winter shut.

He reached into his breast pocket and drew out the scrap of paper. Unfolded it. Her name, given freely, the one thing the Veil could not buy.

He spoke it aloud.

The effect was immediate and catastrophic. Every filament in the wall *flinched*. The courtiers' cries cut off as if a door had closed on them. On the throne, the crowned thing rose — slowly, joints cracking like river ice, King Aldric's beautiful ruined face turning toward them with those lagging, drowning eyes — and when it opened its mouth, three voices came out: the king's, worn thin; the Guildmaster's cadence underneath, Corvane's transactional smoothness; and beneath both, vast and patient and amused, the hunger itself.

"*The ledger-keeper,*" it said. "*Come to audit.*"

"I've come to cancel an account," Theo said. His voice shook. He let it shake. Fear, he had decided somewhere on the frozen road north, was not a thing to suppress but a thing to spend — and he intended to spend all of it tonight.

He stepped toward the cage, and the filaments lashed out like whips of black wire. Maren met them with her blade, cutting through darkness that shrieked, holding the line he needed. Behind him he heard Odile drop to the dais, heard her begin — of all things — to sing, low and steady, a ferryman's crossing-song in a tongue that made the membrane wall ripple with something almost like pain.

The girl's eyes found his through the woven dark. Her lips were cracked. Her hands were nearly transparent now. But she smiled — a small, fierce, human smile — and raised one fading hand to press against the inside of the cage.

And Theo, standing in the heart of everything he had spent his life keeping sealed, put his palm against hers through the threads of absence, and understood with perfect clarity that none of them were promised survival. Not Maren at her line, not Odile at her song, not himself. The night was long enough to hold all their deaths comfortably.

He spoke the girl's name again, louder, and began, word by word in the language under kingdoms, to unpick the knot.

The crowned thing stepped down from its throne.
