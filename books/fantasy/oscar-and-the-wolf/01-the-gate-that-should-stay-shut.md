## 1. The Gate That Should Stay Shut

The kettle sang before the sun did.

Wendell Amberdale had learned to read the mountain the way other people read weather — by small signs, by the language of things that could not speak. The kettle's pitch told them the night had been cold enough to frost the pump handle. The particular silence of the pines below the gatehouse told them no wind had come up from the valley. And the ache in their left hip, old as a childhood fall from the gate's own stair, told them it would be a gray morning, the kind that made the stones of Thornfell Gate look less like masonry and more like the ribs of something enormous sleeping under the hill.

They poured the water over the last of the blackleaf tea and stood at the window with the cup cradled in both hands, watching the light come up slow over the pass.

From here, the gate looked almost gentle. Two pillars of pale granite, taller than three men standing on each other's shoulders, joined by a lintel so massive that no cart, no crane, no army of engineers had ever been able to explain how it had been raised. Between them, where a road should have run down into the high valleys, there was only a wall of fitted stone — seamless, windowless, older than any kingdom whose banners had ever flown in these mountains. Travelers who came up the switchback trail sometimes mistook it for a shrine. They would tie ribbons to the hawthorn at its foot, leave coins in the mossy hollow of the threshold, murmur prayers to whatever saint or spirit they thought lived in such places.

Wendell let them. It was easier than the truth, which was that nothing lived in the gate except what was locked behind it, and that the coins and ribbons were, in their way, a kind of payment — small tolls paid by people who didn't know they were paying, to a debt they didn't know they owed.

Grandmother Amberdale had explained it once, when Wendell was small enough to sit on her knee and still believed the world was a kind place. *Think of it like a door in a house where something mean sleeps,* she'd said, turning her tea spoon slowly, always slowly, as if hurry offended her. *You don't need to know what it looks like. You just need to keep the latch down.*

*And if the latch breaks?*

She'd stopped stirring then. Wendell could still remember the exact quality of the silence that followed — not frightened, exactly. Weary. The silence of a woman doing arithmetic she'd done ten thousand times and never gotten a different answer.

*Then someone has to be the latch,* she'd said. *Drink your tea.*

Wendell drank their tea now, alone, in the same kitchen where she'd said it, in a house that smelled of woodsmoke and dried yarrow and the faint mineral coldness that came off the gate itself on damp mornings. They were thirty-one years old and had lived alone at Thornfell for nine of them, since the day they'd buried Grandmother under the rowan tree and walked back up the path alone, carrying her journal in one hand and a lantern in the other, feeling the way an orphan feels when the orphanage is also a fortress and also a prison and also, against all sense, home.

Comfortable loneliness, they called it, when they called it anything at all. Some nights it felt like a lie. Most mornings it felt close enough to true.

* * *

The routine began, as it always began, with the walk.

Wendell dressed in layers — wool first, then leather, then the long coat Grandmother had sewn for them out of a shepherd's canvas, stiff as bark but proof against the wind that funneled through the pass like water through a weir. They filled the feed pail for the goats, though the goats were more companions than livestock and knew it, complaining with theatrical hunger the moment they heard the door. Then they took the iron ring of keys off its hook by the hearth — six keys, five of them useless, kept for tradition's sake — and stepped out into the gray light.

Frost silvered the rowan's bare branches. The hawthorn at the gate's foot stood black and patient, its winter-ribbons snapping softly in a wind too high for Wendell to feel. Beyond it rose the pillars, and between them the sealed stone face of the gate, running with threads of meltwater that caught the early light like veins of quicksilver.

Wendell pressed their palm flat against the left pillar.

This was the part of the ritual no one else had ever seen, the part they would have died rather than show a living soul. Because the stone was warm.

Not sun-warm. Not the borrowed heat of a rock that had baked all day and remembered. Warm the way a body is warm — from within, faintly, stubbornly, as if somewhere deep in the granite a heart were beating at the pace of centuries. Anyone else who touched the gate would have found it cold as a crypt. Wendell had tested this, carefully, over the years: the tinker who'd camped nearby one autumn, the crown surveyor who'd come up with his instruments eleven summers ago, even Sera-from-the-valley's boy, who'd dared himself to slap the pillar on a dare and yelped at the chill. Cold, every one of them said. Dead stone.

But under Wendell's palm it breathed.

And under Wendell's palm, when they let themselves feel it — really feel it, past the fear, down where the truth of themselves lived — something answered. A warmth that began in the stone and passed into their skin and pooled in their chest like swallowed sunlight. Their grandmother had called it *the knack*, in the flat voice she used for things too large for ordinary words. The priests in the valley would have called it witchcraft, and meant a pyre by it. Wendell had never found a name for it at all. It was simply theirs, inherited like the crooked little finger on their right hand and the amber eyes that had made strangers cross themselves in market squares, a thing in the blood that had skipped their mother entirely and landed on them like a bird choosing a branch.

The warmth spread down their arm. Wendell closed their eyes.

For a moment — just a moment, the length of a held breath — they could feel the whole gate. Every fitted block, every ton of granite, laid out in their mind like a map drawn in heat. They could feel the seams holding. They could feel the deep, slow pressure of the thing behind the gate leaning against its wall the way a sleeper leans against a door, weight without malice, patience without end. And beneath all of it, faint as a pulse heard through a pillow, they could feel the latch — the binding their grandmother had woven with her knack and her blood and, the journal suggested, more than blood — holding, holding, holding.

It held this morning. It held most mornings.

But lately —

Wendell opened their eyes and pulled their hand back, and the warmth drained away like water through fingers, leaving the familiar ache behind, the one that lived in the bones of their palm now the way the ache lived in their hip. Lately the pulse had been harder to find. Lately the seams had felt thinner, the way ice feels thin when you've crossed the same pond a hundred winters and know, in your ankles, which year the freeze came late.

Lately, some mornings, the stone under their hand had shivered.

Not today. Today it was steady, warm, drowsy. Wendell made the rounds anyway — the full circuit, because ritual was ritual and shortcuts were how latches broke. They checked the mortar line at the base of the east pillar, where a hairline crack had appeared two winters ago and refused, despite their patching, to fully close. They cleared pine needles from the threshold channel, where rainwater ran off in a pattern Grandmother had insisted was deliberate, carved by the first builders to carry away more than water. They counted the ribbons on the hawthorn — seven new ones since the last traveler, bright scraps of red and blue and one strip of yellow silk that must have cost somebody dear — and left them, as always, untouched.

*Small tolls,* they thought, and almost smiled.

The goats followed them the whole circuit, complaining. The sky stayed gray. Somewhere far below, lost in the cloud layer, the valley was waking up — dogs barking, a smith's hammer starting its day, the whole small busy machinery of people who would never guess that their peace was maintained by one tired person and a wall of warm stone.

Wendell stood at the edge of the overlook above the switchback trail and looked down into the white nothing where the world went on without them, and felt the loneliness settle over their shoulders like the coat had — heavy, familiar, shaped to them by years of wearing.

It wasn't so bad, they told themselves. It wasn't so bad at all.

They almost believed it.

* * *

Midday brought the chores, and the chores brought the ordinary hours that Wendell had built their life around like a wall around a garden. Splitting wood until their shoulders burned. Mending the goat pen's north fence, where something — probably a fox, probably — had worried the boards in the night. Boiling yarrow and comfrey for the salve they traded down in the village twice a season, wrapped in brown paper, worth more than its weight in gossip: *the Amberdale hermit, still up there, still alone, still odd.* They liked the gossip. Gossip kept people at a distance, and distance was armor.

Once, in the early years, they had gone down to the valley more. Had tried, briefly, clumsily, to have a life among people — had drunk cider at the Harvest Fair and danced with a cooper's daughter and let themselves imagine, for one golden stupid autumn, that they could be a person who happened to have a secret, instead of a secret that occasionally did the work of a person.

Then the headaches had started. Then the dreams — the same dream, over and over, of standing before the gate and finding it open, finding it *open*, the stone swung wide like a mouth, and something on the other side lifting its head. Then, one market morning, a farmer's cow had gone mad and Wendell had put a hand on its sweat-dark neck without thinking, meaning only to calm it, and the animal had dropped as if struck, breathing slow and peaceful, and the farmer had looked at Wendell with an expression Wendell had spent nine years trying to forget.

Wonder, curdling into fear.

They hadn't gone down since, except for salt and flour and the twice-yearly salve trade, and those trips they made at dawn, hooded, brief, speaking to no one longer than courtesy required. The valley children had a rhyme about them now. Wendell had heard it once, sung by girls skipping rope outside the mill:

*Amberdale, Amberdale, keeper of the door,*
*What do you keep in, what do you keep for?*

They'd walked faster. They'd told themselves the rhyme was harmless. They'd told themselves a great many things, over nine years, and most of them had held, the way the gate held — mostly, mostly, with hairline cracks that refused to close.

The afternoon light was going copper when the shiver came.

Wendell was inside, kneading bread, hands floured to the wrist, when they felt it — not in the stone this time, because they weren't touching the stone, but in their teeth, in the roots of them, a tremor like the far-off footfall of something very large. The hanging lamp swayed on its hook. The water in the wash basin rang once, a clear thin note, and went still.

Wendell stood with their fists buried in dough and counted.

One. Two. Three. Four.

Four heartbeats' worth of trembling, and then nothing. The lamp hung straight. The basin lay silent. Outside, the goats had gone quiet — that was the worst part, the goats' silence, the way every creature on the mountainside seemed to be holding its breath and listening, listening, and then, one by one, letting it go.

They washed their hands. They walked out into the copper light with their heart going hard and their face arranged into calm, and they laid their palm against the east pillar, over the hairline crack.

Warm. Still warm. Still beating, slow and deep, the patient heart of the mountain.

But under the warmth, fainter than faint, Wendell felt something new. Something they had never felt in nine years of mornings.

The gate was *leaning*. Not the drowsy lean of the sleeping thing behind it, the pressure it had always made, weight without intent. This was different. This was the lean of a tired person against a door that might not hold — a shift of weight, a testing, the smallest possible redistribution of force from *held* toward *holding*.

As if, after all these centuries, the thing behind the wall had begun to wonder whether it was still locked.

Wendell snatched their hand back. Stood in the yard with the wind pulling at their coat and the copper light dying on the peaks, and made themselves breathe, in and out, the way Grandmother had taught them. *Panic is a luxury, child. Save it for after.*

*After what?* they asked her silently, the way they asked her everything now, the way they would ask her everything for the rest of their life.

The journal. The thought arrived with the certainty of a key fitting a lock. Grandmother's journal, up in the attic trunk where Wendell had put it nine years ago and not opened since the funeral — not because they feared what was in it, they'd told themselves, but because grief was heavy and the attic stairs were steep, and there had always been tomorrow.

There had always been tomorrow, and now the gate was leaning, and the goats were silent, and the light was going, and Wendell stood alone on their mountain with the sudden vertiginous knowledge that tomorrow had possibly just run out.

They went inside. They lit the lamp. They ate the bread they'd ruined with worry, tasting nothing, and watched the dark come down over the pass, and told themselves — one more time, the oldest lie in their collection — that it was probably nothing. A tremor in the earth. A settling of frost. Mountains moved; everyone knew that; it didn't mean anything.

In the morning, they would get the journal down.

In the morning, they would go down to the valley for salt and flour, earlier than usual, because the flour barrel was low anyway and because — though they'd have bitten their tongue bloody before admitting it — because the valley suddenly felt like a place they needed to see with their own eyes, needed to stand among the living and confirm that the world below was still the ordinary world, still safe, still real.

They banked the fire. They blew out the lamp. And in the black quiet of the gatehouse, lying awake in the bed they'd slept in alone for nine years, Wendell Amberdale listened to the mountain breathe, and felt, through the floorboards, through the foundations, through the deep stone bones of the world —

— the faintest tremor. Like a heartbeat skipping. Like a latch, somewhere very old, beginning to lift.

Far to the south, down where the valley smoked with morning fires, a herald's horn sounded on the wind, thin and clear and wrong, a sound that did not belong to any festival day.

Wendell sat up in the dark, and did not sleep again.
