## 2. Proclamation at the Crossroads

The valley smelled of wet wool and woodsmoke, the way it always did on market days in the last gray weeks before spring. Wendell Amberdale came down from Thornfell twice a year — once to buy salt and lamp oil and the coarse brown sugar they could not make themselves, and once more when the oil ran low too soon, which it always did. It was a rhythm worn smooth as the steps of the gatehouse stair. Two days of other people's voices, then back up the mountain to the quiet that fit them like an old coat.

They wore their plainest clothes for it. The gray wool coat with the frayed cuff. Boots gone soft at the heel. Hair bound back under a scarf so that nothing about them caught the eye or invited a question. Wendell had learned long ago that the trick of being unseen was not hiding but being forgettable — being the person people nodded at across a market square and could not describe an hour later. They had practiced it the way other children practiced letters.

The crossroads at Marrowbridge was already thick with carts when Wendell arrived. Farmers from the low fields with turnips piled like cannon shot. A tinker whose pots chimed as he walked. Two women arguing cheerfully over a bolt of blue cloth, holding it against each other's shoulders in turn. Children ran shrieking between the stalls, and a dog wove through everyone's legs with the serene confidence of a creature who had never once been kicked.

Wendell bought what they needed first, because they had learned that lesson too. Salt into the left saddlebag, oil into the right, sugar wrapped in waxed cloth and tucked deep where it wouldn't crush. They were haggling half-heartedly over a bundle of candlewick when the bells started.

Not the chapel bell. Not the mill bell either. This was a sound Wendell had heard only twice in their life, both times from far away, both times carried up the mountain on a wind that made the gate-stones hum: the long, brazen voice of a crown horn, blown three times in a pattern that meant *stop what you are doing*.

The market stopped. It was remarkable how completely it stopped — the haggling cut off mid-sentence, the children snatched up by their mothers, even the dog going still with its ears pricked. A cart pulled by a piebald mare was making its way to the center of the crossroads, and standing in that cart was a man in the crown's blue and silver, a herald's chain bright at his throat, a scroll in his hands.

Beside the cart walked six soldiers in the same colors, pikes shouldered, and behind them —

Wendell's stomach went cold before their mind caught up to what they were seeing.

Behind the soldiers walked a boy in chains.

They knew him. That was the thing that made it worse than any stranger. Tam Ellery, the cooper's son, sixteen years old maybe, seventeen — he'd been fourteen and all elbows the last time Wendell had seen him, hauling staves for his father behind the smithy. He'd grown since then. His face had gone the color of tallow and there was a bruise blooming along his jaw, and his wrists were locked in iron that Wendell could smell from where they stood, a hot, sharp, wrong smell, like lightning trapped in metal. Cold-iron. Made for one purpose only.

His mother was somewhere in that crowd. Wendell saw her push forward — a broad woman in a green shawl, Marta Ellery, who had once sold Wendell honeycomb and refused payment — and saw two soldiers catch her arms and hold her while she screamed words that didn't resolve into anything, just grief shaped into noise.

"By order of His Majesty Aldric the Second," the herald read, in a voice trained to carry over crowds the way a blade carries through cloth, "and by the authority of the Inquisition of the Crown, let it be known throughout the marches and valleys of Thornfell—"

Thornfell. Wendell felt the word go through them like a fishhook.

"—that the practice of the old gift is hereby declared treason against the realm. Any soul found practicing sorcery, hedge-craft, spirit-bargaining, or the keeping of unlawful wards shall be taken into custody. Any soul found *harboring* such a one shall share their sentence. Informants will be paid ten crowns' weight in silver. Ten crowns." He let the number hang. Ten crowns was a horse. Ten crowns was a year of a farmhand's wages. Ten crowns was enough to make a neighbor into a noose.

"The old gift is a corruption," the herald went on, and his voice took on the flat, rehearsed piety of a man reciting scripture he did not believe but had read often enough to perform. "It is a door left open to things that devour. The crown does not hate the gifted. The crown *pities* them, and offers this mercy: confess freely, submit to cleansing, and live."

Nobody in the crowd moved. Nobody believed the mercy. Everyone knew what cleansing meant; there were stories from the southern valleys, told in whispers, about pyres that burned for two days because whatever was inside wouldn't stop burning.

Tam Ellery lifted his head. And this was the part Wendell would carry up the mountain with them, that would sit behind their ribs for years afterward like a swallowed coal: the boy looked out over the crowd, over all those faces turned away from him, and he wasn't looking for help. He was looking for someone who would *look at him*. Just one person willing to meet his eyes and acknowledge that he existed, that he was Tam Ellery who whittled little birds out of linden wood and gave them to children at the well, that he was a person and not a cautionary tale.

Wendell looked away first.

They hated themselves for it with a completeness that surprised them. But they looked away, down at their own boots on the frozen mud, because meeting those eyes meant being remembered, and being remembered, today, meant ten crowns' worth of danger wearing your face. Somewhere in the crowd Marta Ellery was still screaming her son's name, and the soldiers were turning the cart, and the chains dragged a furrow through the mud behind Tam's heels like something written.

*Any soul found harboring such a one shall share their sentence.*

Wendell stood very still in the crowd and did the arithmetic they had spent their whole adult life avoiding. The gate. The wards their grandmother had laid, old work, wild work, the kind of thing this proclamation named in its first breath. The warmth in the stones that answered their hand. The gift they had never asked for and never shown anyone and could not cut out of themselves any more than they could cut out their own heartbeat.

If the crown came up the mountain road, they would find a gatehouse full of exactly the thing the herald had just declared treason.

*They won't come,* said the voice Wendell used for comfort, the one that had kept them safe for years. *It's one valley. One boy. Who knows what's at Thornfell? Nobody.*

But another voice, older and quieter, the one that sounded disconcertingly like Grandmother Amberdale, said: *Ten crowns is a horse. How many horses has the cooper got? How many neighbors owe you nothing and fear you a little? You are strange, child. You have always been strange. Strange is remembered.*

The herald rolled up his scroll. The horn blew again, twice, dismissal. The market resumed the way water resumes after a stone is pulled from it — too quickly, too brightly, everyone talking a little too loud, everyone not looking at the mud-furrow where the chains had dragged. Marta Ellery sat in the trampled snow where the soldiers had dropped her, rocking, and nobody went to her, because going to her was also a kind of confession now.

Wendell paid for the candlewick with hands that did not shake, which cost them more than shaking would have.

* * *

They made themselves walk the rest of the market slowly. That was the discipline of it — the hardest part of hiding was not running. Every nerve in their body wanted to buy nothing else, mount nothing, be on the mountain road within the hour. But a woman who flees a proclamation is remembered, and a woman who finishes her shopping is not. So Wendell bought a bolt of undyed linen, though they had linen enough. Bought dried apples. Let the tinker talk at them for four minutes about the merits of riveted handles and forgot every word of it before he finished. Nodded to the baker's wife. Complained mildly, in the mild local accent they kept folded away like good linens, about the price of lamp oil.

*Lamp oil.* They almost laughed aloud at the absurdity of it, haggling over pennies while a boy in cold-iron chains was marched toward whatever the crown called cleansing. The ordinary world had not stopped being ordinary. That was the horror of it. The bread still needed baking and the turnips still needed selling and the price of oil was still outrageous, and underneath all of it, the ground had quietly shifted, and everyone was pretending it hadn't.

It was at the far edge of the market, near the horse trough, that they passed the two men talking in low voices by a woodcart. Wendell hadn't meant to listen. Their ears simply did what ears did.

"—saw it myself, I'm telling you. Light. Up at the old gatehouse, winter before last. Greenish, it was, like foxfire, coming right out between the stones."

"You were drunk, Osric."

"I know what I saw." A pause, and then, lower, with the particular relish of a man weighing silver: "And I know what light like that's worth these days."

Wendell did not look at them. Wendell kept walking, past the trough, past the farrier's, counting their own footsteps the way they counted everything when panic came — one, two, three, four — until the numbers steadied them and the market's noise closed over the conversation like water over a sunk stone.

Osric Hale. Of course it was Osric Hale. Osric who poached in the high woods, who ranged further up the mountain than any man had reason to, who had been turned away from the gatehouse twice for asking questions about the old ruins. Ten crowns. Osric would sell his own grandmother for five.

The gatehouse had shown light. Had it? Wendell tried to remember — a winter night, wind off the pass, the wards straining at something on the far side of the gate, the stones flaring that deep sea-green they made when they were working hard. Yes. Yes, it had. They had stood in the yard and watched their inheritance burn visible against the snow and had thought only of whether the ward would hold, not of whether anyone was watching from the tree line below.

Grandmother would have known better. Grandmother had lived like smoke — no fixed hours, no patterns, buying in three different villages, letting the valley believe she was merely a widow herbalist with a bad hip and odd manners. She had taught Wendell the wards and never once taught them how she hid the doing of them. She had died before that lesson seemed necessary. She had perhaps assumed it never would.

*I have time,* Wendell thought, guiding the mare onto the north road. *I'll bank the wards down. Muffle them. Learn to do it quiet, the way she did. No one will—*

Then they rounded the bend past the last fence of Marrowbridge and saw the soldiers.

Two of them, mounted, resting their horses at the roadside spring where the north fork began its climb. Crown blue. And with them, gesturing up-slope, talking, pointing — a lean man in a poacher's leather jacket, hands sketching the shape of the mountain above them.

Osric's arm rose and fell, describing the switchbacks. Describing the route. Describing, unmistakably, the way to Thornfell gate.

Wendell did not stop. Stopping was death; stopping was a man on a horse saying *where are you riding, friend*, and then questions, and then the wrong kind of answers. They rode past at a walk, head down, one forgotten farmer among others on the road, close enough to hear the tail end of Osric's patter — "—the old woman's granddaughter's got the place now, keeps to himself mostly, odd sort—" and close enough to see one of the soldiers follow Osric's pointing finger up to where the peaks cut the sky, white and silent and utterly exposed.

The soldier said something to the other. The other wrote something down.

Wendell rode on. Around the next bend, out of sight, they let the mare pick her own pace and sat with their heart slamming against their ribs like something caged, like something that knew about cages.

* * *

The mountain took an hour to give them back to themselves. That was the old magic of it, the honest kind — the way the trees closed behind you and the air thinned and the noise of the world fell away layer by layer, cart-ruts giving way to deer trails, deer trails to bare rock and pine. By the time Wendell led the mare through the last stand of birches into the gatehouse yard, their breathing had slowed and their mind had gone hard and clear, the way it went before a storm.

The gate stood at the head of the yard where it had stood for longer than there were records. Twenty feet of ancient stone, taller than it was wide, set into the throat of the pass like a cork in a bottle, carved over every inch with symbols worn nearly smooth. Behind it, the pass went up and over and down into country no map marked, country the old stories said belonged to the wolves. In front of it, the world Wendell knew.

They unloaded the mare. Fed her. Banked the fire. Did everything in order, everything normal, and then, when the light had gone long and amber across the yard, they walked to the gate and laid their palm against the stone.

Warm. As always, warm — warmer than the day justified, warm as a sleeping animal, and beneath the warmth that faint pulse they had felt since childhood, slow as a great heart. The carvings stirred faintly under their hand, a shimmer like fish under ice, and the stone breathed against their palm.

Usually that warmth was comfort. Today it felt like a lit window in a dark country, like something that could be seen for miles by anyone who knew how to look.

"Not tonight," Wendell murmured to it, the way they spoke to it sometimes, alone, when there was no one living to hear. "Please. Lie low. Be stone. Just for a while. Be only stone."

The pulse under their palm slowed — or seemed to. Perhaps they imagined it. Perhaps it was only the mountain settling into evening. But the flare of green dimmed in the carvings' deepest grooves, and the gate went dark and quiet, and Wendell stood there a long moment with their hand on the warm stone like a hand on the chest of someone sleeping.

Inside, by lamplight, they began the work. Grandmother's books came down from the attic shelf — the ones written in her cramped slanting hand, half herb-lore, half something else, pages Wendell had read a hundred times and understood only a tenth of. There had to be a way. Damping wards. Veiling charms. Something. If the crown climbed the mountain and found nothing but a lonely gatekeeper and a lot of very old rocks, maybe the arithmetic ended differently. Maybe—

That was when Wendell noticed the book they had not seen before.

It was tucked behind the herbals, bound in gray leather gone soft as skin, unmarked on the spine. It was not in Grandmother's hand. The writing inside was older, denser, in an ink that had faded to the brown of dried blood — and it was not a book of wards at all. It was a record. Names. Dates reaching back further than the crown itself. And beside each name, in the same fading ink, a single word:

*Keeper. Keeper. Keeper. Keeper.*

And beside the last entry, the freshest, the one written in a hand Wendell knew better than any handwriting alive — beside *Maren Amberdale*, and the date of the year Wendell was born — there was a second line, added later, smaller, almost an afterthought.

*And after her, the child. If the gate calls, the blood must answer.*

Wendell sat back on the attic floor with the lamplight guttering and the wind rising off the pass, and outside, faint and rhythmic, the gate began — very softly, very patiently — to hum.

Far below, at the edge of hearing, on the north road, hooves.
