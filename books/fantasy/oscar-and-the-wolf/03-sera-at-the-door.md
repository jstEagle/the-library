## 3. Sera at the Door

The rule was simple, and Wendell had made it themselves: no one crosses the threshold.

Not travelers caught in weather. Not the peddler who came twice a year with salt and news and a smile that asked too many questions. Not the shepherd boys who dared each other to knock on the gatehouse door every autumn when the fog rolled up from the valley like something poured. Wendell would speak to them through the window slit if they spoke at all, trade for what they needed with coins pushed out on a wooden tray, and let them walk away wondering why the keeper of Thornfell gate lived like a hermit in a house of stone.

Because the house *was* stone, and so was the gate behind it — forty feet of it, older than any kingdom, older than the road that wound down to the valley — and Wendell had learned young that old stone remembers hands. Every touch left a little warmth. Every warmth was a thread. And threads could be followed.

So they kept their hands gloved in company, kept their voice flat, kept the wards fed the way one feeds a stubborn hearth: daily, patiently, without ever letting it see you flinch.

On the morning the stranger came, Wendell had been re-laying the ward-stones along the eastern face, kneeling in wet grass with their sleeves pushed back despite the cold, because gloves muffled the feel of the work. The stones had been restless all week — humming faintly under their palms like a struck bell that refused to finish ringing — and Wendell had told themselves it was the season turning, frost working into cracks, nothing more. They pressed each stone back into its socket and murmured the small words Grandmother had taught them without explaining them, and the hum settled, mostly, and Wendell chose to be satisfied with *mostly*, because satisfaction was a thing you rationed at Thornfell.

The ravens went up off the wall all at once.

Wendell froze, hand still on the last ward-stone. Ravens didn't startle here. Nothing startled here; the mountain had been empty of anything worth fearing for years, and the birds had grown fat and insolent on that emptiness. But they wheeled now over the battlements in a ragged black spiral, screaming, and beneath their cries Wendell heard — felt, rather, up through their knees where they knelt against the earth — a wrongness coming up the road. Footsteps that dragged. A heartbeat too fast. And underneath it all, thin as wire drawn taut across the whole morning:

*a hum.*

The same note as the stones.

Wendell stood slowly, wiping their hands on their thighs, and looked down the long approach to the gate. The fog hadn't burned off yet. It lay in the hollows like milk spilled and forgotten, and out of it came a figure — small at first, then less small, then unmistakably a person, staggering more than walking, one arm clamped around their own ribs as though holding themselves together by force of grip alone.

They were maybe two hundred paces away when they saw the gatehouse, and something in their posture changed — Wendell watched it happen, watched hope hit the stranger like a blow, watched them try to run and fail, stumble, catch themselves on air, and keep coming anyway. Blood had darkened the left side of their coat from shoulder to hip. Their hair had come loose from whatever tie had held it, and it streamed behind them, black shot through with an early streak of grey, though they couldn't have been past thirty.

*No,* Wendell thought. *No, no, no.* They backed a step toward the gatehouse door. The rule rose up in them like a wall: no threshold, no threads, no one in. The crown's men were in the valleys — everyone knew it now, since the herald and the chains and the boy from the mill hauled away between horses with his mother's screaming still hanging in the market square. Anyone bleeding on this road might be hunted. Anyone hunted would bring hunters. Hunters brought fire, and irons, and questions, and Wendell had spent eleven years building a life with no edges sharp enough to catch on any of it.

The stranger fell forty paces from the door.

They didn't cry out. That was the worst part, Wendell thought later — they went down almost politely, folding at the knees, catching themselves on one forearm, and then they *crawled*. Dragged themselves forward through the mud and wet grass with their chin up and their eyes fixed on Wendell standing in the doorway, and there was no plea in their face. There was something harder than pleading. Calculation, maybe. Or the particular stubbornness of someone who has decided exactly how far they are willing to fall short of a thing and not one step further.

"Gatekeeper." The word came out cracked, half-swallowed. The stranger lifted their free hand — the right one — and in its fist was a stone.

Half a stone, rather. Broken clean across, jagged-edged, the size of a fist, and even from thirty paces even without touching it Wendell felt it the way you feel a shout in a quiet room. It hummed. The same note as the ward-stones. The same note that had been restless all week, that had been creeping up through the bedrock for days like water rising in a well, except that here, in this stranger's bloody hand, it was *loud* — loud enough that Wendell's teeth ached with it, loud enough that the gate itself seemed to lean toward it, forty feet of ancient stone shifting its attention like a sleeping animal scenting meat.

"Where did you get that?" Wendell heard themselves ask. Their voice came out strange — steady, which surprised them, because nothing inside was steady.

The stranger tried to answer and coughed instead, wetly, and folded forward onto the ground.

*Walk away,* said every careful year of Wendell's life. *Close the door. Bar it. Let the fog take her back the way it took everything else that ever came up this road looking for help, and tomorrow the wards will still hold and your hands will still be your own.*

Wendell was already running down the steps.

* * *

She weighed less than she should have. Wendell got an arm under her and hauled, and she was conscious enough to help — barely, feet scrabbling, breath tearing — and together they covered the forty paces to the door, and then Wendell did the thing they had sworn never to do, the thing they had rehearsed refusing a hundred times in a hundred imagined scenarios: they pulled the stranger across the threshold of the gatehouse and shut the world out behind them.

The moment her body crossed the sill, every ward-stone on the eastern face sang.

It wasn't loud, precisely. It was worse than loud — it was harmonic, a chord rising out of the ground, out of the walls, out of the bones of the house itself, and the broken stone in the stranger's fist answered it note for note until the whole gatehouse rang like the inside of a bell. Wendell stood in their own kitchen with a dying woman sagging against their shoulder and felt the mountain wake up around them and thought, with a clarity that felt almost calm: *it knows her. The gate knows what she's carrying.*

Then the chord faded, sinking back down into the stone the way light sinks out of a room at dusk, and there was only the fire to build and the blood to stop and the questions to not ask yet.

Wendell got her onto the cot near the hearth — the cot they kept for themselves in winter, close enough to the chimney to stay warm, and they noted distantly that they'd given it up without a second thought — and cut the ruined coat off her with the good shears. The wound was a slash, long and ugly, from below her left collarbone down across the ribs. Blade-work, not claw. It had been bound once, hastily, with cloth that had soaked through and stiffened, and peeling it away reopened things that had just begun to close. She hissed but didn't scream. She watched Wendell work with those sharp, fever-bright eyes, cataloguing everything — the kettle, the needle, the little clay pot of grandmother's salve, the way Wendell's bare hands moved over the wound without trembling.

"You're not squeamish," she rasped, as Wendell drew the first stitch tight.

"I keep sheep."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the one you're getting." Wendell tied off the stitch and reached for the next. "Drink this when I'm done. Willow bark and something for the fever. You tore yourself open running on a closed road in crown territory, which means either you don't know these mountains or you didn't care, and I haven't decided which is stupider."

Something flickered across the stranger's mouth. It might have been a smile, in a face that had forgotten how. "Didn't care," she said. "I knew exactly where I was going. Took me four days longer than it should have." Her eyes drifted past Wendell's shoulder, toward the inner door, the one that led back to the gate chamber. "I could feel it from the pass. Yesterday. Like a — a toothache in the world. Getting worse the closer I came." Her gaze snapped back to Wendell's face. "You felt it too. Don't lie. Your stones were singing when I crossed your line."

Wendell set down the needle. Slowly. Their hands, they noticed, were not quite steady anymore.

"What's your name?"

"Sera."

"Sera." Wendell sat back on their heels and looked at her — really looked, past the blood and the fever and the wariness, at the half-broken stone still clenched in her right fist. She hadn't let go of it once. Not while being stitched. Not while drinking. Her fingers were locked around it the way a drowning person locks around a spar, and Wendell understood suddenly that letting go had stopped being possible somewhere on that road, if it ever had been. "Open your hand."

"No."

"I'm not going to take it. Open your hand, or we do this the hard way, with you biting down on a leather strap, because I need to know what my gate is singing to."

Sera held their gaze for a long moment. Whatever she found there must have been sufficient, because slowly, joint by joint, her fingers uncurled.

The fragment lay in her palm like a piece of dawn. It was pale grey shot through with veins of silver-white, and where it had broken, the fractured face glittered with a crystalline structure that no quarry in the living world produced. And it *hummed* — audibly now, in the enclosed room, a thin clear note that Wendell realized they had been hearing in their sleep for weeks without knowing it, mistaking it for wind, for tinnitus, for the mountain settling. It had never been the mountain. It had been this, calling to its other half, calling through whatever thin membrane of stone and distance still connected it to the thing it had been cut from.

And the gate answered. Through the inner wall, through forty feet of ancient rock, Wendell felt the deep slow pulse of Thornfell shift to match the fragment's pitch, the way two fires will bend toward each other through a shared chimney.

Sera watched Wendell's face while they watched the stone. "You know what it is," she said. It wasn't a question.

"I know it doesn't belong to you."

"It belongs to no one. That's rather the point." She shifted against the cot pillows, winced, and kept talking through the pain, as if pain were a tax she'd already agreed to pay and resented only the delay. "Three nights ago I was in the under-vaults beneath the Citadel of Vhalen. Crown vaults. There is a room down there that isn't on any plan, behind three locked doors and a guard rotation that never sleeps, and in that room there is a case, and in the case—" she lifted the fragment slightly, and its note climbed, sweet and awful, "—there was *this*. Half of a seal, gatekeeper. One of seven. This one belonged to Thornfell."

The word landed in Wendell's chest like a dropped stone down a well. They heard the splash a long time later.

"How do you know that name," they said quietly. "How does a thief from the capital know what my gate is called."

"Because the vault-keepers catalogued it. Because the clerks who moved it wrote it down in a hand so old the ink had gone brown. And because—" Sera paused, and for the first time since collapsing at the door, she looked uncertain. Small. Human. "Because when I ran with it, it pulled me north. I never decided to come here, understand? My legs decided. This thing has been dragging me across three provinces like a fish on a line, and every mile it sang louder, and I thought I was following a stolen prize to a buyer, and then I crested the pass yesterday and felt *your gate* answering it, and I understood that I hadn't stolen a treasure at all. I'd stolen a piece of a door." Her fever-bright eyes fixed on Wendell's. "And doors, gatekeeper, are meant for opening. That's why the crown keeps them. That's why the crown hunts people like you. They've figured out what their grandfathers buried."

Wendell stood. Walked to the window. The fog was burning off the valley below, and the world looked ordinary and green and doomed. Behind them, the fragment hummed, and the gate hummed back, and somewhere in the walls of their own body, in the marrow the gift had always lived in, something hummed too — a third voice joining the chord, uninvited, inevitable.

"The man they chained in the market," Wendell said, not turning around. "The miller's boy. He had the old gift. Little of it. He could coax a sick animal through a night it shouldn't have survived. That's all. That's what they dragged him away for." Their voice stayed level. It cost them. "If the crown wants what you stole, then you've led it to my door, and everything I've spent eleven years building is already ash. Do you understand what you've done?"

"I understand that the alternative was letting them have it." Sera's voice had gone hard again, the fever burning through the softness. "I understand that whatever wakes up behind your gate when it fails — and it *is* failing, gatekeeper, I could feel it failing from fifty miles out, the way you feel a dam swelling before it bursts — will not stop at the valley. I read the old accounts in the vault archives. I know what the wolf-gates were built to hold. So yes. I brought my trouble to the one place in the world that might know what to do about it." A pause. "I'm sorry about your ash."

Wendell turned. Looked at her — at the stranger bleeding on their cot, holding half a seal like a talisman, too stubborn to die and too honest to lie well. Eleven years of rules stood in them like a palisade, and Wendell watched, from some weary height inside themselves, as the palisade came down log by log.

"Sleep," they said. "Fever first. Then you'll tell me everything — the vault, the route, who else knows, all of it. And then—" they glanced at the inner door, at the deep slow pulse beyond it, "—then you and I are going to have a much longer conversation with my gate."

Sera's eyes were already closing. But her fingers curled back around the fragment, and as she slid under, the last of her strength spent, the stone's note softened — matched itself, Wendell realized with a chill that had nothing to do with the draft, to the rhythm of her slowing heart.

As if it were keeping her alive on purpose.

Outside, far down in the valley, a raven cried once and went silent, and the fog began to lift off the road Wendell had watched her come by — lifting, thinning, revealing the long empty miles she had crawled through — and on the wind, very faint, very far, came the sound of hooves.
