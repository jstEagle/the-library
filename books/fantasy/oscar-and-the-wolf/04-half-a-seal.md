## 4. Half a Seal

Sera woke to the smell of woodsmoke and something sweeter underneath — dried lavender, maybe, or the memory of it, baked into the beams of a house that had stood longer than any kingdom she'd ever sworn an oath against.

For a moment she didn't know where she was. The ceiling above her was low and dark, crossed by hand-hewn rafters, and a bundle of herbs hung from one of them, turning slowly in a draft she couldn't feel. Her shoulder throbbed with a deep, wet ache. Her hands were clean. Someone had cleaned them.

Then it all came back: the pass, the snow, the dogs behind her, the arrow that had kissed her shoulder like a warning. The gatehouse rising out of the dark like a thing grown rather than built. The door opening before she could knock.

She sat up too fast and the room tilted.

"Slowly." The voice came from the corner, quiet as falling ash. Wendell — she was almost certain that was the name, though they hadn't given it; they'd only said *call me what you like* when Sera asked, which was such a strange answer that Sera hadn't asked again — sat on a stool by the hearth, mending something. A sock, of all things. Their hands were steady. Their eyes were not. They watched her the way a deer watches a frozen pond, testing every inch before trusting it.

"You bled a lot," Wendell said. "I stitched you. I'm no healer, but I've had practice."

"With yourself?"

"With myself."

Sera looked down at her shoulder. The bandage was neat, wound with a care that contradicted everything else about this place — the dust, the shutters nailed closed, the way the whole house seemed to hold its breath. "Thank you," she said, and meant it, and hated how thin it sounded.

Wendell shrugged. "You were dying on my doorstep. That's a bad look for anyone."

"And yet you opened the door."

A long silence. The fire ticked. Somewhere below them — below the floor, below the earth — Sera felt it again, that hum. It had been there since she collapsed at the threshold, faint as a second heartbeat, and now that she was awake enough to listen properly, she could feel it answering something. Not her. Something near her.

Her hand went to her coat without her permission.

The fragment lay where she'd hidden it, wrapped in oilcloth inside the lining, and through the fabric she could swear she felt warmth. Not body-warmth. Stone-warmth. The kind of warmth that comes from somewhere very deep and very old.

Wendell's eyes followed the movement. "That's why you came here," they said. Not a question. "Not running from the crown. Running *to* something."

"I didn't know what," Sera said. "I still don't."

She unwrapped it.

The fragment was the size of a man's palm, roughly triangular, its broken edges jagged as a bite mark. It should have been cold. Mountain stone, vault stone, stone that had spent years locked in a crown strongbox beneath three feet of iron and scripture — it should have been dead as any rock. Instead it glowed faintly from within, a color Sera had no name for, amber and silver fighting for dominance, and the light pulsed. Slowly. Rhythmically.

Like breathing. Like a heartbeat.

"It's been doing that since I took it," Sera said. "Three weeks. It got louder every mile I came north. I thought I was losing my mind. Then I saw your gate, and—" She stopped, because saying it aloud made it worse, made it real: *then I saw your gate, and the stone sang.*

Wendell had gone very still. The mending lay forgotten in their lap. They were looking at the fragment the way people look at graves.

"May I?" they asked.

Sera hesitated. Every instinct honed by three weeks of flight said *no, never let anyone touch it, especially not someone who lives alone under a mountain full of old magic.* But those same instincts had told her to trust the pull that dragged her bleeding up the Thornfell road, and that instinct had saved her life.

She held it out.

Wendell reached across the space between them — slowly, the way you reach toward a strange dog — and laid two fingers on the stone's face.

The change was immediate and terrible.

The light flared. The hum that Sera had felt all this time as a whisper became a chord, low and resonant, rising up through the floorboards, through the walls, through the soles of her boots, until the whole gatehouse seemed to be ringing like a struck bell. The herbs swayed overhead. Dust sifted down from the rafters. And Wendell made a sound — small, involuntary, wounded — and snatched their hand back as if burned.

But they weren't burned. Sera could see that. Their fingers were unmarked. What had changed was their *eyes*: for half a heartbeat, the amber in them had lit from within, matching the stone exactly, and then it faded, and Wendell pressed their hand flat against their own chest as if holding something in.

"What was that," Sera whispered. It wasn't really a question either. She wasn't sure she wanted answers anymore. Answers had a way of being worse than mysteries.

"That," Wendell said, and their voice shook only a little, "is my gate. Answering me. Or me answering it. I've never been sure which." They looked at the fragment in Sera's palm, and Sera watched them arrive at the understanding piece by piece, like watching ice calve off a glacier. "Where did you get that?"

"The crown's vaults," Sera said. "Under the Citadel of Vail. Three floors down, behind a door that takes four keys and a confession to open."

Wendell stared at her.

"I was a quartermaster's clerk," Sera said. "Records. Inventories. Nobody looks at clerks. Nobody remembers clerks exist." She laughed, and it came out wrong, brittle. "I catalogued the thing for six years. Item 1147. *Stone fragment, provenance unknown, recovered from the Thornfell region, do not handle without warding.* Six years of walking past it. And then one night last autumn I was doing the winter audit, and I touched the shelf next to it, and the fragment — it turned. Like a head. Like it heard me coming."

The fire crackled. Neither of them moved.

"I told myself it was imagination. I told myself a lot of things. But it kept — *calling*. Quietly. Like something behind a wall, tapping. And then the witch-hunt started, and the heralds came, and I watched them take a woman out of the records office because her grandmother had been a hedge-healer, and I understood that whatever was happening to me, the crown already knew about it. They knew what the stone was. They knew what it wanted. And they were locking it away and burning everyone who might understand why." She met Wendell's eyes. "So I stole it. Four keys. One forged confession. A cart of tallow candles with a false bottom. And I ran north, because the stone pulled north, and it pulled north along the old roads, and every map I ever catalogued said the same thing about where those roads end."

"Thornfell," Wendell said.

"Thornfell."

* * *

They went down to the gate itself after that. Sera insisted; Wendell resisted, argued, and finally yielded with the air of someone conceding a battle they'd known was lost from the start.

The passage descended in a spiral, cut into living rock, older than any masonry Sera had ever seen. No torches — none needed. The walls gave off a faint luminescence, veins of that same amber-silver light threading through the stone like sap through wood. Sera's skin prickled. The fragment in her hand grew warmer with every step, pulsing faster now, eager, and she found herself thinking of a horse feeling home stables under its hooves.

"How far down?" she asked.

"Not far." Wendell's voice had flattened into something careful. "It doesn't like visitors. It tolerates me."

"It talks to you?"

"No." A pause. "Yes. Not in words. In... weather. Pressure. Some days the whole gate feels like a held breath. Some days it feels like a scream that hasn't decided to happen yet." Wendell glanced back at her, and in the strange light their face looked carved, ancient, exhausted. "You have to understand — I inherited this post. My grandmother kept it before me, and hers before that, back past any name we wrote down. Nobody told me what I was keeping. There was no instruction. Just: *keep it sealed, touch the stones each morning, tell no one.* I've done that for eleven years. I don't know what's behind the gate. I don't know what the seal is. I know the stones are warm and I know I'm the only reason they stay that way, and until three days ago I thought that was the whole of it."

The passage opened.

Sera had imagined a door. What she saw instead stopped her breath.

The gate filled the end of the cavern like a cliff face fills a valley — a wall of interlocking stones, each the size of a coffin lid, fitted together without mortar in a pattern that hurt to follow, because the pattern *moved*, slowly, the way clouds move, the way water moves seen from very far above. Across its whole surface ran lines of light, dim and dormant but unmistakably alive, converging toward a single point at the center.

At the center was a gap. A socket, roughly triangular, the size of a man's palm.

Empty.

"Oh," Sera said. It was all she could manage.

The fragment in her hand blazed. The gate answered — a shudder ran through the entire wall, stone grinding against stone with a sound like a mountain clearing its throat, and the lines of light flickered brighter, reaching toward her, toward the shape in her grip, hungry and hopeful and *waiting*.

Wendell grabbed her arm. "Don't—"

"I'm not." She wasn't. Gods help her, some part of her wanted to — wanted to complete it, wanted to see what happened, wanted the calling in her blood to finally be *answered* — but a larger part had spent three weeks running from exactly this, and that part was screaming. "I'm not going to put it in. I just needed to see. I needed to know I wasn't mad."

"You're not mad." Wendell's grip on her arm was trembling. "That's the worst part. I keep hoping one of us is mad, because mad can be survived."

The gate subsided slowly, reluctantly, its light dimming back to embers. But it didn't stop watching. Sera could feel it — the attention of the thing, vast and patient, fixed on the fragment like a starving animal fixed on a held-out hand.

She made herself turn away. Made herself walk back up the spiral with Wendell's hand still on her arm, both of them leaning on each other more than either would admit.

* * *

In the kitchen, over a stew that Sera tasted only distantly, she told the rest of it. The parts she'd told no one.

"There were others in the vault," she said. "Other items from Thornfell. I memorized the registry before I ran — it's what I'm good for, remembering lists. Fragment 1147. A bell, item 903, that rang by itself on certain nights. A book, item 1122, written in a script nobody in the Citadel could read, except that one archivist swore it looked like *wolf tracks in snow*, and he was dismissed for saying so." She stirred the stew without eating. "And a ledger. Old. Older than the kingdom. It referenced a treaty — not between nations. Between *kinds*. Men and something else. And every entry in that ledger pointed to the same family name as keeper."

Wendell had stopped moving entirely.

"Amberdale," Sera said gently. "It said Amberdale."

The silence stretched so long that the fire burned down a log's worth. When Wendell finally spoke, their voice was barely above the crackle.

"My grandmother never spoke of her mother's family. Never. When I asked, once, when I was small, she looked at me the way you'd look at a child who'd asked about a murder, and she said, *some doors are closed because they're locked, and some are closed because of what's behind them, and you will never ask me again.*" They laughed — a hollow, awful sound. "Eleven years I've kept this gate. Eleven years touching these stones, feeling them warm under my hands like living flesh, telling myself it was duty. Tradition. And the whole time—"

"The whole time it was yours," Sera finished quietly. "By blood."

The fragment sat on the table between them, wrapped loosely in its oilcloth, glowing through the weave like a coal through ash. As they spoke, Sera noticed, its pulse had changed. Slower. Calmer. Almost content.

Almost *home*.

"It knows," she said. "Whatever it is — half a key, half a seal, half of something — it knows it's in the right place. It's been pulling me north for three weeks and now it's lying there like a cat in a sunbeam." She wrapped her hands around her cup. "Wendell. I need to tell you the rest, and you're not going to like it."

"They'll come for it," Wendell said. Not asking. "The crown. You stole from their vaults. They'll track you."

"They won't have to track me." Sera set down the cup. Her hands were steady; she was proud of that, in a distant, disconnected way. "The night before I fled Vail, I was summoned to the office of Lord-Inquisitor Rell himself. He didn't accuse me. He didn't even question me. He asked me about my work, my health, my family. And then he said — very kindly, that's what I remember most, how *kind* he was — he said, *Clerk Sera, if you should ever encounter anything in your duties that calls to you, remember that the crown rewards honesty, and punishes theft, and that mountains are very easy to surround.*"

The fire popped. Outside, wind moved through the high passes, and beneath the wind, beneath the floor, beneath the world, the gate hummed its long slow note.

"He knew," Sera said. "He knew what the stone would do to me. He *let* me run. Because I'm not the thief, Wendell. I'm the hound. They put the quarry in my hands and pointed me north, and somewhere behind me — a day, maybe two — there are riders following the trail I couldn't help leaving." She looked up, and found Wendell already looking at her, and saw her own dread reflected there, doubled. "And that's not even the worst of it. The worst is what Rell said when I asked him, one clerk to a lord, why the crown keeps a broken stone in a vault for sixty years and burns hedge-witches in the streets."

Wendell waited.

"He smiled," Sera said. "And he said, *because a lock that cannot be opened is only half useful, Clerk. We are waiting for the other half.*"

On the table, the fragment pulsed once — bright, urgent, like a heartbeat skipping —

and from far below, through layers of stone and silence, the gate answered.
