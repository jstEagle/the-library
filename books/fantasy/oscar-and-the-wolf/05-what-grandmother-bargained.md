## 5. What Grandmother Bargained

The attic smelled of cedar and dust and something older underneath, like the mountain itself had been breathing up through the floorboards for a hundred years.

Wendell climbed the ladder slowly, one hand pressed to the low rafters, and told themselves — as they had told themselves every morning since Sera arrived two days past, fevered and muttering on the pallet by the hearth — that they were only looking for willow bark. For feverfew. For anything in the old stores that might bring the stranger's burning down.

They were not looking for the truth. They were absolutely not looking for the truth.

But their hands knew better. Hands always did. Wendell's gift lived in them the way water lived in a spring — quiet until disturbed — and even now, alone in the dim and raftered dark, they felt it stir against the attic's stillness. The trunks up here answered them. Not with words. With warmth. The same impossible warmth that lived in the gate-stones at the top of the pass, the warmth Wendell had spent thirty years pretending was nothing, was imagination, was the sun's heat lingering in granite long after sundown.

Liar, Wendell thought, and knelt before the first trunk.

It was Grandmother Amberdale's. Everyone in the valley said so, when they said anything at all about the strange woman who had kept the Thornfell gate alone for forty years and then died alone in the same week her granddaughter turned fourteen. *The old witch*, some of them whispered, though never within earshot of the house. *The keeper*. That word too, but softer, almost reverent, the way people speak of things they'd rather not look at directly.

Wendell lifted the lid. Moth-dust rose in a pale ghost. Inside: winter cloaks gone gray with age, bundles of dried herbs tied with faded red thread, a christening gown no one had ever worn, and beneath all of it — flat, square-cornered, bound in leather gone the color of dried blood —

A journal.

Wendell's heart did something complicated in their chest. It stopped, and raced, and stopped again, all in the space of a single breath.

*You are looking for willow bark,* they reminded themselves. Their hands were already lifting the book free.

It was heavier than it should have been. Heavier than paper and leather had any right to be, as though the words inside had weight, as though the years pressed between its covers had settled into the binding like snow on a roof. The clasp was iron, worked into the shape of a wolf's head with its jaws closed around a ring, and when Wendell's thumb brushed the cold metal, the journal grew warm.

Not gradually. Not gently. It warmed under their fingers the way the gate-stones did — sudden, alive, *answering* — and Wendell nearly dropped it.

"Stop," they whispered to themselves, to the book, to whatever old thing was listening from inside the walls of their own inheritance. "Just — stop."

But their hands didn't stop. Their hands had never once obeyed them, not when they healed a dying lamb at seven years old and lied to everyone about it, not when they sealed a hairline crack in the gate at nineteen and told no one at all, not now, as they worked the wolf-clasp open and let the cover fall back onto dust and shadow.

The first page bore a single line of ink gone brown with decades:

*I am Maren Amberdale, and I write this so that someone after me will know what I have done.*

Wendell sat down hard on the trunk's edge. Below them, through the floorboards, they could hear Sera turning restlessly in her fever, murmuring fragments — *the vaults, the vaults, don't let them find the other half* — and the sound steadied them somehow. Someone else was carrying this burden now. Whatever Grandmother had done, it hadn't died with her. It had come bleeding to Wendell's door with half a broken stone in its fist.

They began to read.

* * *

The early pages were ordinary. Accounts of weather. Notes on which families owed what at market. A recipe for elderflower cordial that Wendell remembered drinking as a child, sweet and pale gold, on an afternoon they had thought was unremarkable and now understood to be one of the last simple days of their life.

Then, dated forty-three years past, the handwriting changed. It grew smaller. Tighter. As if the writer feared being overheard even by her own future self.

*The crown's men came today. Not the witch-hunters — those are new — but surveyors, with their instruments and their maps. They measured the gate. They asked me how long my family has kept it, and I told them since before there was a valley, which is true, and they laughed, which is also telling.*

*They do not know what the gate is. They think it is a boundary marker. A relic. They think "keeper" is a title like "reeve" or "miller," passed down like land.*

*Let them think it.*

*But I write here what I would not say aloud: the gate is weakening. It has been weakening since my mother's time. Each year the stones grow colder where they should be warm, and each winter the wolves sing closer, and last month I found a print in the snow inside the wards that no dog made and no wolf made either, because it was shaped like a hand.*

Wendell read that line three times. Outside the small round window, clouds dragged their shadows across the high shoulder of Thornfell, and somewhere below, the gate stood in its ancient silence, warm only where Wendell touched it, and Wendell thought: *I have been pressing my palms to a wound and calling it health.*

*My mother sealed the worst of the cracks with her own blood before she died, as her mother did before her. This is the inheritance, child-of-mine-who-will-read-this: we do not guard the gate. We ARE the gate. Our blood is the mortar. Our lives are the lock. And the lock is failing because there are fewer of us each generation, and less in each of us, and the thing behind the door does not sleep so deeply as it used to.*

*So I have made a decision. I am ashamed of it already, and I have not yet done it.*

Wendell's mouth had gone dry. They turned the page with a finger that trembled and hated itself for trembling.

*There is one among the wolf-spirits who does not want the gate broken any more than I do. This sounds mad. I know it sounds mad; I have read it back twice. But he came to me at the full moon, walking upright through my own wards as though they were morning mist, and he had eyes like a man's, and he spoke my grandmother's name — MY grandmother, who died before I was born — and he said: "Your blood remembers me. Ask it."*

*And I did ask it. And my blood DID remember him.*

*He calls himself Oscar. He says he was a man once, in the time of the first war, and that he chose what our family chose, and that the choosing changed him. He says there are others behind the gate who were never men at all, older and hungrier, and that when the seal fails they will pour through like winter pouring through a broken window, and no army of the crown will stop them, because crowns cannot fight what has no body to cut.*

*He offers me a bargain. He says the seal can be remade — not patched, remade — if both sides give something true. The spirits will give their strength to the stones. And my side must give...*

Here the writing stopped. There was a blot, as though the pen had been held too long in one place while the writer's hand shook. Then, beneath it, in letters pressed so deep they'd nearly torn the page:

*My side must give a keeper. Bound willingly. Living. Into the stone itself.*

*I refused, of course. I refused, and he bowed his head and said he would wait, and I believed him, God help me, I believed him, and I went looking for another way. There is no other way. I have looked for eleven years. There is patching, and patching, and patching, and every patch costs blood and buys only time.*

*Last night I dreamed of the valley drowned in wolves. Last night I dreamed of children's names read out in villages that no longer existed. And this morning I woke and knew what I would do, and I am writing it down so that whoever comes after me will understand that I did not do it lightly, and will hate me if they must, but will NOT undo it.*

*I have sent for him. I have agreed to his terms — almost. I have bargained him down, as one does. I will not go into the stone myself; I am too old, and the binding wants years, wants a life still mostly unlived. Instead I have promised him a keeper of my blood, freely given, in the next generation. He accepts this. He says a promise made at the gate binds deeper than any chain.*

*I have chosen you, little one. You are reading this, so you are the one. I am sorry. I am so sorry. If there is comfort in it, know that I watched you sleep last night and could not imagine anyone else strong enough, or stubborn enough, or loved enough by the mountain to survive what I am asking.*

*The pact will be sealed at your majority. Until then, the bargain holds the gate together — his strength flowing into the stones, our blood holding the pattern — and you will feel it as warmth. Do not fear the warmth. The warmth is him keeping his word.*

*His name is Oscar. Remember it. He will remember you.*

Wendell closed the journal.

For a long moment they simply sat in the dusty light, the book heavy in their lap, hearing their own heartbeat and the creak of the old house and, far below, the small broken sounds of Sera's fever.

*She sold me.* The thought arrived without anger at first, just a kind of vast, cold clarity, like looking down into deep water. *Before I could walk, she sold me. She looked at a child asleep and signed her away to a wolf.*

And then the anger came, rising slow and enormous, and Wendell welcomed it, because the alternative was grief, and grief for Maren Amberdale was a country they could not afford to enter. Not now. Not with a fugitive downstairs and a failing seal in their blood and the whole machinery of the crown grinding toward the mountains with irons and fire.

"You watched me sleep," Wendell said aloud, to a dead woman, to dust. "You watched me sleep and you *chose*."

The attic offered no answer. The rafters ticked in the cooling air. On the trunk beside them, the moth-eaten christening gown lay folded like something waiting to be worn.

Wendell opened the journal again, more gently this time, and read the final entries. They were short. The handwriting deteriorated — the strokes of a woman losing her grip on the world.

*He came again tonight. He does not age. He says the pact needs renewing at each turn of the season, and that he will teach the stones to recognize my successor's touch. He asked about you. He asked your name. I did not tell him. Some things should be given, not taken.*

*The warmth in the gate is stronger this spring. Good. Let it hold. Let it hold past my death, past my daughter's, past all our deaths, until the one I've promised comes into her strength.*

*One more thing, and then I will close this book forever: if the gate begins to fail in YOUR time, little one, it means the bargain is ending, and you will have to choose what I chose — or refuse it, and find another way, and may the mountain forgive us both if there isn't one.*

*Do not trust him. Trust the warmth. They are not the same thing.*

*— M.A.*

Wendell sat until the light through the round window went from gold to copper to ash. Somewhere in those hours the fury burned down to coals, and under the coals, unwelcome as a splinter, was something else: understanding. Not forgiveness. Never that. But a terrible arithmetic — *patching, and patching, and patching, and every patch costs blood* — and Wendell recognized it, because they had been doing that arithmetic their whole life without knowing its name, pressing their hands to cold stones and giving them heat they couldn't spare and calling it nothing, calling it habit, calling it the weather.

Grandmother hadn't betrayed them out of cruelty. She'd betrayed them out of exhaustion. Out of love, even — the awful, devouring kind, the kind that spends other people.

That was worse. That was so much worse.

* * *

Downstairs, the fever had broken. Sera slept genuinely now, deep and even, her color better, the bandage on her arm needing changing but the flesh beneath it knitting with a speed that made Wendell's gift hum uneasily against their own skin. Half a seal-stone, Wendell thought, watching her breathe. Carried against the heart for who knew how long. Of course it left marks.

On the table, wrapped in linen, the fragment waited. Wendell had avoided touching it since the first night, when it had sung against their palm like a struck bell. Now they unwrapped it and looked at it properly for the first time: a jagged half-disc of gray stone shot through with veins of silver, ancient beyond guessing, and along its broken edge a row of tiny notches that might have been writing or might have been teeth.

*Half of the seal binding the wolf-gates,* Sera had said, in one of her lucid hours. *Stolen from the crown's own vault. There are gates like yours in six places, keeper. Did you know that? Six. And the crown has the other halves of five of them.*

Five gates. Five locks, and the crown holding the keys, and a king's men dragging a neighbor's son away in chains for the crime of warming milk with his mind. Wendell set the fragment down as if it had bitten them.

Dusk came early to the valley this time of year. Wendell lit lamps, fed the fire, checked the wards out of habit — and stopped, halfway through the old circuit, with their hand resting on the lintel stone.

The warmth was wrong.

Not absent. Not weak. *Wrong* — flickering, the way a lamp flickers when someone walks past it, the way a voice wavers when the speaker is distracted. For thirty years the warmth had been constant as sunrise. Tonight it pulsed, quickened, slowed, like something breathing. Like something *waiting*.

"Do not trust him," Wendell murmured. "Trust the warmth. They are not the same thing."

But the warmth was him. Grandmother had written that too, hadn't she? *The warmth is him keeping his word.* Both and neither. A riddle with teeth.

Wendell finished the circuit anyway, because ritual was a rope and they needed something to hold. At the top of the pass, the gate loomed against the last violet light, twin pillars and a lintel of stone older than the valley's name, and Wendell laid both palms flat against the pillar as they had ten thousand times before.

The stone shuddered.

Not the flicker-warmth of the lintel. Not a pulse. A *shudder* — deep, structural, the groan of a foundation settling or a tooth beginning to loosen — and it traveled up through Wendell's arms and into their chest and rang there like the fragment had rung, and far below in the house a shelf rattled, and a cup fell, and Sera cried out in her sleep.

Wendell snatched their hands back. The gate stood silent. The mountains stood silent. The evening star hung over Thornfell's shoulder, indifferent, beautiful.

But under their feet, faint as a heartbeat heard through a wall, the ground carried a rhythm that had not been there that morning. Slow. Patient. Counting.

And from somewhere beyond the gate — beyond the stone, beyond the wards, beyond the thin place where the world's skin wore thinnest — came a sound Wendell had heard only in dreams they'd never admitted to having.

A wolf, singing.

Low. Almost gentle. Almost like a name.

Almost like *Oscar*.
