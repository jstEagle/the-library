## 6. Captain Rell

The village of Lowmark smelled of woodsmoke and fear.

Captain Aldric Rell stood in the square at dawn with his boots planted on wet cobblestones and his hands folded behind his back, watching the villagers gather the way cattle gather when they smell blood on the air. They came because the herald's writ required it — every soul between twelve and sixty, assembled by the well — but they came also because they could not help themselves. Fear was a gravity. He had built a career on understanding that.

He counted them without appearing to count. Forty-one adults, nineteen children, three elderly women who leaned on one another like felled trees propped upright. One smith with burn-scarred forearms whose eyes kept flicking to the road north. A miller who had not slept; Rell could see it in the gray beneath his eyes, the tremor in his hands as he held his cap. And near the back, half-hidden behind a fishmonger's cart, a girl of perhaps fifteen who stood too still. Guilt made people fidget. Concealment made them statues. He marked her face for later.

"Captain." The reeve of Lowmark approached with the bowed shoulders of a man carrying an invisible yoke. He was fat and sweating despite the cold, and his chain of office — tin, painted to look silver — hung crooked against his chest. "The village is assembled, as the crown commands. We're honest folk here, Captain. God-fearing. If there's been any talk of— of the old gift, I'd have heard it."

"You would have heard it," Rell agreed, "and done nothing."

The reeve's mouth opened. Closed. Sweat beaded at his hairline.

Rell let the silence do its work. He was a patient man; patience was the whole of his craft. Swords broke doors, but questions opened them, and what lay behind most doors did not want to be found. He had learned long ago — first as a soldier in the border wars, later under the Inquisition's gray cloak — that terror was a blunt instrument. It shattered people into useless pieces. Certainty, on the other hand, was a scalpel. You showed a man that you already knew everything, that resistance was merely a delay of the inevitable, and he would hand you his neighbor's secrets to buy himself a day.

"I am not here for honest folk," Rell said, loud enough to carry across the square. Forty-one pairs of ears strained toward him. "I am not here for the God-fearing. I am here because the crown has learned that the old gift survives in these mountains — in hedgerows and hearth-corners, in midwives' charms and shepherd's blessings — and that it is growing. The king does not punish ignorance. He punishes concealment." He paused, letting his gaze travel slowly across the crowd, and watched them flinch in sequence like wheat bending before wind. "Those who come forward freely will be questioned gently. Those who are *brought* forward will wish they had walked."

* * *

The questioning took place in Lowmark's tithe barn, which Rell had chosen over the moot-hall for its single door and its smell of old grain and dust — a smell that reminded people of confession, though none of them would know why. His lieutenant, Vess, took names at the door. His clerk, a narrow young man named Pellin with ink-stained fingers, recorded every answer in a ledger that was becoming, page by page, a map of the valley's sins.

They came through one at a time. The miller, who admitted his wife kept a horseshoe over the lintel "for luck only, Captain, everyone does." The smith, who confessed to buying fever-charms from a peddler two winters past and wept openly until Rell, weary of the noise, dismissed him with a caution. The still girl from the square, whose name was Maren and whose father had died in autumn — she answered in whispers and knew nothing, but her hands were stained with something dark at the knuckles, and Rell noted *dye? ash?* in the margin beside her name.

By midday the pattern emerged, as patterns always did. Lowmark was a village of small guilts: charms against milk-souring, whispered verses over newborns, a grandmother's ring worn on a cord. Nothing large. Nothing that explained why the crown's diviners, working from the burned observatory records in the capital, had fixed their instruments on this particular fold of mountains and pronounced the words that had brought Rell and thirty soldiers up from the low country:

*"There is a gate that should stay shut, and there is blood that keeps it."*

Rell believed the diviners. This was not faith; it was arithmetic. Three villages within twenty miles of Thornfell pass had reported the same phenomena in the last year — livestock born wrong, wells that sang at night, lights on the high slopes where no shepherd would camp. Two travelers had gone missing near the pass. And six years ago, before Rell's time in this province, an entire hunting party had come down out of those hills empty-handed and silent, and one of them had lived just long enough, in a fever-house in Duncarreg, to say *"the wolf spoke"* before his heart stopped.

Superstition, the local magistrates had called it. Rell had read the report himself, in the archive below the Citadel, and had seen the physician's sketch of the dead man's face: frozen, yes, but frozen around an expression that no cold could carve. Wonder. Or recognition.

"The wolf spoke," Rell murmured to Pellin as they broke bread at noon. "What do you make of that?"

"A dying man's babble, Captain."

"Mm." Rell chewed slowly. He was a lean man, hard-used, with iron-gray at his temples and a face that people described as forgettable until they met his eyes, after which they remembered nothing else about him. "A dying man babbles nonsense. A frightened man babbles truth. Which do you think that hunter was, at the end?"

Pellin considered. "Frightened, Captain."

"So do I." Rell wiped his knife and sheathed it. "Send in the fishmonger's daughter next. No — wait. First, the widow Tarn. She's been sitting outside the barn since morning, watching the door. Watching isn't waiting, Pellin. Watching is deciding."

* * *

Widow Tarn was seventy if she was a day, with a spine like a fence rail and eyes the color of river ice. She sat across the trestle table from Rell and looked at him the way no villager had yet dared: directly.

"You'll be wanting to know about Amberdale," she said.

Rell set down his pen. Beside him, Pellin's nib scratched to a stop.

"The name has appeared in our inquiries," Rell said carefully.

"It would. It will. From every mouth in this valley, sooner or later, though most of them will pretend otherwise while you're looking at them." The old woman folded her cracked hands on the table. "Old Marta Amberdale, dead six years now, God rest or don't. Lived up at the gatehouse on the Thornfell road since before I was married. Kept herself apart. Kept the gate, her family always said, though what keeping a gate means, up a mountain where there's naught to keep out but weather, I couldn't say."

"And the family?"

"Gone, all of them, save one grandchild. Wendell." Something flickered across the widow's face — distaste, or its cousin. "Comes down to market four times a year, no more. Trades wool and dried herbs. Polite enough. Strange enough. Wears their hair long and won't say whether they're a lad or a lass, and folk round here have decided it's easier to say nothing than to be corrected." Her chin lifted slightly. "I'm telling you this, Captain, because I've watched that child my whole life, and I'll tell you what I know for true: nothing ever went wrong near Thornfell while an Amberdale lived in that house. And in the year since the roads got dangerous — since the crown started burning hedge-witches down south — I've begun wondering whether those two things were ever separate."

"You suspect the granddaughter carries the gift."

"I suspect," Widow Tarn said, "that Marta bargained for something, the way our grandmothers' generation bargained, and that the price is still being paid by someone. But suspicion isn't knowledge, Captain, so I'll give you knowledge instead." She leaned forward, and her voice dropped as though the barn walls themselves might repeat it. "Tam Halloway. The cooper's boy. Seventeen, simple-minded since a fever at birth, harmless as a lamb. He grazes his father's geese on the lower Thornfell slope, spring through autumn. Last week — Thursday it was, the night the sky went strange and green over the pass — Tam came home late, white as whey, and wouldn't eat, and when his father shook him he said—" her mouth twisted, remembering — "he said, *the stones were singing, and the light came out of the ground like it was breathing.*"

Rell felt something shift in his chest, cold and precise. "The gatehouse?"

"He'd run from the direction of it. His father beat him for wandering, poor fool, thinking to frighten the fancy out of him. It didn't take." She sat back. "I'll swear it before any priest you like. That boy saw something on that mountain, and there's one house up there, and one person in it."

Rell wrote for a long moment. When he finished, he looked up. "You understand, mistress, that this testimony may bring soldiers to the Amberdale gatehouse."

"I understand that whatever sleeps up there has slept a hundred years on an Amberdale's watch," the widow said, rising stiffly, "and I understand that the crown means to wake it either way, by hunting or by hanging. Better your soldiers than the other thing, Captain. At least yours carry steel."

She left. Pellin stared after her, then at his captain.

"She believes it," the clerk said quietly. "All of it. The gate, the bargain, the singing stones."

"Yes."

"Does it trouble you, Captain, that the faithful and the superstitious tell the same stories?"

Rell gathered his ledger, squared its edges against the table. Outside, the afternoon light had begun to fail, and the mountains to the north had turned the color of old bruises.

"It troubles me far more," he said, "when the stories stop matching. Fetch me the cooper's boy. And then send word to Vess — we ride for Thornfell at first light."

* * *

Tam Halloway was exactly as the widow had described: seventeen years old in body, younger in the eyes, with a cowlick of brown hair and goose-down still clinging to his coat. He sat across from Rell and grinned nervously and answered questions with the unguarded honesty of a mind that had never learned to lie.

"What did you see, Tam? On the mountain. Tell me everything, in order. Don't leave anything out, even if it seems foolish."

The boy's grin faded. His hands found each other in his lap and held on.

"I was up past the birches, where the geese like it, 'cause of the soft grass. And it got dark early, even though it weren't late. And then—" he frowned, reaching, and Rell waited, utterly still, giving him silence the way one gives water to a drowning man — "then the stones started singing. Not loud. Like when you hold a shell to your ear, but... underneath everything. Under the ground. And I thought, I ought to go home, but my feet wouldn't, and then the light came." His voice thinned. "Out of the ground. Up by the big gate. Green-gold it was, like — like the way the sun looks through cider. And it breathed, sir. In and out. In and out. And there was someone standing in it."

Rell's pen had stopped moving. "Someone. Who?"

"A person. Standing in the light with their arms out, like — like holding a door. And the singing got louder, and the light got brighter, and I think it was going to open, I think the door was going to open, and then it didn't. The person shouted something and it all — folded up. Like a hand closing. And then it was just dark again, normal dark, and I ran home." Tears stood in the boy's eyes now, though he wasn't sobbing. "I never told nobody 'cept Da, on account of Da hits. But it weren't a dream, Captain. My boots were muddy right up to the knee and there was a warm spot on the ground where I'd stood, warm as fresh bread, and it stayed warm till morning. Geese wouldn't go near it after."

Rell sat back. For a long moment the barn was silent except for the tick of the cooling stove and, far off, a dog barking at nothing.

"Thursday last," he said finally. "The night the sky went green."

"Green as pond-water," Tam agreed, nodding hard. "Everyone seen that part."

Everyone had. Rell had read the reports from three villages, each describing the same aurora, each attributing it to weather, to God's displeasure, to the northern lights come south. None of them had known that above Thornfell pass, at the same hour, a wounded fugitive was — according to the dispatches from Duncarreg — fleeing east along the smugglers' roads with half of a broken seal-stone in her bleeding hands.

None of them had known, but Rell knew, and the pieces fit together in his mind with a click he felt in his teeth. The stone had been taken. The gate had strained. And someone — some *Amberdale*, standing in green-gold light with arms outstretched like a keeper at a door — had held it shut by sheer inherited blood.

The diviners had said: *there is a gate that should stay shut, and there is blood that keeps it.*

They had been more right than they knew. And that meant the crown's orders — *locate the fragment, recover the fragment, assess the gate's integrity* — were not merely incomplete. They were, in Rell's private judgment, dangerously naive. Because if one untrained keeper could strain against a breaking seal and win, then the thing behind the seal was awake enough to push. And if the crown's councilors imagined they could crack such a gate deliberately and command what came through it—

But that was above his station. His station was a road, a warrant, and a name.

"Thank you, Tam," he said gently. "You've helped the crown today. Truly."

The boy beamed through his tears, and Rell felt nothing at all about deceiving him, because he hadn't deceived him. It was true. It would keep the boy safe, or at least overlooked. Righteousness, Rell had found, was mostly a matter of bookkeeping: you entered every mercy in one column and every necessity in the other, and you made sure the second column balanced the first.

* * *

He climbed to the chapel loft at dusk, where the garrison's field altar stood under a slanted roof, and knelt — not from habit, though habit was there, but because he had learned that certainty required maintenance. Faith left untended curdled into doubt, and doubt, in his trade, killed men.

Below in the square, Vess was drilling the squad for the mountain march. Pellin was copying the day's testimony into the master ledger. Somewhere a woman was singing a lullaby off-key, and the sound drifted up through the floorboards like smoke.

*Lord of the narrow road,* Rell prayed, in the old soldier's cadence, *keep my hand steady and my eye clear. Let me find what festers and cut it clean. Let me not flinch from the cutting, nor glory in it. Let the innocent sleep safe tonight because I did not.*

It was a good prayer. He meant every word of it. That was the thing people never understood about men like him — the inquisitors, the witch-finders, the gray cloaks the ballads cursed. They imagined cruelty, or ambition, or madness. They never imagined this: a tired man on his knees in a cold loft, meaning it completely.

He rose, buckled his sword-belt, and went down into the dark.

At first light they rode north — thirty soldiers, two supply carts, and Captain Aldric Rell at the head with the widow's testimony folded inside his coat, over his heart, like a relic. The road climbed through pine shadow and melting snowmelt. By noon they passed the last farmstead, its fields fallow, its door barred. By midafternoon they reached the place where the map dissolved into guesswork: the old Thornfell track, rutted and half-swallowed by brush, switchbacking up toward a pass that no honest traveler used and no dishonest one survived twice.

Vess drew rein alongside. "Scouts found boot-prints on the lower track, Captain. Recent. One walker, small stride, heading up. Also—" he hesitated — "wolf sign. Plenty of it. But odd. The prints cross our path going up, and nowhere coming down."

"As if the wolves went up the mountain," Rell said, "and did not care to leave."

"Yes, Captain."

Rell looked up the trail. The trees grew sparser here, bent and gnarled, and through their black branches he could see the shoulder of the mountain, and high upon it, small with distance, a slate roof and a thin thread of chimney smoke.

Someone was home.

"Wolf sign going up," he murmured, almost to himself. "Light breathing out of the ground. Stones that sing." He nudged his horse forward onto the track. "Well. Then let us go and meet the keeper."
