## 7. Oscar

The night after the gate shuddered, Wendell did not sleep.

They sat instead in the gatehouse kitchen with every lamp lit — a extravagance of oil they could not afford and no longer cared about — and read their grandmother's journal a third time, as if the words might rearrange themselves into something kinder on the third pass. They did not. *I have made my bargain with the wolf Oscar,* the old handwriting said, steady and unashamed, *and I have signed it in the only ink that binds.* Below that, a pressed sprig of rowan gone brown as rust.

Outside, the wind came down off Thornfell in long, low moans, and Wendell told themselves it was only wind. They had lived beneath this mountain for eleven years. They knew its weather like they knew their own hands.

But wind did not make the wards sing.

It was a small sound at first, the way a kettle begins — a thread of tone beneath hearing, felt in the teeth more than the ears. Wendell's head came up. The wards were woven into the gate itself, into the threshold stones and the lintel and the iron bell that had not rung since Grandmother Amberdale's funeral; they had been silent so long that Wendell had half convinced themselves they were ornament, ritual, the superstitions of a dead woman kept alive by her superstitious grandchild.

The singing grew. Not an alarm — that was the terrible part. It was almost welcoming. A chord resolving.

Wendell set down the journal, took up the iron poker from the hearth, and stood very still in the middle of the kitchen, listening to their own heartbeat argue with the wards' strange music.

Then the light in the room changed.

Not brighter. *Deeper* — as though the lamplight had remembered some older color it used to be, gold going to amber going to something like honey held up against a low sun. The shadows leaned. The fire in the grate bowed without wind.

And through the kitchen doorway, where there had been nothing but dark hall a moment before, stood a wolf.

He was larger than any wolf had a right to be — shoulder-height to Wendell's chest, built like something carved rather than born, his coat the grey of granite at dusk with paler streaks running through it like quartz veins. Frost did not cling to him, but the air around him seemed to remember winter. He stood inside the wards. Inside the house. Inside the circle of lamplight that had burned for eleven years without once admitting anything that meant harm, and he looked at Wendell with eyes that were not a wolf's eyes at all.

They were human eyes. Grey-green, tired, old in a way that had nothing to do with age — the weariness of someone who has watched a great many things end. And they were kind, which was somehow worse.

"Don't," the wolf said, when Wendell raised the poker. His voice was low and rough, like stone rolled by water for a hundred years. "You'll only bruise the poker."

Wendell did not lower it. Their arms shook, and they hated that he could see it. "You're inside my wards."

"I am." He said it simply, without pride or apology, the way one might say *it is raining.*

"Nothing gets inside my wards."

"No," he agreed. "Nothing does. Your grandmother wove them well, and you've fed them faithfully — better than she expected, honestly. She gave you maybe five years before neglect thinned them out. You've given them eleven." His ears tilted, something almost like a smile moving through the stillness of him. "But wards keep things out, little keeper. They don't decide what's already in. And I have been in these mountains longer than your family has had a name."

The word *keeper* went through Wendell like a nail through the palm. They had never called themselves that aloud. They had never let themselves think it whole. It had always been *gate-watcher*, *warden of the pass*, the small dry title of a small dry life — never *keeper*, which was a word with weight behind it, a word from the journal, a word from the pact written in ink that bound.

"You've been reading my grandmother's journal," Wendell said. It came out steadier than they felt. "How."

"The same way you'll learn to, eventually. The gate remembers what it loves." He lowered his great head slightly, and the lamplight ran along his back like water over stone. "You left it open on the table. Windows shut, door barred, and the journal open to the page about me. That's less a lock than an invitation, in this house."

Wendell crossed the kitchen — three steps, each one costing more than the last — and closed the journal, and put their hand flat on its cover, and felt the warmth rise up through the leather into their palm. The same warmth the gate-stones gave. The same warmth, they realized now with a lurching, vertiginous clarity, that had always lived faintly under their own skin, all their life, waiting.

"You're him," they said. "Oscar."

"I'm him."

"Grandmother bargained with you."

"She did." Something moved behind those human eyes — grief, or its fossil. "She bargained hard, too. She was the best of the line of Amberdales, and the worst, depending on which side of the bargain you stood. She got twenty-six years of quiet out of me for a price she never told you about. Ask yourself why, Wendell Amberdale. Ask yourself why a woman who loved you spent her last decade teaching you everything except the thing you'd need most."

The fire cracked. Somewhere far off in the mountain, deep below the hearthstone and the cellar and the roots of the world, something shifted its weight, and the teacups on the shelf sang together in a thin frightened chorus.

Wendell flinched. The wolf did not.

"That," Oscar said quietly, "is why I'm here."

"It shuddered last night." Wendell's voice had gone thin. "The gate. First time in eleven years."

"It will shudder again tonight. Harder. And the night after, harder still, until either the seal is renewed or it fails, and I promise you — I have seen it fail before, in another range, in another century — you do not want to be standing in the valley when it does." He took a step forward, and the floorboards did not even creak beneath him, and Wendell understood suddenly that weight was a courtesy he extended to the world, not a law upon him. "What sleeps behind your gate is not wolves, child. Wolves are what we became so we could walk out and speak to keepers. What sleeps behind the gate is what we were before we chose to be anything at all. It is hunger with a horizon. It is winter that thinks. And it has had a very long time to dream about doors."

The poker in Wendell's hands felt absurd now, a twig raised against a flood. They set it down against the table, slowly, because their arms had begun to ache, and because some animal instinct told them that lowering a weapon before this creature was safer than holding it badly.

"Why tell me?" they asked. "If you wanted the gate open, you could—" they gestured at the walls, the wards, the whole architecture of safety rendered meaningless, "—you're already inside. Why warn me?"

For a long moment the wolf said nothing. The lamplight moved in his eyes, and Wendell saw again how human they were, and thought: *he was not always this. Whatever he is, he was not always this.*

"Because I knew your grandmother," he said at last. "Because I made a bargain with her, and part of my side of it was her bloodline's protection, and I am apparently the last creature in these mountains that keeps its word." His muzzle turned toward the window, toward the black bulk of Thornfell rising against the stars. "And because there is a man climbing my mountain with irons forged to drink magic, and a vault in the capital where men who have never heard the gate sing are keeping half of its seal like a trophy. When they come — and they are coming, Wendell, witch-hunts do not turn back at borders — they will not renew the seal. They will break it on purpose and call the flooding of the valleys a weapon. Your crown has decided the thing behind the gate would make a fine sword."

The cold that went through Wendell then had nothing to do with the wolf.

Sera. The fragment humming on the shelf upstairs like a struck bell that would not finish ringing. Half a seal, stolen from the crown's own vaults, carried bleeding to the one door in the mountains that mattered.

"How do you know about—"

"I know what happens on my mountain." He looked back at them, and there it was again, that terrible gentleness. "Your guest carries the other half of your inheritance in her saddlebag, and she doesn't understand what it is, and neither, yet, do you. But you touched it. Didn't you. It answered you."

Wendell said nothing. Their silence said everything.

"The seal was made to be renewed by a keeper's hand," Oscar said. "Blood of the line, freely given, willingly bound. Your grandmother renewed it every year on the solstice, alone, at midnight, and she never told you because telling you would have meant showing you the cost, and she loved you too much — or feared you too much, with Amberdales it was always both — to let you choose it while she could still carry it herself." He stepped closer, close enough now that Wendell could feel the winter-memory radiating off his coat, could smell pine and snow and something older underneath, something like hot stone and lightning. "The gate is failing because the keeper's line has thinned to one, and that one does not know the rite, and the crown is coming to break what's left. Three threads, all pulling toward the same ending. Unless someone cuts a new pattern."

"What kind of pattern?"

The wolf smiled. It was not a comforting sight. There were too many teeth in it, and too much sorrow behind them.

"Not tonight. Tonight you need to sleep, and burn the rest of that oil, and decide whether you believe me — you won't, not fully, not yet, and that's wise of you. Trust a wolf too quickly and you deserve what follows." He turned toward the doorway, toward the hall and the front door and the night beyond, and paused there, a silhouette cut out of lamplight. "But know this much, Wendell Amberdale, and hold onto it: the gate will fail within the season. The choice is not whether. It is only what stands in the doorway when it opens — a keeper, or a famine."

"And if I refuse? If I run south with Sera and the fragment and let the mountains keep their own secrets?"

"Then I will be sorry," Oscar said, "and the valleys will be drowned, and I will have broken my word to a dead woman, and none of those things will matter to anyone for very long." His human eyes held Wendell's across the warm, bright, doomed little kitchen. "Your grandmother asked me once why I help your family at all, after everything. Do you know what I told her?"

Wendell's mouth was dry. "No."

"Someone has to remember what we were, so we don't stop trying to be better." He inclined his great head, a bow, formal as a courtier's. "Sleep, keeper. Listen to your stones. They'll tell you I'm telling the truth — they've known me longer than you've been alive."

And the light in the room folded back to ordinary gold, and the wolf was gone — not through the door, Wendell was certain of that, not past the wards, but *out* the way rain leaves a field, everywhere at once and nowhere to point to. The kettle-thread hum of the wards faded to silence. The fire burned. The journal lay under Wendell's hand, warm as a sleeping animal.

They stood in the kitchen for a long time. Then they took the lamp and climbed the stairs, past Sera's door, behind which they could hear the uneven breathing of a fevered sleep, to the little room where the seal-fragment rested on a shelf between a chipped cup and a jar of rowanberries.

It was glowing. Faintly, softly, pulsing in time — Wendell pressed their palm against it before they could stop themselves — in time with their own heart.

Below the floorboards, beneath the foundations, beneath the roots of the mountain, something vast turned over in its sleep and began, very slowly, to test the door.

* * *

Dawn found Wendell in the gatehouse yard with frost on their eyelashes and both palms flat against the great threshold-stone, feeling it answer.

The warmth came up stronger than it ever had — no longer the gentle hearth-glow of eleven quiet years but something urgent, something with a pulse in it, quickening hour by hour like a heart running toward a cliff. And threaded through the warmth, unmistakable now that they knew to listen, was a rhythm. A count. The gate was counting down, and it had been counting for longer than Wendell wanted to know.

Behind them, the gatehouse door opened. Sera stood in it wrapped in Wendell's spare blanket, grey-faced, fever-bright, one hand pressed to her bandaged side — and the other curled around the fragment she had evidently brought down with her, because of course she hadn't let it out of reach. Her eyes went from Wendell's face to the stone under their hands, and widened.

"It's doing that because of you," she said. Not a question.

"Last night," Wendell said, straightening, wiping the frost and stone-dust from their palms, "a wolf walked through my wards, sat down in my kitchen, and told me the world ends this season unless I do something about it."

Sera stared at them.

"He also said," Wendell went on, and their voice only shook a little, "that the men who hurt you are already climbing the mountain. So I suggest we decide, quickly, what we're going to be standing in the doorway when they arrive."

Far above, at the tree line, a bird went up in sudden alarm — and beneath it, faint but closing, came the ring of hooves on the high road.
