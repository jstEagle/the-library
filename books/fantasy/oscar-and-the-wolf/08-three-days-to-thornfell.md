## 8. Three Days to Thornfell

Sera had stolen from the crown's vaults, lied to a witch-finder's face, and crossed the Thornfell passes in winter with an arrowhead still lodged near her shoulder blade. She had not, until this morning, tried to argue with Wendell Amberdale.

"You're not listening." She kept her voice low, though the smugglers' road was empty in both directions as far as the fog allowed. "I said I'm *not listening* is your problem. Three days south, there's a ship out of Greyharbor. Three days, and we're past Rell's reach forever."

Wendell walked ahead of her on the narrow track, hood up, hands jammed in the pockets of a coat that had been mended more times than Sera could count. They did not turn around.

"The gate doesn't stop being my problem because I'm on a boat."

"The gate doesn't need you standing next to it when Rell puts irons on your wrists!"

That stopped them. Wendell turned, and even through the fog and the hood, Sera could see the set of their jaw — that stubborn, terrible steadiness she was beginning to hate almost as much as she relied on it.

"Rell isn't riding to Thornfell for me," Wendell said. "He's riding for the stone. For what it opens. And if he reaches the gate while I'm three days south playing sailor—"

"Then at least you'll be alive to be useless about it!"

The words came out sharper than she meant, and she saw them land. Wendell flinched — barely, a small thing around the eyes — and then the stubbornness closed over the hurt like water over a dropped coin.

They walked on. Sera followed, furious at herself, furious at Wendell, furious at the fragment of seal-stone wrapped in wool against her ribs where it hummed its low, patient hum, like something sleeping that dreamed of home.

* * *

The smugglers' road was not on any map the crown possessed, which was the only reason they were still ahead of Rell's riders instead of behind them. It clung to the eastern face of the mountains, half ledge and half wish, threading through pine and scree and the occasional shrine to gods whose names had been chiseled off during the last purge. The fog came up from the valleys in the afternoons and swallowed everything past arm's reach, so that they walked all day inside a grey room with no walls.

By the second morning, Sera had learned the shape of the argument by heart. It went like this:

"South," she'd say.

"Thornfell," Wendell would say.

And then a silence full of everything neither of them knew how to say, and the road again.

It was on the second morning that the silence broke differently. They had stopped at a spring that seeped from the rockface, black and cold, and Wendell was filling the waterskin when Sera said, without planning to:

"My brother stayed once. To hold a door."

Wendell's hands went still.

"It was in Vessany, during the border war. Our house was between two companies, and Tammo — he was sixteen — he said someone had to bar the door or the soldiers would come through, and he'd be quick, he'd only be a moment." She watched the water overflow the skin and run down Wendell's wrist. "We heard the door break from the treeline. That's the whole story. That's what staying gets you."

Wendell capped the skin. When they spoke, their voice was very quiet. "Was it true? That no one else could have barred it?"

"No one else was *there*."

"Then it wasn't a choice, was it. It was just the moment arriving." Wendell stood, and handed her the water first, always the water first, and Sera hated how much that small courtesy undid her. "My grandmother chose Thornfell. She wrote it down, Sera. She sat in that attic with her whole life in front of her and she *chose* it, and she wrote down why, and every reason she gave is a reason I have too."

"She also wrote down Oscar's name," Sera said. "She bargained with a wolf-spirit, Wendell. You keep talking about her choices like they were scripture. Maybe they were just mistakes she dressed up pretty."

For a long moment Wendell looked at her, and Sera braced for anger. What came instead was worse — a kind of weary honesty.

"Maybe," Wendell said. "Maybe she made a mistake. But it was *her* mistake, made with open eyes, and it held for forty years. I'd rather make my own mistake at my own gate than live safe in Greyharbor knowing I ran from mine." They shouldered the pack. "Come or don't. But stop asking me to be someone who runs."

* * *

They heard the riders before they saw them — late on the second day, a sound like knuckles cracking somewhere below the fog, iron shoes on stone. Sera pressed flat against the cliff wall and pulled Wendell down beside her, and together they watched through a gap in the pines as six riders picked their way along the valley road far beneath, torches burning yellow-green in the damp.

Not crown-issue torches. Witch-fire. The kind that burned without heat and showed things that hid.

One rider raised a hand, and the column stopped. Even at this distance Sera could see him lift something to his face — a lens, a glass, some instrument — and turn slowly, scanning the cliffs.

The fragment against Sera's ribs grew warm. Then warmer. Then it *pulled*, a gentle insistent tug toward the riders below, like a dog hearing its name called by the wrong person.

"He's found a way to listen to it," she breathed. "Somehow. He knows it's here."

Wendell's hand closed over hers where it pressed the stone flat. Their palm was hot; it was always hot now, ever since the attic, ever since the journal, ever since the night the gate shuddered hard enough to shake dust from the rafters. Under her fingers, Sera felt the answering pulse in Wendell's skin, twin rhythms falling into step.

"Wendell." Her voice cracked on the name. "If he can hear the stone, he can hear *you*. We can't outrun that. We have to go south, tonight, off this road entirely—"

"Look at them," Wendell whispered. Not the riders. Beyond them. Down-valley, where the fog thinned for a moment, Sera saw it: the far dark line of the Thornfell ridge, and atop it, faint as breath on glass, a glow. Not firelight. Something older. Something like the warmth in the gate-stones, visible now from miles away because whatever slept behind the gate was pressing harder against its seams.

"That's three days' ride from us," Wendell said. "And it's glowing. Tell me again about the ship."

* * *

They argued all through the third dawn, in whispers, huddled under an overhang while witch-fire torches moved along the valley floor like slow green stars.

"You could come with me," Sera said finally, and heard how the words changed shape leaving her mouth — how they stopped being strategy and became something naked. "You. Not the stone. Not the duty. You. Wendell. Come south with me and let the mountain keep its own secrets for once."

Wendell was silent so long that Sera thought they hadn't heard. Then: "You know what's behind that gate?"

"I know what you've told me. Wolves. Spirits. Your grandmother's bargain."

"I know what's behind that gate," Wendell repeated, "because I've started *remembering* it. Not reading it in the journal — remembering. Like it happened to me. There are things back there, Sera, that were old when the mountains were young, and the only thing between them and every village in the valley is a dying ward and a keeper who doesn't understand her own blood." A pause. "*Their* own blood. And you want me on a boat."

"Yes," Sera said. "Because dead keepers keep nothing."

Something moved through Wendell's face then — fast, gone, but Sera caught it, and it frightened her more than the riders below. It was agreement. It was the look of someone who has almost been argued out of the thing holding them upright.

Then Wendell looked down at the valley, at the green stars of witch-fire, and the look sealed over.

"Rell will reach Thornfell in two days whether I'm there or not," they said. "If I'm not, he finds an unguarded gate and a village full of people who've known me my whole life. If I am—" They didn't finish it. They didn't have to.

"And what about me?" Sera heard her own voice rise, and couldn't stop it. "What am I supposed to do, Wendell? I stole that stone out of the crown's own vault. I killed— I left people behind to get it here. My whole life is a burned bridge, and the one thing I built on the far side of it was—" She stopped. Swallowed. Started smaller. "Don't make me watch you walk into that."

Wendell reached out and took her hand. Their grip was warm, fever-warm, gate-warm, and steady as bedrock.

"Then don't watch," they said softly. "Walk beside me. One more day. Help me get the gate through what's coming — and if there's a way after, I swear to you on grandmother's grave, I'll find the ship with you myself."

It wasn't a promise anyone could keep. They both knew it. Sera held on anyway, and above them the fog began to thin toward morning, and far below, the green torches turned as one — all six riders, all at once — and began to climb.

Toward the high pass. Toward Thornfell.

Toward them.

Wendell drew her back from the ledge, into the pines, into the shadows. "Three days to Thornfell," they murmured, and there was something in their voice Sera had never heard before — not fear, exactly. Recognition. As if they were hearing footsteps approach a door they had always known would knock.

Behind them, deep in the folds of the fog where no rider could possibly stand, a single pair of eyes opened — amber, patient, and terribly human — and watched them go.
