## 9. The Smuggler's Toll

The pass was called the Hollow Tooth, and it did not appear on any map that honest people used.

Wendell had known of it since childhood — one of Grandmother Amberdale's half-drunken stories, told over a kitchen table while snow piled against the windows. *There are more roads than the crown knows, little wren. The smugglers keep them, and smugglers keep their bargains better than kings do.* Wendell had thought it a fable then, another of the old woman's embellishments. Now they walked its entrance with Sera at their shoulder and Oscar's presence like a cold draft at their back, and understood that most of Grandmother's stories had been warnings wearing festival clothes.

The Hollow Tooth opened in the mountainside where two ridges met like jaws. From the valley below it looked like nothing — a shadow, a fold of rock, a place where the pines grew crooked and refused to grow tall. But up close there was a path, narrow and switchbacked, climbing into a cleft that swallowed the daylight behind them step by step.

Sera's wound had closed to an angry pink seam, though she still favored her left side and refused, with a flat look, any discussion of resting. She carried the seal-fragment wrapped in wool against her chest, and Wendell had noticed she no longer let it out of arm's reach. Not even to sleep.

"You're limping," Wendell said.

"I'm walking."

"You're walking badly."

"Then walk ahead," Sera said, "and don't watch." But when Wendell slowed anyway — not obviously, just enough — she said nothing about it, which was its own kind of thanks.

Above them, somewhere in the grey morning air, the wolf was pacing. Wendell could feel him the way one feels weather coming: a pressure behind the eyes, a wrongness in the birdsong. He had not spoken since they'd left the burned gatehouse behind. He simply followed, a shadow with too much weight to it, and every time Wendell glanced back expecting empty trail they found instead a shape at the edge of vision, grey as slate, watching with those unbearable human eyes.

*They will smell you,* he had said, three nights ago, crouched at the edge of their fire with his muzzle lifted toward the south. *The gift is in your blood now, keeper. It has a scent. The crown's hounds are bred for it.*

Wendell hadn't asked what he meant by *now*. Some questions were doors best left shut until you had no choice but to open them.

The toll-house sat halfway up the pass, wedged into the cleft like a stone swallowed sideways. It was less a building than an argument between architecture and mountain — walls of dry-stacked granite, a roof of split shingle weighted down with boulders, a door of blackened oak with no lock that Wendell could see. Smoke rose from a chimney hole, thin and greasy. A lantern hung beside the door, unlit, though the day was dim enough to want it.

A man sat outside on a stool, mending a net of some kind, though they were a full day's climb from any water that would hold fish. He was old in the way of mountain things — weathered brown, spare, with hands like roots and eyes the pale grey of quartz. He looked up as they approached, and his gaze went past Wendell entirely, found Sera, measured her, dismissed her, and came back to Wendell with the particular attention of a man pricing livestock.

"Road's closed," he said.

"It wasn't closed last month," Wendell said. "I'm told the Hollow Tooth doesn't close for weather or war."

"The Hollow Tooth closes for whoever I say it closes for." He set down the net. His hands were very still. "You're not smugglers. Smugglers don't come up the front path looking like pilgrims. Who told you this road existed?"

"My grandmother."

Something flickered across the quartz-grey eyes. "Name."

"Amberdale."

The man was quiet a long moment. Somewhere above them, wind moved through the pass with a sound almost like breathing. When he spoke again, his voice had changed — not warmer, exactly, but older. "Old Maren Amberdale. She came through here twice a year for twenty years, carrying boxes nobody was allowed to ask about. Paid her toll in salt and silver and never once in blood." He stood, joints cracking like green wood. "You'll be the grandchild, then. The one who keeps the gate."

Word traveled strangely in the high country. Wendell felt Sera go tense beside them. "You know about the gate."

"I know about everything that happens on my mountain." He jerked his chin toward the door. "I'm called Brannoc. Toll is ten silver for the pair of you, safe passage to the southern mouth, no questions asked of me and none answered by me. That was the old bargain. It stands, if you can pay."

"We can pay," Sera said, and reached into her coat.

But Brannoc wasn't looking at her anymore. He was looking past them both, up the trail, at the place where the grey shape stood at the tree line with the morning fog moving through it. The old man's face did something complicated — fear and recognition wrestling to a draw.

"That's no dog," he said quietly.

"No," Wendell said. "It isn't."

"He comes with you, the price doubles. Things like that unsettle the road. Things like that *attract attention*." Brannoc spat to the side, a ritual gesture, old as the stones. "Twenty silver."

Sera counted coins into his palm without argument. Wendell watched the silver disappear into the folds of the old man's glove and thought about how quickly the world had learned to charge extra for anything touched by the old magic. Even here. Even among smugglers.

Brannoc led them inside.

* * *

The interior of the toll-house was one long room carved partly from the living rock, lit by oil lamps and a hearth that burned something that wasn't quite wood — peat, maybe, or dried dung, or something older. Three other travelers sat around a table near the far wall, playing dice with the concentrated silence of people who did not want to be remembered. A woman turned a spit over the fire. A boy — no older than fourteen, all elbows and wary eyes — stacked firewood near the door and pretended very hard not to look at anyone.

Brannoc pointed them to a bench near the wall. "Food's included. Beds are extra. You move on at first light — the middle stretch of the pass floods with meltwater after noon, and I don't ferry corpses for free."

He brought barley soup and dark bread, and for a while there was only the sound of eating, and the crackle of the strange fire, and the dice clicking like insect shells across the far table. Sera ate mechanically, eyes half-closed, and Wendell knew she was fighting sleep and losing. Three days of hard travel on a healing wound. She'd earned unconsciousness.

Wendell couldn't rest. They sat with their back to the wall and watched the room, and the room watched them back in the small glances people think are invisible.

The dice players had stopped pretending to play. One of them — a heavyset man with a beard gone to iron and a scar splitting one eyebrow — kept letting his gaze slide over and snag on Wendell's hands. On the way Wendell's fingers rested against the tabletop. On the faint warmth that Wendell could no longer fully suppress, the way the air around them seemed very slightly thicker, very slightly brighter, like heat-shimmer in winter.

Oscar had been right. The gift had a scent now, and Wendell was bleeding it into the air of this room with every breath.

*You're leaking,* the wolf had told them once, disgusted, on the second night. *Your grandmother could sit in a church full of witch-hunters and read them all to sleep. You glow like a signal fire.*

*I didn't ask for this.*

*No one asks. That's what makes it a gift and not a wage.* And then he'd laughed, low and terrible, at his own joke.

Now the scarred man leaned over to the woman turning the spit and murmured something. She glanced at Wendell. So did the boy with the firewood, quick and frightened, before dropping his eyes to his work.

Wendell looked down at their own hands and made themselves breathe slowly. Think of the gate, they told themselves. Think of the stones under your palms at dawn, the hum you've spent your whole life pretending was imagination. Contain it. Fold it small. Be a person, only a person, tired and ordinary and traveling south because the roads were bad.

But the thing inside them had been waking for weeks now, fed on the fragment's proximity and the gate's breaking and whatever inheritance was singing in their blood, and it did not fold small on command. It pressed outward like spring sap. Their fingertips left faint warmth on the wooden bench. The lamp nearest them burned a shade too steadily, a shade too bright.

And then the boy dropped an armload of firewood.

It shouldn't have mattered. A clumsy child, a scattered pile of logs — the kind of small disaster that happened in every kitchen in the world. But one log rolled toward the hearth, toward the woman at the spit, and she stepped back to avoid it and her sleeve swung through the flame.

She screamed.

It caught fast — grease and wool and panic, fire climbing her arm while she slapped at it with her other hand and knocked the spit sideways, and then the whole corner of the room was in motion, everyone shouting, someone throwing water that missed, and the flames leapt from her sleeve to the hanging drying herbs to the dry-stacked timber of the wall itself, and the toll-house — old, dry, built of a century of resinous pine — began to burn.

Later, Wendell would understand that it took perhaps forty heartbeats from the scream to the moment they acted. In the moment itself, there was no time at all. There was only the fire, roaring now up the wall timbers, and the woman on her knees, and the boy frozen with a log still in his hand, and Sera lurching awake with her hand going for a knife she'd left in her boot —

— and Wendell moved.

They didn't decide. That was the terrible truth they would carry afterward: there was no decision, no weighing of costs, none of the careful arithmetic of secrecy they had lived by for years. The gift rose in them like water finding a breach, and Wendell *let* it, threw their hands out toward the burning wall, and *pulled*.

The fire came to them.

That was the only way to describe it, though description failed. The flames bent — physically bent, streaming sideways off the wall like banners in a gale, streaming across the room in rivers of orange light, and poured themselves into Wendell's outstretched hands. Heat without pain. Light without burning. They stood in the center of the toll-house holding a sphere of the woman's fire cupped between their palms like a captured sun, and every person in the room was staring at them, and the silence was absolute.

Wendell carried the fire to the hearth and let it sink home into the coals, where it belonged, tame again. Then they turned around.

The woman was cradling her scorched arm, staring. The dice players were on their feet, backs against the far wall. The boy had dropped to the floor and was looking up at Wendell the way children look at things in stories — wonder and terror in equal measure, neither winning.

Sera had gone white. Not at the fire. At Wendell.

"Well," Brannoc said, from the doorway, in a voice like gravel settling. "That's a new one."

* * *

They did not sleep in the toll-house.

Brannoc took them out the back, up a goat track to a hollow beneath an overhang of granite where the smugglers sometimes cached goods, and gave them blankets and a flask of something that burned worse than the fire had. "You can't stay under my roof tonight," he said, not unkindly. "Not after that. What men see, men talk about. But the pass is yours at first light, and the toll stands paid. Old Maren's grandchild gets that much, whatever else is true."

"And after?" Wendell asked. "After we're through your pass?"

Brannoc looked at them for a long time. The quartz eyes held no malice. That was somehow worse.

"After, everyone tells what they saw," he said. "I can't stop that. I wouldn't. A man who controls what travelers say controls the road, and the road belongs to itself." He paused. "But I'll tell you who'll listen fastest. There's a fur-trader wintering at the southern mouth — calls himself Doss. Bought his way into the trade two seasons back, too much money, too many questions. Crown coin, if you ask me, though I've got no proof and won't swear to it. He'll hear about you before you're a mile down the mountain."

Sera cursed softly. Wendell felt the words land like stones dropped into still water, ripples spreading in every direction, impossible to call back.

"Thank you," Wendell said. "For telling us."

"Don't thank me. I'm just an honest traitor. It's the dishonest ones you need to worry about." Brannoc gathered his blankets. At the edge of the overhang he stopped, silhouetted against the stars. "Your grandmother paid her tolls in salt and silver and never once in blood. Remember that, keeper. She knew what you're learning tonight — that the gift always takes its fee eventually. Tonight it cost you your shadow. Tomorrow it may cost more." He tapped his own chest, over the heart. "Guard what's in there. It's the only vault that matters."

Then he was gone down the track, and Wendell sat in the cold under the granite with Sera's shoulder warm against theirs, and looked at their own hands in the starlight.

Ordinary hands. Calloused from gate-work, scarred from a childhood of stone and rope. Hands that had held fire tonight like it was water.

"The boy," Sera said quietly. "The one with the firewood. He looked at you like—" She stopped. Started again. "People will tell stories about tonight. Some of them will be kind. Most of them won't."

"I know."

"Do you? Because I don't think you do, Wendell. I think you've spent your whole life hiding behind a gate, and you think hiding is the same as being safe. It isn't. Hiding just means they find you later, and by then they've had time to get scared, and scared men with irons—" She broke off. Her hand found the lump of the seal-fragment under her coat, unconsciously, the way one touches a wound.

"Rell already knew where we were," Wendell said. "He burned the gatehouse. One more story in one more tavern changes nothing."

"Everything changes. Every hour, everything." Sera pulled her knees up. In the dark her voice came smaller. "When you caught the fire — for half a heartbeat I was afraid of you. Me. After everything." She laughed, short and bitter. "So imagine what strangers feel. Imagine what the crown feels. We're not running from soldiers anymore, Wendell. We're running from a story, and stories run faster than horses."

Above them, at the lip of the overhang, the wolf sat silhouetted against the stars, and Wendell realized he had been there for some time, listening to everything.

*She's right,* Oscar said, and his voice in their skull was gentler than it had ever been, which frightened them more than cruelty would have. *The price is paid now, keeper. There's no unpaying it. The question — the only question left — is whether you'll spend what remains buying your life, or buying something worth a life.*

Wendell lay back on the stone and did not answer, and did not sleep, and far to the north — though they could not have known it — a rider in crown grey took a message from a fur-trader's hand and turned his horse toward the pass, riding through the dark with the certainty of a man who believed the world was simple, and that it was his to put in order.
