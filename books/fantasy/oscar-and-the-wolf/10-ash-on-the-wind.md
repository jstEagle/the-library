## 10. Ash on the Wind

We smelled it before we saw it.

Sera had been counting the switchbacks out of the smuggler's pass — forty-one of them since dawn, each one a small argument with her own knees — when the wind turned and brought the smell down the mountain to meet them. Woodsmoke, yes, that was common enough in autumn. But under it something sweeter and fouler: thatch burning, tallow, wool. The smell of a home dying.

She stopped on the trail and Wendell walked two more paces before they noticed she wasn't beside them anymore.

"What," Wendell said. Not a question so much as a refusal of one.

"You smell it too." Sera's voice came out flat. She had learned long ago, in another life, in vaults and corridors where fear got people killed, that flat was the only safe register. "Don't pretend you don't."

Wendell stood very still against the grey sky. Wind pulled at their coat, at the scarf Sera had made them wrap twice around their throat because they ran hot now, ever since the fragment had begun answering their touch. Their face did the thing it always did when the world pressed in — went smooth, closed, a door pulled shut from the inside. But their hands, hanging loose at their sides, were trembling.

"It could be anything," Wendell said. "A hearth fire gone wrong. A chimney. It happens."

"Thornfell has six houses," Sera said. "You told me. Six houses and a gatehouse, and you know every chimney in the valley by name."

They didn't answer. They started walking again, faster than the trail allowed, stones skidding under their boots, and Sera had to jog to catch up, and the smell grew thicker with every step until she was breathing it through her mouth like a sickness.

* * *

They came over the shoulder of the hill above the valley as the light was going copper and long, and Sera saw it all at once, the way you see a wound all at once — no detail first, just the whole terrible shape of it.

The gatehouse was gone.

Not burned-down, not damaged. *Gone.* The roof had fallen in and burned to skeletons of black timber; the walls stood half their height, cracked and smoke-stained, breathing thin ribbons of grey into the evening air. The old thorn hedge that had grown wild along the gate path for a hundred years — Wendell had spoken of it once, offhandedly, the way you speak of a friend — was charred to wire. And the gate itself, the great stone archway with its carved wards, its ancient worn steps, stood naked in the middle of the ruin like a tooth in a broken jaw.

The stones of the arch were still warm. Sera knew it without touching them. She could feel it from here, a low wrong heat, the way you can feel a fever across a room. The fragment in the pouch at her hip had woken the moment they crested the hill. It hummed against her side through leather and linen, a sound more felt than heard, like a struck bell that would not finish ringing.

Beside her, Wendell made a sound.

It was not a cry. It was smaller than a cry and worse — a single exhaled note, almost conversational, the sound a person makes when they put their hand down on something they expected to be solid. Then they were moving, down the slope, stumbling, running, and Sera ran after them because there was nothing else in the world to do.

The yard was ash and wreckage. A wheelbarrow lay on its side, its handles burned to points. Somewhere under the rubble a kettle had survived, absurdly whole, sitting upright in the black like an egg in a ruined nest. The well rope was cut. The door of the little shrine-niche by the gate — Wendell had never explained it, and Sera had never asked — hung open and empty.

And there, among the ruins, arranged with terrible neatness: soldiers.

Six of them, maybe seven, in crown grey, standing in a loose crescent between Wendell and the gate. They had let Wendell run right up to the edge of them before moving, closing the crescent with the unhurried ease of men who had done this many times and enjoyed the geometry of it. Sera skidded to a stop three strides behind Wendell, her hand finding the knife at her back without her telling it to.

At the center of the crescent, seated on an overturned stone trough as though he had been waiting there for hours — he had, she realized; the cold tea beside him said hours — sat a man in a black half-cloak with the silver chain of office across his chest.

Captain Rell.

She had seen him once before, from a distance, in the market at Crossroads, and even at a distance he had been memorable the way a blade is memorable. Up close he was worse, because up close he was ordinary. Grey eyes, brown hair going early grey at the temples, a face like a patient schoolmaster's. He looked at Wendell the way a scholar looks at a page he has finally been allowed to open.

"Wendell Amberdale," he said. His voice was mild. That was the worst thing about him, Sera thought later. The mildness. "I confess I hoped you'd come back tonight. I gave orders that the house be left standing until morning for exactly that reason." He glanced at the ruin around him with something that was almost regret. "My lieutenant is an enthusiastic man. I'll have words with him."

"You burned my house." Wendell's voice didn't shake. Sera marveled at that, even in the middle of everything — how steady they kept it, how much it must have cost. "There was nothing in it but books and my grandmother's chairs."

"There was a journal in it," Rell said. "Recovered intact, I'm pleased to say. Your grandmother wrote a fine clear hand. She describes the shrine above the falls in some detail." He folded his hands. "Which is why I'm afraid your evening walk ends here."

Sera's blood went cold and then colder. The shrine. The second fragment. He had read the pages they'd risked everything to find, and he was ahead of them, had perhaps always been ahead of them, and the trap hadn't snapped shut at the shrine the way she'd feared — it had snapped shut here, at the ashes, because Rell understood that people go home.

Even when home is a cinder. Especially then.

* * *

"Irons," Rell said, and one of the soldiers stepped forward carrying a case, and opened it, and Sera saw what was inside and her stomach dropped through the floor of the world.

Manacles. But not iron alone — the metal was worked with channels, grooves filled with something dark that drank the last of the light, and set into each cuff a sliver of pale stone that Sera recognized with a lurch. Seal-stone. Crown-cut, crown-hoarded, stolen from the gates themselves and ground down like spice to season a cage.

"They're forged to dampen," Rell said, conversationally, watching Wendell's face. "Old craft. We've had them a long time, waiting for someone worth using them on. You should feel honored. Most of the gifted we've taken this year only rated rope."

One of the soldiers laughed. It was a small sound, ugly in the vast quiet of the burned yard, and it broke something loose in Sera.

Because here was the arithmetic, and she could do it fast, faster than grief, faster than fear — she'd been doing this kind of arithmetic since she was twelve years old in the crown's vaults, weighing exits against odds. Seven soldiers, armed. Two of them, one knife, one fragment of a seal-stone that answered Wendell's touch and nobody else's. Wendell's gift was real and growing — Sera had watched them coax warmth out of dead coals, had watched the ward-stones sing under their palms — but it was wild and untrained and it sputtered under pressure, and these irons were built to smother whatever it became.

If Wendell fought, Wendell lost. If Wendell lost wearing those irons, the gift went dark forever, and the gate behind them — already cracked, already humming its broken-bell hum — would stand unkept with its keeper caged.

And if Wendell surrendered quietly, if they let the irons close—

Then Sera could run. The slope behind her, the dark coming on, the smuggler's roads she'd memorized. One runner in falling dark might make it. Might carry the fragment south, or better, carry it up — to the shrine, to the second piece, to the only future in which any of this meant anything.

She was still doing the arithmetic when she realized Wendell was doing it too.

She saw it happen. Saw their eyes flick — to the soldiers, to the irons, to her, to the treeline behind her — quick as a card sharp's, and then come back to rest on her face, and soften, and harden, both at once, in a way that terrified her more than the irons did.

"No," Sera said aloud.

Nobody had proposed anything yet. That was the horror of it. Nothing had been said, and she already knew, and Wendell already knew she knew.

"Sera—" Wendell began.

"Don't." Her voice cracked on it. She hated the crack. "Don't you dare do the noble thing, don't you *dare*, I didn't bleed across half this mountain to watch you—"

"The journal names the shrine," Wendell said, low, urgent, pitched only for her beneath Rell's patient silence. "He has the pages but he doesn't have the fragment, and he doesn't have *you*. You know the roads. You know what the stone sounds like when it's near its other half — you felt it today, don't lie, you felt it pull." Their eyes were bright and wet and absolutely steady. "I'm the key, Sera. He needs me alive. He won't hurt me. But if he takes us both, it's over — everything Grandmother tried to protect, everyone in the valleys below, all of it. Someone has to carry this."

"I'm not leaving you." It came out of her like a stone flung. "That's not — we agreed, on the pass, we *agreed*—"

"We agreed we'd decide together when the moment came." Wendell's mouth did something that was almost a smile and mostly a wound. "This is me deciding."

Behind them, Rell shifted on his trough, and Sera saw the exact moment he understood what was happening in front of him — saw the mildness in his face sharpen by a single degree, the hunter recognizing the snare he hadn't even needed to build. He said nothing. He let it happen. That was his cruelty: he simply waited, and time did his work for him.

"Take my hand," Wendell whispered.

"What?"

"Take my hand. Now. While they think we're arguing." And when she did — when her fingers found theirs, cold and ash-grimed and shaking after all — Wendell pressed something into her palm, small and warm and humming: a splinter of the fragment, broken off in the night on the pass, a thing they had done secretly and never mentioned, planning for this, planning for *her*, for weeks maybe, for longer than Sera wanted to know.

"South road forks at the burnt pine," Wendell breathed, fast, barely lips moving. "Left fork goes high. The stone will get louder near the falls — follow the loudness. Don't trust anyone who smiles first. And Sera—"

Their thumb pressed once, hard, against her knuckle. A whole sentence in a single pressure. Words they weren't going to say, not here, not in front of the irons and the ash and the mild-eyed man who collected people like her for a living. Words that would keep, or wouldn't, depending on whether there was a world left to say them in.

"If you do this," Sera said, and her voice was wrecked and she let it be wrecked, "if you put those things on, I swear to every god I don't believe in, I will come back for you. I don't care what comes out of that gate. I don't care what I have to become. Do you understand me?"

Something moved through Wendell's face then — terror, and tenderness, and underneath both a fierce, astonished joy, as if no one had ever promised them anything before, as if the promise itself were worth the price of it.

"I believe you," they said. "Now run."

* * *

She ran.

It was the hardest thing she had ever done, harder than the theft, harder than the wounded flight down the mountain to a stranger's door, harder than any of it, because every instinct she owned screamed to turn around, to fight, to die usefully rather than live by abandoning. She ran down the slope into the dark with the splinter of seal-stone clenched in her fist, humming against her skin like a second heartbeat, and behind her she heard it all with horrible clarity:

Rell rising from his trough, unhurried. The mild voice saying, "Wendell Amberdale, by authority of the crown, surrender the gift." The clink of the case opening. And Wendell's voice, steady as a hearthstone, steady as the gate itself:

"I'll come willingly. Let her go. She's nothing — a thief I took pity on. She ran that way; you'll never catch her in the dark."

*A thief I took pity on.* Lying about her, diminishing her, making herself small enough to be worthless — spending their last free breath buying her a head start with a lie.

Sera ran faster. Tears tore sideways off her face in the wind and she did not wipe them. Behind her, fainter now, swallowed by distance and dark and the roaring in her own ears, she heard the snap of the manacles closing, and felt — she would have sworn it to her dying day — the moment the irons took hold. The hum of the splinter in her fist stuttered, dimmed, like a voice calling from farther and farther away, like a lamp carried down a long corridor, like a hand letting go.

Then the mountains closed around her, and the dark took her in, and she ran north-by-east toward the falls and the shrine and the second fragment, alone, carrying half a broken stone and all of a promise.

Behind her, far below, the gate shuddered in its sleep — and somewhere in the deep of the mountain, something old turned over, and began, slowly, to wake.
