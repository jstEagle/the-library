## 11. Irons

The cage swayed with the motion of the wagon, and Wendell learned in the first hour that the irons did not merely dampen the gift — they starved it.

It was not silence. Wendell had imagined silence, a door closed on the warm hum of the gate-stones, and thought they could bear it. It was not that. It was hunger. The gift had lived in their blood since before they had words for it, a low warmth beneath the skin like a banked fire, and now something cold had been wrapped around wrists and throat and it *drank*. Every time Wendell reached inward for that warmth — out of habit, out of fear, out of some animal instinct to check that the hearth still burned — they found the coals greyed over, ashed, cold. And each time, the iron drank a little deeper, as if the reaching itself fed it.

They stopped reaching.

The wagon jolted through the mountain dark. Through the bars of the cage — oak and black iron, built for something larger than a person, which told Wendell things they did not want to know about what the crown expected to transport — they could see the torches of the outriders, six riders ahead, six behind, and beyond them the black shoulders of Thornfell receding. Somewhere behind them was the burned gatehouse. Somewhere behind them was Sera, running south with half a seal-stone against her heart, alive because Wendell had walked into the ruins with their hands open and their head bowed.

*Live*, Wendell had mouthed at her, across the smoking yard, while Rell's soldiers closed around them. *Run south. Don't look back.*

She had looked back. Of course she had. Wendell had made herself watch until the trees took her, because if they were going to burn for this they were at least going to know the burning bought something.

Now the night was cold, the irons were colder, and Wendell sat in the dark of the cage and counted the ways they had failed.

* * *

Rell came to them at dawn, when the convoy halted in a hollow where a stream cut through grey rock. He did not come with theatrics. That was the worst of it, Wendell decided later — no torchlight raked across the face, no circle of leering soldiers. He simply unlocked the cage himself, waved off the guards, and sat on an overturned supply crate across from Wendell as though they were two travelers sharing a fire.

He set down a tin cup of water within Wendell's reach. They did not touch it.

"You're dehydrated," he said. "The irons do that. I'd prefer you functional."

"Functional for what?"

"For questions." Captain Rell was not old — forty perhaps, maybe less, his hair going early grey at the temples in a way that suggested discipline rather than age. His face was the kind that gave away nothing because it had been trained to give away nothing, every feature held in a kind of patient readiness. He wore no insignia of rank beyond a plain steel pin, and his armor was unadorned, and everything about him said: *I am a tool, and tools are honest.* "I'll ask. You'll answer, or you won't. But you should understand one thing before we begin, so that your choices are informed ones." He folded his hands. "I am not cruel. Cruelty is inefficiency. What I am is thorough, and thoroughness, applied to a person over enough hours, arrives at the same place cruelty would have — only more reliably, and with less mess."

"Comforting."

"It isn't meant to comfort. It's meant to inform." He studied them for a moment. "Wendell Amberdale. Gatekeeper of Thornfell. Grandchild of Maren Amberdale, who held the same post for thirty-one years and whose name appears in crown records more often than you might expect, given how quietly she lived." A pause, delicate as a blade laid flat on a table. "You didn't know that last part, did you."

Wendell kept their face still. It cost something. The irons made everything cost something; even a steady expression felt like lifting stones.

"There's a great deal you don't know," Rell said. "Which is why I'm going to skip the part where I threaten you, and go directly to the part where I tell you the truth. Truth loosens people faster than fear. Fear makes them clench." He leaned forward slightly. "So: the crown knows about the wolf-gates. Has known for eleven years. There is a department — you will never learn its name — that has spent those eleven years doing nothing but reading. Old contracts. Keeper's journals. Sealed correspondence between your grandmother and persons in the capital who are also no longer alive. We know there are seven gates. We know five are dead or dormant. We know Thornfell is the last living ward in the northern range." His eyes were pale grey, and utterly without malice, which somehow made them worse. "And we know the seals are failing. Not because of anything you did. Because they were always going to fail. Everything fails. The question is only what stands ready when it does."

"The crown's answer," Wendell said, "is to hunt children out of valley markets."

Something moved at the corner of Rell's mouth — not quite a wince, not quite agreement. "The proclamation was not my initiative. And the boy taken at the crossroads — Tam Ilves, fourteen, hedge-gift, can make milk keep three days past spoiling — was not taken by my order. He was taken by a provincial magistrate following a bounty schedule written by men who have never seen a gate." He said it flatly, factually, and then, with what sounded almost like reluctance: "I have filed objections. They were noted."

"Noted," Wendell repeated. "A boy in chains, and your objection was *noted*."

"Yes." He did not flinch from it. That was the terrible thing about him; he did not look away from any of it. "I serve the crown, Wendell. The crown serves the realm. When the two conflict, I grieve briefly and choose the realm. You may hate me for it. Hate is not inefficient either. It keeps prisoners awake and talking." He unfolded his hands. "Now. The stone."

Wendell said nothing.

"The fragment your companion carried. Recovered from our own vaults in Correnholt four months ago by a thief we have been unable to identify — though I confess I formed a theory the moment I saw her hands." He glanced, clinically, at Wendell's bound wrists. "The vault piece is one half of the Thornfell seal. The other half, per the keeper's records, remained at the gate itself — hidden, we believe, at the shrine above the high pass. Your grandmother split it deliberately. Do you know why a keeper splits her own seal?"

"Because she didn't trust anyone," Wendell said. "Including herself."

"Incorrect." Rell's voice did not change at all. "Because a whole seal can be broken by force. Two halves must be *joined willingly*, by blood of the binding line, in the presence of the gate. She wasn't hiding it from thieves, Wendell. She was building a lock that required a key with a heartbeat. Which means—" and here he paused, and for the first time something almost human crossed his face, something that might have been distaste, "—which means the crown cannot use the stone without you. Or someone like you."

The cold of the irons seemed suddenly very specific, very deliberate. Wendell looked down at their own wrists, at the black bands biting into the skin, and understood for the first time that these were not restraints. They were packaging.

"What does the crown want with the gate?" they asked. Their voice came out low. "You've spent eleven years reading. You know what's behind it. You've read what happened the *first* time—"

"I have read everything," Rell said. "Every account. The war of the Long Winter. The villages that stopped appearing on tax rolls. The thing the old chronicles call the Hunger Under the Mountain, and its children, and what a single opened gate did to the Karst Valley in the year the river ran red." He recited it like inventory. "Yes. I know precisely what sleeps behind Thornfell. And I am going to tell you what my superiors intend to do about it, because I want to watch your face when you understand, and because I find that people argue more honestly once they've stopped being able to pretend they misheard me."

He stood. Walked to the edge of the hollow, where the stream ran thin and loud over grey stones, and spoke with his back half turned, like a man dictating a letter he was ashamed of.

"The war colleges concluded, eight years ago, that the wolf-spirits cannot be fought conventionally. Steel passes through them. Fire annoys them. Prayer, we tested — there is a chapel in Correnholt that no longer has a congregation. So the conclusion was reached that if the weapon cannot be resisted, it should be *owned*." He turned back. "There is a border dispute in the east. There will be others. The crown intends to open Thornfell — under controlled conditions, with the seal-stone, with a compliant member of the binding line — and to direct what comes out of it at the crown's enemies. Wolves that cannot be killed, pointed at armies that can. The strategists call it the Northern Solution. They are very proud of it."

The world went very quiet and very far away.

Wendell heard the stream. Heard a horse stamp somewhere up the slope. Heard their own breath, thin and mechanical inside the cage of their ribs. And underneath all of it, faint as a pulse through floorboards, they felt — or dreamed they felt, or *ached* to feel — the far-off warmth of the gate, miles north and buried in ash, holding. Holding. Holding.

"They're going to let it *out*," Wendell said. The words barely sounded like theirs. "On purpose. They're going to open the door that killed the Karst Valley, and point it at people, and call it a *solution*."

"Yes."

"And you're telling me this because—"

"Because you are the key," Rell said, "and keys work better when they understand what they unlock. Because I have watched you since the crossroads, and you strike me as a person who does not break but *turns*, like water finding stone, and I would rather aim that than fight it. And because—" he hesitated, and Wendell would spend a long time afterward wondering whether the hesitation was real, "—because there is a version of these events in which everyone in this convoy dies, and I have begun, recently, to think about which versions of events I would prefer to survive."

"That sounds like treason, Captain."

"It sounds like arithmetic." He crouched again, level with Wendell, and his voice dropped to something almost private. "Here is what happens next. In three days we reach Correnholt. There is a cell below the archive with your name already carved into the roster. There are men there who will spend years learning how to make a binding-line gift compliant. I have read their methods. I would rather not deliver you to them." A beat. "Unless you give me a reason to believe you'll open the gate yourself, on their terms, in which case I will hand you over with a clear conscience and sleep soundly. That is the bargain I'm offering, Wendell Amberdale. Not mercy. Clarity. Choose which master you'd rather serve — theirs, or the mountain's — because in three days, one of them will have you."

He rose, collected the untouched cup of water, and left it anyway, balanced on the crate. At the edge of the hollow he paused.

"For what it's worth," he said, without turning around, "your grandmother refused them too. For thirty-one years. Read her file sometime, if you get the chance." Then he was gone up the slope, and the guards closed in, and the cage door shut with a sound like a full stop.

* * *

They traveled through the day. Wendell sat in the iron dark and let the truth turn over and over, a stone in a tumbler, wearing its edges smooth.

*They want to open it.* Not fail to hold it — that had been the nightmare Wendell had carried all their life, the dread under every quiet morning at the gate, the reason they checked the wards twice and slept badly. All those years of vigilance, of loneliness worn like armor, of touching the warm stones and whispering *hold, hold, hold* — and the whole time, the true danger had never been the door swinging loose. It had been men in clean armor coming to swing it wide on purpose, and calling the slaughter strategy.

Grief came first. Grief for Tam Ilves, fourteen, whose gift was keeping milk sweet. Grief for the valleys that would be the proving grounds, the fields where they'd test the wolves' appetite. Grief for their grandmother, who had died alone in this same house with this same secret, writing her warnings into a journal no one would read for twenty years, fighting a war no one knew she was fighting.

Then the grief did something Wendell had never felt it do before.

It caught fire.

It started small — a heat behind the breastbone that had nothing to do with the gift, that the irons couldn't touch because it wasn't magic at all, it was older and simpler than magic. It was the heat of a person realizing they have been polite for too long. Wendell sat in the swaying dark and thought of Rell's grey eyes saying *I am not cruel* while the boy Tam wept in chains. Thought of strategists in warm rooms, proud of their *Northern Solution*, drawing arrows on maps toward villages full of people who had never done anything but live downwind of a mountain. Thought of Sera's face in the burning yard, looking back, always looking back.

The fury settled into them like a second skeleton.

And with it — faint, so faint they might have invented it — something else. Deep beneath the dead ash of the damped gift, at a depth the irons did not seem to reach, a warmth stirred. Not the surface warmth the bands drank from. Something lower. Older. The warmth of the stones themselves, maybe, or the memory of them, carried in blood the way a river carries a mountain it has never touched.

*Hold,* Wendell whispered into the dark of the cage, to no one, to the mountain behind them, to whatever of their grandmother still listened. *Not yet.*

Because here was the thing Rell had not considered, in all his careful arithmetic: he had told Wendell the truth. And the truth was a map. Three days to Correnholt. A shrine above the high pass where the other half of the seal waited. A grandmother's journal, pages of which Rell's men had surely copied — but pages Wendell had *lived*, had read by lamplight with their own hands, had memorized in the way you memorize a voice.

And somewhere south, moving fast, angry, and armed with half a stone and a guilty conscience, was a thief who had already broken into one crown vault.

Wendell closed their eyes. Behind the lids, in the iron dark, they began — patiently, furiously, line by line — to plan.

That night, as the convoy camped in the shadow of the pass, a sentry swore he heard singing from the prisoner's cage: a low, wordless thing, like wind through a keyhole. By morning the sentry would report it, and Captain Rell would stand outside the cage for a long moment, listening, and for the first time in his career feel something adjacent to doubt.

And far to the south, at the tree line where the burned road met the green, a figure stood watching the convoy's fires — a woman with a stolen stone against her heart, and beside her, enormous in the dusk, a wolf with human eyes.

"*Three days*," Oscar said, tasting the wind. "We can beat them to the shrine. If she's still in there to save."

Sera looked at the distant fires, and her hands did not shake at all.
