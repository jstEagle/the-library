## 12. The Wolf in the Walls

Sera had stolen from the crown's vaults before. She had picked the lock on a warden's strongbox with a hairpin and a prayer, had lifted a keyring off a sleeping sergeant's belt, had once walked out of a garrison armory wearing a dead woman's cloak and nobody had stopped her. She knew how to be small, how to be shadow, how to be furniture.

None of it helped her now, because the thing walking beside her through the pine dark was not small, was not shadow, and could not be ignored no matter how hard she tried.

Oscar did not walk so much as arrive, over and over, at each new place along the trail. The pines bent their branches away from him as he passed — or perhaps they didn't; perhaps that was only wind, only her nerves painting omens onto ordinary night air. His paws made no sound on the frozen ground. His breath made no mist. When the moon slid out from behind cloud, his coat took the light and gave none of it back, silver-grey and depthless, like looking at snow in a dream. And his eyes — amber, patient, horribly human — watched her the way she imagined a scholar watched a page he had read a hundred times and still found interesting.

"Stop doing that," she said.

"Doing what?" His voice came from everywhere and nowhere, low as a cello string touched once and left to sing.

"Watching me."

"I am guiding you," Oscar said. "Guiding requires attention."

"You're staring."

The wolf made a sound that might have been laughter if wolves laughed — a huff of breath that stirred nothing. "Your grandmother's people stared at the sun too, little thief, and called it reverence. I am merely older-fashioned." He turned his head toward the valley below, where the prison convoy's campfires burned in a ragged orange line against the black of the river bend. "Now. Look with your eyes instead of your fear, and tell me what you see."

Sera crouched at the ridgeline and looked.

The convoy had made camp in the crook of the river where the Thornfell road flattened out before the long climb — forty soldiers at least, she judged, maybe more, though the firelight made counting an art of guesswork. Four wagons stood in a rough square, wheels chained, canvas stretched over iron frames. She could see the cage-wagon at the center: taller than the others, its bars thick as her wrist, and around it a ring of stakes hung with small iron bells that turned and clinked in the windless dark without any wind at all.

Dampers. Rell had told her about them once, in another life, when she'd been someone the crown trusted — iron forged around cold-forged salt, hung to smother the old gift in whatever it circled. She had helped design a better version of them, two years ago, in a workshop in the capital, back when she'd believed the crown's promises about who the gift belonged to.

Her stomach turned over, slow and sour.

"Wendell is in there," she said. "In the cage wagon. Behind the bells."

"Yes."

"And you can't get past them."

For the first time since she'd met the wolf-spirit, something in his stillness shifted — not hesitation exactly, but the memory of hesitation, like a scar aching in cold weather. "Iron remembers what it was made for," Oscar said. "Salt remembers what it was taken from. Together they make a door that is rude to guests like me." He said it lightly, but Sera heard the edge beneath, the way a blade keeps its edge even wrapped in velvet. "But doors are made to be opened by hands. And you, Sera of the vaults, have very clever hands."

She pulled her cloak tighter and did not answer right away, because there it was again — the thing that had been bothering her since the shrine, since the burning gatehouse, since the moment this creature had appeared out of empty air and pointed her down the mountain after Rell's convoy. He wanted Wendell free. That much was plain. What was not plain — what gnawed at her like a burr in a sock, invisible and constant — was *why*.

"You could have done this yourself," she said quietly. "Before. You pass through walls. You told me so. You walked into my dreams without knocking. Why do you need me at all?"

The wolf regarded her for a long moment. In the dark his eyes were the only fixed point, two coals that refused to gutter.

"Because the camp has eyes," he said at last, "and mine are known to some of them. Because the bells ring differently for spirits than for flesh, and a ringing bell brings forty soldiers with crossbows. And because—" a pause, delicate as a knife laid flat on a table "—because there are things in that camp I cannot carry. Keys. Words. A living person who can be seen and believed. You are flesh, Sera. Flesh opens flesh-locked things."

"That's not all of it."

"No," Oscar agreed, pleasantly terrible. "It isn't."

He began to move down the ridge, silent as falling snow, and Sera stood alone in the pine dark for three heartbeats, hating herself, knowing she would follow.

* * *

They went down through the trees the way water goes down — finding every crack, avoiding every stone. Oscar moved ahead and behind and beside her all at once; she kept catching movement at the corner of her eye and turning to find nothing, then turning back to find him there, closer than before. It was like being escorted by a rumor.

"The sentries rotate every quarter hour," he murmured. "The eastern picket is lazy and warm by his brazier. The western one is not lazy, so we will let him see nothing worth reporting. The horses are picketed south, away from the wagons — your grandmother's people taught men long ago that horses smell what sleeps behind gates, and panic." A beat. "Wise animals."

"My grandmother wasn't from here," Sera said automatically, then winced. She meant Wendell's grandmother. The words had gotten tangled somewhere between her mouth and her grief, which was itself tangled somewhere between guilt and terror, a knot she hadn't managed to undo in four days of hard travel.

"No," Oscar said softly. "She wasn't. But she learned our roads fast enough."

"What does that mean? What did Amberdale bargain with you, exactly? What did she—"

"Later." The word fell between them like a dropped stone. "Tonight we steal a prisoner. Tonight is for hands, not histories."

They reached the treeline at the edge of the camp and lay flat in the frost-bitten bracken, close enough now that Sera could hear the camp's small noises: the mutter of dice, a horse stamping, the endless soft clink-clink-clink of the damper bells turning on their stakes with no wind to move them. The sound got under her skin. It sounded like teeth.

"The guards change at moonset," Oscar breathed beside her ear — though his breath made no fog and stirred no hair. "That is your window. The cage wagon's lock is a crown pattern, third revision. You know it."

"I designed the fourth revision," Sera whispered. "Third's easier."

Something flickered across the wolf's face that she chose not to interpret. "And the bells?"

"Bells need the salt-ring intact. Break one stake, take one bell away whole, and the circle reads as broken — it stops dampening everything inside until it's remade." She had written that flaw herself, in a margin, in a report nobody had read. "I need ninety seconds and a reason for the watch commander not to look east."

"You will have your reason," Oscar said, and there was such quiet satisfaction in it that Sera felt a chill go up her spine, unrelated to the frost.

"What kind of reason?"

The wolf smiled. Wolves should not be able to smile. Every tooth in her body told her so, and yet there it was, curving up through the silver fur, ancient and amused and utterly without mercy.

"A wolf," he said, "in the walls."

* * *

It started, as far as she could tell from her hiding place behind the woodpile, with a horse screaming.

Not a whinny — a scream, high and tearing, the sound of an animal that has smelled death standing behind it. Then a second horse, and the picket lines dissolved into chaos, soldiers stumbling out of tents half-armored, torches flaring, someone shouting about wolves, about the treeline, about the mountains being cursed, and through it all Sera ran — low, fast, counting under her breath, ninety seconds, eighty-nine — straight for the easternmost bell-stake.

No one looked at her. That was the horror of it, and the mercy. To them she was a shape, a servant girl, a quartermaster's runner, anything forgettable. The world's oldest magic was not the old gift at all; it was being unremarkable at exactly the right moment.

Eighty seconds. She reached the stake. The bell hung at chest height, iron gone white with frost, and when her bare fingers closed around it the cold bit down to the bone and something else bit too — a wrongness, a greasy pressure, like touching a wound that wasn't hers. The salt inside the iron *hated* her. She could feel it wanting to find the gift in her blood, the thin thread of it she carried, everyone carried, and snuff it out like a wet thumb on a candle.

Seventy seconds. She lifted the bell off its hook whole, cradled it against her chest like a stolen infant, and felt the change ripple outward through the camp like a dropped stone through a pond. The pressure eased. Somewhere behind her ribs, a candle she hadn't known was lit burned a little brighter.

Then the shouting changed key.

"There!" A soldier, young, torch held high, pointing not at the panicked horses but at the treeline — at the enormous grey shape standing at the very edge of the firelight, motionless, watching them with amber eyes. "Wolf! There's a wolf the size of a *pony*!"

"Loose arrows! Loose!"

Sera didn't stay to see whether arrows could touch Oscar. She was already moving, bell hugged tight, slipping along the wagon square to the cage wagon's rear door while forty soldiers emptied their quivers at a treeline that was suddenly, impossibly, empty.

The lock was a crown pattern, third revision. Her picks were slivers of bone hidden in her boot seam. Sixty seconds. Forty. Her hands shook — not from cold, though the cold was vicious, but from what she'd feel when the door opened. From whether what was inside would be Wendell at all, or just a hollowed-out stranger wearing their face, damped and interrogated and broken by Captain Rell's patient cruelty.

Thirty seconds. The lock's tumblers clicked apart like knuckles cracking.

She hauled the door open and looked into the cage.

Wendell lay curled on the bare boards, wrists manacled in black iron, a chain running to a ring bolted into the wall. They were thinner than Sera remembered, grey-faced, lips cracked. Their eyes were closed. For one lurching heartbeat Sera thought: *too late, too slow, they're dead,* and then Wendell's eyelids fluttered and focused, with visible effort, on her face.

"Sera?" Their voice was gravel and rust. "You look — terrible."

A laugh broke out of her, cracked and half-sobbing. "You should see the other guy." She got her picks into the manacles' locks. Crown pattern, first revision — Rell was cheap where it counted. "Can you stand?"

"Ask me in a minute." Wendell's gaze drifted past her shoulder, and Sera saw the exact moment they registered the absence of the damper bells' pressure — saw their pupils widen, their breath catch, and beneath the grime and exhaustion their skin take on the faintest glow, like embers remembering fire. "Oh," they breathed. "Oh, that's better. That's — Sera, what did you *do*?"

"Made a withdrawal." The last manacle fell open. She got her shoulder under Wendell's arm and hauled, and together they staggered down out of the cage wagon into the smoke and torchlight and screaming. "Come on. South side, past the horses, into the trees. Oscar's waiting."

"Oscar." Wendell said the name strangely — not fear exactly, and not trust, but the careful tone of someone handling a blade they'd been told was theirs by inheritance. "Is he here?"

"He's everywhere," Sera muttered. "Don't ask. Walk."

They made it thirty feet.

Then the crowd between them and the treeline parted — not gradually, but all at once, like water before a prow — and Captain Rell stepped through it.

He was not a large man. Sera had always thought that was the worst thing about him: the ordinariness of him, the neat grey coat, the tidy beard, the eyes of a village schoolmaster. He carried no drawn weapon. He didn't need one. Behind him, forty soldiers raised crossbows at two figures swaying between the wagons, and Rell raised one hand, and the bolts stayed loosed.

"Master Amberdale." His voice carried across the camp, mild as morning. "You've caused a great deal of paperwork."

"Captain." Wendell's weight shifted against Sera's shoulder. She felt them gathering themselves — felt the gate-gift wake fully in them now that the bells' grip had slipped, a heat building in the air like the moment before a summer storm. The nearest torch flames leaned toward Wendell, hungry. Frost crawled backward off the wagon boards.

Rell saw it too. Sera watched him see it, watched something move behind the schoolmaster's eyes — not fear, never fear, but calculation, vast and fast and cold. And then, to her astonishment, he smiled.

"Take them," he said — but not to the crossbowmen. To the darkness beyond the firelight. "Take them both, and be quick about—"

The wolf came out of the walls.

There was no other way to describe it. One moment the space between two supply wagons was empty; the next it was full of Oscar, pouring through solid oak and iron-banded plank as though matter had simply agreed, at his approach, to reconsider its principles. He flowed across the camp like spilled mercury, like floodwater, and where he passed torches died and men screamed and threw themselves flat, and the great cage wagon's iron bars — the ones he passed closest to — rang like struck bells, though nothing had touched them.

Rell's composure broke for exactly one second. Sera would treasure that second for the rest of her life.

Then Oscar was between them and the soldiers, immense, his coat drinking the firelight, and his voice rolled out over the camp in a register that Sera felt in her sternum rather than heard with her ears:

"*These are mine.*"

Every soldier froze. Not from command — from something deeper, older, the animal hindbrain recognizing a predator that predated predators. Even Rell had gone still, though his hand had finally found his sword hilt.

"Go," Oscar said, without turning his head. "Both of you. Now. The trees. Run until the river, then follow the river up."

Sera ran. She half-carried, half-dragged Wendell through the gap the wolf's body left, past the screaming horses, past a soldier kneeling with his hands pressed over his ears, into the treeline, into the dark, into the blessed cover of the pines. Behind them the camp dissolved into chaos, torches toppling, officers bellowing, and above it all one clear sound: Rell's voice, cracking across his own men like a whip —

"Form ranks! Form *ranks*, you cowards, it's only a beast — archers to me! *Amberdale comes back with me or the crown burns these mountains to the stone!*"

Sera ran until her lungs tore. She ran until the fires were gone and the river roared beneath them and Wendell's legs finally buckled, both of them going down together into the snowmelt shallows, gasping, soaked, alive.

For a long time neither of them spoke. The river talked enough for both of them.

"He let us go," Wendell rasped at last. They were lying on their backs in the shallows, staring up at the wheeling stars. "Rell. He could have loosed the crossbows. He didn't. Did you see his face?"

"I saw it."

"He *wanted* the wolf to come. He wanted—" Wendell sat up abruptly, water streaming off them, and Sera saw the realization land in their eyes like a dropped lantern. "Sera. The orders. What if opening the gate was never the plan? What if drawing the wolf out — *proving* the spirits exist, taking one alive — what if that's what the crown actually sent him to do?"

The cold that went through Sera then had nothing to do with the river.

Because she had worked in those offices. She had drafted those memos. She had seen the marginalia in the margins of reports nobody was supposed to read, and she had told herself they were jokes, exercises, contingencies.

"They don't want to seal the gate," she said slowly. "Wendell. They want to harness what's behind it. And now they've seen one up close, and they know it can be baited, and they know it cares about you—"

The treeline rustled.

Oscar stepped out of the shadows upstream, unhurried, dry despite the shallows he must have crossed, his amber eyes finding them both in the dark. Arrows had not touched him. Nothing had. He looked exactly as he had at the ridge, hours and a lifetime ago — except that now, as he came closer, Sera noticed for the first time a fine crack running through the fur of his foreleg, like a fracture in porcelain, faintly glowing from within.

He saw her notice it, and tucked the leg beneath him as he settled, deliberate as a card player concealing a hand.

"Well done," the wolf-spirit said. "Both of you. Now we must talk quickly, keeper, thief — because Rell has seen me, and my brothers and sisters behind the gate have felt me use the old roads, and neither of those facts buys us more than a day." His eyes gleamed. "There is a place we must reach before he does. Bring the journal."
