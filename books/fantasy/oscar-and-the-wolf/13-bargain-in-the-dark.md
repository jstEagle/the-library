## 13. Bargain in the Dark

The cage smelled of iron and wet straw, and Wendell had learned, over two days of captivity, that the worst part was not the dampening.

It was the silence where their gift used to be.

They had lived with it so long — that low warmth under the skin, the hum of the gate always faintly present at the back of their skull, like a second heartbeat — that its absence felt like a wound. The irons Rell had locked around their wrists were forged with something cold and hungry in them; when Wendell reached inward for the old warmth, they found only a flat gray nothing, like pressing a hand against a dead hearth. They hadn't realized how much of themselves it had been until it was gone. They were a house with the fire raked out, and they kept reaching for heat that wasn't there.

The cage sat on a wagon bed among forty soldiers and six supply carts, crawling along the valley road toward whatever fortress the crown intended to make use of them. Rell had not spoken to them since the first night. That frightened Wendell more than cruelty would have. Cruelty meant urgency. Silence meant patience, and patience meant the man already knew everything he needed.

On the third night the convoy camped in a hollow below a ridge, close under the shoulder of Thornfell itself. Wendell could see the mountain through the bars, black against the stars, and something in their chest — even damped, even caged — ached toward it like a dog toward a whistled name. *I'm still here,* they wanted to tell it. *Whatever they've done to me, I'm still yours.*

They slept badly, and woke past midnight to find a wolf sitting outside the bars.

Wendell's breath went out of them all at once. They pressed back against the wagon's side wall, heart slamming, and looked around the camp — sentries at the fire, a soldier snoring against a wheel, the whole sleeping sprawl of men — and not one of them stirred. Not one of them saw. The wolf was enormous, gray as slate and silvered at the edges as if starlight clung to him, and his eyes were amber, and human, and patient.

"You," Wendell whispered.

"Me." Oscar's voice came from nowhere and everywhere, low and dry, like wind moving through a stone corridor. "You're difficult to reach, keeper. The irons are well made. Whoever forged them hates magic honestly."

"Get away from me." Their voice shook. "Every time you come near me, my life gets worse. You were at my door, and then Sera came, and then the gatehouse burned—"

"The gatehouse burned because you left it," Oscar said, without heat. "And it will burn worse, all of it, every roof in every valley, if that gate opens while no one stands in it. I told you what sleeps behind Thornfell. I have not lied to you yet, Wendell Amberdale. I would ask you to weigh that."

Wendell wrapped their arms around their knees inside the cage, making themselves small, making themselves nothing. "What do you want?"

"To offer you a bargain."

"I don't make bargains with wolves." Grandmother's journal lay somewhere in Rell's saddlebags now; Wendell had watched him wrap it in oilcloth with a collector's care. "That's the one lesson she left me that I actually understood."

Something moved in the wolf's face — not quite a smile, but the memory of one. "She said nearly the same words, her first winter. She was twenty-three and furious and half-starved, and she told me she'd sooner let the gate crack than shake my paw." A pause. "She shook it eventually. They all do. But she made me wait three years, and she made her terms hard, and I respected her for it until the day she died. Which is why I am here, and why I am willing to wait out your fury too."

"Wait for what? Wait for what bargain?" But even asking, Wendell felt the trap of it closing, the shape of the thing arriving before the words did. Because there was only one bargain the wolf had ever offered anyone in their family, and the journal had been clear enough about that, and Wendell had read those pages by candlelight with their hands shaking.

Oscar stepped closer to the bars. Up close, his fur moved wrong, or rather too rightly — like smoke deciding to be a wolf, like something wearing the idea of a wolf. When he spoke again his voice dropped lower, gentler, and that gentleness was the most frightening thing Wendell had encountered in weeks of frightening things.

"Here is what I know, and you may check each piece of it against what you've seen. One: the seal is broken. Your grandmother patched it with her own years, and the patch failed the season she died. Two: the crown has the fragments now — both halves, taken from your house and from the shrine. Three: they intend to open the gate deliberately. Rell believes he serves a righteous king. His king wants an army of what lies behind Thornfell, pointed south at his enemies. Four: when that gate opens unbound, unkept, unwilled, it will not stop at the king's enemies. It does not distinguish. It never has."

The fire cracked somewhere across the camp. A sentry coughed. The world continued on, indifferent, while Wendell sat very still inside a cage of iron and listened to the end of the world described in a level voice.

"And five," Oscar said, "there is exactly one way to seal a gate of that kind. Not stone. Not chains. Not the fragments, which are only keys, not locks. It takes a keeper. Blood-willing, bound into the threshold, alive inside the ward. Your grandmother's mother did it. Before her, a shepherd whose name is worn off the stones now. And once — a man named Oscar, who walked into a gate eight hundred years ago because the alternative was watching everything he loved get eaten, and who has been walking in the dark behind the mountain ever since."

Wendell's mouth was dry. "You want me to do what you did."

"I want you to live," Oscar said. "Those are not the same thing, though everyone in your line has insisted on confusing them. Listen to me. In six days, maybe seven, Rell's convoy reaches the king's engineers, and they will fit your fragments into a frame and turn your blood in the lock, because your blood is in the stone now — it answered you at the shrine, didn't it? It sang to you. They felt it answer. They will open the gate with you as the key whether you consent or not, and the difference between consent and force is the whole of the world. Forced, the gate tears. Willed, it seals. There is no third door, Wendell. There is only the manner of your walking through it."

*The horror of a choice dressed as a gift.* Wendell thought of Sera — somewhere south, or caught, or dead, carrying the guilt of a killed soldier and the truth of a changed body. Thought of the neighbor's boy in chains at the crossroads. Thought of grandmother's journal, the last page, the handwriting gone thin: *If my grandchild reads this, I am sorry. I tried to buy you time instead of truth. Time seemed kinder.*

It hadn't been kind. It had just been slower.

"If I say yes," Wendell said slowly, "what happens to me? Truly. Not the pretty version."

The wolf was quiet for a long moment. Outside the cage, the night wind moved, and Oscar's edges shimmered with it.

"You become the gate. You feel everything it feels — the pressure behind it, the turning of seasons on both sides of the stone, every hand that touches it warm or cruel. You do not eat. You do not sleep as the living sleep. You watch the mountain wear down, grain by grain, century by century, and you remember everyone you ever loved growing old and dying above you, and you cannot attend a single funeral." He paused. "And you are not alone. That much I can promise, because I will be beside you, and I have had eight hundred years to learn how to bear it, and I can teach you faster than I learned."

"That's not living," Wendell whispered. "That's a tomb with good company."

"It is a wall," Oscar said, "that keeps standing. And walls do not get to choose what they'd rather be." He lowered his head, and for a moment the amber eyes were terribly tired, terribly old, terribly human. "I will not lie to you to win you, keeper. That is the last courtesy I have left. The choice is monstrous. I know it is monstrous. I stood where you sit, in a cage of a different kind, and a wolf spoke these same words to me, and I hated him, and then I thanked him, and both of those took centuries."

Wendell laughed — a small, cracked sound, surprising themself. "And if I say no? If I'd rather die than be a wall?"

"Then you die, and the gate opens torn instead of sealed, and I will stand in the breach alone and hold it as long as an old spirit can, which is days, perhaps weeks, and then the valleys learn what your grandmother spent her whole life postponing." Oscar rose, shaking out his coat like a man settling a cloak. "I did not come to threaten you. The threat exists without my help. I came because you deserved to hear the true price from someone who has paid it, before someone less honest offers you a false discount."

He turned toward the dark beyond the firelight.

"Six days," he said. "Think. Rage. Grieve. All of it is yours to do. But when the moment comes, Wendell Amberdale — and it is coming — remember that your grandmother chose her terms, and her terms held for fifty years. Terms matter. Will matters. A wall raised screaming cracks sooner than a wall raised singing."

Then the firelight passed through him and he was gone, and the sentries shifted in their sleep as if a draft had moved, and Wendell sat alone in the iron silence with their wrists burning cold and their heart hammering out a new rhythm — not fear anymore, exactly. Something colder and steadier underneath it.

They thought of the gate's warmth under their palm on ordinary mornings, the hum like a second heartbeat, the way the stones had always known them. *You were never keeping the gate,* they understood suddenly, and the understanding arrived like ice water. *The gate was keeping you. Growing you. Ripening a key in a quiet house for fifty years.*

Grandmother had known. Had fed them, taught them, loved them, and all the while been fattening the sacrifice — no. No, that wasn't fair. She had bought time, the only currency she had, and time had run out on her terms and was running out on Wendell's.

Across the camp, Captain Rell emerged from his tent, sleepless, and stood looking at the cage. For a long moment his eyes met Wendell's through the bars — and there was something troubled in them, something almost human, quickly shuttered.

"You spoke," Rell said quietly. "To someone. Just now."

Wendell said nothing.

Rell looked at the empty darkness beyond the firelight for a long time, and then at the mountains, and his jaw worked once, twice, as if chewing on a doubt he couldn't swallow. "Sleep, Amberdale," he said finally. "You'll want your strength. What's coming needs you whole."

He turned back toward his tent — and stopped, because at the edge of the camp, where no sentry had challenged it and no hound had barked, a gray wolf sat watching them both, amber eyes reflecting the fire, and this time Wendell saw Rell see it.

Saw the captain go pale. Saw his hand drift to his sword and stop. Saw him mouth a word that might have been a prayer or a curse, and stand there in the firelight between the wolf and the cage like a man suddenly unsure which side of the bars he belonged on.

Oscar's gaze slid from Rell to Wendell, and one corner of the long muzzle lifted — almost, almost a smile.

"Six days, keeper," the wind seemed to say, to both of them. "And fewer than you think."

* * *

Wendell did not sleep. They sat with their back to the bars and watched the stars wheel over Thornfell and turned the bargain over and over like a stone in the hand, feeling for its seams, its traps, its hidden weight.

Escape, Oscar had said. Truth. Survival of everything they loved. And the price: forever. Not death — death was cheap and they'd have taken it gladly by now — but *duration*. Endless witness. To become the thing they had guarded from the outside, and guard it from within, past Sera's old age, past Sera's grave, past every grave, hearing the valley's laughter thin and change above them century by century.

*Terms matter. Will matters.*

So then. If they must walk through that door, they would not walk through it screaming, and they would not walk through it on the wolf's naming either. Somewhere between despair and fury, in the flat cold place the irons had left them, a third thing was taking shape — stubborn, granular, theirs.

They thought of Sera's hands. Of the anchor-words grandmother had written in the journal's margin in a language older than the crown, annotated like a recipe, like a woman planning ahead. An anchor. A voice on the outside, holding the connection open just enough.

*Sera,* Wendell thought, toward the south, toward wherever she was. *If you're alive, if you're listening to anything I'm ever thinking — I need you. Not to save me. To keep me.*

The fire burned low. The sentries changed watch. And on the ridge above the camp, unseen by any living eye except two — one amber, one human and exhausted and beginning, terribly, to hope — the mountain waited, and the hours fell away like water wearing stone.

Somewhere down the column, a horse screamed, and men shouted, and the night tipped over into motion.
