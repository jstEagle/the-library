## 14. Flight Through Thornfell

The camp was supposed to be asleep.

Sera had counted the fires from the ridge above the prison convoy — nine of them, banked low, arranged in a crescent around the wagons like a hand closing over something precious. The cage-wagon sat at the center, iron-banded, its lattice dark. Somewhere inside it Wendell Amberdale was breathing through dampened air, their gift smothered under cold iron, and every hour Sera waited was another hour the gate at Thornfell cracked a little wider without its keeper.

Oscar had shown her the way in. Of course he had. The wolf-spirit moved through the camp's stone walls and iron chains as though they were morning fog, and when he'd led her to the gap in the palisade — a seam no wider than her hand, invisible unless you knew it was there — he had paused and looked at her with those eyes that were not a wolf's eyes at all, amber shot through with grey, patient as deep water.

*The soldiers sleep light,* he'd said, voice coming from everywhere and nowhere, from his throat and from the stones beneath her boots. *But they wake slower than you run.*

She should have known better than to trust an easy rescue. Nothing about this had ever been easy.

* * *

She went down through the pines in the last hour before dawn, when the night is coldest and even sentries hunch into their cloaks and let their attention go soft. She wore Wendell's spare cloak, the one she'd taken from the burned gatehouse ruins weeks ago, ash-stained at the hem, and she told herself the disguise would matter more than it did. Soldiers didn't see cloaks. They saw movement, and shapes where shapes shouldn't be.

The first sentry she passed never turned his head. The second heard her — a footfall on frozen ground, too loud — and lifted his lantern, and Sera dropped flat behind a fallen log and held her breath until the light swept past and the man muttered something about wind and turned back toward his fire.

Her heart was a drum against her ribs. Her hands were steady. It was strange, how those two things could be true at once.

The cage-wagon stood thirty paces from the nearest fire. A driver slept on its bench, chin on chest, and a single guard leaned against the wheel with his spear butt planted, awake but barely — eyes half-shut, breath steaming in slow clouds. Sera circled wide, found the shadow side, and pressed herself against the wagon's flank. The iron was bitter cold. Through the lattice she could smell it: char and wet stone and something older underneath, the scent of the gatehouse that had burned, clinging to Wendell like smoke clings to hair after a fire you couldn't escape.

Inside, in the dark, she could just make them out. Curled small against the far wall. Eyes closed or nearly so. The irons on their wrists caught what little light bled through the lattice and threw it back dull, dead metal, metal that remembered being alive once, ore in a mountain, before men had hammered the wildness out of it.

Wendell's fingers twitched when Sera touched the bars. Just once. Just slightly, like a sleeping person flinching at a sound.

They were dreaming. Sera was almost sure of it. And some part of her — the part that had spent three years learning to read locks and vault-doors and the silences between words — understood that the dream was not a good one. There was a tension in the way they lay, a guardedness even unconscious, as if their body remembered what it carried even when their mind had been forced to forget.

*I'm here,* she whispered through the lattice, so quietly the words barely disturbed the air. *Wendell. I'm here.*

The twitching stopped. Their breathing changed. Their eyes opened — slowly, the way eyes open when someone has woken not from sleep but from something deeper, something darker — and in the faint light Sera saw them fix on her and hold, unblinking, and there was a moment where neither of them moved at all, and the camp's fires crackled on around them like nothing in the world was happening.

Then Wendell's mouth shaped a word without sound. *Go.* Their hands pressed flat against the inside of the bars, pushing, as if to shove her away through sheer force of will. *Leave me. Go south. You have the fragment. Finish it without me.*

Sera had imagined this moment a hundred ways on the road from the smugglers' pass. She had imagined gratitude. Relief. Even anger — Wendell furious at her for coming, for risking everything, for refusing, always refusing, to do the sensible thing. What she had not imagined was this: Wendell awake behind iron, asking to be left, meaning it, meaning it with their whole chest, because they had already done the arithmetic she refused to do. One keeper in a cage. Two fragments of a seal. A gate cracking across the length of a mountain range. If she died here, the arithmetic failed entirely.

Well. She had never been good at arithmetic.

"I'm not leaving," she breathed, and pulled the file from inside her cloak.

It had cost her half the coin Oscar had led her to — a dead courier's cache under a cairn, iron rations and a short sword and a purse of silver that smelled of old blood — and it was a poor tool, soft-edged, meant for padlocks on granary doors. But the lock on the cage was crown work, and crown work had one flaw: it trusted its own weight. Nobody stole a prisoner out of a convoy. Nobody was supposed to try.

The file rasped. Once. Twice. On the third stroke the sleeping driver stirred, rolled, went back to sleep. On the fifth, the guard at the wheel shifted his grip on his spear.

On the seventh stroke, everything went wrong at once.

A dog barked. Not a war-dog, not a trained thing — some mongrel tied by the quartermaster's wagon, half-starved, suddenly awake and loud and certain that something was moving in the dark that shouldn't be. The camp lit up like a struck flint. Lanterns swung. Boots hit frozen ground in a dozen places at once, and the guard at the wheel came around the wagon fast, spear leveled, shouting a name and a number — *Rell! Rell's prisoner! Hold the cage!* — and Sera heard the order ripple outward through the dark, relayed from voice to voice, each one colder than the last.

Captain Rell himself came out of his command tent in shirtsleeves and a breastplate hastily buckled, and Sera saw him clearly for the first time since the shrine — saw the way he moved, deliberate, unhurried, as if the night were simply a problem to be solved rather than a chaos to survive. He raised a hand and the soldiers tightened around the wagons like a noose drawing shut.

There was no time left for files or patience. Sera put her shoulder to the cage door and threw her whole weight against the hasp, and the cheap iron of the staple — not the lock, the staple, the one piece of the mechanism the smiths had never bothered to harden — tore free with a shriek of metal that rang across the entire camp like a bell.

The guard reached her first.

He was young. That was the thing she would remember afterward, the thing that would sit in her chest for days like a swallowed stone: he was young, younger than her, with a wisps of beard on his jaw and wide frightened eyes, and he thrust his spear at her with more panic than skill, and the point tore through her cloak and scored a hot line along her ribs, and she twisted inside the strike the way Oscar had taught her — *the wolf does not block, little thief; the wolf is not where the blow lands* — and then her hand found the short sword at her hip and she did the thing she had sworn on the road she would not do, the thing she had rehearsed and rejected and rehearsed again, lying awake in hollows and ruined barns, telling herself there had to be another way.

She drove the blade up under his arm, into the place where the mail ended, and pushed until it would push no further.

He made a sound. Not a scream. Something smaller than a scream — a startled exhale, almost a question — and his spear fell from his hand and clattered on the stones, and he looked down at her with those wide young eyes and seemed, for one impossible heartbeat, to be waiting for her to explain it to him. To take it back. To say *I'm sorry, I didn't mean* —

She let go of the sword and stepped back and he folded, slow, like something heavy settling, and lay still, and the snow under him began to drink.

Everything after that happened very fast and also unbearably slowly, the way disasters do. Wendell came through the torn door on their own feet, irons dragging, and grabbed her arm and hauled, and she realized distantly that she was screaming — had been screaming since the soldier fell, some raw animal sound with no words in it — and Wendell's hand closed over her mouth, gentle and desperate at once, and said against her ear: *Run. Run now. Grieve later. Run.*

So they ran.

* * *

They went north and east, away from the road, into the trees, following a line only Oscar could see. The wolf-spirit materialized beside them within a hundred paces — huge, pale-grey, his fur rimed with frost, moving alongside them at a lope that looked effortless and covered ground like a falling rock covers distance — and behind them the camp dissolved into pursuit. Horns. Torches spreading downslope in a net pattern, exactly the way Rell would draw it, exactly the way a man who believed in method would hunt.

Sera ran until her lungs burned and her legs shook and the hot line along her ribs opened and wept, and then she ran further, because Wendell ran beside her in dragging irons and if they could run then she had no excuse, and somewhere in that second mile the screaming inside her went quiet, replaced by a numbness she recognized from the vaults beneath the capital, from the night she'd stolen the fragment and discovered what she'd stolen — that flat, floating emptiness where fear should be, where grief should be, where a person keeps going because stopping is unthinkable and feeling is impossible.

They crossed a stream on ice that cracked and held. They climbed through scree that shifted under every step. And near dawn, in a hollow beneath an overhang of granite, they finally stopped, and Wendell broke the chain between their wrists using the same technique they'd used on the gate's old winch — leverage, patience, a stone for a fulcrum — and the irons fell away, and Sera watched Wendell flex their bare hands and feel, for the first time in weeks, the faint warmth come back into them. Not the full gift. The damped iron had left a bruise on their magic, a numbness like frostbite of the soul. But a thread of it. A pulse.

Their eyes met hers, and Wendell said, softly, "You killed a boy."

Not accusingly. Not gently either. Just — factually, the way you'd say *it rained today*, except nothing about it was factual-feeling at all, and Sera felt the words land in the hollow place and shatter something there.

"He was going to kill you," she said. "Or me. He was—"

"I know." Wendell sat down beside her, close, shoulder almost touching shoulder, and looked out at the grey light beginning to seep through the pines. "I know what he was doing. I know why. I watched it happen and I couldn't stop it and I will carry it too, because I asked you to come, in the end. I asked you. When I pushed you away through the bars, some part of me was praying you wouldn't listen."

Sera hadn't expected that. She had expected judgment, or comfort, or the practical talk of two fugitives dividing up what remained of their food. What she got instead was Wendell saying *I will carry it too*, and something in the phrase undid her more thoroughly than any accusation could have. She started shaking. Not loudly. Just a fine tremor, jaw to hands, the body's honest ledger of what it had done, and Wendell wrapped their cloak around both of them and held her and did not say *it wasn't your fault*, because they were too honest for that, and did not say *you had no choice*, because they were too kind for that, and simply stayed, warm against her back, while the shaking burned itself out.

They didn't speak of it again that day. Or the next. It became the silence between them, the shared room they both carried, and Sera learned something in those days of walking and hiding and eating cold food in frozen ditches: that silence shared with one person can be closer than speech shared with anyone. That trauma doesn't always drive people apart. Sometimes it welds.

* * *

Rell did not give up. Sera had counted on many things going wrong in her life, but she had underestimated him anyway. He tracked them through the high passes with a precision that felt less like skill than like faith — reading broken twigs and displaced scree and the direction of frost-melt, cutting sign where Sera would have sworn there was none, and twice he came close enough that they heard his riders talking below them in the dark, voices carrying up the cold air, discussing watch rotations and supply lines and whether the prisoners would dare the Frostback crossing.

"They'll dare it," one rider said. "Amberdale knows these mountains."

"Knew," corrected the other. "Been gone years."

Sera, flattened against stone above them, felt Wendell's hand find hers in the dark and squeeze. Not a word spoken. Just pressure, and warmth, and the knowledge passing between them without language: *they think we're weaker than we are. Let them.*

The Frostback crossing was three days of bad country — a saddle between peaks where the wind came off the ice fields hard enough to peel the breath from your lips, where the path was a shelf of stone barely wide enough for one, where Oscar walked ahead of them the whole way and once, terrifyingly, was simply *not there* for an hour, gone into the mountain, and Sera learned what Wendell's face looked like when the wolf vanished: not relief, not fear, but a kind of bereft alertness, like a person listening for a heartbeat they've lived with so long they've forgotten it isn't theirs.

He came back at dusk. He always came back. He brought news with him the way other creatures brought burrs — fragments of the world below, gleaned from stone and wind and whatever strange senses he kept folded inside himself. He brought word that Rell had lost the trail at the stream and doubled back and found it again. He brought word that reinforcements had entered the valley — a full company, marching for Thornfell, carrying something long and wrapped in oilcloth that the soldiers' gossip called *the key* and handled like a relic.

And on the last night of the crossing, in a cave with a fire small enough to hide, he brought something else.

He sat across from them, enormous and still, firelight moving in his amber eyes, and he said, "The gate is singing."

Wendell went rigid. "Singing."

"Cracking sings, if you listen. Stone under strain has a voice. Your grandmother taught me to hear it, a lifetime ago." His head tilted, and there was something in his posture Sera had never seen before — not the cool unreadable patience he wore like armor, but something leaner. Urgent. Almost grieving. "Three nights ago it changed pitch. It is no longer the sound of a gate holding. It is the sound of a gate failing. Days, Wendell. Not seasons. Perhaps not even weeks."

Sera watched Wendell's face do something complicated — horror and recognition braided together, the expression of a person hearing a diagnosis they had always secretly suspected. "Then we go straight there," Wendell said. "Now. Tonight. We don't rest, we don't—"

"You rest," Oscar said, and for the first time since Sera had known him, the wolf-spirit raised his voice. "You rest, because the journal you carry speaks of a shrine above the tree line where the second fragment waits, and because if you arrive at that gate with empty hands and a broken body, you will die at the threshold of the only thing that can save everyone you've ever not-met — every farmer in every valley that floods when the wolves come through. I have watched keepers fail before. I watched one fail because she would not sleep, would not eat, would not pause, and her name was carved into the gate's memory afterward with a note beside it that said *she gave all she had, and it was not enough*. Do not make me carve yours."

Silence in the cave. Firelight. Wind screaming past the entrance like a thing denied.

Wendell looked at their own hands — bare now, scarred, faintly warm — and Sera saw the exact moment the fight went out of them and wisdom came in. "One night," Wendell said. "Then we move."

"One night," Oscar agreed, and settled onto his haunches like a promise.

Sera lay back against the cave wall with Wendell's shoulder against hers and the fragment — both fragments now, joined with wire and prayer in a pouch against her sternum — humming its low uneasy hum, and she thought about the soldier in the snow, and the boy's face, and the weight she'd carry south someday if she survived to go south, and she thought: *this is what it costs. All of it costs. The stealing, the killing, the keeping, the letting go.* And then Wendell's head tipped sideways onto her shoulder, asleep in a single breath, trusting her absolutely, and the thought dissolved into something simpler and harder to name.

Across the fire, Oscar watched them both with those human eyes, and said nothing, and the nothing had a shape she was starting to recognize.

It was the shape of a countdown.

In the morning the sky over the eastern peaks was stained the color of old blood, and the wind had turned, and it carried on it a thin metallic taste that Sera had smelled only once before — the day the gatehouse burned. Somewhere beyond the saddle, out of sight, the mountain was speaking. And whatever it was saying, it was saying it louder than it had yesterday.
