## 15. The Second Fragment

The journal's last pages were a map drawn in a hand that shook.

Wendell had studied them through the night by the light of a shuttered lantern, Sera asleep against her pack beside the dead fire, Oscar nowhere and everywhere, a pressure at the edge of sight. The handwriting had deteriorated as the years went on — Grandmother Amberdale's letters growing smaller, more cramped, as if she were trying to fold more life into less paper — until the final entry, which was not an entry at all but instructions, written in ink that had gone brown with age.

*If you are reading this, child of mine, then the gate is singing to you too.*

*The second half sleeps where the mountain keeps its oldest promise. The shrine above the frozen falls, where the pilgrims stopped coming. Take the east path past the hanging stone. Do not go in winter. Do not go alone. And do not — whatever you do — let the crown's people read these words before you do.*

Wendell closed the book with hands that would not quite steady.

Outside, the wind had changed again, and it carried that thin metallic taste, stronger now, like blood on snow. Somewhere behind them, beyond the saddle they'd crossed two days ago, Thornfell was speaking. They could feel it through the soles of their boots when they stood still — a vibration, low and patient, like something enormous shifting its weight.

"You didn't sleep," Sera said. She was awake after all, watching them from under the hood she never seemed to take off anymore. Her face was thinner than it had been at the gatehouse door. There was a new scar along her jaw, thin and white, from the pass, from the soldier, from things neither of them said aloud.

"Neither did you."

"I dreamed about the fire." She sat up slowly, wincing. "And then I dreamed about iron, and I woke up, and I thought — Wendell, we're being herded. You know that, don't you? Everything since the shrine page — every road we've taken has been the one someone wanted us to take."

"The journal says the shrine is half a day's climb past the hanging stone." Wendell tucked the book into their coat, against their chest, where the warmth of it felt less like paper and more like a heartbeat. "It's the only place left. If Rell has the other half—"

"He doesn't have it. He has *pages*. Pages tell you where. They don't put it in your hand." Sera rose, testing her leg — the bad one, the one she'd landed wrong on fleeing the convoy camp — and grimaced. "We can still outrun him. We could be over the southern ridge by dark, and south of the border by week's end, and—"

"And the gate opens anyway." Wendell said it quietly, because saying it loudly made it real. "You heard the mountain last night. So did I. It isn't groaning anymore, Sera. It's *cracking*. Whatever my grandmother bound behind that stone is waking up whether we run or not. The only question left is who's standing here when it does."

Sera looked at them for a long moment. In the grey pre-dawn light her eyes were the color of river ice, and there was something in them Wendell couldn't name — fear, maybe, or its stubborn cousin, devotion.

"You're going up there regardless," she said. "Aren't you."

"I have to see what's at the shrine." Wendell shouldered their pack. "But I need you to hear me first. If it goes wrong — if he's there, if anything—"

"Don't." Her voice cracked like green wood. "Don't you dare give me the speech again. Last time you gave me a speech, I watched you walk into irons, and I killed a man to get you out, and I've slept with his face every night since. So no speeches. We climb together or not at all."

For a moment Wendell almost argued. Then they saw her hand, curled at her side, trembling — not with weakness but with the effort of holding herself together — and all the arguments died.

"Together," Wendell said.

Behind them, unremarked, the shadows between the pines deepened by exactly the shape of a wolf.

* * *

The east path past the hanging stone was a goat-track at best, a rumor at worst. It switchbacked up through black firs whose branches held last night's frost like a coat of scales, and the higher they climbed, the louder the mountain spoke beneath them. By mid-morning Wendell could feel the gate's distress in their teeth. Their gift — that wild, inherited thing they had spent a lifetime smothering — kept rising toward the surface like a drowned body refusing to stay down, answering the mountain's call whether they willed it or not.

"Stop clenching like that," Sera said, without looking back. "Every time you fight it, the stones around us hum. I can feel it through my boots. If Rell has anyone watching this slope, you might as well be ringing a bell."

"I'm *trying*."

"Try differently."

They passed the hanging stone at noon — a boulder wedged impossibly in a cleft overhead, poised above the path like a dropped thought — and beyond it the trees gave way to bare granite scree, and beyond *that*, faintly, they heard water. Not running water. Frozen water, creaking, the sound of a waterfall caught mid-fall and turned to blue glass.

"There," Wendell breathed.

The shrine sat in a hollow above the frozen falls: a ring of standing stones older than any kingdom, older than any language Wendell knew, their surfaces worn smooth except for the carvings — wolves, dozens of wolves, circling inward toward a central altar of black stone. Snow lay unbroken across the hollow, pristine, silvered by the afternoon sun.

Unbroken everywhere except one set of tracks.

Sera saw them at the same moment Wendell did. Boot prints. Fresh. Leading up into the ring of stones and stopping at the altar.

"No," Sera whispered.

The trap did not snap shut all at once. That was the terrible elegance of it. It closed the way dusk closes — gradually, then completely. First Wendell noticed the soldiers: four of them, rising out of the rocks on the high rim of the hollow where they'd been lying flat and white-cloaked all along, invisible until they moved, crossbows leveled downward. Then the crossbow bolts' shadow fell across the snow between Wendell and Sera, and both of them froze.

Then, from within the ring of stones, Captain Rell stepped out from behind the altar.

He looked worse than the last time Wendell had seen him, in the ruins of the gatehouse. Thinner. Greyer around the eyes, as though he hadn't slept either, as though something he'd seen since then had been quietly eating him from inside. But his voice, when it came, was as level and cold as ever, carrying clearly across the hollow.

"Wendell Amberdale." He let the name sit in the air like a sentence passed. "I wondered how long the journal would take you. Your grandmother wrote beautifully. Very thorough. Directions, warnings, even a little prayer at the end." He touched the altar with one gloved hand, almost gently. "She hid the fragment well. Under the third stone from the north, beneath a wolf's carved eye. It took my sappers two hours to find what your family guarded for three hundred years."

Wendell's mouth had gone dry. "You don't understand what you're holding."

"I understand precisely." For the first time, something moved behind his eyes — not madness, Wendell realized. Worse. Conviction, fraying at the edges, held together by will alone. "Do you know what I found when I opened the vault records, Amberdale? Correspondence. Three hundred years of it, between the crown and keepers like your grandmother. Every keeper who sealed a gate wrote afterward to ask the same question: *what did we seal?* And every answer came back the same. Nothing. Wolves. Old stories." His hand tightened on the altar. "But the vault also holds the founding charter of the Order of Keepers, and in it, in plain script, is the truth your family died protecting: those gates don't hold monsters *out*. They hold a war *in*. A finished war, buried alive. And the crown means to finish winning it."

"With weapons?" Wendell's voice shook. "You think you can weaponize what's behind Thornfell?"

"I think the crown thinks it can." Rell smiled without warmth. "I think the crown is wrong. And I think that doesn't matter anymore, because last night the eastern face cracked from summit to treeline, and my orders stopped mattering, and yours did too." He lifted something from the altar.

The second fragment.

Even across the distance Wendell felt it — felt *their own half* of the broken seal-stone leap against their ribs through layers of cloth, straining toward its twin like a dog at the end of a chain. The hum that rose from the joined halves' proximity was audible now, a single clear note that made the frost shiver off the standing stones.

Rell held the fragment up and regarded it with something close to reverence and something close to nausea.

"It sings," he said quietly. "All the way back down the mountain, it sang to me. Night after night. Telling me things." His eyes found Wendell's, and for one naked instant, the certainty cracked clean through. "It told me what your grandmother really bargained away. And I have been praying, Amberdale, every night since, that what it told me was a lie."

Above the hollow, on the rim, one of the crossbowmen shifted his aim from Sera to Wendell, tracking the stone-half hidden in their coat.

"Bring me yours," Rell said. "Both fragments, joined, and perhaps we learn together whether the crown's plan is suicide or salvation. Or refuse — and my men put a bolt in your friend, and take it from your body."

The note in the air climbed. Beneath their feet, faint but unmistakable, the mountain began to crack.

And somewhere above the shrine — above the standing stones, above the soldiers, above the whole frozen world — the wind stopped, and the silence took a shape, and that shape had yellow eyes.
