## 16. Steel and Stone

### Sera

The shrine came out of the mist like a thing remembered rather than seen.

Sera had been walking behind Wendell for two days, up switchbacks that clung to the mountain's spine, and she had stopped trusting her own eyes somewhere around the second dawn. But this was real. She could feel it in her teeth — a low, sweet vibration rising through the frozen ground, the same hum she'd felt the night she'd pressed half a broken stone into Wendell's bleeding hands and watched it *wake*.

"There." Wendell's voice was hoarse from cold. They stood at the edge of a shelf of rock where an ancient ring of standing stones leaned together like conspirators, their surfaces furred with lichen and old snow. At the center, a low altar of black granite — split down the middle, exactly as the journal had drawn it.

"The other half's there," Sera said. "It has to be."

"It is." Wendell's hand drifted toward their chest, where the first fragment hung on a cord against their skin. Even at ten paces, Sera could see the stone glowing faintly through Wendell's shirt, pulsing like a second heartbeat. "I can feel it calling. It's been calling since we crossed the river."

"Then let's take it and go."

But Wendell wasn't listening anymore. They were already moving toward the stones, drawn the way water is drawn downhill, and Sera felt the old fear close around her ribs — the fear that had been growing all week, that Wendell was being pulled somewhere she couldn't follow.

She scanned the slopes behind them instead. The mist was thick, but not thick enough. Twice in the last hour she'd heard something that might have been a hawk and might have been a signal whistle. Rell's riders had been a day behind them at the smuggler's toll. After the betrayal — after the ferryman's boy had ridden east with news of what Wendell could do — they might be closer than that.

Oscar had vanished at moonset, as he always did, melting into the rocks without a sound. *If soldiers come,* he'd said, *sing out. I will hear you before you finish the word.* She still didn't know if that comforted her or terrified her more.

"Wendell." She caught their sleeve. "Look at me. We take the fragment, we go south, we—"

The whistle came again. Not a hawk.

Three notes, sharp and deliberate, echoed off the mountainside below. Then another, farther west. Then a third, cutting off the eastern descent.

Sera's blood went cold. "They're here. They're already *here*."

Wendell turned, and Sera saw something in their face she'd never seen before — not fear. Resolve, banked and burning like coal. "Then we don't have time to be careful."

They ran for the shrine.

* * *

### Wendell

The fragment lay in the split of the altar like an egg in a nest, crusted with two hundred years of dust — and when Wendell's fingers closed around it, the world *opened*.

They went to one knee, gasping. The stone was warm. Warm as skin, warm as the gate-stones of Thornfell on the summer mornings of their childhood, and through their palm poured everything: the memory of hands setting it in place, the weight of vows spoken in a language that predated the crown, the long patient hunger of the thing on the other side of the seal pressing against its prison like a tongue against a rotten tooth.

*Two halves,* Grandmother's journal had said. *One to hold, one to hide. A keeper keeps both or keeps neither.*

Wendell rose with both fragments in their fists, and the mountain answered.

It began as a sound they felt rather than heard — a deep groaning beneath the earth, vast and slow, like a ship's hull flexing in a storm. Snow shivered from the standing stones. Somewhere far below them, miles down through the roots of Thornfell, something that had been held shut for centuries shifted its weight.

"No," Wendell whispered. "No, no — not yet—"

The fragments were pulling at each other. Through their closed fists Wendell could feel it, magnetic and insistent, the broken seal wanting to be whole. And every pulse of that wanting cracked the gate a little wider.

"They're coming up the south path!" Sera's shout snapped them back. She had drawn her knife and planted herself between Wendell and the tree line, where the mist was beginning to boil with movement. "Wendell — whatever you're going to do, do it *fast*."

Soldiers emerged from the fog in ones and twos, then in ranks. Grey cloaks, steel caps, the sunburst sigil of the crown's inquisition stitched at the shoulder. Twenty of them. Thirty. More filtering out of the trees along both trails, spreading into a line with the unhurried discipline of men who had done this many times and never lost.

And at their center, walking as calmly as if he'd come to inspect a granary, Captain Rell.

His eyes found Wendell across the shrine and did not waver. He raised one gloved hand, and the line halted.

"Wendell Amberdale." His voice carried easily in the thin air, mild as a man discussing weather. "By authority of the Crown, I place you under arrest for the crime of the old gift. Surrender the stones. Surrender yourself. No harm needs to come to anyone on this mountain today."

"You burned my house," Wendell called back. Their voice shook, but not with fear. With fury. "You burned twenty years of my life because a boy sold a story."

"I burned a nest of corruption before it could spread." Rell tilted his head, studying them the way a scholar studies a specimen. "You have no idea what you're holding, Amberdale. Those stones belong in a vault where they can be *understood*. Controlled. What you're doing — running about the mountains with relics you don't comprehend — you'll kill everyone in these valleys."

"And your vault will keep us safe?"

"My vault will keep us *safe*," Rell said, and the terrible thing was that he believed it. Sera had told Wendell enough about him. He was not a liar. He was something worse: a man who had never once doubted himself.

Behind him, the mountain groaned again, louder now, and several soldiers glanced nervously at the ground. Snow slid from the pines in soft white avalanches.

Rell heard it too. Something flickered across his certainty — the first hairline crack.

"What is that?" he said quietly.

"That," said a voice from everywhere and nowhere, "is the sound of your orders catching up with you."

* * *

### Sera

The wolf came out of the mist like winter itself.

One moment the tree line was empty; the next, Oscar stood at the edge of the shrine, and he was not the lean shadow-creature Sera had traveled beside for a week. He was *vast* — shoulder-high as a horse, fur the grey of storm-clouds, eyes like banked coals — and the sight of him broke the soldiers' line the way a stone breaks a window.

Someone screamed. Someone else shouted a prayer. The line wavered, and Rell's voice cracked across the chaos: *"Hold! Hold your ground, you cowards, it's only—"*

Only nothing. Only a spirit older than the crown, older than the walls of the capital, stepping forward with the terrible patience of a thing that had watched empires be born and die.

"Hold the line," Sera breathed to herself, and almost laughed at the irony. That was her job now. Not the wolf's. Hers.

Because the soldiers were regrouping. Fear froze men for a heartbeat, but Rell's were professionals, and professionals recovered. A sergeant was bellowing them into a shield wall, spears leveling toward Oscar, crossbows cranking. And past that wall, past the captain, Wendell knelt at the altar with both fragments raised over the split stone — and six soldiers were peeling away from the line, moving to flank the shrine from the east where the wards of standing stones thinned.

Over my dead body, Sera thought, and found she meant it without any drama at all. It was simply a fact, like weather.

The first soldier reached the stones. She took him by surprise — a woman with a knife, crouched among the lichen-covered pillars like something the mountain had grown. Her blade caught his sword arm and he dropped his weapon with a howl. The second swung at her; she ducked inside the arc and drove her shoulder into his chest, sending them both sprawling on the frozen mud.

The third was smarter. He waited for her to rise, spear leveled, steady hands — and Sera saw her death in the set of his stance, plain as writing.

A blur of grey hit him sideways.

Not Oscar. A smaller wolf, lean and dark-eyed, one of the shapes that had been haunting the treeline for two days that Sera had told herself were dogs. It took the soldier's spear-arm in its jaws and *pulled*, and the man went down screaming, and the fourth soldier turned and fled outright, dropping his shield, crashing down the slope through the pines.

More shapes in the mist now. Not many. Five, perhaps six — gaunt wolves with human intelligence in their eyes, harrying the flanks, never quite closing. The shield wall facing Oscar was holding, barely, spears bristling — but the flanks were dissolving, and Rell was shouting orders that contradicted each other, and above it all the mountain groaned again, so loudly this time that the fight stuttered.

Every head turned toward the altar.

Wendell was standing. Both fragments hovered an inch above their open palms, turning slowly, glowing like captive suns — and Wendell's eyes had gone the same coal-red as Oscar's.

"Sera!" Their voice came out doubled, harmonized with something deep and resonant underneath. "The anchor-words! Get ready!"

"We agreed we'd have time—"

"We don't have time! The gate's going whether I'm ready or not — I can feel it — Sera, *please*—"

The crossbow bolt came out of nowhere.

Sera saw it late — a flicker from the western rocks, a marksman who'd circled wide while everyone watched the wolves. It was aimed at Wendell's back, and there was no time to shout, no time to move, no time for anything except the decision her body made without asking her.

She threw herself into its path.

The impact spun her. Pain bloomed under her collarbone, shocking and bright, and the ground came up and hit her hard, and the sky wheeled overhead — grey mist, black pines, the leaning stones — and somewhere very far away she heard Wendell scream her name.

*Get up.* She'd been wounded before. Smuggler's work, border work, a life spent in the margins of other people's wars. *Get up, get UP—*

She got to one knee. Blood soaked hot down her front. Across the shrine, the marksman was cranking another bolt, and three soldiers were rushing the altar, and Wendell stood unmoving amid it all, arms raised, lost in a light that was swallowing them whole.

The dark wolf reached the marksman first. What happened next, Sera made herself look away from.

But the soldiers — the three, then five, then eight of them converging on the altar with blades drawn — those she could not look away from. And she could not reach. Thirty feet of broken ground and killing steel lay between her and Wendell, and her body would not carry her thirty inches.

So she did the only thing left. She filled her ruined lungs and screamed the first word of the anchor-verse into the wind — the words Oscar had taught her, the words meant for the end of all this, spoken now in desperation like a thrown torch:

"*Stone remembers!*"

The mountain roared back.

* * *

### Wendell

Inside the light, there was no battle.

There was the gate. There was the whole of it at last, both fragments joined in the air above Wendell's hands, singing to each other in a frequency that lived in the bones of the world — and through the song poured the memory of the mountain, centuries thick, keepers and wars and bargains, so much of it, too much, a flood with Wendell's name written all through it—

*Amberdale. Amberdale. Amberdale.*

Their grandmother's hands, younger than Wendell had ever known them, pressing the first fragment into a hiding place. A man with kind, tired eyes — Oscar, *Oscar before* — saying *the gate must have a willing heart or it will fail.* The original war, vast and terrible, wolves and humans dying together in the passes until someone thought to build a door instead of a grave—

And beneath it all, the pressure. The thing behind the gate, the hungry dark the wolf-spirits had once been kin to, pushing, always pushing, and the seal thinning like ice in springtime.

*It's breaking.* Wendell understood it with total clarity, the way one understands falling. *It has been breaking for years. My keeping slowed it. Nothing less than everything will stop it.*

Somewhere outside the light, steel rang. Someone screamed their name in a voice they loved. The world was ending in small human ways all around them, and Wendell stood in the eye of it with a god's power humming in their palms, and they had perhaps a minute — perhaps less — to choose.

Destroy the gift. Oscar's binding. Or —

Or the third thing. The thing that had been forming in the back of their mind since the prison camp, since the flight through the passes, since the moment in the burned ruins of their home when they'd understood that every choice offered to them had been designed by someone else.

*I'm done choosing between doors other people built.*

The joined fragments descended into their hands. The light contracted. And Wendell opened their eyes on the battlefield — on Sera kneeling in her own blood with the anchor-words blazing from her lips, on Oscar tearing through the shield wall like a grey avalanche, on Rell standing motionless amid his breaking line with his sword half-drawn and his face full of dawning horror, staring not at any of them but at the ground.

At the cracks.

They were spreading through the shrine floor in branching veins, glowing gold, and beyond the shrine they raced down the mountainside — Wendell could see them now, could see them *everywhere*, luminous fissures zigzagging down through the snowfields and the forests and the distant smoke-grey shoulders of Thornfell itself, converging on a point miles below. On the gate.

A sound rolled up out of the earth then that none of them would ever forget: not a groan but a *crack*, enormous and splintering, the sound of a mountain's foundation giving way, echoing from peak to peak across the whole range until it seemed the sky itself had split.

The fighting stopped. Every soldier, every wolf, every living thing on that mountainside stood frozen in the ringing silence after.

Wendell walked to Sera. The soldiers parted before them — whether from awe or terror, they didn't care. They knelt in the bloody snow and took her face in their hands, gentle, so gentle, thumbs wiping at tears she didn't seem to know she was crying.

"You're hit."

"I'm fine." She wasn't. Her voice was paper-thin. "Wendell, the gate—"

"I know." Wendell looked up at Oscar, who stood panting amid the wreckage of the shield wall, red-jawed, coal-eyed, waiting. Always waiting. "Both of you listen. I'm not taking his bargain. I'm not destroying the gift. I'm making a new one — mine — and I need you both to hear the terms before the mountain finishes deciding for us."

Above them, the sky darkened though it was barely noon. Below them, the cracking came again, closer now, and the golden veins in the earth pulsed brighter, like a heart accelerating toward its final beat.

Wendell drew a breath that tasted of stone and starlight, and began to speak.
