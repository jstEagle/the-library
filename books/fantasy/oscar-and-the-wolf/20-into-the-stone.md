## 20. Into the Stone

The gate was singing.

Wendell had heard it all their life — a hum beneath the stone, felt more than heard, the way you feel your own heartbeat in your teeth. But now, with both fragments of the seal joined in their hands and the mountain cracking open behind them like a chest being broken for its heart, the song had become a roar. Every ward their grandmother had woven, every knot of old Amberdale magic laid into the gate across three generations, was unraveling at once, and the sound of it was not dying. It was *inviting*.

"Wendell." Sera's voice, raw from the fight, from the smoke, from the words they had not yet said to each other. "There's still time to run. There's always time to run. That's the one thing we've proven."

They stood together on the shelf of granite before the gate, the two of them and the wolf. Below, in the shrine's courtyard, the battle was over — Rell's soldiers scattered or dead, the messenger's body cooling in the trampled snow, Rell himself kneeling with his sword planted in the earth like a man at prayer. Above, the peaks of Thornfell rang with a sound like a struck bell that would not stop ringing. And through the widening seam in the gate, Wendell could smell what was coming: cold, and old, and hungry, the breath of a dark that had waited a thousand years behind stone for someone foolish enough to knock.

"There isn't," Wendell said. "You know there isn't. The gate doesn't want a keeper who runs. It never did."

They looked down at the joined seal. When the two halves had come together in their hands at the shrine, something had happened that Wendell still could not find words for — the fragments had not merely touched, they had *recognized*, flowing into one another like water finding its old bed, and through the mended stone Wendell had seen everything. Centuries of keepers. The war the histories had forgotten. A young man with a wolf's shadow standing where Wendell stood now, making a choice that had cost him his name and given the valleys their peace.

Oscar. Oscar, who had been a man once. Oscar, who had stood at this gate and let it take him, and had walked out of it a spirit with human eyes and a debt he had spent four hundred years trying to pay.

"It doesn't have to be like it was for him," Wendell said, and was surprised at how steady their voice came out. They had expected terror. They had expected the screaming refusal they'd given him in the dark of the prison camp. But standing here, with the mountain groaning and the gate singing and Sera's hand white-knuckled around theirs, what Wendell felt was not horror. It was *certainty*, and certainty was a thing they had never once owned in their life. "That's what I told him. That's what I told all of you. I'm not doing it his way. I'm doing it mine."

"Wendell—"

"Listen." Wendell turned to face Sera fully, and made themselves look at her — at the burn scar along her jaw that she'd gotten for them, at the blood on her sleeves that was not all hers, at the eyes that had gone from stranger's eyes to something Wendell could not name and did not dare to. "The old pact was a cage. Grandmother bargained like a woman buying her grandchildren's safety with someone else's freedom, and Oscar paid it because someone had to, and the gate has been rotting ever since because a bargain made in fear makes a rotten lock. I'm not making it in fear."

Behind them, Oscar said nothing. He had said nothing since the shrine, since Wendell had spoken the words *on my own terms* and the gate itself had seemed to lean in and listen. He sat at the edge of the shelf like a wolf at the edge of firelight, and his eyes — those impossible human eyes — were wet.

"You'll be alone in there," Sera said. Her voice cracked down the middle. "Wendell, you'll be *alone*."

"No." Wendell lifted the mended seal. "I'll be the gate. There's a difference. And you—" they pressed the seal into her hands, folding her fingers over it, "—you're going to be the anchor. The other half of the bargain. The part the old keepers never had."

* * *

Sera could not stop shaking, and she was furious about it.

She had stolen from the crown's vault. She had crossed three kingdoms with half a stone that whispered to her in her sleep. She had killed a man — she still saw his face, she would always see his face — and she had stood in a burning gatehouse and watched Wendell Amberdale offer themselves to Captain Rell's irons so she could run, and through all of it her hands had been steady. Steady on the lockpicks. Steady on the knife. Steady on the reins.

Now they would not stop shaking, because Wendell had put a piece of the mountain into her palms and it was warm, warm like a living thing, warm like the stones of the gatehouse had always been when Wendell touched them, and she understood — with the terrible clarity of someone watching the one good thing in their life walk into the dark — that this was not a metaphor. This was a covenant. The stone in her hands was Wendell's heartbeat, or it would be, or it had always been, and the words Wendell was teaching her were not a spell. They were a promise.

"Say it back," Wendell said.

"*Stone remembers, stone keeps.*" Sera's voice came out wrecked. She said it again, harder. "*Stone remembers, stone keeps. As long as one voice answers the gate from outside, the keeper inside is not lost.*"

"Not lost," Wendell repeated, and smiled — that crooked, self-deprecating smile Sera had first seen over a bowl of barley soup three weeks and one lifetime ago, the smile of someone who had spent years believing they were small and had only just learned they were not. "You see? The old pact bound a keeper and left the world outside to forget them. Grandmother forgot Oscar. The villages forgot him. He became a story, then a shadow, then nothing. I'm not permitting that. You will remember me. You will *speak* to me. Every dusk, if you can bear it. And as long as you do—"

"You're not alone," Sera said. "And the gate isn't just a lock. It's a *door*."

"It's a door," Wendell agreed. "And doors are meant to be answered, not just barred."

Oscar rose. He came toward them across the granite without a sound, because he never made a sound, and when he reached Wendell he did something Sera had never seen him do: he bowed. Low, deliberate, the gesture of an old courtier or an older soldier, and when he lifted his head his voice was rough in a way she had not heard from him before.

"Four hundred years I have waited for a keeper who would bargain back," Oscar said. "Your grandmother gave. I took. It shamed us both, though neither of us had the grace to say so while she lived. You—" his muzzle lifted toward the singing gate, "—you are the first Amberdale to understand that a pact is not a sacrifice. It is a marriage. The gate will be lucky."

"The gate will be *negotiated with*," Wendell said, and Sera laughed — a broken, sobbing, helpless laugh, because of course, of *course* Wendell would correct the grammar of eternity even now — and then the mountain screamed.

The seam in the gate split wide. Beyond it was not darkness, exactly. It was the absence of everything — of light, of time, of the small mercies of a world with edges — and through it Wendell heard the old hunger turning toward them like a head turning, vast and slow, and knew that the last ward had minutes left. Perhaps less.

Wendell looked at Sera.

There were a hundred things to say. Wendell said none of them, because they had already said the truest one without words, in the burned gatehouse, in the prison dark, in every stubborn, infuriating, unasked-for kindness since the night she bled on their doorstep. Instead they took her face in their hands — their hands, already going faintly luminous, the old gift no longer something to hide but something to *spend* — and pressed their forehead to hers.

"Every dusk," Wendell whispered.

"Every dusk," Sera promised. "I'll be insufferable. I'll tell you about the weather. I'll complain about the goats."

"Good." Wendell's breath caught. "I'll want to hear about the goats."

And they stepped back, and turned, and walked into the gate.

* * *

The stone did not swallow Wendell. That was the first thing they understood, in the last instant of having a body to understand with. The stone *received* them, the way the earth receives rain, the way a word receives meaning. The seam closed around them like water closing over a diver, and the roar of the hungry dark was cut off — not silenced, *answered* — as Wendell's gift poured out of them and into the gate and became, at last and entirely, the gate.

They felt themselves spread. That was the only word. They felt their awareness run through the granite like sap through a tree, up into the peaks and down into the roots of the mountain, into every ward and every knot of old magic their grandmother had laid and her mother before her and the keepers before *them*, back and back, centuries deep. And at the center of it all, where the joined seal had lodged itself into the heart of the stone, Wendell gathered themselves — not a body anymore, but a *voice* — and spoke to the dark.

*I am the keeper of Thornfell. I am Wendell Amberdale, and I am here of my own will, on my own terms.*

The dark surged against them, and Wendell held.

It was not like holding a door. It was like being the door. The hunger pressed and Wendell pressed back, and the gate — their body now, their bones of mountain and mortar and grandmother's stubborn love — held as it had never held, because every keeper before had locked it from outside and left it to stand alone, and Wendell was *inside*, woven through every stone, and the dark could not break what it could not find the edge of.

Somewhere above, impossibly distant and impossibly near, a voice was speaking.

*Stone remembers, stone keeps.*

Sera. Sera, on her knees on the granite shelf, the seal pressed to the gate, tears freezing on her face, saying the anchor-words through her sobs because she had promised and Sera kept her promises the way mountains kept their weight. And where her voice touched the stone, Wendell felt it — felt *her* — a thread of warmth running down through the gate into the dark like a lamp hung in a window, and Wendell understood at last what the old keepers had never had, what Oscar had never had, what had made four hundred years a cage instead of a home.

Someone outside, answering. Someone outside, *remembering*.

*Every dusk,* Wendell thought toward her, and felt the thought arrive, felt her gasp against the stone, felt her laugh and weep in the same breath. *I heard that. The goats, Sera. Tell me about the goats.*

* * *

The gate closed.

Sera felt it happen — felt the seam draw shut like a wound healing, felt the mountain's screaming fade to a hum, felt the terrible pressure of the thing behind the stone recede and settle and *sleep*, held now by something it could not wear down because the something was not a wall. It was a person. It was Wendell. It was Wendell, and Wendell was everywhere in the stone at once, and when Sera laid her palm flat against the gate it was warm. Warm the way it had always been, all those years Wendell had touched it without knowing why.

The seal in her hands had gone quiet. Not dead — she could feel it, faint as a pulse — but quiet, its work done, its two halves at rest in the whole they had always meant to be. She set it into the niche at the gate's center where Wendell's hands had pressed it, and it sank into the stone like a coin into snow, and was gone, and was not gone.

Oscar stood beside her. The wolf looked up at the sealed gate for a long time, and then he did the last thing she expected: he sat down on the cold granite, in the falling snow, like a dog at a graveside, and stayed.

"Will he be alone?" Sera asked. Her voice was ash.

"No," Oscar said. "Not now. Not ever again, if you keep your word." He turned his human eyes on her, and for the first time since she had known him they held no unreadable distance. They held something that looked, impossibly, like gratitude, and something beneath the gratitude that looked like hope. "Four hundred years I kept that gate from the outside and told myself it was enough. It was never enough. But a keeper inside and an anchor without—" he looked back at the stone, "—that is a covenant. That is what we should have made, she and I. Your Wendell has mended more than a gate today."

*Your* Wendell. Sera did not correct him. She could not have said why she didn't, only that the words settled into her chest like a coal into a brazier, and that she did not want them taken out.

Snow fell. The mountain quieted, peak by peak, the great bell-sound fading into the ordinary silence of winter. Far below, in the shrine courtyard, Captain Rell still knelt by his planted sword, a man whose whole world had broken open in an afternoon and who had not yet learned what to rebuild from the pieces. That reckoning would come. There would be letters to write, truths to carry down the mountain, a crown to defy or deceive. But not tonight.

Tonight Sera climbed the path to the gatehouse — burned, blackened, its roof open to the stars — and stood in the ruin of the only home she had found in years, and put her hand on the doorpost stone.

It was warm.

"*Stone remembers, stone keeps,*" she said, and her voice cracked, and she said it again, and again, until it stopped sounding like a spell and started sounding like a greeting. "Hello, Wendell. It's dusk. The goats got into the barley again. You'd have hated it."

The stone hummed beneath her hand — faint, unmistakable, amused — and Sera laughed and wept and sat down in the snow to keep her promise, while somewhere inside the mountain, a voice that was no longer quite a voice but was still, entirely, Wendell, listened.

And in the deep of the stone, where the long memory of the mountain unrolled like a road with no end, Wendell turned and found Oscar waiting — and together, the two of them began to walk.
