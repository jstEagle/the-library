## 22. Oscar and the Wolf

Time moved differently inside the stone.

Wendell had learned that in the first turning of what they still called a year, though no year passed here — only long tides of memory that rose and fell through the mountain like breath. Above, seasons turned. Below, in the warm dark of the gate's heart, there was only the slow procession of everything that had ever happened, laid out like a road that had no end and no beginning, and Wendell walked it.

They were never alone.

"You're brooding again," Oscar said, falling into step beside them. He wore his man-shape today — he often did, now, when it was just the two of them walking. Tall and grey-eyed, with the wolf's shadow pooled at his heels like spilled ink. "I can tell because you've got your grandmother's frown. The one she wore whenever I told her something true."

"I'm not brooding. I'm listening."

"To what?"

"The mountain." Wendell tilted their head, and the gesture was half theirs and half the gate's now, and they had stopped being able to tell where one ended and the other began — which had frightened them once, and did not anymore. "There's water moving under the north ridge. Snowmelt. And someone is climbing the shepherd's path."

Oscar's ears — the wolf's ears, pricked through the man's silhouette — turned toward a sound no body could hear. "A child," he said. "Small feet. She climbs too fast for her mother behind her."

"They always climb too fast." Wendell smiled despite themselves. "Come on. You know the way."

* * *

The long memory of the mountain unrolled around them as they walked, and Wendell had learned to read it the way they'd once read weather over the valley: by feel, by pressure, by the taste of old grief on the air.

Here — this fold in the stone, this shimmer of grey-green light — was the war. It always came first, the way a scar always aches before rain. Wendell felt it as they passed: the thunder of armies on the lower slopes, the smell of pitch and burning fir, the terrible bright shapes of the first keepers standing in a ring around a gate that had not yet learned to be gentle, holding back things that had poured out of the dark between worlds hungry and endless.

And here, further along, quieter: the first bargain. A woman with amber eyes kneeling in the snow, offering her blood to the stone, offering her line forever. *Keepers,* the memory whispered in a voice like wind through a keyhole. *Keepers until the last of you chooses otherwise.*

"Your grandmother hated that word," Oscar said softly. "Forever."

"I know. I read her journal a hundred times now. It lives in my bones the way the gate does." Wendell ran their hand along the memory's edge, and it rippled like the surface of a pond. "She hated *forever*, but she loved the valleys. That was her whole tragedy, wasn't it? She couldn't hold both."

"No one can. Not without help." Oscar looked ahead, down the long road of memory, where the images grew brighter and closer to the present. "That's why there are two of us now."

Wendell glanced at him. In the gate's heart-light, his man-shape was translucent at the edges, and through it they could see the other thing he truly was: vast, patient, older than the war itself. Once a man who had made the same choice. Once a keeper who had walked into the stone four hundred years ago and become voice and ward both, until the pact frayed and the gate began to forget him, and loneliness had worn him thin as winter ice.

Until Wendell.

"Do you ever regret it?" they asked. They asked it sometimes. They always would, maybe. "Becoming this. Giving up the sun."

Oscar considered it the way he considered everything — thoroughly, and with a wolf's honesty. "I regretted it for three hundred years," he said. "Then your grandmother bargained with me, and I had someone to hate, and hating her was better than the silence. And then she died, and the silence came back, and I thought: this is what I am now. A watchdog at an empty door." His mouth curved, not quite a smile. "And then a stubborn gatekeeper with their grandmother's eyes broke every rule they'd ever been taught and let a bleeding stranger inside."

"And you decided I was interesting."

"I decided you were *hopeful*. Which is worse." He laughed then, low and rough, and the whole mountain seemed to laugh with him — a tremor in the deep stone that Wendell felt in their changed blood. "But to answer you truly, keeper: no. I don't regret it. Not anymore. Regret needs solitude to grow in, and I haven't been alone since you came."

Wendell said nothing. There was nothing to say that the stones between them didn't already know.

* * *

They walked on, and the memories brightened toward the present tense.

Here was last autumn, held in amber: Sera at the gatehouse door, bleeding, clutching half a broken seal-stone, and Wendell breaking their oldest rule to open the door. Here was Rell's riders on the high pass, torches like a string of falling stars. Here was the shrine, the battle, the joined fragments singing, the gate cracking across the mountains like ice on a spring river. Here was the choosing — Wendell's own voice, steady and clear, rewriting a pact four centuries old:

*I will be your keeper. But I will not be your prisoner. I will hold this gate from within, and I will hold my name, and I will hold the ones I love in the light above. Name your terms, wolf, or find another fool.*

And Oscar's answer, which Wendell heard again now every time they passed this place in the memory-road, and which never failed to move them: *Those are the best terms any keeper has ever named. Done.*

The gate had sealed that night. The wolves behind it had gone quiet — not dead, not caged, but *held*, the way a held breath is held, the way a promise is held. And Wendell Amberdale had stepped into the stone as its living keeper, and Sera had spoken the anchor-words with tears freezing on her cheeks, and the connection had opened — just enough, just wide enough — and it had never closed.

It was open now. Wendell could feel it the way they felt their own heartbeat: a thin golden thread running up through the mountain, out through the warm stones of the rebuilt gatehouse, into the world of sun and snowmelt and living people.

Sera kept the thread alive from her end. Every dusk she sat by the hearthstone and spoke to it — reports, complaints, jokes, sometimes just her breathing — and every dusk Wendell answered, their voice rising through the stone like warmth through a floor. Some nights she sang, badly, on purpose, because she knew it made Wendell laugh and the laughter shook the whole gate like a bell.

"She's coming up the path tomorrow," Wendell said. "For the spring rite. I felt her packing last night. She argued with herself about whether to bring the honey-cakes, and she's bringing them."

"She always brings the honey-cakes."

"She thinks I can't tell." Wendell grinned. "I can always tell."

* * *

They climbed.

That was the strangest grace of the new pact, the one Wendell had written with their own blood and their own naming: inside the gate, they could go anywhere the gate went. And the gate went everywhere — through bedrock and root, under rivers, up through the shoulder of Thornfell itself, until the stone thinned around them like fog and the world above showed through in glimpses: green valley, white peak, the small dark figure scrambling up the shepherd's path.

The child.

She was perhaps seven years old, red-cheeked, furious with the steepness of the climb, with a scarf her mother had tied and immediately lost to the wind. She crested the ridge below the old gatehouse — Sera's gatehouse now, smoke curling sweetly from its chimney — and instead of going down to it, she did what children had done at Thornfell for generations, before the witch-hunts, before the fear, before all of it.

She put her hand flat against the boundary stone.

Wendell stilled. Even Oscar went quiet beside them, the wolf-shadow at his heels lifting its head.

The girl's palm pressed the stone, and the stone — *their* stone, warmed from within by the thread of a keeper's presence — warmed further under her touch. And the girl felt it. Wendell watched her feel it: the small startle, the widening eyes, the way her fingers spread wider as if to hold more of whatever she'd found. She looked around, certain someone was behind her. No one was. She looked back at the stone.

And Wendell, gently, carefully, the way one speaks to a bird so as not to startle it from the branch, let the warmth answer.

Not words. Not yet — she was too young, and the gift in her was a seed, not a flame; Wendell could feel it sleeping in her the way they'd once felt their own, a wild inherited thing barely understood. Just warmth. Just presence. Just the unmistakable sense of being *greeted*.

The girl laughed — a bright, startled sound that went skipping down the mountainside — and pressed both hands to the stone now, and the stone sang back, a faint harmonic hum that Sera would later swear she'd felt through the floorboards of the gatehouse, though she'd never be able to explain it.

"She has it," Oscar said quietly. "The old gift. Third generation since the choosing. It's waking early."

"I know." Wendell watched the child with an ache that was not grief and not joy but some third thing, larger than both. "Her grandmother will be scared. Her mother will be scared. Someone will remember the hunts, even now, even after Rell—" They stopped. Even after Rell had turned his blade on his own superiors' messenger; even after the edicts had burned and the bounty boards come down; even after the crown, embarrassed, had declared the old gift a matter for scholars rather than pyres. Fear lived longer than law.

"And so?" Oscar prompted.

"And so we do what keepers do." Wendell knelt in the memory-stone, close to the surface, close to the child's small hands. "We watch. We wait. We keep the gate warm. And when she's ready — when she comes back year after year because the stone feels like home and she doesn't know why — we'll teach her. Slowly. Kindly. On her terms, not ours." They looked up at Oscar. "That's the difference, isn't it? That's what grandmother got wrong, and what you got wrong, and what I refused to get wrong. No more pacts made over someone's head. No more inheritances forced on children who didn't choose them."

Oscar was silent a long moment. When he spoke, his voice was rough. "Four hundred years I kept this gate alone, and in one turn of the seasons you've rewritten everything I thought I knew about what keeping means."

"Don't be sentimental, wolf. It'll make the stone damp."

He laughed, and the child on the mountainside shivered delightedly as the stone hummed beneath her palms, and far below in the valley the first lambs of spring were being born in a dozen barns, and none of it — not one small piece of it — was afraid.

* * *

Evening came to the mountain, the way evening came everywhere now: honestly, gently, without dread.

From the gate's high places Wendell watched the sun go down over the valleys their grandmother had loved, the valleys they had given up the sun to protect. The light lay gold on the thawing fields, on the road where a cart rolled south with the season's first wool, on the gatehouse chimney-smoke where Sera was setting out honey-cakes on a plate out of pure stubborn hope, because she'd felt the child on the path and knew Wendell would want to hear about it at dusk.

Dusk came. The thread brightened.

"Wendell." Sera's voice, warm through the stone, worn soft by a year of speaking to it. "You're glowing again. The hearthstone's doing the thing. Marta's daughter says the whole path lit up like a festival — she's half convinced the mountain's haunted, half convinced it's a blessing, and fully determined to bring the girl back next week regardless, so prepare yourself."

"She already touched the boundary stone," Wendell said, and their voice came up through the floor, through the hearth, through the very bones of the house, and they felt Sera smile the way they always felt it — as light. "She laughed. She has the gift, Sera. Sleeping, but there."

A pause. Then, quietly: "Another keeper?"

"Maybe. If she chooses. Only if she chooses." Wendell settled deeper into the stone, into the vast slow company of the mountain, Oscar's presence steady at their side like a second heartbeat. "How are the honey-cakes?"

"Burnt on one side. Like always."

"Like always," Wendell agreed fondly. "Save me the burnt ones."

"Always." And then, softer, the words she said every dusk and meant every dusk: "Goodnight, keeper. The gate is warm. The valley is safe. Come walk with me in the morning."

"Always," Wendell answered, and closed their eyes, and the stone held them like a hand.

* * *

Later — in the timelessness that was the gate's only hour — Wendell walked the memory-road with Oscar, as they would walk it tomorrow, and the day after, and for all the long patient ages to come.

They passed the war, and mourned it. They passed the first bargain, and forgave it. They passed their own choosing, and did not flinch from it. And ahead of them, further down the road than either of them could see, the memory shimmered with things not yet happened: a red-cheeked girl grown tall and sure, her hand on the gate; travelers on the high road telling true stories about the warm stones; winters and springs beyond counting, each one guarded, each one free.

"Where does it end?" Wendell asked once, the way they asked every few turnings, not because they expected an answer but because the question itself had become a kind of prayer.

Oscar walked beside them, man and wolf and mountain all at once, and his answer was the same as it always was, and it was enough:

"It doesn't, keeper. That's rather the point."

And somewhere above, in the blue dusk of a spring evening, a child pressed her ear to a warm stone and heard — faint and kind and unmistakably real — two voices keeping watch together, singing the mountain to sleep.
