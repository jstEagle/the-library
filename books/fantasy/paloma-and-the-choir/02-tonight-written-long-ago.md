## 2. Tonight, Written Long Ago

The returned volumes came up from the receiving room at the ninth bell, and Ada Brightwater—though no one in the Ledger Guild called them that, because no one in the Ledger Guild knew that name existed—took the trolley of books into the stacks with the particular contentment of a person doing exactly what they were made to do.

There were forty-one volumes tonight. Ada counted them twice, because counting was a kind of prayer, and because the Guild punished miscounts not with shouting but with a small red mark beside your name in the internal ledger, and a red mark was a debt, and debts at the Guild were never merely debts. They accrued. They compounded. They waited.

Ada had been Clerk Third Class for six years now, which was long enough to be trusted with the Returned alone after hours, and short enough that no one had begun to wonder why they never spoke at supper, why they declined every festival invitation, why their face did the thing faces did when you tried to recall it later—that pleasant, unremarkable blur. Six years of being nobody. Six years of ink under the fingernails and lamplight on the wrist-bones and the deep, animal peace of a life so small it fit inside its own margins.

*You are safe here,* Ada told themselves, as they told themselves most nights, wheeling the trolley through the stacks where the shelves went up past the reach of any ladder, into the dark where the oldest holdings slept. *Nobody looks for nobody.*

The first thirty volumes were ordinary returns: ledgers recalled for audit, a merchant's contract of years, a widow's receipt for three months of her late husband's voice, paid out to a singer who had wanted it for a funeral eulogy and had wept over it beautifully, and the widow had signed, and the widow had walked home lighter and poorer and would spend the rest of her life reaching for his voice and finding only the shape of where it had been. Ada shelved that one without letting themselves think about the widow's hands. Thinking about the hands was how the guilt started, and guilt was a luxury for people whose names were still attached to them.

Volume thirty-two was a fisherman's luck, seven years of it, but that entry belonged to yesterday's shift and Ada had already written it down in their own hand, in the careful clerk's script they'd practiced until it looked like everyone's script and therefore like no one's. *Seven years of fair winds, one voyage.* The man had grinned while he signed. He'd had a daughter with a fever and no other coin. Ada had watched him sign away seven years of good weather and thought, with the cold professional part of the mind: *a fair price*, and then thought, with the other part, the buried part, the part with a name attached: *what will his daughter remember of him, when the luck is spent?*

Ada shelved volume thirty-two and did not answer the question.

It was volume thirty-eight that stopped the trolley.

It shouldn't have. It was a plain thing, calf-bound, spine cracked, the leather gone soft and dark as old bread. Returns came in like this all the time—books the Guild had lent out decades ago against collateral no one living remembered, books that surfaced from estate sales and flooded cellars and the occasional improbable grave. The receiving clerks logged them by weight and seal and sent them up without ceremony.

But this one was heavy wrong. Not weight—heft. Ada had handled enough old bindings to know when a book carried more than its pages, the way a locked chest carries more than its wood. And there was a seal pressed into the back cover, wax gone grey as ash, and something about the shape of it—

Ada set the book flat on the trolley's top shelf and stood very still, listening to the stacks breathe.

The lamps along the aisle hummed. Somewhere below, the night-shift clerks murmured over their entries, a sound like rain on paper. Nothing else.

*Open it here or shelve it and walk away,* said the sensible voice, the clerk's voice, the voice that had kept Ada alive for six years. *Unlogged inspection of a returned holding is a red mark. Red marks compound.*

Ada opened it.

The pages were ragged-edged, hand-ruled, filled in a script that slanted hard leftward, the writing of someone who wrote fast and feared interruption. A century at least, judging by the iron-gall burn-through, the way the ink had eaten brown halos around every letter. It was a journal. No—a ledger. The distinction mattered; Ada could feel the difference in their fingertips the way a musician feels a false note. This was a book of accounts.

Except the accounts were not of money.

*Paid: the harbor pilot's memory of his mother's face, against safe passage through the Teeth.*
*Paid: a midwife's knowledge of her own birth-name, against the mending of her son's leg.*
*Paid: the king's—*

Ada turned the page quickly, heart tapping at the ribs, telling themselves it was historical, it was archival, it was the sort of thing the Guild bought back and buried precisely so that clerks like Ada would never have to read it. That was the arrangement. That was the whole quiet architecture of the place: the Guild collected, the Guild recorded, the Guild forgot on behalf of everyone else, and the forgetting was called mercy, and the mercy was called business.

The final entries changed hands. The leftward-slanting writer gave way to a rounder, slower script, and then to another, and another—the book passed keeper to keeper down a century, each one adding entries, each one's handwriting a little more frightened than the last. Ada read the dates as they marched forward, decade by decade, and felt the stacks grow colder around them, or imagined they did, which amounted to the same thing.

Then the dates stopped being history.

They entered Ada's own lifetime. They passed it. They arrived at this year, this season, and Ada's breath went thin and high as the final page approached, because the entries were no longer payments received. They were instructions. Predictions. A schedule.

And the last dated line—the ink barely dry-brown, the page still holding a faint ghost of dampness as though the words had settled there recently, recently, impossibly—

*bore tonight's date.*

Ada read it four times. The date did not change. The lamp did not flicker. The stacks held their breath with them.

The entry was longer than the others. It was written in the oldest hand, the fast leftward-slanted original, though the ink was fresh, which was impossible, which Ada filed away in the part of the mind that handled impossibilities, a part that had grown fat and well-organized over six years of guild work. The entry read:

*On this night the book comes home to the shelves, carried by the one who buried the name. The harvest is nearly full. The crown is nearly ripe. Let the collectors be gentle with her; she has been ours since before she was born, and she will come to understand that nothing was ever hidden from us—not her flight, not her master's grief, not the name she drowned in the river at*

The sentence stopped.

Not faded. Not torn. Stopped. Mid-word, almost—Ada leaned close and saw the pen had lifted cleanly from the page between one letter and the next, the way a hand lifts when someone speaks behind you. The rest of the page was blank. The rest of the book was blank. But the blankness had a quality Ada had never encountered in blank paper, and it took three long moments to name it:

The blankness was waiting.

Ada straightened up too fast and the trolley rattled. In the silence after, they heard their own breathing, quick and shallow, and beneath it—no. Beneath it, nothing. There was no one in the stacks. There was never anyone in the stacks. That was the entire point of the stacks.

*Carried by the one who buried the name.*

Ada looked down at the book in their hands and understood, with the slow horrible clarity of ice forming across a pond, that they had not found it. Books did not get found in the Ledger Guild. Books got *delivered*. The receiving clerks logged forty-one volumes by weight and seal, and thirty-seven of them had been ordinary, and this one had been put on Ada's trolley—or Ada's trolley had been put in its path—with the same calm precision with which the Guild did everything, because the Guild did nothing accidentally, the Guild had never once in its history done anything accidentally—

*she has been ours since before she was born.*

Her.

The word landed in Ada's chest like a stone dropped down a well. They stood in the lamplight with a century-old book open in their hands and let the well-bottom of it come up to meet them: the name. The name they had buried. Not changed—changed names could be traced, changed names had paperwork, and paperwork was the Guild's native element. Buried. Drowned, the entry said, *the name she drowned in the river at*—at some point, some place, cut off mid-sentence by a pen lifted a hundred years ago by a hand that had known exactly where its sentence would end, because the sentence ended wherever the reader needed it to end, and the reader was here, now, tonight, and the sentence was finished.

Ada's birth name rose in them the way drowned things rise: slowly, turning, unwilling.

They had been nineteen. They had knelt on the bank of the Greywash with their master's ring of keys in their fist and a bundle of papers wrapped in oilcloth, and they had spoken the small hedge-witch rite their master had taught them for putting things out of reach of finding-spells, and they had said the name aloud one last time—said it *into* the river, given it to the current the way you'd give a coin to a ferryman—and the water had taken it, and Ada had walked into the city the next morning nameless and free and had signed the Guild's intake ledger with a mark instead of letters, because a mark could belong to anyone, and anyone was safe.

Six years. Six years of being Clerk Third Class, of ink and lamplight and the blessed gray anonymity of the middle shelves. Six years, and the whole time—

*nothing was ever hidden from us.*

The Guild had known. The Guild had always known. Every shift, every promotion, every gentle assignment to the quiet night work that suited Ada so well—all of it arranged, all of it watched, all of it a long patient leash dressed up as a career. And now the leash had shortened, and the collar had a name on it, and the name was the one at the bottom of the Greywash, and the book in Ada's hands knew it too.

Ada closed the book.

The sound of the covers meeting was very loud in the stacks, a sound like a door shutting, or a trap.

They should log it. That was procedure. Returns were logged, shelved, sealed; anomalies were reported to the Receiving Master; the Receiving Master reported upward; and whatever this book was, whoever had sent it, reporting it meant handing it—and the name inside it, and the girl who had drowned that name—directly into the machinery that had been waiting a century to collect.

*Let the collectors be gentle with her.*

Gentle. The word was worse than cruelty. Cruelty you could run from. Gentleness meant they considered running already accounted for.

Ada stood in the aisle with the book hugged to their chest like a child, and around them the Guild went on murmuring its nightly murmur, thousands of entries, thousands of lives weighed and priced and struck through, and for six years Ada had believed they stood outside all of it, an ink-stained ghost among ghosts, invisible, safe. Now the safety inverted itself and showed its other face: invisibility was not freedom. Invisibility was a cage painted the color of the air. Someone had built the cage. Someone had chosen its dimensions. Someone had decided, a hundred years ago, with a fast leftward-slanting hand, exactly which shelf Ada would wheel a trolley past on exactly this night.

And the book had come home. *The book comes home to the shelves.* Which meant it had also, somewhere in its century of traveling, learned everything else it wanted to say, and was choosing—page by waiting page—to say it slowly.

Or the rest had been removed. Ada turned that possibility over too, colder: perhaps the sentence continued somewhere, in another hand, in another vault, and what Ada held was only the bait half of a hook.

Either way, the hook was set. Either way, Ada had picked it up.

They wheeled the trolley to the end of the aisle, and instead of logging the return they slipped the book inside their coat, against their ribs, where it lay warm and heavier than any book had a right to be, heavier than paper and board and a century of frightened handwriting, heavy the way a debt is heavy, the way a name is heavy once you agree to carry it again.

The clock above the stacks floor showed the eleventh bell. Two hours until shift's end. Ada smoothed their apron, dimmed the aisle lamps one by one, and became once more the unremarkable clerk walking the unremarkable rounds, face blurring pleasantly in the memory of anyone who might glance up.

Only the book knew different. Only the book pressed its warmth against Ada's side like a hand, like a summons, like a sentence resumed.

*She has been ours since before she was born.*

Outside, far off toward the palace hill, the crown of the king caught the moonlight above the sleeping city, as it had every night of Ada's six safe years, and gleamed, and waited.

Ada finished the round. Ada signed out at the desk with the mark that belonged to anyone. And Ada walked home through the wet streets carrying a century-old secret against their heart, knowing two things with absolute certainty:

That tomorrow they would have to find out what the seal on the back cover meant—and there was exactly one person in this city disgraced enough, desperate enough, and archivist enough to read it.

And that somewhere in the Guild's great house of ledgers, an entry had already been written recording this night, this choice, this fear—in a neat clerk's hand that looked like everyone's hand, and sounded, now that Ada finally listened, exactly like their own.
