## 3. The Archivist Who Fell

The annex smelled of tallow and old lies.

Sef had worked in the palace archive for eleven years, back when the air was beeswax and vellum and the particular sweetness of paper that had never been touched by anyone who couldn't afford to touch it. The annex smelled like none of that. It smelled like a place where documents went to be forgotten on purpose—where provenance was scrubbed, where seals were forged, where a bill of sale for a human being could be made to look three hundred years older than it was, or three hundred years younger, depending on which lie paid better.

He liked it here. That was the shameful part. He'd expected to hate it—the way a man expects to hate the gutter he's fallen into—and instead he'd found the work honest in a way the palace never was. In the annex, everyone knew the books were lies. There was a kind of cleanliness in that. Nobody pretended the archive served truth. It served whoever paid, and Sef had stopped serving anyone at all, which meant he served the room: cataloguing, authenticating, occasionally burning things that were too dangerous to exist even as forgeries.

He was doing exactly that when the bell over the door rang—a cheap tin thing, nothing like the silver chimes of the royal collection—and looked up to see a clerk.

Not just any clerk. Sef had spent enough years around the Ledger Guild's people to know the breed: ink to the second knuckle, eyes trained to slide past faces without landing on them, a posture of such deliberate unremarkability that it became its own kind of loud. This one wore the grey apron of a junior keeper, hood damp from the rain outside, and stood in the doorway the way people stand when they've rehearsed being somewhere they shouldn't be.

"We're closed," said Oskit from the counter, not looking up from his dice. Oskit was always telling customers they were closed. It was a negotiation opener.

"I'm not selling," the clerk said. "Or buying."

"Then you're lost."

"I'm looking for someone who knows seals." The clerk set something on the counter with the care of a person handling a sleeping animal. "Old ones."

Sef should have gone back to his burning. That was the rule of his new life: see nothing, be nothing, want nothing. He'd been a royal archivist once; he'd learned what wanting got you. Wanting had gotten him a hearing before the Lord Chamberlain's tribunal and a sentence read aloud in a voice like wet gravel—*stripped of office, stripped of access, stripped of name from the rolls*—and eleven months of learning how to sleep in rooms that weren't his.

But the clerk said *seals*, and Sef's hands were already moving before his caution caught up, because there are hungers that outlive disgrace, and his had always been this one.

He crossed the shop. "Show me."

The clerk hesitated. Up close, Sef revised his estimate—not a junior keeper. Something more careful than that. Someone who had chosen the grey apron the way other people chose masks. Young, maybe, but young the way a knife is young.

"It's not mine," the clerk said. "I shelve returns. This came back into circulation after—" A pause, recalculating. "It came back. I wasn't supposed to open it. I opened it anyway."

Everyone in the annex opened things they weren't supposed to. It was practically the entry requirement.

On the counter lay a book wrapped in oilcloth, and beneath the oilcloth, leather gone the color of dried blood, and stamped into that leather—

Sef stopped breathing.

He knew the stamp. Of course he knew it. He had spent four years of his archivship cataloguing the sealed collections of the old reigns, and there were perhaps nine impressions of this device in all the indexed holdings of the palace, each one guarded behind triple-locked iron, each one assigned a handler who took an oath of silence so binding that two of them, historically, had died rather than break it. A circle of interlocking scales. Within the scales, a crown. And within the crown, small enough that you'd miss it if you didn't know to look, a single letter that no herald had ever been able to identify: a character from a script that predated the kingdom itself.

"The Wyrmscale Press," Sef said quietly. "Where did this come back *from*?"

"I don't know." The clerk's voice was steady, but their fingers weren't. "A private collection, dissolved. The guild bought the lot by weight. Paper is paper. Most of it was tax rolls and love letters. This was at the bottom of a crate."

"And you opened it."

"I told you. I opened it anyway."

Something in the way they said it—*anyway*, with a small defiant weight, the word of a person who has built a whole life out of small transgressions—made Sef look at them properly for the first time. And looking, he recognized something else, something under the ink stains and the careful blankness: fear. Not the fear of a thief. The fear of someone who has read something true and cannot unread it.

"May I?" He didn't wait for permission. His hands were already opening the book, and some distant part of him noted that this was exactly the kind of behavior that had ended his career the first time—touching what he hadn't been cleared to touch—but the larger part of him was reading.

The book was a ledger. Or it wore a ledger's clothes: columns, dates, entries in a hand that changed across decades as keepers died and were replaced. But the entries weren't debts. They were observations. Weather. Harvests. Names of children born in riverside parishes. And threaded through them, every few pages, lines in a different ink—a darker ink, an ink Sef had only ever seen described in treatises on forbidden bindings—

Prophecy. Century-old prophecy, written forward, dated like a diary of a future.

His mouth had gone dry. "How much have you read?"

"To the last written page."

"Which says?"

The clerk's jaw tightened. "It has tonight's date on it."

The rain filled the silence, drumming on the annex's tin roof. Somewhere in the back, Oskit's dice clicked against each other, idle, rhythmic.

"And," the clerk continued, and here the steadiness finally cracked, just at the edge, "the last line names someone. Mid-sentence. It stops mid-sentence, like the writer was interrupted, or like the rest hasn't happened yet." They swallowed. "It names me. My birth name. A name I haven't used since I was sixteen years old, that I buried so thoroughly I sometimes forget it was ever mine, and it's sitting in a hundred-year-old book in your— in *this* shop, dated tonight."

Sef closed the book slowly. Very slowly. Because his hands wanted to shake and he would not let them.

There were protocols for this. There had been protocols for everything, in the palace—chains of custody, sealed reports, the quiet machinery by which dangerous knowledge traveled upward to people who decided what the kingdom was allowed to know about itself. He had followed those protocols for eleven years. Then he had found a gap in the coronation records—a gap exactly one king wide, a reign that had been excised so cleanly that even the excision had been excised—and he had followed protocol, and reported it, and three weeks later he had been disgraced for *falsifying* records that he had merely noticed were false.

So. No protocols. Protocols were how institutions ate people politely.

"What do you want from me?" he asked.

"I want to know what the seal means. I want to know who wrote this. I want to know why the guild bought a crate of paper by weight and didn't notice—or did notice—that the bottom of it was a century-old book that knows my name." The clerk met his eyes then, fully, for the first time, and Sef saw the thing underneath the clerk-mask: anger. Good, clean anger, banked like a coal. "And I want to know why asking these questions makes my hands shake, when I've handled debt-books that took people's whole lives for a winter's grain and never shook once."

"You're shaking because the guild didn't write this," Sef said. "The guild collects. It doesn't predict. Whatever made this book is older than the guild, and the guild is afraid of it, and when institutions are afraid of something, they don't destroy it. They *acquire* it." He tapped the cover, beside the scales-and-crown. "This press operated under direct royal warrant. Pre-Interregnum. Its output was classified above the Lord Chamberlain's clearance—which is to say, above mine, and I was the youngest senior archivist in the history of the office. Nine surviving volumes. Each one catalogued with a warning I was ordered to copy verbatim and never translate." He recited it from memory, because of course he had; he remembered everything, that had always been his gift and his ruin:

*"Do not read aloud. Do not read in the presence of the crowned. Do not index by name."*

The clerk had gone very still. "Why not by name?"

"Because the books are accurate." Sef heard his own voice flatten, go archival, the tone he used when the truth was too large for inflection. "That's what nobody understood about the Wyrmscale Press. It wasn't fortune-telling. Fortune-telling is fraud with good lighting. These books *record*. Something wrote down what happens before it happens, the way a customs officer writes down cargo. And if your name is in it, then whatever it describes isn't a possibility. It's an inventory item. You're already counted."

"That's not comforting."

"It isn't meant to be. It's meant to be precise." He turned the book toward the lamp, angling the final page so the light caught the last line, the unfinished line. He did not read the name aloud. Some instincts survived disgrace intact. "The interruption bothers me more than the naming. Books like this don't stop. Unless—"

"Unless what?"

"Unless the stopping *is* the content." He straightened. "Unless tonight is where the record ends because tonight is where the record gets decided. Written futures are still futures. Something can meet them. Something can change them." He paused. "Or be changed by them."

Outside, the rain thickened, and the annex's single lamp guttered, and for a moment the two of them stood in half-darkness over a book that knew too much, and Sef felt the old vertigo of his palace years rise in him—the sense of standing at the edge of an archive whose bottom floor no map admitted to.

Then the lamp steadied, and the clerk spoke, practical again, mask reassembling: "What's your price?"

Sef laughed despite himself. "You're guild-trained. Everything's a transaction."

"In the guild, everything *is* a transaction. Outside it, everything pretends not to be. I prefer honesty." But there was something else in their face now, something almost hopeful, badly hidden. "Name it. Favors, coin, forgery work—I'm told you're good at forgery work."

"I'm good at *authentication*," Sef said. "Forgery is authentication with ambition." He considered. What did he want? Coin—he needed it, the annex paid in credit at Oskit's cousin's pawnshop. Safety—he'd given up expecting it. What he wanted, what he had wanted since the tribunal, since the wet-gravel voice reading his name off the rolls, was—

"Access," he said. "When this is done—if we survive whatever tonight is—you get me into the guild's deep stacks. The ones below the waterline. I know they exist. Every institution has a floor it doesn't put on its own maps, and the guild's maps are the best-forged in the city, which means the real floors are *very* well hidden. I want to see what the Ledger Guild does with everything it collects. All of it. Years, luck, names—" he watched the clerk flinch at *names*, filed that away, "—whatever else. One night. Unsupervised."

"That's not a small price."

"No. But you're not buying a small thing. You're buying a disgraced royal archivist, and the disgrace came from exactly one skill: I notice what institutions erase." He held out his hand. "Do we have terms?"

The clerk looked at the hand. Looked at him. And Sef understood that they were doing the same calculation he was—weighing whether the other person was a trap, an agent, a liability—and that underneath the calculation was the same raw, stupid hope. Two people who had each fled an institution, meeting in the annex where institutions sent their secrets to die, recognizing each other the way fugitives recognize fugitives: not by face, but by the specific quality of the flinch.

"My name's Ada," the clerk said. "My *guild* name. Don't ask for the other one. It's in the book, and I'd like at least one day where it isn't."

"Sef." He shook the offered hand. Ink met ink. "Formerly of the Royal Archive. Currently of nowhere, which has excellent security."

Ada almost smiled. Almost. "Tonight," they said. "The date is tonight. Which means whatever the book recorded starts—" they glanced at the annex's grim little window, where the rain was turning the lamplight of the street to smears, "—soon. Maybe already."

"Then we read it properly," Sef said, and felt the old hunger light in his chest like a lamp being carried downstairs, floor by floor, toward the parts of the building that were never on any map. "Not here. Here has ears, and Oskit sells information to both sides of every conflict in the city. My rooms. I have three years of confiscated indexes hidden under my floorboards, and if your name is in a Wyrmscale volume, then there will be cross-references, and cross-references mean context, and context—"

"Context means leverage," Ada finished.

"Context means *truth*," Sef said. "Leverage is just what truth looks like when institutions are holding the other end."

They gathered the book, wrapped it, hid it inside Ada's coat. At the door, the clerk paused, hand on the latch, rain roaring beyond.

"One more question," Ada said. "The warning. *Do not read in the presence of the crowned.* Why would a book care who's listening?"

Sef looked at the shape of the hidden volume beneath the grey apron, at the ink-stained hands, at the fear and fury of someone who had come asking questions and was about to get answers that would rearrange their life.

"Because the crowned," he said, "is probably the subject."

Ada opened the door onto the rain, and the cold air rushed in carrying the smell of the river and the far-off bells of the palace quarter ringing the hour—and Sef, pulling up his hood, counted the bells and felt the bottom drop out of his evening.

Nine o'clock.

Three hours until midnight. Three hours until the date on the last page became the present tense.

"Walk fast," he said. "And whatever you do—don't read the last line aloud until I tell you to."
