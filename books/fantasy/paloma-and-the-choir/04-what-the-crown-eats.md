## 4. What the Crown Eats

The archive annex smelled of tallow and old lies.

Sef had worked in the royal archive for nineteen years, first as a junior collator with ink under his fingernails and a future, later—after the disgrace—as a ghost who shelved things no one asked for. The black-market annex was where the palace sent books it wanted forgotten without the expense of burning them. He knew every shelf by its rot. He could tell you which crate held tax ledgers from the reign of the old king's grandfather, which one held love letters confiscated from a duchess, which one held nothing at all because the contents had been "withdrawn for study," a phrase that meant eaten.

So when the hedge-witch's runaway came to his counter at the hour when the lamps were trimmed and the guards changed, carrying a book wrapped in her shawl like an infant, Sef did what he always did with dangerous things.

He pretended not to see it.

"You want a valuation," he said. "Fine. Put it down. Don't open it."

"I need someone who can read it."

"Everyone can read."

"Not this." Ada Brightwater—the guild called her Clerk Nine-Sixteen, but Sef had spent three days pulling records after she'd first appeared at his counter, and he had found the name buried under two layers of redaction, which told him more than the name itself—set the book on the scarred oak anyway. "It's sealed. Look at the spine. There's a wax seal under the leather, and I've seen that seal before, in the guild's charter room, and I'm not supposed to have seen it, and neither are you."

Sef looked at the book.

He wished, afterward, that he hadn't. But looking was his whole ruined profession. The seal pressed into the vellum beneath the rebacked leather showed a crown resting on an open book—a crown *feeding* on the book, if you read the iconography honestly, roots of gold sinking into pages. He had catalogued that seal once, early in his career, on documents stored in the Restricted Vault beneath the palace, the ones even the First Archivist needed written permission to touch. Documents bearing dates. Documents that arrived already catalogued, as though they'd come from somewhere that knew the archive better than the archive knew itself.

"Where did you get this?" he asked.

"The guild's returns shelf. It came back tonight with a batch of collected debts. A century overdue." Her voice stayed flat, clerk-flat, but her hands didn't. "The last entry is dated today. My name is in it."

Sef picked up the trimmer knife he used for cutting binding cord and set it down again, deliberately, where she could see it. A small honesty between professionals.

"Then we don't read it here," he said. "We read it below."

* * *

The sub-basement of the annex had been a wine cellar before the palace grew over it, and it still kept the cool, dead air of a place where things were stored against their will. Sef lit three lamps instead of one, not for light but for witness. Then he laid the book on the stone table and cut the seal.

The wax parted like skin.

Inside, the text was in Old Continuance—the ceremonial script used for coronation oaths and founding charters, a language designed to sound older than it was. Sef had translated coronation records for six years before his fall. His Latin-of-the-court, his superiors had called it, approvingly, right up until the day he'd pointed out that a certain foundational document had been forged, and the forgery went all the way up, and perhaps they should tell someone. They had told someone. They had told him to pack his desk.

"This is a prophecy cycle," he said, turning pages. "But not folk prophecy. Folk prophecy rhymes. This is *administrative* prophecy—it has marginalia. Cross-references. Someone maintained this book the way you'd maintain a ledger." He glanced up. "Which means whoever wrote it expected it to be checked against reality. Annually. Like accounts."

Ada stood across the table with her arms folded tight, a woman holding herself together with her own elbows. "Read the last entry."

He read it.

*On the night the fire gutters low, the unbound name shall surface through paper. What was paid into the dark shall be demanded back. The crown shall know its keeper's hand, and the keeper's hand shall know the price.*

Beneath it, in a different ink, older and browner: *Ada Brightwater.*

And then the sentence simply stopped, mid-phrase, the rest of the page blank—not worn blank, not damaged, but *reserved*, the way a ledger reserves lines for entries yet to be posted.

"That's my name," Ada said. Not a question. She said it the way people say the names of the dead.

"It's your birth name," Sef said. "Your guild registration lists a different one. So do the city rolls. Someone buried 'Brightwater' under redaction in four separate archives, and did it competently, which costs money." He closed the book gently, the way you close a door on a sleeping animal. "You didn't just run away from your master. Someone erased you. And now a hundred-year-old book has un-erased you, on schedule, to the day."

The lamp flames leaned in a draft from the stairwell. Neither of them moved.

"Tell me about the seal," Ada said.

* * *

Sef told her, and telling it aloud in the cold made it worse than knowing it alone ever had.

"A century ago, the old king died without an heir—or that's the story. The record shows a coronation eight days later. The new king was crowned with a crown that appears in no inventory before that date. No smith's mark. No treasury receipt. Crowns are accounted for, Clerk; they're treasure; they're audited. This one simply *begins*. And from that date forward, the archive begins to change character."

"How?"

"Gaps." He pulled a ledger off the wall rack—one of his own private indexes, compiled during nineteen years of noticing what wasn't there. "Every institution keeps records of its own losses. Wars, plagues, fires. But look at the pattern after the coronation. Records don't burn. They *thin*. Whole categories go quiet. Grief-censuses. Mourning registers. The lists of war-dead that parishes used to keep so widows could claim pensions. All of it tapering off within twenty years of the coronation, smoothly, like a fever breaking." He tapped the page. "A kingdom doesn't stop grieving on purpose. Something stopped it *for* us."

Ada had gone very still. "The fisherman," she said.

"What fisherman?"

"Tonight. Before I found the book. I entered a payment. Seven years of luck, for one safe voyage. That's a standard exchange—we take the luck out of his next seven years and hold it, and he gets home safe once." Her voice had developed a hairline crack. "I've done nine hundred of those entries. Maybe more. Luck, first words, the smell of a dead parent's coat, the memory of a wedding—I thought—" She stopped. Started again, flatter. "I thought the guild held them. In trust. That debts were debts, and holding was neutral."

Sef looked at her for a long moment. He had spent his career among people who believed records were neutral, and he had learned the hard way that neutrality is a costume power wears.

"Clerk Brightwater. Where does the guild send its holdings?"

"I don't know. Clerks post entries. The vaults are above our grade."

"But you've seen the transfer schedules."

She hesitated. Then, because she was honest in the way of people who had never been allowed the luxury of lying well: "Quarterly. Sealed wagons. Palace escort."

"Palace escort," Sef repeated. "For a private guild's collateral." He opened the prophecy book again, to the middle sections he'd been circling all evening, and turned it toward her. "Now read this with me. Because I think we're about to ruin each other's lives."

* * *

The passage took him an hour to unpick, and he would spend the rest of his life wishing he'd been slower.

*The crown is not worn. The crown wears.* That was the opening line, and it was not metaphor. The Old Continuance distinguished carefully between *worn-by* and *wears*—between a thing carried and a thing that carries you—and the grammar here was unambiguous. Whatever had been set on the king's head a century ago had not been jewelry. It had been a colonization.

*It feeds on what the fire once illuminated,* the text continued, and here Sef had to cross-reference three other passages to understand that "the fire" was not a hearth or a beacon but something singular, capitalized in the way scripture capitalizes: *the last light, the last breath given shape, the flame that remembers everything it shines on.* A dragon. He wrote the word in his notes and then sat looking at it. Dragons were nursery stories, heraldic furniture, the beasts on the old king's banner. But the prophecy treated the dragon's death not as an ending but as a *condition*—a vast slow combustion still ongoing, a fire that burned on memory the way other fires burned on wood.

And the crown ate from that fire. And the fire, guttering, needed feeding. And the feeding—

"Look at the accounting language," he said hoarsely. "Here, and here, and here. *Harvest. Tithe. Collected sums. Held in trust against the final settlement.* Brightwater, this isn't a sorcerer's rant. This is a *charter*. Whoever bound the crown to the king built a collection system to keep it fed. Regular payments. Denominated in—" he made himself say it— "in memories. In luck. In years. In names."

The silence in the cellar had weight now, like water rising.

"The Ledger Guild," Ada said. Barely a sound.

"Was founded eleven months after the coronation," Sef said. "I checked the charter while you were walking here from the city. Its stated purpose is arbitration of impossible debts. Its actual function, per this text, is harvest. Every entry you've ever posted—every seven years of luck, every first kiss sold for rent, every grief pawned by a family that couldn't afford to feel it—that wasn't held in a vault, Clerk. It was delivered. Quarterly. Under palace escort. To the thing on the king's head."

He watched her take it. He had broken bad news to archivists and nobles and once, catastrophically, to the First Archivist herself, and he knew the stages: denial, anger, the search for procedural error. Ada skipped all of it. She went straight to arithmetic, because she was a clerk, and clerks do sums even while drowning.

"Nine hundred entries," she said. "At least. Over six years. If each entry averages—" She couldn't finish. Her hands flattened on the stone table as though steadying it. "I did the deliveries. Me. My neat little hand. I *fed* it."

"You were also hired by it," Sef said quietly. "There's a difference, and the difference matters. You didn't know. The guild made sure you didn't know. That's not innocence, exactly, but it's not guilt either. It's—" he searched for the archival term— "it's being an instrument. Instruments get cleaned. People get tried. We'll figure out which you are later."

"Generous of you."

"I'm not being generous. I'm being frightened." He said it plainly because fear hidden curdles into cruelty, and he had watched it happen in the archive for years. "If the prophecy named you—if the book reserved a line for your name a century before you were born—then you're not a random clerk who stumbled onto this. The system *expects* you. Which means the guild expects you. Which means by tomorrow morning, possibly by now, someone above your grade knows that a returned volume surfaced on your shift, and that the clerk who handled it has a buried name that matches a restricted text."

Ada's face did something complicated and then settled into the clerk-flat mask again, but he saw the mask cost her more than it used to.

"So I run."

"If you run, you're a fugitive with no allies and no records, and the guild owns the records." Sef began wrapping the prophecy book in oilcloth, his hands steady because his hands always were; it was the rest of him shaking. "If you stay, you're a debt coming due. Either way, we have maybe a day. But there's a third option, and it's the reason I'm doing this instead of reporting you, which would have been the sane choice."

She looked at him. Waiting. She was good at waiting; the guild had trained it into her.

"The prophecy says *the crown shall know its keeper's hand.* Keeper. Not wearer, not maker—keeper. Somewhere in this kingdom is a person whose job, whose whole inherited life, is tending the fire the crown feeds on. The text references a shrine, a snowline, a lineage of keepers going back past the coronation." He tied the knot. "Find the keeper, and we find out what the crown actually is, what it fears, and how a thing that eats memories can be starved. That's the third option. It's a terrible option. It involves mountains."

"And why are *you* doing this?" Ada asked. "What does the disgraced archivist get?"

Sef considered lying, decided against it, and gave her the truth in the flattest voice he owned.

"Nineteen years ago I proved a founding document was forged, and the palace destroyed me for it, and I have spent every year since shelving books nobody reads and telling myself the record would outlive the lie. Tonight I learn the lie isn't one document. It's the whole archive. It's a century of thinned records and missing grief and a kingdom that forgot how to mourn because mourning was *harvested*." He lifted the wrapped book. "I don't get anything, Clerk Brightwater. I get to stop being furniture. That's my price. That's all I want."

For the first time since she'd walked into the annex, Ada almost smiled. It was a thin, cold, exhausted thing, but it was real.

"Then we understand each other," she said. "Because I want my name back."

They climbed the stairs into the lamplit annex, two people carrying a book that had waited a hundred years for them, and Sef was reaching for his cloak when he saw it: a slip of guild stationery on his counter, weighted under a coin, placed there by hands unknown while they'd been below.

The message was brief, in a beautiful bureaucratic hand:

*Clerk Nine-Sixteen is recalled for audit. Effective immediately. The Guildmaster thanks the archivist for his discretion, and reminds him that discretion is also a debt.*

Below it, in smaller script, as if added as an afterthought:

*We know you have the volume.*

* * *

Outside, rain had begun to fall on the palace district, soft and indifferent, washing the streets of a city that no longer remembered what it had lost. Sef folded the note into quarters, very precisely, and put it in his pocket beside his old, revoked credentials.

"We leave tonight," he said, "before the audit finds us."

Ada was already pulling her shawl tight. "Where?"

"Toward the snowline," Sef said. "To find the keeper—before the guild finds us first."
