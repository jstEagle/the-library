## 5. A Debt Called Ada

The summons came at the worst possible hour: the hour when the guild's lamps burned lowest and the clerks moved through the stacks like moths who had forgotten what they were looking for.

A junior runner—twelve, maybe thirteen, with ink on her knuckles and fear in her posture—appeared at my desk without sound. That was the first wrong thing. Nobody came to my desk. I had spent six years making certain of it. My corner of the Counting Room was positioned precisely in the blind spot between two shelving columns, under a lamp that flickered just enough to discourage loiterers, beside a drawer of misfiled entries nobody wanted to claim. People brought their questions to Brannic or to Mistress Corvel. People forgot I was there within a minute of looking at me. It was the finest work of my life.

"Entry clerk Brightwater," the girl recited, staring at a folded slip rather than at me, as if reading it might make it true by force. "The Guildmaster requests your presence. Third bell."

"He's never requested anything from me," I said. "There must be an error. Check the ledger."

"The ledger says you're to go." She set the slip down and fled, and I understood then that she hadn't been sent to persuade me. She'd been sent because sending someone was part of the procedure, and the procedure had already decided how this night would end.

I looked at the slip for a long time. Heavy stock, cream-colored, sealed with grey wax—the guild's working seal, not its ceremonial one. The handwriting was Vane's own: small, upright, economical, the hand of a man who never wrote a word he couldn't later point to as evidence. *Third bell. Do not bring work.*

*Do not bring work.* As if I might have hidden a ledger inside my coat like a stolen loaf.

The rain had started an hour before, a fine persistent drizzle that turned the guild's courtyard into a sheet of hammered pewter. Through the high windows of the corridor I watched it while I walked, and I made myself walk slowly, because a person walking slowly toward something is still, technically, a free person. I counted the bells. I counted my breaths. I did not count the ways out of the building, because I had memorized those years ago—four, if you included the coal chute—and knowing they were there was enough. Knowing the exits exist is most of what freedom is, when freedom is only a thing you keep in reserve.

The Guildmaster's office occupied the top floor of the tower, above the vaults, above the counting rooms, above all the floors where people traded pieces of their lives for pieces of other people's lives. The stair up was marble. Marble, in a guild whose founding charter promised *frugality in all things but accuracy*. Someone had chosen that stone deliberately. You were meant to feel the weight of the institution under your feet before you ever reached the door.

Vane was standing at his window when I entered, hands clasped behind his back, watching the rain. He didn't turn around.

"Do you know why I keep this office at the top?" he said.

"No, Guildmaster."

"Because everything below me can be replaced. Every clerk, every ledger, every debt. If the building burned tonight, we would be operational again within the year, because the guild is not the building and not the people. The guild is the *system*. Systems survive anything." He turned then. He was smaller than I remembered from the one ceremony I'd attended, a narrow man with a dry face, like paper left too long near a lamp. His eyes were the color of old ink. "Sit down, Brightwater."

I sat. There was one chair, placed across the desk from him, and I noted with a professional's eye that it sat lower than his own chair. Everything about the room was arithmetic.

"You filed four hundred and eleven entries last year," he said. "Your error rate is zero. Your overtime is unrecorded, which is itself a kind of error, though a useful one. You have requested no advancement in six years. You have declined it twice, quietly, through intermediaries." He opened a folder on the desk. "You are, by every measure, our best invisible asset. And yet."

"And yet," I said, because he paused as if expecting me to fill the silence, and I refused.

"And yet last night you touched a restricted volume." He slid a page across the desk. A copy, in a clerk's hand, of a shelf record: *Codex Ashfall, returned from private collection, reshelved, accessed 11th hour, E. Brightwater.* "Tell me what you read."

"I shelved it. That's my work. Shelving."

"Brightwater." He said my name the way a man sets down a heavy object—carefully, so as not to break either the object or the floor. "I am going to tell you some things now, and you are going to listen, because the alternative is that I stop telling you things, and you do not want to learn what happens to people about whom the guild stops telling things. Are we agreed?"

I said nothing. In the guild, silence was consent. Silence was also, sometimes, the only property you had left.

"The Codex Ashfall," Vane said, "was written ninety-eight years ago by a seer named Ondrel, who died badly and was right anyway. Its final entry bears tomorrow's date. It names a person. The guild has known of this entry since before you were born. We have been waiting for it the way farmers wait for rain—with preparation, and without surprise."

My mouth had gone dry. "It names a person."

"It does." He opened the folder fully now, and I saw pages I recognized—not the prophecy itself, but commentary, correspondence, marginalia in a dozen dead hands. A century of clerks worrying at the same sentence like dogs at a bone. "And here is what I find interesting, Brightwater. When the volume passed through your hands, you could have reported it. Protocol requires it. Instead you finished your shift. You went home. You walked past three archive annexes on your way to the river district this morning—I have the times—you did not enter any of them. And then, at midday, you entered one."

The rain filled the pause. Somewhere below us, a cart rattled over cobblestones, carrying goods to market, carrying nothing that mattered.

"You went to the palace annex," Vane said. "The black-market one, off Cordwainer Street, run by a man named Hesk who sells provenance to anyone with coin. You asked questions about a seal. And the disgraced archivist Sef—" he pronounced the name with the faint distaste of a man handling spoiled meat, "—who has been drinking himself bankrupt in that annex for three years, told you what the seal meant. Or began to. Am I accurate?"

"How do you know all this?"

"Because the guild pays Hesk," Vane said simply. "We pay half the informants in this city. That is not corruption; that is redundancy. A system survives anything, Brightwater, because it has already survived everything, including its own people." He closed the folder. "Now. The part that concerns you."

He stood and went to a cabinet behind his desk—a tall iron-banded thing, older than the furniture, older probably than the building—and drew from it a single ledger. Not a guild ledger. The binding was wrong for ours: tooled leather, a spine stamped with a sigil I knew better than my own heartbeat, because I had spent my childhood watching it burn on the back door of a hedge-witch's cottage.

A circle. A flame. A moth rising through both.

"This," said Vane, "is a collector's register. Kept by one Madam Ferrow, licensed hedge-witch, operating in the eastern fens, twenty-two years ago. She collected on behalf of the guild. Memories, mostly. Childhood memories are prized—they're clean, unencumbered, no debts attached. She took them as collateral against her own obligations." He laid the register open on the desk and turned it to face me. "Do you see the entry?"

I saw it. Of course I saw it. I had seen it every night for twenty years behind my eyelids, in the shape of the hole it left.

*One (1) name, birth-given, surrendered voluntarily by ward of the collector. One (1) memory-set, early childhood, seven items, surrendered under standard terms. Collateral status: active. Designation: ADA.*

Not my name. My *designation*. The word I had been called before I chose another, before I buried the first one so deep I sometimes needed a moment to remember which face went with which. Ada. Just Ada. A name with nothing behind it, a door painted on a wall.

"That's not possible," I heard myself say. "That register should have been destroyed. She said—she told me when I ran, she said the terms lapsed—"

"The terms never lapse. That is the entire principle on which this institution stands." Vane sat down again, folding his dry hands. "When you fled her, Brightwater, you did not escape the guild. You applied to it. Did you truly believe a girl of sixteen with no papers, no coin, and no history could simply walk into the Ledger Guild and be hired? No one hires the undocumented. No one *sees* them. But we took you in, gave you a bed, a wage, a name short enough to lose. Because we had been waiting for you. The Codex named you ninety-eight years before you were born, and Madam Ferrow's register confirmed the designation, and so when you appeared at our doors we did what any careful institution does with an asset of known value."

He smiled. It was the worst thing I had seen in years, worse than the prophecy, worse than the hole in my childhood, because it was patient. It was the smile of a man who had never once been surprised.

"We hid you," he said. "In plain sight. The best place to keep a thing is somewhere no one looks, and no one looks at a nobody. Six years, Brightwater. You thought your invisibility was your own achievement. I confess I've enjoyed letting you believe it. But it was always ours. Every unremarkable day, every clerk who forgot your face within a minute—it was arrangement. Custody. You have been in the guild's keeping since the day you arrived, and you remain in our keeping until the date passes."

"Until the date passes," I repeated. "And then?"

"And then, presumably, whatever the Codex describes will occur, and the guild will discharge its obligation regarding you, and you may return to being nobody on your own recognizance." He said it lightly, the way a man says *and then the tide goes out*, describing a thing far larger than himself that he has made peace with serving. "Until then, I require your cooperation. Tomorrow evening you will present yourself at the guild's main hall. There will be documents. You will sign a formal acknowledgment of your designation and your custodial status. It changes nothing practical. It merely makes the paperwork honest."

"And if I don't sign?"

"Then the acknowledgment will be signed on your behalf, with a mark, witnessed by two clerks, and filed. It is equally valid. The signature is a courtesy." He tilted his head. "You seem distressed. I want to be clear: nothing bad is happening to you. Nothing bad has *ever* happened to you here. You were fed, sheltered, employed, protected. Compare that to the fens. Compare that to what Madam Ferrow would have sold you for eventually, because she would have—collateral always gets called in eventually. We are not cruel, Brightwater. We are *accurate*. There is a difference, and the difference is that cruelty ends."

I stood up. My legs worked, which surprised me. The room seemed very far away, as if I were hearing Vane through water, through rain, through six years of a life that had never actually belonged to me.

"I won't sign," I said.

"Noted," said Vane, and made a small mark in a notebook, and that smallness—that bureaucratic tininess of it—did more to terrify me than anything else he'd said. I was a line item. I had always been a line item. Even my refusal was going into the books.

"One more thing," he said, as I reached the door. "The archivist. Sef. Whatever he told you, whatever he thinks he knows—he is a drunk who was expelled for falsifying provenance, and everything he touches becomes suspect. If you have involved him, uninvolve him. For his sake. Assets that associate with liabilities become liabilities." A pause. "Goodnight, Brightwater. Sleep well. You have a long day tomorrow."

I walked down the marble stairs. I did not run. Running draws eyes, and the whole architecture of my survival was built on drawing none. I walked past the Counting Room, past my desk with its flickering lamp, past the drawer of misfiled entries, and I felt the building differently now—not a refuge, not a shell, but a mouth. All those floors beneath me, all those ledgers, all those lives filed in other people's handwriting. I had lived six years inside the thing that ate memories, and I had kept its books, and I had been *proud* of my neat figures.

Outside, the rain had thickened. It came down in ropes now, hammering the courtyard, drowning the lamps. I stood in it and let it soak me, because the cold was simple and simple was what I needed. Betrayal does its real damage slowly, I think. In the first minutes you only feel the shape of it, the way you feel a broken bone before the pain arrives.

They had known. Before I was born, they had known. My whole flight from Ferrow's cottage—three days walking, feverish, eating nettles—had been not an escape but a delivery. I had carried myself to them like a parcel, addressed and expected. And the invisibility, the beautiful invisibility I had crafted stroke by stroke, year by year, the lamp that flickered, the corner between the shelves, the face nobody held onto—

Arrangement. Custody. Someone else's design, wearing my life like a borrowed coat.

I thought of Sef, in his smoke-stinking annex, telling me about the seal with shaking hands. *Assets that associate with liabilities.* They'd watch him now. Maybe they already had men outside his door.

I thought of the Codex, and the sentence that ended mid-word, and the date that was tomorrow.

Four exits. Coal chute, cellar door, the postern by the drying yard, the front gate. I had promised myself I'd never need them. But a promise made to yourself by a person who didn't exist isn't worth the paper it isn't written on.

I went out through the drying yard, past the lines of ledgers hung to air, their pages fluttering in the wet wind like the wings of trapped birds. The postern gate's lock was old and I had oiled it two winters ago, on a night I'd told myself was only prudence. Prudence. Arrangement. Maybe even that had been foreseen.

The rain took the city apart as I walked—signs, gutters, the shapes of buildings, all of it dissolving into grey noise. I had no plan. I had a name that wasn't mine, a designation in a dead woman's register, and a date, and somewhere across the city a disgraced archivist who owed me answers and whom I had just, by the simple act of existing, made into a target.

Behind me, the guild tower rose black against the rain, its top-floor window still lit.

Vane would find the empty chair soon. He would make a note. Somewhere in the great machine of the guild, a small mark would be entered against my name, and collectors would be assigned, and the system would begin, without anger or haste, to retrieve its property.

I put my head down and walked into the dark, toward Cordwainer Street, toward the one liability in this city who might still help me—and prayed the rain would fall hard enough, long enough, to wash away the sound of my footsteps before they reached him.
