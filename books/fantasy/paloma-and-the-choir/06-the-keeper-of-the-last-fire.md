## 6. The Keeper of the Last Fire

The fire had gone down to the color of an old bruise.

Ysol saw it the moment she came up from the snow tunnel into the shrine's high chamber: a dimming at the heart of the flame, a violet shadow where there should have been gold. She had watched that light for sixty years, and she knew its moods the way a mother knows a child's breathing in the next room. Tonight it breathed wrong.

She set down her bucket of pine resin and stood very still, letting the cold of the mountain settle back into her bones after the climb. The shrine was not warm. It had never been warm. That was the first thing they taught you, before they taught you anything else: you do not keep this fire for heat. You keep it because it is the last of something, and last things must be tended whether or not they are useful.

"Slow down," she told it. Her voice came out cracked from three days of silence. "Nobody's rushing you."

But something was rushing it. Something below the snowline, down in the fat soft city where the river bent and the guild-houses stacked their ledgers like grain. Ysol could not have said how she knew. Keepers did not get visions; that was for saints and frauds. What she got was arithmetic. Sixty years of measuring the flame every dawn and dusk, and she had learned its rate of spending the way a miser learns his own purse. And the rate had changed. In the last season, the fire was burning faster than any natural guttering. Someone was drawing on it. Drinking through a straw, from a hundred miles away.

She knelt on the stone ring and began the morning work. Resin first, a spoonful, no more—the fire took what it needed and no more, and if you gave it too much it flared and spent itself faster, which was the whole temptation of keeping, wasn't it? To feed it. To see it blaze just once more the way it must have blazed when there were others like it, a sky full of them, a world lit from above by wings.

There had been a dragon under this mountain once. Everyone said so, though no one living had seen it, and the order had done its best to make even the saying stop. Ysol had been a girl of nine when they brought her up the stair, and the old keeper then—a man named Corvess, who smelled of tallow and patience—had put her hand flat against the floor of the shrine chamber and made her listen.

"What do you hear?" he'd asked.

"Nothing," she'd said.

"Listen longer."

And after a long while, longer than a child should be asked to hold still, she had heard it: not a sound exactly, but a weight, a vast slow pressure beneath the rock, like the sea heard through a hull. The dragon was dead. Had been dead for centuries. But death, Corvess told her, is not an event. It is a process, and some processes are long enough to outlast nations. The fire above them was not a fire in any honest sense. It was the dragon's dying, still going on. A death so large it burned.

"And our job?" she'd asked.

"Our job is to witness it," Corvess said. "Nothing more. We are not priests. We are not guards. We are witnesses. The worst job in the world, and the most necessary. Somebody has to watch a thing end, or it ends twice."

Ysol was ninety-one now. She had been watching for eighty-two years, and she was tired in a way that sleep did not touch. Her hands had gone to paper over the knuckles. Her eyes were failing at close distances but still good for the flame, which was the only mercy the mountain had ever shown her. She measured it against the iron rod set into the wall—three marks and a finger's width, same as yesterday—and fed it its spoonful of resin, and spoke to it the way she always did, the small litany of names.

Not prayers. Names. Every keeper learned the list: the dragons, all of them, the ones history remembered and the ones only the order remembered. You said their names to the fire each day because a name is the smallest unit of memory there is, cheaper than a candle, and the order had learned—had known for a thousand years, maybe—that things unremembered do not simply fade. They are taken. There was always something that ate what no one watched.

She finished the litany. The violet shadow at the heart of the flame did not go away.

* * *

In the afternoon she went down to the scriptorium, which was one room with one desk and one lamp, because the order had once filled a cloister and now filled a corner. The shelves held what remained of the records: the keepers' journals, unbroken back eleven hundred years, and the books. One book especially. It lived in an iron box behind a false shelf, and Ysol took it out only four times a year, on the quarter days, and read it, and hated herself a little each time for the relief she felt when nothing in it had changed.

Today was not a quarter day. Today she took it out because the fire was spending too fast, and because she was old, and old women are permitted to break their own rules.

*The Book of the Binding*, the spine said, in letters that had been gilded once. Inside, the handwriting changed hands a dozen times across the first pages—this was the record of the last great work of the order, the shame and the necessity both. When the dragons died, their deaths did not die with them. A death that size keeps burning unless it is bound, and so the order had bound it: had woven the dying into a vessel, a shape that could hold the slow catastrophe of a species' ending without letting it spread. They had used a crown. Crowns hold well; crowns are already made to carry more meaning than any object should bear.

They had put it on a king's head, because a king would agree to anything that made him more than a man, and because a crowned head sits in a palace, guarded, stable, predictable. For a century the arrangement had worked. The fire burned slow in its far mountain shrine. The crown sat quiet on its far throne. The order kept the accounts between them.

Until the order discovered that the account could be skimmed.

Ysol turned to the page she dreaded, the marginal note in Corvess's own hand, written the year before he died:

*It has learned to feed. The crown draws memory through the flame as a wick draws oil. Whoever tends the fire will feel the draft. Find the leak in the city. Stop the harvest.*

He had never found the leak. Three keepers after him had looked and failed, and then the order had dwindled to Ysol and two novices who had left—one for love, one for sense—and then to Ysol alone, and looking for a leak in a city of forty thousand souls from a mountain shrine was a task for an army, not an old woman with bad knees.

But she had found something. Last spring, trading resin-baskets in the valley town, she had seen a Ledger Guild clerk buying memories. Not metaphorically. Buying them outright, seven years of luck for one safe voyage, a fisherman signing himself out of his own past with a smile on his face, pleased with the bargain. Ysol had stood in the market square with her baskets and understood, with the particular horror of the very old, that the leak was not a crack in the system. The leak was the system. The guild was not skimming the crown's harvest. The guild *was* the harvest. A hundred years of debts paid in years and names and other impossible things, piped straight up the wick into the crown, and the crown drinking, and the fire here paying the interest.

She had reported it to no one, because there was no one.

Now she opened the book to the last leaf, the prophecy, which the order had received a century ago from a source no keeper had ever identified—a sealed scroll, delivered by a courier who would not say who sent him, written in a hand none of them recognized. It was short. Prophecies usually are; verbosity is a sign of fraud. It named the binding, and the crown, and the fire, and the end of the arrangement, and it gave a date.

Tonight's date. She had checked the almanac four times.

And it gave a name.

Ysol read the name again, though she had read it perhaps two hundred times over the years, and each time it meant nothing, because the person it belonged to had not yet been born when the scroll arrived, and even now was—what? Where? A child grown. An adult somewhere in the world, walking around with a name they might not even use, carrying without knowing it the one quality the rite required: a memory unbound. Unbought. Unharvested. The prophecy was maddeningly thin on explanation—it said only that the ledger of the binding could be read aloud only by a name written in it whose memory had never passed through the crown's hunger—but thinness had never stopped a prophecy from being true.

Ada Brightwater. That was the name. Born to nobody, in nowhere, twenty-some years ago. The order's records said the family had come and gone like weather. No trade, no title, no debts on file anywhere Ysol could find—which, given what she now knew about who kept the files, either meant the person was genuinely free, or meant someone very powerful had scrubbed them clean. She had prayed, in her dry transactional way, for the former.

She closed the book and put it back behind the false shelf, and sat a while in the lamplight with her ruined hands folded.

Eighty-two years of duty, and it came down to this: she could not climb down the mountain. She had tried the lower path in autumn and turned back at the second switchback, her chest seizing, her legs shaking, humiliated and alone with the crows laughing at her. If the prophecy's date was tonight, and the person it named was in the city—or anywhere—then the mountain had to go to them. The order had a way. It was old, and it cost, and she had sworn never to use it except at the end of everything.

The violet shadow in the flame said the end of everything had arrived.

* * *

She climbed to the fire chamber one last time that evening, slower than in the morning, and knelt at the ring, and did not begin the litany. Instead she reached into the pouch at her belt and took out the charm.

It was a moth, worked in copper wire and ash-glass, no bigger than her thumb, and inside its glass body was a sliver of the fire—not flame, which could not be carried, but the next thing down: a spark of the dragon's dying, cooled into a solid gleam like a chip of sunset. The keepers had made six of them, a thousand years ago, for emergencies. Five had been spent. This was the last, and it had waited in its box through the fall of the order, through plague and war and the long forgetting, for an emergency worth its price.

"You'll want wind," she told it. "You'll want a moon. You've got neither, so you'll have to be clever."

Moth-charms flew toward what they sought. That was the whole craft of them—you gave the spark a name to look for, and the spark went, because the dragon's dying wanted, above all things, to be witnessed, and a name was a door. Ysol held the little copper body in her cupped palms and said the name into it, quietly, the way you tell a secret to a child you're sending off alone: *Ada Brightwater.* Then, because names can be buried and hers might be, she added what she knew of the person beneath the name: fled a master, hid among clerks, loved being no one. The spark warmed in her hands at that last part. Even a chip of a dead god's ending, apparently, could appreciate the irony of a seeker who wanted to be unfound.

"I'm sorry," she said, because you should apologize to things you send into danger. "I know you'd rather rest. So would I. But somebody has to watch a thing end, or it ends twice, and I can't make the stairs anymore."

She stood—knees cracking, breath short—and carried the moth to the shrine's eastern window, the one that faced the city, the river, the distant smudge of roofs and smoke where forty thousand people were going about their evenings, spending their memories without knowing they were currency.

The fire behind her guttered. She heard it—the sound was unmistakable, a wet hiss like a candle drowning—and her heart clenched around a grief so old it had worn smooth, the way a pebble wears in a stream. Eighty-two years. She thought of Corvess's hand on her small shoulder. She thought of the weight beneath the floor, the vast slow sea-pressure of the dying, and how she had listened to it every day of her life until listening had become the shape of her soul, and how soon now there would be nothing left to listen to, and no one left alive who knew how.

"Not tonight," she said—to the fire, to the mountain, to whatever was drinking at the far end of the wick. "Not while I'm keeper."

She opened the window. Cold poured in. Below, the world dropped away into blue dusk and snow-shadow, and far off, one light in all that dark was the city, burning its borrowed brightness.

Ysol lifted the moth to her lips, breathed on it once—keeper's blessing, spark to spark—and let it go.

It hung in the freezing air for a heartbeat, wings of copper wire trembling, the ash-glass body kindling from within. Then it turned, found its bearing on some current older than weather, and flew—small and bright and impossibly steady, out over the void, down toward the lights, toward the river bend, toward whoever Ada Brightwater had become.

Ysol watched until her eyes lost it. Then she shut the window, banked the fire with the last of the evening's resin, and sat down on the cold stone to keep her vigil, as she had every night for eighty-two years.

Somewhere below, the moth was flying. Somewhere below, a stranger was about to have their life taken apart by a piece of copper and a dead dragon's longing. And somewhere closer than that—closer every hour—the crown was drinking, and the flame was falling, and the date on the prophecy's last line was arriving one minute at a time.

"Hurry," Ysol told the dark, though she knew the dark did not take requests. "Whoever you are. Whatever you're afraid of. Hurry."
