## 7. Moths and Messages

The flophouse on Candlewick Row charged four coppers a night and asked no questions, which was exactly what it was worth. The bed sagged like an old boat. The blanket smelled of tallow and other people's dreams. Through the wall, someone coughed in the wet, grinding way of a man who had been poor for a long time and expected to stay that way.

Ada lay on top of the blanket with their boots on, counting.

Not money. They had eleven coppers, half a loaf gone hard at one end, and a guild token they should have thrown in the river but hadn't, because throwing it away felt like admitting something. No — they were counting hours. It was past midnight when they'd climbed the stairs. The Guildmaster's summons had been at dusk. Vane's people would expect a clerk to go home, sleep badly, and present themselves at the morning bell, chastened and signable. That gave Ada until dawn, if anyone was even looking. If the Guildmaster had meant what she said. If *property* had been a figure of speech.

It hadn't been a figure of speech. Ada could still hear the word sitting in the air of that green-lit office, patient as ink.

*You are entered in our books, Brightwater. Everything entered is owed.*

They had run from a hedge-witch once, six years ago, over something they still couldn't look at straight-on — a memory with a hole in it, smooth-edged, like a coin worn blank. They had run then without a plan, and it had worked, because nobody looks for a person who makes herself nothing. But the guild made its living finding what people tried to hide. The guild had *invented* hiding, more or less, as a service industry.

So: not dawn. Now.

Ada sat up, and the room tilted, and that was when they saw the moth.

It came through the gap under the door the way light comes under a door — all at once, as if it had been waiting there. It was pale, the color of bone or old paper, and it did not behave like a moth. Moths blunder. This one flew the way a thought arrives: directly, inevitably, stopping an arm's length from Ada's face and hanging there, wings opening and closing with the slow patience of a book being read.

"Go away," Ada whispered. "I'm not — whoever you think I am."

The moth drifted closer.

Heat bloomed in Ada's palm before they understood what was happening. They flinched back against the headboard, but the pain wasn't burning, exactly. It was *writing*. A line drew itself across the meat of their hand, fine as a pen-stroke, then another, crossing into letters. Ada watched their own skin darken into script they recognized with a sick lurch — the looping, formal hand of the old ledgers, the century-old hand, the hand from the prophecy book shelved in the dead stacks where returned volumes went to be forgotten.

The name finished itself. Ada read it upside down, which didn't matter; they'd have known it blindfolded.

Their birth name. The one they had buried under three false names and six years of deliberate grayness. The one that appeared in a book written a hundred years ago under tonight's date, in an entry that ended mid-sentence —

*and the unbound hand shall carry the fire to*

— to what? To where? The sentence just stopped, the way sentences do when the person writing them runs out of time, or nerve, or ink.

The moth settled on the headboard above them, folding its wings like a letter being sealed.

"I don't know what you want," Ada said. Their voice came out steady, which surprised them. Underneath the steadiness everything was shaking. "I'm a clerk. I copy numbers. I'm not anybody's unbound anything."

The moth opened its wings again. On the underside, where the pale scales caught the streetlight through the window, there was a mark: a spiral, coiled tight, like smoke frozen mid-rise. Like fire seen from very far away.

Somewhere below, on Candlewick Row, a door closed. Boots on cobbles — two sets, unhurried, the walk of people being paid by the hour rather than the task. Collectors walked like that. Ada had filed enough of their expense entries to know.

They looked at the moth. The moth looked at Ada, insofar as a thing with no face can look, and Ada got the distinct impression of being measured. Weighed. Found — not sufficient, exactly. Found *necessary*, which was worse.

"If I follow you," Ada said slowly, "where does it go? The road. Where does it end?"

The moth lifted off the headboard, circled the room once — past the sagging bed, past the water stain shaped like a weeping woman, past the window with its view of rooftops going blue-gray toward morning — and came to rest on the latch.

Out. It wanted out. And out meant the mountain road, the one that left the city through the Salt Gate and climbed into country Ada had only ever seen as columns of figures: tolls collected, tithes recorded, luck sold and repurchased. Out meant leaving the only shelter Ada had built since the hedge-witch — mean little shelter, invisible little life, but shelter. Out meant becoming visible again. Findable. *Named.*

Staying meant the guild. Staying meant Vane's green office and a pen already dipped, waiting for Ada's hand. Staying meant discovering, at last and in detail, what a person was worth when everything about her was owed.

The boots on the cobbles stopped outside.

Through the floorboards, the coughing man in the next room rolled over and muttered something in his sleep — a name, maybe, or half of one. Ada found themselves absurdly grateful to him. Somebody in this building still had a name to mutter.

Ada stood. Took the hard loaf. Left the guild token on the pillow, face up, where anyone sent to fetch them would find it first thing — a small defiance, meaningless and enormous. Let them enter *that* in the books. *Asset departed voluntarily. Token surrendered. Whereabouts: carried off by a moth.*

The latch was cold under their burned palm. The name stung when they gripped it, and Ada decided, with a clarity that felt almost giddy, to take that as agreement. Pain was a signature too. They were done signing things they hadn't chosen.

The stairs creaked. The street door stuck, then gave. Cold air, rain-washed and coal-tanged, hit Ada's face, and the two sets of boots turned at the end of Candlewick Row — one of them wearing the gray livery coat, the brass ledger-clasp catching the lamplight — and Ada did the bravest cowardly thing available, which was to walk the other way, quickly, without running, past sleeping stalls and a drunk singing very quietly to himself about somebody named Paloma, until the Salt Gate rose up ahead of them, barred for the night except for the wicket-gate, except for the small door within the large one that always stood open for carters and late travelers and people leaving.

Nobody guards the exit. That was the whole philosophy of the city. Grief comes in; debts come in; only goods go out, and those taxed.

The gate-warden looked up from his brazier, took in Ada's clerk's coat and ink-stained fingers, and lost interest immediately. Nobody looks for a person who makes herself nothing. Ada paid a copper for the road-tax — the last easy transaction of their old life — and stepped through.

Behind them, faint across the rooftops, a bell began to ring. Not the morning bell. Not any hour bell Ada knew. Three strokes, a pause, three strokes — the guild's recall signal, the one that brought every collector in the city back to the counting-house. Except tonight it wouldn't bring them back. Tonight it would send them out.

The moth rose from Ada's shoulder — when had it landed there? — and flew up the dark throat of the mountain road, pale as a page turning, and waited.

"All right," Ada told it. Their voice cracked on the second word and they let it. There was no one to hear, and that, for the first time in six years, was a thing they had chosen. "All right. I'm coming. But I want it known — I want it *entered somewhere* — that this is mine. My debt, my road, my name." They flexed the burned palm, watching the letters catch starlight. "Whatever's at the end of you."

The moth beat its wings once, twice, and went on up into the dark, and Ada Brightwater — Ada *nothing*, Ada *unbound hand*, Ada whatever the century-old sentence intended to finish saying — put the city at their back and followed.

The road climbed. Somewhere above the snowline, a fire was guttering, and it knew their name.

* * *

By full dark-plus-one-hour, Ada had learned three things about the mountain road.

One: it was steeper than any column of figures had ever suggested. Two: the way-side shrines along it were wrong — little stone niches holding votive candles, and every single candle cold, and every niche swept clean of ash, as though no one had lit one in years, as though the lighting of them had been *forgotten how*. Three: the moth did not wait for stragglers. It flew twenty paces ahead, paused, glowed faintly — actually glowed, a heat-shimmer in its wings — and waited with the terrible patience of paperwork.

At the fourth shrine, Ada stopped, because in the niche where a candle should have been there was a scrap of parchment, pinned under a pebble, weathered soft as cloth. They bent close. Old handwriting, the same looping formal hand as the prophecy, as their own palm:

*If you are reading this, the fire is lower than we hoped. Hurry.*

No date. No name. Just that, and beneath it, smaller, pressed so hard the pen had nearly torn through:

*It will ask more than you have. Bring it anyway.*

Ada stood in the middle of the road with the wind pulling at their coat, and laughed — one short, cracked sound with no humor in it at all. Then they folded the note into their pocket beside the hard loaf, flexed their stinging palm, and kept walking, because the moth had already turned the bend above, glowing like a coal someone refused to let go out.

Behind, far below, tiny with distance, lights were moving through the Salt Gate. Not two sets of boots' worth. More like ten.

Ada walked faster.
