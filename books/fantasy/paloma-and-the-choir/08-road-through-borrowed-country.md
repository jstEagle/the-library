## 8. Road Through Borrowed Country

*Ada*

The city let go of us easier than I expected. Cities usually hold you by the collar — rent, debts, the name someone shouts after you down an alley. But Ashfall had already forgotten how to look at people like me. By the time we crossed the north gate, Sef and I were just two more shapes in the gray morning, a disgraced archivist and a runaway clerk walking out of the ledger of the world.

I kept checking over my shoulder anyway.

"No one's following," Sef said, not turning around. He had a way of answering questions I hadn't asked yet, which I would learn to hate and then, eventually, rely on.

"You don't know that."

"I know collectors." He adjusted the strap of his satchel — all he'd salvaged from his old life, besides a forged travel writ and a bitterness he wore like a coat. "They don't chase. They *wait*. Vane knows exactly which road we're on and exactly how fast our legs move. Chasing costs energy. Waiting costs nothing. We're not being followed, Brightwater. We're being *scheduled*."

That was worse. I walked faster anyway.

The road north climbed through country I didn't know, which was the point. Every mile put stone between me and the Guildhall, between me and the Guildmaster's voice saying *you were never invisible; you were held*. Three days out of the city, the rain turned to sleet, and the sleet turned to snow, and the world went quiet in a way I told myself was peaceful.

It wasn't peaceful. It was *thin*. I couldn't have said why until we came to the first village.

It sat in a river bend, maybe forty houses, a shrine with its door off its hinges. The innkeeper was a broad woman named Marta who fed us barley soup and asked nothing about us, because asking questions is how debts start — old habit in her voice, though she wouldn't have known why.

We talked. That's what travelers do. And somewhere in the second bowl of soup I said something careless — "My mother made soup like this" — and Marta lit up and said her father used to make it, before the war took him.

"The war," Sef said carefully. "Which war?"

Marta frowned at him. "The war," she said, as if there were only one war in the history of the world, as if wars were weather.

"Your father died in it," Sef pressed gently. "Do you remember when?"

"He..." She looked into her soup. Her face did something terrible then — not grief, but the *searching for* grief, the face of someone patting their pockets for keys they can feel should be there. "He died. In the war. I know that the way you know you've got ten fingers. But I can't—" She laughed, embarrassed. "Isn't that funny? I can't see his face."

She could describe his hands. She remembered he whistled while he worked. Everything except him.

Sef thanked her for the soup and paid too much, and outside, in the cold, he said: "Count them."

"Count what?"

"How many things she couldn't find. His face. His voice. The year. The name of the war. Who fought whom. Why." He held up fingers as he listed them. "Five gaps in one conversation with one woman. Now multiply her by every person in that village, and that village by every village on this road."

"That's just... time," I said. "Time wears memory down. My master always said—"

"Time wears memory down slowly, and unevenly, and leaves the shape of the wound. Time doesn't take a man's whole face and leave his hands." Sex's breath steamed. "Something drank from this place. Recently. Repeatedly. Like a tap."

And I thought of my ledgers back in Ashfall. Neat columns. Seven years of luck for one safe voyage. A first kiss for a gambling debt. A dead brother's laugh, entered against a loan, marked *paid in full*.

"I recorded those entries," I said.

"Yes," Sef said. "You did."

He didn't say it cruelly. Somehow that was worse.

* * *

*Sef*

Here is the thing they don't tell you about falling from an institution: you don't fall once. You keep falling, a little, every day, whenever you reach for a habit that no longer has anything underneath it.

In the royal archive, my word had been law. A document existed if Sef catalogued it. When I was cast out — for asking who authorized the destruction of four hundred years of coronation records, a question apparently worth my career — I learned that authority lives entirely in other people's belief in it. Strip that away and an archivist is just a man who remembers where things are.

On the mountain road, remembering where things are became survival craft again, and Ada Brightwater became my student whether either of us wanted it or not.

"Show me your hands," I said, the fourth night, in a shepherd's hut with a fire going.

"They're hands."

"Show me anyway."

They held them out — ink-stained, chapped, quick fingers. Clerk's hands. Good hands for small precise work.

"Now," I said, "when you copied an entry at the guild, what did you do first?"

"Read it."

"*Before* that."

Ada frowned. "Washed the nib?"

"Before that. Before the pen ever touched paper." I leaned forward. "You looked at the document itself and asked three questions. Who made this, why does it exist, and what does it want me to believe. Every document is an argument. Every ledger is a confession. The guild taught you the mechanics of copying and hoped you'd never ask the third question."

"And the third question is—"

"What it wants you to believe. Because whoever controls that controls you." I pulled a scrap of birch bark from the firewood and a stub of charcoal. "So. Lesson one of staying alive on this road: read everything like a hostile witness. The innkeeper's story. The road sign. The shape of the gaps."

"The gaps."

I sketched quickly: a village, and beside it the same village with holes punched through it. "A town that's forgotten its war isn't just sad, Ada. It's evidence. Forgetting leaves a *shape*. You saw it tonight — Marta reaching for her father's face and finding nothing. Nothing doesn't feel like nothing. It feels like a hole, and people flinch around holes even when they can't see them. Watch for the flinch. The flinch maps the theft."

Ada was quiet a long moment, watching the fire. "You're teaching me to read a country the way I used to read a ledger."

"I'm teaching you that they're the same book."

They turned the birch bark over in their fingers. "Why?" they said finally. "Why teach me? You don't trust me. I watched you not-trust me for three days."

"I don't trust anyone," I said. "But I watched you at the annex. When I showed you the prophecy seal, you didn't ask what it was worth. You asked what it *cost*. People who ask that question are rare, and right now they're the only company worth keeping." I hesitated, then gave them the truth, because trust is a debt and I prefer to pay early: "Also, I need you. Whatever your name is doing in that prophecy, whatever the guild thinks you are — I can't decipher a century-old binding text alone, and I can't walk into the palace alone, and you're the only person alive who both hates the guild and knows its handwriting from the inside."

"So we're allies of convenience."

"We're allies of necessity. It's sturdier. Convenience snaps in the cold. Necessity just gets colder."

Ada almost smiled. It was the first almost-smile I'd seen on them, and I noted it the way I note everything — catalogued, dated, cross-referenced against future loss. Old habits. Falling, still falling.

* * *

*Ada*

The second village was worse than the first, and the third was worse than that, and by the fifth I had stopped sleeping well.

This one was called Harrowmere — or it called itself that, though half the folk pronounced it Harramere and one old man insisted it was Harrow-mere-the-First, which meant nothing to anyone, including him. There was a graveyard on the hill above it, and that was where I understood what Sef had been trying to show me.

The graves were tended. Flowers, fresh. Stones scrubbed clean. Someone loved these dead recently and thoroughly.

And not one stone bore a legible name.

Not worn — *blank*, as though the masons had carved names and the air had eaten them. I ran my thumb over one and felt the ghost-grooves of letters beneath the smoothness, the way you feel a scar under paint.

An old woman was laying rosemary on the stones. I asked her, as gently as I could, who lay here.

"My family," she said.

"Which family?"

"Mine." She smiled at me with real warmth and absolute emptiness. "They're all here. My mother, and her mother, and the little ones from the fever year, and my husband." She patted the nearest stone. "Him especially. Forty years come spring."

"Do you remember him?"

"Every day," she said, and her eyes were bright and wet and perfectly blank behind the brightness, like windows painted shut. "Every single day."

I walked down the hill with my heart going like a hammer. Sef met me at the foot of it, and I didn't say anything, and neither did he, because we'd stopped needing words for it. The country was forgetting how to grieve. Grief needs a name, a date, a face — grief is a kind of record — and the records were being eaten. These people still performed mourning the way you hum a tune whose words you've lost. They laid the flowers. They scrubbed the stones. They could not have said for whom, or why it mattered, only that it *did*, that some debt was owed here and the creditor's name had been struck from the book.

"It's accelerating," Sef said that night, spreading his map on the floor of a hay barn. He'd been marking villages with two symbols: a circle for intact memory, a slash for gaps. The circles thinned as we went north. The slashes thickened. "Near Ashfall, the feeding is surgical — specific memories, priced and collected. Out here it's broader. Sloppier. Or hungrier."

"The crown," I said. The word still tasted wrong in my mouth. "It's getting closer to the fire. Ysol's shrine is north. If the fire is guttering—"

"Then it drinks deeper to keep its fuel up. Yes." He tapped the map. "And notice where the gaps cluster. Not random. Along the old pilgrimage roads. The roads that lead here." His finger stopped on a smudge of mountains, and beneath it, in faded ink, a name I'd seen only in the prophecy: *the Shrine Below the Snowline.*

"It's drinking the approaches," I said. "Clearing its own doorstep."

"Sweeping the stairs before guests arrive." Sef rolled up the map, and his hands weren't quite steady. "Or preparing a table."

We lay in the dark of the barn after that, listening to wind work at the roof, and I did the thing I'd been avoiding since the Guildhall: I opened my own memory like a ledger and started reading it the way Sef had taught me. Who made this? Why does it exist? What does it want me to believe?

My childhood was full of soft places. Blank spots I'd always taken for ordinary forgetting — everyone has them. But now I checked them for flinch, and found it. There was a hole where my sixth winter should be, and around the hole a ring of unease, like frost around a door. There was a gap where my mother's singing should be, and I flinched from it so hard sitting alone in the dark that I made a sound.

Someone had harvested me long before I ever signed a guild page.

My master. The hedge-witch I'd run from. The woman whose face I realized, lying there, I could no longer fully see.

"Sef," I whispered across the dark.

"Mm."

"When we get to the shrine. When we read that ledger, if we read it — I'm going to be in it, aren't I. My name. My memories. Line items."

The dark was quiet a long time.

"Yes," he said at last. "Almost certainly. And Ada — when we find your entries — you'll want to burn the page. Everyone does. Don't. Burning the record doesn't return the memory. It just makes the theft permanent."

"I know how records work."

"No," Sef said softly. "You know how ledgers work. Records are different. A ledger says what was taken. A record keeps faith that it mattered. Learn the difference before we arrive, because the shrine vault will be full of both, and you'll need to tell them apart with your hands shaking."

I lay awake most of that night, and near dawn I dreamed of my mother's voice, and woke reaching for it, and found only the frost-ring shape of where it had been.

Two days later, the moth found us.

I want to say I saw it coming, that I sensed it the way the prophecy might suggest, some fate-sense humming in my buried name. I didn't. It was simply there at dawn on the last stretch before the snowline, circling our campfire's ashes, wings the color of banked coals, impossibly alive in killing cold. It landed on the back of my hand without weight and without heat, and where its feet touched, a mark bloomed — a small burning glyph, painless, patient, like a coal wrapped in silk.

Sef stared at it. "That's a keeper's charm. That's very old craft."

"It burns," I said.

"It *invites*," he corrected. "Look north."

Beyond the last trees, past the white shoulder of the pass, the mountain stood with its head in cloud. And in the cloud, faint as breath on glass, something glowed. Not sunlight. Firelight. The color of the mark on my hand.

Somewhere up there, a dying keeper had spent the last of something precious to send a moth looking for a runaway clerk — because a century ago, someone wrote my true name in a book, and books, as a disgraced archivist had taught me, are arguments. This one wanted me to believe I still had a part to play.

The moth lifted off my hand, circled once, and flew toward the glow.

I picked up my pack and followed it, and Sef fell into step beside me, and behind us the whole borrowed country lay silent in the snow, holding flowers for names it could no longer read.
