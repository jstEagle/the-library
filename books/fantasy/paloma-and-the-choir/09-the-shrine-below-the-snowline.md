## 9. The Shrine Below the Snowline

The fire spoke to Ysol first.

It always did, these days—a thin whine in the flame like a kettle left too long on the hob. A century ago it had roared. Fifty years ago it had sung. Now it whimpered, and Ysol had learned to hear the difference the way a mother learns to tell a cry of hunger from a cry of fear, and every morning she woke knowing which one it was.

She was counting embers when the moths came back.

Not her moths—her moth-charm, the one she'd sent down the mountain three weeks past with a scrap of the dragon's shed scale sewn into its thorax and a name stitched in fire-thread that she'd copied from the oldest book in the shrine: *Brightwater*. She'd sent it half expecting nothing. Charms failed. Names went stale. The world below the snowline was a country of gaps now, and you could not deliver a message to someone who had been subtracted from their own life.

But here it came, out of the grey morning, riding a wind that smelled of pine and cold iron, and it was not alone. Two more shapes climbed the switchback below the shrine's broken stair, one tall and stooped like a man carrying an invisible shelf of books, one small and hooded and walking the way people walk when they have spent years practicing how not to be noticed—and failing at it today, because the mountain noticed everyone.

Ysol set down her ember-tongs and stood in the doorway of the shrine, letting the heat spill out behind her like a banner.

"Stop there," she called. Her voice carried the way old voices do on stone. "State your debts."

The tall one flinched. The small one stopped walking entirely, as if the word had been a hand flat against their chest.

"We don't—" the tall one began.

"You do," Ysol said. "Everyone does. That's rather the whole trouble. Come up slowly. And don't touch the walls."

* * *

She watched them climb. The tall one—she marked him immediately as archive-bred, the way he counted the steps without meaning to, the way his eyes snagged on the carved glyphs flanking the stair and his lips moved, translating. Sef. She knew the name from a dispatch decades old, a royal archivist cashiered for asking questions about continuity. Asking questions was how they got you, in the palace. Or how they got rid of you. Sometimes both.

The small one was harder. Hood up, hands in sleeves, gaze fixed on the middle distance where nobody lived. Ysol had been keeper for sixty-one years and had met perhaps four hundred pilgrims, and she could sort them by their silences the way a shepherd sorts sheep. This one's silence was not grief or awe or fear. It was *practice*. Someone had taught this person to be a hole in the world, and they had gotten very good at the lesson.

That was either exactly what the prophecy wanted or exactly what the crown wanted, and Ysol would know which soon enough.

At the top of the stair, breath ragged, the two of them stood before her. The tall one bowed—archive manners. The small one did something more interesting: they looked past Ysol, through the shrine doorway, at the light within, and their whole body changed. The practiced emptiness cracked like ice on a spring pond.

"You've seen it before," Ysol said quietly. "Fire like that."

"No," said the small one, and then, honestly: "I've seen it *in*. Does that count?"

"It counts," Ysol said. "Come in. Mind the threshold. It takes a toll."

"What kind of toll?" the tall one asked sharply—the voice of a man who had learned what thresholds cost.

"A memory," Ysol said. "A small one. You'll get to choose which. That's the mercy of this place, and I'll thank you not to confuse it with kindness, because it isn't. The shrine is honest, and honesty is expensive." She stepped aside. "Choose well. Most people choose wrong."

* * *

Inside, the fire filled the hollow of the mountain like a heart in a chest of stone.

Ysol never tired of watching strangers meet it. The tall one—Sef—went still in the way scholars go still, cataloguing, measuring, already composing footnotes. But the small one—Ada, she assumed; Brightwater, anyway—walked straight toward the flame as if pulled on a line, and stopped only when the heat pressed back against their face like a palm.

"It's dying," Ada said.

"Yes," Ysol said.

"It's *afraid*."

"Yes," Ysol said again, softer. "Now you understand my last sixty years."

Sef had gone to the walls instead, because of course he had. The shrine's inner chamber was ringed with niches, and each niche held a lamp of green glass, and each lamp held a moth—not living things anymore, but husks, wings of ash and gold leaf, thousands of them, ranked like a choir at rest. He touched the glass of one gently, reverently, the way you touch a reliquary.

"Moths," he murmured. "Fire-moths. They're in the old bestiaries. *Pyralis veritatis.* Said to nest only where truth has burned long enough to leave a residue." He turned. "These are all dead."

"They're not dead," Ysol corrected. "They're *spent*. There's a difference, and the difference is the entire history of this kingdom, but we'll come to that." She gestured them to the stone bench before the flame, worn smooth by six generations of keepers' sitting. "Sit. Both of you. Before the toll collects itself, I want names. True ones. The shrine will taste them anyway—it may as well come from your mouths."

Sef gave his readily enough. Sef Ardenne, formerly First Archivist to the Crown, currently no one, currently tired.

The small one took longer. Ysol watched the war happen behind their eyes—the practiced hole fighting the pull of the flame, the buried name straining upward like a plant under a paving stone. When it finally came, it came quietly, almost apologetically:

"Ada. Just Ada. The other name is—" They swallowed. "The prophecy used the other name. I'd rather you didn't."

"The shrine already knows it," Ysol said. "Knowing costs it nothing. Saying costs you something. So keep it, for now. We'll see whether you can afford to keep it later." She leaned forward. "You read the ledger at the guild. The prophecy book. Tell me the last line you saw."

Ada recited it without opening anything, which told Ysol they had not slept since. "*On the night the fire gutters low, one whose name is written twice shall stand beneath the crown and speak the debt aloud—*" They stopped. "It ended mid-sentence. Mid-*word*, nearly."

"It ends mid-word in every copy," Ysol said. "Because the last line hasn't happened yet. Prophecies are written backward, child, from the ending forward. Whoever wrote that book sat where you're sitting and looked into this flame and wrote down what they saw, and the flame showed them everything except the part that hadn't decided itself yet." She let that sit. "You are the undecided part. Congratulations. I'm sure it feels terrible. It is."

* * *

"Now," Ysol said, "the toll."

She rose, joints cracking like green wood, and went to the flame's edge where the heat drew a shimmering line across the floor. She had done this four hundred times. It never got easier, watching people decide what they could bear to lose.

"Hold out your hand," she told Ada. "Palm up. Over the line. Not touching the fire—touching the *light*. The fire will take one memory. One. Small, it prefers. It doesn't get to choose; you do. Think of something true about yourself, something ordinary, something you'd miss least. Offer it. If the fire accepts, you pass. If it refuses, offer another. It refuses what it doesn't need, so don't insult it with lies."

"And what does it need memories *for*?" Sef asked. His voice had gone careful, the carefulness of a man assembling an argument he dreaded finishing.

"That," Ysol said, "is the lesson. Pay first. Understand after. That's the order of everything in this country, isn't it? You pay and pay and the explanation never arrives until you've already lost the thing you needed the explanation to save." She turned back to Ada. "Hand out."

Ada hesitated. Ysol watched them search themselves the way a poor woman searches her house before the rent collector comes—turning over cushions, checking jars, weighing everything they own against the possibility of keeping it. Finally Ada held out their hand, palm up, over the shimmering line, and closed their eyes, and Ysol felt the offering arrive like a change in air pressure.

Something about a market. A smell of oranges, maybe. Something domestic and warm and cheap.

The flame flickered. Considered. Took it.

Ada made a sound—not a cry, just a small exhalation, the sound of a door closing somewhere inside—and opened their eyes, and for a moment they stared at their own open palm as though expecting to find it lighter. Their brow furrowed faintly, hunting for the missing thing, finding the shape of its absence and nothing else.

"There," Ysol said gently. "Gone. You won't remember losing it, which is the cruelest part, but right now you remember losing it, so look at that feeling. Hold it. Because everything I'm about to show you is going to ask you to feel exactly that, a thousand times over, on purpose, with witnesses." She looked at Sef. "And you. Your toll comes due too, archivist. Everyone who enters pays."

He offered a memory of a library afternoon, some golden nowhere, and the fire took it, and he winced and mastered it and said nothing, and Ysol thought: *proud one, proud ones burn fastest.*

* * *

"Now the truth," she said, "which you paid for, so attend."

She walked to the far wall, where the niches of spent moths climbed into darkness, and put her hand on the stone. It was warm. Sixty years, and it was still warm.

"This mountain was a dragon once. Not metaphor. Not lineage. The actual animal. Her name was Vessarax, and she died here nine hundred years ago, in the wars your grandparents cannot remember and your parents were never told about. Dragons do not die the way we die. Their deaths are too large to fit through the door of a single moment. So hers is *still happening*. Still arriving. This fire—" she nodded at the great flame breathing in its hollow, "—is not a fire. It is her death, burning. It has been burning for nine centuries, and it will finish sometime in the next year, and when a death finishes, everything it illuminated goes dark with it. That's the natural order. That's how it was meant to be: slow, vast, dignified. A whole age dimming gently into the next."

She turned to face them.

"Someone decided that wasn't good enough. A hundred years ago. Men—founders, they called themselves—came up this mountain with ledgers and contracts and a crown, and they bound the king to the fire's edge, and they built a machine out of paperwork. Here is how it works, and I want you to listen the way you'd listen if your life depended on it, because it does."

She pointed at the flame. "The fire illuminates. That's all fire does. Everything its light touches becomes *clear*—true, remembered, undeniable. For nine hundred years, its light fell on this kingdom and kept it legible. People remembered their dead because the light lay on the graves. They remembered their wars, their vows, their crimes, their names. The fire was the kingdom's memory, the way the sun is the field's harvest."

Her finger moved, tracing an invisible line from the mountain down, south, toward the city none of them could see from here.

"But light is expensive to make, and a dying dragon makes less of it every year. So the founders found a substitute fuel. Memory burns beautifully. A memory given freely is candle-fat. A memory taken as a *debt*—" her lip curled, "—burns hotter. The guild collects them. Seven years of luck. A first kiss. A mother's voice. Every entry in every ledger in that building you fled, Ada Brightwater, is a log thrown on this fire, except it doesn't feed the fire. It feeds the *crown*. Because the crown sits between the fire and the king, and everything the fire illuminates passes through it first, and the crown keeps what it likes."

"The king—" Sef started.

"The king is a man in his nineties who has forgotten his own coronation eleven times," Ysol said flatly. "They hold a feast for it every year. Feast of Continuity. Very pretty. Very obscene. He forgets so the crown can eat cleanly. And the thing that lives in the crown—it isn't a demon, it isn't a god, it's worse, it's an *accountant*—has spent a century growing fat on everything the fire's light ever touched. Every memory the guild harvests doesn't restore the kingdom's remembering. It *replaces* it. The crown eats the original and lends back a copy with the dangerous parts removed. That's why the villages on your road here had gaps in them. That's why no one remembers the old king, the war, their own dead. The light still falls on them. But it's been filtered."

Silence, except for the fire's thin whine.

Sef had gone grey. "The ledgers aren't records," he said slowly. "They're *fuel manifests*."

"Now you're doing archival work," Ysol said, with no warmth in it. "Yes. The guild was founded by the same men who bound the crown. Its charter is a feeding schedule. Its interest rates are appetite. And when the fire dies—when Vessarax's death finally finishes burning through the last of its own fuel—all that light goes out, all those copies collapse, and whatever holds the crown inherits a century of hoarded memory in a single instant. It will become the kingdom's remembering. All of it. It will be able to tell people who they are, and they will believe it, because believing is all anyone will have left."

Ada had not moved since the toll. Now they spoke, very quietly, still looking at their empty palm.

"The prophecy. *One whose name is written twice.*"

"Written twice means written in two ledgers," Ysol said. "The crown's ledger, where the guild entered you as property—I'd wager your apprenticeship papers say something interesting if you ever dare reread them. And the shrine's ledger, which is older, and which does not record debts. It records *debts owed to you*." She crossed to the iron-banded chest beneath the flame's hollow, the one she had dusted every day for sixty-one years, and rested her hand on the lid without opening it.

"Everything the crown took from this kingdom is indexed in there. Every stolen name, every harvested year, every borrowed mother. And it can only be unbound by being *read aloud*—all of it, in order, in the crown's presence, by someone named in it. Reading restores. Each line spoken gives back what was taken." She looked up at Ada, and let herself be honest, because the shrine was honest and she was its keeper and honesty was expensive. "Each line spoken also takes. From the reader. The ledger balances itself. It always balances. That's the first law of accounting and the last law of magic, and they are the same law."

"How much?" Sef whispered. He already knew. Ysol could see him knowing.

"All of it," Ysol said. "Line by line. Until there's no one left inside you who remembers being anyone. The prophecy ends mid-sentence because whoever wrote it couldn't see past that point. Either there's nothing past that point, or there's something no one has ever come back to describe."

The fire whined. Somewhere in the ranks of spent moths, a husk shifted in its niche, settling like a sleeper turning over.

Ada stood there in the firelight, and Ysol watched them do the arithmetic every keeper's visitor eventually does—the sum of a self weighed against a kingdom—and watched them arrive, too fast, far too fast, at the answer that frightened Ysol most, because it was the answer the flame had shown her too, in her own vigils, for sixty years.

"When do we start reading?" Ada said.

* * *

Ysol did not answer at once. She turned instead to the chest, lifted its lid, and took out the ledger—the real one, vellum gone soft as cloth, iron corners stained with a century of handling—and laid it on the bench between them.

Then she heard it. Faint, far below, down through the stone, down the switchback and the pinewoods and the frozen river: bells. Many bells. The particular cadence of guild collectors traveling in formation, ringing the road-clearing chime, coming up a mountain where no road was supposed to be cleared.

Sef heard it too. He was on his feet before the third peal.

"They followed us," he breathed. "Or they were always coming."

"They were always coming," Ysol said, and reached for her ember-tongs, and felt sixty-one years settle onto her shoulders like a second cloak. "The fire told me at dawn. It always tells me. I only hoped it would be wronger than usual."
