## 10. Ledger of a Kingdom

The vault door was not locked. That was the first thing Ada noticed, and the thing that frightened them most.

Everything else in Ysol's shrine was sealed—doors bound with iron bands gone black with age, windows shuttered against the snowlight, even the pantry cupboard tied shut with red thread knotted seven times. But the vault at the bottom of the stair, behind the dragon's ribcage where the bone curved like a cathedral arch, stood open. Waiting.

"It has been waiting," Ysol said, reading their face. She moved slowly now, her hands wrapped in wool against the cold that came off the fire below them—the fire they had seen only once, through a grate, and which Ada still felt in their teeth like a struck bell. "A hundred years of keepers have walked past this door and left it shut. It opens for the one it's waiting for."

"And that's me."

"That's whoever the book named." Ysol's voice was dry as old vellum. "Don't flatter yourself into thinking you were chosen for anything but your name."

Ada almost laughed at that. Almost. It would have been easier to be chosen for something noble than to be chosen because some scribe a century dead had written down *Ada Brightwater* on a page and let it sit there, patient as a debt accruing interest.

They went down together, though Ysol stopped at the threshold and would come no further. "Keepers don't enter," she said. "We tend what's inside. We don't read it." Her eyes went to the dark beyond the doorway, and for the first time since Ada had met her, the old woman looked afraid—not of what was in there, but of wanting it. "Go. Read nothing aloud. Touch nothing with wet hands. And Ada—" she caught their sleeve, her grip surprisingly strong, "—whatever you find in there about yourself, remember that a ledger is a story told by the winner. The winner here has been winning for a hundred years."

Then she let go, and Ada stepped alone into the vault.

* * *

It was warmer inside than out, which made no sense until Ada understood that the warmth came from below—from the fire sleeping under the shrine, breathing up through the dragon's bones the way breath moves through a flute. The walls were bone, pale and veined, and set into them were shelves. Not wooden shelves. The bones themselves had been shaped, hollowed, polished into long troughs, and in each trough lay books.

Hundreds of them. Thousands. Ledger-books, Ada saw at once—they had the look the way a shepherd knows sheep, the way a clerk knows the droop of a well-used binding, the way a spine cracks when it has been opened ten thousand times. Guild bindings. Guild corners. The double-rule border stamped in gold along every cover: the mark Ada had pressed into fresh leather a thousand times in the workroom above the counting floor.

But these were older than any ledger Ada had ever handled. The gold had gone green at the edges. The leather had darkened to the color of tea left standing. And the first volume, lying open on a reading stand carved from a single vertebra the size of a wagon wheel, bore a date from before the guild had a charter. Before the guild had a name.

Ada approached it the way you approach a sleeping dog of unknown temper—one hand extended, weight on the back foot, ready to run.

The page read:

*Year One of the Binding. Item the First: from the King Aldric, called the Just, one memory entire: the moment of his crowning, taken at the hour of its making, that the crown might know itself. Witnessed and entered by the undersigned, founders of the Compact of Continuous Account.*

Below the entry, two signatures. Ada knew both names. Every clerk in the Ledger Guild knew both names, because both names were carved over the doors of the guildhall in letters a hand high: **VANE THE ELDER** and **MOTHER TALLOW**.

The founders. The saints. The two faces on the medallions given to clerks who completed their tenth year of service—medallions Ada had never earned, because Ada had never intended to stay, because staying meant belonging and belonging meant being known.

Ada turned the page.

*Item the Second: from the King Aldric, one memory: the face of his mother.*

*Item the Third: from the Queen, one memory: the sound of her own name spoken in love.*

*Item the Fourth: from the Court Physician, one memory: the plague year, entire, that he might forget his failures and serve on.*

On and on. Page after page after page. The handwriting changed over the years—hands died, new hands took up the pen—but the entries never did. Names Ada recognized from history books. Names nobody recognized at all. A miller who gave up the memory of his drowned daughter so he could keep working. A soldier who sold the war so he could sleep. A bride who traded her wedding day for her husband's gambling debts, entered fair and square, witnessed twice, no coercion noted.

No coercion noted. Ada's eyes kept snagging on those words. *No coercion noted.* As if the absence of a whip was the same as the absence of a chain.

And then, three pages deep into the second year of the Binding, Ada found the harvest schedule.

That was the only word for it. A table, ruled clean and true, columns headed in a fine commercial hand: *Source. Quantity. Interval. Destination.* Sources listed by district. Quantities listed in memories per annum. Intervals listed in years between collections. And destination—a single word, repeated down the whole long column:

*Crown.*

The guild hadn't been collecting debts. Or rather—it had been collecting debts, honestly enough, in the way a spider collects flies honestly enough by building a web. The debts were real. The contracts were real. People really did trade seven years of luck for a safe voyage, and the guild really did hold what they traded, and no one was cheated in the narrow sense of the word. But the guild had never wanted the memories for themselves. The guild was a pipe. A great, patient, century-long pipe running from every hearth and harbor in the kingdom straight up into the crown on the king's head, feeding it, keeping it full, keeping it—

Keeping it alive.

Ada thought of the fisherman. Three nights ago, ordinary as bread, a fisherman with salt-cracked hands had paid seven years of luck for one safe voyage, and Ada had written it down in a fair hand without a tremor, and the fisherman had walked out lighter and grateful, and Ada had felt—that flicker. That small guilt, quickly shelved like a returned volume. *The ledger is neutral,* Master Vane said. *The ledger does not judge. It only records.*

The ledger did not judge. The ledger just fed.

Ada's hands were shaking. They gripped the edge of the vertebra-stand and made themselves turn forward, year by year, decade by decade, watching the harvest grow. The quantities increased. The intervals shortened. Somewhere around Year Forty, a marginal note appeared in a fussy, self-satisfied hand: *Yield exceeds projection. Crown appetite steady. Recommend expansion into rural districts.* Around Year Sixty: *Note—subjects who retain strong grief resist collection. Grief anchors memory. See attached proposal re: suppression of mourning customs in the western provinces.*

See attached proposal. Ada had walked through those western villages on the road here. Villages with gaps in them. Villages that couldn't remember their dead. Someone had proposed that, in an office, in ink, and someone else had approved it, and it had been implemented, and it had worked, and the yield had gone up.

Rage came up in Ada like floodwater coming up a stairwell—slow, then all at once, finding every level. Their vision blurred. Their breath went short. They wanted to burn the vault. They wanted to burn the guildhall with its carved founder-names over the doors, burn the charter, burn the medallions, burn every fair-handed entry they had ever made in their neat invisible clerk's script—

And then they found their own name.

Not Ada Brightwater. Not yet. Eleven years earlier than tonight's prophecy, in a year when Ada had been six years old, in an entry written in a hand Ada knew better than any hand in the world:

*Item: from the hedge-witch Maren of the Fenmarch, in settlement of arrears, one apprentice-child's memories as follows: [list appended]. Note—child retains unusual resistance; recommend continued observation. Recommend placement within the guild at majority. The child will make an excellent collector. Unbound memories read true.*

Unbound memories read true.

Ada read the sentence four times. The vault was very quiet. The fire breathed below them, patient as tide.

Their apprenticeship—the flight to the city, the guild entrance exam passed on the first try, the pride of invisibility, the comfort of being nobody among the ink-stained clerks—none of it had been escape. It had been *placement*. Maren hadn't lost Ada to the city. Maren had delivered her, per recommendation, per schedule, per the long slow harvest plan drafted before Ada could write their own name. Even the running—even the one thing Ada had ever done that felt like freedom, fleeing Maren's cottage in the fen with a bundle and a false name—had been anticipated, accommodated, *entered*. Somewhere in these shelves there was probably a ledger with Ada's flight recorded in it, filed under *assets, mobile*.

They were not invisible. They had never been invisible. Invisibility was a service the guild provided to its best tools.

Ada sat down on the bone floor, because their legs had decided the matter without consulting them, and let the rage have its full weight for a while. It was enormous. It was older than they were. It was eleven years deep and a century wide and it had their mother's voice in it somewhere, or what they could still recall of their mother's voice, which was less every year, which was maybe already gone, which had maybe been *sold*, itemized, entered fair and square, no coercion noted—

The fire breathed. The vault waited.

And slowly, the way floodwater becomes a river—still going somewhere, but with banks now, with direction—the rage settled into something else. Something colder and more useful.

Because here was the thing Ada understood, sitting on the floor of a vault built from a dead god's skeleton: rage was a fuel, and the guild knew all about fuels. Rage burned hot and fast and left ash. What Ada felt settling into place beneath the rage was quieter and would last longer. It was the feeling of a ledger balancing. Of accounts finally, finally open instead of hidden. Of a debt identified, and a debtor named, and a payment scheduled.

The guild had taught them this, ironically. Everything balances. Nothing is free. Every entry has two sides.

Fine. Then let there be an entry. Let the whole century be read back, line by line, name by name, memory by memory, until every stolen thing went home to its owner and the crown stood empty and the account closed forever. Let the harvest be harvested. Let the collectors learn what it was to be collected.

Ada stood up. Their legs held. Their hands had stopped shaking, mostly.

They took the first ledger off the shelf—the Year One volume, the founding entry, the king's stolen coronation—and carried it toward the light. Then stopped. Turned back. Took the harvest schedule too. And the proposal regarding grief. Evidence, Sef would call it. Sef, who had spent his career in an archive and been thrown out of it, who would know exactly what to do with a paper trail a century long.

At the top of the stairs, Ysol was waiting, wrapped in wool, watching Ada's face.

"Well?" she said.

"They've been farming us." Ada's voice came out flat and steady, which surprised them. "For a hundred years. My master sold my childhood to pay her debts, and the guild used me as a planting. I'm in the ledger, Ysol. I've been in it since I was six years old."

Ysol closed her eyes briefly, the way you do when news confirms something you'd hoped was paranoia. "And?"

"And I want to read every line of it aloud." Ada shifted the books in their arms. "To the right audience. In the right room. Where it can't be unwritten."

Something moved across the old keeper's face—grief and hope fighting for the same ground. "You understand what reading aloud means, in this place. You understand the price."

"I understand ledgers," Ada said. "I've spent nine years learning that nothing is free. So we find out what this costs, and then we pay it, and then—" they looked back down the stair, at the bone-vault and its patient shelves, at a century of theft indexed and shelved and beautifully bound, "—then we close the book."

They were halfway across the shrine chamber, arms full of the founding ledger, when the moth-charms began to die.

All of them at once—the little white moths that hung in every corner of the shrine, dozing on their threads, Ysol's eyes and ears and early warnings—folded their wings and dropped like snow. Dozens of them, falling silent to the flagstones.

Ysol went very still.

"That," she said quietly, "is what it looks like when someone is climbing my mountain who knows how to kill a charm."

She looked at Ada. Ada looked at the fallen moths, and at the ledger in their arms, and thought: *of course. Of course the guild always knows the balance. Of course they've sent someone to collect.*

"How long do we have?" Ada asked.

Ysol was already moving toward the stair that led down to the fire, her weariness burned away like fog in sun. "Longer than they'll expect," she said, "if we start now."
