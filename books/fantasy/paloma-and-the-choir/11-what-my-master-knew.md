## 11. What My Master Knew

I did not sleep in the bone-hollows. I sat with the ledger open on my knees and let the cold of the dragon's ribs seep into me, and I told myself I was resting my eyes.

Sef slept the way archivists sleep: curled around a book like it might be stolen from him. Ysol was somewhere above, on the stair, feeding her moths. And I sat in the dark beneath a dead dragon's breastbone with a century-old ledger in my lap, reading names by the light of a fire that had once been an animal.

That was when I found my mother's name.

It wasn't where I expected it. I'd been looking for myself—*Ada Brightwater*, or worse, the name I'd buried—and instead there she was, three lines down a page dated the winter I turned six. *One lullaby, sung nightly, taken as surety against the debt of Maren Brightwater.* The entry was in a hand I knew. A hand I had watched make letters across a kitchen table while bread rose under a cloth. A hand that had taught me to write my own name and then, apparently, sold the sound of her voice singing to me.

My master's hand.

The ledger didn't say *Maren Brightwater owed*. It didn't need to. Debts like that were never written down; they lived in the space between what a woman said to a collector at her door and what she agreed to because her child was sick and hedge-magic cost more than coin. My mother had gone to Wren Halloway for a cure. Wren Halloway had cured me. And somewhere in that transaction, folded into the price like a seed in bread, was a clause: *the child's memories held as collateral.*

I had run from Wren Halloway when I was seventeen. I had told myself a hundred stories about why. That she was cruel. That she worked magic too old and too hungry. That I wanted a life in the city, ink under my nails, anonymity like a warm coat. All true, in the way that a wall is true when you're standing very close to it and can't see the shape of the house.

Here is what actually happened, which I could only see now, reading it in someone else's accounting:

* * *

I was seventeen, and it was autumn, and Wren Halloway called me into her workroom to help her close the year's books.

Her books were not like guild ledgers. They were fat, cracked things bound in leather gone soft as skin, and they smelled of tallow and dried yarrow, and I loved them the way you love anything that has been the whole architecture of your childhood. She kept them locked in a chest under the floorboards, and she let me touch them exactly twice a year: at closing, and at opening.

"Read me the third column," she said, which was unusual. Usually she read aloud herself, tapping each line with one finger, and I copied. That night she sat back with her tea and watched me instead, and something in the way she watched made the room feel smaller.

I read. Names, prices, terms. A widow's grief for two years of clear skies. A farmer's memory of his wedding day for a sow that wouldn't farrow. Ordinary harvest. Ordinary cruelty, dressed as kindness, the way it always is in villages where the alternative is dying.

Then I came to a page near the back, and my own name was on it.

Not Ada. The other one. The birth name I would bury four years later, written in her careful hand, followed by entries going back to my sixth winter. *Lullaby, sung nightly. The smell of rain on the south road. First snowfall, age seven. Mother's face, as seen by candlelight.*

Page after page. My entire childhood, itemized.

"What is this," I said, and my voice came out flat, which is what happens when feeling is too large to have any shape.

Wren Halloway looked at me over her tea for a long time. She was not a cruel woman, whatever I told myself later. She was a tired one, and tiredness done long enough looks identical to cruelty from the inside.

"It's collateral," she said. "You knew that."

"I didn't know that."

"You signed nothing. Your mother signed nothing. That's rather the point." She set down her cup. "Ada. Listen to me, because I'm only going to say this once, and then we'll never speak of it again, and you'll hate me quietly for twenty years, which is fair." She rubbed her eyes. "I don't collect for myself. I never did. I'm a branch office. There's a guild in the capital that holds the master ledger, and everything I take goes to them, and everything they hold goes—" she stopped, and I watched her decide how much truth a seventeen-year-old could carry, and I watched her decide on almost none. "Goes upward," she finished. "To the crown."

"To the king?"

"To what wears the king."

I want to tell you I threw the book at her. I want to tell you I ran that night. What I actually did was finish copying the third column, because she asked, and because obedience was so deep in me it operated below thought. Then I went to my cot and lay awake until the grey hour, listening to her move around downstairs, and I understood two things with terrible clarity.

The first was that every good thing in my childhood had been bought with pieces of itself. The lullaby I remembered as warmth—I didn't remember it. I remembered being told I'd liked it. The candlelit face of my mother, which I would have sworn on any altar was the truest memory I owned: gone before I ever knew I'd had it, replaced by a description of itself. I had grown up in a house where the furniture was real and the love was a receipt.

The second thing was that Wren Halloway had raised me anyway. Fed me, taught me letters and herb-lore and the grammar of ledgers, kept me fed through the sick winters. She could have collected the whole account years ago and closed the book. Instead she'd left most of it standing, drawing it down in small sips, and I understood—lying there in the dark—that this was the closest thing to mercy our world had a word for. She was spending my childhood slowly because she couldn't bring herself to spend it all at once.

I ran in spring. Not because she was cruel. Because she was kind in a system that converted kindness into inventory, and I could not bear to watch her do it to me forever. I fled to the Ledger Guild—the very machine she served—because I thought if I stood inside the thing, I would finally understand it. If I became a clerk, the ledgers would stop being weather and start being arithmetic. Something I could survive.

And the guild took me in gladly, because I was already theirs. Collateral doesn't apply for a job. It reports for duty.

* * *

In the bone-hollows, I closed the ledger and pressed my palms against its cover until the edges bit.

"Only an unbound memory can read it aloud."

Ysol's voice, from the stair. I hadn't heard her come down. She descended slowly, one hand on the rail, moth-light drifting around her shoulders like slow snow. She lowered herself onto the bench across from me with the groan of someone whose joints predate several governments.

"You heard that?" I said.

"I've heard everything since you arrived. The mountain carries sound the way the city carries debt." She studied me. "You found your name in it."

"I found my mother's name in it."

"Ah." Ysol nodded as though I'd confirmed a diagnosis. "That's worse. Yes."

"How can I be unbound? My whole childhood is in there. My name is in there. Sef says the rite needs someone whose name appears in the ledger, but that means—" I stopped, because saying it made it real. "It means the crown already owns the reader. It means whoever reads it aloud pays with whatever they have left."

"It means," Ysol said, "that the guild made a mistake a hundred years ago and has been compounding interest ever since." She leaned forward. "When the founders indexed the binding, they needed a control. A memory outside the harvest, against which all the stolen ones could be measured. So they wrote one person into the ledger who was never collected—only named. A reference point. An unbound entry in a book of bound things." Her moth-light flickered. "They chose a bloodline. Yours. And they waited a hundred years for the prophecy's date, and the guild has been grooming your family toward this desk, this night, this book, without any of you knowing why the road always bent this direction."

"Wren Halloway knew."

"Wren Halloway knew her orders. Whether she knew their purpose—" Ysol shrugged. "Collectors are given sums, not reasons. But she guessed. I think that's why she drew your account down so slowly. I think she was trying to keep enough of you intact to survive what's coming."

Something in my chest cracked along a seam I hadn't known was load-bearing.

All those years hating her. All those years telling the story of my escape like a victory, like I'd slipped a leash. And the whole time she'd been rationing me—not hoarding, *rationing*—keeping the lullaby and the candlelight and the first snowfall in the vault of me like coins saved against a famine she saw coming. She'd let me hate her because hatred travels light. Grief would have slowed me down.

"I ran from her," I said. My voice sounded like someone else's. "I built my whole life on running from her."

"Yes," Ysol said gently. "And look where running brought you. To the exact place the road was always going." She reached over and tapped the ledger with one crooked finger. "Every step you took to disappear made you harder to find and easier to use. The guild loves a fugitive. A fugitive has no one to notice what's missing from them."

I wanted to argue. I searched for the argument the way you search a pocket you know is empty.

Instead I did the thing I had spent ten years avoiding. I sat with it. I let the grief come up out of the ground where I'd buried it alive, and it was not clean and it was not shaped like anger. It was the smell of bread rising under a cloth. It was a woman's hand making letters by lamplight. It was the knowledge that the last time I saw Wren Halloway, I went out the window rather than the door, because the door meant goodbye, and I was too much a coward to say it, and now the ledger said her name too—*Wren Halloway, collector, second class, account settled in full*—and I did not know if *settled in full* meant she was dead or merely emptied, and I could not ask her, and the not-knowing sat on my chest like a stone.

"She's in here," I said. "Her account. Settled in full."

Ysol was quiet a moment. "Then she paid everything she had too."

* * *

Sef woke near dawn, or what passes for dawn inside a mountain, and found us both sitting vigil over the ledger like it was a sickbed.

He read my face the way he reads spines—quickly, cataloguing—and didn't ask. Instead he sat down beside me, opened his satchel, and produced the thing he'd been working on all night: a fair copy of the rite, transcribed from Ysol's dictation, annotated in his cramped archival hand.

"There's a problem," he said. "There's always a problem. The rite requires continuous reading. No pause longer than a breath, or the crown reasserts. At current length, the ledger runs—" he counted, lips moving, "—eleven hours. Perhaps twelve."

"So I read for twelve hours."

"And every line returns a memory to the world and takes one from you." He met my eyes. "I've been trying to find a loophole. There isn't one. Archivally speaking, it's airtight. Whoever wrote this rite understood accounting better than the guild does."

"Then I'll pay," I said. "I've been paying my whole life. At least this time I get to choose what it buys."

Sef looked at me for a long moment, and something passed over his face that I recognized, because I'd seen it in mirrors during my first year at the guild: the expression of a person watching someone else walk calmly toward the exact cliff they fell off themselves. He'd lost his post for refusing to falsify an entry. He knew what institutions did to people who paid their own bills.

"We should reach the palace tonight," he said instead. "The Feast of Continuity is tomorrow. After that, the king forgets even this conversation ever happened, and the crown gets another year fatter." He began packing his satchel with the brisk motions of a man outrunning his own doubts. "Which means today you learn the rest of the rite. And Ada—" he paused, and his voice dropped into the register he used for facts he wished weren't facts, "—the collectors will come before we leave. Vane won't let the vault burn itself. We have hours, not days."

Above us, faint through the mountain's throat, I heard Ysol begin to sing—a thin, old song in a language the fire remembered—and the moths rose from the stair in answer, thousands of them, spiraling up toward the shrine's mouth like ash deciding to become birds again.

I put my hand flat on the ledger, on the page where my mother's lullaby waited to be returned to the world, and I felt—for the first time since I was six years old—not fear, but readiness.

Let them come. I had debts of my own to settle now, and for once, I was the one holding the pen.
