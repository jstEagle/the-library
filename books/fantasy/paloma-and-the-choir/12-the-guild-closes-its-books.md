## 12. The Guild Closes Its Books

The morning report came up on the freight lift with the fish deliveries, because Guildmaster Vane had long ago learned that nothing arrived faster than something someone wanted to eat.

He read it standing at his window, which overlooked the Counting Yard, where forty clerks were already bent over their slates in the cold. He did not sit. Sitting was for decisions that could wait.

*Item the first:* a moth-charm of shrine-make had entered the city two nights past and gone to ground in the Ropewalk district. A flophouse there had burned its tenant rolls in panic—moths of that kind made paper nervous—and among the ash fragments, one name recovered by a collector with steady hands: *Ada Brightwater.*

Vane read the name twice, the way he read all figures twice, because an error compounded was worse than an error alone.

*Item the second:* Brightwater had not reported to their shift. Their desk stood empty. Their inkwell was full. Their last entry, logged four days prior, concerned a cooper's wife who had traded eleven years of her wedding day for her husband's release from a debt of salt. The entry was clean. The handwriting was steady. There was no sign Brightwater had intended anything except another ordinary night of taking things from people who no longer wanted them.

That, more than anything else, told Vane the prophecy had found them. Guilt left fingerprints on a page if you knew how to look. Brightwater's page was scrubbed clean, and only fear scrubbed that well.

He set the report down and considered it as he considered everything: as a ledger problem.

A ledger problem had three parts. What is owed. To whom. And what it will cost to collect.

---

He summoned his senior collectors at the ninth bell, all seven of them, into the Long Room where the guild's charter hung behind glass and the founders' portraits watched from the walls with the mild, oil-painted satisfaction of people who had died rich.

Vane did not raise his voice. He never raised his voice. A man who raised his voice was a man whose accounts were out of order.

"The Brightwater matter," he said. "You've all seen the morning summary. I want retrieval before the moon turns. Alive, if convenient. Dead, if cheaper."

Marrow, the oldest of them, a woman built like a dock crane with hands that had closed on thirty years of debtors' throats, tilted her head. "And the moth?"

"The moth goes back where it came from. It will lead you." Vane turned to the great map of the city and the country beyond it, the one with the trade routes inked in red and the memory-yields penciled faintly along each road like rainfall figures. "The charm was sent by the keeper at the snowline shrine. That means the old woman knows the fire is guttering. That means she has found her reader."

"And the reader is Brightwater."

"Brightwater is a clerk," Vane said. "A very good clerk. I hired them myself, or rather, I arranged for a hedge-witch of my acquaintance to find them, starve them gently, and offer them a desk. You do not retrieve a prophecy. You retrieve a person, and a person is only a set of obligations wearing a coat."

Marrow almost smiled. Almost. Marrow knew better than to smile at anything before it was collected.

"Second item," Vane continued, and here he allowed himself the smallest pause, because even he understood ceremony when it served a purpose. "The shrine vault."

The room went still in the particular way rooms went still when men who counted things heard about a thing that should be counted and wasn't.

"You'll burn it," he said. "Everything. The original ledger of the binding above all. Ash, then water, then ash again. I don't care how old the paper is. I don't care what seals are on it. If it can be read aloud, it can be destroyed, and I am paying you to make certain nothing in this world can be read aloud ever again."

One of the younger collectors—a sharp boy named Pell whom Vane had marked for advancement—shifted his weight. "Master. Forgive the question. But the vault holds every memory the crown ever took. A century of harvests. Indexed. Some of those entries would fetch—"

"Fetch," Vane repeated, softly, and Pell stopped shifting.

Vane walked to the window. Below, the clerks worked on, pens scratching, small and diligent as insects turning dead wood into something useful. He loved looking at them. It was the closest thing to love he permitted himself before noon.

"Pell," he said, without turning around. "Tell me what the crown eats."

"Memories, Master."

"Wrong." He turned. "The crown eats *light*. Memory is only what light leaves behind when it passes through a person—the shadow a life casts on the inside of the skull. The dragon-fire illuminated everything it touched. Everything it touched could therefore be harvested. That is why the founders bound the crown to the king and the fire to the mountain: a lamp at one end of the kingdom, a wick-trimmer at the other, and between them a guild to carry the trimmings down and sell them by the ounce."

Pell's face had gone carefully blank, which was the correct response, though not the intelligent one. Intelligence would come later or not at all.

"Now tell me what happens when the fire dies."

Silence.

"I'll tell you, since none of you have done the reading. When the fire dies, the crown starves. The parasite unravels. The king wakes—or doesn't; I've met him; it hardly matters—and every memory the crown is holding either returns to the world or burns with it. We don't know which. *We don't know which.*" He let that stand a moment. "Do you understand what an unknown variable costs? An unindexed century of harvest, released at once, with no one holding the book? No. Of course you don't. None of you have ever held a book that mattered."

He returned to the table and laid out the orders with the flat finality of a man dealing cards he had already counted.

"So we do not let the fire die *unmanaged*. We let it die on our terms. The vault burns first. The ledger burns first. The reader is retrieved or removed. And then—" and here Vane permitted himself one sentence of plain speech per season, and spent it now— "and then we wait for the crown to starve, and we take what falls out of it."

Marrow frowned. "Take what falls out of it, Master?"

"A century of harvested memory, Marrow, sitting in a dying vessel with nowhere to go. The founders bound it to the king because they were sentimental monarchists. We are not sentimental. We are not monarchists. When the crown cracks, whatever it was feeding on does not simply evaporate—it *settles*, like coin settling in a strongbox. Someone must be holding the strongbox." He straightened the papers on the table until their edges aligned. "I intend to be holding the strongbox."

---

They filed out, and Vane remained a moment with the charter.

It hung behind glass, brown with age, its wax seal the size of a fist. He had read it perhaps four hundred times and could recite it, and like everything recited often enough it had ceased to be words and become weather. *The Ledger Guild holds in trust the debts of the realm, in years, names, and other impossible things, unto the Crown and its heirs forever.* Forever was a long word. Vane had always suspected it was also a negotiable one.

His predecessor had believed in the guild. Old Corvane had wept, actually wept, the day the yield reports began declining—the memories coming up thinner, grayer, less nourishing—and had died insisting it was a seasonal fluctuation. Sentimental to the end. The yields weren't declining seasonally. They were declining because the fire was dying, because everything the guild had built for a hundred years was built on the last breath of a murdered animal, and breaths end.

Corvane had looked at that arithmetic and chosen grief.

Vane had looked at it and chosen the other thing.

He unlocked the drawer beneath the charter and took out the thin black volume he kept there—not a ledger, exactly, though it was ruled and numbered like one. On its first page, in his own hand, dated a hundred years minus nine days ago by way of a joke only he would ever appreciate, was a single line:

*Asset acquired. Prophecy located. Reader identified.*

Below it, columns. Projected yields post-transition. Contingency schedules. A page headed, in his neatest script, *What We Keep,* and beneath that heading, so far, nothing—which was not emptiness but restraint. A good accountant did not fill in figures before they existed.

He had been asked, once, long ago, by a guild auditor with honest eyes, whether he felt anything about the work. The auditor had meant it kindly, or as kindly as auditors meant anything. Vane had answered: *I feel the entries balance.* And the auditor had nodded, satisfied, because in the Ledger Guild that was the only confession anyone required.

---

At noon the dispatches went out.

To Marrow: the retrieval order, sealed, with Brightwater's description updated—their height, their gait, the scar on the left thumb from a penknife at fourteen, the hedge-witch's mark burned discreetly into their shoulder blade as collateral, which Vane himself had authorized and which would make them easy to identify stripped and easier still to return.

To the shrine party: the burning order, with instructions Vane had drafted three times to get the sequence right. Vault first, ledger first, keeper second. Ysol was to be taken alive if she surrendered the rite's final verse, dead if she did not. The verse mattered. Vane had never learned the whole rite—he had never needed to—but he knew its shape the way a man knows the shape of a lock he intends one day to pick. A reading binds. A verse completes. Whoever completed the rite would be standing closest to the crown when it broke, and proximity, in accounting as in war, was everything.

To the city offices: a routine circular, bland as milk, ordering all branches to reconcile their year-end books early. Nothing in it mentioned the prophecy, the fire, or the end of the world. The end of the world, properly administered, appeared on no schedule anywhere. It appeared as a line item.

He sealed each packet with the guild's seal—two hands exchanging a coin, pressed deep—and sent them down on the freight lift with the afternoon fish, because nothing traveled faster than something someone wanted.

---

Evening found him at the map again, alone, the lamps trimmed low.

His finger rested on the mountain road, on the little pencil figure that marked the snowline, and he thought—not with dread, dread was for men with unbalanced books, and his books balanced—thought, rather, with the cool pleasure of near-completion, about how neatly it had all come round.

A hundred years ago the founders had written a prophecy into their own ledger—Vane had found it in the third decade of his tenure, misfiled, half-eaten by damp, and he had understood immediately what it was: not a warning, but an *inventory*. The founders had listed, in advance, everything that would one day need collecting, including the collector of the collection. A child born under a buried name. A reader with an unbound memory. They had even scheduled it, the arrogant saints, down to the date.

Tonight was the date.

Somewhere north, a dying keeper was spending her last strength on moths and messages. Somewhere on the road, a runaway clerk was learning, no doubt painfully, that they had never been invisible at all—that their whole beloved anonymity had been a cage Vane had paid a hedge-witch to build, one gentle starvation at a time. Somewhere in the palace, the king was preparing for his Feast of Continuity, rehearsing the annual forgetting that kept him soft and the crown fed.

And tomorrow, or the day after, the books would close. All of them. The guild's books. The crown's books. The kingdom's great unwritten ledger of everything it had lost.

Vane dipped his pen, opened the thin black volume to the page headed *What We Keep*, and wrote the first entry, because the moment had finally earned one:

*Begins tonight.*

Then he blotted it, squared the volume in the drawer, locked it, and rang for his supper, a man entirely satisfied with his accounts.

Outside, the first snow of the season was coming down on the Counting Yard, covering the clerks' footprints, covering everything, erasing the record of every person who had crossed the yard that day—as if the world itself had finally agreed to keep his kind of books.

By dawn, the collectors would be on the road.

By dusk, the shrine would be smoke.

And somewhere between the two, if Marrow's hands stayed as steady as they had been for thirty years, a clerk named Ada Brightwater would learn what the guild charged for trying to close your own account: everything you had, plus interest.

Vane slept well. His conscience, like his ledgers, showed no discrepancies.
