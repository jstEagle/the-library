## 13. Collectors on the Stair

The first sign was the moths.

They had been drifting through the shrine all evening in their lazy spirals, orange as embers, and then, all at once, they stopped drifting. Every moth in the great vaulted chamber rose toward the entrance stair in a single motion, like a held breath drawn upward. I had spent thirty years in archives; I knew what it meant when a system changed behavior without a written order. It meant something outside the system had arrived.

Ysol knew too. She was already moving, one hand pressed to the stone of the fire-hearth, her head cocked as though listening through the mountain itself.

"They're early," she said. "Vane always did lack patience."

"You knew he'd come?"

"I've been keeper for sixty years, boy. The guild comes every time a ledger leaves its hands." She turned to Ada, who stood frozen with the original binding-ledger clutched against their chest like a shield made of paper. "And this time we took the largest one there is."

"How long do we have?" Ada's voice came out thin.

"However long an old woman and a thousand moths can hold a staircase." Ysol smiled, and it was the worst thing I'd seen in years, because it was genuinely kind. "Go down. Into the bone-hollows. There are rooms below the dragon that even I haven't opened. Take the ledger. Don't stop for anything."

"We can't just leave you—"

"You can, and you will, and you'll hate yourselves for it later, which is proper. Shame is how the living pay the dead." She was unbuckling something from her belt as she spoke—a lantern of pierced copper, ancient, its glass smoked black. "I have work to do up here. Sixty years of it. I'd rather not be interrupted by clerks."

The mountain shook.

Not an earthquake—I'd felt earthquakes in the capital, the deep indifferent shrug of the world. This was smaller and more deliberate. A sound like a door being forced, translated through a hundred yards of granite. Then another. Rhythmic. Patient.

"They're breaking the ward-stones," Ysol said. "Each one takes about four minutes. There are eleven left between them and us." She looked at me, and her eyes were bright and terribly clear. "Archivist. You kept records once. Keep this one: whatever happens tonight, whatever they tell you happened, write it down. Memory is only real if somebody outside the event survives to hold it."

"I will," I said, and meant it more than I have ever meant anything.

"Then go."

Ada didn't move. They stood there with their jaw set in a way I would come to know very well over the following weeks—the look of someone about to do something stupid out of loyalty—and I saw the exact moment Ysol saw it too.

"The ledger," Ysol said gently, "is heavier than it looks. Not the book. What's in it. You'll need both hands free for what comes next, child, and you won't have them if you stay here arguing with an old woman who has already done the arithmetic."

Ada went. I went. And the last I saw of Keeper Ysol, she was standing before the great hearth where the last dragon-fire burned, opening her black-glass lantern so the moths could pour into it like light poured backward, and she was singing something low and old in a language that predated the kingdom, and the fire bent toward her voice like a dog hearing its name.

* * *

The bone-hollows began forty feet below the shrine chamber: passages carved—or grown—through the skeleton of the dragon itself, ribs vast as cathedral arches, vertebrae the size of houses, everything fused with age into a single seamless architecture that might have been bone or might have been stone that had forgotten the difference. The air was warm. It moved. I tried not to think about the fact that we were walking through the throat of something that had died a century ago and was somehow still breathing.

Ada carried the ledger. I carried a stub of candle I'd found on a shelf in the shrine's antechamber, because apparently the most sophisticated archival facility in the realm had no contingency lighting. Some institutions never change.

We had descended perhaps three turnings when the sounds started above us.

I want to record accurately what those sounds were, because my instinct—trained into me by thirty years of court service—is to soften them, to file them under "disturbance, unspecified." But Ysol told me to keep the record honest, so: there was light, orange-white, strobing down the stairwell after us. There was the shriek of metal on stone. There were voices, many voices, flat and procedural, calling out positions the way soldiers do. And there was Ysol's song, still going, still steady, threaded through all of it like a wire holding up something immense.

Then there was a sound I had no name for. A wet crackle, like pages burning in rain. The song stopped.

Ada stopped walking. I felt it happen beside me in the dark—the sudden absence of motion, the candle flame leaning toward them.

"Don't," I said.

"That was—"

"Don't. Listen to me." I gripped their shoulder. In the candlelight their face was gray. "If you go back up those stairs, everything she did was for nothing. That's not rhetoric, that's arithmetic. It's the only kind Vane understands, so let's beat him with it."

For a moment I thought they'd strike me. I deserved it, probably. Then Ada turned and walked on, faster than before, and I followed, and neither of us said anything else for a long time, because there was nothing true left to say that wasn't unbearable.

* * *

The collectors found the lower passages within the hour. Of course they did. Vane had run the Ledger Guild for decades; he understood buildings the way I understood books, as systems of access and control. He wouldn't send people wandering. He'd send them with maps.

The first sign was the cold. The warm breath of the mountain stopped, and behind us, down the passage we'd come through, torchlight bloomed—guild-lanterns, white and clean, the light of institutions rather than fires.

"Off the main passage," I whispered, and hauled Ada sideways into an alcove formed where two ribs met the spine of the mountain. We crouched among drifts of ash—ash, centuries of it, soft as snowfall—and killed the candle.

Six of them passed. Collectors, but not the shabby street-level debt-men I'd imagined from Ada's stories. These wore gray uniforms with the guild's seal stitched at the collar, and each carried a case of black lacquered wood, and each case, when the light caught it, hummed faintly, like a struck bell heard from very far away.

"What's in the cases?" Ada breathed against my ear.

"Ledger-tools. I saw one once, at court, before—" I stopped. Before my disgrace. Before they'd marched me out of the archive with my life's work sealed in evidence-boxes. "A memory-hook. It takes a recollection whole, out of a person's head, and files it. Used to be restricted to sentencing. Apparently Vane has widened the license."

One of the collectors paused. Ten feet away. Close enough that I could hear him breathing, could see the small professional boredom of his face. He looked directly at our alcove—for one endless second I believed we were dead—and then his gaze slid off us like water off wax.

Ada had done something. I didn't see what. Their hand was pressed flat against the ash on the floor, fingers spread, and their lips were moving without sound, and the collector frowned slightly, adjusted his case, and moved on.

Later, much later, Ada would tell me what they'd done: they had reached into the collector's attention the way their master had once taught them to reach into a sick animal, and they had given him a memory to look at instead of us—a trivial memory, a fish on a line, sunlight—which cost Ada nothing except the ability to ever again see sunlight on water without feeling sick. Everything in that shrine cost something. We were learning the exchange rate.

When the footsteps faded, we ran.

* * *

We ran through the ribs of a god for two hours, and the collectors kept finding us, and each time it got worse.

They weren't tracking us by sight or sound. I understood that by the third near-discovery. They were tracking the ledger. The book was full of names—the names of everyone whose memory the crown had eaten for a century—and names, in the guild's cosmology, are addressable. Every time Ada's grip tightened on the binding-ledger, somewhere above us a lacquered case hummed a little louder, and gray boots redirected toward the sound.

"We have to put it down," I said finally. We were wedged in a hollow beneath a vertebra the size of a granary, both of us bleeding from scrapes I couldn't remember acquiring, and I could hear the cases humming now even without the boots—two of them, three, converging. "Wrap it in ash-cloth, leave it in a side chamber, come back for it—"

"No."

"Ada—"

"It's *mine*." Their voice cracked on it. "It's my name in there. It's my master's name. It's everyone Vane sold. If I put it down, I'm doing exactly what I've done my whole life—leaving the record where other people can decide what it says." They were shaking. "You of all people should understand. You were archivist. What's a record worth if the powerful can just... take custody of it?"

"Alive," I said. "It's worth nothing if we're dead. Dead archivists don't testify."

That landed. I watched it land, watched the fury in their face collide with the truth of it, and I hated myself for using it, because it was a courtier's argument, a survivor's argument, the argument of a man who had watched colleagues be destroyed one by one and had kept his head down and his records copied until the copying itself became his crime.

But Ada thought for three seconds—three seconds with the humming getting louder—and then did something better than either of my options.

They opened the ledger.

Right there, in the dark, in the ash, with the hunters closing in. They opened it to a page near the middle and pressed their palm flat against it, and I saw their lips move again, and the humming of the cases stuttered, wavered, scattered—because for a moment the ledger's names were being read aloud, silently, and reading aloud is binding, and even half-voiced in a whisper the book became briefly impossible to locate, the way a bell rung continuously cannot be tuned by ear.

It cost them. I watched it cost them. When they closed the book their nose was bleeding and they looked at me with the expression of someone who has misplaced something in their own house and can't remember what.

"What did you lose?" I asked.

"I don't know," Ada said. "That's the point."

We ran again, and this time the running worked, because the guild's instruments had lost the thread, and we went deeper, past the last of the carved passages, into raw tunnels where the dragon's bones gave way to ordinary rock and the warmth came back—not the warm breath of the beast anymore but something older, slower, a heat rising from far below like a heart beating once per minute.

And in a widening of the tunnel—a chamber, almost circular, ribbed with ancient bone fused into the walls—we found the room Ysol had promised. Rooms below the dragon that even she hadn't opened. The door was bone. There was no lock. There was a recess in the shape of a hand.

Ada looked at it. Looked at me. Put their hand in the recess.

The door remembered them open.

I have thought about that since—about what it means that a door sealed a century ago recognized the hand of someone born twenty-three years ago—and I have an answer now, and the answer is in the ledger, on a page I have read a hundred times, and I am not ready to say it aloud yet even here, even in my own head. Later. After.

Inside: dry air, shelves cut from vertebrae, and along one wall, a case of black lacquered wood identical to the collectors'—but empty, lid open, waiting since before either of us was born for something to be put inside it.

Ada set the ledger on a shelf, and then picked it up again, and then set it down and sat on the floor beside it with their back against the bone, and put their head in their hands.

Above us, muffled through a hundred feet of stone, we heard the shrine chamber's great doors boom shut, and then a silence, and then—faint, rhythmic, patient—the sound of ward-stones breaking. Four minutes each. Eleven had remained, hours ago.

"Ysol held the stair," I said, because someone had to say it, because a fact unspoken rots into guilt. "She bought us the night. Whatever they took from her, she chose the price herself. That matters. In the record, it matters."

Ada didn't answer. Their shoulders were shaking.

I sat down beside them in the dark, in the belly of the mountain, with the ledger between us and the collectors somewhere above and the last dragon-fire guttering in its hearth a hundred feet overhead, and I did the only thing I know how to do. I took out the stub of pencil I'd taken from the shrine antechamber, and a scrap of paper, and I wrote:

*Night of the breaking of the wards. Keeper Ysol held the stair against six collectors and was taken alive. The ledger of the binding survived. Two fugitives survived. The rest is owed.*

Because shame is how the living pay the dead. But the record—that's how we make the debt collectible.

Somewhere below us, in the deep slow heat, something shifted its weight. And the bone door, which had closed behind us, began, very softly, to glow.
