## 14. Reading Aloud Is Binding

The dragon's bones were not what I expected.

I had imagined a skeleton the way the chapel murals showed them: white ribs like cathedral arches, clean and holy. But the hollows beneath the shrine were dark and brown as old blood, and the bone was warm. Not fever-warm. Warm the way a hearthstone stays warm hours after the fire dies—warmth that had nowhere left to go.

Ysol's lamp swung ahead of us, throwing shadows that moved wrong in the curved corridors of rib and vertebrae. Sef walked close behind me, one hand on my shoulder when the passage narrowed, not because I needed steadying but because he did. He hadn't said much since the stair. Since Ysol turned to hold it. Every few minutes his hand would tighten without warning, and I would pretend not to notice, and we would keep walking down into the dead animal that had held up the sky once.

"She's alive," Sef said again, quietly. "Collectors want leverage, not corpses."

"Vane wants me," I said. "Ysol is just the wrapping paper."

He didn't answer that, because there was nothing honest to say to it.

The passage opened at last into a chamber so large the lamplight failed before it reached any wall. We stood inside something like the curve of a lung, or a heart—the walls were ribbed bone, and between the ribs ran veins of ember-light, faint orange threads pulsing with a rhythm too slow to be a heartbeat. More like breathing. The breath of something that had been exhaling for a hundred years and had not yet finished.

And in the center of the chamber, floating above a basin of black stone, burned a fistful of flame no bigger than a candle.

The last dragon-fire. The thing the whole kingdom was being fed into.

It looked small. It looked tired. It looked, more than anything, *alone*, and I hated myself for the thought, because a fire cannot be alone, fire doesn't know anything—but this one had been burning since its body died, and it had been burning through us, through every memory the guild had carried up the mountain in its ledgers, and maybe that was how a fire learned about loneliness: secondhand, from everything it consumed.

"Sit," Ysol said—not Ysol, she was on the stair, buying us time with fire-moths and whatever else a keeper of a century has left to spend. This was her voice though, coming from everywhere, from the ember-veins in the bone. Her voice in the walls. "No. Don't sit. Stand where you are and look at the basin. Look at what's under the flame."

I looked. Beneath the little guttering fire, in the black stone of the basin, something was written. Lines upon lines of it, cut deep and rubbed with ash, circling the stone like text around a coin.

"That's not writing," Sef said, stepping forward. Then he stopped. "That's—that *is* writing. That's First Ledger script. That's the oldest form of guild-hand there is." His voice changed, the way it always changed when he touched something archival: steadier, hungrier, a man finding solid ground. "Ada. That's the original ledger."

* * *

Ysol had told us, back in the shrine above, before the stair: *the ledger isn't a book. Books are copies. The ledger is the binding itself.* But hearing it and standing inside it were different animals.

Sef circled the basin slowly, reading by lamplight and by ember-light both, his lips moving. I watched him work because watching him work was easier than looking at the fire. He read the way other people prayed—with his whole spine.

"It's indexed," he murmured. "Every entry indexed by... oh." He stopped. "Oh, Ada."

"Don't say my name like that."

"By district. By year. By—" he swallowed, "—by price paid. Seven years of luck. One mother's face. A war nobody remembers because the whole war went into the crown at once, here, this entry, year ninety-one of the binding, 'the late unpleasantness, settled in full.'" He laughed, a terrible small sound. "They wrote *settled in full*. They wrote it like an invoice."

"The guild writes everything like an invoice," I said. My voice came out flat and strange. "That's the trick of it. You can make yourself do anything if you write it small enough."

He looked at me across the basin then, over the dying fire, and I saw him decide to tell me something he'd been carrying since the shrine. "There's a colophon," he said. "At the base. Founder's marks. Four names." He touched the stone. "You should see them."

"I don't want to."

"You need to."

I made myself walk around the basin. Made myself look down past the flame, past the heat-shimmer, at the four names cut into the foundation of the binding itself, in the hand of whoever had first taught the crown to eat.

One of them was Brightwater.

Not Ada Brightwater. *Maren Brightwater.* My master. My hedge-witch. The woman whose kitchen smelled of rosemary and burnt sugar, who taught me which mushrooms lied, who took payment for charms in eggs and gossip and never once in years. Who was, all along, a collector. Who took my childhood as collateral against debts I never knew I owed, and filed me away until the date came due.

I had known it since chapter eleven of my own life—I had faced it on the road, or told myself I had. But knowing a thing and seeing your teacher's name carved into the floor beneath a fire that eats memories are two different weights, and this one went through my boots into the bone.

"She founded it," I said. "She didn't just collect for them. She built it."

"Or she was built into it," Sef said gently. "Founders' marks can be signatures or sentences. Without her papers—" He stopped himself. He was learning, on this road, which comforts were worth offering. Most weren't.

The fire flickered. Low. Lower than it had been an hour ago. I could feel it now that I stood close—a kind of pressure dropping, the way air goes still before snow.

"How long?" I asked the walls, meaning Ysol's voice, meaning the keeper's knowledge soaked into this place.

"Soon," said the ember-veins, in her voice, fainter than before. "A day. Maybe less. When the fire gutters out, everything it holds goes with it—and everything the realm still remembers is only what the crown hasn't gotten to yet. You've seen the villages. You've seen the gaps."

We had. Villages that could not grieve their dead because they couldn't remember having any. A woman on the mountain road who introduced herself three times in an hour and each time the name was different and none of them were hers. Children who didn't know their grandparents' faces. A country forgetting how to mourn, one quiet ledger-entry at a time.

"So we read it," I said. "That's the rite. You told me on the road. Read the ledger aloud in the crown's presence and every line comes home."

"Yes," said the voice in the bones. And then, after a silence in which even the fire seemed to listen: "Come closer, child. Both of you. There are terms you don't understand yet, and I'd rather you heard them from me than from the crown."

* * *

We sat—we sat after all, our backs against the great warm curve of a vertebra the size of a wagon—and Ysol's voice taught us the unbinding rite, and the rite had teeth.

"It must be read aloud," she said. "Silent reading is nothing; the binding was spoken into the world and it must be spoken out of it. It must be read in the crown's presence, within sight of the king who wears it, because the parasite lives in the space between his head and that ring of metal, and you cannot unmake a thing except where it stands. And—" here the voice paused, gathering itself, an old woman deciding how to say a hard thing plainly, "—it must be read by someone whose name appears in the ledger. A party to the debt. Not a bystander. The words won't come otherwise. They'll be just words. You'll read until your throat bleeds and nothing will move."

Sef and I looked at each other.

His name wasn't in it. I knew that before he shook his head—he was archivist, witness, thief of documents, but he'd never sold anything to the guild and the guild had never collected from him. Disgraced, yes. Indebted, no. He was the freest man in the room.

My name was in it. Had been since Maren Brightwater signed me over, entry by entry, year by year of my childhood. *The girl's fourth summer. The girl's sixth winter. The girl's memory of her mother singing, taken against future consideration.*

"And the price?" I asked. Because there was always a price; I had worked in the Ledger Guild long enough to know that no door opens without a toll, and the doors that open easiest charge the most.

The ember-veins pulsed, slow, slow.

"Every word you read aloud returns a memory to the world," Ysol said. "That part is true and it is beautiful and it will work. What no one tells you—what the founders' texts omit, because omissions are how the guild prays—is where the returned memory *comes from*."

The chamber was very quiet. Even the fire's breathing seemed to hold.

"A reading is a transfer," she went on. "The binding doesn't give back. It trades. For every memory the ledger releases, it takes one of equal weight from the mouth doing the reading. You speak a fisherman's luck back into him, you lose something of your own to fill the shape. You restore a mother's face to a village, and somewhere behind your eyes a face goes blank. Not chosen. Not chosen at all—you don't get to pick which ones go. The ledger picks. The ledger has been picking for a hundred years and it has gotten very good at knowing what a person can least afford to lose."

I felt cold, sitting next to a fire.

"How much is in it?" Sef asked, and his voice was careful, archivally careful, the voice of a man cataloguing a catastrophe. "How many entries?"

"Ninety-one years of harvest," said the keeper in the bones. "From a kingdom. Read at full length, start to colophon, the reader would empty entirely. There would be a body walking out of this mountain with no one home in it. The rite doesn't require completeness, mind. The crown cracks when enough of its hoard returns to destabilize it—half, perhaps, read with true intent. Half of ninety-one years." She let the arithmetic sit there between us, enormous and patient.

Half of a kingdom's stolen memory, given back. Half of mine, taken.

"That's why the prophecy named you," Ysol said, softer. "Not because you're strong. Because you were already partly spent. Your master sold your childhood off in pieces before you were ten years old, Ada Brightwater. There's less in you to lose than almost anyone. The ledger reads its own kind. It's cruel arithmetic, but it's arithmetic."

*I take pride in being nobody,* I had written, once, in my own neat clerk's hand, recording some fisherman's seven good years against one safe voyage. *Invisible among the ink-stained clerks.* I had thought my emptiness was a gift. A talent. A way to survive. Turns out it was inventory. Turns out someone had counted me decades ago and found me conveniently light.

Sef put his hand over mine. His fingers were ink-stained even now, even here; some things survive disgrace.

"There might be another way," he said. "There's always another way. We haven't checked the marginalia—"

"Sef."

"We haven't *checked*, Ada. I'm an archivist. I don't accept a text's account of itself until I've read the footnotes."

"And what do the footnotes say about Vane?" I asked. "About the collectors on the stair? About Ysol?" The name cracked something in my chest when I said it. She was up there right now, holding a doorway with moths and stubbornness, and every minute we spent learning the cost of saving the world was a minute she paid interest on.

The voice in the bones answered before Sef could. "Go and check your marginalia later, archivist. Tonight the girl needs to understand what she'll be carrying into that palace." A pause. "Because she will carry it. Won't you. I've kept this fire a hundred years and I know the type. The ones who think being nobody means owing nothing. It took me sixty years to learn that nobody owes nothing—nobody owes *everything*. Somebody has to stand in the space where the payment goes through. It's the only job nobody volunteers for and everybody understands."

"I'm not brave," I said.

"No," she agreed, kindly. "Brave people frighten me. I want someone who's frightened and does it anyway. That's rarer."

The fire guttered. Actually guttered, visibly, the little flame bending sideways toward nothing, and for one horrible moment the whole chamber dimmed, the ember-veins stuttering, and I felt the absence reach into my skull and rummage—not taking, just *looking*, the way a collector looks over goods—and I understood with total clarity that the thing wearing the king's crown could feel me sitting in its larder, reading the menu.

Then the flame steadied. Barely.

"It's time," Ysol's voice said, and now it sounded thin, stretched, a voice at the end of a long stair. "The collectors bought an hour. The hour is spent. You have to go tonight—through the old ways, down the mountain, to the city. Feast of Continuity is in three days. The king forgets his coronation; the crown feeds openly; that's your one window when it stands still long enough to be read to." A cough of embers. "Take the ledger."

"You can't move the ledger," Sef said sharply. "The basin—"

"The basin is a desk, archivist, not an anchor. The binding travels with its text. Cut free the first leaf and the rest follows—it wants to travel; it's been hungry a long time." Something in the ember-light shifted, and I swear the whole vast ribcage around us sighed. "But hear me, both of you, and hear me exactly: the ledger cannot be forced open, cannot be copied, cannot be read by proxy. It opens for the named. It opens for Ada. And once she begins to read aloud—in the crown's presence, with true intent—she cannot stop of her own will. The rite holds the reader. She stops when the crown breaks, or when she's empty, whichever arrives first."

"So she reads until she breaks it," Sef said, "or until there's no Ada left to read."

"Now you understand the terms," said the keeper. "Welcome to accounting."

* * *

Cutting the first leaf took both of us and a knife Sef kept for opening sealed dispatches, and it was the worst physical sensation I have ever felt, worse than the moth-charm burning my palm. The ledger did not want to be cut. The ledger *screamed*—not aloud, but in the way the air screams, in pressure and wrongness—and as Sef sawed through that first page's edge I felt every entry in it turn its attention on me at once, ninety-one years of harvested lives noticing the knife, noticing *me*, and hating me, and needing me, both at once, the way the indebted always hate and need their creditor.

When the leaf came free, the rest followed. The pages lifted out of the stone like smoke pouring upward, gathering, folding themselves into a book—a real book now, heavy and grey, bound in something that might have been dragon-hide and might have been grief—and dropped into my arms with a weight like a sleeping child.

Like something entrusted. Like something that intended to feed on me and wanted me comfortable first.

Sef packed while I sat against the vertebra with the book in my lap. He moved fast and quiet, the way he'd moved through the archive annexes of his former life, wrapping the lamp, checking the passages. Once he paused beside me and looked down at the ledger, and I watched him physically restrain himself from touching it, hands curling shut at his sides.

"If we find another way," he said. "Marginalia. Precedent. A loophole—"

"You'd love that, wouldn't you. A footnote big enough to hide in."

"I'd love *anything*," he said, with sudden force, "that keeps you from paying ninety-one years of other people's bills out of what's left of your own head. Yes. I'd love that. Sue me."

For a moment—just a moment, there in the dark of the dead dragon's heart—I wanted to laugh. It startled me, the wanting. I hadn't wanted to laugh in years. That's what fear does at high altitude, maybe. Or maybe that's what Sef does. Maybe that's what it costs to stop being invisible: you start wanting ridiculous things, loudly, where witnesses can see.

"Read the last line to me," I said instead. "Of the prophecy. The one that cuts off mid-sentence."

He crouched beside me. He knew it by heart now; he recited it soft, in the lamplight, in the breathing dark:

"*And on the night the fire fails, the one called Ada Brightwater shall open the book of what was taken, and shall give back the kingdom to itself, and the price shall be—*"

"And the price shall be," I finished. "That's the whole sentence. That's where it ends."

"Prophecies are drafts," Sef said. "Someone finished it. Someone always finishes it. We just have to find the ending—or write it." He stood, offered me his hand, and pulled me up. The ledger shifted in my arms, settling, patient as a debt.

Above us, far up the mountain, I thought I heard—though it may have been wind in the ribs of the world—the sound of the stair finally falling.

Ysol had bought us an hour. The hour was spent.

I put the ledger under my coat, against my chest, where I could feel it breathing, and we went down into the dark toward the city, toward the crown, toward the reading.

Every word would cost. I knew that now, fully, the way you know the weight of a thing only once you've agreed to carry it.

I carried it anyway.
