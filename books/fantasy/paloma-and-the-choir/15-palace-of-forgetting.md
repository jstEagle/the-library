## 15. Palace of Forgetting

The credentials still worked.

That was the first horror of it, standing at the servants' gate of the palace with a forged writ in one hand and Ada Brightwater's cold fingers in the other: that the palace had not bothered to strike me from its rolls. Three years since the scandal, three years since they'd carried my desk out into the courtyard and burned my papers in front of the junior clerks as a lesson about ambition—and still the gate-warden glanced at my seal, grunted, and waved us through like a man waving through weather.

"They didn't remove you," Ada murmured as we passed under the arch. "They just stopped looking."

"That's worse," I said. "Removal is an act of memory. This is just—" I gestured vaguely at the whole shining edifice above us, the towers going gold in the late light, the banners snapping with their stitched continuity. "Housekeeping."

Ada had dressed for it. That had been the hardest part, harder than the forgery, harder than the bribes we no longer had money for. The guild had taught them invisibility so thoroughly that putting Ada in court clothes was like dressing smoke. The doublet kept sliding off them somehow—not literally, but to the eye. Twice on the road up from the shrine I'd turned around and been genuinely startled to find them still beside me, as if some part of me had already filed them under *not here*. Tonight that would be useful. Tonight I needed a person who could stand in a crowded hall and simply not be perceived.

But first they had to get through the door.

* * *

I had forgotten—no, *chosen* to forget, which is different and more shameful—how beautiful the palace was during the Feast of Continuity.

They held it every year on the anniversary of the king's coronation, though no one called it that anymore; they called it the Feast because calling it anything more specific felt rude, the way you don't say the word *dead* at a bedside. The Great Hall blazed with a thousand candles whose wax had never been permitted to gutter. Musicians played from the gallery, something old and circular that never quite resolved. And through the middle of it all moved the court, in silks the color of candlelight, orbiting—there was no other word for it—*orbiting* the dais where the king sat beneath the crown.

I watched them circle and thought of Ysol's moths. The keeper had sent her charm across half a kingdom to find Ada, and here was the same motion performed by people in embroidered shoes: drawn helplessly toward a light that would eat them. They never touched the dais. They circled to a precise radius and then wheeled away, laughing, drinking, complimenting one another's continuity.

"Look at the crown," I said quietly. We stood near the wine table, which is the best place in any hall to be ignored. "Don't look at it. But look at it."

Ada looked. I saw their pupils do something complicated.

"It's not gold," they said. "Underneath. It's—"

"Bone. Dragon bone, if Ysol's right, beaten flat and gilded over. A century ago." I turned my back to the dais, which took effort; the crown has a gravity, even seen sideways in a polished spoon. "And once a year, on this night, the king sits under it and forgets his own coronation. That's the ceremony. That's what we're celebrating."

"How does that work? As a feast?"

"The herald announces the king's virtues. All of them. Every title, every deed, every name-day, read out in full, because by evening he'll have lost them all and the list has to be rebuilt from scratch." I picked up a cup of wine and did not drink it. "It takes four hours. There are clerks who do nothing else. I trained two of them." My voice did something I didn't authorize. "Before."

Ada was watching the king. I let myself watch them watch him, which was safer.

From this distance His Majesty was only a pale shape under the canopy, hands folded in his lap, wearing the patient expression of a man waiting for a haircut. Once an hour, by the clock, an attendant leaned in and whispered something to him—the hour, probably, or his own name—and the king would nod and smile at the hall, and the hall would sigh with affection, and the orbiting would continue.

"He looks kind," Ada said.

"He was," I said. "Is. Whatever's left. That's the thing nobody at court will say aloud, Ada. The king isn't mad. Mad kings you can depose. He's *hollowed*. Something got into him a century ago—I don't know how, Ysol doesn't know how, maybe the binding *was* the coronation—and it eats him a little more every year, and it pays for its dinner with everyone else's memories, harvested through the guild's ledgers, and the realm calls the resulting appetite a *feast*."

A chamberlain drifted past us and paused, just long enough. He was a soft man with a face like risen dough, and I knew him—knew him the way you know anyone from the archive, by their signature. Chamberlain Odo Vess. His hand appeared on forty years of supply requisitions.

"You're not on tonight's list," he said pleasantly, to me.

"No one's on tonight's list," I said. "That's rather the point of the evening."

He smiled at that, the way you smile at a child who has said something accidentally profound, and his eyes went over my shoulder to the dais and came back, and there was a moment—a very small moment, the length of a swallowed word—where I understood that Odo Vess did not know why he served the crown either. None of them did. They had all signed pieces of themselves away somewhere down the years, a luck here, a grief there, a war, a father's face, and what remained was devotion with the reason scraped out of it.

"Enjoy the feast," he said, and orbited on.

Ada exhaled beside me. "He looked right through me."

"He looked through both of us. That's the dress code." I set down the untouched wine. "Now listen, because we're past the point where I can be subtle. The vault beneath the east wing connects to the chapel crypt, and the crypt connects to this hall through the antechamber behind the dais. I helped design the cataloguing system for that vault when I was twenty-three and insufferable, and my seal still opens the outer door because—as established—no one remembers to close accounts here. We go down through the servants' stair, open the vault, and wait for the reading to reach its midpoint, when the hall empties of anyone with sense. Then we come up behind the dais, and you do whatever it is you have to do with that ledger, and I keep the doors."

"And if the crown notices us before then?"

I looked at it again, despite myself. Under the thousand candles it seemed to drink the light and give back something older, and for half a heartbeat I had the sensation—faint, obscene—of being *read*. Of being an entry.

"Then we find out what it offers us," I said, "and pray we're cheaper than a kingdom."

* * *

The servants' stair smelled of tallow and honest work, which after the hall was almost restful. We descended past laundry yards and the kitchens, where cooks were preparing the ceremonial dishes—foods the king had loved in his first year of reign, remade annually from recipes preserved in my old archive, because taste, apparently, was too important to trust to the man himself. Ada trailed a hand along the wall and said nothing, but I could feel them counting exits. The guild teaches its clerks architecture whether it means to or not.

At the bottom, the corridor to the east wing ran long and cold, lit every ten paces by lamps that were, I noticed with archival unease, unlit flames—wicks burning without oil, without consumption. Dragon-fire leavings, maybe. Or the crown's attention, pooled in the corners of its own house.

My seal opened the vault door with a sound like a held breath released.

Inside: the smell of paper. Ten thousand documents under dust sheets, the kingdom's continuous memory, and I stood in the doorway of it feeling like a priest returned to a god he'd renounced. Ada walked straight past all of it to the far shelf, where I'd told them the coronation regalia inventories lived, and pulled the ledger from their coat—the true ledger, Ysol's ledger, the original record of the binding, wrapped in oilcloth against the mountain damp.

"We have until the fourth bell," I said. "The herald reaches the military titles around then. Half the officers leave the hall to be sick with sentiment, and the guards rotate. After the fourth bell, the antechamber stands empty for perhaps twenty minutes."

"And the king?"

"Never leaves the dais. Not during the feast. He isn't permitted." I hesitated. "He tried, once. Year before my disgrace. Got as far as the third step down. They read him back to himself until he sat again."

Ada was quiet a moment. In the lamplight—those wrong, oil-less flames—their face had the look I'd started to recognize as their thinking face: nothing moving, everything moving underneath.

"When I read it to him," they said, "the ledger. When I start returning things." They turned the wrapped bundle over in their hands. "Sef. The rite takes from the reader. You know what I'll lose."

"I know the arithmetic."

"Then say the arithmetic."

I could have lied to them. It occurred to me, standing in that vault among ten thousand truths filed and shelved, that lying was available, easy, and would change nothing except how much they trusted me later. So I did the archivist thing. I cited sources.

"Ysol's notes. Each line read aloud returns its memory to the world and strikes one from the reader. She estimated the full ledger at—" I made myself say it. "At nine hundred lines. Ada, you have maybe six hundred memories left that are properly yours. The math doesn't close. It was never going to close. You'll burn out before the last page, unless something interrupts us, or unless—" I stopped.

"Unless the fire finishes what the reading starts." They said it evenly. They had done this sum on the road, days ago, while I slept. They had known longer than I had allowed myself to know. "So don't interrupt."

Above us, muffled through floors of stone, the orchestra changed to the second movement of the circular piece, and faintly, like weather, the herald's voice began the recitation of titles. *His Majesty, by grace, by flame, by the covenant of the crown—*

"Covenant of the crown," I repeated bitterly. "We wrote that phrase. Archive staff. 1120. I've catalogued the draft. There were three versions and the other two mentioned *payment*."

Ada looked up at me then, and for a moment the invisibility fell off them entirely, the way it sometimes did, and I was looking at a person the crown itself had named a century before they were born. "Then let's go correct the record," they said. "You're the archivist. Isn't that the job?"

It was. It had always been the job. I had disgraced myself refusing to falsify one entry, one entry, three years ago, and here was the whole falsified kingdom asking to be set right by a hedge-witch's runaway and a burnt clerk.

"Fourth bell," I said. "Twenty minutes. Stay behind me until the antechamber, and whatever happens—whatever it *offers* you—keep walking toward the king."

We climbed toward the light and the music, two entries in the ledger of the realm, unwritten yet.

Behind the dais, the antechamber was exactly as empty as promised. Through its open doors I could see the hall blazing, the court mid-orbit, and the pale king beneath the crown, hands folded, smiling his patient smile at a list of virtues being read back to him like a lullaby.

And then, between one bell-toll and the next, the king stopped smiling.

He turned his head—all the way, unnaturally, sweetly—toward the antechamber doors. Toward us. Toward Ada.

"There you are," said the crown, in a voice like many names spoken at once. "The ledger is overdue. Come in and settle your account."
