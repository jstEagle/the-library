## 16. The King Who Isn't There

The Feast of Continuity is a beautiful thing to be invisible inside.

Sef's old credentials got us through the first three doors. His face did the rest—archivists are furniture, and furniture is never stopped at feasts. By the fourth door I had a tray of candied plums in my hands and a livery sash someone had abandoned over a chair, and I was simply another servant in a palace full of them, carrying sweetness toward people who had forgotten they'd ever asked for it.

The hall was gold. That's the honest description. Gold leaf on the pillars, gold thread in the banners, gold in the wine cups, and above all of it, at the far end of the hall, the king's dais burning with a hundred candles whose light seemed to bend toward one point: the crown.

I had seen drawings of it in Sef's notes. A band of black metal set with a stone the color of deep water. The drawings hadn't prepared me for the way the room *leaned* toward it. Courtiers drifted near the dais the way moths drift near a lamp—not hungry, not desperate, just pulled, their faces slack with a contentment that looked, from a distance, like love.

"They orbit," Sef had told me on the road. "They've given the crown little pieces of themselves for years. A grudge here, a grief there. It keeps them docile and it keeps itself fed between harvests." He'd said it flatly, the way he said everything, but his hands had been tight on the reins.

Now I carried my plums through that gentle gravity, and felt it tug at me too. Not memories—I was mostly hollow there, thanks to the reading's price still waiting ahead of me—but *want*. The crown hummed at the edge of hearing, a note like a struck glass, and every time I looked at it I thought: *you could stop running. You could put down the ledger. You could sleep.*

I bit the inside of my cheek until it bled and kept walking.

* * *

The king sat apart.

That surprised me. I'd expected him enthroned at the center of the feast, the parasite wearing him like a glove. Instead he was in a small alcove off the eastern colonnade, behind a screen of gilded reeds, with a single cup of watered wine and no guards. As if the palace knew—as if the *crown* knew—that no one would ever think to look for the king anywhere but the throne.

He was smaller than his coins. That was my first stupid thought, standing in the shadow of the reed screen with my tray of plums: *he's smaller than his coins.* A thin man in a plain white robe, no jewels except the one on his head, his hands folded in his lap like a boy made to sit still through a long sermon. Grey at the temples. Eyes the color of rain over the harbor.

He was talking to himself. Or to someone.

"—and then she said the garden was my mother's, but I don't have a mother, do I, I must have had one, everyone has one—" He stopped. Turned his head. Looked directly at me through the gap in the reeds, and his eyes were wet and terribly clear. "You're not a servant."

"I'm carrying plums," I said, which was true and useless.

"You're carrying plums," he agreed, gently, as if humoring a child. "But you're not a servant. Servants look at the floor. You look at me like you know something about me." He smiled, and it was the loneliest smile I have ever seen. "Everyone looks at me like that lately. Like I'm a door they're afraid to open."

I should have walked away. Every plan Sef and I had made said: approach the throne during the toast, when the hall's attention pooled around the crown like grease around a wick. This alcove, this man, this moment—it wasn't in the plan.

But he said, softly, "Please. Whoever you are. Tell me who I am."

And I understood, suddenly, why Ysol had spent sixty years alone on a mountain. Some questions you can't refuse. They get into you through the ears and live there.

* * *

I set the tray down on a bench. Sat beside him. Close enough that I could see the crown's stone shift color when I moved—a slow pulse, deep water going shallow and back again, like something breathing under ice.

"What do you remember?" I asked.

"Today?" He thought. Genuinely thought, the way you watch a man search pockets he knows are empty. "Today there was a feast. There is always a feast. They call it the Feast of Continuity, which I have always thought is an odd name for a party." He laughed a little. "Every year. Every year the same feast, and every year I wake up the next morning and the year before is... smooth. Like a stone worn by water. I know I was king. I can't remember being crowned."

"Do you want to?"

His hands unclenched in his lap. "That's the terrible thing," he said. "Yes. More than anything. And also—" his voice dropped, went young, went scared— "I'm frightened of what's under the smooth parts. When I press on them, something presses back."

The crown pulsed. Once. Slowly. Like a heart deciding whether to bother beating.

"Your name," I said. "Do you know your own name?"

"Aldric," he said promptly. Then, slower: "Aldric. Yes. But I only know it the way you know a word in a language you don't speak. I can say it. I can't feel it." He turned to me fully, and the candlelight caught the wet in his eyes, and I had to look away because looking at him was like looking at someone drowning in a very calm lake. "You know me. I can tell. Your face does the thing faces do when they're holding something back. Please. Tell me one true thing about myself. One. I'll give you anything."

"The last person who traded with this palace gave seven years of luck for one safe voyage," I said, before I could stop myself. "Be careful offering things here. Everything in this building is priced."

He flinched. Actually flinched, like I'd struck him. "What is this place?" he whispered.

And the crown woke.

* * *

I didn't hear it wake so much as the room changed pressure. The distant roar of the feast dimmed, as if a door had closed. The candle flames stood perfectly straight. And the stone in the black band turned the color of a pupil—wide, dark, attentive—and I felt it *look* at me.

Not at the alcove. At me. At the place where my memories used to be.

"There you are," it said.

It spoke inside my skull, in a voice assembled out of other voices—the way a choir makes one note out of many throats. I recognized some of them. The Guildmaster's dry cadence. My hedge-witch master's lilt. A fisherman's burr. Voices I had read into ledgers. Voices I had read *out* of myself.

"You came all this way," the crown said, warm as a hand on the back of the neck. "Through the rain, past the collectors, down into the bone-hollows and up again. Carrying that heavy book. For what? To hurt a man who has done you no harm? Look at him."

I looked. Aldric had gone still, his eyes fixed on the middle distance, breathing shallowly. Held, I realized. The way you hold a door shut.

"He is comfortable," the crown said. "He is kind. He is beloved. Every year we take the sharpness out of him and he wakes grateful and gentle and free of grief. You would take that from him. You would give him back his dead and his failures and his fear, and call it mercy." A pause, delicate as a knife laid on silk. "Why?"

"Because it's his," I said.

"Is it?" The voice curled around the word. "Was anything ever anyone's? You of all people know how fluid ownership is. You worked in the ledgers. Debts move. Names move. Memories move." The pressure in the room increased, gentle, insistent, like deep water. "You fled your master. You fled your guild. You have been running your whole life from the people who claim to own you. And now you carry a book that says you belong to a prophecy. Tell me, Ada Brightwater—"

My birth name. In the crown's mouth. The guild had fed it everything.

"—what is yours?"

And then it offered.

* * *

It didn't show me gold or thrones. It knew better. It showed me a kitchen.

A low-ceilinged kitchen with herbs hanging from the beams, and rain on the window, and a woman humming while she chopped onions, and I knew her voice before I knew her face—knew it the way you know a word in a language you don't speak—and the crown said, tenderly, *your mother*, and the smell of the onions filled my nose, real, immediate, and I was crying without having decided to.

"We kept them," the crown murmured. "Everything the reading will burn out of you—we keep it safe. Your master's face. Her laugh, when she laughed. Your mother's voice, saying your name. The dog you had when you were six. The first spell you ever cast, and how proud she was." The kitchen glowed. The woman turned, smiling, onion knife still in hand, and began to say my name—

"All of it comes back to you," the crown said. "Whole. Tonight. You walk out of this palace, you leave the book in the bone-hollows, you let the fire gutter out quietly, and tomorrow morning you wake up *full*. Every memory you were promised to lose, returned. No debt. No price. Consider it—" and here it used the Guildmaster's exact dry tone, and I hated how much I loved hearing it— "*a struck entry.*"

The woman in the kitchen opened her mouth.

"Stop," I said.

She stopped. Frozen, smiling, mid-breath. And I understood what I was looking at: not my mother. A memory of my mother, held in a thing that ate memories, served back to me as bait. She was as much mine as the fisherman's luck had been his. Something taken, warehoused, and sold back at interest.

"That's not her," I said. My voice shook. "You don't keep things safe. You keep things. There's a difference."

The kitchen flickered. The crown's attention cooled by a degree, the way a merchant's does when a customer sees the trick.

"She begged too," I said, standing, my knees unsteady, my eyes streaming. "In whatever voice you took her in. Everyone you ever ate begged. And you wore their voices at me just now like a coat." I wiped my face with my sleeve. "You want to offer me a trade? Here's mine. Let him go. Give him back his coronation, his griefs, his name with the feeling in it. And I'll read the book slowly."

Silence. The candles leaned.

"No," said the crown, in a hundred stolen voices at once, and the gentleness was gone, and underneath the gentleness was the sound of a century of harvested lives all screaming politely in unison. "No. We had rather—"

The feast hall roared. A toast. Hundreds of cups rising, and the crown's attention snapped toward the sound like a dog to a whistle, and in that half-second of divided attention, King Aldric's eyes cleared.

He grabbed my wrist. His grip was stronger than his size suggested, and desperate.

"Don't listen to it," he whispered, fast, urgent. "Whatever it shows you—it showed me my coronation once. Made-up. Beautiful. I cried. It was lies, Ada Brightwater, it was all lies, it only ever gives you back what keeps you quiet—" His eyes went to the ledger under my cloak, then to my face, and something in him understood everything at once, the way the drowning understand water. "You're here to end it."

"Yes."

"Then end it," he said, "and don't count what it costs you. That's the trap. It wants you counting." He released my wrist and stepped back, and lifted his chin, and the crown settled onto his brow like a bird folding its wings, and his eyes went smooth and distant again, and the last thing the clear part of him said, before the tide came back in, was:

"It's almost midnight. It gets *hungry* at midnight."

Down the colonnade, the feast hall erupted in cheering. Somewhere beyond the doors, boots on marble—many boots, moving fast, in step. Vane's collectors had found the palace too.

And in my palm, faint but unmistakable, the moth-charm Ysol had pressed there began to burn.
