## 18. The Reading

The ledger was heavier than it looked.

That was the first thing I understood as Sef pressed it into my hands behind the pillar of unlit candles: weight is not mass. The book was ordinary calfskin, ordinary size, its corners worn soft the way any ledger wears soft after a century of hands. But holding it was like holding a sleeping animal. It had a pulse. It had an opinion about being carried. It wanted, very badly, to be put down.

"Don't put it down," Sef said, reading my face in the dark. He had his old credentials strung around his neck like a noose he'd decided to wear as jewelry. "It knows what you're thinking. Everything in this palace knows what you're thinking tonight."

"Comforting."

"It's not meant to be." He glanced out across the Great Hall, at the Feast of Continuity in full glitter. "Ada. Once you start, you can't stop. You know that."

"I wrote the rules of debt for six years," I said. "I know how a contract works. You open your mouth, you've already paid."

The hall before us was a bowl of gold light and music. Long tables ran its length, loaded with food no one would remember eating. Courtiers moved through the candle-glare in their feast-clothes, orbiting the dais the way I'd once seen moths orbit Ysol's shrine-lamps — patient, hungry, not knowing why. And on the throne sat the king, wearing his crown, wearing his gentle ruined smile, while a hundred people toasted a man who could not have told them his own mother's name.

Above him, the crown did something I will never be able to describe properly. It didn't glow. It *attended*.

The thing inside it had noticed me the moment I entered the hall. I felt its attention like a cold finger drawn down the back of my neck, patient as an auditor's pen.

"You could still walk out," Sef said quietly. "I mean it. There are worse fates than being nobody. You were good at it."

"I was," I agreed. "And it cost everyone else everything."

I stepped out from behind the pillar.

* * *

Crossing that hall was the longest walk of my life, and I have since walked through fire, so let the comparison stand.

The music didn't stop — that was the crown's first move. It simply made the music *mean* something else. As I crossed the marble, the notes rearranged themselves in every listener's ear into a memory of me: a clerk, yes, but a thief-clerk, a guild traitor, someone who had once shorted a fisherman seven years of luck and laughed about it. I watched a duchess's face curdle with recognition of a crime she had never witnessed. I heard the whisper travel table to table like oil finding seams: *that's her, that's the one, they say she sold her own name—*

False memory, flooding the room. The crown didn't need to move. It only needed to lend the court a past, the way a moneylender lends coin, and the court spent it instantly, gratefully.

I kept walking. My legs knew the trick of being unnoticed even when a whole hall was being taught to hate me; six years of invisibility doesn't leave you just because a parasite objects. I reached the foot of the dais. I looked up at the king.

Up close he was worse than in the distance. His eyes were kind and vacant, twin windows with the shutters painted on. His feast-clothes fit a body someone else had chosen for him years ago.

"Do I know you?" he asked. His voice cracked with hope. That was the worst part — the hope. A century of forgetting, and he still hoped each new face might be the one that told him who he was.

"No," I said. "But I'm going to tell you."

I opened the ledger.

* * *

Here is what the rite required, and here is what no one had told me plainly enough, though perhaps no one could have: reading aloud is binding. Not metaphorically. The words in that book were debts, and a debt spoken by the debtor becomes a payment. Every line I read aloud would return a stolen memory to the world —

and take one from me.

Not a random one. A corresponding one. The ledger was indexed, and I was in it, and the crown's accounting was exact. For every year of luck I gave back to a fisherman's grandchildren, I would lose a year of something of mine. The book would choose what. The book always chooses what.

My name appeared in it. That was the point. Only an unbound memory can read the ledger aloud — someone whose name was written there but never collected, a loose thread in a century of careful stitching. Master Wren had known. That was why she'd marked me as a child. That was why I'd run.

And running had been the payment all along. Running had kept me unbound.

I found the first line. My finger shook against the vellum. Around me the court was rising now, the crown pouring fresh falsehoods into them — guards drifting toward me with the dreamy confidence of men who remembered arresting me twice already. Sef was somewhere behind me doing something loud and archivally illegal to buy seconds. None of it mattered. What mattered was the line under my finger:

*Year 43 of the Binding. Harvested: the taste of river-water in high summer, from the city entire.*

I read it aloud.

"The taste of river-water in high summer — returned."

The word *returned* left my mouth and left *me*. I felt it go: a small bright fact lifted out of my chest like a splinter drawn clean. I have no idea now what river-water tastes like in summer. I know the sentence. I can recite it. The knowing is gone, and in the great hall a thousand people gasped at once, mouths filling with a flavor they'd lost without ever missing it, and three old women began to weep into their wine cups because suddenly they were eight years old again, knee-deep in the Brightwater, and they didn't know why.

That was the arithmetic. That was the trade. One small joy of mine, purchased back for a city.

The guards stopped. They stood blinking, holding halberds they couldn't remember why they'd raised.

I turned the page.

* * *

The crown fought back the only way it knew: it lied.

As I read, the hall filled with false memories like smoke fills a room. It poured them into everyone — into the courtiers, into Sef, into the king, and hardest of all into me, trying to drown the true ledger-lines in counterfeit ones. I read *harvested: the lullaby of the northern weavers, returned,* and the crown answered with a lullaby of its own, gorgeous and wrong, a song no one had ever sung, and for one terrible moment half the hall hummed along, moved to tears by a fiction. I read *harvested: the name of the dragon, returned,* and the crown offered up a magnificent false name, silver and heroic, and I felt the real one — the true name, the dead dragon's name, the one Ysol had whispered to us in the shrine like handing over a child — begin to slip.

I gripped it. I said it aloud, the true one, ugly and plain and real: "*Vessh.*"

The false name shattered like sugar-glass. Somewhere in the rafters the actual moths — Ysol's fire-moths, the few that had followed us — flared white with grief or joy or both.

The king stood up from his throne.

He shouldn't have been able to. The crown held him the way a hand holds a puppet, and I felt its grip wrench tight as I read on, line after line, each one a small theft from me and a large restitution to the world. The harvest of the war — returned, and I lost my memory of the road east with Sef, the good days, the shared bread, gone like water down a drain, worth it, worth it, keep reading. The harvest of the old king's face from his son's mind — returned, and the man on the dais made a sound like a door opening in an empty house, staring at his own father's features surfacing in his memory after a century drowned.

"*Who are you?*" he shouted at me over the roaring of returning things. "*What are you doing to me?*"

"Paying your debts," I said. "You've been paying them wrong for a hundred years. Someone else has been eating the payments."

The crown turned its full attention on me then, and I learned what it meant to be audited by a god of accounting.

* * *

It showed me my own life. All of it. Offered freely, the way Vane offers contracts, the way the guild offers terms: here is everything you surrendered coming back, girl. Here is Master Wren's kitchen, the smell of nettle stew, the scratch of her handwriting teaching you letters. Here is your mother's voice saying your true name — your *real* name, the buried one, the one the prophecy knew — saying it softly, the way she said it when you were sick. Here is the winter you were nine. Here is the dog. Here is the reason you ran, restored and forgiven. Walk away now, and keep all of it. Stop reading, and keep yourself. Isn't a self what you came to the guild to protect? Wasn't anonymity the whole prayer?

The hall had gone silent except for the reading. Even the crown's lies had stopped, because the truth was louder, spreading outward through the room like warmth from a lit hearth — people remembering their dead, their weddings, their crimes, their childhoods, weeping and laughing and clinging to strangers who were suddenly sisters and creditors and first loves.

And I stood in the middle of it with my mother's voice held out to me like a cup of water in a desert, and I understood the trap completely, because the voice was *real*. The crown wasn't counterfeiting this one. It was offering escrow. It had been holding my mother's voice for years, harvested early, collateral from a hedge-witch's apprentice, and it would give it back the moment I closed the book.

"Your terms," I said aloud, to the crown, to the whole listening hall, "are declined."

I read the next line.

The voice went.

I don't mean it faded. I mean one moment it was hers, warm and specific, saying my name — and the next the sentence *my mother's voice* referred to nothing at all, a label on an empty drawer. I knew I had loved her. I knew I had lost the sound of her. I could not lose the knowing of the loss; the ledger saw to that, keeping the wound and taking the salve. I read on with tears running into my mouth and I did not stop, because in front of me a blacksmith dropped to his knees hearing his drowned daughter laugh again, and behind me the whole city's century was coming home.

Master Wren's face went next. I read the line about the harvest of the border villages' grief, and paid with her — the stooped shoulders, the ink-stained fingers, the eyes that had marked me for this before I could write my own name. I tried to hold her face as it went and it was like holding water. What remained was the fact of her. A name attached to a shape of guilt. I had loved her. I had fled her. I could no longer check either against anything.

Keep reading. Keep reading. Clarity isn't calm; clarity is seeing the price and paying it anyway, eyes open, which is the only way anyone has ever paid honestly.

Sef caught up to me at the dais steps, bleeding from somewhere along the hairline, holding off two guards with a candlestick and archival fury. "How much left?" he shouted.

I looked at the ledger. More than half. Maybe more than that. The pages weren't thinning the way pages should.

"A lot," I said.

"Can you stand for a lot?"

"I can stand for all of it."

He believed me. That was somehow the thing that nearly broke me — not the crown's lies, not the losses, but Sef's absolute stupid faith that I meant it. I turned another page.

* * *

Near the end — I think it was near the end; time had come loose — the crown changed tactics one final time.

It stopped attacking me and attacked the room. It flooded the hall with a single shared false memory, vast and beautiful: a memory of *me*, but crowned. Everyone in the hall suddenly remembered a queen named Ada, wise and beloved, who had ruled them gently for a generation, who had ended the famine, who had saved them all. Hundreds of faces turned to me wet-eyed with love for a person who had never existed, and the crown whispered through their love the way it whispered through everything: *this could be yours. This is what memory is worth. This is what you could be, instead of nothing.*

The court began to kneel. Not commanded. *Remembering.*

"That's not me," I said, and my voice cracked, and I hated that it cracked. "That's the lie it tells instead of a soul. Get up. Get *up* — I'm not your queen, I'm your bookkeeper, and I'm here to close the accounts—"

I looked down at the last open spread of the ledger.

And the last lines were not about the city at all.

They were about the keeper. About Ysol — harvested: the keeper's own name, taken a century ago so that the fire's warden would forget she had ever been anyone, would serve forever as a function and not a woman. Taken the day the binding was made. Still held. Still held *now*, in the guild's cells, in Vane's careful custody, a prisoner who did not even know herself enough to escape.

If I read that line, she would wake. Wherever she was, whatever they'd done to her, she would come back to herself like a struck match.

If I read that line, it would cost me the largest entry of all. I could feel the book weighing me, pricing me. It wanted, in exchange for a hundred-year-old name, the last big thing I had.

It wanted my choice. My freedom itself — the one thing I had built my whole life around, the invisibility, the nobody-ness, the blessed unowned self. Read this, said the ledger, and you will never again be able to disappear. Everyone will know your name, because you will have given it to a stranger as a gift.

I thought of Ysol on the stair, holding it alone with her moths, buying us minutes with her body.

I thought: *she bought her freedom a hundred years ago and never got the receipt. I am holding the receipt.*

"Ada—" Sef started.

I read it.

"*Harvested: the name of Ysol, keeper of the last fire. Returned.*"

* * *

Something went out of me that I cannot itemize, because itemizing was exactly what it took. I stood at the foot of the throne lighter than air and less than I was, emptied to the walls, and across the whole hall every flame — candles, hearths, the little lamps in the gallery — bent sideways at once, as if the room had inhaled.

Far away, in some guild cell, a woman opened her eyes and remembered she had a name.

And on the king's head, with a sound like lake ice groaning at thaw, the crown *cracked*.

The thing inside it screamed — not with one voice but with a century of them, all the harvested names crying out at once from inside their gilded prison, a chorus of the taken, and the crack spread down through the gold like lightning through a tree—

—and the king, the gentle hollowed king, put up both his hands and *caught* the halves before they fell.

Behind me, from the doors of the hall, I heard footsteps. Many. Unhurried. Guild boots, and beneath them a voice I knew, calm as a closing bell:

Guildmaster Vane had arrived to collect.

And Ysol — free, furious, and awake for the first time in a century — was with them.
