## 19. Unbound

The collectors' chains were cold iron, but the cold was the least of it. The least of everything.

Ysol knelt on the marble floor of the great hall while Guildmaster Vane stood at her shoulder reading from a document she could not see, and above them all the crown burned its slow, hungry light over the Feast of Continuity. She had been carried here in a cart like goods. She had been inventoried. Somewhere in Vane's ledgers there was, no doubt, an entry with her name in it — *one keeper, aged, used up, value nil* — and the terrible thing, the thing she would carry out of this hall if she carried anything at all, was that some part of her had spent ninety years feeling exactly that way about herself. A keeper was not a person. A keeper was a hinge between the fire and the world, and hinges did not grieve.

Except that she did. She had always grieved. That was the whole of her office, if she was honest, and she had never once been honest about it until tonight.

Ada's voice filled the hall.

Ysol knew what it cost before she heard the first line, because she had taught the girl the shape of the rite in the shrine below the snowline, and she had watched Ada do the arithmetic of it — every word read aloud returns a memory to the world, and takes one from the reader — and she had seen the girl choose anyway, the way you step off a cliff you have already decided is a door. Now Ada stood in the open center of the court, small and ink-stained and utterly unhidden for perhaps the first time in her life, holding the ledger of the binding open against her chest, and she was reading names.

Not the names of kings. Not the names of heroes. She was reading fishermen. She was reading millers. She was reading the dead of the border war whose graves stood empty in villages that no longer knew why they mourned.

*"Maren Ostrow, who mended nets at Greyharbor and sang badly to her daughters—"*

And in the third row of courtiers, a woman Ysol had never seen made a sound like a fish being pulled from deep water, and put both hands over her mouth, and wept. Maren Ostrow had been gone a century. Her daughter's granddaughter stood in the Feast of Continuity wearing silk she could not remember buying, and suddenly she remembered her great-grandmother's voice, badly singing, nets mending, salt on everything — and the memory went into her like a lamp going back into a window.

The crown screamed.

It did not scream with a mouth. It screamed the way a building screams when its keystone is drawn — a long structural groan through the whole palace, through the marble under Ysol's knees, through her own old bones. The light above the throne guttered from gold to something bruised and violet, and the courtiers flinched away from their king as though he had become a fire, which in a sense he had, which in a sense he had been for a hundred years.

*Keep reading,* Ysol thought, uselessly, kneeling, chained. *Girl, keep reading.*

But Ada's voice stumbled.

Ysol heard it happen — heard the moment a line came up out of the ledger that Ada did not want to give away, some memory of her own that the rite had reached down and taken as payment, and the words caught in the girl's throat like a fishbone. The hall held its breath. The violet light steadied. Began, horribly, to brighten.

And Vane smiled.

Ysol had watched that man's face across ninety years of guild business conducted through intermediaries and letters sealed with wax the color of dried blood. She had never seen him smile. He smiled now, thin and satisfied, the smile of an accountant watching a column come out even, and he took one step toward Ada and said, gently, in the voice of a man offering a chair to a tired guest:

"You've paid enough, child. Look at you. You're nearly empty. Put the book down and I'll see you fed. There are kinder ways to be forgotten."

*No,* Ysol thought.

*Yes,* said the fire, somewhere far below the world, in the mountain where a dragon's death still burned. And the fire, dying or not, caged or not, remembered her.

* * *

Here is what nobody understood about keepers, because keepers made sure nobody understood: they were not custodians of the flame. They were its *collateral*. The binding that had shackled the parasite to the crown a century ago had needed a human anchor on the other end — someone to hold the fire's death steady so it could burn instead of rage — and the first keeper had signed herself into that role the way debtors sign anything: desperately, and forever, and without reading the last clause. Every keeper since had inherited the signature. Ysol's mother had held the stair. Her grandmother before her. Ninety years of women walking down into the shrine each dawn to feed the fire their own warm memories, one handful at a time, so that the thing in the crown would have less to eat from the living.

That was why Ysol could not remember her mother's face. That was why she could not remember the color of the sky over the shrine in summer, though she had seen it ten thousand times. She had poured those things into the flame the way you pour oil into a lamp, and the lamp had drunk, and she had called it duty because the alternative was to call it what it was.

But the chain ran both directions. It had always run both directions. The fire held her just as she held it.

And now the fire felt Ada falter, felt the rite stutter, felt a hundred years of its own death-light bending toward the girl like a hand reaching for a candle — and the fire reached down the length of its own dying body, along the buried root of itself that ran mountain-to-palace beneath the whole kingdom, and found the oldest knot in that root, and pulled.

The knots were the keepers.

The chains fell off Ysol's wrists like startled birds.

She did not question it. There would be time later — she suspected there would be very little time later, but there would be some — to wonder at the mechanics of it, the way the iron simply forgot its purpose and slid loose, the way the two guards holding her arms found themselves suddenly embracing air and each other. Later. Right now there was a girl in the center of the hall going gray around the mouth, a book sagging in her grip, and a guildmaster smiling his column-even smile, and above it all a crown gathering its light for the lunge.

Ysol stood up. Ninety years old, three days without sleep, half a person by any honest accounting of memories. She stood up the way her mother must have stood up, the way all of them had stood up at the foot of the shrine stair every morning for a century: because standing up was the office, and the office did not care how you felt about it.

"Vane," she said, and her voice cracked across the hall like a whip made of paper. "Your books are about to close."

* * *

Everything happened quickly after that, and Ysol remembered all of it, which surprised her; she had expected to lose pieces, the way old people lose pieces, the way she had lost pieces her whole life. But the fire was pouring back into her now through the broken knot, refunding nothing, restoring nothing — it had eaten her payments a century too well for that — but lending her, briefly, its clarity. Its terrible single-mindedness. A dying thing does not waste attention, and for the next few minutes Ysol had the attention of a dying god.

She saw Sef move. The disgraced archivist had been hovering at the edge of the dais steps with a coal of dragon-fire cupped in an iron dish, stolen from her own shrine, still burning after all these miles — and she saw him understand, in the same instant she did, what the coal was for. Not the charter; the charter was already ash, and good riddance, paper burns the same whether it binds kingdoms or debts. The coal was for *light*. For the last thing the rite required.

Because here was the clause none of them had known, the clause the first keeper had died without writing down, the clause that lived only in the fire's own memory and that the fire was telling her now, directly, urgently, the way you shout instructions to someone standing on your chest: *the reading opens the lock, but a keeper must turn the key.* The final verse was not written in the ledger. It had never been written anywhere. It existed only in the mouths of keepers, passed mother to daughter down ninety years of forgetting, worn smooth as a river stone, and Ysol had carried it her whole life without knowing what it was for, reciting it to the flame each night the way you'd hum to a sick animal:

*What was taken, let it go home.*
*What was bound, let it stand alone.*
*What wore the borrowed light, let it be*
*only what it was meant to be.*

Nonsense, she had always thought. A lullaby. A charm against despair.

It was the key. It had been the key the entire time, and the guild had hunted keepers for a century to keep the key from ever standing in this room.

"ADA." Ysol's voice again, and this time the girl heard it, lifted her head, found her across the crowded terrified court. "Finish the line! Whatever it costs — finish the line! I'll take it from here!"

Ada looked at her — really looked, the way you look at someone you owe — and then she bent her head back to the ledger and read.

*"—and the last of them, the smallest, the one who hid inside her own name until the name wore through, shall stand in the hall where the light eats, and shall not look away—"*

The words hit Ysol like weather.

*Shall not look away.* She understood then what the prophecy had asked of her all along. Not strength. Not magic. Just presence. Just the unbearable act of witnessing, performed by someone built to witness — a keeper, a hinge, a woman who had spent her life watching a fire consume her own past and calling it devotion. The parasite in the crown survived on the *unwitnessed*. It ate memories precisely because no one ever stood in the room where the eating happened and refused to leave. Everyone always left. Kings forgot themselves and the court stepped back. Villages lost their dead and shrugged, and plowed, and slept. A hundred years of everyone looking politely away.

Ysol walked toward the throne.

Vane moved to stop her — she saw him signal, saw two collectors peel off toward her with the flat efficiency of men moving furniture — and she did the only thing a keeper knows how to do with fire. She opened her hands and *gave*. What remained in her, the fire lent back for one moment: a breath of heat rolled off her skin, nothing, almost nothing, a candle's worth of a god's death — but the collectors had grown up in a city that feared dragons the way children fear the space under beds, and their nerve broke, and they broke formation, and she walked through the gap they left.

The king sat on his throne with his hands open on his knees, palms up, the posture of a beggar or a man waiting to receive communion. Up close, in the failing violet light, Ysol could see what a century of the crown had done: the man was worn thin as vellum, translucent at the temples, his eyes the pale washed-out blue of things kept too long in water. But he was looking at Ada. He had been looking at Ada the whole reading with an expression Ysol recognized from her mirror, from her mother's face, from every keeper who ever lived:

Hope. Terrible, embarrassed, half-apologizing hope.

"Is it me," the king whispered. "In the book. Is any of it me?"

"It's all of you," Ysol said. "Every year of you. They just kept it in the wrong place." She turned to face the crown. She did not look away. "Now pay attention, Your Majesty. This is the part where you get yourself back."

* * *

Above her, the crown gathered itself.

She felt it do what she had felt it doing from the mountain all these years, but from underneath now, from point-blank: the light contracting, the hunger focusing, the whole vast parasitic architecture of it — a century of harvested evenings, first kisses, griefs, recipes, faces of mothers, the smell of particular rain — pulling inward toward a single decision. It could not eat the rite. It could not eat the ledger. So it reached for the reader, and it reached for the witness, and it offered them the only currency it had ever truly owned.

It offered them back what they had lost.

The hall filled with false memory the way a cellar fills with water — silently, from below, rising. Ysol felt it lap at her ankles first: her mother's kitchen, sudden and vivid, bread and tallow smoke, her mother turning from the stove with her sleeves rolled, saying *Ysol, wash up, it's ready* — except her mother had never said that, could never have said that, because Ysol had fed the real kitchen to the fire forty years ago, and this was a forgery, a lure, a fishing hook dressed in her own longing. Around her she heard the courtiers gasp and moan as the crown baited hooks for all of them: a dead child laughing in a garden, a lover's voice on a stair, a home that never was, offered so sweetly, offered free, free, free —

All you have to do is stop listening. All you have to do is look away.

Ysol looked at the forgery of her mother and loved it, and grieved it, and refused it. "You're not her," she said, aloud, to the light. "She's gone, and you're a theft wearing her apron, and I will not trade what's true for what's comfortable. Not today. Today I'm expensive."

Then she lifted her voice, cracked and old and certain, and spoke the verse.

*What was taken, let it go home.*

Ada's reading met it — the girl had reached the ledger's final pages, the founding entries, the signatures of the guild's ancestors, and her voice had gone ragged and raw, and every name she read struck the hall like a bell being returned to a belfry. Memories flew outward. Ysol watched them go: a courtier dropping to her knees with her hands full of a sister she'd forgotten she'd lost; a guard soldier weeping at attention for a war he suddenly remembered marching in; old Sef standing rigid at the dais with tears cutting clean lines down his dusty face as some piece of the archive, some piece of *himself*, came home to roost.

*What was bound, let it stand alone.*

The king made a sound. Small. Human. His hands turned over, palms down now, gripping the arms of the throne — and Ysol saw the change begin at his fingertips, color flooding back into them like ink into water, the washed-out translucence thickening, a man re-entering his own body room by room. "I was—" he said. "I was crowned in the spring. It rained. My father's hands shook when he set it on me and he said—" His voice broke. "He said *wear it lightly, boy.* Oh. Oh, I never did. I never once did."

"No," Ysol said gently. "Something else wore it for you. But it's almost done now."

*What wore the borrowed light, let it be*

The crown screamed again, and this time it was not a building's scream. This time it was voices.

A hundred years of them. That was the horror and the mercy of it, tangled past separating: the thing in the crown had never had a self of its own. It was a hunger that had learned to wear a shape, and the shape was woven entirely out of what it ate, and as Ada read the ledger to its end and Ysol spoke the verse to its heart, the weaving came undone — and the hall filled with the dispossessed. Names crying out. A fisherman's laugh tearing loose from the light. A midwife's humming. Ten thousand ordinary voices, held prisoner in a circle of gold, suddenly remembering they were not the crown, they were only *held* by it — and dispersing. Rising. Going home. Ysol stood in the middle of it with her face upturned and her eyes open, refusing to look away to the very last, and the voices passed through her like wind through a doorway, and some of them, she would swear until she died, touched her cheek in passing the way her mother might have.

*—only what it was meant to be.*

The crown cracked.

Not shattered — cracked, one clean line running from rim to rim like ice letting go of a pond in spring, and through the crack came a light that was not the parasite's bruised violet but something older, warmer, redder, the light of the shrine far below the snowline, the light of a death that had burned faithfully for a century so that this exact moment would be possible. The two halves of the crown tipped forward off the king's brow and fell to the marble with a sound like a coin dropped in an empty cathedral, and rolled, and stopped at Ysol's feet.

Inside the gold, where a century of stolen light had coiled, there was nothing at all.

Just metal. Just craft. Just a thing a smith had made for a young king on a rainy spring day, meaning nothing by it.

The king stood up. He swayed; Sef caught his elbow; the king gripped the archivist's arm and stared around his own hall with the naked bewilderment of a man waking in a strange bed, and then his gaze landed on the courtiers — his courtiers, weeping, remembering, clutching each other — and his face did something complicated and ruined and beginning to heal.

"How long," he asked the room. Nobody answered. He asked again, quieter. "How long has it been?"

"A hundred years," Ysol said. "Give or take a bad winter."

He looked at her then — this old woman in chains that no longer held, this stranger with a moth-charm scar on her wrist and firelight fading out of her eyes — and the king of the realm bowed to her. Low. Long. A hundred years late.

Behind them all, unnoticed by nearly everyone, the great chandeliers dimmed. The hearths sank. Somewhere far to the north, under the snowline, a fire that had been dying for a century finally received permission to die — and Ysol felt it go out of her like a tide withdrawing, taking the lent clarity with it, leaving her exactly what she had always been: old, half-spent, grieving, and — for the first time in ninety years, for the first time in the whole history of her line —

free.

Free, and finished, and standing in a hall where a broken crown lay at her feet and a girl with a ledger was swaying on her feet like a candle in wind.

Ysol turned. Ada had closed the book. She was staring at her own hands as though checking they were still hers, and Ysol saw it immediately, the way a keeper sees everything about fire: the girl was flickering. Not visibly — not yet — but underneath, the way a lamp gutters when the oil runs low, and Ysol knew with sick certainty what the reading had cost, knew what was missing behind the girl's eyes, and knew — because the fire had told her, in its last clear moments, in the plain urgent voice it saved for emergencies — what came next.

The crown was broken. The parasite was ash. But the fire itself, the true fire, the last dragon's death — it was guttering now for good, and when it went out, it would not go quietly. It would pull the light out of every lamp in the kingdom on its way down, unless someone stood at the shrine and gave it a new place to live.

Someone whose hands it already knew.

"Ada," Ysol said, and crossed the marble to her, and took the girl's ink-stained hands in her own scarred ones. "Listen carefully. The reading's done, and you did it, and I know what it took — don't argue, I know. But there's one more page. It's not in the book." She glanced north, toward the mountains, where the last light in the world was going out. "It's in the fire. And it's asking for you."
