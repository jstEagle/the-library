## 22. Paloma and the Choir

The shrine had been a granary once, before the war nobody remembered well enough to mourn properly. That was the story the village told about it anyway—granary, then ruin, then shrine—and Ada had long since stopped correcting the details. The truth was that the building had never held grain at all. It had held a dragon's death for a hundred years, and now it held something gentler, and the villagers could believe whatever they liked about the roof beams.

Ada swept the floor at dawn because the fire liked a clean floor. This was not superstition. It was simply true, the way some things were true now: the flame dimmed when the stones were littered with straw, brightened when Ada sang off-key, leaned toward the north wall whenever someone entered carrying grief. It had opinions. It had moods. It was, in every way that mattered, alive.

"Good morning," Ada told it.

The fire answered in its usual way—a soft rising, a color like the inside of a shell, a warmth that found the ache in Ada's chest and pressed on it kindly. The ache was permanent. It lived where Ada's mother's voice used to live, and their master's face, and the smell of the Ledger Guild's ink room on a winter morning. Those rooms inside them were empty now, swept clean by their own mouth, line after line read aloud until there was almost nothing left to give. But the fire sat in the emptiness like a tenant who paid rent in warmth, and most days Ada did not mind the arrangement.

Most days.

They set the morning offerings on the ledge: bread from the Halloway kitchen, a sprig of rowan cut by one of the Ferrow girls, a button. Buttons were popular this season. Children brought buttons the way children everywhere brought treasures—shiny ones, mismatched ones, one carved from antler that Ada suspected had been pried off a grandfather's coat without permission. The fire didn't eat the offerings. It illuminated them. That was the whole liturgy, if you could call it one: you gave the fire something small, and the fire showed it back to you brighter than it was, and for a moment you remembered why you'd carried it all this way.

Ada had written the liturgy down once, on a single page, because Sef insisted that even a shrine should have documentation. The page said:

*1. Bring something true.*
*2. Set it down.*
*3. Let it be seen.*

Sef had reviewed the draft and made three corrections, all of which Ada ignored, and the final version in the honest archive read exactly as above, with a marginal note in Sef's hand: *Approved against my objections.*

The door opened on cold morning air and the sound of many feet.

Here they came—the choir, though no one had named it that yet. It would be Sef who named it, eventually, in one of his interminable reports, and then the name would stick the way names stuck to things in this kingdom now: imperfectly, willingly, a little sideways. But this morning they were just eleven children from the village school, aged six to thirteen, herded up the hill by Mistress Ferrow, who had lost two sons to the forgetting years and remembered neither of their faces but had taught herself to say their names out loud every day anyway, because a name spoken is a memory planted.

"Good morning, Keeper Brightwater," Mistress Ferrow said. She still used the honorific. Ada had given up objecting around year three.

"Just Ada," they said, as always.

"Just Ada," the children echoed, delighted, because the ritual of it pleased them, and because children love a person who has been reduced to their simplest self. They did not know what the reduction had cost. They knew only that the keeper of the fire was called Just Ada, and that this was funny and holy at the same time, which was, Ada had come to believe, the correct relationship between those two qualities.

"Who's first?" Ada asked.

A small girl came forward—Pell, six years old, gap-toothed, ferociously serious. She held out her offering with both hands: a feather. A dove's feather, gray as smoke.

"I found it on the road," Pell announced. "It's a paloma."

The word went through the children like a spark through dry grass. *Paloma, paloma.* They had coined it themselves two winters ago, Ada still didn't know how or why—some game of translation, some half-remembered song from a grandmother who'd grown up across the sea. *Paloma.* The dove. And because children are the true archivists of any kingdom, the name had climbed out of the schoolyard and into the village and up the hill, and now everyone called the fire paloma, and the fire, as far as anyone could tell, was pleased about it.

Ada took the feather and set it on the ledge.

The fire did what it always did with feathers. It rose. It opened. For three heartbeats the whole shrine filled with light the color of a wing against a bright sky, and the children gasped the way they gasped every time, because wonder does not wear out with repetition; it only deepens its roots.

"There," Ada said quietly. "Seen."

Mistress Ferrow wiped her eyes and pretended she hadn't.

* * *

After the choir left, Ada sat with the ledger.

Not a guild ledger. Those were ash, every one of them, burned in the courtyard of the dissolved Guildhall while Vane watched from a prison cart with an expression Ada had never managed to forget—not rage, not grief, just the flat arithmetic of a man watching bad debt get written off. No. This ledger was different. This one Ada kept themselves, in a plain bound book Sef had gifted them with the words "Every archive begins as somebody's notebook."

In it, Ada wrote down what other people remembered of them.

This was the work of the years. It was slow. It was often strange. A person would come to the shrine with an offering and stay to talk, and somewhere in the talk they'd say, *You know, I think I met you before the Unbinding—you sold my father a fair wind, or something like it,* and Ada would write it down. *Sold Tam Osk's father a fair wind, autumn before the Unbinding, per Tam Osk.* Other entries were thinner. *Kind to horses, says the farrier's widow.* *Had a scar, left eyebrow, source unknown.* *Laughed at funerals, which some people found disrespectful and others found necessary.*

The book was maybe a third full. Ada had made peace with the fact that it might never be more than half. You cannot reconstruct a life from testimony alone; Sef had lectured on this very subject at the new archive, standing before rows of students, saying, "We do not recover the past. We negotiate with it." But a third of a life, freely given by strangers who owed nothing—that was worth more than a whole one taken as collateral. Ada believed this with their whole hollow chest.

Today's entry came from an unexpected quarter.

The knock at the shrine door was formal. Three measured raps. Ada knew only one person who knocked that way, and sure enough, Sef stood on the step in his good coat, snow in his hair, holding a document case the way he held all documents—as though it might confess something.

"You're supposed to be in the capital," Ada said. "You gave a lecture Tuesday. I have the announcement. It's very pompous."

"I rescheduled." He stepped inside, stamped the snow off his boots with unnecessary violence, and warmed his hands at the fire with the ease of a man who had done it a hundred times. Then he opened the case. "I found something. In the deep stacks. Under the old palace wing, in a box that was catalogued as 'miscellaneous devotional objects, value nil.'"

"Sef."

"It's the prophecy book, Ada."

Ada's hands went still on the ledger. Even now—even after everything—the phrase landed in them like a hook finding old flesh. *The prophecy book.* The century-old volume they'd pulled off a returned shelf in another life, whose final entry bore tonight's date and a buried name. The book that had started everything. It should have burned with the guild's holdings. Everyone assumed it had.

"The collectors missed it," Sef said. "Or one of them hid it. We may never know. But here's the thing—" He drew it out: plain covers, guild seal cracked down the middle where he'd broken it open that morning. "The last entry isn't unfinished anymore."

"What?"

"When we read it, back then, it ended mid-sentence. *And on this night the debt comes due, and the one called—*" He recited it from memory; of course he did; Sef's memory was a fortress with a librarian in it. "Mid-sentence. We assumed damage. Age. But look."

He turned the book around and laid it in Ada's lap.

The final page was complete. The ink was fresh. Not new—Ada knew new ink—but *finished*, the way a thing finishes itself when the conditions are finally right, the way a wound closes, the way a chord resolves. The handwriting was the same antique hand as the rest of the volume, and it ran on past the point where Ada had once read it with cold dread crawling up their spine, past the date, past the name, into lines that had not existed seven years ago:

*...and the one called [the name] shall stand in the crown's presence with the book open and the throat unguarded, and shall pay out the kingdom's stolen years from their own account, and the crown shall crack, and the fire shall gutter, and the fire shall be caught.*

*And this is not a warning.*

*It was never a warning.*

*It is an invitation.*

Ada read it twice. Three times. The fire leaned close over their shoulder, warm and curious, reading along.

"It updated," Ada said finally. "Prophecies don't update."

"This one did." Sef sat down across from them, and for a moment the great disgraced archivist, head of the Royal Honest Archive, terror of sloppy cataloguers everywhere, looked simply tired and human and a little afraid. "I've thought about nothing else for three days. My best theory—and I want you to hear how much I hate this theory—is that the book was never finished. It was *waiting*. A prophecy written by someone who could see the shape of a thing but not its ending, because the ending depended on a choice. Your choice. And when you made it—" he gestured at the fire, at the shrine, at Ada's emptied chest— "the last lines had somewhere to land. So they landed."

"And the last line." Ada touched the words. *An invitation.* "What does it mean, an invitation? Invited to what? It's over. We won. Or—we survived it, anyway. Nobody wins anything."

"Read it again. Slowly. As an archivist, not as the person it's about."

Ada read it again. Slowly.

*It was never a warning. It is an invitation.*

And slowly, the way dawn comes up—not all at once but in stages, each one obvious only after it arrives—they understood.

"A century ago," Ada said, "someone saw all of this. The crown, the harvest, the guild, the fire guttering out. And they couldn't stop it. Writing couldn't stop it. So they did the only thing a writer can do." Their voice went strange. "They left instructions. Not for stopping it—for surviving it. For whoever came after. The prophecy was a door, and it stayed locked until someone fit the lock, and the moment I read that ledger aloud—" 

"—you weren't fulfilling it," Sef finished softly. "You were accepting it. RSVP'ing, if you'll forgive the archival imprecision. The invitation was: *when the fire dies, catch it.* Someone a hundred years dead spent their last ink making sure that when everything fell apart, one person would be standing in the right place, holding the right book, knowing the price in advance." He shook his head. "All this time we thought we were fighting fate. We were corresponding with it."

Ada looked at the fire.

The fire looked back, in its way—rose, settled, threw a long gold arm of light across the open page, across the final line, as if confirming the signature.

"An invitation," Ada repeated. "To what, though? To carry it? I already carry it. That part's done."

"No." Sef leaned forward. "Read the whole thing again, Ada. Every entry, start to finish. I've been reading it for three days and I keep snagging on the early passages, the ones we skimmed in panic back then. There's a structure. There's a *shape*. And the shape doesn't end with you." His voice dropped. "The shape ends with the children."

* * *

Ada did not sleep that night.

They sat in the shrine with the prophecy book open on their knees and the fire keeping watch, and they read the century-old text the way Sef had taught them to read—slowly, laterally, asking of every line not *what does it predict* but *what does it ask*. And the shape emerged the way shapes emerge from tapestries when you finally stand back far enough.

The early entries described the binding: the crown, the parasite, the theft of memories to feed a dying fire. Cruelty, all of it. But threaded through the cruelty, sparse as stars, were other lines. Lines about a keeper. About a moth sent seeking. About a name buried so deep that only paper could find it. About a reader whose memory was unbound—*unbound*, not erased, and Ada had sat up straight at that word at midnight, because they had spent seven years assuming the reading had *destroyed* their past, when the prophecy's own terms suggested something else entirely.

*Unbound,* the text said. *Not destroyed. Released. What is released may be gathered again—by choice, freely offered, in small hands.*

Small hands.

Ada looked toward the door, through which, twelve hours ago, eleven children had come trooping with their buttons and their feathers and their terrible beautiful singing.

"They're going to give it back," Ada whispered. "That's the invitation. Not to me. Through me. I carried the fire so it wouldn't die alone in a mountain—and now the fire lives gently, and gently, piece by piece, the kingdom gives back what I paid. Not the guild's way. Not debts. Offerings." They laughed, and it came out wet. "Every feather. Every button. Every kid who walks up that hill and lets the fire see something true—"

"—they're re-binding you," Sef said. "Voluntarily. Memory by memory. The exact inverse of the crown." He was smiling, that rare unguarded smile he saved for correctly shelved books and nothing else. "The prophecy wasn't warning you that you'd lose yourself. It was inviting the whole kingdom to help you find yourself again. It just needed a hundred years and one stubborn invisible clerk to set the table."

Outside, the sky was going gray at the edges. Dawn soon. And with the dawn would come footsteps on the hill path—small ones, eager ones, the choir arriving with whatever truths they'd found overnight to lay on the ledge.

Ada closed the prophecy book and held it against their chest, above the hollow, above the fire.

"Come back tomorrow," they said to Sef. "And the day after. Bring your notebook. If the kingdom is going to remember me back into existence, I want the head of the Honest Archive taking minutes."

Sef stood, bowed—an archivist's bow, precise and slightly ironic—and let himself out into the snow.

Ada opened the shrine doors wide, so the morning could get in.

And when the children crested the hill, breathless, red-cheeked, Pell in front as always with something cupped careful in her small hands, the fire rose up behind Ada like a wing opening, and the whole hillside filled with light the color of a dove against a bright sky, and somewhere in the warmth against Ada's chest—in the place where a mother's voice used to be—something stirred. Not returned. Not yet. But stirring. Reaching. Remembering its way home.

"Good morning," Ada said, and meant it with everything they had left, which was everything that mattered.

"Good morning, Just Ada!" sang the choir.

And the fire, gently, began to learn their names.
