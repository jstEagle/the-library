## 1. The Taster's Tongue

The king's breakfast arrived at half past six, as it always did, on a lacquered tray carried by a boy who couldn't have been more than fourteen and who held it the way soldiers hold shields.

Lena Vasquez was already awake. She was always already awake. Five years in the palace of Vhalen had taught her that the difference between living and dying was roughly ninety seconds — the time it took poison to find the tongue, the time it took a taster to find the poison. She had spent those ninety seconds every morning for one thousand eight hundred and twenty mornings, and she intended to spend them for many more.

"Set it down," she said. "You can go."

The boy didn't move. His knuckles were white around the tray's edge, and his eyes kept flicking to the covered dishes the way a man looks at a dog he suspects is going to bite him.

"I said you can—"

"He told me to watch," the boy whispered. "Cook Brannic. He told me to stay and watch you eat it."

Lena looked at him properly then. Pale. Sweat at the hairline despite the cold kitchen corridors. A tremor in his jaw that had nothing to do with fear of her and everything to do with fear of what might be under those silver covers.

Someone had told the boy to watch because someone thought she might die this morning.

"All right," Lena said, keeping her voice level, warm even, the voice she used on frightened kitchen staff and skittish noblewomen alike. "Stay. But stand by the door. If I fall over, run for the steward. Don't try to catch me. You'll just get blood on your uniform."

He made a sound that might have been a laugh if laughter hadn't been outlawed in the royal kitchens since the incident with the eel course two winters ago. He set the tray on her tasting table and retreated exactly three steps, which was as far as his instructions and his terror would let him go.

Lena sat. She pulled the tray toward her and began, as she always began, with her eyes.

Five dishes. Porridge with honey and cream. Smoked river trout, flaked. Bread, still warm, wrapped in linen. A small dish of stewed plums. And the king's particular weakness, smuggled past three separate physicians' objections: a cup of dark chocolate, spiced with cinnamon, thick enough to hold a spoon upright.

She checked each dish the way Odile — no, she corrected herself, the way *no one*, because there was no one to check with — had taught her grandmother's generation to check. Color first. Then smell, long and slow, nose almost touching the surface, reading the steam like a page. Then texture, a fingertip drawn along the rim of each dish, feeling for grit or oil or the faint slickness that certain ground roots left behind.

The porridge was clean. The trout was clean. The bread was clean, though the linen wrapping smelled faintly of rosemary, which meant someone in the bakery was still following the old preservation customs, which meant someone in the bakery was old enough to remember them, which meant someone in the bakery was probably selling charms out the back door like half the city. Lena filed that away and moved on.

The plums were clean.

The chocolate was not.

She didn't flinch. That was the first rule, older than any Edict, older than the palace itself: *the taster does not flinch.* Flinching tells the poisoner they've succeeded. Flinching tells the watchers you know something they don't. Flinching is a confession written on the body.

So Lena lifted the cup, inhaled its cinnamon-dark steam, and said, pleasantly, to the boy by the door:

"What's your name?"

"T-Tomas, my lady."

"Lena," she said. "Not my lady. Just Lena." She swirled the chocolate and watched how it clung to the sides. Too fast. It ran off the porcelain like water off a duck's back instead of coating it slow and heavy the way good chocolate did. Someone had thinned it. Thinning was clever — it disguised texture changes, diluted bitterness, made the tongue lazy. "Tomas, has anyone new started in the kitchens this week?"

"A carter. Delivers the milk." He swallowed. "And a spice-seller came Tuesday. Cook bought saffron from him."

Saffron. Of course. Saffron was expensive enough that no one questioned its provenance, and bitter enough on its own to hide a dozen other flavors beneath it. And cinnamon — sweet, loud cinnamon — was the classic mask. Whoever had done this knew palace kitchens. Knew the king's habits. Knew, possibly, about her.

Lena set down the cup without drinking.

"The chocolate's gone off," she announced, loud enough for the corridor. "Cream's turned. I can taste it. Tomas, take the whole tray back and tell Cook Brannic the king will have plain porridge and the trout, nothing else, and tell him I said so twice."

"But the king asked specially for—"

"The king," Lena said, "will have what doesn't kill him. Go."

Tomas went, tray rattling, and Lena remained seated at her little table by the window with her hands folded, listening to her own heartbeat slow itself back down. Ninety seconds. She'd beaten it again. Somewhere in the palace above her, a man she had never spoken to in five years of service would eat his breakfast and live another day, and he would never know, and she would never be thanked, because a taster who is noticed is a taster who is poisoned next.

That was the arrangement. Anonymity for survival. Service for silence.

She was very good at both.

* * *

By seven she was walking the long service corridor toward the steward's office to file her morning report — *all clear* — when Tomas came pelting back around the corner, breathless, and pressed something into her hand.

It was small and cold and silver: a charm worked in the shape of a knot, the kind dockworkers wore braided into their bootlaces, the kind that cost three coppers in the Nightmarket and ten years in the quarries if the witch-hunters caught you with it. The Edict of Ash, thirty years old now, had burned the charm-shops and the shrines and most of the people who remembered how such things were made. Possession was a flogging. Sale was a pyre.

"Tomas," Lena breathed, closing her fist around it. "What are you doing?"

"You knew," he whispered. His eyes were wet. "About the chocolate. Before you even tasted it, you *knew*, everyone says you always know, and my mam said — she said if I ever met someone who knows things, someone with the sight of it, I should give them this, it was hers, it kept my da alive through the fever years, and—" He stopped, gulping air. "Please don't report me. Please. I'll lose the position. My mam needs the position."

Lena stood very still. In five years, no one had ever handed her anything. People handed things *to* the palace, and the palace handed things to her, and she swallowed or refused to swallow them, and that was the whole shape of her life. She was a hinge. Nobody thanks a hinge.

"Who is your mother?" she asked quietly.

"Marta. Marta of the fish-stalls, by the Salt Gate."

Marta. Lena knew the name. Marta bought luck-threads from her twice a season, always through an intermediary, always haggling shamefully, always leaving a sprig of dried lavender tucked in the payment pouch because she believed — correctly, though she could never be told this — that the seller liked the smell.

"She sounds like a sensible woman," Lena said. She slipped the silver knot into her sleeve, where it lay against her wrist like a second pulse. "Tell her the lavender was appreciated."

Tomas stared. Then understanding flooded his face — the specific, terrified, luminous understanding of a boy who has just realized the secret thing he suspected was true — and he nodded once, hard, and fled.

Lena walked on to the steward's office with her heart doing something complicated in her chest. She should throw the charm in the first privy she passed. She should melt it down. She should report Tomas to Captain Sorrel's hunters herself, prove her loyalty, buy herself another quiet year.

Instead her thumb found the knot's worn edge through her sleeve, and she thought: *someone made this by hand. Someone prayed over this. Someone's father lived because of it.*

She kept walking.

* * *

The steward's office smelled of ink and cold stone. Maren, the court steward, was already at her desk, a narrow woman of indeterminate age whose ledger-keeping was famous in three provinces and whose face was famous in none, which Lena suspected was exactly how Maren preferred it.

"Report," Maren said, without looking up.

"All clear. Except the chocolate."

Now Maren looked up. Her eyes were the gray of unpolished silver, and they missed nothing, which made her either the most dangerous or the safest person in the palace; Lena had spent five years failing to decide which.

"Explain."

"Thinned. Masked with cinnamon. Probably carried in the saffron bought Tuesday from an outside spice-seller. I turned the whole tray. The king should take plain porridge and trout today, and I'd recommend the kitchens source no outside spices for a month."

Maren wrote this down in her small, precise hand. "A spice-seller. Description?"

"Didn't see him. Ask Cook Brannic."

"I will ask Cook Brannic." Maren's pen paused. "Cook Brannic instructed a kitchen boy to remain and observe you this morning. Why would he do that, do you suppose?"

The question hung in the air like smoke. Lena kept her face smooth. *Because someone warned him,* she thought. *Because someone in these walls wanted witnesses to a dead taster, and Cook Brannic is either a conspirator or a coward, and both kinds grow on every vine in this palace.*

"Perhaps he wanted the boy to learn proper procedure," Lena said. "Observation is part of training."

"Perhaps." Maren returned to her ledger. "The king takes his midday meal in the west solar. There will be guests. Foreign ones. Be prompt."

"I'm always prompt."

"Yes," Maren said, and there was something strange in the way she said it — not agreement, but notation, as if Lena's punctuality were being entered into some private record alongside everything else. "Dismissed."

Lena went. At the door she glanced back once, an old habit, and caught Maren watching her with that silver gaze, pen motionless, expression unreadable. The look lasted half a second before Maren bent to her ledgers again.

Lena carried it with her anyway, all the way down the corridor. It had felt less like suspicion than like *recognition*. As if Maren had looked at her and seen not the palace's anonymous hinge but something else — something with a name, a history, a grandmother.

Which was ridiculous. Nobody in Vhalen saw Lena Vasquez. That was the entire point of her.

* * *

The rest of the day passed in the usual liturgy of small survivals. The midday meal: clean, all nine courses, though the foreign guest — a trade envoy from the free cities, sharp-eyed and sunburnt — watched Lena taste the wine with an expression she couldn't read and chose not to investigate. The evening meal: clean. Between them, the endless administrative tastings, the gifts from petitioning lords (a jar of candied ginger, suspiciously generous; clean), the medicines prepared by the royal physicians (bitter, bitter, bitter; clean, clean, clean).

By nightfall Lena's tongue was numb and her feet ached and the palace had taken everything from her that it usually took, and she gave it all willingly, because the alternative was the pyre or the quarry or worse.

But the palace also gave her one thing back, every night, without knowing it: darkness. And in the darkness, Lena Vasquez stopped being a hinge.

* * *

The Nightmarket opened when the moon cleared the temple ruins — or what had been the temple ruins, before the Edict of Ash, before the burning, before the stones were carted off to line the harbor breakwater so that the sea itself would grind the old gods' house into silt. Now it was just a hollow in the eastern cliffs below the Salt Gate, reached by stairs that smelled of kelp and lamp oil, lit by paper lanterns in defiance of every ordinance Captain Sorrel's hunters had ever enforced.

Lena came down the stairs with her hood up and her hair braided flat and three knots of her own making hidden in her bodice, and the Nightmarket received her the way the sea receives a river: without comment, without question, as simply one more tributary coming home.

It wasn't much of a market. Thirty stalls, maybe forty on feast nights, selling charms that worked and charms that didn't and nobody quite knew which was which anymore, because the knowledge had been burned along with the temples and what remained was guesswork dressed up in ritual. Luck-threads for sailors. Fever-knots for mothers. Small clay figures of the drowned shrine gods, their features worn smooth by thirty years of illegal thumbs.

Lena's stall was a plank across two barrels at the far end, between a woman who sold protective salts and a man who claimed — implausibly, gloriously — to sell bottled thunder. She laid out her wares: luck-threads braided from three colors of wool, fever-knots tied in red cord, and her specialty, the thing that had built her reputation in four years of night work — *listener-charms*, small woven circles that mothers pinned above cradles so they'd hear a child stop breathing in the next room, that fishermen wore so they'd hear the weather turn in the rigging above the storm.

They worked. She didn't entirely know why. She tied them the way her grandmother had taught her, humming the old cadences under her breath, and they worked, and in Vhalen that was treason worth dying for, and so she sold them cheap and fast and never learned her customers' names.

Business was steady. Dockworkers mostly, coming off the late tide with salt in their beards and coin in their fists. A young couple wanting a blessing-thread for a marriage the temples could no longer witness. An old woman who bought a listener-charm every month and never said for whom, and Lena never asked, because asking was how networks got burned.

Near midnight, a big man detached himself from the crowd — a stevedore Lena recognized by his shoulders rather than his face, one of her regulars, a rigger named Halloway who bought a luck-thread every payday and complained every time about the price.

"Hallo, witch-girl," Halloway rumbled, grinning under his hood. "Got anything new? Got anything *strong*?"

"Strong costs more."

"Everything costs more." He counted out coppers onto her plank, one by one, and leaned close. "Listen. My daughter's got the lung-fever. Bad. The physicians want a fee I couldn't pay in a lifetime. So I need strong. I need—" his voice dropped to something raw, "I need a miracle, girl. I know you're not that. I know nobody's that. The gods are dead or gone or locked up, whatever the Edict says happened to them. But tie me the strongest thread you've got, and say whatever words you say, and I'll believe it. That's worth coin, isn't it? Believing?"

Lena's hands were already moving, choosing the wool, before her caution could catch up. This was against her rules — she had rules, hard-won, carved out of four years of staying alive. Never sell hope, only protection. Never promise outcomes. Never let them see you care, because caring was a debt and debts got people burned.

But Halloway's daughter was coughing blood somewhere above the cliffs tonight, and Halloway's hands were shaking as he counted coppers, and the rules suddenly seemed like the thin things they were.

"This one," she said quietly, tying as she spoke, letting the old cadences hum through her chest. "For the fever to break. For breath to come easy. For the child to—" *live*, said the cadence, in the language her grandmother had used, the language with no written form left anywhere in Vhalen. "Wear it against your skin. Don't thank me. Thanking draws attention."

Halloway closed his fist around the thread like a man closing his hand around a rope thrown into deep water. "Bless you," he said.

"Don't—"

"No, really." He grinned, wiped his eyes with the back of one huge hand, embarrassed. "Bless *you*, witch-girl. Somebody ought to. Nobody else will."

He lumbered off into the lantern-light, and Lena sat looking at her empty plank and the few coppers on it, and felt the loneliness arrive on schedule, the way it did every night after the last customer left. Weary competence, that was her whole self. Competent enough to save a king she'd never met, five times a day. Too criminal to save a child openly. Too alone to be thanked by anyone except a stevedore who thought she was a fraud with good knots.

She was gathering her threads when the woman selling salts two stalls down went suddenly rigid, her head tilting like a bird's.

"Did you hear that?" the salt-woman whispered.

Lena listened. Wind in the cliff-cracks. Lantern-paper fluttering. The sea, grinding the old temple stones into silt at the harbor's edge.

Then, underneath it — or through it, or *inside* it — a voice. Not loud. Not human. Coming from everywhere the way damp comes through stone, gentle and vast and unmistakably *awake*:

*"…heard. The child is heard. Tell the father: the fever breaks at dawn."*

Every stall in the Nightmarket went silent. Forty sellers and a hundred buyers, frozen, staring at each other across thirty years of enforced silence, while somewhere above them a rigger named Halloway walked home with a wool thread against his chest and a prayer that had, for the first time in living memory, actually been answered.

Lena's hands were shaking. She pressed them flat against the plank to still them, and against her wrist, inside her sleeve, the little silver knot that Tomas had given her grew warm — warm as a living thing, warm as a pulse.

Somewhere in the dark of the cliffs, the voice faded like a tide going out. But it left something behind in the silence, the way tides leave lines of foam on the sand: a single sensation, clear as a bell, that settled into Lena's bones and stayed there.

The voice had known her. It had passed over her, through her, around her — and in that passing it had paused, the way a traveler pauses at a familiar door.

*Oh, little taster,* it had seemed to say, in a tone she almost recognized. *You're still here.*

Lena packed her wares with hands that would not quite obey her, blew out her lantern, and climbed the stairs toward the palace with the silver knot burning gently at her wrist and one thought repeating in her skull like a struck bell:

The gods weren't dead. The gods were waking up.

And one of them knew her grandmother's name.
