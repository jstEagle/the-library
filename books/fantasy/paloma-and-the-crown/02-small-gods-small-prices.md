## 2. Small Gods, Small Prices

The Nightmarket opened at the hour when the tide turned and honest people went to bed.

Lena knew it by smell before she saw it — tar and frying onions and the particular sweetness of beeswax candles burning in defiance of a law that had outlawed them forty years ago. The Edict of Ash said no flame for prayer, no knot for luck, no word spoken over bread or water or salt. It said nothing about selling fried eels on skewers, which was why the market wore its disguise like a mask: every stall was food, or claimed to be. The fishwife's cart held more than herring under its cloth. The man who sold roasted chestnuts kept his fingers warm on coals that had been consecrated once, long ago, by someone who hadn't known they'd be passing prayers through them ever after.

Lena's own stall was a blanket and a basket. On top: dried figs, candied ginger, honeycomb in paper twists. Beneath the figs, wrapped in oilcloth and folded like contraband because it was: knots.

She'd made thirty-two of them since the king's breakfast that morning. Protective knots, three loops and a lock, tied with her left hand while her right hand did something ordinary — shelling peas, stirring soup, holding a pen — so that if anyone watched, it looked like idleness. That was the whole trick of a charm, Odile-the-absent-priestess would have told her if Odile existed yet in Lena's life; she didn't, not for another week, but the principle was already true: a charm is just attention, braided. You paid attention until the paying became a thing you could hold.

The first customer came while the tide was still going out. A dockworker, big through the shoulders, moving like his back hurt, which it did. Tomas. She knew him the way she knew all of them — by their troubles.

"Figs," he said, loud enough for anyone listening. "And the ginger."

"Two coppers." She counted them into her apron pocket with the ceremony of an honest transaction, then bent to wrap the fruit, and when she passed him the paper twist her thumb pressed once against his knuckles, and the knot she'd tied into the wrapping's string transferred itself without a seam showing. Three loops and a lock. For the back. For the shoulder that the crane cable had nearly taken off him two winters ago.

He grunted, tested his spine with a small twist, and something in his face unclenched. "Good figs this year."

"Wet spring," Lena said. "Everything grows strange."

That was the liturgy, such as it was. Figs. Coppers. Wet spring. No god named, no blessing spoken, nothing a witch-hunter could write down in his little ledger and carry away to a fire. The Edict of Ash had burned the words out of the city, but it couldn't burn out the wanting, and where there was wanting there was a market, and where there was a market there was Lena Vasquez, royal taster by daylight, fig-seller by night, breaking the law one knot at a time.

* * *

By midnight she'd moved eleven charms. A luck-thread into the braid of a girl who worked the counting-houses and needed her numbers to come out kind. A sleep-knot for a stevedore whose hands shook from dreams he wouldn't describe. Two warding-braids for the fishwife herself, who paid in smoked eel and never asked what Lena charged anyone else, because the fishwife remembered when the shrines were open and considered this the least she could do about it.

It was good work. It was the only part of her life that felt like hers.

In the palace, her tongue belonged to the crown. Every morning she stood in the white-tiled tasting room and put the king's breakfast in her mouth and waited to die, and everyone called it an honor, and she had gotten so good at the waiting that she could do it with her heartbeat slowing instead of quickening, which the physicians said proved her constitution was remarkable, and which Lena privately suspected proved something else entirely. But that was daylight. Here, under the awning strung with unlit lanterns, her hands belonged to her. Her attention belonged to her. Thirty-two knots' worth of attention, given away at two coppers each, and if that was sin then the whole dockside was damned and glad of it.

"You look tired," said the fishwife, sliding onto the crate beside her stall. Marta was sixty and built like a mooring post and had buried two husbands and one son, and she looked at Lena the way she looked at weather coming in off the water.

"I'm always tired."

"Tired like sleep would fix?"

Lena thought about it honestly. The palace had been strange lately. Not dangerous-strange — she'd know dangerous, her whole body was an instrument for knowing dangerous — but *thin*-strange. The corridors echoed wrong. The soup in the kitchens had been tasting of things soup shouldn't taste of: rain on stone, candle smoke, somebody else's grief. She'd assumed she was coming down with something.

"Tired like I'm listening too hard," she said finally. "To nothing. There's nothing to listen to, and I'm listening anyway."

Marta nodded slowly, the way old women nodded when the young described symptoms the old women recognized. "My grandmother said that happened, the year before the gods went quiet. Everyone walking around with their ears pricked like dogs, hearing nothing, ashamed to say so." She took an eel skewer off her own cart and handed it over. "Then one day there was something to hear, and half the city had forgotten how."

"The gods didn't go quiet," Lena said, around eel. "They were silenced. There's a difference."

"There is," Marta agreed. "And the difference got a lot of people burned. So we sell figs."

"We sell figs," Lena said, and they sat together in the candle-smell and the onion-smell, two criminals having supper.

* * *

The boy came near the end of the night, when the tide had turned again and the market was folding itself back into innocence — cloths thrown over contraband, knots unpicked, candles pinched out with wet fingers.

He wasn't a regular. He was maybe nineteen, dockside-born, with rope burns on his palms and the particular hungry politeness of someone who has spent his whole life being careful around other people's property. He stood at the edge of her blanket for a long moment before he worked up the nerve to approach, and Lena catalogued him automatically, the way she catalogued everything now: healthy, frightened, sincere. Not a hunter. Hunters never looked hungry for anything except your name.

"Figs?" he said.

"Last of them. One copper."

He counted it out coin by coin, and she saw that it was the last copper he had, and she gave him extra ginger without making a production of it, and he took the twist in both hands like it might be snatched away.

"My sister," he said, and stopped. Started again. "She's sick. Fever, six days now. The physickers want four silver just to look at her and my mother says fever breaks or it doesn't and God—" he caught himself on the word *God*, flinched, corrected course like a sailor avoiding rocks, "—and medicine is for people with money."

Lena looked at him. The market was emptying. Somewhere behind the warehouses, a witch-hunter's patrol bell rang the hour, two streets over, unhurried.

"What's her name?" she asked.

"Sefa."

"Sefa," Lena repeated, and reached under the figs for the last knot in her basket — the thirty-second, the one she'd tied that morning while stirring soup, when the soup had tasted of rain on stone, and she hadn't known why she'd made it or what it was for, only that her hands had insisted.

Three loops and a lock. But this one she'd tied differently, without deciding to: a fourth loop, open, unfinished, like a door left ajar. She'd never tied a fourth loop before. She didn't have a theory about it. Her hands had wanted it and her hands were usually right about things her head hadn't learned yet.

"For the fever," she said quietly. "Tie it to her wrist. Left wrist. And—" she hesitated, because the next part was the illegal part, the actual crime, the part with words in it, and she hadn't spoken charm-words aloud in five years, not once, not even alone in her room with the door barred. "And say her name over it. Just her name. That's all. Names are—"

She stopped. Because she had been about to say *names are how the gods find you*, and she had no idea where that sentence came from, and it scared her more than the patrol bells did.

"Just her name," she finished. "Twice."

The boy nodded like a man taking orders from a captain. He pressed another coin into her hand — he didn't have another coin, she could see that he didn't, it must have come from somewhere desperate — and he turned and ran for the stairs up to the dockside lanes, and Lena watched him go and felt the fourth loop of the knot sitting in her mind like a splinter.

*Open,* her hands had said. *Leave it open.*

For what? Nothing answered. Nothing had answered in Vhalen for forty years. That was the whole architecture of the world: the Edict of Ash, the burned shrines, the gods gone silent as drowned bells. You lived inside that silence the way fish live inside water. You didn't question it. You sold figs.

* * *

She was packing her basket when it happened.

Later — in the weeks after, when everything had changed and she had time to take the memory apart bolt by bolt — she would try to reconstruct the exact sequence, and she never quite could. The human mind, she decided eventually, was not built to hold the moment the water starts flowing uphill. It keeps trying to file it somewhere sensible.

What she retained was this:

The boy's voice, from the top of the stair, thin with distance and exhaustion, cracking on the second syllable because he was crying as he ran: "*Sefa. Sefa.*"

Just her name. Twice. Exactly as instructed.

And the silence of Vhalen — the great, permanent, architectural silence, the silence Lena had lived inside for twenty-five years the way fish live inside water — *broke.*

Not like glass. Like ice on a pond in thaw. Like something underneath shifting its weight after a long, long stillness.

Every candle in the Nightmarket relit at once.

All of them. The ones pinched out with wet fingers, the ones burned down to stubs, the ones that had been cold for hours in their lanterns. Forty small flames standing up straight in the windless dark, and the light they threw was the wrong color — gold shading toward something older, something like the color of the inside of a shell — and every person left in the market froze, and nobody screamed, which Lena would remember as the strangest part. Nobody screamed. Twenty dockworkers and smugglers and fishwives stood in a circle of impossible candlelight and held their breath like children hiding from a father coming home drunk.

Marta's hand found Lena's arm and gripped hard enough to bruise.

On the blanket, in the basket, under the folded oilcloth, the remaining knots — the eleven unsold ones, the ones with nowhere to go yet — grew warm. Not hot. Warm, the way a sleeping animal is warm. Lena could feel them through three layers of cloth, feel them the way you feel a heartbeat through a wall, and her hands, her clever criminal hands, began to tremble for the first time since she'd started this trade.

"*Sefa. Sefa.*" The boy's voice had faded up into the lanes, but the name seemed to hang anyway, suspended in the candle-gold, and the air itself leaned toward it—

—and somewhere above the rooftops, or below the cobblestones, or inside the space between one heartbeat and the next, something *heard.*

There was no thunder. No voice. That was almost the worst of it — how small it was. What came back was barely a sound at all: a single note, low and round, like a bell heard through fathoms of water, like the hum a tuning fork leaves in your bones after your ears stop reporting it. It lasted perhaps two seconds. It touched the market the way dawn touches a room — everywhere at once, changing the color of everything — and then it was gone, and the candles guttered down to ordinary flame, and the tide sighed against the pilings, and the world resumed.

Nobody spoke for a full minute.

Then the fishwife crossed herself — an old gesture, pre-Edict, technically punishable by branding — and whispered, in the voice of a woman talking to herself in a shrine forty years closed:

"It answered."

"It answered," Lena said. Her voice came out steady, which surprised her, because inside she was standing in a room where the floor used to be. "Forty years, and it answered a fever-prayer said over a two-copper knot."

"That's not how it used to work," Marta said slowly. "Before. Before, you needed the priests, the rites, the—"

"I know how it used to work."

"Then how—"

*I don't know,* Lena almost said. *I tied a fourth loop and left it open and I don't know why and something walked through the door I made.*

But she didn't say it. Because saying it would make it hers, and some animal instinct deep beneath her cleverness told her that whatever had just woken in Vhalen, the safest place to be was unnoticed by it. She was a woman who survived palaces. She knew how to be furniture.

"Bad omen," she said instead, loudly, for the benefit of anyone watching. "Candles do that. Drafts. Go home."

They went home. The market dissolved into the fog with the guilty speed of people leaving the scene of something. Marta squeezed her arm once — *we will speak of this later, and carefully* — and rolled her cart away, and Lena stood alone on the empty dock with her basket of warm knots and listened to the water.

Her hands had stopped trembling. That was the thing she noticed last, the detail that would keep her awake until the palace bells rang prime: her hands weren't afraid.

Her hands were *listening.* Pricked forward like dogs, like Marta's grandmother had said, like the whole city had been for forty years without admitting it. Listening to the silence that was no longer quite silent, and finding the sound of it familiar.

Like a voice they'd known before they were born.

Lena picked up her basket. Eleven knots slept inside it, warm as sleeping birds, each one carrying a fourth loop she had no memory of deciding to tie. Above her, on the heights, the palace glowed with its lawful, soulless lamplight, and somewhere in it the king sat eating a dinner she would taste at dawn, and none of it — none of it — felt solid anymore.

She climbed the stairs toward the lanes, past the wall where the Edict of Ash was chiseled in letters every child could read: NO FLAME FOR PRAYER. NO KNOT FOR LUCK. NO WORD OVER BREAD OR SALT. THE GODS ARE ASH AND THE ASH IS LAW.

Someone had chalked a small mark beneath the inscription. Fresh. Tonight-fresh. A circle with a dot in the center — the old sign for *the ear that hears* — and under it, in a hurried hand:

*IT ANSWERED IN THE DOCKSIDE. TELL EVERYONE.*

Lena stared at it for a long time.

Then she pulled her hood up, and walked home through a city that had just, very quietly, stopped being the world she knew — and behind her, faint as a bell through fathoms of water, the silence went on humming.
