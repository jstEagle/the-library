## 3. The Edict of Ash

The cell smelled of wet stone and old fear, and Captain Sorrel had learned to read both the way other men read ledgers.

He sat across from the prisoner with a slate on his knees and a lamp between them, and he let the silence do its work. Silence was the first tool of his trade, before the irons, before the questions, before — if it came to that — the pyre cart in the yard below. Most men broke in the quiet. They filled it with confessions the way a leaking hull fills with sea.

This one was filling it already.

"It wasn't mine," the charm-seller said. "The stall. I was minding it for my sister."

"Your sister." Sorrel wrote nothing. Writing too early made prisoners perform; they began composing instead of remembering. "Name?"

"Tamsin. Tamsin Corr." The man's hands were folded on the table like a penitent's, but the knuckles were white. He was maybe forty, a dockworker's build gone soft, salt-cracked fingers. A man who'd spent years hauling rope and had lately turned to easier money. "She sells — she sold — thread. Luck-thread. For sailors' wives. It's nothing. It's superstition, is all. You know how they are down at the harbor."

"I know how they are everywhere," Sorrel said.

That was true, and it was the whole trouble. The Edict of Ash had been written for a world that no longer existed — or so everyone had believed for five quiet years. Sorrel had joined the witch-hunters under that belief, which was to say he had joined a constabulary that mostly chased frauds: hedge-wives charging coppers for knotted string, mountebanks selling bottled fog as blessing-water. Honest work, mostly. Sad work. He had never burned anyone who could actually do what they were accused of, because in five years of service he had never met anyone who could.

Until this spring.

Until the prayers started coming back answered.

He looked at the man across the table — Corr, or whoever he was — and thought about the report from three weeks ago. A fisherman in the Saltrow district who had prayed aloud over his drowned nets and watched them come up heavy the next morning, every mesh silvered with fish, while four other boats went out into the same water and caught nothing. The fisherman had wept with gratitude in front of witnesses. Then he had come to the witch-hunters himself, terrified, asking to be arrested, because he understood what an answered prayer meant now. It meant the Edict had teeth again. It meant someone upstairs had noticed.

Sorrel had taken his statement. The man had described the sensation of being heard — *heard*, that was the word he kept using, like a child describing being seen by something enormous through a window — and Sorrel had written it all down in his neat clerk's hand and filed it under Fraudulent Claims, Category Three, and then he had gone home and drunk half a bottle of plum wine and not slept.

Because you could file a fraud. You could not file a god.

"The ledger," Sorrel said.

Corr flinched. Good. The flinch told him more than another hour of questions would have.

"We found it in your sister's stall. Hidden under the false bottom of the thread box. I'm going to read you some marks from it, and you're going to tell me what they mean." He opened the oilcloth wrapping he'd brought, unfolded the ledger, and turned it toward the lamplight.

It was a working book, well-thumbed, water-stained at the corners. Not a grimoire — nothing so dramatic. A tradesman's record. Columns of dates. Columns of items: *knots, red thread, nine-count. Salt bags, palm-sized. Iron nails, blessed-sounding.* And prices, mostly small. Copper, mostly. Sometimes bread. Once, memorably, a pair of boots.

And beside each entry, in a hand that was not Corr's — smaller, quicker, surer — a mark.

Sorrel had been staring at that mark for two days.

It wasn't a name. It was a small drawn sign, repeated down the margin of every page where the goods changed hands: a circle with a single line descending from it, and at the bottom of the line, a second, tinier circle. Like a plumb bob. Like a drop falling from a ring. Like —

Like a tongue, he had thought, the first night, and hadn't known why the word came to him, or why it sat wrong in his chest afterward, heavy as a swallowed stone.

"This mark," he said. "Whose?"

"I don't know."

"You do know."

"I don't." But Corr's eyes had flicked — just once, just a hair's width — toward the left side of the ledger, and Sorrel turned back to the earlier pages without letting his face change.

The early entries were older. Two years old, by the dating. And there the mark appeared less often, and beside some entries there was something else instead: a name, written small. *Lena.*

*Two knots, red thread, for the Vasser girl's cradle. Lena.*

*Salt bag, one copper, widow Hesk. Lena.*

*Luck-thread, three lengths, harbor pilot's wife. Lena.*

"Lena," Sorrel said aloud, and watched the prisoner's face go carefully, elaborately blank, which was its own confession.

"I don't know any Lena."

"No," Sorrel agreed mildly. "You wouldn't. She'd be careful. Someone supplying charms to a dozen sellers across the Nightmarket would want to be very careful." He turned pages with one finger, unhurried. "But she wasn't careful enough, or your sister wasn't, because here she is. Two years of entries. Same hand throughout. Same mark when she stopped signing her name — and note, if you will, that she stopped signing her name about fourteen months ago. Right around the time the witch-hunters started paying informants in the Nightmarket. She adapted. That's not a fraud's instinct. Frauds run. This one got *careful*."

Corr said nothing. Sweat had started at his hairline despite the cold of the cell.

"Here's what happens now," Sorrel continued, in the flat tone he used for procedure, because procedure was a kindness when it was honest and a weapon when it wasn't, and tonight he honestly didn't know which it was. "You're going to tell me everything you know about her. What she looks like. Where she sources her materials. Who taught her the knots. Whether she ever said anything — anything at all — that sounded like doctrine. Prayer-forms. Invocations. Names of—" He stopped himself before *gods*. Even in a cell, even alone, the word had weight now. It had weight everywhere now. "Old words."

"She never said anything like that." Corr's voice cracked on the denial. "She was — look, captain, I only met her twice. Twice! She came at night, left the stock, took the coin. Kept her hood up. Small woman. Quick hands. That's all I—"

"Twice." Sorrel tapped the ledger. "Then explain to me why your sister's own hand appears in this book exactly eleven times, and this mark appears two hundred and six."

The silence stretched. Somewhere above them, boots crossed the guardroom floor, and both men tracked the sound automatically, the way sailors track weather.

"She supplied everyone," Corr whispered finally. "Everyone. Half the Nightmarket, maybe more. The dockworkers wouldn't buy from anyone else once they'd bought from her once. There was a woman — Olla, at the fish stalls — her boy had the fever last winter, and Lena gave her a knot and told her to put it under the pillow and *say nothing over it*, just set it there, and the fever broke in two days, and after that—" He spread his cracked hands. "After that it wasn't commerce anymore, captain. People were leaving offerings. Little ones. Bread crusts, buttons, flowers. At her stall when she wasn't there. Like it was a shrine."

"And did the knots work?"

"What?"

"You heard me." Sorrel leaned forward. The lamp threw his shadow up the wall behind him, long and thin and official. "Did the fever break because of medicine and luck, or did it break because of the knot? Think carefully. Your answer determines whether I write *fraud* next to your name or something else entirely."

Corr looked at him with something raw in his face — not defiance, not quite pleading. Something closer to a man standing at a door he has spent his whole life pretending isn't there.

"They worked," he said quietly. "Captain, I've tied those knots myself since, a hundred times, following her pattern exactly, and mine don't do anything. Mine are string. Hers were — hers were *listened to*." His voice dropped further, as though the walls might be listening too, and after this spring, Sorrel could not honestly say they weren't. "Whatever she has, it isn't craft. It's not something you learn. It's something she *is*."

*It's something she is.*

Sorrel closed the ledger.

* * *

He walked the long way back through the palace corridors, past the night watch changing shifts, past the kitchens where the ovens were already banked for the morning's baking, and he carried the ledger against his ribs like a wound.

The Witch-Hunter's Office occupied three rooms off the eastern range, and it was empty at this hour except for the clerks' desks and the wall. The wall was his least favorite thing in Vhalen, and he had helped build it.

Every name of every person processed under the Edict of Ash, going back twenty years, inked onto parchment panels in columns. Convicted, acquitted, pending. And along the bottom edge, in a separate, smaller script — the names of the burned. Not many. Forty-one in five years, which the Office considered restraint, and which Sorrel privately considered forty-one people whose knots had been string and whose sins had been poverty.

He stood in front of the wall now, holding a ledger that might add a forty-second.

Except it wouldn't, would it. That was the thing gnawing at him like damp getting into mortar. Every case on that wall, every one, had been a person doing something. Saying something. Selling something. Actions. The Edict of Ash forbade acts: the making of charms, the speaking of invocations, the claiming of divine favor. Acts could be witnessed, weighed, punished. The law knew what to do with acts.

But this Lena — this supplier with the plumb-bob mark and the quick hands — according to Corr she had done nothing. Given a knot, said *set it there, say nothing.* No invocation. No claim. And the fever had broken anyway.

If that was true, then the crime wasn't in her hands. It was in her blood. And the Edict had no category for blood.

The state had noticed the same gap, of course. That was why the orders had come down in the last month, sealed orders, from above the Office, from above the Ministry, from whatever room the Lord Protector's council actually happened in. New directives. Interrogations were to include questions about lineage. Grandmothers. Family shrines. Anything inherited. Because the scholars had finally admitted what the priests had whispered since the first answered prayer: the divinity returning to Vhalen wasn't arriving fresh. It was arriving *down family lines*. Old blessings, sleeping in bloodlines for generations, waking up all at once like a house full of clocks striking the same hour.

Which meant the witch-hunters' work was changing shape. They were no longer hunting deeds. They were hunting descent.

Sorrel had read the new directive three times and signed for it once, and the signature had felt different in his hand than signatures usually felt. He had spent ten years believing he was a broom — sweeping up fraud, protecting fools from their own desperation. Brooms were useful. Brooms were clean.

Nobody had told him he was a bloodhound.

"Captain."

He turned. Under-clerk Pellin stood in the doorway with a dispatch box, young and earnest and up too late, the way under-clerks always were.

"Report from the harbor watch," Pellin said. "Another incident. Third this week."

"Read it."

Pellin unrolled the paper, and his voice, reading, went carefully neutral in the way clerks' voices did when the content frightened them. "Fishwife named Olla — the same, sir, the one mentioned in your case notes — reports that on the night tide she spoke a thank-you over her stall. Out loud. To no one in particular. Says the words quote 'came up out of her on their own,' end quote, old harbor dialect, pre-Edict form. And the candle she'd lit for it—" Pellin paused. "Sir, the candle flame stood straight up. In harbor wind. For the length of the prayer. Four witnesses."

"Names of witnesses?"

"All four willing to swear to it, sir. That's the problem, isn't it. They're not hiding it anymore. They're—" Pellin hesitated, choosing the word with visible care. "They're *glad*, sir. Down at the harbor. People are glad. They're lighting candles on purpose now. There's talk of reopening the drowned shrine at the seawall. Unofficially."

The drowned shrine. Sorrel knew it — every native of Vhalen knew it, the little sunken alcove at the base of the seawall where the old-timers' grandparents had left offerings to some minor deity of tides and grandmothers, back before the Edict flooded it on purpose, a lesson in priorities. He'd walked past it a thousand times. Children dared each other to dangle their feet over it.

"Add it to the file," he said. "Category Three."

"Sir?" Pellin shifted. "It's — with respect, a candle that won't gutter in wind isn't really fraud, is it. Shouldn't that be—"

"Shouldn't it be what, Under-clerk?"

Pellin looked at the wall. At the forty-one names along the bottom. He was young enough that he'd joined after the last burning, Sorrel realized. Young enough to think the list was finished.

"Nothing, sir," Pellin said, and left.

Sorrel stood alone with the ledger and the wall and the lamp, and he did the arithmetic he'd been avoiding for two days.

A supplier with a gift she hadn't asked for, running charms through half the Nightmarket for two years, careful, adaptive, unnamed. A city where prayers were starting to answer themselves. Sealed orders telling him to hunt bloodlines. And somewhere in the palace above him — he'd seen her once, months ago, a small dark-haired woman tasting the king's breakfast with perfect professional calm — somewhere above him worked a royal taster who, by every account he'd gathered tonight, matched the description of the most successful illegal charmer in Vhalen.

Small woman. Quick hands. Careful.

He didn't have proof. He had a mark, a first name, a prisoner's memory of a hood, and a coincidence of build. In ten years he'd convicted men on less, and he'd have called that good work.

But he stood there under the lamp with the ledger open to the last page, where the final entry sat in that quick sure hand — *knots, six, for the flood families. No charge. — L* — and he found that his pen, dipped and ready, hovered over the warrant form and would not come down.

No charge. Six knots for families whose homes had flooded, free, the week the river rose. That wasn't a criminal building a network. That was — what? What was that?

He thought of the fisherman who'd begged to be arrested. Of Olla's candle standing straight in the wind. Of Corr saying *hers were listened to* with awe cracking his voice open.

The old texts said that before the Edict, before the crowns centralized everything, blessings had moved through the world the way water moved through it — everywhere, ordinary, unowned. That kings had needed tasters not merely against poison but because a blessed tongue could taste truth, could carry messages between the high and the low. He'd always assumed that was embroidery. Priests' nostalgia.

Now he held two years of evidence that the embroidery was stitching itself back into the world, one copper-charm at a time, and his orders said burn it out at the root, and his hand said *wait*, and he did not know which of those voices was duty anymore.

Sorrel capped the ink.

He did not write the warrant. Instead he pulled a fresh sheet toward himself and began, in his precise clerk's hand, a request for expanded surveillance authority — watching, not arresting. Buying time without knowing, yet, whose time he was buying, or why.

At the top of the page, where the subject's name belonged, he wrote: *Lena — mark of the falling ring.*

Outside, far below the palace, at the drowned shrine by the seawall, someone had lit a candle. He couldn't see it from here. He didn't need to.

He could feel it anyway — the whole city beginning, very softly, to pray — and for the first time in ten years of service, Captain Sorrel was afraid of his own orders.
