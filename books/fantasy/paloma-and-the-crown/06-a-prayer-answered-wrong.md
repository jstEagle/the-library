## 6. A Prayer Answered Wrong

The knock came before dawn, three quick raps and then silence, which was how Brannock the cooper always knocked when he had bad news and didn't want the neighbors to know it.

Lena dressed in the dark, pinned her taster's badge to her collar where it caught what little light there was — a habit, armor of a kind — and opened the door on a man gray as old wood.

"Tam Wicker," Brannock said. "He's dead."

The word went through her like a fishhook finding flesh. She gripped the doorframe. "Dead how?"

"Found this morning under Saltcrane Three. Drowned." Brannock's eyes were red-rimmed and puzzled, and it was the puzzlement that frightened her more than the grief. "In four inches of water, Lena. In the shallows by the pilings, where a child could stand up. He was fifty-one years old and he'd worked those docks since he was nine. He knew every current in that harbor like his own teeth. And they pulled him out of four inches of water with a smile on his face."

*With a smile on his face.*

She heard herself say the right things. She heard herself promise to come, to help with the widow, to bring bread. Her voice sounded like it belonged to someone standing slightly behind her, watching all this happen over her shoulder. Brannock touched his cap and went, boots loud on the stairs, and Lena stood alone in her narrow room above the chandler's shop with the last of the night pressed against the window, and thought about the sound a prayer makes when it is answered.

She had heard it two nights ago. A sound like a struck bell underwater, like a door opening in a house that had been locked for a hundred years. Tam Wicker, salt-crusted and gap-toothed, holding the luck-thread she'd sold him for three coppers, laughing because his knee had stopped aching for the first time in a decade — and then going quiet, going *still*, and saying it out loud, not even really to her, the way men say things to the dark:

*"If you're listening — if anything's listening — I'd give anything to see my boy again. Just once more. He's been in the water six years and I never got to say goodbye."*

And something had listened.

* * *

The Wickers lived in a lean-to row behind the rope-walks, houses so close together you could shake hands across the gaps. Mara Wicker met Lena at the door with her sleeves still wet from laying out the body, and she didn't cry, which told Lena everything about how much crying had already happened in that house.

"You were kind to him," Mara said. "At the market. He talked about it. The little knot you sold him." Her mouth twisted. "Three coppers for a piece of string, and he wore it like a crown prince wears a signet."

"It wasn't string," Lena said, before she could stop herself.

Mara looked at her. In the dim interior, behind her, the body lay on the table under a sheet, and the smell of salt and lamp oil hung in the air, and somewhere a neighbor's child was practicing a fiddle badly, and the whole ordinary world went on as if the laws inside it hadn't changed.

"No," Mara said quietly. "It wasn't. Come in, taster. Come look at him, since you're the only other person who'll understand what I'm about to show you."

The room was small and clean and poor. Tam Wicker lay with his hands folded on his chest, and his face —

Lena had seen drowned men before. Everyone in Vhalen had, sooner or later; the harbor took its tithe. Drowned faces were swollen, gray-blue, terrible. This face was none of those things. It was relaxed. It was *happy*. The smile on it was not the rictus grin of death but the wide, unguarded, creased-eyed smile of a man hearing his name called from a doorway by a voice he'd stopped hoping to hear.

"Look at his hands," Mara said.

His hands were open. His palms were cupped, slightly, the way you cup them to hold water, or to hold a child's face.

"There's more," Mara said, and her voice cracked at last, just once, like ice on a pond. "Come to the window."

On the sill, arranged with care, sat a collection of things that made Lena's skin prickle: a child's wooden boat, paint flaked to ghosts of blue. A knucklebone. A clay whistle shaped like a gull. Six years of a dead boy's leavings, kept in a box under the bed, and now laid out in a neat row facing the harbor.

"I didn't put them there," Mara said. "I found them like that this morning. He must have gotten up in the night — he took his coat, he took the *knot you sold him* — and he walked down to the water and he laid out Jossy's things like he was setting a table for him." She turned. Her eyes were dry and enormous. "And then he walked into four inches of water and drowned, smiling, with his arms open like he was picking the boy up off a dock. Like Jossy ran to him. Like—"

She stopped. She pressed the back of her hand against her mouth.

"Like the prayer was answered," Lena said.

"That's the thing," Mara whispered. "That's the thing that's going to make me mad, I think, in a day or two, when I can feel things again. Because it *was*. Don't you see? He asked. Something gave. My husband died happy, happier than I've seen him in six years, and I'm supposed to be grateful, and I want to burn this city down instead."

* * *

She left the widow's house with the sun fully up and the streets loud, and none of the loudness reached her. She walked the long way to the palace, along the harbor road, because she needed to see the water.

Saltcrane Three stood black-legged against the morning glare, gulls wheeling its guy-ropes. Below it, the shallows lay flat and innocent as poured glass. Someone had already chalked a rough circle on the pilings — dockside custom, a warding mark against the drowned — and someone else had left a heel of bread and a cup of wine on the lowest step, offerings to whatever watched over sailors, though the Edict had stripped the watching-out of every name twenty years ago. People still left the offerings. They just weren't allowed to say to whom.

Lena stood looking at the water and did the arithmetic she'd been avoiding since Brannock's knock.

Two nights ago, the gods had begun answering again. Not the great gods, not yet — nothing with a name anyone remembered — but small ones, threshold-things, gutter-things, the kind that lived in knots and threads and chalk circles and the space between a seller's murmur and a buyer's hope. She had felt it happen. Every charm-seller in the Nightmarket had felt it happen; they'd all gone quiet at once, the way birds go quiet, and then old Sefa had laughed out loud because the fever-blessing she'd tied into a rag doll had *taken*, visibly taken, heat blooming off the child's brow like bread from an oven.

And Tam Wicker had prayed. Openly, gratefully, with a luck-thread in his fist and no idea what he was talking to.

*I'd give anything to see my boy again.*

Anything. The words had meant, in the language of living men, *I miss my son.* In the language of whatever had heard him, they apparently meant exactly what they said. A price named is a price collectable. And the thing that had collected it hadn't been cruel — that was the part that made her stomach turn over and over. It had been *generous*. It had given him exactly what he asked for, wrapped in joy, delivered with a smile, and it had clearly, sincerely believed it was doing him a kindness.

Her grandmother's voice, five years dead and usually silent, surfaced in her memory with terrible clarity: *"A blessing without manners, little dove. That's what you get when you wake something that's been asleep. It doesn't know the new customs. It doesn't know we've learned to mean less than we say."*

The gods had gone to sleep — or been starved, silenced, outlawed root and branch by the Edict of Ash — back when people still prayed plainly. They had woken into a world of careful words and half-hopes, and they were answering the words, not the hopes.

Lena looked at the flat bright water and felt the weight settle onto her shoulders with the specific gravity of guilt. Not the vague guilt of the merchant who sells a knife that's later misused. This was precise. She had tied that thread. She had murmured the waking-words over it — the little invocation her grandmother taught her, the one that was technically illegal in eleven ways — and she had put it into Tam Wicker's hand and told him, laughing, *hold it when you pray, it carries the words further.*

It carried the words further. Gods, it had carried them further.

* * *

The palace consumed the middle of her day the way it consumed everything: the king's breakfast tasted under the steward Maren's cool gray gaze, the ceremonial sip and swallow and count of sixty, the sweetbread safe, the honeyed pears safe, the small ritual theater of loyalty performed flawlessly by a woman whose hands, she noticed distantly, were not quite steady on the tasting spoons.

Maren noticed too. Lena saw her notice — saw the steward's eyes flick to her fingers, file the tremor away in whatever vast archive lived behind that composed face — and could do nothing about it. Her body had learned, in five years of court service, to keep her face pleasant while her mind screamed. But her hands had always been honest. It was why she was good at knots.

*You are a royal food-taster,* she reminded herself, walking the colonnade afterward with her tray. *Your job is poison. Poison has rules. Poison is chemistry and malice and it can be learned, catalogued, countered. What killed Tam Wicker has no rules. What killed Tam Wicker is love, translated badly.*

Because that was the horror she kept circling back to, the drain at the bottom of the day that everything slid toward: it hadn't been malice. If some witch-hunter's toxin had stopped Tam's heart, she could have hated it cleanly. Instead the harbor god — or gutter god, or whatever small listening thing haunted Saltcrane Three — had loved him the way a dog loves, wholly and without comprehension. *You want your boy. Here is your boy. Here is the moment you have wanted for six years, complete, perfect, forever.* It had probably held the moment open for him. It had probably let him stand in four inches of water with his arms full of his son for what felt, inside it, like hours. Days. Long enough to say goodbye properly, this time.

And then it had set him down gently on the bottom of the shallows, still smiling, and gone back to sleep, satisfied with a job well done.

Lena set her tray down in the servants' passage and put both hands flat against the cold stone until the shaking stopped.

Here was what she knew now, knowledge arriving in her like a tide filling a channel: the returning gods were raw. Unfiltered. They had none of the mediation the old priesthoods used to provide — the liturgies, the qualifiers, the thousand years of theological cushioning between a human wish and a divine act. The priestesses used to be translators. *What she means, lord, is not 'give me my son' but 'ease my grief.'* Without translators, prayers went through naked. And most human hearts, she suspected, did not survive contact with their own plain words.

Someone had to learn the new customs fast. Someone had to go into the Nightmarket tonight and tell Sefa and the others: stop selling waking-words. Stop letting them pray with the threads in their fists. Tie the charms deaf, if you have to tie them at all.

And underneath that, quieter, colder, the thought she couldn't shake: *and someone has to find out what else woke up.* Because Tam Wicker's gutter-god had been small and gentle and stupid with love. Two nights ago, in the deep water past the harbor bar, something else had turned over in its sleep, something whose answer would not be a smile. She'd felt it the way you feel weather coming in your knees. The whole city had felt it, if it admitted it — dogs barking at nothing, babies waking at midnight, the king's kitchen cats refusing to enter the pantry.

* * *

Dusk found her back in the Nightmarket, in the alley behind the ropemaker's stall where the charm-sellers gathered when the watch-torches were slow.

They were subdued tonight. She'd expected celebration — two nights ago there had been laughter, tears, old Sefa dancing a jig with a fever-cured toddler on her hip. Tonight the stalls were shuttered early and the talk was low, and when Lena pushed through into the lamplight she understood why before anyone spoke.

They'd heard about Tam. Of course they had. The docks were a village.

"He came to my stall first," said Petch, the knot-seller, a broad soft-spoken man with dye-stained fingers. He was turning a luck-knot over and over in his hands like he was trying to find the flaw in it. "Before you, taster. I told him the thread was just thread. I told him the old days were done and he shouldn't go putting his heart into string." His jaw worked. "Then you sold him the waking one. And now he's dead."

"And smiling," said Sefa sharply, from her stool. "Don't leave that part out, Petch, because it matters. He died happier than any man I've buried in forty years of burying dockfolk. You want to tell me that's a crime?"

"I want to know," Petch said, "who's going to tell his widow it was a kindness."

The argument broke around Lena like water around a post. She let it run a while, because she deserved to stand in it. Then she climbed onto the ropemaker's cutting bench, and the alley went quiet the way it went quiet for the watch, except this was voluntary.

"Listen to me," she said. "Something answered him. Something real. And it will answer the next prayer too, and the next, and it isn't cruel, and that's the problem — it's *literal*. It hears what we say, not what we mean. We've spent twenty years learning to pray sideways, in hints, because praying straight got our grandmothers burned. Well, the thing listening now doesn't understand sideways. It understands *yes*."

Old Sefa squinted up at her. "So we stop?"

"We don't stop." Lena heard her own voice come out steady and was vaguely proud of it. "We can't stop — it's happening whether we sell charms or not. People are praying all over this city right now, in chapels and cellars and harborside, and the answers are coming, and most of them are going to land like Tam's did unless somebody teaches the city how to ask." She looked around at their faces — Petch's misery, Sefa's fierce curiosity, the young ones at the back with their hungry eyes. "Somebody has to learn the customs again. Somebody has to stand between the prayers and the answering, and translate. That used to be a job. Priestess. Before the Edict burned them all."

Silence. Somewhere above, a gull cried once and thought better of it.

"And where do we find one of those?" Petch said bitterly. "The last order was scattered twenty years ago. Burned, broken, or worse. There's nobody left who remembers the old forms. Nobody—"

He stopped. Because Sefa had turned her head, slowly, and was looking at Lena with an expression the taster couldn't read at all.

"Not nobody," Sefa said. "There's a woman under the Cistern Quarter who buys candle stubs off me every month and pays in old temple coin. Never lights them. Just buys them. And once, when I asked her why, she looked at me like I'd stuck a knife in her and said, *because someday they'll need lighting again, and I want to be the one who knows how.*"

The alley held its breath.

"She's real?" Lena asked. Her heart was doing something complicated against her ribs — hope and dread fighting for the same ground. "A priestess? Actually one of the old order?"

"She's real," Sefa said. "And she's hiding, which means she's smart. And if the gods are waking up the way you say—" the old woman's eyes glittered, "—then she's the only person in Vhalen who might know what's going to happen next. Which, girl, given that you're the one who handed Tam Wicker his miracle, means you'd best go find her before the witch-hunters find out it was *your* thread in his fist."

Lena opened her mouth to answer — and the lamplight in the alley guttered, all at once, every flame leaning the same direction, toward the mouth of the lane, as if drawn by a draft.

But there was no wind.

Down the alley, far off, iron-shod boots rang on cobblestone in the crisp cadence of a patrol — too many boots, moving too fast, and among them a sound she knew from nightmares: the dry rattle of ash-sifters, the witch-hunters' tools, being carried ready.

"They're coming," Petch breathed. "Now. Tonight."

Sefa's hand closed hard around Lena's wrist. "Cistern Quarter," the old woman hissed. "Go *now*, girl, and pray — no. Don't pray. Whatever you do tonight, don't pray."
