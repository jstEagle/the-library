## 7. The Priestess in the Cistern

The raid came at the wrong hour, which meant it came at the right one, because witch-hunters were never wrong about hours.

Lena had been mid-sale — a luck-thread for a rope-maker's daughter, three coppers and a whispered wish — when the lamps along Cooper's Row went out. Not dimmed. Out, all at once, in a line, like a throat being closed finger by finger.

"Edict men," someone said, and the Nightmarket did what the Nightmarket always did: it became nothing. Stalls folded into shadows. Charm-sellers swallowed their wares or dropped them down grates. The rope-maker's daughter was gone before Lena finished blinking, her coppers left on the crate between them like an apology.

Lena's hands knew what to do even while her mind was still catching up. She swept the knots and threads into her apron pocket, kicked the crate into the canal, and turned for the sewer mouth behind the tannery — the one that smelled so bad even the hunters' dogs refused it.

She made it four steps.

A lantern flared white at the end of the row, and a voice she knew from two interrogations she'd been forced to watch said, pleasantly, "Everyone stay exactly where you are. This is a kindness. Running is how people get hurt."

Captain Sorrel. Of course it was Sorrel. He never raided with more than six men; he never needed to. He stood in the middle of Cooper's Row with his grey coat buttoned to the chin and his warrant held up like a small sun, and around him his hunters moved through the crowd with the gentle, terrible efficiency of men who had done this a hundred times and would do it a hundred more.

Lena turned the other way.

There was no other way. There was only the tannery wall, and above it, the old aqueduct arches where the city's forgotten water still dripped, and somewhere in her memory, a drunk dockworker named Tam saying: *if the hunters ever come, girl, go down. The city keeps its old priests under its feet.*

She'd laughed at him then. She wasn't laughing now.

The grate behind the tannery was rusted but not locked — nothing in Vhalen locked what everyone was too disgusted to enter. Lena pulled it open, lowered herself into the dark, and let the smell of a century of hides close over her head like a lid.

Behind her, muffled by stone, she heard Sorrel's voice again, still pleasant, still terrible: "No one here is guilty of anything yet. Let's keep it that way."

Then boots on the grate above, and she stopped listening and started running.

* * *

The tunnels under Vhalen were older than Vhalen. Everyone said so, and no one could prove it, because the maps that showed them had been burned in the first year of the Edict along with everything else that remembered too much.

Lena followed water. That was the whole of her plan: follow water downhill, stay ahead of the sound of boots, and pray — silently, guiltily, because praying was also illegal now, hadn't she heard that read aloud in the palace yard last spring? — that the water went somewhere the hunters didn't.

Her grandmother would have known these tunnels. The thought arrived uninvited, the way thoughts of the dead did. Her grandmother had grown up in the Salt Quarter before the purges, back when the shrines still had names instead of rubble. She'd taught Lena hand-signs for blessings and then made her swear never to use them. *Blessed things get found,* she used to say. *Found things get burned.*

Lena's feet found a junction where three channels met, and the water here ran clear — clearer than any sewer had a right to run — and cold enough that she felt it through her boots. She stopped, breathing hard, and that was when she saw the light.

It came from below. A soft gold glow, rising up the channel like warmth rising off bread, and with it a smell she hadn't encountered since she was seven years old: incense. Real incense. Ash-of-cedar and salt, the kind the Edict had outlawed by name, listed right there in the second clause between *graven images* and *unlicensed prayer*.

Every sane part of her said *run*. But she had spent five years tasting poison for a living, and five years of that teaches you one thing above all others: the dangerous thing and the true thing are usually the same thing.

She climbed down.

* * *

The cistern opened beneath her like a held breath.

It was vast — a vaulted hall of black stone, older than any architecture she'd seen above ground, its pillars furred with pale mineral bloom. Water lay ankle-deep across the floor, perfectly still, perfectly clear, and in it the gold light reflected upward until the whole ceiling shimmered like the underside of a lit sea.

And there were candles. Hundreds of them, stubs and ends, crowded onto every ledge and pillar base, all burning without smoke. Someone had been collecting candle-ends for years. Someone had been keeping a light going down here in the dark for longer than Lena had been alive.

"You're bleeding," said a voice.

Lena spun, water slopping around her ankles. Between two pillars stood a woman — tall, grey-streaked hair cut short and practical, wearing a robe that had once been fine and was now patched with what looked suspiciously like sailcloth. She held a knife the way kitchen women hold knives: not for show.

"Your hands," the woman said. "From the grate. You're dripping on consecrated floor."

"It's a sewer."

"It was a basilica," the woman said. "Before it was a cistern. Before that, it was a shrine to Paloma, who carried messages between gods and kings, and whose name you will not find written anywhere in this city anymore, because we were thorough when they erased us." She tilted her head. "You came down following the water. They always do. The water remembers the way even when the people forget."

"I'm not— I sell charms. Knots. Luck-threads. Nothing—"

"I know what you sell." The woman didn't move. "I've watched the Nightmarket for eleven years. You're the one who charges dockworkers half price and tells the young ones their charms won't stop a knife, only soften the luck around it. You're careful. Careful sellers survive." Her eyes narrowed. "So why did you come down tonight?"

"Hunters."

"Hunters come every month. You've never come down before." The woman stepped closer, and the candlelight caught her face — lined, severe, with the particular patience of someone who has waited a very long time. "Something changed tonight. Something answered you."

Lena's mouth went dry. "I don't know what you mean."

"Your hands are shaking."

"My hands are *cold*."

"They're shaking," the woman repeated, "and you followed holy water down into the dark toward a fire you should have run from, and you're standing in the shrine of a dead god as though you were expected." She sheathed the knife, abruptly, as if some question had been answered. "Take off your left glove."

"I don't wear—" Lena looked down. She was wearing her left glove. She wore it every day at court, under the excuse of a healing burn, and every night at the market she forgot to take it off, and she had told herself for five years that this meant nothing.

"How did you know—"

"Take it off. Or leave, and take whatever happens next with you."

The candles flickered, all together, though there was no draft. Lena told herself it was a draft anyway. Then she peeled the glove off, finger by finger, and held her bare hand up in the gold light.

Across her palm, faint as a watermark, ran a mark she had never shown anyone: a thin silver line, curving from the heel of her thumb to the base of her middle finger, like a thread being drawn through skin. It had been there since birth. Her grandmother had kissed it once, wept once, and never spoken of it again.

The woman went very still.

"That's a vessel-line," she said quietly. "That's a *taster's line*. Full and unbroken." She looked up, and for the first time her severity cracked into something almost like fear. "Who was your grandmother?"

"Lena Vasquez was my mother. My grandmother was Sefa Vasquez. Of the Salt Quarter."

"Sefa." The woman said the name the way you say the name of someone at a funeral. "Sefa Vasquez kept the last shrine of Paloma open for nine years after the purges began. She fed the poor from the temple stores until the stores were confiscated. She was supposed to be executed." A pause. "She wasn't. Do you know why?"

"No."

"Because a taster's blessing can't be burned out. It lives in the blood, and blood goes where it goes." The woman pressed her own palm briefly against her forehead — an old gesture, a priest's gesture, done so quickly it might have been habit rather than faith. "My name is Odile. I was third voice of the Order of the Drowned Bell, before the order stopped existing. And you, Lena Vasquez, are standing in my church bleeding blessed blood on the floor, and I have been waiting eleven years for someone like you to fall down here."

"I'm not falling anywhere. I'm hiding."

"Those are often the same thing." Odile turned and walked deeper into the cistern, and after a moment — hating herself, hating the tunnel, hating the hunters and the crown and the dead grandmother whose blood had apparently signed her up for something — Lena followed.

* * *

Odile's sanctuary was a corner of the cistern screened off with hanging sailcloth: a cot, a stove, shelves of salvaged books with their spines scraped blank, and a basin of the clear water that Lena now understood was not cistern water at all but something older, fed by a spring that had never learned the Edict existed.

Odile poured two cups of something hot and bitter — chicory, mostly, and a whisper of consecrated salt that made Lena's tongue hum against her teeth the way the broth had hummed, that morning, in the king's kitchen.

"The broth tasted back," Lena said. She hadn't planned to say it. It simply fell out of her, the way confessions do when someone else speaks first. "This morning. At the palace. I taste for the king — officially I detect poison, unofficially I'm very good at my job — and this morning the soup showed me my grandmother praying at a drowned shrine. Underwater. Candles burning underwater."

Odile set down her cup. "Which shrine?"

"I don't know. Stone, drowned, carved with doves."

The silence that followed was long enough that Lena counted her own heartbeats in it. Eleven, twelve, thirteen.

"There are four drowned shrines in Vhalen's history," Odile said at last. "Three were filled in during the reign of Aldric's grandfather. One was never found." She looked at Lena with an expression that was no longer severity at all — it was hunger, the reverent kind, the dangerous kind. "The soup didn't show you a memory, child. It showed you a *door*. The gods are waking, and something in your blood is a key, and you came down my ladder the same night it started turning."

"I came down your ladder because of *witch-hunters*."

"Yes. Both things can be true." Odile leaned forward. "Listen to me carefully, because I will only be this honest once. I stopped praying twenty years ago. I watched my order burn and I decided the gods were a debt that cost more than they paid. I am not asking you to believe anything. I am telling you what I know, and what I know is this: the blessing in your line was never chemistry. Royal tasters were vessels. Intermediaries. The rite that crowned kings required a tongue that could carry words between the throne and the divine without being poisoned by either. Your family held that office for nine generations. Then the Edict came, and the office was renamed 'food-taster,' and the blessing was left to fade." Her eyes dropped to Lena's bare palm. "It hasn't faded."

"And if it hasn't?"

"Then you are the last unbroken vessel in Vhalen, and the gods waking beneath this city will find you whether you want them to or not." Odile sat back. "I would very much prefer you wanted to."

Above them, faint through forty feet of stone, came the tolling of the palace bells — the signal that the raid was over, the quarter sealed, the night's arrests tallied. Somewhere up there, a ledger with her mark in it was probably already traveling toward Captain Sorrel's desk.

Lena looked at her bare hand, at the silver thread running through it, at the water in the basin that had begun — very slightly, very gently — to glow.

"I want to go back to selling knots," she said. "I want tomorrow to be a normal day."

"I know," Odile said, not unkindly. "Drink your chicory."

Lena drank. The salt sang on her tongue, and far away, deep under the city, something that remembered her grandmother's voice turned over in its sleep — and, hearing hers, began to wake.

* * *

She slept badly on Odile's spare blanket, in a niche between pillars that had probably held saints once, and woke to the sound of Odile arguing with someone.

Not aloud. That was the worst part. Odile stood at the basin with her sleeves pushed back and her jaw tight, and the water glowed and dimmed, glowed and dimmed, like slow breathing, and Odile's lips moved without sound, and whatever she was saying, she was saying it to something that answered in light.

Lena sat up. "Who are you talking to?"

Odile didn't startle. She'd known Lena was awake; probably she'd known before Lena did. "Paloma," she said. "Or what's left of her. Or what's growing back. The distinctions matter less every day." She wiped her hands on her robe. "She wants to see you."

"No."

"She hasn't asked me for anything in eleven years."

"*No.*" Lena stood, and her voice cracked on it, and she hated that. "Yesterday I sold a man a luck-thread and today he's dead — did you hear that too, down here in your glowing water? Tam. Dockworker. His wish came true so hard it killed him. These gods of yours aren't gentle, priestess. They're starving animals waking up in a house full of people, and I am not walking into the den because the animal asked nicely."

Odile was quiet for a moment. "You loved him?"

"He was a customer."

"You loved him," Odile said again, gently, and Lena turned away because there was no answer that wasn't yes.

"The gods killed him," Lena said to the pillar in front of her. "Or the answering did. Either way. And now you want to walk me deeper toward the thing that did it."

"I want to walk you toward the truth, which is the only thing that has ever protected anyone." Odile crossed the water to stand beside her. "But I'll tell you a secret, since we're being reluctant allies. I don't trust them either. I fear them more than I love them — I have for twenty years. So hear my offer, and judge it fairly: I will not lead you to the shrine. I'll only tell you where it is. You decide whether to go. That's the whole of my priesthood now — pointing at doors. Whether to open them was always the worshipper's part."

Lena stared at the basin. The water had gone still and clear and innocent, glowing faintly at the bottom like a coin at the bottom of a well.

"And if I say no?"

"Then you hide here until the hunt cools, and I never mention it again, and we both grow old in the dark being careful." Odile's mouth twisted. "Careful sellers survive. Isn't that what you believe?"

It was. It had been the whole architecture of her life: charge fairly, promise little, wear the glove, never be found. Five years of tasting poison for a dead-eyed king, five years of selling small protections at small prices, all of it built on one principle — *found things get burned*.

But the broth had tasted her back. The ledger with her mark was on its way to Sorrel's desk. And a dead dockworker's smile was waiting behind her eyelids every time she blinked.

"Where," Lena said, "is the shrine?"

Odile told her. It was worse than she'd feared: not under the city at all, but out past the harbor bar, in the drowned chapel off Gull Point, reachable only at low tide, exposed to the open water and the open sky and every eye the crown kept on the coast.

"At low tide tomorrow," Odile said. "There's a fisherman who owes me. He won't ask questions. He'll just be frightened, which is the correct amount." She paused. "You could still say no."

Lena flexed her bare hand. The silver line caught the candlelight and held it, a thread being drawn through her skin toward something she couldn't see yet.

"No," she said. "I mean — yes. I mean I'll go." She laughed, short and joyless. "Five years I've kept my head down and my gloves on, and in two days I've got a hunter's ledger with my name on it and a dead god sending me invitations through soup. Say I go out to this chapel. Say I kneel at this drowned altar. What exactly do you think happens then?"

Odile picked up her cup and looked into it for a long moment.

"I think," she said, "that the gods are waking all over this city, Lena Vasquez, and most of them are waking angry, and hungry, and unfiltered, and the prayers they answer are coming true sideways. I think something wears the king like a coat, though I don't know what, and I think it has noticed the same stirring we have. And I think that if the waking gods find a vessel — a true one, unbroken, of the old line — before the crown does..." She looked up. "Then maybe what answers us answers *gently*. Maybe there's a hand on the leash. Or maybe," and here her voice dropped, honest and bleak, "maybe you walk into that water and something old looks at you and sees nine generations of service and decides you belong to it. I genuinely don't know. That's the truth. That's everything I have."

"Wonderful," Lena said. "Very reassuring."

"I told you. Reluctant allies."

Outside the cistern, far above, the city was waking to its ordinary morning — ovens lit, barges loaded, the palace kitchens beginning the breakfast that Lena would not be there to taste. For the first time in five years, she would miss a day. The absence of it opened in her chest like a missing tooth.

"One condition," Lena said.

"Name it."

"We do this my way until it stops working. I plan, I prepare, I bring contingencies. I've survived the deadliest kitchen in Vhalen by never trusting anything I couldn't test. If I go to this shrine, I go with salt in my pocket, a blade in my boot, and a story ready for the fisherman. And you—" she pointed at Odile, "—you tell me everything. Every rite, every risk, every word of the old language you remember. No more pointing at doors and letting me walk through blind. If I'm the last vessel in Vhalen, then I'm not walking into anything without knowing what's on the other side."

For the first time since Lena had fallen down her ladder, Odile smiled. It transformed her face — took ten years off it and put twenty back on, all at once, so that she looked briefly like a woman who had once believed in something enough to laugh about it.

"Nine generations of tasters," she said, "and every single one of them a control-freak. It's in the blood." She extended her hand. "Agreed. Everything I know. Tested, planned, prepared."

Lena took it. The priestess's grip was dry and strong, and where their palms touched, Lena's silver line warmed — just slightly, just once — like a word being passed from one hand to another.

Neither of them mentioned it. Both of them felt it.

"Now," Odile said briskly, turning to her shelves of scraped books, "we have until low tide, which gives us perhaps fourteen hours, and I have eleven years of confiscated theology to teach you, and you—" she glanced back over her shoulder, eyes bright and grim, "—you need to learn the difference between a blessing and a summons before something in this city mistakes you for the latter. First lesson. Sit."

Lena sat. Above them, the bells of Vhalen rang the hour, and beneath them, in the drowned dark, something patient turned another page.
