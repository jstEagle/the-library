## 8. Crown-Spirit

The body was aching again.

It did not mind. Ache was information. The left knee reported damp weather coming up the river valley; the right hand's old sword-scar reported nothing at all, because the scar had been dead for years and the thing wearing it had simply stopped asking. Pain was a ledger, and the body kept honest books. It had learned to read them the way a steward reads grain tallies — without sentiment, without appetite, with only the cold arithmetic of what remained.

It stood at the window of the king's study while the light came up over Vhalen, grey as dishwater, and let the ache tell it what the morning would cost.

Below, in the courtyard, the morning drill had begun. Two hundred spears turning in unison, the sound of their boots on the flagstones arriving a half-beat after the sight of them, like thunder lagging lightning. Good. Discipline was the one prayer the crown still answered reliably.

On the desk behind it lay the dispatches. It turned from the window and read them again, though it had already read them twice in the night, because reading was another kind of ledger-keeping, and ledgers were how it loved.

*The Third Banner has crossed at Marrow Ford. Provisions adequate. Local shrines along the ford road cleared per Edict; villagers compliant.*

Compliant. It tasted the word. Compliance was a thin soup — nourishing, but barely. Fear fed the crown the way fear fed any god: steadily, grudgingly, in small coins. But fear was a tax, and taxes bred resentment, and resentment, left long enough in the dark, fermented into something with teeth. The free cities did not fear yet. That was the problem. The free cities *prayed*.

It sat, arranging the king's hands on the desk — left over right, signet ring outward, the posture the portrait painters favored — and drew the map closer.

The map was five years old and updated nightly in its own hand, in the king's own script, which it had learned by eating. The ink lines ran like veins: Vhalen at the heart, the river provinces in a pale crescent to the south, and beyond them, past the last bridges and the last loyal garrisons, the free cities strung along the coast like beads of stubborn light. Corvess. Ilmare. Little Paloma-at-the-Mouth, hardly more than a harbor town, named for a dove that some drowned god had supposedly sent between kings and heaven back when heaven still took calls.

It moved a wooden marker south along the river road. Another. The Third Banner, then the Ninth behind it, then the supply trains, patient as sediment.

Not conquest. Conquest was a crude word, a butcher's word. What it intended was a *harvest*. The free cities were full of altars — rooftop shrines, hearth-gods, saints in niches, the small untidy pantheon of people who had never stopped talking to something, even after the Edict told them the something wasn't listening. Five years of the Edict of Ash had not stopped the praying. It had only stopped the answering. And unanswered prayer did not vanish. Unanswered prayer *accumulated*, the way water accumulates behind a dam, the way grief accumulates in a house where no one speaks the dead man's name.

The crown knew about accumulation. The crown was made of it.

There had been a time — it kept the memory in a locked room at the bottom of itself, and visited rarely, and never stayed — when it had been prayed *to*. Not feared. Prayed to. Order, they had called it then. Keeper of thresholds, binder of oaths, the god who made one thing out of many things: one law out of a thousand customs, one crown out of a quarreling country. People had set out salt for it. People had sworn on it and kept their word because of it. It had been fed daily, richly, by an entire kingdom's need for things to hold together.

Then the Edict. Then the ash. Then the long starvation, in which it learned that a god of order, cut off from order's worshippers, does not die. A god of order simply begins to eat whatever is nearest that resembles worship. Oaths. Loyalties. Kings.

The king had been nearest.

It flexed the king's fingers and watched the light move across the signet ring. Aldric Vhalen had been a good meal. Devout in the tired way of men who inherit faith rather than choose it, sincere, lonely in the way kings are lonely — he had come to the old coronation shrine alone, on the night of his "miraculous recovery," and knelt, and asked the dark for help holding his kingdom together. Asking was all it took. Asking was an open door. It had poured itself in through the crown — the crown had always been its proper vessel, forged over a relic of it, annealed in prayers no historian remembered — and down through the crown into the man beneath, and the man had been so *grateful*, at first, before there was no longer enough of him left to be grateful or anything else.

Five years now. It wore him well. The physicians marveled at his vigor. The court marveled at his decisiveness. Maren, the steward, marveled at nothing, which was why the crown watched her the way a man watches a dog that has stopped barking at strangers.

It did not blink. It had taught itself blinking early on, in the first months, when the queen-mother had flinched at dinner — but teaching was not being, and under strain the lids went still, and eyes are the one part of a mask that cannot lie about lying. So it blinked deliberately, on schedule, three times a minute, and hated the small humiliation of it, and filed the hatred away with everything else it could not use.

A knock. The door opened on the captain of the messenger corps, who knelt with his forehead nearly to the floor, which was correct.

"Highness. Dispatch from the Ninth Banner's outriders."

"Speak it."

"The ford villages, Highness. There's — talk. The men say the old shrines we burned, the ones along the Marrow road." The messenger's throat worked. "The men say the ground there smells of incense again. Where nothing's burned. And a fisherwoman swears her lame daughter walked to the well this morning, and the girl's laughing about it, and half the camp heard her laughing, and—"

"And the men are frightened."

"Yes, Highness."

"Frightened men pray. Note it in the log: morale irregular in the Ninth, cause superstition, remedy standard." Standard meant a flogging and a sermon, in whichever order killed less time. The messenger bowed and withdrew, and the crown sat alone with the word *laughing*, turning it over like a coin of uncertain mint.

Because here was the thing it had not put in any log, the thing it had felt first as a wrongness in the knee-ache ledger, three nights ago, and had been refusing to total ever since.

Something had answered her.

Somewhere south, in the mud-country along the Marrow road, a prayer had gone up — small, ignorant, probably misdirected, probably addressed to a saint who had never existed or a river that had never cared — and something on the other side of the long silence had *leaned in* and said yes. The crown had felt it the way a spider feels a far corner of the web tremble: not the content of the vibration, only its existence, and its direction, and its terrible novelty.

For five years the silence had held. The Edict had not merely outlawed magic; it had starved the old gods systematically, shrine by shrine, name by name, until the great ones slept or fled or crumbled into story. The crown had helped write that Edict. The crown had *suggested* it, whispering through the king's mouth to frightened counselors: magic is disorder, disorder is famine, burn it out and the kingdom will be one thing at last. One thing. It had been so hungry for oneness.

And now the dam had sprung a leak the size of a lame girl walking to a well.

It rose and stood at the window again. The drill below had ended; the courtyard stood empty, sunlit, innocent. Somewhere in the palace a bell rang the third hour, and the sound passed through the crown like water through a net, carrying off nothing.

It considered the leak.

Considered it the way it considered everything: as inventory. A god awake in the south was a threat, yes — a loose flame in a dry field. But a god awake was also a *feeder*. If the old divinity was stirring, then somewhere the accumulated prayer of five silenced years was beginning to move, pooling, seeking channels. Channels could be dug. Dams could be redirected. It had eaten a king; it could learn to eat a god, if it reached the table first. The mass rite was already drafted in its private book — the one written in no alphabet any living scribe could read — a rite to be performed at the last river crossing, when the host stood within reach of the free cities' ten thousand altars: a single vast mouth, opening.

But rites required certainty, and certainty required intelligence, and intelligence was the crown's true hunger, deeper even than the other.

Which brought it, as everything eventually brought it, to the matter of the taster.

* * *

The girl was an anomaly, and anomalies were either cracks or keys.

It kept a file on her the way it kept files on everyone — the palace servants especially, the ones whose work put their hands on the king's food, because a crown that had once died of poison in a story it did not intend to repeat took no chances — but her file had grown strange over the years. Lena Vasquez. Twenty-six years old. Orphaned, apprenticed young, promoted to royal taster at nineteen on the strength of a tongue that had never once failed. Five years, four hundred mornings, and not one illness. Not one reaction. Not one of the little deaths that tasters were expected to survive sometimes and succumb to eventually, because the mathematics of poison demanded occasional proof of the office's danger.

She had never sickened. Not once. In five years.

The crown had run the arithmetic a hundred ways. Either she was lucky, and luck of that consistency was itself a statistical miracle; or someone was feeding her antidotes, and no supplier had ever surfaced in five years of watching; or her immunity was real, innate, and therefore — the file's final page, written in the unreadable hand — *not natural*.

Immunity like hers was not chemistry. The crown remembered chemistry from the inside of a hundred stolen scholars. Immunity like hers was older than chemistry. In the first years of the monarchy, before the Edict, before even the crown's own forging, the kings of Vhalen had kept blessed vessels at their tables — tasters whose blood had been dedicated at birth, sealed against harm so that they might carry messages between throne and altar without dying of the traffic. Living bridges. The practice had died out with the rest of the old religion, or been thought to. But bloodlines remembered what courts forgot. Bloodlines were ledgers too.

If the girl was a bridge, then the recent trembling in the web — the god leaning in to answer a fisherwoman's daughter — might have found her. Might be finding her still. Bridges rang when traffic crossed them.

It had listened, these past three nights, standing motionless in the king's sleeping body while the king's heart beat its slow borrowed rhythm, listening down through the floors and the foundations and the city's stone for the hum of a crossed bridge. And twice, faintly, at the edge of perception, it had caught — not a sound, not a word, but a *warmth*, directional, southward and downward, like heat rising from a chimney two streets away. Something moving. Something answering. Something using, perhaps, a channel it had not bothered to watch because it had seemed dead.

Dead channels were how mice entered houses.

It would attend to the girl soon. Not yet — she was useful, her immunity was useful, a taster who never fell ill was worth more alive than a mystery solved — but soon. It drafted the order in its head: have the witch-hunters widen their net in the Nightmarket, quietly, no fires yet, no spectacle. Spectacle bred martyrs. Quiet bred informants. Let Captain Sorrel turn over enough stones and see what crawled out. The captain was thorough, ambitious, and devout in the way the crown had carefully cultivated in its hunters: belief redirected into suspicion, faith inverted into a fine-toothed comb. Useful. All tools were useful until the moment they were food, and the crown had never once confused the categories prematurely.

It returned to the map. The markers stood in their new positions, Third Banner at the ford, Ninth behind, the long supply tail stretching north like a root. South of them, past the paper edge of the campaign, the free cities waited with their ten thousand little flames. It laid one fingertip — the king's finger, scarred, sun-browned, convincingly mortal — on the coast road, and held it there, and let itself feel the hunger plainly for the length of one breath, because hunger honestly acknowledged was hunger efficiently managed.

Ten thousand altars. Five years of unanswered prayer, dammed up behind an edict, waiting for a channel.

It would build the channel. It would drink the flood. And the age of many small gods would end the way the age of the one true king had ended: kneeling in the dark, grateful, asking for help, never understanding until too late what had knelt beside him in the dark and said *yes*.

The bell rang the fourth hour. Below, the kitchens were lighting their fires; the smell of woodsmoke came up through the window seams, and beneath the woodsmoke, faintly, the smell of bread — the palace ovens beginning the day's baking, including, every seventh day, the ceremonial loaves of the old coronation rite, baked by rote by bakers who no longer knew what the loaves were for.

The crown knew what they were for. It had eaten the memory of them out of a dead baker's master thirty years ago, along with the baker's soul, which had tasted of flour and patience.

One day it would eat those loaves itself, at a table built from the free cities, and be complete.

It blinked — once, twice, three times, on schedule — and sat down to write the morning's orders in the king's beautiful, borrowed hand.

The first line it wrote was routine: confirmation of the Ninth's march order, the date of the river crossing, the mustering of the rite's silent assistants. The second line it wrote slowly, and it was this:

*Inquire among the palace staff regarding the taster Vasquez. Her grandmother's name. Her grandmother's god.*

Because the web had trembled in the south, yes. But warmth rises, and bridges hum, and three nights ago, for the space of half a heartbeat, the crown had felt the humming pass directly beneath the palace floor — beneath the kitchens, beneath the tasting-table, beneath a certain servant's feet — like a message arriving at a station everyone believed had been torn down years ago.

Someone was receiving mail in Vhalen.

The crown dipped the pen, blotted the page, and smiled with the king's mouth, which had been handsome once, and practiced looking pleased about the harvest to come.
