## 9. Immunity Is a Kind of Love

The cistern had been a temple once. Odile could still see it in the bones of the place — the way the pillars fell into ranks like a congregation, the way the water lapped at a plinth that had been an altar before some clerk with a chisel had made it a reservoir. The Edict of Ash had done to Vhalen's holy places what rot did to fruit: left the shape intact and hollowed everything sweet out of it.

She had lived down here eleven years. She knew every drip by name.

The girl was asleep on her cot — no, not asleep. Odile watched from the shadow of the broken aqueduct arch and corrected herself. Lena Vasquez lay with her eyes open, staring at the ceiling where the lamplight swam, and her breathing was too even. That was a woman pretending to sleep because she didn't know what else to do with her hands.

Odile understood. She'd spent twenty years not knowing what to do with her hands. Prayer had been the answer once; you folded them and they had somewhere to be.

She crossed the wet stone and sat on the stool across from the cot, close enough that the girl would feel watched when she opened her eyes. She let her. It was kinder than speaking first. Speaking first meant questions, and questions meant answers, and answers meant the thing Odile had been circling since the moment she'd taken the girl's wrist in the dark and felt the blessing hum in her blood like a struck bell.

Lena's eyes opened. Steady, immediately. A taster's eyes. You learned not to flinch when you woke up in strange places, or you died of the flinching.

"You're not going to ask if I slept well," Lena said.

"I'm going to tell you why you're alive," Odile said. "And then you can decide whether you slept well."

* * *

She lit the good lamp — the one with the whale oil she hoarded like a dragon — and set it between them so their shadows leaned together against the far wall. Then she went to the niche behind the third pillar, where she kept the only thing she had saved from the purge: a book no bigger than her two palms, its cover gone soft as cloth from handling.

It wasn't a forbidden book. That was the joke of it, the bitter joke she'd laughed at alone for two decades. It was a service manual. *Offices of the Tasting*, hand-copied, intended for the instruction of palace staff. The Edict's censors had burned theology and spared housekeeping, and so the last true account of the old coronation rite survived in a volume shelved between instructions for candying violets and the proper rotation of cellar stores.

"Read this aloud," Odile said, and put it in Lena's lap.

Lena turned pages with a taster's care, fingertips at the edges, the way you handled anything that might be dosed. Her lips moved. Then stopped moving. Then she read aloud, slowly, the way people read when they've found their own name in a stranger's sentence:

"*The vessel who tastes for the crown shall be sealed against all harm of cup and bowl, that she may carry the word of the gods to the king's ear unpoisoned, and the king's word back again, undefiled.*"

Her voice caught on *sealed*. Odile heard it catch. She had been waiting twenty years to hear someone's voice catch on that word.

"What does that mean?" Lena asked. "Sealed how? Sealed by whom?"

"By whom is the easy part." Odile took the book back gently, found the page she wanted — she could find it blind, had found it blind a thousand nights down here, running her thumb over the ink like a rosary. "There were nine orders under the old rite. Nine offices. You know them all by their rotten descendants: the salt-wardens became customs men, the bread-bearers became bakers' guild masters, the cup-bearers became — well. But the tasting office, the *gustatores*, they weren't poison-catchers originally. Poison-catching was a side effect. A courtesy."

"A courtesy." Lena said it flatly, the way she said things when she was deciding whether to be insulted.

"The taster was a messenger," Odile said. "That's all. That's the whole of it. In the old days — and I mean the real old days, before the Edict, before your grandmother's grandmother — the gods didn't speak to kings directly. Kings were too loud. Too armored. A crown is a kind of deafness; you put it on and everything below it goes quiet. So the gods spoke to vessels instead. People with clean channels. And the office they built for it was the tasting, because eating and drinking are the oldest prayers there are. You take something of the world into your body and trust it. Every meal is a small act of faith, if you do it honestly."

"And the immunity?"

"The seal." Odile tapped the page. "A vessel carrying a message couldn't afford to die of a bad oyster halfway through delivering it. So the god of the threshold — Paloma's order, the dove-order, the go-betweens — marked the tasters. Marked their whole line. Nothing of cup or bowl could harm them. Not arsenic, not henbane, not the hundred quiet deaths a court invents. The body was made untouchable so the voice could stay open."

The lamp hissed. Somewhere above them, muffled through forty feet of stone and city, Vhalen went about its morning: cart wheels, a fishmonger's cry, the bells of the usurper's chapel ringing the hour with their new, tinny enthusiasm.

Lena was very still. Odile had seen stillness like that before, in the years before the purge, in novices who had just understood something too large. It was the stillness of a person holding a full cup and realizing that any movement will spill it.

"My grandmother," Lena said. "Sera Vasquez. She was a taster before me. And her mother before her."

"Three generations," Odile said. "Maybe more. The seal runs in blood but it thins if it isn't used. Like a path nobody walks. Your family kept walking it."

"They thought they were just... tough. Resistant. My grandmother used to say our stomachs were made of ship's hull." Lena's mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "She prayed, though. At night. I used to hear her. I thought she was just old."

"She wasn't just old. She was keeping the channel warm." Odile closed the book. "And five days ago, when the gods started answering again — whose prayer do you think reached the drowned shrine first? Whose line was already walking the path?"

* * *

The girl got up then. Odile let her. Lena walked to the edge of the black water and stood looking at her own reflection the way you look at a document you suspect has been forged.

"So all those years," she said to the water. "All those mornings I knelt at the king's table and swallowed death for wages. It wasn't a job. It was — what? An inheritance I never agreed to?"

"Yes."

"That's not an answer that helps."

"No," Odile admitted. "It isn't. But it's the true one, and I've found the true ones age better."

Lena turned around. In the lamplight her face had the look Odile remembered from mirrors, long ago, in another life: fury and grief sharing one chair, each refusing to give it up.

"Then explain the rest of it," Lena said. "Because if I was born to carry messages between gods and kings, someone forgot to deliver the rest of my instructions. Nobody told me. Nobody trained me. I learned charms off a smuggler in the Nightmarket and I sell luck-knots to dockworkers for copper, and the sum total of my sacred office is that I don't die when the kitchen poisons the soup wrong. If there was an office, Odile, someone burned the desk."

"They burned more than the desk." Odile heard her own voice go quiet, and hated it, and let it. This was the part she had rehearsed for twenty years and it still came out of her like a splinter. "Sit down, child. Because this is the part where I confess, and confessions go easier sitting."

Lena sat.

"When the Edict of Ash came down — you know the story they teach. Magic outlawed, gods disestablished, the shrines closed for the public good. What they don't teach is the order it happened in. They didn't start with the gods. They started with the messengers." Odile folded her hands, because her hands needed somewhere to be. "You can't silence a god while her voice still has throats. So the first edicts weren't against worship. They were against *intermediaries*. The priestesses first, yes. But before us — quietly, years before, no bonfires, nothing dramatic — they dissolved the tasting office. Rebranded it. Turned the blessed vessels into hired throats. Took women who had been the living wire between heaven and the throne and made them food servants. Gave them wages instead of ordination. And the families — the lines, the sealed lines — they were scattered on purpose. Married out. Moved between cities. Broken up like a litter of pups, so the blood would thin and the path would grow over."

Lena's face had gone white. Not fear-white. Recognition-white. Odile had seen that too, once, in a mirror.

"How do you know?" Lena whispered. "How do you know all this?"

"Because I was the last woman ordained under the old rite before the doors shut." Odile said it plainly, because dressing it up would be worse. "Order of the Dove. Paloma's own. We kept the registers of the sealed lines. I held your family's page in my hands, Lena Vasquez. Three names, four names, five — a column of tasters going back two hundred years, each one marked *vessel, sealed, in service*. Your great-great-grandmother's name is written in ink I mixed myself, when I was nineteen years old and certain the world would always want what we carried."

The lamp flame bent in a draft from the flooded stairwell and straightened again.

"And then the purge came," Odile went on, "and I ran, and I took the one book I could carry, and I have spent eleven years in a hole under the city telling myself I hid. That's the word I use. *Hid.* It's a kind word. The truth is I abandoned the register, and the register was the map of every sealed line in Vhalen, and without it the lines scattered blind, and the gods went unanswered, and I let it happen because staying meant dying and I discovered — late, uselessly — that I loved being alive more than I loved the work."

She hadn't meant to say that last part. It came out anyway. Twenty years of silence will do that; the words get pressurized.

Lena was watching her with the expression Odile dreaded most, which was not contempt. It was the taster's expression — the measuring one, the one that weighed a spoonful and named what was in it.

"You're afraid of them," Lena said. "The gods. Even now. Even after they started answering."

"Yes."

"But you stayed near the shrine. You kept the book. You memorized the rite."

"Yes."

"Why?"

Odile looked at the water for a long time. The honest answer was ugly and she gave it anyway, because the girl had earned it by bleeding at a king's table for five years.

"Because love doesn't care whether you're brave," she said. "I stopped praying twenty years ago. I have not stopped loving them for one day of it. Do you understand the difference? Fear is about what they might do. Love is about what they *are*. And what they are is the voice that used to answer my grandmother's grandmother when she asked whether the harvest would hold, and the warmth in the bread at offering-time, and the — " her voice cracked, and she let it, one crack, like a bell admitting it had been struck — "and the feeling, when I was a novice, of being *known*. Completely known, and not found wanting. There is nothing in this world like it. The Edict took it away and called the taking safety, and I have been starving politely ever since."

Silence, then. The good kind. The kind where two people are both looking at the same wound.

* * *

It was Lena who broke it, and she broke it practically, which Odile was beginning to understand was simply how the girl was built.

"The seal," Lena said. "If it's real — if it's really a blessing and not just my stomach — then it can't be the only piece. A messenger needs more than immunity. She needs the message. The words. The counterwords, whatever they are."

"There's a rite," Odile said. "The old coronation rite. It was never just a party with a hat. It was a binding — the whole apparatus, salt and bread and blood and name, the four anchors. It bound the crown to the kingdom and the kingdom to the gods, with the tasters as the wire running through the middle of it. Done properly, no spirit could wear the crown that wasn't the rightful king. Done properly, what sits on that throne upstairs *couldn't exist*."

"Done improperly?"

"Improperly," Odile said, "you get a door with no lock. And something has clearly walked through it."

Lena absorbed that. Odile watched her absorb it — watched the taster's mind do what tasters' minds did, break the problem into doses, weigh each one.

"The relics," Lena said. "The anchors. Salt, bread, blood, name. Do they still exist?"

"Some. Scattered. Consecrated salt keeps, if it's kept dry — there was a chapel on the riverfront, before the flooding took the lower district. The coronation bread is baked fresh for each rite, but the recipe and the oven both belong to the palace, which means they're behind walls you already have keys to." Odile allowed herself the ghost of a smile. "You see why the gods waited for you specifically. Five years inside the palace. A sealed tongue. Access to the ovens. If I'd tried to write this as a prophecy, the other priestesses would have called it too neat."

"And the blood." Lena said it evenly, but Odile saw her hand close, once, on her own wrist — the same wrist Odile had gripped in the dark, the wrist where the blessing lived. "Royal blood. The rite wants royal blood, doesn't it. That's how these things always work."

"It does," Odile said. "And the only royal blood left in Vhalen is either dead, worn, or — " she met the girl's eyes — "five years steeped in royal service, sealed by the oldest blessing in the kingdom. Blood is a language, Lena. Yours has been learning to speak court for half a decade."

"That's not the same thing."

"No," Odile said. "It's something older. But we'll come to that when we come to it. One anchor at a time."

Lena stood. Walked to the water again. Stood there with her back straight and her arms crossed, and Odile recognized the posture from the book's own illustrations — the vessel at the threshold, facing the deep water, waiting to be told what she carried.

"My grandmother prayed every night," Lena said quietly, "to a god who never answered. Twenty years of silence, and she kept praying. And I used to think it was habit. Stubbornness. Old-woman foolishness." She turned. "It was maintenance, wasn't it. She was keeping the line open for me. For whoever came after. She died still paying on a debt she never explained."

"Yes."

"And nobody — not the order, not the priests, not one single person in two hundred years — thought the vessels themselves deserved to be *told*."

"We told them," Odile said, and the grief in it was hers, all of it, twenty years of it. "We told them everything. And then the Edict came, and telling became a hanging offense, and the ones of us who survived chose silence over slaughter and called it mercy. It wasn't mercy. It was theft. We stole your inheritance to save our necks, and we've been calling it wisdom ever since." She rose, joints cracking, and held out the little book. "So take it back. Whatever part of it is yours. Take it back now, while the gods are awake enough to receive it."

Lena took the book. Held it the way you hold a sleeping animal.

Above them, faint through the stone, the palace bells rang the change of watch. And beneath the bells — so faint that Odile might have imagined it, except that the water in the cistern shivered in rings from the center outward, rings with no wind to make them — something answered.

Not words. Not yet. Just attention. The particular pressure of being listened to, which every priestess knows and never forgets, the way you never forget being held.

Lena felt it too. Odile saw it hit her — saw the girl's breath go ragged, saw her grip the book to her chest like a shield, saw her lips move around a word she didn't know she knew.

"*Paloma,*" Lena whispered.

The rings on the water widened, touched the stone, and stilled.

Odile sat back down slowly, because her legs had decided the matter without consulting her. Twenty years of silence, and the first prayer in the room had not been hers. It should have stung. Instead it felt like hearing, through a wall, the sound of your own house being swept clean by someone younger and braver than you.

"The salt first," she said, when her voice worked. "Tomorrow, at low tide. Sleep if you can."

But neither of them slept, and neither pretended to, and far above them — though neither woman knew it yet — a witch-hunter captain was turning a confiscated ledger in his lantern light, reading a list of names written in a hand he almost recognized, and pausing, longer and longer each time, over one name in particular:

*Vasquez.*
