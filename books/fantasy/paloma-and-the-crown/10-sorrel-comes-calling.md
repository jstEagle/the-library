## 10. Sorrel Comes Calling

They came for her on a Tuesday, which was the worst possible day, because Tuesdays the king took breakfast in his private chambers and Lena had already tasted everything and found it clean — bitter plum conserve, black bread, a soft-boiled egg with too much pepper — and she'd been looking forward to the long gray hour afterward when the kitchens went quiet and she could sit by the flour sacks and do nothing at all.

Instead, a page in livery too new to be creased right appeared at the scullery door and said, "The Captain of the Ash Guard requests the taster Vasquez."

Not *summons*. *Requests.* That was worse. Summons you could dread openly. A request meant someone wanted to pretend this was polite.

Lena wiped her hands on her apron, then thought better of it and untied the apron entirely. If she was going to be interrogated, she would not do it smelling of fish stock. She folded the apron over the rail, checked — without moving her head, without letting her eyes move at all — that the little iron key to her footlocker still hung inside her boot where it always hung, and followed the page.

*Don't run*, her grandmother's voice said in her skull, the way it always did when Lena was afraid. *Running tells them what your legs already know.*

* * *

Captain Sorrel had taken over the counting room off the buttery — a small stone chamber with one high window and a table scarred by two hundred years of clerks' knives. He sat behind the table with the ledger open in front of him.

Lena knew the ledger before she saw the mark. She knew it by its cover: oxblood leather gone soft as ear at the spine, corners worn round from years of pockets. She'd sold that ledger to a chandler's apprentice named Wick three winters ago, back when she'd been careless enough to keep records. Prices, customers, marks. A whole criminal career in a merchant's hand, and hers stamped at the top like a brand: the little knot-and-dove she scratched into every charm that left her hands.

Her mouth went dry. Her body, traitor that it was, remembered the taste of every poison she'd ever swallowed and offered each one up as a candidate for what fear actually felt like. Hemlock, she decided. This was closest to hemlock.

"Taster Vasquez." Sorrel didn't stand. He was younger than she expected — everyone said the Captain of the Ash Guard was old iron, but up close he was maybe thirty-five, with a face like a door somebody had locked and then lost the key to. "Sit down."

"I'd rather stand."

"Sit down anyway." He waited until she sat. Then he turned the ledger around so it faced her, and laid one finger on her mark. "Tell me what this is."

"It looks like a laundry tally," Lena said. "We mark linens in the palace the same—"

"Vasquez."

"Captain Sorrel." She let herself meet his eyes. That was the trick of it, the thing five years in the tasting chair had taught her: never flinch first, never look away first, and if you must lie, lie slowly, because fast lies taste like fast poisons and everyone can tell. "That's my mark. I'll grant you that. It's also on about forty napkin rings in the east wing. I have neat handwriting. It's been the ruin of me."

"Where did you get this book?"

"A dead man gave it to me." That part was even true; Wick had died of a fever last winter, poor boy, and she'd bought the ledger back off his mother for six coppers because leaving it out in the world had been gnawing at her ever since. "He worked the docks. He owed me money. When he died his mother sold me his things."

"And what did you buy it *for*?"

"For the paper." She shrugged. "Good vellum's dear."

Something moved behind Sorrel's eyes — not belief, nothing so generous as belief, but the pause of a man who has heard ten thousand lies and is grading this one on a curve. He turned a page. Then another. Lena made herself breathe through her nose, slow, the way Odile had taught her to breathe during the cistern lessons, when the water rose and there was nothing to do but wait.

Odile. The name surfaced in her chest like a body in a flooded well. If they had the ledger, how far behind it were they? How many threads led from Wick's marks through the Nightmarket down under the city, to the disgraced priestess who kept relics in jars and called the blessing in Lena's blood by its true name?

"You've been in palace service five years," Sorrel said. "Tasting since the year of the king's recovery. Your record is unblemished. You've caught four attempts on the royal person." He counted them off on blunt fingers. "The arsenic in the coronation wine — which, I note, killed the cupbearer instead. The foxglove in the honey. The ground glass — though anyone could catch ground glass. And the nightshade broth last spring."

"Five," Lena said. "You're forgetting the rat poison in the salt cellar, except that wasn't aimed at the king, that was just a cook with a grudge against the under-steward."

"The rat poison doesn't count."

"It counted for the rat."

For one unguarded half-second, the corner of Sorrel's mouth twitched. Then it was gone, locked away again behind the door of his face. He closed the ledger and put both hands flat on it, the way a priest puts hands on a book he means to swear an oath on.

"There's a woman missing from the Nightmarket raid," he said. "Three weeks ago. We burned eleven stalls and took nine sellers, and the tenth — the one whose stall sat exactly where this ledger says most of these sales happened — wasn't there. Witnesses say a woman got her out. Through the drainage ways." His finger tapped once, twice, on the leather. "Small woman. Quick. Dockside accent. Knows the under-city better than people who live in it."

"That sounds like half the port," Lena said.

"Yes," Sorrel agreed. "It does."

The silence stretched. In the buttery beyond the door, someone was churning butter, the slow wet knock-knock-knock of it, absurdly domestic. Lena thought about the drowned shrine. About the god who spoke in her grandmother's voice. About the word Odile used when she talked about the Edict, spitting it like a seed husk: *ash*. Everything burned to ash, and the ash sifted down over everything else until nobody could tell what had been sacred and what had merely been flammable.

"Here's what troubles me," Sorrel said, and now his voice changed, dropped into something quieter and more dangerous than authority — sincerity. "I've read your file. Five years, no errors, no favors sold, no bribes taken. You catch poison like a hound catches scent. Do you know what we call tasters like you, in the Guard?"

"No."

"We don't," Sorrel said. "Because there aren't any. Good tasters are careful. Careful people miss things — that's what careful means. You don't miss things, Vasquez. You taste arsenic at dilutions our own assayists need three hours and a silver reagent to find." He leaned forward. "I pulled your intake papers. No family listed. Grandmother deceased — a washerwoman, parish of Saint Ferren, drowned chapel district. Interesting neighborhood."

The floor seemed to tilt very slightly, the way a ship tilts. Lena kept her face still by thinking about eggs. Soft-boiled. Too much pepper. Count the seconds. Count the breaths.

"My grandmother washed clothes," she said.

"She prayed, too. There's a parish record — half burned, but half survives. Saint Ferren's kept a list of communicants, back when communicating wasn't a crime." Sorrel opened a folder — an actual folder, wax-sealed, official — and slid a scrap of parchment across the table. It was scorched at one corner. On it, in a clerk's fading hand: *Vasquez, Miren. Devotee, the Drowned Lady. Blessing recorded at font, year of the comet.*

Lena looked at her grandmother's name and felt the room go very far away and very close at the same time.

"Blessings aren't real," she said. "That's the whole point of the Edict. The gods are gone. Everyone knows the gods are gone."

"Do they?" Sorrel asked, and there it was again, that crack in the door of his face — not doubt about her, doubt about *himself*, about the warrant in his pocket and the fires he'd set. He smothered it fast, but she'd seen it. She was, after all, extremely good at detecting small amounts of poison in large quantities of something else. "The Edict says the gods are gone. The Edict has said so for sixty years. But the Edict also pays me a wage to find people who disagree, and lately—" He stopped. Reclosed the door. "Lately there are more of them than there used to be."

* * *

"So here is what happens now," Sorrel said, some minutes later, after Lena had denied knowing anything about the Nightmarket, any drainage ways, any woman, any priestess, with the flat tireless patience of someone reciting a grocery list. "Option one. I arrest you. You go to the Ash Cells under the White Tower. There's an inquisitor there, Brother Cassian, who is very skilled and very patient, and within — I would estimate — nine days, you will confess everything you know and several things you don't. Then you burn. Publicly. They've started doing it publicly again; morale, apparently."

"Cheerful," Lena said. Her heart was slamming against her ribs like a prisoner against a cell door, but her voice came out level, and she clung to that levelness like a rail in floodwater.

"Option two." He pushed the ledger aside and produced a single sheet of paper, blank except for a seal. "You walk out of this room. Nothing changes. You keep tasting the king's breakfast, and you keep your neat little life, and in exchange you bring me things."

"What kind of things?"

"Names. Movements. Who's selling, who's buying, who's praying. If the woman from the Nightmarket contacts you — and I think she will, or already has — you come to me." He tapped the blank paper. "This stays blank as long as you're useful. The moment you're not, I fill it in, and it says everything I already suspect about you, which is quite a lot, and then option one happens with none of the delay."

"And if I say no?"

"Then I admire your spine at your funeral."

Lena sat very still. The butter churn knocked on, patient as tide. She ran it the way she ran a tasting — separate the flavors, identify each one, find the poison. Sorrel was not lying about the cells; men like him didn't bluff, they priced. He was not entirely lying about his doubts either, which was more dangerous, because a man with doubts wants company in them and will lean hard on whoever provides it. And underneath both — she could almost taste it, faint, alkaline, like lye in a broth — was something she hadn't expected in a witch-hunter at all.

He didn't want her to be guilty.

He wanted her to be useful instead. It was the coward's mercy, the bureaucrat's absolution: not innocence, just deferral. And she could work with deferral. Deferral had a rhythm. Deferral could be played the way you played any slow poison — small sips, long intervals, survive until the antidote arrives or the patient adapts.

"If I agree," Lena said slowly, "and I'm not saying I do — what happens the first time I bring you nothing? Because I promise you, Captain, I know nothing worth knowing. I sell luck-threads to dockworkers and I taste soup. That's the whole of my criminal empire."

"Then bring me soup," Sorrel said. "Bring me the kitchen gossip. Bring me who visits whom. You see more of the palace in a morning than my hunters see in a month, and lately—" the crack again, wider this time, and he didn't bother hiding it, which frightened her more than anything else he'd done "—lately there are things in that palace I cannot explain. The king has not aged, Vasquez. Five years, and not a gray hair, not a new line. His physicians' logs stop making medical sense. And three nights ago a guard swears he saw His Majesty standing at his window at the hour of the dead watch, perfectly still, facing out, for four hours. Not sleeping. Not watching. *Still.*"

The words went through Lena like a blade of ice, because she had seen the king not blink. She had stood three feet from him every morning for five years, holding the little golden spoon, and she had told herself the stillness was dignity, was grief, was the weight of the crown. She had lied to herself with the same slow care she lied to Sorrel now.

"What do you think it is?" she asked — quietly, and hated herself for asking, because asking was a door opening, and doors open both ways.

Sorrel held her gaze for a long moment. When he answered, his voice was nearly a whisper, and it was the most honest sound she'd heard in the palace in five years.

"I think I don't want to know," he said. "I think I want to keep not knowing for as long as my wage allows. And I think you know something, taster, because you've gone pale as milk, and good liars don't go pale." He straightened. The door of his face shut. "So. Do we understand each other?"

Lena thought of Odile in the cistern, candlelight on wet stone, saying: *They always offer you the choice between the fire and the favor. The trick is to take the favor and make it into a weapon.*

She thought of the drowned shrine, and the voice like her grandmother's, and the price of being remembered.

She thought of the king at his window, still as a painted man, and of whatever wore him like a glove.

"Understood," she said.

Sorrel slid a slip of paper across the table — an address in the Tanner's Row quarter, nothing written on it but a number. "If you learn anything, leave word with the cooper there. Don't come to the White Tower. Don't write. And Vasquez—" he gathered the ledger, the folder, the burned scrap with her grandmother's name, and stood for the first time, taller than she'd calculated, broader, blocking the high window so that his shadow fell across the whole table "—the Nightmarket woman. The one who got away. When she contacts you — and she will — remember that I know she exists. If I find you've been playing me for a fool, the blank page fills in. If I find you've been honest, even honest about terrible things—" he paused, and something almost human crossed his features and was gone "—then perhaps we're both wrong about what's coming, and neither of us burns."

"Is that a threat or a promise?"

"It's arithmetic," Sorrel said. "Go back to your soup."

* * *

She walked out through the buttery with her spine straight and her knees loose, past the churn and the boys scraping pots, and nobody looked at her, which meant everybody had been told to look at her, and she smiled at the head cook and asked after his daughter's cough because that was what an innocent woman would do, and her voice only cracked a little.

In the scullery passage, alone between the flour sacks where she'd planned to waste her quiet hour, Lena let herself slide down the wall until she was sitting on cold flagstones, and shook.

Furious. That was the feeling under the terror, the one she'd known would be there. Furious at Sorrel for his polite arithmetic. Furious at herself for the ledger, three winters of carelessness she couldn't unsell. Furious at Odile, irrationally, gloriously furious at Odile for being right — *they offer you the fire or the favor* — and for having said it calmly, months ago, in a voice full of candlelight and wet stone.

And underneath the fury, colder than all of it: she had just agreed to spy on the only people in Vhalen who were trying to save it, for the man paid to burn them, while wearing the smile of an innocent woman.

*Take the favor,* Odile had said. *Make it a weapon.*

Fine. Then she would feed Sorrel truth the way she fed the king's plate to the dogs when she needed a morning free — enough to satisfy, never enough to harm. She would give him kitchen gossip and guard rotations and the cook's grudge against the under-steward. And if the day came when Sorrel's hunters marched on the cistern, she would stand in front of him in her taster's grey and lie to him with the best lie she owned:

The truth, carefully portioned.

She got up. Brushed off her skirts. Went and washed her hands, because a taster's hands must always be clean, and stood a moment watching the water spiral down the drain.

Tomorrow she would find Odile. Tonight she had a report to invent.

But first — she pressed her palm flat against her sternum, where the blessing sat like a swallowed coal — first she allowed herself one minute of pure, private, useless rage, fists clenched, jaw tight, silent as the king at his window.

Then the minute ended, because it had to, and Lena Vasquez went back to work.

She was halfway through the afternoon's tasting — a leek pottage, clean, a roast hen, suspiciously delicious but innocent — when the under-steward hurried in with a message that stopped her spoon midair.

A visitor had arrived at the servants' gate. A woman. Small, quick, dockside accent, asking for the taster by name.

And she wouldn't leave.
