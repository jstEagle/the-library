## 12. Letters to a Dead Man

The archive beneath the Counting House smelled of dust and tallow and the particular melancholy of paper that no one would ever read again.

Maren had been coming down here for eleven years, first as an assistant archivist with ink-stained cuffs, then as steward of the royal household, which meant — though no one said it aloud — that she was also steward of everything the household preferred to forget. She knew where the tax ledgers from the Salt War slept. She knew which box held the petitions of the weavers' guild, unanswered for a decade. She knew the exact shelf where the king's private correspondence lived: third row, behind the ceremonial armor inventories nobody consulted, in boxes labeled by year in her own careful hand.

She had not come down here for the letters. Not at first.

She had come down because she could not sleep, and because the ledger she kept under her mattress — the one with the dates, the observations, the small terrible column of facts she told herself was merely administrative diligence — had grown too heavy to lie on top of at night. A record needed corroboration. That was all. A good clerk did not trust memory alone. Memory could be argued with. Paper could not.

So she pulled the box marked *Year Nine* and carried it to the reading table under the one lamp that still burned, and she began at the beginning, because that is what clerks do when they are afraid: they impose order.

* * *

The early letters were dull in the way that only genuinely happy correspondence can be dull.

*To His Grace Duke Ferren of Callowmere, greetings. The orchards at Vhalen have done well this year. The queen sends her regards and a jar of quince preserve, which I am told you will fight your brother over, so guard it.*

*To my cousin Aldous in the southern provinces. The boy has lost his second tooth and is insufferable about it. He insists on showing everyone. Yesterday he made the chamberlain stop mid-audience so he could demonstrate the gap. I have never been prouder of anything in my life.*

Maren read that one twice. She tried to picture the king doing such a thing — halting the business of a kingdom for a child's missing tooth — and found, unsettlingly, that she could. There had been years, early ones, when the court had felt almost warm. When the king had laughed at things. When the queen's death had been a fresh grief rather than ancient history, and he had worn it openly, like a man, instead of not wearing anything at all.

She turned pages. The handwriting changed as the years changed — looser in spring, cramped in winter, once slanting drunkenly across the page after what the letter itself admitted had been "a long night with the ambassador from Osterhagen and his inexhaustible cellar." Human handwriting. Handwriting that got tired.

That was the thing she had not let herself think clearly until now, holding the evidence in both hands: handwriting gets tired. Ink pools where a hand rests too long. Letters lean when the writer is rushing. Over eleven years of archival work, Maren had developed an eye for script the way a physician develops an eye for fever — and the king's hand, in these boxes, lived. It fluctuated. It aged, very slightly, the way all hands age.

She set Year Nine aside and drew out Year Ten.

* * *

The change came in autumn. She noticed it the way you notice a room has gone quiet — not at any single moment, but as an accumulation.

The letters grew shorter. The jokes thinned. Where Year Eight's letters had run two and three pages, Year Ten's ran half a page, then a paragraph, then a few lines. He apologized for brevity, in one, blaming "pressing matters of state," but the pressing matters of state were never specified, and the apology itself was written in a hand that had begun to look — Maren leaned closer to the lamp — *even*. Perfectly even. Every letter the same height. Every loop closed with the same mechanical precision, as if the words had been drawn rather than written.

She told herself it was a new pen. Scribes changed pens; scribes changed styles; men changed after their wives died. She had a whole drawer full of reasonable explanations and she reached into it now, one after another, like a woman counting coins she already knew were clipped.

Then she found the last letter.

It was dated the fourteenth day of the Month of Embers. It was addressed to Aldous, the cousin in the south, the one who received the tooth stories and the quince preserves. And it stopped.

Not ended. *Stopped.* Mid-sentence, mid-thought, the ink trailing off at the end of a line as if the pen had simply been set down:

> *—and I confess I have been tired lately in a way sleep does not touch. The physicians say it is nothing. They say it is grief, finally arriving, five years late, and perhaps they are right. But some nights I sit in this chair after everyone has gone and I think: I am the king, and I am alone in this room, and if I called out, who would—*

Nothing after that. No closing salutation. No signature. The next page in the box was blank, and the page after that, and then the box was empty, because there were no more letters, because there were no more letters ever again, in any year, in any box, anywhere in the archive.

Maren sat very still under the lamp.

She was not a woman who frightened easily. Eleven years in a palace teaches you that most horrors arrive wearing livery and carrying paperwork. But her hands, resting flat on the table on either side of the unfinished letter, had begun — without her permission — to tremble.

Because she knew the date. Of course she knew the date. Every soul in Vhalen knew the date, the way they knew the date of the Great Fire or the founding of the city. The fourteenth of Embers was the night the king *died*.

And the fifteenth was the morning the heralds stood on the palace steps and announced, to a kingdom holding its breath, that the king had *recovered*. Miraculously. Against all expectation. The physicians themselves could not explain it; the fever had broken in a single hour; the gods, it was said, had relented. There had been bells. There had been a service of thanksgiving. Maren remembered standing in the chapel with the rest of the household, singing gratitude through a throat tight with something she had not then known how to name.

Five years ago. Five years ago tonight, nearly.

The letter in front of her was dated the day he died. It broke off mid-sentence — *if I called out, who would* — and no other letter followed, and the man who supposedly recovered and went on ruling for five more years never wrote to his cousin again, never wrote to anyone, never put pen to paper in his own hand at all, because —

Because.

Maren became aware that she was counting. Her lips were moving silently, ticking off the arithmetic she'd been assembling for months in a ledger hidden under her mattress, and now the numbers closed like a door.

The king did not blink. She had watched him across a hundred audiences and he did not blink, or blinked so rarely and so evenly that it seemed scheduled rather than reflexive. The king did not sweat, even in the sweltering month of Embers, even in furs in winter he showed neither flush nor shiver. The king did not age. She had thought, idly, uncharitably, that he aged *well* — but well was the wrong word. He aged *not at all*. The portrait painted in Year Ten and the face above her at last week's audience were the same face, down to the gray at the temples, which had not spread by a single hair in five years.

And the handwriting. She flipped back through the later boxes — Year Eleven, Twelve, Thirteen — looking for anything, a marginal note, a signed decree, a jotting. There were documents bearing the royal seal in abundance. There were proclamations and warrants and gracious replies to foreign dignitaries. All of them in that same perfect, tireless, even hand. Not one of them tired. Not one of them human.

A scribe, she told herself. A very good scribe. Kings employ scribes; it is normal; it is—

But kings *dictate* to scribes, and dictation breathes. You can hear the pauses. And no scribe alive could replicate a dead man's hand so perfectly that Maren herself, comparing the Year Nine letters against the Year Thirteen decrees side by side under the lamp, could find no seam at all.

No seam. As if the same hand had simply continued. As if whatever had picked up the pen on the night of the fourteenth of Embers had been *practicing* for years before that — or had learned the hand the way a thief learns a key, pressed into wax, taken whole.

*If I called out, who would—*

"Who would come," Maren whispered into the dark of the archive. "That's how it ends. Who would come."

She knew how it ended because she had spent thirty years finishing other people's sentences in draft correspondence. It was a clerk's instinct. And the sentence finished itself in her mind with terrible ease, because the answer, for a king, was supposed to be *everyone*. Everyone would come. The whole palace would come running. Unless the man writing it already suspected, on the last night of his life, that no one would. Or unless the sentence had been interrupted not by sleep or interruption or a dropped pen —

— but by the moment the man himself stopped being there to finish it.

* * *

She did not remember climbing the stairs. She found herself standing in her own office with the letter folded inside her coat, over her heart, like contraband, which she supposed it now was. Possessing evidence of regicide — or whatever this was, this word she still could not shape — was treason by any measure the Edict cared to apply. Captain Sorrel's witch-hunters burned people for less than a suspicious ledger. What would they do to a woman caught carrying a dead king's final, unfinished cry?

She should destroy it. That was the sane course. Feed it to the office brazier, tell herself she had misread, go back to managing menus and linen inventories and the endless small machinery of a household that served a thing wearing a crown.

Instead she unlocked the bottom drawer of her desk and took out the private ledger — the real one, the one that lived under her mattress at night and traveled in her sleeve by day — and opened it to the current page, and added a line in her smallest, steadiest hand:

*Correspondence ceases 14th of Embers, Year Ten. Mid-sentence. No further correspondence in the king's own hand thereafter. Date matches the "miraculous recovery." The recovery was not a recovery.*

She looked at the words. Words make things true; that was the first law of her profession, and she had built a life on it. Now the law had turned around and bitten her.

There was a knock at her door, and she flinched hard enough to snap the ledger shut.

"Maren?" It was Tally, the under-steward, a girl of nineteen with flour on her sleeve and no guile anywhere in her. "Beg pardon, but the kitchens are asking about Thursday's banquet — the king's recovery feast, five years, they want to do the whole thing again, roast swan and all. Do you approve the budget?"

The recovery feast. Five years of celebrating the night a man stopped existing, every year, with swan.

"Approve it," Maren heard herself say, in her level steward's voice, while her heart hammered against the folded letter in her coat. "Spare no expense. His majesty does so love an anniversary."

Tally beamed and went. Maren stood alone in her office, listening to the palace breathe around her — the footsteps, the clatter from the kitchens, the distant murmur of the king's courtiers preparing to toast his miraculous life — and understood, with the cold clarity of a completed ledger, three things at once.

First: the king had been dead for five years, and everyone who served him had been serving his grave.

Second: whatever wore him had done it seamlessly, patiently, and with access to everything — every document, every seal, every secret the crown possessed. Including, quite possibly, the archives. Including, quite possibly, a record of who came and went from the Counting House cellars at midnight.

Third: she was not the only one who had started to notice. She had seen the taster girl's eyes at yesterday's audience — quick, frightened, fixed on the king's unblinking face with the look of someone counting the same terrible column. And she had heard, through channels a steward hears everything through, that Captain Sorrel had pulled the Nightmarket files, and that among them was a charm-seller's ledger bearing marks that traced, somehow, back into the palace itself.

The threads were drawing together. Maren had spent her life watching threads; she knew a knot forming when she saw one.

She wrapped the unfinished letter in oilcloth, hid it behind the false spine of a tax ledger from the Salt War, and blew out her lamp.

Outside her window, far off toward the river, the Nightmarket district glowed with its illegal little lights — charmsellers and knot-menders and pray-ers of outlawed prayers, all of them committing small treasons against an Edict that had apparently been protecting something far worse than sorcery all along.

Five years ago tonight, a man had written *who would come* and never learned the answer.

Tomorrow, Maren decided, she would begin finding out what else had happened on the fourteenth of Embers — starting with the one person in the palace whose tongue was blessed, whose blood was strange, and who had looked at the king like a woman who had already tasted the truth.

She would need to speak with Lena Vasquez. Carefully. Without either of them ending up ash.

Maren took up her cloak, and went to see how one asked a question that could get you burned.
