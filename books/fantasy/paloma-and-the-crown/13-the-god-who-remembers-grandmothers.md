## 13. The God Who Remembers Grandmothers

The tide gave them two hours.

Odile said it like a sentence of execution, which, knowing her, meant it was worse than that. "Two hours to get in, find what we're looking for, and get out before the water remembers its old borders." She stood at the lip of the sea-stair with a lantern in one hand and an expression in the other that Lena had learned to read as *I have done something this foolish before and it cost me everything.*

"You could wait up here," Lena said.

"I could," Odile agreed, and started down the steps.

The drowned shrine sat below the tide line on the eastern face of Vhalen's harbor wall, in the crook where the city met the sea like two people leaning into an argument. Once — and Lena only knew *once* because of the broth, because of the vision the soup had shown her — it had been a chapel of white stone above the waterline, with a bell and a garden and a door that opened for anyone who knocked. Now the sea owned it. The stairs descended into green dark. Barnacles had colonized the saints. The bell tower rose out of the shallows like a drowned man's last reaching hand.

Lena went down after Odile, lantern held high, boots finding each step by faith more than sight. The air changed as they sank below the street level — cooler, salt-thick, tasting of iron and kelp and something older underneath both. Something that tasted, she realized, the way her grandmother's kitchen had smelled when the woman was praying over the soup pot. Rosemary and smoke and a sweetness like honey left too long in the sun.

Her grandmother. Who had prayed here. At this shrine, or one shaped exactly like it, kneeling on these same stones before the sea took them.

"Odile," she called softly. "Why did my grandmother come here? Do you know?"

Odile paused three steps below, lantern light carving her face out of the dark. "Because your family was taster-stock. Taster-stock always came to the shore shrines. It was part of the blessing — you can't pour a message through a vessel that's never been washed." She resumed descending. "The old rite wasn't just immunity, girl. It was correspondence. Gods wrote to kings through blessed tongues, and kings answered back. Your grandmother didn't pray *at* this shrine."

"Then what did she do?"

"She checked the mail."

* * *

At the bottom of the stair, the water waited, black and glass-still, filling the nave of the sunken chapel to about knee height. Their lanterns made twin coins of light on its surface. Beyond the water, the chapel opened into shadow: broken pillars, an altar half-collapsed under a fallen lintel, and along the walls — this was the part that made Lena's skin crawl and sing at once — rows of small niches carved into the stone, hundreds of them, each one the size of a loaf of bread.

Each niche was empty.

"They were god-houses," Odile said quietly. She'd gone still in a way Lena recognized now, the stillness of a woman standing in the wreckage of her own life. "Every household god in Vhalen had a public niche here too, a second home, so the gods could travel between the people and the sea and back again. When the Edict came, the witch-hunters didn't just burn charms. They evicted. Every niche emptied. Every little god put out on the tide like a bag of refuse."

"And they drowned."

"Some drowned." Odile lifted her lantern higher. "Some learned to breathe water. Small things are hard to kill entirely. That was their whole nature — smallness. A god of one doorstep, one kettle, one child's scraped knee. You can't burn a kettle-god out of existence. You can only make everyone forget it ever kept the kettle from cracking."

Lena waded forward. The water was cold enough to ache but not cruel, and it moved around her legs with an attention she didn't like thinking about. Not current. Current pushed indiscriminately. This *circulated*. It gathered toward her and then away, the way a shy animal circles a stranger holding food.

She stopped in front of the altar. The lintel had crushed most of it, but one corner survived, and on that corner someone — long ago, before the drowning — had carved a single word in the old script that the Edict had also outlawed, the script Odile was teaching her letter by letter in the cistern at night, tracing shapes in spilled flour:

*Paloma.*

Dove. Messenger. The word her grandmother used to call her — not her real name, never her real name, because names were dangerous, but the name for what she might be someday. *Come here, paloma. Taste this. Tell me what it says.*

The water around her ankles shivered.

Then, gently, horribly, wonderfully — it spoke.

"Come here, paloma. Taste this. Tell me what it says."

Lena's whole body tried to leave without her. She caught herself against the altar corner, heart slamming, and stared down at the black water where the voice had come from, and the voice came again, and this time she heard what it was doing. It wasn't mocking her. It was *searching.* Like a musician tuning to a remembered pitch. Like someone feeling along a shelf in the dark for a book they hadn't touched in years.

"Odile," she managed. "It knows her voice. My grandmother's voice. How does it know—"

"Because your grandmother spoke to it every week for forty years." Odile had come up beside her, and her lantern-hand was shaking, though her voice stayed level the way only the truly frightened can manage. "Gods don't have voices of their own, girl. That's the first lie the crown tells. A god sounds like whoever loved it best. Whoever prayed longest, most faithfully, most *specifically*. You're hearing an echo of a woman who has been dead eleven years, wearing a thing that lives in seawater."

The water rose — not much, an inch, two — and formed itself, briefly, terribly, into a shape. A shape with rounded shoulders and hands knotted with arthritis and hair pinned up the way no one under sixty pinned their hair anymore. It lasted maybe three seconds before collapsing back into the sea, but those three seconds were enough. Enough that Lena's eyes filled before she could stop them.

"Mija," the water said. "You grew."

"That's not—" Lena pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes. "That's not fair. You don't get to do that. She's dead. I buried her. I *tasted* the fever-herbs myself to make sure the apothecary hadn't cheated her, and then I sat with her for three days, and you don't get to wear her like a coat."

The water went very still.

Then, in a voice that was no longer quite her grandmother's and had never been anything else either — a voice underneath the borrowed one, dry and small and patient as sediment — it said:

"You're right. Forgive me. I forgot how much it costs, being seen. I've been unseen so long I reach for whatever shape opens the door."

Lena lowered her hand. In the lantern light the surface lay flat and honest, reflecting her own face back at her, and beside it Odile's, and nothing else.

"What are you?" she asked. "Really."

"A door-kettle-hearth-and-harbor thing." A ripple, almost like a shrug. "I was never large. Before your Edict, fishermen poured me wine at spring launching and I kept their boats off the rocks, and mothers brought me their colicky infants and I taught the infants sleep, and once — once — a king sent a taster to me with a question sealed in honey, and I answered it, and that taster was a woman named Sera Vasquez who had hands like yours and a habit of talking to her soup."

Lena's throat closed. "Sera Vasquez was my great-grandmother."

"Yes." The word settled into the water like a coin sinking. "Your line checked the mail here for six generations. And then the letters stopped, and the niches emptied, and I slept in the cold dark with barnacles growing over my face, and I dreamed, all that time, of one specific flavor." Another ripple. "Rosemary and smoke and honey gone too far. Your grandmother's prayer-broth. She made me a bowl every Drowning Day, set it on the top step, and let the tide take it. No one had fed me in fifty years until five nights ago, when a certain reckless charm-seller boiled a pot of soup in a flooded chapel and it tasted *back*."

"That was an accident."

"Most mail is." 

Odile made a sound that was half laugh, half sob, strangled immediately. "This is why I stopped praying," she muttered, mostly to herself. "They're all like this. All of them. Enormous feelings in tiny bottles."

* * *

They climbed onto the broken altar where the water couldn't reach — Odile insisted, muttering about boundaries, about how the first lesson of dealing with anything divine is *do not stand inside the thing that wants something from you* — and the god talked.

It talked the way small gods talk, Lena was learning: sideways, in flavors and associations, circling its actual meaning like a gull circles a fish. Its name, it said, had been taken from the niches and struck from the rolls, so it would answer to anything they cared to call it, though it privately favored *Brackish*, which had been a joke between it and some long-dead ferryman. It told them about the other survivors — a handful, scattered along the coast, sleeping in wells and culverts and one particularly comfortable rain barrel behind a tannery. It told them the crown's witch-hunters had missed them not through incompetence but through contempt. "You cannot hunt what you have already decided is beneath hunting. Your king's mistake. Your king's *gift*."

And then it said, in a voice gone suddenly, deliberately simple, the way con artists and grandmothers both go simple before the ask:

"But you didn't come all this way to hear an old puddle gossip. You came because the priestess told you the relics of the true coronation are waking up, one by one, and the first of them is consecrated salt, and consecrated salt was kept — before the Edict — in chapels like this one. Under the altars. In lead boxes. And you want to know if the box under this altar is still there."

Lena looked at Odile. Odile looked at Lena.

"It is," the water said, before either of them answered. "I can feel it. Lead doesn't rot. Salt doesn't spoil. Fifty years underwater and it's still holy, because holiness is just *memory with weight*, and nobody's been down here to forget it." A pause. The surface dimpled, smoothed, dimpled. "There is, however, a problem."

"There's always a problem," Odile said.

"The problem is me. Or rather, the problem is what I am, and what I want, and the fact that those two things are about to become inconvenient for you." The water rose slightly, an inch, attentive. "I can give you the salt. I can lift the box from under the stone; I've had fifty years to learn the shape of every crack in this floor. I can tell you where the second relic sleeps, because bread and salt were always stored together and I remember the provisioning ledgers better than the clerks who burned them. I can teach you the counterwords your Edict erased, because I *am* the counterwords, or I was, before they cut us all out of the world."

Lena felt it coming the way you feel a wave gather before it breaks — the swell under the generous words, the undertow.

"And the price?" she said.

The water was quiet for a moment. When it answered, it used her grandmother's voice again, but carefully this time, asking permission with the borrowing, and somehow that was worse.

"Remember me."

"That's it?" Lena said. "Remember you?"

"That is everything." The voice cracked along some fault line, ancient and sudden. "Do you understand what forgetting does to a thing my size? I am not made of fire or storm. I am made of *attention*. A fisherman's thanks. A mother's whispered request at midnight. Every prayer is a brick and every rememberer is a wall, and when the last person who knew my name died, I began to come apart at the seams. I have spent fifty years holding my own shape together by remembering myself, and self-memory runs thin, girl. It runs thin like broth stretched over too many bowls." A ripple moved across the whole nave, and every empty niche seemed to lean in. "So yes. Remember me. Say my name — any name, Brackish, I'll answer to Brackish — to someone who'll pass it on. Teach it to children. Carve it somewhere the tide can't reach. Keep one bowl of soup on a step once a year. That is the entire price. That is the smallest price any god will ever offer you, and I need you to understand that the others will not be so gentle."

Odile had gone gray. "She's right," she said quietly. "Lena. Listen to me. I served the great gods, the temple gods, the ones with marble and incense budgets. Their prices were blood and years and obedience. But the small ones — the small ones bargain like starving men selling heirlooms. They'll take almost nothing because almost nothing is all they think they're worth anymore." Her jaw tightened. "And they will still take *everything you love* if the hunger gets bad enough. I watched it happen."

"I'm not asking you to trust me," the water said, mild again, reasonable again, and that reasonableness had teeth in it somewhere, Lena was sure of it, buried deep. "I'm asking you to be kind. Those are different things, and only one of them is a trap, and I promise you I am too small and too tired for traps."

Lena sat on the broken altar and looked at the black water for a long time.

Here was the thing she kept circling and couldn't land on: the voice was a manipulation. She knew that. Of course it was — a god wearing her dead grandmother's cadence, saying *mija, you grew*, precisely calibrated to open her ribs. Any witch-hunter would call it exactly what it was: bait. Sorrel would burn this whole chapel on principle.

And yet.

Her grandmother had believed in these things. Had fed them, tended them, taught Lena the names of herbs by teaching her the names of the gods who kept the herbs from bittering. And her grandmother had not been a fool. Her grandmother had been the sharpest person Lena had ever known, sharp enough to survive the Edict's first decade with her prayers intact, just quieter, just moved indoors, whispered over soup instead of shouted from shrines. If Sera Vasquez — no. If *Mama Rosa*, her grandmother, the one who'd actually raised her — if Mama Rosa had trusted the small gods with her evenings, her griefs, her colicky grandchildren, then there was something there worth more than Lena's fear.

Also, less nobly: she needed the salt. The usurper's armies were weeks from the free cities at most. There was no version of this where caution won.

"All right," Lena said. "But I want terms. Spoken plainly, none of your sideways talk."

"Ask."

"One: you give us the salt tonight, freely, before any remembering happens. Faith first, payment after — that's the human order and it's going to be the order here too. Two: you tell me the truth even when it costs you, starting now. Three—" she hesitated, and then said it, because it was the real term and they both knew it, "—you stop wearing her voice unless I say otherwise. If I'm going to remember you, I'll remember *you*. Not a costume."

The water was silent for so long that Lena thought she'd offended it, or worse, that she'd named its game too accurately and it would simply withdraw, sulk, let them drown in the rising tide with no salt and no allies.

Then, very slowly, the surface gathered itself up into a shape it had not used before. Not her grandmother. Not anything human. A low, broad form like a hearthstone with water running over it, worn smooth, unglamorous, patient. A shape that looked like it had been sat on by generations of tired people.

"This," it said, in a voice like gravel and tide together, "is closer. Thank you. Terms accepted. And, paloma — one honesty, freely given, as payment on account." The hearth-shape trembled. "I chose your grandmother's voice because I wanted you to say yes. I would have earned your kindness eventually, I hope, but I did not want to *wait* eventually. That is the manipulation you should weigh. Everything else I have told you is true."

"Even the salt?"

"Especially the salt. It's under the third flagstone to the left of the altar. Lift it and step back — the box is lead, but the seal is wax and wax gets *ambitious* after fifty years."

* * *

Getting the box out nearly killed them anyway, because the tide did not care about their negotiations. Odile pried the flagstone while Lena bailed and braced and swore, and the box came up streaming black water, and the wax seal split with a sound like a sigh and released a smell that hit them both like a fist — clean, mineral, ancient, the smell of every oath anyone had ever sworn and kept. Consecrated salt, packed in a lead-lined jar, white as bone, dry as truth after half a century drowned.

Lena held it in both hands and felt it hum against her palms, faint as a sleeping bird's heartbeat.

"It recognizes your line," Brackish said, hearth-shaped, watching from the shallows. "Six generations of Vasquez fingers. Put it in your pack and walk fast, girl, because the water's coming up the stairs and I can hold the tide back for exactly as long as it takes you to climb, and not one breath longer."

They climbed. At the top step Lena turned back. Below, the lantern light barely reached the nave now, and the god was already folding itself back into the ordinary sea, becoming scenery, becoming nothing, becoming the forgotten thing the Edict had made of it.

"Brackish," she said.

The water stilled.

"Drowning Day. Next year. There'll be a bowl on the steps of whatever's left of this chapel, and there'll be people with me when I set it down. That's a promise, not a payment. Learn the difference."

For a moment the whole flooded nave glowed faintly, phosphorescent, like moonrise underwater. Then it faded, and only the sea remained, and Lena climbed out into the night with salt in her pack and a god's name in her mouth, feeling like a woman who had signed something she couldn't read yet.

Odile said nothing until they reached the top of the sea-stair. Then she stopped, and looked east, toward the harbor mouth and the dark road beyond it, and her face did something complicated.

"Lena," she said. "Look."

Out past the breakwater, on the headland where the old beacon tower stood dead and dark — dead for fifty years, dead since the Edict — a light kindled. One flame. Then a second, further south along the coast. Then a third.

Beacons. Free-city beacons, lit in the old warning pattern, the pattern from the history books, the pattern that meant one thing and had only ever meant one thing:

*The army has crossed the river. Come, or mourn us.*

The salt hummed in Lena's pack, and far below the water, something that remembered her grandmother began, very softly, to pray.
