## 14. Free Cities, Burning Beacons

The dispatches arrived the way bad news always arrived at Vhalen's court: late, redacted, and smelling faintly of someone else's panic.

Maren found them in the third tray — the one marked *Routine Correspondence, No Action Required*, which in her twenty-two years of service had never once contained anything routine and always required action. The seal on the topmost letter was broken and re-sealed badly, a child's imitation of the Crown's wax stamp, pressed by someone who had learned forgery from a description rather than from practice.

She sat down slowly at her desk in the records office, lit both lamps despite the early hour, and read.

*From the Office of the Lord Marshal, Corvess-on-the-Water, to His Majesty's Court at Vhalen:*

*We write again, fourth time this season. The beacon towers along the Serrano coast have been lit for eleven days. Our watchers count banners beyond number moving down the King's Road — not the seasonal levies Your Majesty's letters describe, but supply trains stretching horizon to horizon, siege engines on ox-carts forty wide. Fishermen report warships taking on water at the Salt Quays without asking leave of any harbor master.*

*We ask plainly: are these Your Majesty's armies? And if they are — where are they going?*

*The free cities have fed this crown through two famines. We do not ask for soldiers. We ask for an answer.*

*Under the old covenant and the salt-oath of Aldric's line,*

*Lord Marshal Edran Voss, Corvess-on-the-Water*

Maren read it twice. Then she did what she had trained herself to do over five careful years: she copied it into her private ledger, in her own shorthand, in the margin code only she could decipher, and she filed the original where it would be found by no one who mattered.

Then she opened the second dispatch.

* *

*To the Court at Vhalen, from the Guild Council of Brasse, city of forges:*

*Honored sirs, we are told our previous letters were "received with interest." Interest is not grain. Interest is not iron. Interest will not stop what is coming down the river road.*

*A tinker reached us three nights past who walked out of the occupied territories. He says villages between here and the capital are being emptied. Not burned — emptied. Whole populations marched east under guard, toward the army's line of march, with wagons of salt and rope among the baggage train. Salt and rope, honored sirs. What army carries salt and rope?*

*Our envoys to the capital have been turned away at the Gate of Bells four times now. The gate captain tells them the king sees no petitioners. The king has seen no petitioners in five years. We remember when he saw everyone. We remember his face.*

*Forgive an old smith her bluntness: something is wrong at the heart of your palace, and whatever it is, it is marching toward us wearing your colors.*

*Send word. Send anyone. Send truth.*

Maren's pen hovered over the ledger. *Salt and rope.* She knew what salt was for. Every clerk in the records office knew what consecrated salt had been for, before the Edict of Ash, though none of them wrote it down anymore. It preserved. It sealed. It kept things from spoiling — or from rising.

She wrote it down anyway. Her ledger was full of things no one wrote down anymore.

* *

The third letter was different. Not a plea. A fragment.

It had been pulled from the body of a courier, according to the attached note from the Border Wardens — a rider found dead in the shallows of the Mirren ford, three arrows in his back, still clutching this against his chest inside his coat. Whoever killed him had searched him and missed it, or hadn't cared. The paper was water-stained. Half the ink had run.

Maren held it close to the lamp.

*...cannot hold the pass more than six days. They do not fight like men. I watched a hundred of ours break and run at nothing — at shadows on the ridge — and the shadows moved after them. Captain says it is terror weapons, sorcery, the old forbidden arts, and that the Crown will punish us for saying so. But I stood my ground and I looked, and what came down off that ridge wore armor of the Royal Guard, armor I polished myself as a boy in Vhalen, and the man inside it did not turn his head when his horse fell beneath him. He walked. He walked and the horse screamed and he did not turn his head.*

*If any loyal hand finds this: the free cities are not the target. We are the road. Whatever marches east is marching to the sea, and the sea is where the shrines are, and someone or something wants every drowned god in Vhalen's waters dragged up and made to kneel.*

*Tell them. Tell whoever still reads—*

The rest was water and time.

Maren set the fragment down. Her hands were steady — she prided herself on steady hands; a steward whose hands shook invited questions — but it took her three attempts to open the inkwell.

*He did not turn his head.*

She thought of the king. Of course she thought of the king. She had been thinking of the king for months now, in the way one thinks of a tooth that aches only when pressed. The private record she kept — locked in the false bottom of her writing desk, beneath forty years of innocuous household accounts — had grown fat with observations she could no longer call coincidences. The king did not blink during audiences. Did not sweat in July. Had not aged a day, not one gray hair, not one new crease, since the night five years ago when the whole court had celebrated his "miraculous recovery" from the fever that should have killed him.

She had written *miraculous recovery* in her ledger and then, later, crossed out *miraculous* and written *recovery* and then, later still, crossed out *recovery* too and left only the date.

The date matched. She had checked it eleven times. The correspondence stopped mid-sentence that same night — a letter to his sister in exile, broken off after the words *I have been thinking about what Father said regarding mercy, and I believe he was right that a king must sometimes—* and never resumed. Five years. A king who wrote daily letters all his life, stopped by one sentence, and no one in the palace thought to remark upon it because no one in the palace was paid to notice, and noticing was Maren's entire life.

Now the free cities were noticing too, from three hundred miles away, and they had never seen the king's face at breakfast.

* *

There was a fourth document in the tray. It was not a dispatch. It was a court circular, issued over the Steward-General's signature, and it instructed all senior staff that petitions from the eastern cities were to be received "with courtesy and without commitment," that no envoy was to be granted audience before the winter session, and that references to "irregular troop movements" were to be answered with the standing formula: *His Majesty's armies go where His Majesty wills, and His Majesty wills the prosperity of all his realms.*

Maren had drafted that formula herself, nine years ago, for use against border lords who complained about tolls.

Someone had taken her polite fiction and aimed it at a burning coastline.

She sat with the circular for a long moment. Outside the records office window, the palace grounds lay white with morning frost, orderly and beautiful, gardeners moving along the hedges like slow clockwork. Somewhere below, the kitchens were beginning the day's work. Somewhere above, behind doors no one entered uninvited, the king was not blinking.

The clock started ticking somewhere in her chest. She could not have said when it began — perhaps with the first letter, perhaps years ago, perhaps the day she first noticed he never aged — but she felt its rhythm now, hard and clear, like a second pulse.

Eleven days the beacons had burned. The army had been on the road longer than that. Even at forced march, even with the river barges, the host would reach the free cities' outer walls within the month. And the free cities had sent four letters, and gotten a formula.

* *

That afternoon, Maren did something she had done perhaps twice in five years: she left the records office in the middle of the day.

She told herself it was to verify a requisition. The requisition existed; she carried the paperwork; a steward walking with purpose through the palace drew no eyes. But her feet took her past the armory annex, past the mustering yards where — she noted, filing it away with cold precision — three times the usual number of supply wagons stood under canvas, and past the map room, whose doors stood open because the room was in use.

She did not enter. She stood in the corridor with her requisition papers and looked through the doorway at the great table map of the realm, at the officers bent over it, at the small carved markers being moved eastward in long patient lines.

An officer caught her looking — a young captain, harried, ink-stained. "Can I help you, mistress?"

"The Steward-General requested confirmation of provisioning figures," Maren said, which was true in the sense that the Steward-General had once, generally, cared about such things. "Are the eastern columns drawing from the central granaries?"

Something flickered across the young man's face. Not guilt exactly. The look of a man who has been ordered not to answer and has decided obedience is easier than courage. "All provisioning matters are consolidated under central command now, mistress. You'd want to speak to Colonel Aske."

"Of course. Thank you." She inclined her head and moved on, and heard, as the door began to swing shut, another officer say quietly, "—doesn't matter what they draw, so long as it holds until the crossing. After the crossing there won't be any need to feed half of them at all—"

The door closed. Maren walked on, unhurried, her requisition papers squared in her hands, while inside her the clock ticked louder.

*After the crossing there won't be any need to feed half of them at all.*

Salt and rope. Emptied villages. Armies that did not turn their heads. And somewhere ahead of it all, the drowned shrines of the coast, where the old gods slept under the water the Edict had never managed to drain.

* *

Evening brought one last paper, and this one she had not expected at all.

It was delivered to her desk by a page — a nervous boy of perhaps twelve who set it down, said "from the under-clerk pool, mistress, no reply requested," and fled. The envelope bore no seal. Inside was a single sheet, unsigned, in a cramped clerk's hand she almost recognized: one of the under-pool copyists, some grey ghost of the archives she had passed ten thousand times without learning a name.

*Steward Maren — forgive the anonymity. You are the only senior staff who has ever thanked a copyist by name, which is how I know you read everything, which is why I am writing.*

*You will have seen the eastern dispatches. Everyone in the pool has copied them. Everyone has noticed they die in the third tray. I want you to know the pool has noticed something else. We copy the army orders too. For six weeks the requisitions have included items no quartermaster can explain: ceremonial vestments in the old style. Incense by the ton. Chalk by the hundredweight. And chalk, mistress — chalk is for circles. Old women in my village still remember what chalk circles were for, before the Edict.*

*There is a mass rite planned at the river crossing. That is what the pool believes, and the pool does not believe things lightly; the pool only knows what passes through its hands. If the gods are waking anywhere — and the servants whisper that they are, that charms are working again, that prayers are being answered wrong — then whatever sits on the throne means to meet them at the water, and it does not mean to negotiate.*

*Burn this if you are afraid. I have already burned my draft twice. But someone senior has to start connecting pages, and you are the only one who reads everything.*

*— a copyist*

Maren read it three times. Then she took it to the candle flame and watched it curl, because the boy was right about one thing: she was afraid.

But fear, in a steward of forty years' service, expressed itself as filing.

She unlocked the false bottom of her desk. The private ledger came out, and beside it she laid tonight's copies: the marshal's plea, the smith's blunt grief, the dead courier's fragment, the circular with her own formula turned to cruelty, the copyist's warning. Five documents. She read them in sequence, as a rite, as a coronation of evidence, and at the end she wrote a single line at the bottom of the page in her tightest hand:

*The armies are not invading the free cities. They are harvesting the road to the sea. Something in the palace intends a reckoning with the old gods at the water, and the free cities are standing in the field where it will be fought.*

Below that, after a moment, she added:

*And I cannot stop it alone, and I do not know who else even sees it.*

She sat with that last line a long while. It was the most honest sentence she had written in five years, and honesty, in this palace, was the most dangerous ink of all.

There were people, she thought. Fragments of people. There was a food-taster with clever eyes who had asked once, too casually, about the palace ovens. There were whispers in the servant halls about charms that worked, prayers answered, a Nightmarket that the witch-hunters raided and raided and somehow never ended. There was a witch-hunter captain, Sorrel, whose reports had begun arriving with suspicious gaps in them, gaps shaped exactly like mercy.

And beneath the city, if the oldest rumors were true, there were cisterns, and things that lived in cisterns, and a priestess the Edict had never quite managed to drown.

Maren closed the ledger. Through the high window, far to the east, the sky over the distant hills was clear and cold and innocent, and no beacon light showed yet this far from the coast — but it would. Eleven days of burning beacons meant eleven days less than yesterday. Tomorrow there would be ten.

She snuffed one lamp, kept the other, and began, methodically, to plan how a clerk might do what no soldier, no envoy, no prayer had been permitted to do: carry the truth across the palace, page by page, hand by hand, before the water rose and the chalk circles closed and whatever wore the king got everything it had marched for.

The clock ticked. Ten days.

She picked up her pen.
