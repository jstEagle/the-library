## 15. Charm-Seller's Confession

They came for her at dawn, which was a courtesy. Sorrel's men usually came at the hour when a household was softest — mid-meal, mid-prayer, mid-sleep — because frightened people made fewer decisions and better confessions. Dawn meant he wanted her awake. He wanted her to see it coming.

Lena had been awake anyway. She had not slept since the raid on the Nightmarket three nights past, since she'd run through the drainage tunnels with Odile's hand clamped over her mouth while witch-hunters' boots crossed the grating overhead like weather. She'd come back to her rooms in the servants' wing of the palace, washed the tunnel-smell off, braided her hair, and waited for exactly this.

There was no point running. A royal food-taster who fled was a taster who confessed.

She opened the door before they knocked.

"Captain Sorrel," she said. "You're early for breakfast."

He didn't smile. Captain Sorrel did not smile, as far as anyone in the palace kitchens could determine; there were wagers on it. He stood in the corridor with two of his hunters behind him, gray-cloaked, ash-gray badges stitched at their throats — the Edict's little tongues of flame, inverted, as if fire itself had been put out and only its memory remained. His eyes went past her into the room with the flat thoroughness of a man counting spoons.

"Taster Vasquez," he said. "You'll come with us."

"Am I under arrest?"

"You're summoned for questioning." He said it precisely, the way a man lays down cards he has already counted. "There is a difference. For now."

*For now.* She let that sit between them like a knife on a table, then took her cloak from its hook, slow enough to show him she wasn't reaching for anything hidden, fast enough to show him she wasn't afraid. Both were lies. Her hands wanted to shake and she would not let them.

The palace corridors at dawn belonged to the sweepers and the scullery boys and the ghosts of last night's feast. Servants pressed themselves against walls as the hunters passed, and Lena felt their eyes on her back like small cold hands. *She's done,* the hands said. *She's taken.* By evening the story would be all through the kitchens: the king's own taster, hauled out by the Ashmen. Whatever happened next, her name was already ruined. Sorrel knew that too. Everything about this morning was built to make ruin feel inevitable, so that confession would feel like mercy.

She had been a servant in Vhalen long enough to know how mercy was manufactured.

* * *

The questioning rooms were in the old mint, down where the city's foundations met the river's stone, and the sound of water came through the walls like something breathing. They sat her in a chair that had been built for the purpose: straight-backed, armless, positioned so the single lamp fell full on her face and left the rest of the room in shadow. Somewhere behind the light, Sorrel arranged his papers. She heard the particular whisper of a ledger opening.

Her ledger. Or a copy of it.

"Tell me what you know about this," Sorrel said, and set the book on the table between them, just inside the lamplight.

It was hers. She recognized the water stain shaped like a gull on the back cover, the thread she'd used to rebind the spine when it cracked two winters ago. The Nightmarket raid must have turned up her stall's strongbox after all — or someone had talked. Someone had talked, and now here it was, every knot sold, every luck-thread priced, five years of small illegal mercies written in her own careful hand.

"I know it's a ledger," she said.

"Lena Vasquez." He said her name the way a priest says an absolution, testing whether it would take. "Royal food-taster, seven years' service, commended twice by the Master of Kitchens. No family listed. No debts. No vices worth noting." He turned a page. "And a second trade."

"The ledger isn't mine."

"It has your mark."

"My mark is a knot," she said. "Half the charm-sellers in the Nightmarket use a knot. It's the oldest sign there is. If you're arresting everyone who ties one, you'll need a bigger room."

He looked up at her then, and she saw the thing she had been told about and hadn't quite believed until now: that Captain Sorrel's face was not cruel. It was worse than cruel. It was *careful*. Cruelty wasted motion; care hoarded it. Every question he asked had already been paid for somewhere, in someone's burned stall or broken fingers, and he intended to get value from each one.

"Page forty-one," he said. "A luck-thread sold to a dockworker named Tam Weller, four coppers, marked *for the cranes to hold*. Page fifty-eight, the same man, *against drowning*. Page sixty-three—" he paused, and she watched him decide whether to say it, and say it anyway— "*that he find what he asks for.*"

The room got very quiet. Even the water in the walls seemed to lean closer.

Tam Weller. Dead smiling on the dock with his wish granted too literally, the harbor giving up its drowned dead like a mother emptying her pockets. Lena had seen the body herself. She had closed his eyes, or tried to; they wouldn't stay closed, as if he were still looking at whatever he'd found.

"He died," Sorrel said. "Three days ago. The same week your ledger records his third purchase."

"People die," Lena said. "Dockworkers especially. It's wet work."

"He was smiling. The physician noted it. Men don't drown smiling, Taster Vasquez. Not unless something gave them a reason." He closed the ledger and laid both hands flat upon it, and for a moment — only a moment — his voice lost its official grain and became simply a tired man's voice in a cold room. "I have been doing this work for eleven years. I have burned charms and I have burned people, and most of them deserved neither. And in eleven years I have never once seen a prayer answered. Not once. Until this month. Now I have a drowned man smiling at the sky, and a ledger that promised him exactly that, and a city where the shrines are waking up. So I am going to ask you plainly, and you are going to answer me plainly, because I am tired of burning smoke instead of fire."

He leaned forward into the light.

"What did you sell him?"

* * *

This was the moment, and she had known it was coming since the tunnels, and she had rehearsed it the way she rehearsed everything: the taste of almond under honey, the bitter green ghost of foxglove in a stew, the thousand small deaths she caught and swallowed on the king's behalf. You didn't survive the tasting bench by being brave. You survived by knowing, always, exactly how much truth a moment could hold without breaking.

Odile was beneath the city. Odile was the whole of it — the rite, the relics, the drowned shrine, the god who spoke in her grandmother's voice. If Sorrel followed one true thread down to the cisterns, everything ended: the unbinding, the dead king, the free cities, all of it, burned for the price of one old woman's hiding place.

So she would give him the charms. All of them. The whole small, guilty, human history of her double life, spread out like goods on a blanket — and nothing else. The charms were real. That was the beauty of it. She didn't have to invent a lie big enough to hang her; she only had to tell the truth narrowly.

"Yes," she said. "I sold it. I sold all of it."

Sorrel didn't move, but something in the room moved — the two hunters by the door shifting their weight, the lamp flame steadying, as if the space itself had been holding its breath.

"Say it again."

"I'm a charm-seller." Her voice came out level, and she marveled at it from a distance, the way she marveled at her own tongue detecting henbane in wine. "Have been for six years. Knots against drowning, threads for luck, blessings on new boats, charms for childbirth and fever and bad dreams. I learned the tying from my grandmother, who learned it from hers, back when it wasn't a crime to ask the old gods for small things. I broke the Edict of Ash every market night for six years, one copper at a time. That's what your ledger is. That's everything it is."

"And the answers?" Sorrel said softly. "Six years of selling, and nothing answered. Then, in one month, a drowned man comes up smiling. Explain that."

"I can't." And that was true too, and she let the truth of it show on her face, because a hunter can smell a manufactured expression the way she could smell arsenic. "I've been asking myself the same question, Captain. What changed? I tie the same knots I've always tied. Same words my grandmother used. And suddenly they *work*, and I don't know why, and it frightens me more than it frightens you, because I sold that man a thread and he's dead and smiling and I have to live with the arithmetic of that."

She let herself shake, then. Just a little. It wasn't even performance; the guilt was real, had been sitting in her chest since the dock like a swallowed stone. Sorrel watched her hands tremble and wrote something down, and she understood with cold clarity that her honesty was the most dangerous thing she could give him — because it was credible, and credible things get believed, and believed things get acted on.

Unless he asked the wrong next question.

"Who taught you the words?" he said.

"My grandmother. Dead nine years."

"Who taught the others? The Nightmarket sellers. There were fourteen stalls in that raid. Fourteen ledgers. Whose school is this?"

"No one's. That's not how it works." She leaned forward, matching him, letting the lamplight catch her face full-on so he could search it. "Captain, do you know what a charm actually is? Before the Edict, when the shrines were open, a blessing was a conversation. Priestess on one end, petitioner on the other, god in the middle taking messages. The Edict killed the priests and boarded up the shrines, but people still needed messages sent. So we became the message. A knot tied right, a word spoken right — it's a letter with no address, dropped in the river and trusted to the current. Nobody teaches it. Your grandmother's hands teach your hands. That's all. There's no school, no order, no conspiracy. Just grandmothers."

Something crossed his face at the word *grandmothers* — quickly, gone before it finished arriving. She filed it away. Everyone had a grandmother. Even witch-hunters. Especially witch-hunters, maybe; the Edict had been thirty years in the making, and Sorrel is perhaps forty, which meant he'd been a boy when the shrines still smoked with incense instead of ash.

"Your grandmother," he said. "Name."

"Maren Ottavane. Called Lena after her, if you must write it down."

He wrote it down. She watched the pen move and thought, absurdly, of Maren the steward, of her precise hand and her private records, and pushed the thought away before it could reach her face.

* * *

They questioned her for four hours. She gave them the charms in exhaustive detail — prices, materials, customers described by trade rather than name, six years reconstructed ledger-page by ledger-page until Sorrel's hunters stopped writing and started simply listening, because there was more than any of them had bargained for. She made herself boring. Boring was armor. A man could burn a mystery; nobody burns a tax record.

Once — only once — she nearly slipped. He asked about the night of the raid, where she'd gone when the stalls scattered, and she felt the cistern rise in her throat like bile, Odile's hand over her mouth, the dark water breathing below. She turned the cough into a cough.

"Away," she said. "Down an alley, home by the service gate. Ask the gate watch; they logged me in."

He would ask. The gate watch would confirm it, because the gate watch had been paid three months ago to confirm exactly such things, a taster's small insurance. She watched Sorrel weigh it and saw him decide, provisionally, to believe her — and hated how much of her life now depended on other people deciding to believe her.

Near the end, he set down his pen.

"Do you know what happens to charm-sellers under the Edict?" he asked. Not officially. Quietly, the way one colleague asks another about the weather.

"Burning," she said. "Or the Ash Cells, if the court wants it quiet. I taste for the king, Captain. I know what the crown does with things it swallows."

"First offense, burning is discretionary. The code allows fines and the lash for sellers who confess fully and name their suppliers." He recited it without inflection, a man reading his own sentence aloud. "Full confession. Named suppliers. Then the magistrates weigh it."

"I've confessed fully."

"You haven't named suppliers."

"There are none. I told you—"

"You told me a story about grandmothers." He rose, gathered the ledger, and stood with it against his chest like a breastplate. In the lamplight his face was finally legible, and what was written on it was exhaustion — eleven years of it, deep as floodwater. "Here is what I think, Taster Vasquez. I think you are lying to me about almost everything, and telling the truth so carefully that the truth itself becomes the lie. I think you know why the prayers are answering. I think you were in the Nightmarket the night we raided it for a reason that had nothing to do with selling knots. And I think that if I press you, you will break, because everyone breaks — but I am not certain, anymore, that what's on the other side of your breaking is something I want to see."

The room was silent. The water breathed in the walls.

"So here is what I am going to do instead, and you will understand that this costs me more than it costs you." He set the ledger down and slid it across the table toward her. Her own book, returned. "You will remain in the palace. You will continue to taste for the king. You will report to me weekly — anything unusual in the kitchens, the court, the shrines, the streets. Anything answered. Anything *waking*. And if I learn you've withheld from me, the discretion ends, and the code takes its course, and I will stand there and watch it done, because that is my oath and I do not get to refuse it either."

She stared at him. "You're making me your spy."

"I'm making you my *ears*. There's a difference. Spies want something." He moved to the door, then stopped, his back to her, and said the last of it almost too quietly for the hunters to hear. "My grandmother used to tie knots too. Before the Edict. Against storms, for the fishing boats. I was very young. I had forgotten until tonight." A pause. "The drowned man's funeral is tomorrow. His daughter asked the temple for rites and the temple refused — Edict rules, no prayers for the dead. So the dockworkers are going to sing over him themselves, old songs, banned ones. There will be a crowd. There may be trouble."

He opened the door.

"Why tell me that, Captain?"

"Because you're going to report it to me anyway," Sorrel said, "and I'd rather hear it from you than discover it in the ashes." He stepped into the corridor, then added, without turning: "Watch what the songs do, Taster. Watch closely. And then tell me honestly what you saw — because honest is the only currency either of us has left."

* * *

They walked her back through the waking palace, and at the mouth of the servants' stair he released her with a nod, formal as a treaty. She climbed the stairs slowly, the ledger heavy under her cloak, and let herself into her narrow room, and bolted the door, and stood in the middle of the floor with her heart going like a hammer on a stuck hinge.

She had kept Odile safe. She had kept the cistern dark. She had traded her freedom for a leash and called it survival, and it was survival, and the leash was real, and every week now she would sit across from that careful, exhausted face and feed him truths chosen like poisons — enough to satisfy, never enough to kill.

But it was the last thing he'd said that she couldn't stop turning over.

*A crowd. Old songs. Watch what the songs do.*

Tomorrow. Tam Weller's funeral. Dockworkers singing banned prayers over a smiling drowned man, on the docks, in daylight, with the temple refusing and the Edict standing right there like a wall with a crack running through it.

Lena sat on the edge of her cot and untied the ledger from under her cloak and held it in her lap, her whole criminal life in one worn book, and outside her window the bells rang prime, and far off toward the harbor she could almost imagine she heard them already — voices, gathering, tuning, remembering.

Whatever happened tomorrow, she would be there. And Sorrel would be watching her watch.

She only hoped she'd know what the songs did before he did.
