## 16. The Second Relic: Bread

The recipe for coronation bread had not been used in five years.

Lena knew this because she knew everything the kitchens knew. It was part of her trade — a taster who didn't know her kitchens was a taster already dead — and so she kept the ovens' rhythms the way other people kept prayers: the morning loaves at the fourth bell, the festival bakes when the steward's office sent down its sealed requisitions, and the one oven, deep in the oldest wing, that hadn't held fire since the night the king "recovered" from his illness and the whole palace lit candles in relief.

That oven was called the King's Mouth. It was built into the foundation stones of the palace itself, older than the Edict, older than the dynasty, maybe older than Vhalen's walls. Every king of the old line had been crowned with bread baked in it — a small dark loaf, salted, broken once by the priest and once by the king, half given to the gods and half eaten standing up, because you were not supposed to sit while taking on the weight of a country.

Odile had told her this in the cistern, her voice flat as slate, her hands wrapped around a cup of barley tea she never drank.

"Salt to bind the tongue. Bread to carry it. Blood to wake it. Name to aim it." She'd counted them off like coins. "You have the salt?"

"I have the salt."

"Then get the bread. And Lena—" Odile had looked at her then with those ruined-church eyes, "—it has to be *that* bread. From that oven. The crown-spirit will smell anything else before it touches it. It has spent five years eating that king's memories. It knows what his coronation tasted like."

*It knows what his coronation tasted like.* Lena had turned that sentence over for three days, and every time she turned it, it got heavier.

Because there was a problem, and the problem was this: the King's Mouth had no fire in it, and no fire could be put in it without a requisition signed by the steward's office, and any requisition touching the old rite would go straight up the chain to whoever actually ran this palace now — which was not, she was increasingly sure, the man wearing the crown.

So the loaf would have to be an old loaf. A preserved one. And there was exactly one place in Vhalen where a five-year-old coronation loaf might still exist.

The archive vaults beneath the steward's wing kept relics of state the way a spider keeps flies: pinned, labeled, and forgotten until needed. She'd heard kitchen gossip about it for years — *they've still got the old queen's wedding cup down there, they've got the founder's sword, they've got bread from every crowning back to Bren of the Bridges* — gossip that had always sounded like the kind of story servants tell each other to make a big cold building feel smaller.

Tonight she was going to find out if it was true.

* * *

She went in through the pastry kitchen, because the pastry kitchen's outer door had a lock that predated the Edict and opened to a charm her grandmother had taught her — a small illegal thing, a knot of intent tied into a length of twine, that made stiff iron remember being soft. The lock sighed open like an old man settling into a chair. Lena pocketed the twine and told herself, as she did every time, that this was the last time she'd use it.

She'd stopped believing herself around the third lie.

Inside, the palace at night was a different animal than the palace by day. By day it was noise and hierarchy, steam and shouting, the great machine of feeding three hundred mouths that let her move unseen in plain sight. At night it was stone and breath. Torch brackets empty. Her own footsteps coming back to her from the high dark of the corridor like someone following.

She wore her serving grays and carried a covered tray. This was the oldest trick in her book: nobody stops a woman carrying food. Guards looked through trays the way they looked through walls. She'd walked past Sorrel himself once with a tray of jellied eel and he'd stepped aside to let her pass, and she'd thought about that afterward with something between amusement and nausea.

The stewards' wing smelled different from the kitchens — ink and cold stone and lamp oil, the smell of people who worked with paper instead of dough. Two turns down the main corridor, then the narrow stair behind the record room that the clerks used to haul ledgers up from below. The stair door had a proper lock, new brass, Edict-era.

But locks were only ever as good as the habits of the people who used them, and clerks going up and down forty times a day propped doors with their heels and wedges and, once, a folded bill of lading that had jammed the strike plate so long it had worn a groove. Lena pulled the door. It caught. She breathed out through her nose, took the twine again, and gave the lock the softest touch of unmaking she could manage — just enough for the bolt to dream it was open.

The stair went down farther than she expected. That unsettled her. Palaces grow upward; their roots are older than anyone admits.

At the bottom, lamplight.

Not torchlight — lamp, steady and yellowed, the light of long hours and careful work. It came through a doorway at the foot of the stairs, along with the sound of a nib scratching paper. Scratch. Pause. Scratch. Someone was down here. Working. At — she checked the small clock in her head — nearly midnight.

Lena stood in the dark of the stairwell and did the arithmetic she hated doing. Every plan has a point where it meets the world, and the world does not care about your plan. She could wait. The clerk might work till the second bell or dawn; archives people were either monks or ghosts and she couldn't guess which. Or she could go around — there had to be another way into the vaults, some goods entrance — but every minute she stood here was a minute closer to the change of watch, and Sorrel's people had started walking the corridors in pairs since the Nightmarket raid, since the ledger with her mark in it, since he'd looked at her across his desk and said *we'll speak again, taster* in a voice that had promised nothing and meant everything.

Scratch. Pause. Scratch.

She eased forward and looked through the doorway's gap.

A single desk under a single lamp. Rows of shelving vanishing back into the dark, loaded with boxes and bound bundles and objects under dust sheets, the whole room breathing that dry paper-and-iron smell of a place where things are kept so they can be forgotten properly. And at the desk, a gray-haired woman in steward's black, writing.

Maren.

Lena's stomach dropped through six floors of palace.

The court steward. The king's own shadow. The woman who signed the requisitions, who knew every schedule, every delivery, every mouth the palace fed — who had, more than once, watched Lena taste the king's breakfast with those pale unfooled eyes and said nothing. If anyone in Vhalen could end her with a word, it was the woman at that desk.

And the coronation bread, if it existed, was somewhere behind her in those shelves.

Lena stood very still in the stairwell dark and listened to her own heart argue with itself.

There was no way past. There was no way back worth taking. Somewhere above her, the watch was changing, and somewhere across the city, Odile was counting days on her fingers by cistern-light — the tide that would drown the flooded chapel chapel-road twice a day, the moon that governed the rite, the armies that governed everything else. *Get the bread,* she'd said, in the voice of a woman who had stopped asking the gods for anything and only ever asked people, carefully, knowing what people cost.

Scratch. Pause.

Then the scratching stopped, and Maren said, without turning around:

"You may as well come in. The stair creaks on the fourth step and you skipped it."

* * *

Every instinct Lena owned screamed at her to run. She had survived five years in this palace by knowing exactly which instincts to obey and which to starve, and she starved this one, and stepped through the doorway with the tray held level and her face arranged into the mild blankness of a servant who has perhaps become lost.

"Forgive me, mistress. I was looking for the—"

"Don't." Maren did not look up. Her nib moved steadily across the page. "Don't build the excuse while I'm watching. It's insulting to both of us."

Silence. The lamp hissed faintly. On the desk beside Maren's ledger lay a stack of papers weighted with a stone, and beside the papers, incongruously domestic, a heel of ordinary brown bread on a napkin, half-eaten. Lena fixated on it absurdly — the mundane bread, the midnight meal — as if studying it hard enough might rewrite the rest of the room.

"You're the taster," Maren said. "Vasquez. Five years' service, commendations from the master of kitchens, and last month your name appeared in a witch-hunter's ledger in connection with unlawful charms, though the matter seems to have gone... quiet." The nib paused. "Nothing goes quiet in this palace unless someone is holding it quiet. I've wondered for weeks who."

"And now?"

"Now I'm wondering what a food-taster wants in the archive vaults at midnight." She set down the pen at last and turned, folding her hands on the ledger, and looked at Lena the way she looked at the king's breakfast — thorough, unhurried, missing nothing. "Take your time. I find lies age badly under lamplight, but I'm willing to be surprised."

Lena's mind ran the exits again, uselessly, like a rat testing a cage. Run, and be reported by morning — or worse, remembered. Talk, and hang herself with whatever rope Maren chose to offer. There was a third option, the one she'd used with Sorrel: give ground slowly, keep the center hidden, survive the hour and worry about the week later.

But she was tired. Five years tired. And something in the woman's flat gray patience made the third option feel like a debt she'd already defaulted on too many times.

"The bread," Lena said. "From the last coronation. I need it."

Maren's expression did not change, which was itself a kind of thunderclap. Most faces, hearing a sentence like that, flinch or laugh or reach for a bell-pull. Maren simply continued looking at her, and after a moment said, quietly, as if confirming a figure in a column:

"Why?"

"Because the king is dead," Lena said. "And the thing wearing him needs unbinding, and the rite needs four things, and I have one of them, and bread is the second."

The words came out steadier than she felt. Saying them aloud in this room — saying them to *the steward*, to the machine of the palace in person — should have felt like stepping off a roof. Instead it felt like setting down a weight she'd forgotten she was carrying.

Maren looked at her for a long time.

Then she rose, took the lamp, and walked back into the shelves. She didn't ask Lena to follow. But she didn't ask her to stay, and after a heartbeat Lena followed, because what else was there now — there was only forward, only deeper, only the strange gravity of a thing begun.

Maren walked the aisles like a woman walking her own memory, turning left, right, left, trailing one finger along shelf edges without looking. Dust hung in the lamplight like slow snow. Here a sheeted shape the size of a chair; there a case of small drawers labeled in clerk's shorthand; here a rack of ceremonial weapons gone soft with grease-cloth and years.

"Everyone assumes the vaults hold treasure," Maren said, conversationally, in the tone of a lecture she'd given no one for twenty years. "Gold, jewels. Mostly they hold paperwork and leftovers. States are made of records. Kill the records and the state forgets it exists." She stopped at a low cabinet, iron-banded, its lock green with age. "Which is why I have always found it interesting that certain records from five years ago were removed. Not archived. Removed. By order of an authority I could not identify and could not refuse."

Her hands, Lena noticed, were not entirely steady.

"The recovery," Lena said softly. "The night the king recovered."

"The night the king *changed*," Maren corrected, and there it was — the crack in the clerk's voice, thin as a hairline in glaze. She unlocked the cabinet with a key from her chatelaine, and the smell that came out was dry and sweet and ancient: grain, salt, beeswax, stone.

Inside, on a shelf lined with linen, sat a small dark loaf.

It looked like nothing. That was the first shock — how little it looked like destiny. A round of bread gone hard and dark as wood, salt-crusted, its split top sealed with a thread of gold wire and a wax seal bearing the arms of the old priesthood: a dove carrying a key in its beak. The label tied to it was in faded clerk's hand, and Lena read it upside down before Maren even lifted it out.

*Coronation of Aldric, son of Meric. Half-loaf, god's portion retained per rite. Do not discard.*

*Aldric.* The name went through her like cold water. Odile had never spoken it. No one spoke it. The king had a name, of course, the official name, spoken at ceremonies — but this label was older than ceremony, written by some dead clerk's honest hand, and it said *Aldric, son of Meric* the way you write a real person's name, a man who had a father and a favorite meal and, presumably, a way he liked his letters read to him.

"He was crowned with this," Maren said. "Half was eaten. Half belongs to the gods — that's the rite, isn't it? I've read enough to know the shape of what you're describing." She held the loaf in both hands, and her thumb moved over the seal almost tenderly. "I filed it myself, five years ago. And then in the spring I came down to check on it, because I checked on everything in those days, and the box was empty. Empty except for this label, which someone had left behind like an apology."

"But it's here."

"It is here because I spent four months finding out where it had gone, and two years arranging to have it returned to my custody under a false inventory entry, and three years since pretending I didn't know why I'd done that." Maren's voice stayed level, but her hands had gone white-knuckled around the loaf's linen wrapping. "I am not a brave woman, taster. I am a thorough one. Thoroughness is braver than bravery, in the long run. Bravery spends itself in an evening. Thoroughness keeps accounts."

She held out the loaf.

Lena reached for it — and stopped. "Why?" she asked. Her turn, now, to ask the question that mattered. "You could hand me to Sorrel instead. You could have, ten minutes ago. Why give me this?"

For the first time, Maren smiled, and it was the saddest thing Lena had seen all year.

"Because I write letters to a dead man," she said. "Have done for years. I'll explain that some other night, if we're granted other nights. For now: the bread leaves this vault, the inventory shows a transfer to the ceremonial stores, signed by me, dated correctly, unimpeachable. If anyone asks, you are running an errand." She pressed the loaf into Lena's hands, and it weighed far more than bread should — or perhaps it only felt that way, the way promises weigh. "Go. Fourth step creaks. Skip it going up too."

* * *

Lena made it eleven steps toward the stair before the door at the far end of the vault corridor rattled, and voices came through it — two of them, bored, male, the specific boredom of men walking a corridor they walk every night.

Witch-hunters. Patrol pair, Sorrel's new protocol, early by a quarter bell, or late, or simply the world refusing to cooperate one final time tonight.

There was nowhere to go. The shelves offered cover but no exit; the vault had one door and she was standing in the middle of its throat with contraband in her arms and a stolen blessing stitched into her sleeve. She pressed herself into the aisle shadow, heart slamming, and heard boots approach the archive door — heard them stop.

"Maren's lamp's burning," one said. "Late even for her."

"She lives down there, near enough. Leave her to it. Last man who interrupted her inventory got a lecture on filing conventions that lasted till dawn."

Boots receding. A laugh. The door closing.

Lena stood shaking in the dark among the state's forgotten possessions, and realized she was gripping the loaf hard enough to crack the crust, and made herself stop.

When she came out past the desk, Maren had resumed her seat, pen moving, lamp steady, as if nothing in the world had happened — as if harboring treason were merely another entry in the ledger. Only her eyes lifted as Lena passed, and held her for one moment.

"The third thing you'll need," Maren said quietly, not pausing her pen. "Blood, if your rite runs true to form. Royal blood. You won't find any in this palace worth having." The nib scratched. "Whatever you intend — be quick. The army marches within the fortnight. I read the dispatches. Nobody else bothers."

Lena stood in the doorway with a dead king's bread against her chest and stared at her.

"You know what I'm doing."

"I know what you're *carrying*," Maren said. "The rest is your affair. Goodnight, taster."

Lena climbed the stair, skipping the fourth step, and did not breathe fully again until she was through the pastry kitchen and out under the open sky, where the stars over Vhalen looked — for the first time in five years — less like cold holes in the dark and more like eyes, opening.

Two relics. Salt and bread. Blood and name remaining, and one of them, apparently, did not exist anymore anywhere in the world.

She turned toward the cistern, toward Odile, toward the drowned shrine where a god waited in her grandmother's voice — and wondered, with a chill that had nothing to do with the night air, how exactly you were meant to steal a name that had been erased on purpose.

Somewhere behind her, deep under the steward's wing, a lamp burned late over letters addressed to a dead man.

Lena walked faster.
