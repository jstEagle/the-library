## 18. Odile's Fall

The cistern had a sound that Odile had learned to call silence, though it was nothing of the kind. Water moved somewhere in the dark — always moving, never arriving. Drips counted themselves off the vaulted ceiling in irregular time. Rats conducted their small economies along the ledges. And beneath all of it, faint as a pulse under a wrist, the city above went on breathing: cart wheels, gulls, the bells of the Ashen Cathedral ringing the hours for a god nobody was allowed to name.

Odile sat with her hands flat on her knees and listened to it the way she used to listen for a voice in the reed-curtains of the shrine, twenty years ago, when listening was still her whole vocation. She had been very good at listening once. That was the cruelty of it. The gods had given her an ear like a keyhole, and then the Edict of Ash had come through the kingdom nailing doors shut, and she had spent two decades pretending she couldn't hear anything at all.

Now there was a girl asleep on the pallet across the cistern chamber — the taster, Lena Vasquez, curled on her side with one hand under her cheek like a child, breathing the slow breath of someone who had finally run far enough to stop. Odile watched her the way you watch a fire you have lit in a dry season: with tenderness, and with a rising, shameful dread.

Because the girl's blood was loud. Even sleeping, even doing nothing but existing, Lena's blessing rang against Odile's trained ear the way a struck bell rings against the palm pressed to its bronze. Odile could feel it from six feet away. It had taken everything she had not to flinch when she first touched the girl's wrist to check for fever.

*Immunity is a kind of love,* she had told her. *Your line was meant to carry messages between gods and kings.*

All true. All true, and still a lie by omission, because what Odile had not said was this: she had known within a minute of meeting Lena what slept in her veins. And she had hesitated. A full day and a night she had hesitated, feeding the girl fish stew, showing her the safe passages, saying nothing — while underneath the hesitation something old and cold turned over in her chest and whispered: *this is how they found us last time.*

She feared the gods. That was the plain truth of it, and she had built twenty years of masonry over the truth, and now it was coming up through the cracks, and the girl asleep across from her deserved better than masonry.

Odile rose, took the hooded lamp, and went down to the lowest gallery of the cistern where she kept the things she had sworn to burn and never had. There was a flat stone near the old sluice-gate that lifted. Underneath, wrapped in oilcloth gone brittle as leaf-skeleton, lay the only objects she had saved from the Priory of the Drowned Bell.

A bell, no bigger than a fist. A prayer-book with half its pages torn out. And a strip of linen, stained brown, embroidered in red thread with a single word in the old script: *LISTEN.*

She knelt down in the dark with these things laid out before her like a confession waiting to be made, and she let herself remember — properly this time, without the mercy of fog — the night her order died.

* * *

The Priory of the Drowned Bell stood on the salt marshes south of Vhalen, three days' walk from the walls, on ground the sea took back twice a day. You reached it along a causeway that vanished at high tide, so that the priory was either an island or a prison depending on the hour, and the sisters joked that they were married to both. Forty-one women when Odile arrived at seventeen. Forty-one women who kept the shrine of Paloma-of-the-Reeds, the small gray god of messages, doves, and safe arrival — a god so minor that even the Edict's compilers had nearly overlooked her, listing her in an appendix between a spirit of door-hinges and a saint of beekeepers.

That near-oversight should have saved them. Instead it doomed them, because when the witch-hunters came, they came not with armies but with ledgers. Captain Aldous Vane — Sorrel's predecessor, Sorrel's teacher, though Odile would not learn that connection until years later — arrived on a low tide in a wagon drawn by two mules, carrying a list of names and a brazier.

It began politely. Everything about the Edict was polite in those first years; the terror hadn't yet learned to enjoy itself. Vane sat in the refectory drinking barley tea and explained, with the patience of a man addressing children, that the Crown required the priory to demonstrate compliance. No prayers aloud. No charms given out. No blessings spoken over travelers, no knots tied with intent, no bells rung for any purpose other than marking time. The sisters would sign a renunciation. The drowned bell in the marsh-shrine would be filled with lead and sunk past recovery. In exchange, the priory might continue as a charitable house for widows and orphans, its records amended, its history sanded smooth.

The Abbess, Mother Sef, who had baptized Odile into the listening life with river water and a whisper, thanked him courteously for his clarity and said the sisters would need the night to pray on their answer.

Vane smiled at that. "Pray quietly," he said.

They did not pray quietly. They prayed as they had always prayed, which is to say they listened — and what Odile heard that night in the reed-curtain chapel she had never told another living soul, because telling it made it real, and keeping it unspoken had been the last wall between herself and a thing too large to survive.

Paloma answered.

Not in words. Small gods rarely speak in words; they are made of accumulated attention, decades of fishermen's wives asking for safe passage, of mothers tying luck-thread to sons' wrists, and what they offer back is less like speech than like weather. But that night, with the Edict's shadow already falling across the marshes, the weather of Paloma came down on the priory like a storm, and every sister heard it, and every sister understood the same thing in the same instant:

*You are remembered.*

Forty-one women wept in the dark of the chapel, silently, so as not to wake the mules, while their god poured remembrance over them like warm water, and Odile — nineteen years old, kneeling on cold stone with tears running into her mouth — felt more loved in that single hour than she would feel again for thirty years.

And here was the thing she could never forgive, the thing that had curdled inside her ever since and soured every prayer before it could leave her lips:

In the morning, forty of them signed the renunciation anyway.

* * *

Not Mother Sef. Sef stood in the refectory doorway at dawn with the tide going out behind her and told Vane, in the same courteous voice, that a god who could be renounced was not worth the name, and that the bell stayed in the marsh where the sea had put it, and that he could fill his brazier with whatever he liked but it would not be her faith.

Vane nodded, unsurprised, almost gentle. He had come expecting exactly one sister like this; his ledger had a column for it.

"Then the rest of you will watch," he said, "and understand."

They burned her at low tide, on the exposed mudflats, so the whole priory could see from the causeway and so the ash would wash away cleanly with the evening water. Odile stood in the front rank of the watching sisters with her hands locked behind her back and her fingernails dug into her palms, and she did the arithmetic that has poisoned every quiet moment since: forty lives bought with one. A good trade. Any steward of a household would make it. Any sensible woman.

Except that halfway through — halfway through, when the smoke turned black and Mother Sef's hymn broke off into a sound Odile still heard in the space between waking and sleep — Paloma answered *again*.

Right there, over the mudflats, in broad daylight, in front of the witch-hunters and the mule-cart and the forty signed renunciations. Not warmth this time. Not remembrance. Grief. A grief so vast and so specific that it had texture, weight, direction — it fell on the watchers like wet wool, and Odile felt it pass through her and knew, with absolute certainty, that the god was grieving not for the woman burning but for the forty who had chosen the paper over the bell.

And then — this was the part that ended Odile's praying forever — the grief *turned*.

She felt it turn. Felt the vast sad attention of her small gray god swing around and fix on her, on Odile specifically, on the girl whose ear was the sharpest in the order, the girl everyone had always said would be the next to hold the shrine — and in that attention there was no comfort at all. There was only the question, wordless and enormous:

*Why did you sign?*

She didn't answer. She couldn't. She stood on the causeway with her signed renunciation in her pocket and let her god look at her, and whatever Paloma saw in her face made the attention withdraw — slowly, the way a hand withdraws from a thing that has proven hot — and after that morning Odile never clearly heard the god again. Whether Paloma stopped speaking to her, or whether Odile stopped being able to listen, she genuinely did not know. Both, probably. Listening is a muscle of trust, and hers had torn clean through.

The priory was dissolved by winter. The bell was found — Vane's men dragged the marsh for nine days — filled with lead, and sunk in the deepest channel. The sisters were scattered to charitable houses across the province, each one assigned a watcher, each one required to report quarterly. Odile went to Vhalen itself, of all places, into the belly of the beast, because she had reasoned with the cold clarity of the newly damned that the safest place to hide was inside the machinery of the hunt. She became a laundress in the witch-hunters' barracks. Then a cook. Then, using forged papers and a dead cousin's name, a seller of protective knots in the Nightmarket — selling, of all profane things, the exact small blessings she had renounced in writing, priced in coppers, to dockworkers who had no idea their charm-seller had once been the finest listener in the last free priory in Vhalen.

She told herself it was penance. Some nights she believed it. More often she knew it for what it was: a woman standing at the edge of the water her god had drowned in, throwing pebbles in, hoping to hear a splash.

* * *

The lamp guttered. Odile realized she was gripping the little bell hard enough to hurt her palm, and made herself open her fingers, joint by stiff joint.

Twenty years. Twenty years of not-praying, and she had gotten fluent in it, the way you get fluent in any language you speak daily. She had excuses arranged like arguments in a debate she'd won so long ago the winning had gone stale: the gods are dangerous, the returning gods especially, raw and unfiltered, granting wishes too literally, killing the men who ask them for simple things. Look what happened to the dockworker with the wish for a quiet winter. The gods don't love us; they *notice* us, and notice from something that size is like sunlight through a burning glass. Better to be small. Better to be unheard. Better to sign the paper.

All true. All true, and still a lie by omission — she could hear her own voice making the argument and recognized, with sick clarity, that it was Vane's voice. Polite. Reasonable. A ledger with a column for surrender.

Because the truth underneath was simpler and worse: she had signed the paper because she was afraid, and the god had looked at her, and she had never once in twenty years asked forgiveness — not because she thought she wouldn't receive it, but because she was terrified she would. Forgiveness would mean the listening opened again. Forgiveness would mean feeling that attention fall on her and having no paper in her pocket, no excuse, no fog. She had spent her whole adult life choosing the smaller pain because the larger one had love in it.

Shame, Mother Sef had taught her, long ago on the causeway, shelling mussels into a bucket, is just grief that refuses to become action. "Feel it, then get up, child. The tide doesn't wait for your feelings, and neither does the work."

The work. The girl asleep upstairs in the cistern gallery, with a dead king's name waiting somewhere in the dark and a crown-spirit counting its soldiers toward the free cities. The work had a taster in it now, a girl whose blood rang like a struck bell, a girl who had looked at Odile yesterday with those tired, competent, lonely eyes and trusted her — trusted her the way you trust anyone who feeds you — and Odile had held the trust up to the light for a day and a night and examined whether it could be spent or must be returned.

Returned. As if faith were currency and she still had any.

Odile picked up the scrap of linen — *LISTEN*, in Sef's own cramped red stitching — and folded it into her sleeve instead of back under the stone. Then the prayer-book. Then, last, after a long moment, the little bell, which she hung at her belt where the Nightmarket customers would take it for a beggar's cup.

She climbed back up through the galleries, following the drip-count and the far-off breathing of the city, and stopped at the entrance to the sleeping chamber. Lena was awake — sitting up on the pallet, hugging her knees, watching the doorway with the wary patience of someone accustomed to waiting out other people's silences.

"You were gone a while," the girl said.

"I was settling accounts." Odile lowered herself onto the stone bench, joints cracking, and found that the truth, once the seal was broken, wanted out all at once, the way water wants through a cracked dam. "I'm going to tell you something, taster, and then you can decide how much of me you want to trust. Twenty years ago I signed a piece of paper that saved my life and betrayed my god, and my god looked at me while I did it, and I have not prayed since — not because I stopped believing they answer, but because I am afraid of what it feels like when they look at you. Do you understand? Every knot I ever sold you in that market was a coward's apology. I fear them, Lena. I fear the gods more than I love them, and I have never once said that sentence out loud, and now I've said it to you, and you're the only person alive who needed to hear it. Because you're about to walk into a throne room and call a name, and if you're carrying my kind of fear, it will kill you faster than any witch-hunter."

Lena was quiet for a long moment. Water dripped. Somewhere above, the Ashen Cathedral began to ring the hour.

"No," the girl said finally, and there was something in her voice Odile hadn't heard before — not pity, thank all forgotten gods, but recognition. "I don't think it'll kill me. I think—" She paused, choosing. "I think the soup remembers things, and the knots hold, and the dockworker died smiling. I think being noticed isn't the worst thing that can happen to a person. Being forgotten is. You weren't forgotten, Odile. Your god spent twenty years leaving a light on for you."

Odile looked at her — at this exhausted, hunted, blessed stranger handing her back a hope she'd buried twice — and felt the listening-muscle move in her chest for the first time since the mudflats, a scar tissue flexing, painful as any first stretch.

And then she heard it. Faint, distant, up through the stone and the water and the ringing bells — not a voice, nothing so merciful as a voice, but weather, a change in pressure, a gray-winged attention turning toward the cistern like a dove banking on the wind.

Toward *them*. Toward the girl. And, trembling on the edge of it, unmistakably, toward her.

"Lena," Odile said, and her own voice sounded young and terrified and nineteen years old. "Get your shoes. Something's found us — and I think, this time, it's not the hunters."

Boots sounded on the cistern stair. Many boots. And beneath them, threading through the dark like a needle through cloth, a smell Odile had hoped never to smell again: consecrated salt, and torch-pitch, and witch-hunters' iron.
