## 19. The Third Relic: Blood

The cistern smelled of wet stone and old incense, and by the light of three guttering candles Odile had laid out the rite like a surgeon lays out knives.

Salt in a clay bowl. Bread wrapped in linen, still faintly warm from the palace ovens two nights past. And now a third space, empty, waiting on the cloth between them — a shallow dish of black glass that caught the candleflame and held it without burning.

"Blood," Odile said. "That's the third relic."

Lena had known. She'd known since the flooded chapel, since she'd read the fragment of the rite aloud and watched Odile's face go carefully blank at the third line. But knowing a thing and hearing it said aloud were different animals, and the second one bit.

"Whose blood?" she asked, though her tongue already knew. Her tongue always knew first. It was the curse and the gift of her trade.

Odile didn't answer immediately. She knelt by the water's edge and rinsed her hands, a priest's habit worn into her body so deep it survived disgrace, exile, twenty years of silence. When she spoke, she spoke to the water.

"The rite was written for kings. Salt for the earth they were sworn to. Bread for the people they were sworn to feed. Blood for the line itself — the covenant made flesh. It has to be royal blood, freely given. Taken blood poisons the working. Bought blood thins it to nothing. It must be *given*, which means the giver must understand what they're giving."

"And there are no royals left," Lena said. "Except the one wearing the crown. And he's not giving anything."

"No."

"So whose blood?"

Odile turned then, and the candlelight did something unkind to her face, hollowing her cheeks, deepening the shadows around eyes that had seen an order burn. "You know whose."

* * *

They argued about it for two days. Or rather, Lena argued and Odile endured, which was its own kind of argument, the kind where one person throws stones and the other is a wall.

"It's not my blood," Lena said, pacing the narrow walkway above the black water. "I'm a kitchen servant. I taste soup. My grandmother sold charms out of a basket. There's no crown anywhere in my family tree unless someone was stealing firewood from the palace orchard."

"Tasters weren't servants." Odile sat cross-legged before the glass dish as if patience could be practiced into mastery. "Not originally. You've heard this."

"I've heard you say it. I don't believe it applies to me."

"Your immunity isn't chemistry. I told you what it is. A blessing, old enough that the families who carried it forgot it was a blessing and started calling it a talent. The Vhalen court stopped keeping records of taster lines two hundred years ago because the Edict of Ash made such records evidence. But the blessing doesn't care about paperwork, Lena. Blood remembers what archives burn."

"Then let the blood remember somewhere else. Find another taster. There are dozens in the palace—"

"There are dozens of *tasters*. How many of them have tasted poison and lived? How many of them have felt the broth look back?" Odile's voice stayed level, but something under it vibrated, a wire pulled tight. "How many of them has a god spoken to through their dead grandmother?"

Lena stopped pacing. The water below was perfectly still, and her reflection hung in it upside down, a woman suspended over darkness, which felt about right.

"That wasn't a blessing," she said quietly. "What happened at the shrine. That thing used her voice. It wanted something. Gods don't give gifts, they make trades — you taught me that yourself."

"Yes." For a moment Odile looked away, toward the dark mouth of the tunnel that led up toward the Nightmarket ruins, and Lena saw her go somewhere else — to a fire, maybe, or a prayer that went unanswered, or worse, answered. Then she came back. "And I will not pretend the price of this rite is small. That's exactly why it can't be anyone but you. The blood must be given by someone who knows what the gods cost. A princess raised on flattery would offer her blood the way she offers her hand at a ball. You'd offer yours knowing precisely what you're losing."

"What am I losing, exactly?" Lena folded her arms. "Say it plainly. You owe me plain words."

Odile rose. She crossed the walkway and stood close, close enough that Lena could smell the mineral cold of the cistern on her robes, and took Lena's right hand, palm up, and turned it over with a scholar's gentleness.

"A blessing is a shape," she said. "It holds your blood in a particular form — the form that lets you carry poison and messages and prayers without being destroyed by them. That's what immunity *is*. Not armor. Capacity. The rite will pour that capacity out. All of it." Her thumb pressed the center of Lena's palm, where five years of silver tray-heat had left calluses over the older, invisible mark. "After tomorrow, you'll be able to be poisoned like anyone else. You'll taste soup and nothing more. If a god speaks near you, you'll hear it the way ordinary people hear it — dimly, or not at all. Whatever your grandmother's line kept safe for however many generations, it ends with you, poured into a dish, spent on a dead king."

The cistern dripped. Somewhere far off, water found water.

"That's all?" Lena heard herself say, and hated the flippancy, knew it for what it was — a hand reaching for a railing over a long drop. "I lose my job?"

"You lose the only inheritance you have ever had," Odile said, "and you choose to lose it, which is the one thing inheritance never asks of us. That's why it works. That's why taken blood fails and bought blood fails. The rite runs on consent the way a lamp runs on oil." She released Lena's hand. "And I won't lie to you: there may be more. Old rites are honest about the small costs and silent about the large ones. I've read every surviving fragment. None of them record a taster dying on the third relic. But none of them record much of anything anymore. We're reconstructing a house from its ashes, and some rooms we'll only learn by walking into them."

* * *

She didn't sleep that night. She climbed instead, up through the tunnels Odile had mapped over years of hiding, up past the roots of the city, until she came out through a broken grate into the graveyard behind the Shrine of Drowned Saints, where the tide had long ago swallowed the lower crypts and the headstones leaned like tired dancers.

Her grandmother's grave wasn't here. Her grandmother had died in the provinces, in a village whose name Lena had to work to remember, and there was no grave at all because the parish had burned the bodies of charm-sellers even when the charms were only knots against headache. But graveyards weren't for the dead anyway. They were for the living, and Lena needed somewhere the living weren't.

She sat on a leaning stone and pulled her knees up and looked at her hands in the moonlight.

Five years. Five years of tasting. She tried to count what the immunity had actually been worth, itemized like a ledger, because Maren the steward had taught her — without ever meaning to — that fear shrinks when you write it down.

It had kept her alive through four assassination attempts she'd intercepted and one she hadn't, the night the fish course came up from the kitchens laced with river-hemlock and she'd spat it into the napkin and drunk the antidote she kept sewn into her sleeve and said nothing, because saying something meant investigations, and investigations meant witch-hunters, and witch-hunters meant questions about how a kitchen girl survived hemlock. It had let her do the work. That was the whole of it. The blessing had let her do the work, and the work had let her stay in Vhalen, and staying in Vhalen had let her find the Nightmarket, and the Nightmarket had let her sell luck to dockworkers who needed it more than the palace needed its breakfast tasted.

And underneath all of it, unexamined, like a coin at the bottom of a well: the immunity had made her *safe*. Safe in a way no one else she loved had ever been. Her mother hadn't been safe. Her grandmother certainly hadn't been safe. Every charm-seller Sorrel had dragged out of the Nightmarket hadn't been safe. Lena had walked through a city that burned people like her, carrying the mark of the oldest magic in her veins, and the mark itself had been her disguise — because who suspects the food-taster? Who burns the king's own spoon?

If she poured it out, she would be like everyone else. Flammable. Poisonable. Ordinary.

A nightbird called from the drowned crypts, three notes, descending.

"You'd think," she said aloud to no one, to the bird, to whatever small gods haunted graveyards now that the big ones were waking, "that after everything, I'd be relieved. Just be a woman. Taste soup, get sick sometimes, live a small life."

The bird didn't answer. Small gods, apparently, kept banker's hours.

But the thought wouldn't leave, and she turned it over the way she turned a suspicious custard over the tongue, letting it sit, waiting for the bitterness to declare itself. Because it wasn't relief she felt, imagining the loss. It was grief. And grief, she had learned in the Nightmarket, selling mourning-knots to widows who couldn't afford coffins, was just love with nowhere to put itself.

She loved it. The strange inheritance. The half-forgotten blessing. The thing in her blood that remembered her grandmother's grandmother praying over a kettle, that had kept her alive, that had let a god speak to her in a dead woman's voice. She had never once thanked it. She had treated it like a tool, like a good knife, like the trick of reading a room — something she had, not something she *was*.

And now the choice was to spend it, and spending it felt like cutting off a hand, and the fact that it felt like that told her everything about how much she'd owed it and never paid.

"Freely given," she murmured. "That's the trick of it, isn't it. It was always freely given. Down the whole line. Someone chose, every generation, to keep passing it down. To keep the vessel open." She laughed, short and ugly. "And I'm the generation that gets asked to close it."

The moon moved. The tide came in below the graveyard, filling the drowned crypts with a sound like slow breathing.

Somewhere south of here, an army was marching on the free cities, and a thing wearing a dead king was preparing to drink the prayers of thousands. Somewhere beneath her feet, a disgraced priestess was copying fragments by candlelight, reconstructing a rite from ash. Somewhere in the palace, a steward was keeping a private ledger of a monarch who never blinked. None of them could give what the rite required. Only the vessel could pour out the vessel.

Lena looked at her palms a long time. Then she stood, brushed the moss from her skirt, and went back down into the dark.

* * *

"I'll do it," she said.

Odile looked up from her papers. She didn't smile — Lena suspected Odile had retired smiling along with praying — but her shoulders came down half an inch, the way a wall comes down, brick by brick, when the siege lifts.

"Tonight?" Odile asked.

"Not tonight." Lena held up a hand. "Tomorrow. And there are conditions."

"Conditions."

"You said the blood must be given by someone who understands what they're giving. Fine. So I want to understand all of it. Every fragment you have. I want to read the rite myself, start to finish, including the parts you've been keeping in the back of the stack because they frightened you. I'll know which ones those are. You get a particular look."

"Lena—"

"I'm not finished." Her voice came out steadier than she expected, and she decided to ride it. "Second condition. If the rite asks for more than the blessing — if it starts taking things you didn't warn me about — you stop it. You break the circle, you spill the salt, whatever the emergency word is. Swear it. Swear it on whatever you still pray to, even if the answer is nothing."

Odile was quiet for a moment. In the candlelight her hands, folded over the papers, looked suddenly old — ink-stained, cracked at the knuckles, a scribe's hands and a survivor's.

"On my order," she said at last. "On the ashes of the Sisters of the Quiet Water. If it turns, I end it, whatever it costs the working. That I swear."

"Good." Lena exhaled. "Third condition."

"You're enjoying this."

"I'm terrified," Lena said, "and terror makes me bossy. Third condition: nobody crowns anybody. Not me, not a restored Aldric, not some cousin they dig out of a provincial attic. This rite unbinds a dead man. That's all it does. The moment anyone starts talking about what comes *after* the unbinding as though it involves a throne, I walk out and take my blood home."

Something moved across Odile's face — not quite a smile, but the ghost of one, the memory of a smile. "Agreed. Though I suspect you'll find the world less interested in thrones than you fear, once the gods come home."

"The world is always interested in thrones. That's the problem." Lena sat down across from her, beside the black glass dish, and looked at it properly for the first time — shallow, empty, patient. A thing built to hold what a person could not take back. "Show me the fragments. All of them. Start with the part you've been hiding."

Odile held her gaze for a moment longer, then reached beneath the main stack of papers and drew out three pages gone soft with age and damp, their edges browned as if they'd once been near a fire. Which, Lena thought, they probably had.

"The third relic," Odile said, laying them flat, "is called in the old texts the *Covenant Poured Out*. And there's a passage here — the one I kept back — that the sisters argued about for a hundred years before the Edict ended the argument permanently." Her finger traced a line of faded script. "It says the blood must be given *knowing*, and it says something else. Listen." She read aloud, her voice dropping into the cadence of liturgy despite herself:

"*Let the vessel consider: what is poured may not be poured back. But what is given in love is not lost; it is planted. The taster who gives her blood gives her protection to the rite, and the rite gives it onward — to the king unbound, to the city, to the small gods waking in the gutters. Immunity is a kind of love, and love spent is not spent.*"

The cistern was silent. Even the drips seemed to pause.

"That's a comfort," Lena said finally, "written by someone who wasn't holding the knife."

"Probably," Odile agreed. "Most comforts are."

Lena picked up the page. The script swam, then steadied. She thought of her grandmother's basket of knots and threads, of dockworkers pressing coins into her palm for luck they'd already stopped believing in, of the moment in the Nightmarket when a stranger's prayer had been answered out loud and everyone had frozen like deer — wonder and terror in the same breath, because the gods coming home meant the world was about to become a place where actions had weight again.

She thought of the dead king, kneeling somewhere inside his own stolen body, five years deep, waiting.

"All right," she said, and set the page down, and rolled up her sleeve. "Tomorrow at moonrise. Get the salt and the bread ready tonight. And Odile—" she paused, hand on the black glass dish, feeling how cold it was, colder than the water, colder than stone, "—when it's done, if I'm still me, remind me to be sad about it for a while. Properly. I've gotten too good at skipping that part."

"I can do that," Odile said softly. "Grief is the one rite I never forgot how to perform."

* * *

Moonrise came gray through the cistern grates, thin as skimmed milk.

Lena knelt where the cloth was spread, salt to her left, bread to her right, the black glass dish centered before her like a small dark mouth. Odile stood over her with the oldest of the fragments, reading in a voice pitched low, and the words were strange and older than the Edict, older than the palace, words that had been spoken over coronations when coronations meant covenant and not conquest.

Lena opened the little knife she'd brought from her taster's kit — the one she'd used a thousand times to pare fruit for the king's table, never once suspecting — and held her hand over the dish.

*Freely given,* she thought. *Knowing. In love, even.*

She thought of her grandmother, and of a dove on a drowned shrine, and of a dead man who deserved his name back.

She cut.

The blood fell black in the gray light, drop by drop, and the moment the first one struck the glass, every candle in the cistern went out at once —

— and far above them, through miles of stone and street and sleeping city, in the throne room of the palace, something that wore a king lifted its head.
