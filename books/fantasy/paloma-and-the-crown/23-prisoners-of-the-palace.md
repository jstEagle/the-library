## 23. Prisoners of the Palace

They came for Maren at the hour of the second ledger.

She knew the hour well — the quiet stretch after the midday accounts were posted and before the evening requisitions began, when the steward's office smelled of ink and cold tallow and nothing in the palace moved except dust. It was the only hour of her day without a bell attached to it, and she had learned, over nineteen years of service, to guard it the way a miser guards a coin.

So when the knock came — three raps, evenly spaced, too formal for a servant and too soft for a soldier — she set down her pen with deliberate care, blotted the line she'd been writing, and squared the page against the desk's edge before she answered.

"Enter."

Two witch-hunters in grey. Not Sorrel. She noted that first, the way she noted everything: automatically, in columns. A captain named Vess, whom she had seen twice at court functions and who had once complimented her on the accuracy of the wine inventories. And behind him a younger one whose name she didn't know, carrying a document case chained to his wrist.

"Steward Maren," Vess said. "You'll come with us."

"Shall I bring my keys?" she asked. "The evening stores won't issue themselves."

Vess blinked. He had expected, she supposed, trembling. Or pleading. Or at minimum the particular wet-eyed dignity that noblewomen produced when arrested, which was its own kind of performance and one Maren had never learned because she had never been noble enough to need it.

"No," he said. "You won't be needing anything."

"That," said Maren, rising and smoothing her apron, "remains to be seen."

* * *

The interrogation rooms were in the Undercroft, beneath the east wing, which Maren knew because as steward she had signed for their candles. This was the detail she fixed upon as they walked her down the stairs — not fear, not prayer, but the small absurd fact that she had personally approved an order of four hundred tallow dips per quarter for this corridor and had always thought it excessive. Now, watching the flames gutter in wall sconces as they passed, she revised the figure upward. The drafts down here were terrible. Someone should speak to the masons.

The room they gave her had a table, two chairs, and a window slit so high and narrow that no light worth having came through it. They sat her facing the wall. She considered mentioning that the chair wobbled, decided against it, and folded her hands.

Vess unchained the document case himself. The younger hunter stood by the door with the particular stillness of a man who has been told to watch and is taking the instruction seriously.

"You understand why you're here," Vess said.

"I understand you've brought me to a room I pay to heat," Maren said. "Beyond that, you'll have to tell me."

Something flickered across his face — irritation, or possibly respect; after nineteen years of stewards' reports she had learned the two were often indistinguishable. He laid a paper on the table. Turned it to face her.

It was a copy of a requisition. Her own hand, her own seal: *Bread flour, coronation grade, twelve measures, issued to the palace ovens, night of the 14th.* Below it, in someone else's ink, a note: *Coronation-grade flour has not been milled in Vhalen since the king's recovery. No rite requires it. Explain.*

"I can," Maren said. "Someone requested bread."

"Who?"

"A request was submitted through proper channels. I processed it through proper channels. That is what channels are for."

"The request bears no author."

"Then it bears a clerk's error, and clerks' errors are corrected by clerks, not by witch-hunters." She let a breath of weariness into her voice, measured to the drop. "Captain, do you know how many requisitions pass through this office in a year? Eleven thousand, four hundred and six last count. I sign perhaps sixty of them myself on a slow day. If your office intends to interrogate me over each one, I'll want additional chairs, and better ones than these."

Vess studied her. She studied the table. Somewhere above them, faintly, the bells rang the third hour, and she counted them out of habit, and hated herself a little for how much comfort the counting gave.

"The Nightmarket raid," Vess said. "Three weeks ago. We seized ledgers from six charm-sellers. One of them contains entries in a cipher we've partially broken. Payments. Deliveries. A route that enters the palace through the service gate on the north wall." He paused. "The north gate's log shows deliveries authorized under your office."

"Everything enters the palace under my office," Maren said. "That is what an office is."

"The cipher uses a mark. Three lines crossed by a fourth."

Maren kept her face still. Inwardly, coldly, she performed the arithmetic she had already done once, alone, at night, and had hoped never to do again: the mark was hers. An old private shorthand from her clerk days, a way of flagging entries for review. Lena must have copied it from a discarded draft of some delivery note — or Odile had told her to use it, knowing the palace would read it as internal correspondence, knowing it would point here, to the one person inside the walls who could be trusted to hold the line when the pointing started.

Or — and this possibility she examined with the same dispassion she applied to everything — they had simply used her, the way institutions used everyone eventually, and she had walked into it with her eyes open because the alternative was letting the crown eat the world.

Either way. The mark was hers. The answer was hers to give.

"Yes," she said. "That's my verification mark. I use it on documents requiring personal follow-up."

"And did these require follow-up?"

"I couldn't say without seeing the entries."

"You've just admitted the mark is yours."

"I've admitted the mark is mine. Whether it was correctly applied is another question entirely, and one your broken cipher is not equipped to answer." She unfolded her hands and refolded them, a small motion, deliberate, the gesture of a woman settling in for a long afternoon. "Captain, I'll make you an offer, since you've gone to the trouble of candles. Bring me the ledger. I will review every entry bearing my mark and tell you which are genuine and which are forged. I have kept this palace's accounts for nineteen years. I know my own hand the way you know your own sword. That is more than your cryptographers can offer you, and it will take me less time."

Vess looked at her for a long moment.

"You're offering to help the investigation."

"I'm offering to help the truth," Maren said. "I'm told that's what your office serves."

* * *

They did not bring her the ledger. Of course they didn't. Instead they brought her questions, in shifts, for hours: the north gate logs, the oven schedules, the names of every servant rostered to the kitchens on the 14th, the disposition of the salt stores, whether she had ever heard the word *unbinding*, whether she had ever corresponded with any person formerly of the Order of the Drowned Shrine, whether she prayed.

To that last one she answered, truthfully, "Not in some years," and watched the younger hunter write it down as though it meant something, and supposed that to him it did.

Between shifts they left her alone in the room, and Maren discovered something about herself that surprised her: she was good at this. Not the defiance — defiance was cheap, and she had seen too many proud people break on it to romanticize the word. What she was good at was the other thing. The paperwork of resistance. Every question they asked, she answered precisely, completely, and uselessly — each true statement surrounded by so much accurate context that the truth drowned in it. Yes, she had issued flour on the 14th; also on the 13th, the 15th, and forty-one other days that season, and here was the full schedule if they wished to check. Yes, she knew the name Odile; the Order of the Drowned Shrine had held shrine-tithes administered through the palace until the purge twenty years ago, and here were the account numbers, still open, still accruing interest, a bureaucratic ghost that made both hunters stare at her as though the ledger itself were haunted.

She could not protect Lena by lying. Liars were caught; she had watched it happen from the safe side of a desk her whole life. But she could bury them. She could make the truth so heavy, so dense with corroborating detail, that digging through it would take the witch-hunters a month — and the army, she knew, was not a month away. She had read the dispatches. She had filed the dispatches. Whatever else happened in this room, the free cities needed the crown's attention pointed inward, tangled, delayed.

So she buried them. Entry by entry. Line by line. It was, she reflected somewhere in the sixth hour, the only kind of courage she had ever possessed, and it turned out to be the kind the moment required: not the sword's courage, not the martyr's, but the clerk's — the stubborn, unglamorous refusal to let a record be simplified.

Near midnight they brought her bread and water. Coronation-grade, she noticed, from the crumb. Someone in the ovens was still baking it. She ate slowly and thought of Lena passing her in the corridor with flour on her sleeves, and of the look they had exchanged — the look she still could not fully explain even to herself, the silent accounting between two women who had each decided, separately and without discussion, that the thing wearing the king's face was not the king.

"You're smiling," Vess said, from the doorway.

"I'm resting my face," Maren said. "It's been a long day."

* * *

On the second day they changed tactics, which told her they were getting desperate, which told her the clock was running faster than she'd estimated.

They brought in a confessor. Not a priest — those had been purged — but one of the Edict's licensed examiners, a soft-voiced man named Callow who spoke to her about duty and the burden of secrets and the mercy of confession, and who never once raised his voice, and who was, Maren recognized with professional admiration, running a script older than the Edict itself: isolate, exhaust, offer absolution.

"Callow," she said, when he paused for breath. "Do you file your own reports, or does someone transcribe for you?"

He blinked. "I... dictate to a clerk."

"Then you'll appreciate my position. Nineteen years of dictation. You develop a horror of imprecision." She straightened in the wobbling chair. "You asked whether I believe the king is well. Here is my precise answer, and I'll thank you to record it verbatim. I believe His Majesty's health is a matter for His Majesty's physicians, of whom there are currently none listed on the household rolls — a staffing gap I flagged in a memorandum eleven months ago, reference number—" and she recited it, from memory, because of course she remembered the reference number, "—and received no reply. If the crown wishes to investigate the absence of royal physicians, I will gladly assist. If it wishes to investigate bread, I refer you to my previous testimony, pages nine through fourteen."

Callow left within the hour. Vess returned, looking like a man who had been chewing the same piece of gristle for two days.

"You're stonewalling."

"I'm documenting," Maren said. "There's a difference, though I understand it's subtle from the outside."

"Steward." He leaned on the table, close enough that she could see the exhaustion under his eyes, and something else under the exhaustion — the same crack she had begun, in recent months, to glimpse in Captain Sorrel, the hairline fracture of a man whose certainties were being eaten from within. "Five years ago the king nearly died. Then he recovered — miraculously, they said. Since then he has signed fewer documents than any monarch in living memory. He has appeared in public less. His handwriting has changed. I have spent two years building files on charm-sellers, and every trail, every single one, goes cold either at the Nightmarket or—" his jaw tightened, "—or here. Inside. And now the free cities are burning their beacons, and the army marches, and His Majesty reviews troop maps at midnight alone, and I am sitting in a cellar interrogating a bookkeeper about flour."

"Then you understand my difficulty," Maren said quietly. "Because everything you've just described is in my records too, Captain. Every anomaly. Every gap. I keep a private register of irregularities — it's what a competent steward does. And I will tell you what I told myself, the night I finally wrote the dates side by side and saw what they made." She held his gaze. "There are questions this palace cannot survive answering out loud. Not yet. Ask me again when the army reaches the river, and see what I say then."

For a moment — one long, suspended moment — Vess said nothing, and Maren thought: *there, that's the seam, that's where he'll tear or hold.*

Then boots in the corridor. The younger hunter opened the door, saluted, and handed Vess a dispatch tube bearing the black wax of the war office.

Vess broke the seal and read. Maren watched his face the way she watched all faces: in columns. Confusion first. Then a coldness that wasn't discipline. Then, briefly, unmistakably, fear.

"What is it?" she asked.

He looked at her — really looked, the interrogation forgotten, the script abandoned — and for a moment he was not a witch-hunter and she was not a prisoner. They were two servants of a dead man's household, reading the weather.

"The host reached the Graywater," he said. "Last night. And His Majesty has ordered..." He stopped. Started again, as though the words needed to be handled with gloves. "His Majesty has ordered a rite conducted at the riverbank. A mass rite. He's requisitioned every licensed examiner in the capital — Callow among them — and every confiscated charm and relic in our vaults. Everything we've seized in five years of the Edict. He wants it all sent to the river."

"To burn?" Maren asked.

"He doesn't say burn." Vess's voice had gone very flat. "He says *to feed*."

Above them, muffled by stone and earth, the palace bells began to ring — not the hour, not the quarter, but the emergency peal, the one that summoned every officer in the household, the one Maren had heard only twice in nineteen years and never for anything good.

Vess stood. For a moment his hand rested on the table, near hers, almost touching.

"When this is over," he said, "one way or the other — I'm coming back to ask you what you know. All of it. And I think you're going to tell me."

"Bring the ledger," Maren said. "And a better chair."

He went. The door locked. The bells went on ringing, and Maren sat alone in the candlelight with her hands folded and her heart going like a hammer, doing the only thing she knew how to do with terror, which was to file it — *emergency peal, third occurrence, cause pending* — and wait, and count, and plan what she would say when the door opened again, and to whom, and exactly how much of the truth the world could survive hearing at once.
