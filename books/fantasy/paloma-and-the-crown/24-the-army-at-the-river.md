## 24. The Army at the River

The river was called the Serein, and it did not care who knelt on its banks.

The thing wearing King Aldric stood at the water's edge in the grey hour before dawn and watched forty thousand men make camp along the far side of the ford. Fires bloomed out of the dark like a rash. Somewhere behind them, the baggage train stretched back eleven miles along the coast road — grain carts, siege engines broken down into their parts, iron for a hundred thousand more boots. It had taken four years to build this. It had taken one night, five years ago, to take the body that now commanded it.

The body was tired. The thing inside it was not.

It flexed the king's hands — still strong, still a soldier's hands, though the knuckles had begun to ache in ways they hadn't the first year. Bodies wore. That was the trouble with them. It had learned to manage: oiling the joints at night with preparations the physicians believed were for the king's dignity rather than his preservation, drinking the tinctures that kept the flesh from noticing its own age. But there was only so much a glove could do about its hand.

Soon, though. Soon it would not need the glove at all.

"Your Majesty." General Voss came up the bank at a jog, breath steaming, and dropped to one knee in the mud without waiting for permission. A good officer. He had never once looked too long at the king's face, which was either loyalty or wisdom or the animal instinct that keeps prey from staring at predators. "The scouts report the free cities have fortified the crossing. Palisades on both banks. Ballistae on the eastern tower."

"How many men?"

"Perhaps six thousand. Militia, mostly. Guildsmen with spears." Voss hesitated. "And priests, sire. They've put priests on the walls. They're — singing."

The crown-spirit turned its borrowed eyes toward the far shore. In the pre-dawn murk it could just make out the palisade lines, the squat tower, and above them, faint as heat-shimmer, the sound. Not words. Not yet. But the cities had woken to what was coming down the coast road, and they were doing the one thing the Edict of Ash had spent two centuries training them not to do.

They were praying. Out loud. In numbers.

It felt the prayers the way a man feels weather change against his skin. Thin threads of it drifting across the water — fear, mostly, and defiance braided through the fear, and underneath both a stubborn, ordinary love of home. Six thousand people's worth of small desperate hope, rising off the far bank like smoke.

Its mouth — Aldric's mouth — watered.

"Sire?" Voss said carefully. "Your orders?"

"The army crosses at first light," the crown-spirit said. "But not before I've spoken with the river."

Voss blinked. "With — "

"You heard me, General." It let a little of the cold come up through the king's voice, enough to close the subject. "See to your columns."

* * *

It walked alone along the bank while the camp woke behind it, past the pickets, past the last of the fires, until the noise of forty thousand men was a distant surf and it stood in a stand of drowned willows where the Serein spread wide and slow over gravel shoals.

Here it stopped, and here it let itself be seen.

The crown woke fully. It always hurt the body when that happened — Aldric's scalp had bled for the first month, five years ago, until the flesh learned to accept what sat upon it — but pain was an old negotiation now, settled and dull. The spirit unfolded outward through the crown's seven points, through the iron filigree beneath the gold, through the old old metal at the heart of it that had been a god's own brow-band once, before hunger taught it new shapes. It reached down into the water. Into the mud. Into the long memory of the riverbed.

Rivers remembered everything that drowned in them. That was useful.

*Little brother,* it said to the Serein, in the language that predated speech, the grammar of pressure and current and cold. *You are old and you are patient and no one has prayed to you properly in two hundred years.*

The river said nothing. Rivers rarely did. But it listened; everything listened, now, because something had changed in the world's skin three months ago and the old channels were running again. The Edict of Ash had starved the small gods down to embers, and embers could be ignored. But the Edict was failing. Someone had unstoppered the wells. Prayers were getting through again, all across Vhalen, all across the coast — small prayers, clumsy prayers, dockworkers' charms and kitchen-girls' knots and the frightened mutterings of soldiers who had been told all their lives that nothing was listening and had begun, very quietly, to test the theory.

The crown-spirit had felt the first of them wake like a struck bell and had known, in that instant, exactly what was happening and exactly what it meant.

It meant food. It meant a feast such as the world had not seen since before the Edict. And it meant danger, because somewhere in that spreading tide of returning divinity was whatever had pulled the stopper — and things that could pull stoppers could, given time, learn to close doors.

*Little brother,* it said again, and this time it made an offer. *Two hundred years ago they built their temples on your banks and threw their offerings in your water, and then the ash-priests came and burned the shrines and told the people you were a story. I remember. I was worshipped then too.* This was true, in the way that a leech remembers being loved. *Help me cross, and when I take the cities, I will give you their prayers. Every prayer spoken within sight of your water, for a hundred years. You will drink until you are a god again.*

The river considered. The current slowed over the shoals, thinking.

*And if I refuse?*

*Then I cross anyway,* the crown-spirit said pleasantly, *and I take the cities anyway, and I burn every shrine on your banks so thoroughly that no one ever speaks your name again, and you go back to being gravel.* It let the truth of this settle into the water like silt. *I am not asking, little brother. I am being polite, because politeness is cheaper than force. But I want you to understand the shape of the choice.*

The river was old, and rivers are practical. After a long moment the current shifted, subtly, drawing back from the eastern shallows where the ballista tower stood and gathering instead toward the western ford, deepening the water there to a brown roaring weight that would swallow the first ranks of any assault — unless the assault came with the river's blessing.

*You may pass,* the Serein said at last. *For the price we spoke of.*

*Done,* said the crown-spirit, and it sealed the bargain the old way, letting a thread of its own substance bleed out through the crown and down into the water, where it sank glittering into the gravel like a coin paid into a palm. It felt the river take it, and swallow, and grow by one small hungry degree.

Everything it touched grew hungrier. It had stopped pretending otherwise, even to itself, a long time ago.

* * *

By full light the host was forming on the west bank in column of march, and the crown-spirit stood on the low rise above the ford with Voss and the staff around it, watching six thousand militia watch them from behind their palisades.

"They'll break," Voss said. "Militia always breaks. First volley, maybe second."

"Probably," the crown-spirit agreed. It was not watching the militia. It was watching the priests on the tower — nine of them now, robed in the colors of half a dozen different orders, most of those orders officially extinct. They had linked hands. They were singing something with a rhythm to it, a working rhythm, a wall-building rhythm, and the crown-spirit could see the song doing what songs like that did: laying a lattice across the crossing, thin as frost, stubborn as frost.

A prayer-wall. Crude, improvised, assembled from guild-priests and hedge-blessers and probably a few outright criminals — but real. Three months ago it would have been impossible. Three months ago there had been nothing left in the world strong enough to answer.

Someone had fed the wells. It turned the thought over again, the way it had turned it over every day since the Nightmarket raid reports had started coming in wrong — raids finding empty stalls, empty cellars, charm-sellers gone to ground ahead of the hunters every time, as if the city itself were warning them. As if something under Vhalen were organizing what should have been scattered, terrified, alone.

A taster, the reports kept circling back to. A palace taster with an impossible constitution and a grandmother the archives swore had never existed. Small. Ignorable. Except that nothing else explained the pattern, and the crown-spirit had lived long enough to know that patterns were how gods announced themselves before they knew they were gods.

It would deal with her. There was time. There would be time after the river, after the cities, after the harvest.

"Sound the advance," it said.

The horns went down the line, and forty thousand men began to move toward the water, and the Serein rose up to meet them — not in fury, not in flood, but in the smooth complicit way of a thing keeping a bargain, parting its currents around the columns, holding the footing firm on stones that should have rolled. The militia on the far bank saw the river make way for the enemy and something went out of them; the crown-spirit watched it go, watched the prayer-song on the tower falter and scramble and begin again higher and more desperate, and it drank the new flavor of that desperation as the host splashed forward, rank on rank, banners dripping, the whole enormous machine of the conquest rolling across the last obstacle between it and the richest praying-ground left in the world.

The first arrows fell. The first militiamen broke and ran. It was all entirely ordinary, and entirely beside the point.

Because the crown-spirit had not brought this army across the Serein merely to take six towns of guildsmen and fishermen. The cities were the granary, yes — millions of souls, generations of quiet unlicensed prayer soaked into their foundations like smoke into rafters. But prayer was only fuel. What it needed now, what it had crossed a kingdom to assemble, was the rite.

Tonight, when the camps were set and the towers taken, it would build the working on the riverbank. Seven circles of salt and iron. Forty thousand witnesses, because massed belief lowered resistance the way massed bodies lower a door. And at the center, the crown itself, opened at last to its full circumference, reaching out along every prayer-thread in the region — not answering them, never answering them, but drinking. Every whispered fear in the besieged towns. Every blessing muttered over a barricade. Every desperate bargain the free cities struck with their newly returned gods. It would take the prayers before the gods could spend them, strip the divinity out like marrow from bone, and leave the cities praying into silence.

And the small gods waking all down the coast — the dockside spirits, the hearth-lingers, the drowned shrine-things stirring in their waters — they would feel their worshipers' voices go out one by one, and they would starve in the open, mid-resurrection, before they were strong enough to matter.

It had been starved once. Two centuries of ash and edict, sealed in iron beneath a mountain while its worshipers forgot its name. It knew precisely what it intended for them, and it intended it without malice, the way winter intends snow.

Voss rode up through the shallows, mud to the thigh, flushed with the ease of the crossing. "The east tower's ours, sire. The priests jumped. Most of them." He paused. "There's — one thing. A dispatch came in overnight from the capital. From Captain Sorrel's office."

"Sorrel is at the capital?"

"He was recalled ten days ago. The dispatch says—" Voss frowned at the paper, visibly deciding how much of it to believe. "It says the witch-hunters have lost their prisoner. The taster. She escaped custody during transfer, with assistance from persons unknown inside the palace itself. Sorrel's warrant is — the seal says it's void, sire. Burned. His file is closed."

For a moment the crown-spirit stood very still among the noise of the victorious army, water streaming off the king's boots, and let the report settle into it.

Sorrel. Its best hound, its coldest instrument, the man who had burned his way through the Nightmarket districts with such faithful thoroughness. Lost. Turned, or fled, or worse — converted. And the taster loose somewhere in a country that was, at this moment, learning to pray again.

Patterns. Doors. Stoppers.

"General," it said, "when the camps are set, I will require the army drawn up on the north bank in full parade order. Every man. Every banner."

"Sire? There's no one to parade for. The cities are behind us—"

"There is someone," the crown-spirit said, looking south along the river road, toward the distant smudge of the free cities and the faint bright lattice of their singing, and beyond them, further than any scout could ride, toward a capital where a dead man's name was being remembered by people who had no right to know it. "There is everyone. Tonight the whole world watches us kneel."

It smiled with the king's mouth, and the smile did not reach anything behind the eyes, because there was nothing behind the eyes except appetite and arithmetic, and both had just been revised upward.

The rite would begin at moonrise. And somewhere between here and Vhalen, it suspected, a girl with an immune tongue was already walking toward it — and it found, examining itself honestly, that it was not afraid.

It was hungry. And for the first time in two hundred years, the whole table was set.
