## 25. Nightmarket Rising

The Nightmarket had burned for three days before the crown let it stop burning.

Lena knew this because she could see the smoke from the palace kitchens, a gray smudge on the southern sky like a thumbprint on clean glass. She'd stood at that window with her tray of tasting spoons and watched it, and no one had thought it strange — servants were always looking out windows, always wanting something they couldn't have. She had wanted the smoke to mean nothing. She had wanted Odile to be far away by then, deep in the cisterns where fire couldn't follow.

Now, nine days later, she walked into the ashes to find out what was left.

She came at dusk, hood up, in the clothes of a fishwife rather than a palace servant, because a taster walking into a burned market would be noticed even if the witch-hunters hadn't left watchers behind. They had left watchers. She counted two of them at the mouth of Tanner's Row, standing with the particular stillness of men who had been told to stand somewhere boring and were resenting it. She gave them the wide, dull-eyed courtesy of a woman who had walked past soldiers her whole life and learned that invisibility was a skill you practiced, not a gift you were born with.

Past them, the market opened up like a wound.

She had known it would be bad. Knowing didn't help. The Nightmarket had been the one place in Vhalen where she was entirely herself — not the king's taster, not the quiet woman with the careful face, but Lena who sold luck-threads to dockworkers and could tell you which knot would hold against a drowning and which only against despair. Five years of nights lived under those awnings. Now the awnings were black lace draped over skeletons of stalls, and the cobbles were furred with ash, and the smell was the smell she'd grown up knowing from her grandmother's stories about purges: wet char and something sweeter underneath that she made herself not name.

*They burned the charms first,* she thought, turning in a slow circle. *Then the sellers.*

Except the sellers had mostly run. That was the thing about people who sold illegal blessings for copper — they were very good at seeing trouble coming. It was their entire trade. Trouble was the product; hope was just how they packaged it.

So why did the ash feel empty in a way that wasn't grief?

She knelt and pressed her palm flat against a cobblestone, the way Odile had taught her, the way you'd check a horse's hoof for heat. Her immunity hummed the way it always did now — that low tide-sound beneath her skin, the blessing in her blood that was never chemistry, never had been. And through it, faint as a voice through a wall, she felt—

*Something moving. Under the ash. Under the city.*

Not dead. Not gone. *Waiting.*

"Oh," Lena said softly, to no one. "Oh, you clever, stubborn—"

"Careful," said a voice behind her. "Kneeling in ash like that, people will think you're praying."

She came up fast, knife already in her hand — old habit, older than the blessing — and then stopped, because the man in the ruined doorway of what had been Marisol's knot-stall was Bramble, the old net-mender who bought more luck than any man in Vhalen and needed less of it than anyone she'd ever met. He was soot to the elbows. There was a burn scar fresh along his jaw. He was grinning.

"You're supposed to be dead," Lena said. "Half this market is supposed to be dead. Sorrel's hunters took names off a ledger, Bramble. Your name was three lines below mine."

"My name," Bramble said, "is whatever I write it as that week. That's the first thing you ever taught me, girl, though I don't expect you remember. You said: *never sell a charm under the same name twice.*" He jerked his chin down the ruined row. "Come on. You'll want to see this. And put the knife away — you'll want your hands free."

* * *

They went down.

That was the part that undid her, later, when she let herself feel it: they went *down*. Past the burned stalls, past the cellar doors with their new scorch marks, down stairs that smelled of river silt instead of smoke, into the old salt-cellars and smugglers' vaults and drowned chapels that riddled the ground beneath the Nightmarket like the holes in a honeycomb. The city above had been built on the city below and had spent two hundred years pretending otherwise. The Edict of Ash had done the same thing to the gods. Lena was beginning to understand that these were not separate facts.

Bramble lifted a hanging oilcloth and the noise hit her.

Voices. Dozens of voices. Hundreds.

The vault beyond was enormous — an old tithe-cellar, she guessed, from the days when grain was owed to shrines instead of kings — and it was full. Not of refugees. She'd seen refugee crowds, huddled and silent, all the noise pressed down under fear. This crowd was *working*. Long tables ran the length of the cellar, lit by lamps with hoods angled to keep the light off the ceiling, and at the tables sat the charm-sellers of Vhalen: Marisol with her knots, her hands still quick despite the bandage on one finger; the twins from the luck-thread stall whose names Lena had never actually known, only their matching laughs; fat old Hesk with his prayer-bones; a dozen faces she knew and fifty she didn't, and every table was covered in the raw material of small miracles.

Thread. Salt. Bread crumbs swept from chapel floors. Ash — real ash, blessed ash, saved from shrines the Edict had missed. Knots half-tied. Knots finished and stacked in rows like loaves.

"What is this?" Lena breathed.

"This," said a voice she knew better than her own heartbeat lately, "is what happens when you burn a market and forget that markets are just the roof."

Odile came around the end of the nearest table, and Lena's chest did something complicated and painful, because the priestess looked *terrible* — thinner, grayer, a bruise going yellow along her temple — and also more alive than Lena had ever seen her. Twenty years of hiding had worn Odile down to a wick. This was the flame.

"They raided us on a Tuesday," Odile said, taking Lena's arm and steering her down the aisle between tables, talking low and fast. "Sorrel's people. Good raid, thorough, they got the ledger and they got six of ours who were too slow or too proud to run. But they burned the *stalls*, Lena. The roof. And everything under the roof walked down here and kept working." Her grip tightened. "Do you know why?"

"Because—" Lena started.

"Because for five years we sold charms," Odile said, "and the charms were *lies*. Pretty lies, kind lies, placebos with knots in them. We both knew it. You knew it better than anyone — you felt it every time a customer came back and said the luck-thread worked, and you smiled and took their copper and hated yourself a little." She stopped at the head table, where a map of Vhalen was pinned down with salt-cellars and bread-knives. "And then the gods woke up. And every lie we ever sold became true."

Down the tables, Marisol looked up from her knot-work. Around her, others looked too. Word moved through the cellar like current through water, and the work slowed, and then stopped, and then every face in that vast dim room turned toward Lena Vasquez, royal food-taster, illegal charm-seller, blessed vessel, and waited.

Lena's mouth went dry. She had faced down poisoners and witch-hunters and a god wearing her king, and none of it had prepared her for being *looked at* by people who hoped she'd say something worth hearing.

"I'm not your leader," she said. It came out rougher than she meant. "I want to be clear about that. I'm a taster. I taste soup. Two months ago my biggest problem was whether the king's cook was watering the wine."

A laugh went through the room — not mocking, warm, the laugh of people who recognized the shape of the joke because they'd all been living inside it.

"But," Lena said, and the word pulled the laughter back in like a tide, "you're not here because of me. You're here because of *them*." She spread her hands at the tables, at the threads and salts and knots. "Every one of those things used to be a story we told scared people. And now —" She closed her eyes, reached out through the blessing in her blood the way Odile had taught her, the way she'd practiced alone in her narrow servant's bed with her hand on her own sternum. The hum rose. And she felt them: the charms. All of them. Every knot on every table carried a faint warmth now, a seed-crack of attention, like eggs with something breathing inside.

She opened her eyes. "Now they're *listening*."

The silence lasted three heartbeats. Then Hesk, fat old Hesk, slammed his prayer-bones down on his table and said, in a voice that cracked in the middle, "Told you. Told you all. My bones haven't lied once since the solstice."

* * *

They showed her the rest, and the rest kept getting bigger.

There was a second vault, and a third, connected by smugglers' tunnels that the Nightmarket had maintained for generations without ever writing down, because writing things down was how ledgers happened and ledgers were how witch-hunters happened. In the second vault, dockworkers — actual dockworkers, men with rope scars and salt in their beards — were learning to tie protective knots themselves, Marisol shouting corrections over their shoulders. In the third, women from the fish-gut rows sat in a circle around a chipped bowl of shrine-salt, passing it hand to hand, each whispering into it a blessing for someone specific: a son on a boat, a daughter in a bad marriage, a neighbor's fevered child. The bowl hummed. Lena could hear it from the doorway without even reaching for her blood's tide-sound.

"We're not making army charms," Odile said quietly, watching the circle. "I want you to understand that, because I know what you're afraid of. After the dockworker — after Tomas—" She said the name carefully, respectfully. The man whose wish had been granted too literally, who had died smiling with his wish come true wrapped around him like a shroud. "After that, we sat down and made rules. Small blessings. Named blessings. Nothing open-ended, nothing that says *give me* — only things that say *protect this*, *hold that*, *carry her safely*. A god answering a named protection has to be gentle. There's no way to protect someone too well."

"There's always a way to do anything too well," Lena said. "You taught me that too."

"Yes." Odile's mouth twisted. "But there's a difference between a knife and a flood, and we're handing out knives. Knives can be held." She turned to face Lena fully, and in the lamplight her eyes were fierce and exhausted and young. "Here's what I need you to hear, because you're the one who's going to have to say it to them, eventually, when they ask. We have maybe four thousand charms ready. Working charms. True charms. Scattered through the lower city in cellars and boats and mattress seams. And the usurper's host is on the road with — what did Maren's dispatches say? Forty thousand? Fifty? And a mass rite planned at the river to drink every prayer between here and the free cities."

"I know the numbers."

"So do they." Odile nodded at the workers, the tables, the humming bowl. "And they're not running. Do you know why? Because for twenty years the Edict told them their prayers were crimes. And then the gods came back, and the first thing the crown did was burn the only place the prayers lived. So now every knot they tie is a sentence being answered. Every charm is a *no* spoken back to the throne." She exhaled. "Hope isn't a feeling, Lena. I thought it was, most of my life. It's a practice. It's hands doing work while the heart objects. Look at them. Look at the hands."

Lena looked.

Marisol teaching a boy of maybe twelve to tie a sailor's guardian-knot, guiding his fingers, saying *no, cross it under, the god reads intention but the knot reads grammar*. The twins, laughing again, threading luck through fishhooks by the dozen. Hesk sorting prayer-bones into piles with the gravity of a general deploying troops. A woman Lena didn't know, old enough to be her grandmother, dipping her thumb in blessed ash and marking a doorway lintel with a sign so old that Odile had gasped the first time she saw it drawn.

Four thousand small illegal blessings. Against an army and a hungry god.

It should have felt hopeless. Standing there in the lamplight, ash still in her hair from the world above, Lena waited for the hopelessness to arrive, the way it always did, cold and familiar as an old coat.

It didn't come.

What came instead was the hum — the tide-sound in her blood rising until she could hear, distinctly, without reaching, the sound of four thousand small gods breathing in the dark under Vhalen. Not roaring. Not commanding. Just *breathing*. Waking up the way spring wakes up, one thaw at a time, patient because patience was all small things had ever had.

Her grandmother had prayed at a drowned shrine to a god small enough to fit in a bowl of broth. That god remembered her grandmother. That god had helped her, at the price of being remembered. And now here were hundreds of grandmothers' gods, and fishermen's gods, and gods of knots and thresholds and gutting knives, all of them stirring because a few thousand frightened people had refused to stop whispering to them.

"The free cities think the capital is lost," Lena said slowly. "Maren's dispatches say the envoys get turned away at the gate. The beacons are burning and nobody answers them. Everyone with power has decided this war is already over."

"Everyone with power," Odile said, "has never been down here."

* * *

Lena worked until her fingers ached.

She couldn't help herself. She sat down next to Marisol and learned the sailor's knot properly — grammar and all — and tied forty of them, and then moved to the salt table and learned how much a blessing weighed, literally, in the hand, when it was true: almost nothing, and somehow more than the bowl could hold. A girl beside her, no older than Lena had been when she first came to the palace, showed her how to whisper a name into ash without letting the name escape past your teeth, because a name spoken aloud could be overheard by things other than gods.

Somewhere in the third hour, she realized she was crying, quietly, without stopping her hands, and that nobody was looking at her strangely, because half the room was doing the same thing.

This was what the palace had starved her of for five years, she understood. Not safety — the palace had safety, of a poisonous kind. Not purpose — she'd had purpose, measured in spoonfuls and swallowed. What she'd been starving for was this: a room full of people building something together that none of them could build alone, each knot useless by itself and unbreakable in company. Her loneliness had never been about having no one. It had been about having no *we*.

"You're smiling," Bramble said, dropping onto the bench across from her with two cups of something hot and terrible. "First time I've seen it off-duty."

"Don't ruin it."

"Wouldn't dream of it." He pushed a cup at her. "Though you should know there's a complication. There's always a complication."

Lena drank. It was chicory and burnt barley and possibly a small crime. "Tell me."

"Bramble!" Marisol called from down the table, sharp. "Don't you dare dump the whole night on her before she's finished the knot she's holding."

"She's finished it. Look at her hands. She's been finished for ten minutes and enjoying herself too much to admit it." He leaned in, and his grin faded into something graver. "Two things. One: there are watchers topside, like you saw, but they're not Sorrel's regulars. New faces. Palace livery under plain cloaks. Someone sent them personally, and personal means the usurper knows the market mattered even burned."

Lena set down the cup. "And two?"

"Two." Bramble glanced toward the tunnel mouth, then back, and lowered his voice to almost nothing. "Someone's been asking after you. Down here. Quietly. Not the hunters — they don't ask, they kick doors. This one asks. An old woman, some say. A child, some say. Comes into the camps where our runners sleep, sits down, buys a bowl of whatever's cooking, and asks one question: *where does the taster go when she leaves the palace?*" His eyes held hers. "Nobody's answered. Nobody will. But whoever it is knows you're coming here, Lena. Knew it before you did, maybe. And tonight, an hour before you arrived, it left something at the top of the stairs. Addressed to you."

He reached into his coat and set a folded scrap of oilcloth on the table between them.

Lena looked at it for a long moment. Through the blessing in her blood, the scrap was silent — no god-hum, no warmth, nothing. Whatever it was, it was only paper and ink and human hands.

She unfolded it.

One line, written in a clerk's precise, cramped, unmistakable hand — a hand she'd seen on palace requisitions and private ledgers and, once, trembling, on a page hidden inside a recipe book:

*I know what the king's letters stopped mid-sentence. I have the fragments of his name. Come alone, or don't come at all. — M.*

Lena read it twice. Then she looked up at Odile across the crowded, humming, hopeful dark, and found the priestess already watching her, already reading her face the way Lena read soup.

"Maren," Lena said. "The steward wants to meet."

Odile was quiet a moment. Then, slowly, she began to smile — tired, fierce, the smile of a woman watching a second candle catch from the first.

"Then the palace," she said, "is cracking from the inside too. Go carefully, Lena. But *go*."
