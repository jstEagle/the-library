## 26. Sorrel's Choice

The order came at dusk, written in the king's own hand, which meant it was written in something that wore the king's hand like a glove.

*Burn what remains of the Nightmarket. No survivors. No records. Ash to ash.*

Captain Sorrel read it twice, standing in the doorway of his office while the lamplighter made his rounds below the window. He had burned warrants before. He had burned a tannery in Saltrow with four charm-sellers inside because they refused to come out, and he had told himself for six years that they had chosen their own deaths, that the law was the law, that a man who did not enforce the Edict of Ash was a man who let poison into the well.

He was running out of things to tell himself.

"You're not reading it," said Corporal Vess from the desk, sharpening her knife with the patience of someone who had done it ten thousand times and would do it ten thousand more. "You're staring through it."

"I'm reading it."

"Then why do you look like a man reading his own death warrant?"

He folded the order along its crease. The paper was good paper — crown stock, heavy enough to feel like a promise. Everything the crown touched felt like a promise right up until it didn't.

"Because I might be," he said.

Vess stopped sharpening. In eleven years she had never questioned an order, and she wasn't questioning this one; she was questioning him, which was worse, or better, depending on how the night went.

"Say it," she said. "Whatever it is. Say it out loud so I know if I'm following you into treason."

Sorrel looked at the files stacked on his shelf — the witch-hunters' secret archive, twenty years of interrogations, confessions, names, marks, ledgers. Names like Lena Vasquez. Ledgers with her mark on them, copied out in his own careful hand. He had spent months building the case against her and then, in one afternoon in the palace corridor, had watched her talk circles around him with nothing but a steady voice and the truth worn thin as a coin, and he had walked away knowing — *knowing*, the way you know weather coming — that she was not the thing the Edict existed to burn.

What she was, he still didn't fully understand. But the old priestess in the cistern, the dead gods waking up all over the city like lamps being relit one by one, the dockworker who had died smiling with his wish granted too literally — none of it fit the world the Edict had built. And a law that only works by refusing to look at half the world isn't a law. It's a blindfold.

"Vess," he said, "when we raided the Nightmarket, what did we find?"

"Charms. Knots, threads, dried herbs. A few prayer-scraps." She shrugged. "Rubbish, mostly. Dangerous rubbish."

"Did any of it hurt anyone?"

She opened her mouth. Closed it. He watched her run the ledger of her career the way he'd been running his all week, and watched her arrive at the same sum.

"No," she said slowly. "We found a boy selling luck-threads to dockworkers so their lines wouldn't snap. We found an old woman charging two coppers for knots against fever. We burned a stall and arrested a girl whose worst crime was arithmetic." Her jaw tightened. "And then the gods started answering prayers again, and the crown sent us after *that* too, and now we're ordered to burn people for praying."

"The gods aren't the point. The point is order—"

"Don't give me the speech, Captain. I've heard you give it. I've given it myself." She set down the knife. "I'm asking whether you believe it anymore."

The lamplighter passed below. Sorrel thought about the interrogation room, and the girl in the chair, confessing to charms she'd sold to keep children alive through winter, protecting someone she wouldn't name even when the sentence was already in his eyes. He had hesitated. She had seen him hesitate. That hesitation had been living in his chest ever since, growing like something watered.

He went to the shelf. He took down the archive — every file, every ledger copy, every name the witch-hunters had gathered in secret for twenty years — and set it on the desk between them.

"Pack your kit," he said. "We're going to the Nightmarket."

"To burn it?"

"To warn it."

* * *

They went down through the city as the bells rang compline, past the shrine where someone had left bread and salt on the step — bread and salt, the old offering, done openly now, done in daylight almost, the whole city sliding toward something no one had voted for. Sorrel kept his cloak hooded and his warrant-case under his arm, and felt the weight of it like a second spine.

The Nightmarket had been burned once already, weeks ago, on his orders. The stalls were black skeletons. But the alleys behind them were full of light again — small lights, shuttered lanterns, cookfires, and figures moving between them with the quick economy of people who have learned to live in ruins. Charm-sellers. Survivors. The girl from the interrogation, Tally, directing traffic with a stick of charcoal behind each ear. Dockworkers carrying crates of knot-thread like munitions.

And near the old fountain, talking to a circle of maybe thirty people, Lena Vasquez.

She saw him before anyone else did. Of course she did. A taster learns to notice the person in the room who wants something, and Sorrel wanted something so badly it must have radiated off him like heat.

The circle went silent. Tally put her hand into her coat, where Sorrel knew there was a knife and probably three charms he'd have confiscated a month ago.

"I'm alone," he said, and then, because Vess stepped out of the dark beside him: "Nearly alone."

"You brought a corporal," Lena said. "That's either reinforcement or hostage-taking."

"It's testimony." He set the warrant-case on the lip of the fountain. Opened it. Took out the order in the king's hand and held it up so the lantern-light caught the seal. "*Burn what remains. No survivors.* It comes tonight. Two hundred hunters, fire-pots, the river gates sealed so nobody runs. I was given command because I've never failed an order yet."

"And you're here instead," Lena said. Not a question. Her voice was careful, the way a taster's voice is careful around an unknown dish.

"I'm here instead."

Tally spat at his feet. "You burned my mother's stall."

"Yes."

"You took my brother."

"He's in Ashcell Block C. Alive as of last week's roll." Sorrel held the man's glare. There was nothing he could say that would make it right, and he was done pretending there was. "I can't unburn anything. I can't unarrest anyone. What I can do is this—" he turned the case toward the crowd, showing the files, hundreds of them, twenty years of the hunt laid bare, "every name we hold, every mark, every ledger, every confession extracted and filed. Every place we watch. Every code we use. If you're going to survive what comes tonight, you need to know exactly how we hunt. So: ask me anything. I'll answer all of it. Then decide whether to trust me, and I'll understand if the answer is no."

Silence. The kind of silence that has weight and temperature.

Lena came forward and lifted one file. Read the name on it. Set it down. Lifted another. He realized, watching her, that she was looking for her own, and found it, and looked at it a long moment — her whole double life reduced to his handwriting — before closing it gently, like shutting a door on a sleeping child.

"This one," she said quietly, "you wrote yourself."

"Yes."

"Why bring it? Why show me the worst of what you are?"

"Because redemption isn't a feeling," Sorrel said. "It's a payment. And this is everything I have to pay with."

Lena looked at him for a long time. Behind her, the crowd murmured — dockworkers, charm-sellers, a priestess's messenger in cistern-damp robes. Somewhere a baby cried and was hushed.

"The hunters come within the hour," she said finally. "You're going to stand with us."

"I'm going to stand with you."

"They'll call you a traitor. They'll hunt you the way you hunted us."

"I know how the hunting goes," he said. "I wrote the manual."

Something that was almost a smile crossed her face, gone as fast as it came. She turned to the crowd. "Two hundred hunters," she called. "Fire-pots, sealed gates. Here's what they don't know: their captain is ours now, and he's brought us their whole book." She raised her voice. "Get the children to the cistern. Get the thread and the knots distributed. Everyone who carries a charm, carry it *openly* tonight — the gods are awake, and it's time the crown learned what that costs."

The crowd broke into motion. And in the middle of it, Sorrel knelt at the fountain, took the order in the king's hand, and struck flint to it until the good crown paper caught and curled and went to ash in the water.

It was a small fire. It was the largest thing he had ever done.

* * *

They came at moonrise, two hundred strong, torches bobbing down the ruined market road like a procession of angry stars. Sorrel stood at the head of the Nightmarket defenders — forty dockworkers with boat-hooks, thirty charm-bearers with knots in their fists, Vess at his shoulder wearing her hunter's blacks with the badge torn off.

The lead hunter raised a horn. "By order of the Crown! Surrender the charm-sellers and burn nothing but what we tell you to burn!"

"That's funny," Vess muttered. "I was about to say the same to them."

"HOLD!" Sorrel stepped forward into the torchlight, bareheaded, hands visible. "Captain Sorrel. Your commanding officer. Hear me before anyone strikes flame."

A ripple ran down the column. He saw faces he knew — Brannic from the Saltrow raid, young Pell who owed him his life from the tannery fire, old Hessa who had trained half the corps. Good people. People who enforced the law because they believed, the way he had believed, that the alternative was poison in the well.

"I've read the files," he called. "All of them. Twenty years of them. You know what's in there? Knots against fever. Luck-threads for fishermen. An old woman's prayers. And orders — like tonight's order — signed by a king who hasn't blinked in five years." He let that land. He saw it land; he saw men glance at each other. "I stood in the throne room. I've looked at His Majesty. Ask yourself why the Edict burns grandmothers while the crown marches an army on free cities that have done nothing but pray differently."

"TRAITOR!" someone shouted, and it was Brannic, and Sorrel loved him like a brother and did not step back.

"If you burn this market," Sorrel said, "you'll burn it through me first. And I'll tell you what I told them—" he pointed back at the crowd, at Lena, at Tally, at the knots and threads glinting like a field of small stubborn stars, "—the law that needs a blindfold isn't a law. Go home. Or stay, and see what wakes up when you light the fire. Because I've felt it. The gods are answering again, gentlemen, and they remember *everything* — including who lit which pyre."

For a long moment, nothing moved except the flames.

Then old Hessa lowered her torch. Just lowered it, to her side, like a woman setting down a tool at the end of a shift. And Pell followed. And then a dozen, and then half the column, torches drooping one by one like flowers at dusk — while the rest stood rigid, furious, leaderless.

From the dark of the crowd, Lena walked forward to stand beside Sorrel. She carried no weapon. She carried a bowl of bread and salt, and she held it out toward the wavering line of hunters like an offering, like a dare, like a door.

"Eat," she said. "Or don't. But nobody burns tonight unless you choose it — and if you choose it, you should know the god of this fountain is awake, and she was a grandmother once, and she remembers every face that ever prayed at her water. Including yours, Captain Brannic. You were a boy. You asked her for rain."

Brannic's torch shook.

In the silence, far away across the rooftops toward the palace, a bell began to ring — not the compline bell, not any bell on any schedule Sorrel knew. It rang fast and urgent and wrong, and every head turned toward the sound.

Lena went very still. "That's the palace alarm," she breathed. "Something's happened at the palace."

Beside her, Sorrel felt the night tilt. Whatever was coming next, the choice had already been made — his, hers, Hessa's, all of theirs — paid for in ash and standing in the open where everyone could see it.

"Then we'd best go find out together," he said, and turned toward the ringing bells.
