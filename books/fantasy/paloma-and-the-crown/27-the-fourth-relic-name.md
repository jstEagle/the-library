## 27. The Fourth Relic: Name

Odile had spent twenty years learning to hate paper.

Not the way scholars hated it — not the boredom of it, the dust and the tedium. She hated it the way a burn victim hates fire. Every document in Vhalen that touched her order's history was a weapon someone else had already swung. The Edict of Ash hadn't just outlawed charms and shrines; it had outlawed memory itself, systematically, page by page, in a purge so thorough that Odile could stand in the ruins of her own temple and not remember which alcove had held which saint. The Inquisition hadn't burned only people. It had burned *records* first, because a faith without its books is a body without bones, and boneless things are easy to sweep into gutters.

So when Lena came down into the cistern with salt crusted white on her sleeves and bread wrapped in oilcloth against her heart, and said, "There's one relic left," Odile already knew what it would be. She had known since the drowned shrine. She had known, maybe, since the day she was defrocked.

"Name," she said, before Lena could say it.

Lena blinked. "How did you—"

"Because it always is." Odile set down the lamp she'd been trimming and looked at the girl across the water-dark room, at this twenty-something taster with her grandmother's blessing in her blood and her grandmother's stubbornness in her jaw. "Salt is easy. Salt is everywhere, if you know where the old chapels flooded. Bread is harder but it's *bread* — ovens still bake, even palace ovens, even for dead kings. Blood you can carry in your own veins. But a rite of unbinding needs the true name of the bound, and names—" she laughed, and it came out ugly, "—names are the first thing they take."

"The king's name isn't taken," Lena said. "It's on coins. It's on proclamations. King Aldric of Vhalen, defender of the—"

"That's not his name." Odile watched the words land. Watched Lena go still the way prey goes still, the way Odile herself had gone still once, twenty years ago, when an old priestess told her the same thing about her own order's founder. "That's what the crown answers to. That's what the usurper lets the world call him, because a king called Aldric is a shape the crown-spirit can wear comfortably. The man underneath — the man who was born, who was baptized, whose mother whispered something over his cradle — that name was erased five years ago. Or four. However long ago the 'miraculous recovery' was." She turned back to the lamp. "You cannot unbind a thing with the name it wears. You need the name it *is*."

"How do you erase a name?" Lena asked quietly. "From everything? Every record, every register, every gravestone?"

"You don't do it all at once. You do it the way rot does its work. A decree here. A corrected ledger there. Scribes paid to copy documents *without* certain lines. Archives reorganized so the wrong shelf goes missing. And then, when enough time has passed, everyone simply remembers the version on the coins, because the coins are everywhere and the truth is nowhere." Odile trimmed the wick with a fingernail. "I watched them do it to my goddess. Her name was Vessia. Do you know what they renamed her in the official histories? *The Unnamed One.* They made the erasure itself her title. It was very elegant. I hated it completely."

Lena sat down on the wet stone beside her, careful of her skirts, careful of the bread. "Maren," she said.

"What about her?"

"Maren keeps records. Private ones. She showed me once — no, she didn't show me, I saw her writing, late, in the steward's office. Little notes. Dates. Observations." Lena's voice dropped. "She's been keeping a record of the king for years. Of everything wrong with him. The not-blinking. The not-aging. And if she noticed those things, she might have noticed others. She's been in that palace longer than the usurper has. She knew Aldric before he was a glove."

Odile looked at her for a long moment.

"You understand what you're asking," she said. "You're asking a woman who has spent five years surviving by being invisible to hand us the one document that makes her the most dangerous person in Vhalen. Names are treason now. Possessing the king's true name isn't heresy — it's regicide with ink on your fingers."

"I'm not asking yet," Lena said. "I'm telling you where I think the last relic is. There's a difference."

"There isn't," Odile said. "But fine. There's a difference."

* * *

Getting word to Maren took three days and two intermediaries and a charm Odile hadn't woven since the purge — a listener-knot, braided from canal-reed and black thread, that carried a spoken message along waterlines the way rumor carries along barroom benches. It was illegal in six separate ways. She enjoyed every one of them.

The answer came back on the fourth night, folded inside a fishmonger's invoice: a single line in Maren's small, upright clerk's hand.

*St. Verren's Day. After compline. Bring nothing. Ask nothing. I will show you what I have.*

Odile read it twice and felt something in her chest she refused to name, because naming things gave them power and she had sworn off giving power to anything except, lately, the cause of unseating a god.

"She'll come," Lena said, reading over her shoulder.

"She'll come," Odile agreed. "And she's terrified. Look at the phrasing. *Ask nothing.* She doesn't want to be complicit in conversation. She wants to be complicit in silence. That's a woman who has rehearsed this moment for years and still hopes she can pretend afterward that it never happened."

"Can she?"

"No," Odile said. "But let her hope. Hope is load-bearing. Take it out of a person too fast and the whole structure comes down on you."

* * *

St. Verren's was a minor feast nobody kept anymore — a day for clerks, supposedly, honoring some long-dead archivist whom the church had absorbed and the Edict had forgotten. Maren chose it, Odile suspected, because it was the one day of the year the palace archive ran half-empty, the junior scribes given leave, the senior ones drunk on sacramental wine.

They met in the records annex beneath the west wing, a low brick vault that smelled of vellum and mouse and cold stone. Maren stood waiting among the shelves with a shuttered lantern and the posture of a woman attending her own trial. Up close, Odile could see what five years of watching a dead king walk around in had done to her: a thinness around the eyes, a habit of listening past the person in front of her to the corridor beyond.

"You're the priestess," Maren said. Not a question.

"I'm a woman who used to be a priestess. The crown decided the second part was a crime."

"And the taster sent you because she's being watched."

"Lena is being watched, followed, and formally conscripted by Captain Sorrel, yes. So I come instead. Fewer questions if I'm caught, and worse ones." Odile smiled without warmth. "You have something for us."

Maren's hands went to the lantern, then stopped, then went again — a stammer of motion, a woman fighting her own training. "I have *fragments*," she said. "I need you to understand that. If I had the whole name I would have burned it years ago, or died holding it, one of the two. What I have is worse than nothing and better than nothing, and I have spent five years not knowing which."

She led them down the aisle between shelves to a section Odile recognized on sight: the accession rolls. Royal genealogy. The official spine of the dynasty, every birth and baptism and coronation recorded in copperplate hands going back three centuries.

Except.

Maren pulled a volume and opened it to a page near the end, and Odile felt her breath go shallow.

There was a gap in the text. Not a torn page — a *repaired* one. Someone had cut a strip from the margin and pasted fresh paper over the register lines, and the paste had yellowed, and beneath the patch, faint as a watermark, the ghost of older ink bled through: the descender of a letter, the crossbar of another. A name, scraped and covered, still whispering itself through the repair like a voice through a wall.

"They did it while he was still alive?" Lena breathed — she had come after all, of course she had, Odile should have known better than to think a warning would hold her.

"Three days after the recovery," Maren said. "I was the one sent to fetch the volume for correction. I was told the previous entry contained a scribal error." Her voice was perfectly level, which is how Odile knew it was costing her everything. "I read the entry before they patched it. I was a junior clerk. Nobody thought to stop me. Nobody thought a junior clerk would *remember*."

"And you remembered," Odile said.

"I remembered." Maren closed the volume gently, the way you close a coffin. "And then I began collecting. Quietly. Across five years. Every place the old name survived — a marginal note, a private letter, a household account, a servant's testimony taken during some inquiry and misfiled. I couldn't keep any of it together; a single packet would hang me. So I scattered it. False covers. Wrong shelves. A fragment sewn into the binding of a tax roll. A scrap pasted under the lining of a hatbox in the queen's — in the *late* queen's stored effects. Pages disguised as recipes. I have spent five years hiding pieces of a man's name inside paperwork so boring that no one will ever read it." She looked up, and there it was at last, the crack in the clerk's composure, grief showing through like light under a door. "Do you know what that's like? To spend half a decade protecting a dead man from being forgotten by making him impossible to find?"

"Yes," Odile said simply. "That's what my whole order became. We hid our goddess in hymns with the theology stripped out. We hid her in children's counting songs. *One for sorrow, two for mirth* — that was Vessia, once, before they ground her down to magpie superstition. Archives aren't the opposite of resistance, madam steward. Archives *are* resistance. Every tyrant knows it. It's why they burn libraries before they burn people."

Maren stared at her. Something shifted behind her eyes — recognition, terrible and relieving, the look of a woman discovering she has been at war alone for years and suddenly has comrades.

"Then you'll want these," she said.

* * *

She wouldn't let them take the fragments out of the palace. Too risky, she said, moving them all at once; the collection had survived five years by staying scattered, and it would survive the transfer the same way. Instead she walked them through the annex and the adjoining rooms for two hours, pointing — never touching, never lingering — to the hiding places one by one, reciting each fragment from memory as she went, because of course she had memorized all of them, because of course she had, she was Maren, she was the kind of woman who kept a private record of a king's failure to blink and filed it under laundry inventories.

A christening date, recovered from a midwife's fee record: *the third hour past midnight, in the month of storms.*

A godparent's name, from a letter fragment: *his aunt Seraive stood witness, and the child was given to the keeping of—* and there the page tore.

A nickname, from a kitchen account, absurd and humanizing: *extra honey-cakes for the little lord who would not sleep,* annotated in a dead cook's hand, *he answers to—* and the rest eaten by damp.

Piece by piece, the man assembled himself out of scraps. Not a king. A baby born in a storm-hour. A sleepless child bribed with honey. A boy with an aunt who loved him enough to stand witness. Odile found her throat tightening and resented it. This was the danger of archives, the reason the Inquisition feared them more than armies: they made the dead *specific*. A name on a coin is a symbol you can hate. A name in a midwife's ledger is a person, and persons are much harder to feed to a crown-spirit.

"The liturgical name," Odile said at last. "That's what we actually need. Not the birth-name alone — the name spoken over him at consecration, the one that ties him to the rite. The usurper erased the civil records, but the church records—"

"Were purged first," Maren said. "I've looked for six years. The cathedral registers were confiscated the week after the recovery. Burned publicly, in the Coronation Square, as instruments of superstition." Her mouth twisted. "He burned the church records to protect the church's god. There's a joke in that, and I refuse to make it."

"But there's a copy," Lena said.

They both looked at her.

"There's always a copy," Lena said. "You taught me that. You — both of you, everything you've said tonight. Tyrants burn the original and miss the copy, because copies live in boring places. Where would a cathedral register get copied?"

Maren was already shaking her head, then slowing, then stopping. Odile watched the clerk's mind work, watch it move down the same corridors it had walked for years, checking doors.

"Chancery," Maren said slowly. "Every consecration required a chancery countersignature. Duplicate registry, kept in the Office of Seals, for tax purposes." Her laugh was short and bitter. "Tax purposes. The gods' names survive because of *tax purposes*."

"Is it still there?"

"It was there when I was transferred out of that office eleven years ago. Whether it survived the purge—" She stopped. Straightened. And Odile saw her make the decision, saw it happen in the set of her shoulders, the way a soldier's decision shows before the orders do. "I'll check tomorrow. Slowly. Through the requisition files, so the query looks routine. If the duplicate exists, I can sign it out under pretext of a boundary dispute. There's always a boundary dispute."

"Maren." Odile caught her sleeve as she turned. "If anyone sees the request—"

"Then I am an old woman with an interest in property law," Maren said, "and nothing else. I have been nothing else for five years, priestess. I am rather good at it." She paused at the aisle's end, lantern lowered, face half-shadow. "But if it's there — if I bring you that page — then it's real, isn't it? All of it. The rite happens. We try to save him."

"We try to free him," Odile corrected gently. "Saving may not be what he wants. It usually isn't, at the end."

Maren held her gaze a moment, and nodded once, a clerk acknowledging receipt.

"St. Verren's Day next year," she said, "you can buy me a drink and tell me whether it was worth it."

Then she was gone, footsteps precise on the stair, and Odile stood in the dark among the shelves with a taster-girl and the ghost-assembled bones of a dead king's name, and felt — for the first time in twenty years — something adjacent to hope.

* * *

They were three streets from the cistern when the bells started.

Wrong bells. Not the hour, not the alarm-fire pattern — the *hunt* pattern, the triple-strike that Odile had heard only twice in her life and had hoped never to hear again: witch-hunters mobilized, districts sealed, a warrant in motion.

Lena grabbed her arm. "That's the palace quarter's bell."

"It's more than that." Odile listened, counting the strikes, reading the sequence the way she'd once read psalms. "Sealed districts. Plural. They're sweeping toward the river wards." She looked at Lena, and saw the same realization land on the girl's face that had landed on hers.

The river wards. The Office of Seals sat in the river wards.

"Requisition files," Odile said grimly. "Boundary disputes. *Nothing else.*"

"She signed it out today," Lena whispered. "She must have. She found it, and she signed it out, and someone saw."

Above the rooftops, the hunt-bells struck their third round, and somewhere ahead, in the direction of the chancery offices, a glow was beginning to rise against the clouds that had nothing to do with lamplight.

Odile started to run.
