## 28. March of the Small Gods

The road to Vhalen had been a pilgrim's road once. You could still see it in the stones — the worn hollows where knees had knelt, the way-shrines every mile or so, their niches empty now, scrubbed of icons by a century of Edict men with chisels and pious faces.

We filled them again as we went.

It started small, because everything we did started small. On the first morning out of the Nightmarket ruins, a cooper named Brannic set a luck-thread around his daughter's wrist and asked the road itself to keep her feet from turning. He didn't expect an answer. None of us did, not really — we'd spent our whole lives asking into a silence that swallowed prayers like a well swallows coins.

The road answered. A soft hum under our boots, like a plucked string somewhere beneath the earth, and the girl's blistered heel stopped hurting by noon.

By evening there were forty people at that shrine, and Brannic was teaching anyone who'd listen how to tie the knot properly, three loops and a breath between them, *because the breath is the offering, see, the thread is just the handle.*

By the third day there were four hundred of us, and I stopped counting after that because counting felt like the kind of thing that could jinx it, and also because there were simply too many to count. Dockworkers and dyers, laundresses and lamplighters, a whole street of cobblers who'd walked out of the Tanners' Quarter with their awls still in their aprons. Old women who remembered the old words from their grandmothers' kitchens, whispered for decades in cellars while the witch-hunters' boots crossed the floorboards overhead. Children who didn't remember anything, who were learning the names of small gods the way you learn the names of new friends — eagerly, greedily, without the fear that had kept the rest of us quiet.

And carried among us, wrapped in linen and oilcloth and one memorable case of quilted sailcloth, the minor gods themselves. The ones Odile had gathered, and the ones that had gathered *us* — because that was the truth nobody said out loud on the first night and everybody said out loud by the fourth: we hadn't organized this. We'd been organized. Called. A god of thresholds woke in a doorframe in the Salt Quarter and half the neighborhood followed it out of the city before they'd decided to go. A river-god rose out of the Vhal near the fish market and moved upstream beside us for a day, a long silver suggestion in the water, before it slid off toward its own business.

"They're not following you," Odile told me as we walked. She'd gone gray-faced and silent through the first two days, and I'd thought it was grief for the burned market, or fear of what waited ahead. It took me longer than it should have to understand it was neither. "You understand that, don't you? They're not following *you*. They're following the praying."

"Isn't that the same thing?"

"No." She said it flatly, the way she said all her important things. "That's exactly the mistake the crown made. One vessel, one voice, one throat through which every prayer must pass. That's how you get a thing like *that*—" she nodded back south, toward the capital, toward the palace, toward whatever wore the king's face these days, "—starving on a throne. The gods don't want a mouthpiece. They want a *choir*."

I looked down the column behind us. Four hundred people, then six hundred, then — by the time we passed the boundary stone where the king's writ officially began and the free cities' stubborn independence ended — more than a thousand, strung out along the old pilgrim road like beads on a wire. And everywhere we passed, the road lit up.

That's the only way I can describe it, and I was there, and I still can't do better than that. Prayers sparked along the way like fire in dry grass. Someone would kneel at an empty shrine with a crust of bread and a handful of salt, and the air above the stone would warm and shimmer, and a quarter mile later someone else would feel it in their chest — a tug, a warmth, a name rising to their lips that they hadn't known they knew — and they'd kneel too, and light another patch. By nightfall you could stand on a hill and watch it move across the valley: little blooms of gold-green light, kindling and fading and kindling again, a slow fire working its way toward Vhalen.

Sorrel rode at the column's edge and did not look at the lights. That was his way of handling it. He'd burned his warrant in front of his own hunters eleven days ago — I hadn't seen it, but Odile had described it to me twice, savoring every detail, and I suspected she'd described it to him too, just to watch his ears go red — and he'd brought the witch-hunters' files with him, six crates of them, which turned out to be the most valuable cargo in the entire march.

Because the files had names.

Every charm-seller the Edict had ever arrested, every knot-tier and bone-caster and hedge-healer, recorded with meticulous bureaucratic horror: name, district, offense, disposition. Executed. Imprisoned. Fled. And — this was the part that made Sorrel hand over the crates with something close to shame — *watched*. Hundreds of them marked not as threats but as assets, left in place, observed for years, because some clerk in the Office of Ash had understood what the captains never did: that the small gods couldn't be killed, only starved, and that the best way to keep them starved was to keep their practitioners frightened and isolated and alive.

"We can find them," Sorrel said, when he showed me the first crate. His voice was careful, the way it always was now, a man walking on ice he himself had cracked. "Every one who survived. Every one who fled to the free cities. They'll come, if we send word."

"Will they?" I said. "Come back to the city that burned them?"

"Some will come to fight. Some will come to forgive." He looked at the road, at the light blooming along it. "Some will come just to see if it's true. I would. If I'd spent thirty years being told the thing I loved was ash, and then saw it burning on the horizon — I'd walk toward it even if I expected to burn."

They came. Of course they came. Runners went out with copies of the file pages, and the runners came back with news of gray-haired women packing bundles, of free-city militias volunteering escorts, of shrines in towns we wouldn't reach for weeks already being swept clean and relit in anticipation. The fire ran ahead of us faster than we could walk. By the sixth day, villages along the road were setting out bread and water for the column without being asked. By the eighth, they were joining.

* * *

I want to record what it actually felt like, because the songs are going to get it wrong. They always do. The songs will say it was triumphant, and it was, but triumph is a cold word for it. What it felt like was *hunger ending*.

Every mile, another god woke up, and waking up after a century of the Edict apparently feels like the first meal after a famine, and the first thing a starving god does is *give thanks*. That sounds backwards. It isn't. They thanked the people who prayed, because the praying was the food, and they thanked in the only currency they had: small miracles, poured out recklessly, almost drunkenly.

A god of mending whose shrine stood in a ditch outside Ferrow's Mill fixed the axle of a broken supply cart and then, giddy, mended every torn cloak in the column, so that we walked into the next town looking like an army of tailors' dummies, seams glowing faintly gold. A hearth-god in a ruined way-station lit every cold fireplace within a bowshot and had to be gently persuaded — by Odile, in the exasperated tone of a woman talking down a drunk relative — to let some of them go out again. A little god of lost things, whose niche I found myself kneeling at one dusk without quite deciding to, returned to a laundress named Petra the ring she'd lost nineteen years ago, producing it from the pocket of a man standing three feet away, to the man's considerable alarm.

"That one," Odile said, watching the laundress weep over her ring, "is going to be a problem."

"A god of lost things?"

"A god of lost things with a *century of backlog*." But she was almost smiling when she said it, and I caught her later that night, sitting alone at the edge of the campfire-light with her hands open on her knees, palms up, in the old posture of receiving. She wasn't praying. Not yet. But she was holding her hands the way you hold them when you've remembered how.

She prayed on the eleventh night. I won't write down what she prayed or to whom, because that's hers, but I heard the moment it landed — a sound like a bell underwater, felt more than heard — and I saw her shoulders drop an inch, twenty years falling off them all at once, and I had to look away because some things are private even in the middle of a thousand people.

* * *

The dreams started on the ninth night.

I should have expected them. My blood was built for carrying messages between gods and kings; my grandmother's line, the tasters' line, blessed vessels all the way down. A thousand waking gods marching in one direction was bound to spill over into whatever channel was nearest, and I was, apparently, the widest channel on the road.

Each night, a different god visited. Not speaking, mostly — the small ones didn't speak so much as *arrive*, filling the dream with themselves the way a room fills with the smell of bread. The threshold god showed me every doorway in Vhalen, all at once, and I understood that it knew which doors would open for us and which would be barred, and that it was telling me, in its door-shaped way: *some will open. Not all.* The river-god gave me a dream of the palace cisterns running backward, water climbing the walls, and I woke with salt on my lips. The hearth-god dreamed me a throne room with a fire in it, and the fire was shaped like a man kneeling, and I woke crying without knowing why until I remembered Aldric, and then I cried a little more, on purpose, because he deserved the tears even if he'd asked for something stranger instead — to be remembered, not saved. I was working on the remembering. I did it every night now, deliberately, like a task: *King Aldric. Your name was King Aldric. You liked honey in your wine, your steward Maren says so in a letter she wrote you that you never got to answer. You were afraid of the dark as a boy. You were kind to kitchen boys.* I didn't know if any of it reached him. I did it anyway. Prayer, I was learning, is mostly doing the thing whether or not anyone receives it.

On the twelfth night, the big dream came, and it wasn't a small god at all.

I stood on the pilgrim road at night, except the road stretched farther than any real road, and the lights along it weren't scattered blooms anymore but a single unbroken river of gold running toward the capital. And above Vhalen hung a darkness shaped like a crown, vast, patient, drinking the light as fast as it flowed — and not worried. That was the worst part. In the dream I could feel its mood the way you feel weather, and it wasn't afraid of us. We were a meal arriving on schedule. It had eaten prayers for five years; it looked at our river of light the way a fat spider looks at a fly-heavy summer.

But underneath the darkness, deep under the city, something small and human-shaped was kneeling in the dark, and when I bent my attention toward it, it lifted its head, and though the dream gave it no face, I knew it was waiting for a name, and I woke with my own mouth already shaping the syllables — *Aldric, Aldric* — into the black tent of the tent, into the sleeping camp, into the ground itself.

Around me, softly, a hundred sleepers murmured in their dreams. And the ground hummed.

* * *

Maren met us at the ferry crossing on the fourteenth day.

I saw the boat come across the Vhal with the dawn mist still on it, and I saw the single figure standing in the bow with a ledger strapped to her chest like armor, and I knew her before I could make out her face, because Sorrel had described her — the steward who kept records the crown didn't know it needed kept — and because no one else in the world would cross a war-torn river alone at dawn carrying paperwork.

She stepped onto the mud of the far bank, straightened her coat, and addressed me with the crisp formality of a woman delivering a quarterly report.

"Lena Vasquez. Formerly royal food-taster, currently, I'm given to understand, a heretic of some prominence." She held out a leather folder, tied shut with plain cord. "The name-fragments. Complete, or very nearly. There's a gap in the middle years — the usurper was thorough — but Odile believes the rite can bridge it, provided the surrounding context is spoken correctly. I have memorized the context. I have memorized everything. I was arrested for it, interrogated for nine days, and escaped through a laundry chute, which I mention only so that history records the undignified manner of my departure." A pause. Her voice, for one sentence, lost its clerk's polish. "He answered none of my letters. Five years of letters. I need you to understand that I am not here for the cause. I am here for the correspondence."

"I know," I said. "I've been remembering him every night. Honey in his wine. Kind to kitchen boys."

Something in her face cracked, just slightly, the way good plaster cracks — cleanly, along a line that was always there. "Kind to kitchen boys," she repeated. "Yes. That's him. That's the part the crown couldn't digest, do you see? It ate his memories and that one kept coming back up, like a stone. I believe — I have *documented* — that it's why the rite failed the first time. You cannot wear a man who keeps insisting on being kind to kitchen boys."

Behind her, the mist was lifting off the river, and beyond the river the road climbed toward the last ridge before Vhalen, and along that ridge, as the sun came up, the way-shrines were already lighting — one, then five, then dozens, villagers and deserters and freed prisoners kneeling at them, the fire in the dry grass running ahead of us toward the city walls.

Odile came and stood beside me, and Sorrel on my other side, and behind us the column was waking, a thousand people rising and tying knots and breaking bread and saying names, the small gods stirring in their wrappings like birds in boxes.

"One day," Maren said, consulting a pocket-watch with the composure of a woman announcing a council meeting. "One day, perhaps two, depending on the gates. The usurper knows we're coming. The beacon chain has been lit since Tuesday; I counted the smoke from my cell window, which is how I knew to escape when I did." She snapped the watch shut. "It is preparing something at the river. The soldiers who deserted spoke of a mass rite — prayers being collected, chained, *harvested*. Whatever it intends, it intends it soon."

"Then we'd better get there first," I said.

And the whole road, top to bottom, a thousand throats and a thousand small gods and every relit shrine from the Nightmarket to the ridge, seemed to agree — a hum under the world, a warmth in the chest, a fire running joyfully through dry grass toward the walls of Vhalen, where a hungry darkness wearing a dead king's face was waiting with its mouth open.

We marched.
