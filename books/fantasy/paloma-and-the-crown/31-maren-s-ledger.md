## 31. Maren's Ledger

They had taken her boots.

That was the detail that finally made it real. Not the cell — a proper cell, dry, with a stool and a blanket folded with bureaucratic precision by someone who had clearly been told to treat the King's First Steward of Records with courtesy. Not the charges, read aloud in a flat clerk's voice that Maren privately graded as adequate: *sedition, consorting with unlicensed practitioners, theft of crown property.* Not even Captain Sorrel's face when they brought her past him in the corridor, the way he had looked at her like a man reading a ledger whose columns refused to balance.

It was the boots. Thirty-one years of service, and they had taken her boots, because laces could be turned into rope and rope into escape, or so the manual said. She had written half that manual herself.

Maren sat on the stool in her stockinged feet and did what she had always done with unbearable facts: she filed them.

*Item:* arrested on suspicion, no formal charge entered yet, which meant someone upstairs was still deciding whether she was a traitor or an embarrassment. *Item:* her office sealed, her ledgers impounded, her private records — the ones that mattered, the ones hidden inside forty years of grain tallies and candle requisitions — not yet found. *Item:* the interrogation scheduled for tomorrow morning, ninth bell, conducted by a witch-hunter named Vess who had once, in another life, asked her to correct his arithmetic and had blushed when she did.

She had until ninth bell.

Maren folded her hands on her knees and began, methodically, to plan her own escape from the palace she had served since she was nineteen years old.

* * *

The trick to escaping a building, Maren had learned long ago from watching thieves' trials cross her desk, was never to escape it at all. You escaped the *people*. Buildings were honest; they stayed where you put them. People were the ones who looked for you, and people ran on paper.

So at the seventh bell, when a junior clerk named Tamsin came to bring her water — a girl Maren had hired four years ago for her beautiful hand and her incurable habit of losing things — Maren said, "Tamsin. I need you to fetch me my shawl from my office. The grey one. And while you're there, the second ledger on the third shelf, the one with the cracked spine."

"I'm not supposed to—"

"You're not supposed to speak to me either, and yet here we are." Maren kept her voice at the exact register she used for correcting misfiled documents: mild, inevitable, already true. "The guard outside will let you pass if you show him your requisition chit. Tell him it's authorized under seal of the Steward's Office, which it is, because I am authorizing it now. Here." She tore a strip from the hem of her blanket, pressed her thumb to it — inkless, but the gesture carried weight; gestures always had — and handed it over. "Grey shawl. Cracked spine. Don't lose either."

Tamsin lost the shawl. She came back twenty minutes later white-faced and clutching the ledger against her chest like a rescued kitten, saying the office was full of hunters going through everything, saying one of them had shouted at her, saying she'd grabbed the first book with a cracked spine she saw and wasn't that the right one, please, was it the right one?

"It's the right one," Maren said, because it was. She had three cracked-spine ledgers in that office, and all three held pieces of a dead king's name buried in columns of flour deliveries and lamp oil. She had chosen the third shelf because hunters always started at eye level and worked down, and the third shelf was below their patience.

"Thank you, Tamsin. Now go home. Take tomorrow off. Say you're ill."

"Are you ill, madam?"

"Not yet," Maren said. "But I'm working on it."

* * *

The ledger, opened on her knees in the lamplight of the cell, gave up its secrets the way it always had: grudgingly, in order, column by column.

*Flour, fine-milled, 40 sacks — Ald.*

*Lamp oil, whale, 12 gallons — Ric.*

*Candles, tallow, 200 — Al-dric.*

The king's name, broken into syllables and scattered across five years of household accounts, because no hunter would ever think to look for a man's soul in a grocery list. Maren had assembled it over months, late at night, matching fragments the way she matched duplicate entries — *Aldric, Aldric, Aldric*, stitched together out of the last honest paperwork the king had ever signed. Five years ago his hand had changed. She knew hands the way other women knew faces. The new hand was the old hand's twin, but it wrote left-slanting where the king had written right, and it never, ever smudged.

She had the name whole now. *Aldric Vael Corvane,* third of that name, crowned under a salt sky, sworn on bread and blood. She had checked it eleven times. There was a twelfth fragment she'd never found — a middle initial in the coronation oath, perhaps, some small piece the usurper's clerks had missed — but Odile had said the rite needed the name as it was spoken, and it had been spoken *Aldric*. Names were spoken, not written. That was the whole point of writing them down: so that someday, someone could say them aloud again.

Ninth bell came. Then tenth. No one came for her.

At the eleventh bell, someone came for everyone.

* * *

The sound reached the cells before the news did: bells, all of them at once, the alarm pattern Maren had catalogued years ago as *siege, external, gates compromised.* Shouting in the corridors. Running feet. The smell of smoke — real smoke, not hearth smoke — threading under the door.

Her cell door rattled, stuck, and burst open to reveal Tamsin, of all people, breathless, soot-streaked, holding a ring of keys she had very obviously stolen.

"The city's at the gates," the girl gasped. "Everyone's running to the walls, nobody's watching the cells, I couldn't just leave you, I *couldn't*—"

"Tamsin." Maren stood, took the keys, and kissed the girl on both cheeks, which was the most unprofessional thing she had done in thirty-one years and she regretted nothing. "You are the finest hire of my career. Go home. Now. Do not argue with me; arguing is my department."

"But—"

"*Go.*"

The girl went. Maren put on her borrowed slippers, tucked the ledger into the front of her shift where it lay against her heart like a second heartbeat, and walked out into the burning corridor of the only home she had ever known.

* * *

The palace was chaos wearing livery. Servants streamed toward the river stairs; soldiers streamed toward the gates; somewhere above, the great bell tolled and tolled as though it too had gone mad. Maren moved against every current, because she knew this building the way she knew her own ledgers — not the grand rooms, but the service veins beneath them, the records corridors, the dumbwaiter shafts, the archive stair that officially did not exist because she had removed it from the plans herself, years ago, in case of exactly this night.

She was two turns from the archive stair when the hunters found her.

Three of them, coming up from the lower cells with torches, and the one in front was Vess — her former arithmetic student, now grown broad and hard-eyed, with a brand-new captain's pin on his collar and a warrant scroll in his fist.

"Maren Corvane's steward," he said. "Stop."

"Captain Vess." She stopped. Old habits. "Your men are needed at the gates. The city is falling and you are standing in a hallway."

"The city can fall," Vess said, and something in his voice made her stomach drop, because it wasn't cruelty, it was *fear*, fear dressed in orders. "I have my warrant. The taster woman has associates in the archives. We're sealing the records. Everything burns tonight, by order of—" He stopped himself before the title, which told her everything. Even the hunters were starting to flinch from the word *king*.

"Burning the archives," Maren said, "will not save you."

"Nothing will save us," Vess said, almost gently. "That's rather the point of orders." He gestured, and his men moved toward her, torches high.

Maren did the arithmetic she had been doing since the seventh bell. Three hunters, one corridor, one old woman in slippers with a book against her heart. The numbers were bad. They had always been bad. She had simply hoped, in the small dishonest corner of herself where hope lived, that she wouldn't be the one who had to pay them.

Then the wall behind Vess exploded inward.

Later, she would learn it was Sorrel — Sorrel with a sledgehammer and six defected hunters and the wild eyes of a man who had burned his own warrant and found it freeing — but in the moment there was only dust and screaming stone and Vess turning, torch swinging, shouting about traitors, and Maren did the only sensible thing a steward could do in a collapsing corridor.

She ran.

* * *

The archive stair was where she had left it, behind the false panel shaped like a wine rack, and she went down it into the dark with her lungs burning and her heart hammering against the ledger like a fist on a door. Behind her, above her, the palace roared. Somewhere in it, the thing that wore the king was learning what it felt like to be besieged by its own city.

The cisterns were lit. That was the first miracle — candlelight rising up the flooded chapel steps, voices, many voices, and when Maren waded out into the glow she found the Nightmarket had come underground: charm-sellers and dockworkers and knot-makers, dozens of them, kneeling around a stone altar where Odile stood with her sleeves rolled back like a surgeon, and beside the altar—

Lena Vasquez. The taster. Younger than Maren expected, wearier than anyone should be, with salt-crusted hands and the look of a woman who had already died once and found it inconvenient.

"Maren," Lena said. Her eyes went straight to Maren's chest, to the shape of the book beneath the shift. "Please tell me you have it."

"I have it." Maren drew out the ledger, and her hands — steady through thirty-one years of audits, arrests, and one exploding corridor — shook now. "The name. Whole. *Aldric Vael Corvane.* I've verified it eleven times. It's in flour accounts and lamp oil and candle requisitions, and it's *true*, and I will swear to it on any god you like, including the ones who've just come back, several of whom appear to be standing behind you."

One of them was. A small wet god of the drowned shrine, flickering at the water's edge like a lantern underwater, and it turned its attention on Maren with an expression she could only describe as *approval*, and she found, absurdly, that she wanted to cry.

"Eleven times isn't twelve," Odile said quietly. "Read it out. All of us need to hear it, and the rite needs witnesses."

Maren opened the ledger.

And that was when the ceiling came down.

Not all of it — a section, a slab of vaulted stone sheared loose by Sorrel's hammer-work above, or by the siege, or by simple old age, the palace itself deciding it had held long enough. She heard the crack, saw the light change, saw Lena scream her name, and Maren had perhaps half a second to make the only decision that had ever truly mattered in her careful, counted life.

The book. Or herself.

There was no arithmetic for it. For the first time in thirty-one years, Maren Corvane declined to do the math. She wrapped the ledger in her arms, curled over it the way she had once curled over her mother's letters during a house fire, and let the weight of the palace come down on her back.

* * *

Pain, afterward, was a country she visited without staying. Voices moved around her like weather. Someone cut the ledger free of her arms — gently, so gently — and she heard pages turn, and a voice, Lena's, reading in a shaking whisper: "*Aldric Vael Corvane.* It's here. It's all here. Every fragment."

"Good," Maren managed. Her mouth tasted of stone dust and copper. "Check the candle entry. Two hundred tallow. He always ordered too many candles. Sentimental man. Nobody ever noticed."

"Hold still," Odile said, hands pressing hard against Maren's side, and Maren understood distantly that the warmth spreading under her was hers, leaving. "Hold still and be quiet, you impossible woman, you magnificent clerk, hold *still*—"

"The twelfth fragment," Maren whispered. "Middle initial. Check the oath record, box nine, red ribbon. I never found it. But names are spoken, not written. *Aldric* is enough. It was always enough. Tell him—" A cough took her, and cost her something she could not count. "Tell him a clerk remembered. That's all any of us get. To be remembered accurately."

Lena knelt and took Maren's hand, and her grip was strong, blessed, immune, warm as bread.

"You're not dying," Lena said fiercely. "I forbid it. You're the best record-keeper in Vhalen and I need you alive to audit whatever comes next."

"Then I'll file a complaint," Maren whispered, and felt the small drowned god lean close, and felt — impossibly, unmistakably — a prayer being answered, gently, the way she had always believed prayers ought to work, and the pain receded like a tide going out, and the last thing she saw was the ledger open in Lena's hands, the name whole at last, the columns balanced after five long years.

Above them, the throne room waited. Below them, the city rose. And in Maren's fading hearing, the small god of the drowned shrine spoke with a voice like water over stones:

"She paid the price. The name is free. *Go and say it.*"

Lena stood, and the candlelight bent toward her like wheat toward wind.

"To the throne room," she said. "We say his name tonight."
