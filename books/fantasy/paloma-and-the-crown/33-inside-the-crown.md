## 33. Inside the Crown

The counterword broke against her like a wave against a seawall.

One moment Lena was kneeling at the heart of the unbinding rite, salt in her left hand, bread crumbling under her right, King Aldric's half-remembered name burning on her tongue — and the next the throne room was gone. The candles, Odile's chanting, Maren's bleeding hands pressed flat against the floor tiles, all of it swallowed whole.

She was falling through something that was not dark and not light but *worn*, like cloth washed so many times it had gone soft and thin. She fell through five years of it. She could feel every year. They were frayed at the edges, thinned by chewing, stretched where something had gripped them hard and pulled.

*The crown-spirit knows every counterword,* she thought, distantly, the way you think things when a roof is coming down on you. *It has eaten this rite before.*

Then the falling stopped, and she opened eyes that were not her own.

She was looking out through the king's face.

She knew it instantly, the way you know your own reflection even distorted. This was the view from behind Aldric's eyes: the throne room seen from the dais, the long carpet worn gray down its center, courtiers arranged below like pieces on a board. But everything was wrong with it, blurred at the margins, colors slightly too loud, as if someone else were painting the world over the king's shoulders and didn't quite have his palette right. The crown sat heavy on her — on *his* — brow, and she could feel it thinking. Not thoughts she understood. Thoughts like weather. Vast slow pressure systems of appetite rolling across a sky that used to belong to a man.

*Lena.* Her name came from somewhere inside, small and hoarse, a voice that had been calling for years into a room no one entered. *Lena Vasquez. You're in here. Oh, gods help you, you're actually in here.*

"I'm here," she said aloud, though aloud meant nothing here; the words went nowhere and everywhere at once. "I'm the taster."

*I know what you are.* And then, with something like wonder cracking through the hoarseness: *You're the one who caught the nightshade. Five years ago. My third breakfast of that winter. You spat it into the silk and saved my life, and I never even learned your name, and I am so sorry, I am so sorry—*

"Stop." Her voice shook. "Don't apologize to me. I'm going to get you out."

The silence that followed was worse than grief. It was the silence of someone who has already done the arithmetic and is trying to decide whether to share the answer.

*Walk forward,* he said instead. *If you can walk. Time moves strangely in here, but it moves. Come and see what he's made of my years.*

---

She walked.

It was not walking in any body she recognized. She moved through the king's memory the way smoke moves through a house, pouring under doors, rising along staircases, filling rooms without disturbing them. And the memory was a palace, because of course it was — his mind had built itself a palace, or what remained of one, and the crown-spirit lived in it now the way a squatter lives in a burned manor, cooking its meals on the embers.

The first door she poured through showed her the day it happened.

Five years ago. She was new to the taster's bench then, three months in, still learning which of the kitchen boys lied about washing their hands. She remembered that morning from the outside — everyone did. The king had been dying for a month. The physicians spoke of fever, of the lung-rot, of God's mercy. The whole court held its breath.

From the inside, she watched him lie in his bed and know exactly what he was dying of, because the old priestess who came secretly at night — Odile's sister, though he hadn't known that either — had told him plainly: *the crown is eating you, majesty. It was always going to. That is what crowns are for.* He had laughed at that, weakly, and asked her if there was any way to refuse an inheritance.

And then the usurper had come, with the relic-boxes and the rites of succession, and the king had been too far gone to fight, and the crown — the crown had simply changed hands, and something had crossed the space between the two heads like a spider on a thread.

Lena stood in that bedroom, in that last hour, and felt him make a choice that no one would ever write down. He could not stop the thing taking his body. But he could take himself somewhere it wouldn't think to look. He had folded himself up small — smaller than a prayer, smaller than a secret — and hidden in the deepest room of his own mind, behind memories of his mother, behind the smell of rain on the palace gardens, behind a lullaby in a language the usurper's scholars had never bothered to learn.

He had locked the door from the inside.

*And I've been knocking on it ever since,* the hoarse voice said beside her, and she turned and there he was.

Not a vision. Not a ghost-light. A man, kneeling.

King Aldric knelt on the stone floor of a corridor that had no end, in a shirt with sweat stains at the collar, his beard grown wrong, his hands folded the way men fold their hands when they've been praying so long that prayer has become posture. He looked older than thirty-five. He looked like a map of the last five years drawn in a face. His eyes found hers and they were — this was the part that broke something open in her chest — they were *kind*. Tired past all endurance, hollowed down to the wick, and still kind.

"You're real," she said stupidly.

"As real as anything left in here." He tried to smile. It kept sliding off. "They told stories about you, you know. In the early days, when I could still hear the kitchens through the walls. The little taster with the quick tongue who'd caught poison four times in her first season. I liked knowing you existed. It was — " his voice failed and restarted — "it was proof the world outside still had people paying attention."

Lena knelt down across from him, because standing over a kneeling king felt like a kind of cruelty, and because her legs were shaking. "Aldric. Your name. I know it. Odile found it — Maren — there are records, we have almost all of it, we came tonight to say it and take you back—"

"No."

The word landed between them like a dropped knife.

"Not take me back," he said gently. "Listen to me, taster, because I've had five years to think and nothing else, and I need you to hear the shape of it. If you say my name and pull me up out of this room, the crown comes with me. It's woven through everything now. Every memory you walked through on your way here — it's chewed those. It knows my mother's voice better than I do. It knows the lullaby. It learned my name the hard way, syllable by syllable, the way you learn a language by living in it." He spread his ruined hands. "You restore me, you restore it. A dead king wearing a live crown, and this time it doesn't even have to pretend."

"That's not—" Her throat closed. "We bled for the relics. Maren nearly died carrying pages. Sorrel burned his warrant. I'm not going to kneel here and tell them it was for nothing."

"It wasn't for nothing." He leaned forward, urgent now, the first fire she'd seen in him. "It was for *this*. For you getting in here. Do you understand? No one gets in here. I hid so well that even I lost the way back some nights. But you walked straight through five years of eaten memory and found me anyway, because your blood remembers how to carry messages between gods and kings, and I have a message that only a taster can deliver."

"What message?"

"Tell them I choose to die."

The words went into her like cold water.

"He can't keep the body," Aldric said, fast now, leaning close, gripping her wrists with hands that trembled like an old man's. "That's what the rite needs to finish — not restoration. Release. Say my name, yes, but say it as a *farewell*, and I will let go of everything he's holding onto, and when the anchor lets go the crown loses its grip on this flesh forever. Half of it dies with me. Maybe more than half. It's spent five years digesting me, Lena, and you cannot digest a man who refuses to be food."

"And the other half?"

His face did something terrible and honest. "The other half will be very angry. And very hungry. And loose." He didn't look away. "I won't lie to you. I've watched it plan. Whatever's left after tonight will go looking for the free cities with its teeth out. So whatever you and your priestess and your clerk and your hunter do next — do it fast."

Lena stared at him. Five years she had tasted this man's food. Five years she had sat ten feet from his body every morning and read its health like scripture — the pallor, the stillness, the strange smooth skin that never aged — and told herself it was illness, medicine, mystery. Never once had she imagined that behind the eyes she was forbidden to meet, a living man was folding napkins out of his own memories just to remember what hands were for.

"Why hide?" she whispered. "Why not fight it from inside? Why let it wear you?"

"Because fighting is what it eats best." He smiled, and this time the smile stayed, crooked and sad. "My grandfather fought it. My father fought it. Both of them burned out from the inside like lamps. I decided the bravest thing a king could do was be very, very quiet and wait for someone worth talking to." He tilted his head. "And look. Here you are."

Somewhere above them — or around them, or through them — the palace of memory shuddered. Dust sifted down from a ceiling that wasn't stone. Far off, muffled as thunder through walls, she heard Odile's voice raised in the true throne room, chanting the counterword again, holding the door open at terrible cost.

"It knows you're in here," Aldric said, rising stiffly to his feet. He stood like a soldier, out of habit older than his pain. "It's turning around. We don't have long, and I want to spend what we have properly." He offered her his hand. "Come. There's something I've saved for five years, and I'd rather give it to a person than lose it to a spider."

---

He led her down through the palace of himself, past doors already eaten to frames, past corridors where the wallpaper was peeling into static, down stairs that grew older as they descended until the steps were packed earth and the walls were living root. And at the bottom was a garden.

Not the real one. Better than the real one. A boy's version of it — the palace gardens as Aldric had known them at eight years old, when the hedges were towers and the fountains ran sweet and his mother was alive somewhere in the house, singing that lullaby in the old tongue. Rain hung in the air without falling. Everything smelled the way memory smells: too bright, too exact, unbearable.

"The crown never found this," Aldric said quietly. "Too small to bother with. Too happy. Hunger skips the things that don't fight." He crouched and touched a hedge that rippled like water. "But I've been keeping something in here. Something I took out of its mouth, piece by piece, every time it fed. Little bits of myself it couldn't swallow because I made them too bitter. Regrets, mostly. Love, a little. The names of everyone I ever failed." He looked up at her. "Tasters carry messages between gods and kings. So carry this one. Tell them — whoever rules after, your councils and your guilds and your many-handed coronation — tell them the crown isn't a treasure. It never was. It's a leash, and it was always hungry, and the only reason kingdoms survived wearing it is that good people kept paying the price quietly, in rooms no one visited."

"Is that the message?"

"No." He stood, and took both her hands in his, and his eyes were clear as a boy's. "The message is: *tell my daughter's story if she has descendants, and tell the kitchen boys, and tell the dockworkers, and tell anyone who'll listen, that the king did not abandon them. He was here the whole time. Waiting.*"

Above them the garden began to shake. The rain fell at last, and where it struck, the memory blackened and curled. Through the dissolving leaves Lena could see it coming down through the levels of the stolen mind — the crown-spirit's attention, vast and cold and *turning*, a searchlight the size of a season sweeping toward the deepest room of the house.

"Aldric," she said. "When I speak your name out there — it will hurt."

"I know."

"It might hurt a great deal."

"I know," he said again, and smiled, and for one moment he looked exactly like the portrait in the great hall, the young king with the kind eyes, the one the court had mourned five years ago without knowing it. "Everything worth doing does. Now go, Lena Vasquez. Go before it finds us. And when you say my name—" his voice cracked clean through, and he laughed at himself, wet-eyed, unashamed — "when you say it, say it like a bell. Not a dirge. A bell. I want to leave the way bells leave. Still ringing in people."

The garden tore apart around them. Darkness poured in like ink into milk, and the last thing she saw was the king of Vhalen standing upright in his own collapsing mind, spine straight, chin lifted, waiting — he had always been waiting — for someone finally to say his name.

Then she was gasping on the throne-room floor, salt scattered, bread crushed, Odile screaming the counterword through bleeding lips, Maren hauling at her arm —

and the crown turned its attention upon them, and Lena opened her mouth, and the name came up from somewhere deeper than her lungs:

"*ALDRIC—*"
