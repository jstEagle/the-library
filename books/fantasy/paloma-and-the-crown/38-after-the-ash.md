## 38. After the Ash

The warrant burned better than he'd expected.

Sorrel had imagined it would resist — that the Edict of Ash, which had outlived three captains-general and a war, would fight the flame the way it had fought every conscience that ever turned against it. But the vellum curled and blackened like any other paper, and the seal of the Office of Purification popped in the heat with a sound like a knuckle cracking, and then it was ash on the wind off the harbor, and Captain Sorrel stood in the ruins of the Nightmarket with empty hands.

The fire had gone through here three weeks ago. Not his fire — he'd been at the river by then, holding a gate for people he'd been ordered to arrest — but the crown's fire, set in spite as the thing wearing the king came apart at the seams. It had eaten the stalls first, then the awnings, then the stairways of rope and plank where the charm-sellers kept their lofts. What remained was charcoal geometry: the black skeletons of tables, the fused glass of a lantern-maker's stock, a child's luck-thread melted into the cobbles like a vein of tarnished silver.

He walked the rows alone because no one would walk them with him yet.

That was fair. He understood it the way you understand weather. For eleven years he had been the reason people stepped into doorways when he passed. He had carried the ledgers. He had signed the transports. He had stood in the square at Coppersmith's Row with his hands folded behind his back, watching, while four charm-sellers were put to the pyre, and he had not looked away, because looking away was a luxury his oath did not permit.

One of those four had been a woman named Bess Tanner, who sold fever-knots to sailors' wives for two coppers and had once, when he was a boy new to the Office and still soft, pressed a protection charm into his palm at a checkpoint and told him *come home safe, love*, as if he were someone worth protecting.

He still had the charm. It was in a drawer in a room he no longer slept in, wrapped in oilcloth, never worn. He could not have said why he kept it. He could not have said why he had never reported it, either, though reporting it would have been the only act of those eleven years that cost him nothing.

* * *

They started rebuilding on a Tuesday, because Tuesday was market day and had always been market day, and the dockworkers decided that if they waited for permission they would be waiting until the sea dried.

Sorrel came down before dawn with a crowbar and a pair of work gloves, intending to clear debris quietly and leave before anyone recognized him. He got as far as the third row of stalls before a voice stopped him.

"That's my table."

He turned. A broad woman with forearms like mooring rope stood in the ash with her fists on her hips. He knew her face from the files — Marta Quill, unlicensed charm-broker, seventeen entries in the ledgers, twice escaped custody. She looked at him the way you look at a rat in your grain store.

"I'm here to work," he said.

"You're here to feel better." She spat into the ash. "There's a difference, and my table knows the difference."

He considered several answers and discarded all of them. Then he set down the crowbar, picked up the charred frame of her stall, and began carrying it, piece by piece, to the scrap pile at the end of the row. She watched him do it without speaking. When he came back for the second load she was already sorting what was salvageable, and neither of them acknowledged that anything had changed, because nothing had, and also everything had, and both of them were professionals enough to keep working through it.

By midmorning there were forty people in the rows. By afternoon there were a hundred, and hammers were ringing on fresh timber, and somebody had strung a kettle over a driftwood fire, and the smell of the Nightmarket — tar and tea and woodsmoke and, underneath it, faintly, the ozone tang of small waking blessings — began to come back to the place like blood returning to a numb limb.

Sorrel worked until his shoulders gave out. Nobody thanked him. Nobody spoke to him except to direct him. But when he left at dusk, Marta Quill called after him:

"Same time tomorrow, hunter. Bring your own hammer."

It was not forgiveness. He catalogued it carefully, in the way he catalogued everything, and filed it under something that might, given years and labor, eventually become adjacent to forgiveness. He brought his own hammer the next day.

* * *

The Edict fell formally in the third week — repealed by the Councils of Many Hands in a session that ran two days and ended with the old law read aloud one final time, in full, so that everyone present could hear exactly how mad it had been. *That no blessing shall be sold, given, or made; that no prayer shall be answered aloud; that the gods are dead and shall be kept dead by fire.* Sorrel attended the reading standing at the back of the guildhall, and around him he watched grown men and women weep at words they'd lived under so long they'd stopped hearing them.

Afterward, an official from the new administration found him in the corridor. Young, earnest, ink-stained fingers. "Captain Sorrel? There's a question about the archives. The witch-hunters' files — the ledgers, the informant lists, the interrogation records. The Council wants them preserved."

"Preserved," he repeated.

"As historical record. As evidence, some say. There's talk of a commission on redress — names restored, property returned, families notified. Someone has to identify who was taken, and where they were sent, and which entries were fabricated to fill quotas." The young man hesitated. "You're the only senior officer who surrendered his files intact. The others burned theirs during the siege."

Of course they had. Sorrel had watched the smoke rise from the Office of Purification on the last night and known precisely what was burning. Eleven years of his colleagues ensuring their own names stayed clean.

"The files are yours," he said. "All of them. Including the ones that name me."

The young man blinked. "That wasn't — I'm instructed to ask whether you'd prefer certain pages be—"

"No." He heard his own voice come out flat and final, the voice he'd used for death warrants, repurposed. "Read them all. Every entry. If they're going to build something honest on top of what we did, they can't start by sanding me smooth. My signature is on four hundred and six transport orders. People should know that. People should count."

The young man wrote it down, which Sorrel supposed was the whole point of the young man.

Walking home afterward — walking past the rows, where lamplighters were hanging lamps on posts that hadn't held lamps in eleven years — he caught himself doing the arithmetic he always did. Four hundred and six. Minus the ones who survived transport. He didn't know that number. He suspected he would spend the rest of his life learning it, one face at a time, in market rows and shrine queues and the new school by the river where the taster Vasquez was teaching children to listen.

He had tried, once, in the weeks after the unbinding, to make the arithmetic come out even. To find some weight of service or suffering that balanced the ledger. He had stood in the cistern dark where Odile's people had hidden and understood, finally, that there was no balancing. Restitution was not payment. Restitution was just staying. Staying in the room with what you'd done, year after year, doing the next repairable thing, and the next, and never asking the ledger to close.

* * *

In the fifth week, the Nightmarket reopened, and someone asked him to guard it.

It happened without ceremony. He was repairing a roof beam for the lantern-maker — his third week of carpentry had taught him humility and dado joints in roughly equal measure — when Marta Quill climbed the ladder, handed him a mug of something hot and bitter, and said, "Council's posted a watch budget. Shrines get theirs from the temple fund, the docks from the harbormaster. Ours comes from the market levy, and we can pay one guard to start."

"Congratulations," he said. "I hope you find someone trustworthy."

She looked at him for a long moment with an expression he couldn't parse, and he braced himself for the joke to land, for her to laugh and tell him he was the last man in Vhalen who'd ever wear that badge again.

Instead she said: "The charm-bearers voted. All hundred and forty of us who've got stalls now, plus the ones still coming back off the roads with the march. They voted this morning." She took a folded paper from her apron. "You'll want to see the tally."

He unfolded it. One hundred and thirty-one marks beside his name. Nine abstentions. No votes against — but nine abstentions, and he looked up and found Marta watching him count them.

"They wouldn't say no," she said. "But they couldn't yet say yes. And that's honest, and I won't pretend otherwise, and neither will you." She jabbed a thumb toward the rows. "So here's how it goes. You wear no uniform. You carry no warrant — nobody carries warrants anymore, we burn them on principle, it's become a bit of a festival honestly. You stand at the north gate on market days and you watch for pickpockets and drunk brawlers and the occasional zealot who thinks the gods coming back means the burning should too. That's the job. Small gods, small prices, remember?" Her mouth twitched, not quite a smile. "Small guard. Small wages."

"What are the wages?"

"Two coppers a day and all the fever-knots you can carry."

He thought of a drawer across the city. Of oilcloth. Of *come home safe, love.*

"I'll take it," he said.

* * *

His first day as the Nightmarket's guard, a girl pickpocketed a luck-thread off a stall and he chased her three rows and caught her by the collar and then, crouched there in the ash-dusted mud with her wrist in his grip, found he had absolutely no idea what to do next. Under the Office there had been a procedure for everything. Arrest, entry, transport, and the fire took care of the rest. The machinery had been monstrous but complete, and he had spent eleven years inside it, and now the machine was scrap and he was a man kneeling in mud holding a frightened child and inventing justice from scratch.

"You stole," he said.

She nodded, chin tucked.

"Why?"

"Healer charms cost three coppers," she whispered. "Mum's coughing blood."

The rows had gone quiet around them. Forty faces pretending not to watch.

Sorrel let go of her wrist. He dug in his pocket and came out with his day's wages, all of it, and pressed it into her hand. "Buy the charm," he said. "And then come find me at the north gate, because Marta Quill runs a work roster and there's always kindling needs splitting, and nobody in this market respects a thief, but everybody respects somebody who works off a debt. Understand?"

She stared at the coins. Then at him. Then she ran — but she ran toward the healer's stall, not away from it, and that evening she appeared at the north gate with a hatchet taller than she was and split wood until dark, glaring at him the entire time, and when she finished she dumped the last armload beside his stool and said, "Tomorrow I'm stealing nothing, but I'm still taking your coppers, because Mum says interest is owed," and left before he could respond.

Marta Quill appeared at his elbow with two mugs. "Word travels," she said. "Half the market thinks you're soft now. Other half thinks it was a performance." She handed him a mug. "I told them the truth. That you were a hard man learning to be useful, which is harder than the reverse, and rarer."

"And the nine?"

"The nine are watching." She drank. "Good. Let them. Trust that arrives whole isn't trust, it's just forgetting. They'll give it to you a copper at a time, same as the girl. Same as me." She looked out at the rows — the relit lanterns, the knot-sellers and thread-menders, the little shrines springing up between stalls like mushrooms after rain, each one no bigger than a bread box, each one tended, each one *answered*. Somewhere a dockworker was praying aloud over a safe return, and the small god of the harbor pilings was answering in its voice like wet rope, and no one flinched anymore, or almost no one. "Eleven years we hid," Marta said. "You hunted us. Now look at it. Ash grows the fastest gardens, my gran used to say. Doesn't make the fire a favor. Just makes the ground generous after."

He drank his bitter tea and did not answer, because there was nothing in him equal to the sentence, and he had learned, late, that silence was sometimes the only honest coin a man like him could spend.

* * *

On the first night of the new moon, he went to Coppersmith's Row.

The square had changed. Where the pyres used to stand there was now a low stone basin, and in the basin a fire burned that was not a pyre — a hearth-fire, tended in shifts, warm and orange and domestic, the kind of fire you cooked over. Around it, the city had hung strips of cloth on lines: hundreds of them, thousands, each one written with a name. Names of the burned. Families came and tied the strips themselves, and the wind moved through them all night with a sound like slow rain.

Sorrel found the line where his own signatures were remembered. Bess Tanner's strip was there, tied by a granddaughter he had helped locate through the archive commission, in one of the few entries where the transport records had been accurate because he had insisted, early on, that they be accurate, back when accuracy was the only rebellion available to him and he hadn't known that's what it was.

He stood a long time. Then he took out his knife and cut a strip of his own shirt hem, and borrowed the charcoal stub hanging on a string by the basin, and wrote on it not his name but hers: *Bess Tanner, fever-knots, two coppers, kind to a boy who didn't deserve it.* He tied it high on the line where the wind would find it first.

A voice behind him said, "She'd have laughed at that."

He turned. Lena Vasquez stood at the edge of the square with a loaf under her arm and salt in a twist of paper — grave goods, he realized; she was on her way to Aldric's resting place — and she was looking at him with the level, unhurried gaze of a woman who had tasted poison for five years and was not easily surprised by what bitterness turned out to be.

"Lady Vasquez."

"Don't." She came closer. "Paloma now, they tell me. Though I'm still getting used to answering to it." She studied the strip of cloth turning in the wind. "You could have hidden the files. Everyone else did."

"Yes."

"Why didn't you?"

He considered lying, and found, with something like relief, that the muscle for it had finally atrophied. "Because a taster once told the whole city that inheritance can't be refused, only chosen," he said. "I inherited four hundred and six signatures. Choosing them meant reading them out loud. Forever."

She was quiet for a moment. Then she broke the loaf in half and held half out to him — plain bread, palace-oven recipe, stolen or formerly stolen or simply made now, who could say — and he took it, because refusing bread offered plainly seemed suddenly like the one unforgivable thing left in the world.

"Eat," she said. "Then walk with me. There's a grave up the hill, and a story about a king who chose to die rather than be used, and I think you and I both know how heavy a choice like that gets when you carry it alone."

They walked up through the strips and the wind and the small warm fire, and behind them the Nightmarket glowed in its valley like a banked ember that had decided, against every precedent, to become a hearth.

At the top of the hill, Paloma paused, and looked back at the city, and said, almost to herself: "Someone should write all this down. Not the battles — those will get written whether we like it or not. The rest. The coppers at a time." She glanced at him sideways. "You kept ledgers for eleven years, Captain. You're the best record-keeper the old regime ever produced. It seems a waste of a terrible skill."

He understood what she was offering. A post with no power in it. A duty with no fire in it. A ledger of restitution instead of purification — names entered, debts counted, coppers tallied, forever, in a book that would never close but might, page by page, begin to balance toward mercy.

"I'd need a good pen," he said.

Her smile was small and real. "The market sells them. Two coppers."

Below them, faint on the wind, a child was splitting kindling at the north gate, and a god of harbor pilings was answering prayers in a voice like wet rope, and somewhere in the dark beyond the walls the free cities were lighting their lamps one after another, all the way to the horizon, like a prayer going out and out and out — and being answered.
