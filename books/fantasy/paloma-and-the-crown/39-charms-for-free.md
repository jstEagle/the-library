## 39. Charms for Free

The last charm I ever sold was a knot of red thread with a river-stone at its heart, and I gave it away on a Tuesday.

I want to be precise about that, because precision is the whole point. For five years in the Nightmarket I priced my charms the way every charm-seller does — by what the buyer could bear to pay and what the Edict made them risk. A luck-thread for a dockworker cost two coppers and a glance over the shoulder. A protective knot for a fisherman's wife cost five and a whispered warning about witch-hunters. Every transaction had a price and a fear attached, and I told myself for years that the fear was part of the craft, that it kept the charms honest, that anything given freely might be given carelessly.

That was a lie I sold along with the thread.

The buyer on that Tuesday was a girl of maybe fourteen, one arm gone at the elbow from a press-gang accident back when the usurper still needed sailors more than he needed mercy. She'd come to the school — because that's what the Nightmarket had become, or was becoming; the burned stalls rebuilt as benches, the ash swept into the gutters where it fed something green — she'd come to the school to learn listening, which is what we called it now instead of charming, because *listening* sounded like a skill and not a crime. She stood at the edge of the courtyard while the morning class practiced hearing the small gods in running water, and she watched me the way people used to watch me across a market stall: hungry, and pretending not to be.

"You're Lena Vasquez," she said, when I finally came over. "The taster."

"I was a taster."

"They say you tasted poison for the king for five years and never once got sick."

"They say a lot of things." I held out my hand. "Show me your knots."

She had good hands, even missing one. Her half-knots were tight and even, tied with the economy of someone who'd learned to do everything one-armed and had no patience for wasted motion. The thread was cheap market-dye, the stone just a stone off the quay, but her intent was clean. That's the thing nobody understood before the gods came back — the materials never mattered much. It was always the attention. A charm is a prayer you can hold.

"This would have cost you eight coppers last year," I said, finishing the knot she'd left open and closing it with the old double-turn, grandmother's turn, the one Odile swore was older than Vhalen itself. "Maybe ten, if I thought you could pay it."

"And now?"

"Now it costs you this." I pressed the finished knot into her palm and closed her fingers over it. "You give the next one away. To whoever needs it most. And they give the next one after that, and you tell them the same."

She frowned at her closed fist like I'd handed her a live coal. "But then nobody makes any money."

"Then nobody has to be afraid," I said. "It turns out those were the same price all along."

She looked at me for a long moment, and then she did the thing that undid me a little, the thing I wasn't expecting: she bowed. Not deep, not courtly — a dockworker's bow, quick and practical, the kind you give someone whose respect you need rather than whose favor you want. Then she turned and walked out through the courtyard gate, and I watched her stop at the first person she passed — an old man hauling a cart of scrap — and press the knot into his hand, already talking, already passing it on.

Odile appeared at my shoulder the way she always did, silent as spilled water. "You gave away your last one," she said. It wasn't a question. She'd been watching too. She watched everything now, our disgraced priestess, cataloguing the rebirth of a faith she'd once sworn off like strong drink.

"Somebody had to be last," I said.

"You could have kept it. Sentimental value. First charm you ever tied, wasn't it? Or near enough."

"Near enough." It had been remade so many times the original thread was long gone, but the shape of it was the shape of the first knot my grandmother ever put in my hand, down at the drowned shrine, before any of us knew what her bloodline was actually *for*. "Sentiment keeps things. I'm trying to spend them."

Odile snorted. "You sound like a sermon."

"Twenty years of hiding under a city and that's what comes out of you? Insults?"

"It's what comes out of me when you're right and I hate it." She settled onto the courtyard wall, robes — new robes, undyed, no longer the ash-grey of her order but plain brown wool she'd bought off a peddler — pooling around her ankles. Below us the class went on. Twenty-three students, ages nine to sixty, holding cups of harbor water and learning to hear what moved inside it. Most of them heard nothing yet. Two of them were hearing quite a lot, and one of those was the one-armed girl, who had stopped at the gate and turned back and was now kneeling at the fountain with her eyes shut and her whole face lit up like a lamp.

"She'll be trouble," Odile said fondly.

"They're all trouble. Trouble is the raw material." I watched the girl laugh at whatever the water was telling her. "That's the whole curriculum, if you want to know. Trouble, attention, and somebody to catch you when the listening goes wrong."

"And bread," Odile said. "Don't forget the bread. You promised them bread at midday and you've baked nothing."

"I'm a taster, not a baker."

"You're neither, now. You're a founder. Founders bake."

* * *

The school had no name for its first month, because naming things felt dangerous in a way I couldn't explain even to myself. Then Maren came — walking again, mostly, though she favored her left side where the hunter's blade had caught her during the siege, and her handwriting had gone from beautiful to merely legible, which she mourned like a lost limb — and Maren took one look at the nameless school and said, "This is absurd. Everything needs a ledger entry. What do I write?"

"Write 'school,'" I said.

"Of what?"

"Tasting. Listening. Knots. Bread, apparently. How to tell when a prayer has been answered and whether the answer is kind." I shrugged. "How to carry messages between gods and kings without either side eating you alive."

Maren's pen hovered. Then she wrote, in her damaged but determined script: *School of Tasters and Listeners, formerly the Nightmarket, formerly the ash.* And underneath, because she was Maren and could not help herself: *Enrollment: 23. Tuition: none. Founder: L. Vasquez.*

"There," she said. "Now it exists. Things that exist in ledgers are very hard to kill. Believe me, I've tried to kill several."

We taught in shifts. Odile took doctrine, or what passed for it — twenty years of silence had stripped her faith down to load-bearing beams, and what she taught was less theology than survival: how to pray without begging, how to receive an answer without drowning in it, how to know when the voice in the water was a god and when it was only your own wish wearing a mask. That second lesson she taught with a hardness that came straight from her own scars, from the purge, from the years she'd spent hating the gods for surviving when her sisters hadn't. The students loved her for it. Fear, honestly confessed, turns out to be a better teacher than certainty ever was.

Sorrel came on rest days, off-duty, out of uniform, looking perpetually like a man who couldn't believe he was allowed in a building without breaking something. He taught protection — real protection, the kind witch-hunters knew best: how to spot a raid forming, how to hide a shrine, how to burn a ledger so completely that not even the Inquisition's own methods could raise a ghost from the ashes. He taught it flatly, without apology, and the students listened the way soldiers listen, because Sorrel had spent fifteen years being the thing they feared and he knew exactly where it lived.

And I taught tasting. Which is to say I taught attention, dressed up in broth.

The first lesson was always the same. I'd set out three bowls of soup, identical to the eye, and ask the students to find the difference. They'd sniff, sip, squint. Eventually someone would get it — not the salt, not the heat, but the third bowl had been prayed over, quietly, by Odile, that morning, a blessing for safe travel folded into the steam. Once they found it, they never un-found it. The world was full of third bowls. It always had been. We'd all just been eating too fast.

"You taste memory," Odile told them, the day one of the students asked why the soup lesson mattered. "Everything that was ever done to a thing stays in it. Salt remembers the sea. Bread remembers the field and the hands. A king's breakfast remembers every hand that prepared it, every fear in the kitchen, every hope. A taster is someone who agrees to know all of it. That's the job. That was always the job. The immunity was just the door."

"The door to what?" the student asked.

"To the room where somebody has to stand between the powerful and the poisoned," I said, "and stay standing."

I didn't say the rest of it. I didn't say that the room had been empty for five years while a dead man wore a crown and a spirit wore the dead man, and that the poisoning had been going on the whole time, slow and total, a kingdom drinking something bitter one day at a time. They'd learn that soon enough. There was time. For the first time in six years there was time, and I was still learning how to live inside it without flinching.

* * *

Aldric's grave is not in the royal crypt.

We argued about it, afterward, the councils and guilds and the rite-of-many-hands people, everyone suddenly an expert on where dead kings belong. The traditionalists wanted him in the crypt, under marble, with an effigy and an epitaph and a list of his virtues that would have been lies, since the last five years of his life weren't his at all. The reformers wanted no grave at all — wanted him remembered as a victim, a caution, a name spoken in schools alongside the names of the crown's other casualties.

Lena Vasquez wanted him under a plum tree on a hill above the river, with a view of the water, because the drowned shrine was downstream and I liked to think the little god could visit.

I won. It turns out that refusing a throne buys you a surprising amount of say in things.

So on a soft evening in the first true week of autumn, I climbed the hill with a loaf under my arm and a twist of cloth in my pocket, and I sat down in the grass beside a low stone that read, in Maren's careful hand:

*ALDRIC. He waited to be remembered. Be kind to each other, which is what he would have asked for, if anyone had asked him anything in five years.*

Maren had fought me on the epitaph for two days. "Too informal," she said. "Kings get formal epitaphs."

"He wasn't a king at the end," I said. "He was a man in a room, waiting for someone to say his name. Write that."

She wrote it. She cried a little while she chiseled it, though she'd deny it under torture, and she'd survived actual interrogation, so her denials carried weight.

The bread was coronation bread — the real recipe, the one I'd stolen from the palace ovens with Maren's silent complicity, back when stealing it was treason and now the palace bakers sold it openly in the lower market and couldn't keep it on the shelves. I broke a piece and set it on the stone. Then I opened the twist of cloth: consecrated salt, the last of the chapel salt, the salt Odile and I had waded chest-deep into a flooding crypt to retrieve while the tide came for us both. A few grains went onto the stone beside the bread. The rest I kept. Some things you don't give away all of, even when you've sworn off selling.

"Hello, Aldric," I said.

The hill was quiet. Downriver, the drowned shrine glimmered where the evening light hit the water, and I could feel — faintly, the way you feel a fire in the next room — the small god down there, paying attention. It always paid attention when I came here. It had opinions about Aldric. It had known his grandmother, it claimed, which made them practically family, and family, in its experience, was the only currency worth minting.

"I brought the bread you never got to eat," I said. "At your actual coronation, I mean. The rite needed it and we stole it and then everything went sideways and I don't think anyone ever sat down and had a proper meal in that whole palace until it was over. That's not a criticism. It's just — it seems like the kind of thing you'd want noted. You struck me as a man who noticed when meals went wrong."

The grass moved. Just wind. Probably wind.

"Maren visits Tuesdays. She reads you the dispatches from the free cities, the good ones, so you know it worked. The armies stopped at the river. The mass rite never happened. The cities are sending delegates now instead of envoys begging for their lives — delegates, with trade proposals, which honestly might be worse, but it's the good kind of worse." I smiled at the stone. "Sorrel guards the Nightmarket now. He's terrible at smiling at children and excellent at scaring off the thieves, so it balances. Odile prays every morning. Out loud. Where people can hear. Last week she cried doing it and pretended it was incense smoke, and forty people saw, and nobody let her pretend, and I think that's the holiest thing I've ever witnessed."

I sat with him a while. That was the whole ritual, really — the bread, the salt, the sitting. No prayers asked. Aldric had spent five years being prayed *over*, being used, being worn, being a vessel and a glove and a throne's warm body. The least any of us could do was sit with him without wanting anything.

Except.

There was one thing. There had been one thing since the night in the throne room, since I walked his stolen years and found him kneeling in his own mind, waiting. One thing I'd said to him as he chose to die, taking the crown-spirit's heart with him, and one thing he'd tried to say back before the light took him. I'd spent months telling myself I'd imagined it. Grief invents voices. I'm a professional listener; I know exactly how unreliable the instrument is.

But the small god at the drowned shrine had started saying it too. In my grandmother's voice, gentle as tide: *He left something unfinished, dove. Words go somewhere. Even last words. Especially last words.*

So I leaned close to the stone, close enough that my breath stirred the crumbs of bread, and I said what I'd been carrying down the hill and up again for a season:

"Whatever you were trying to tell me — at the end, in the dark, when you chose — I'm listening. I'll keep listening. However long it takes."

For a moment, nothing. Wind, water, the far-off bells of the school marking the hour.

Then the salt on the stone — the consecrated salt, the chapel salt, the salt that had been waiting in a flooded crypt for thirty years for someone to come collect it — shifted. All at once, all together, the way iron filings answer a magnet, sliding into a pattern no grain of salt should make on its own.

A circle. Broken, incomplete, a fragment of a larger design — but unmistakably a piece of the old coronation sigil. The fifth mark. The one that appears nowhere in any rite, any relic, any record Maren ever recovered.

The one that means *there is another heir.*

Down at the drowned shrine, in my grandmother's voice, the small god began — very softly, very patiently, the way you speak to someone standing at the edge of deep water — to laugh.
