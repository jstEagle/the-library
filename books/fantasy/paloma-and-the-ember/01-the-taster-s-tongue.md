## 1. The Taster's Tongue

The first rule of being a royal food-taster is that you never, ever flinch.

The second rule is that there are no other rules. Flinching is the whole job description, really — I am paid a modest wage and fed an excellent diet to be the most flinchable woman in Emberfall Valley. Everything else is decoration.

I took the silver spoon from its velvet sleeve, dipped it into the pale green soup that had traveled three hundred miles to sit in front of me, and tasted it with the attention of a jeweler examining a stone for flaws.

Nettle. Goat's milk. A whisper of sorrel. And underneath it all, threaded through the cream like a hair in butter — bitter almond.

Not enough to kill. Enough to notice.

"Interesting," I said, and set the spoon down with a small, deliberate click.

The kitchen went quiet around me. Cook Brannic stopped kneading. The scullery boy, Tam, froze mid-scrub with a pot in each hand like a man caught carrying stolen saints.

"Interesting how?" Brannic asked. His forearms were as thick as my head and his face had gone the color of old porridge.

"Interesting that someone in the Sunward Provinces thinks we won't notice bitter almond cut with fennel seed." I swirled the spoon again, mostly because it made them nervous, which I confess I enjoy. "It's a masking trick. Amateur hour. Whoever sent this has read about poisons but never actually watched anyone die of one, which is, frankly, the only way to learn anything useful."

"And you can tell that from one spoonful?"

"I can tell that from one spoonful," I agreed, "because I have been poisoning myself professionally since I was nine years old."

This was true. The crown had started feeding me trace doses when I was a child — arsenic on my porridge, hemlock in my honey, a slow education in dying by degrees until my body learned every poison's handshake and refused to return its greeting. It is not a glamorous education. There were years I don't remember fondly, or much at all. But it left me with a tongue that could pick a lie out of a broth and a body that treated venom like rude weather: unpleasant, survivable, beneath comment.

The tribute dishes came north twice a season, one from every province that still remembered it owed King Aldric fealty, and every dish passed through my mouth before it ever crossed the ward-line toward the capital. That was the arrangement. The king ate safely in his distant palace; I got to eat pheasant in saffron and candied quince and seven kinds of honey cake, and occasionally detect a murder.

Best job in the valley. Worst job in the kingdom. Depends on the day.

Today was a detection day.

"Dump the soup," I told Brannic. "Flag the province. And send word up the hill — if the Sunward delegation arrives expecting thanks, someone should meet them with questions instead."

* * *

Sef arrived at dusk, which meant Sef arrived exactly when he intended to arrive, which meant he'd been lurking somewhere within sight of the kitchen since mid-afternoon waiting for drama, the way he always did.

He came through the back door without knocking, because Sef regarded doors as suggestions, and stood in the warm light of the ovens looking rumpled and insufferable. He wore his exile like a coat he'd outgrown but refused to replace — court mage's training in the sharp angles of his posture, vagrant's dust on everything else. Grey shot through his black hair now. He pretended not to know I'd noticed.

"I hear the soup tried to kill you," he said.

"The soup didn't try hard enough." I slid the second dish across the table toward him — spiced lamb, this one, sealed in wax with the southern duke's crest. "Taste."

"I'm not the taster."

"You're the courier. Couriers carry consequences. Consider this professional development."

He took the fork I offered, mostly because refusing would mean admitting the game mattered to him, and ate a bite with theatrical suspicion. Then a second bite without the theater, which was the real review. "Good. Too much clove. The south always over-cloves when they're nervous."

"They're nervous?"

"Everyone's nervous." He said it lightly, the way people say things they've thought about too long. "The wards have been singing off-key for a week. Haven't you noticed?"

I hadn't. Or rather — I had, and had decided not to, which in my experience amounts to the same sin with better manners.

But now that he mentioned it, I felt it. You live your whole life under wards and you stop hearing them the way you stop hearing your own heartbeat — except tonight the heartbeat had skipped, or stuttered, or changed its mind about something. A hum under the floorboards. A taste in the air like the moment before lightning, except the sky was clear and had been clear for days.

"It's probably nothing," I said.

"Probably," Sef agreed.

We let that lie between us on the table alongside the lamb.

Here is what you should understand about Sef: he was exiled eleven years ago for an act of magic so classified that even I, who make a living knowing secrets, was never told what it was. He loved the court once. He loved the king once, in the distant loyal way. And once — briefly, badly, in the years before either of us knew what we were doing — he loved me, and I him, and then the exile happened and we both pretended that chapter had ended cleanly.

It had not ended cleanly. Chapters like ours never do. We simply kept meeting at this table twice a season, trading barbs across tribute dishes, and calling the arrangement friendship because neither of us had a better word we were willing to say out loud.

"Tell me about the wards," I said. "Actually tell me. Not the courtier version."

He turned his fork over, set it down. For a moment he looked tired in a way that had nothing to do with travel. "They were built to keep magic *out*. Old wards, older than the kingdom. They work by forgetting — that's their whole cleverness. Anything magical that touches them gets unmade gently, unraveled into ordinary matter. Stone becomes stone. Fire becomes warmth and nothing more." He glanced at my hands. "You'd never hold a spark of true magic in this valley. It would forget itself before it reached your palm."

"That sounds peaceful."

"That sounds like a dam," he said. "Peaceful right up until the water finds a reason not to be."

Outside, the light was failing. I lit the lamp, and the flame caught strangely — burned a half-shade too blue, steadied, behaved. I watched it longer than I meant to.

* * *

By full dark the humming had a rhythm, and the rhythm was wrong.

I walked the kitchen after everyone had gone, checking latches out of habit, tasting the air out of profession. Copper and ozone and something faintly sweet underneath, like fruit going over. The wards didn't hum so much as *strain* now — a held note, a singer running out of breath.

From the window over the basin I could see the whole valley spread below the palace kitchens: lamplight in the town, torchlight on the wall, and above it all the stars, thick as spilled salt, the way they always were inside Emberfall's borders. Travelers swore our sky showed more stars than anywhere else in the kingdom. The wards, Sef had told me once, long ago, with wine involved: the wards polish the night. Magic keeps the dark honest.

I looked up at them while the strange note climbed and climbed beneath my feet, and I thought — with the calm, detached certainty of a woman whose entire career is noticing the wrong thing in time — *something is coming.*

I wasn't afraid, exactly. Fear implies surprise. I had spent my life loving a valley that existed behind a wall, eating well under a sky that was polished rather than real, and some part of me had always known the bill would come due. Contentment bought on credit. You don't get to be surprised by the collector.

What I felt, standing alone in the dark kitchen with a dead man's soup cooling in the slop bucket and the wards singing their last long note, was closer to grief arriving early. Grief with its boots on, waiting politely at the door for its appointed hour.

I touched the window glass. Cold. Below, a dog began to bark, then another, then a dozen, all along the valley road, all at once, as if something had passed among them that only animals could smell.

And then the humming stopped.

Not faded. Stopped — the way a heart stops, total and absolute, and the silence afterward is louder than any sound. My ears rang with the absence of a thing I'd never consciously heard in thirty-one years of living.

In the silence, high above the valley, the brightest star in the northern sky flickered.

Once.

Twice.

Like a lamp deciding whether to stay lit. Like a spoonful of soup in which something bitter has just been detected, too late, beneath the cream.

I stood very still, and waited, with my taster's tongue pressed to the roof of my mouth, for the world to tell me what it had poisoned itself with.
