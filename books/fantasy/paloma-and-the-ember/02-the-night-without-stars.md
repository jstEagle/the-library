## 2. The Night Without Stars

I want it on the record that I went to bed early because of the wards.

Not because of Sef, though his parting shot that afternoon — "Try not to die of boredom before I'm back with the winter tribute" — had lodged under my skin like a fishbone. Not because of the tribute dishes either, six of them, all clean. My tongue had found nothing but salt and ambition in the chancellor's honeyed quail, which was its own kind of poison, but not mine to taste for.

No. It was the wards. All evening they'd been humming wrong.

You live your whole life inside a sound and you stop hearing it, the way you stop feeling your own heartbeat until something reminds you it's there. The wards around Emberfall Valley were that sound for me — a low, patient thrum in the back teeth, in the floorboards, in the water. Every taster learns to notice when the bread is off before the bread knows it itself. Same instinct. And all through supper, through the washing-up, through my walk home past the granary where old Maretta was already snoring behind her counter, that hum had been climbing a note at a time, like a kettle deciding whether to scream.

So I banked my fire, checked my door-latch twice, and lay down telling myself: *if the wards are going to do something dramatic, they'll do it at midnight. Wards are theatrical like that.*

I was right, which is the worst thing I've ever been right about.

* * *

The shattering woke me before the sound did.

That's the only way I can describe it. There was a moment — one long, held breath of a moment — when I was sitting up in bed with no memory of moving, heart slamming, every hair on my arms standing like soldiers called to attention. Then the sound arrived, and it was glass. All the glass in the world, dropped from a great height, all at once, and underneath it a groan so deep I felt it in the mortar of my walls, in the bones of my skull, in the well-water trembling in its bucket.

The wards hadn't failed. They'd *broken*. There's a difference. A dam fails; a mirror breaks.

I got to the window in time to see the sky go out.

I don't mean clouds came. I mean the stars — every star, the whole spilled-salt scatter of them I'd mapped my whole life against, the Shepherd's Cart, the Two Sisters, the slow red eye of Vessk's Star that sailors swore by — went dark. Not dimmed. *Extinguished*, in the same instant, as if a hand had closed over the sky like a mouth over a candle.

And then Emberfall Valley, which had never once in my twenty-six years known true darkness — because the wards glowed faintly at night, a pearl-shimmer dome we all pretended not to see, the way you pretend not to see the king's tax collectors — Emberfall Valley went black as the inside of a closed fist.

I stood at my window in my shift with my arms wrapped around myself and I did not scream, which surprised me. What came out instead was very small and very practical: "*Oh.*"

Because there was light coming. Far off, past the palace roofs, past the terraced gardens where I'd tasted the chancellor's poisoned wine two springs ago and earned my scarlet sash — a light was rising. Orange. Moving. Falling.

Falling *toward* the kitchens.

I have thought about this since, about what made me run out my door barefoot into a night without stars or wards, down the cold lane, past Maretta's dark window (she wasn't snoring anymore; nobody in Emberfall was asleep), toward the falling fire instead of away from it. The honest answer is that I didn't decide anything. My feet decided. Or something in the light decided for me, and used my feet.

* * *

The palace kitchens were my second home. I'd learned my trade at that great scarred table, learning to hold arsenic on my tongue without swallowing, learning that almond and cyanide are cousins. So I knew, as I burst through the servants' entrance, that something was deeply wrong beyond the obvious: the ovens were lit. All of them. Nobody had lit them.

They burned empty and roaring, and their light was nothing compared to what stood in the middle of the flour-dusted floor.

It was small. That's the thing I couldn't get over, then or since. After the sky broke and the stars died, after the whole architecture of my safe little world cracked like an eggshell — what fell into the palace kitchens was a coal. A dragon's-heart of a coal, big as my two fists together, hanging a handspan above the flagstones, burning white-gold at its core with veins of red running through it like the last words of a letter written in blood. It didn't flicker. Fire flickers. This *breathed*.

The heat hit me from across the room, and it wasn't oven-heat. It was older than ovens. It smelled of lightning-struck sand and the inside of mountains, and beneath that, faintly, absurdly, of woodsmoke and bread — of home, of every hearth I'd ever warmed my hands over.

Every instinct I owned said *run*. My feet, traitors still, walked me forward.

"You should know," I told it, because talking to dangerous things is how I keep my hands steady, "that I am extremely flammable."

The ember pulsed. Once. Like laughter.

Then it moved — not rolled, not floated, but *leaned*, the way a tired traveler leans toward a lit window — and I did the single stupidest and most important thing of my life. I put out my hands to catch it.

The pain was instant and total and, strangely, clean. No blistering, no charring. It was as if the fire had gone *through* my palm and settled somewhere behind my ribs, and what remained in my hand was warmth — fierce, specific warmth, shaped exactly like a coal pressed into flesh, except my flesh was unbroken. When I pried my fingers open, the ember sat in my cupped palm like a bird that had chosen its perch, and the burn-mark it left was not a wound but a seal: a small whorled circle, gold-edged, that pulsed when I breathed.

"Oh," I said again, more sincerely this time. "Oh, no."

Because I could feel it now. Not heat — *attention*. Something vast and dying and impossibly gentle had looked at me through that coal, the way you'd look at the last dry stick in a flooded world, and it had found me *suitable*.

The kitchen doors banged open.

* * *

Sef came through them like a man thrown by a horse.

He was bleeding from a cut above his eyebrow that would need stitching, his courier grays torn at the shoulder, and he had a look on his face I'd never seen on him in eleven years of trading insults across that scarred table — Sef, who was too proud to bleed visibly, too clever to be caught running. He was running.

He stopped dead when he saw me. Saw the ember. His face did three things in one second: relief, horror, and a terrible kind of confirmation, like a physician who'd hoped the lump was nothing.

"Yara." My name in his mouth sounded different tonight. Stripped of the usual armor of irony. "Don't drop it. Don't give it to anyone. Don't let anyone take it."

"Hello to you too. You're bleeding."

"The border fell." He crossed the kitchen in five strides, took my wrist — the unburned one — and turned my hand to look at the seal, and his breath went out of him in a long shaky stream. "From the *inside*, Yara. Do you understand? The wards didn't break. Someone unmade them. Someone wearing the king's own—"

He stopped himself. Looked at the doors. Looked at the dead, starless black beyond the high windows.

"Sef." I kept my voice level, the way I do when a dish is bad and the cook is standing right there. "What is this?" I lifted my other hand, the ember pulsing gently in it, warm as a sleeping cat. "Why does it feel like it knows me?"

"It doesn't know you." He said it fast, too fast. "It chose you. Those aren't the same thing, and the difference might be the only thing that saves any of us." He pressed the heel of his hand against his eyes for a moment, and when he lowered it he was steadier, and older, and I understood that whatever had happened between sundown and now had aged him years. "The king sent me. Aldric. Before—" His jaw worked. "There isn't time. The Regent's hunters will be here within the hour, and they don't knock, and they don't leave witnesses."

"The Regent." I'd heard the word whispered in the servants' hall for a year now, always quietly, always followed by someone changing the subject. "Sef, the stars are *gone*. The wards are *gone*. I'm holding a piece of the sky in my hand and it's stuck to me like a burr, and you're going to have to explain faster than that."

"I know." He looked at me then, really looked, and for one unguarded moment he was just a boy from the lower city again, the mage's apprentice who used to steal apples with me and swear he'd be court mage someday, before the disgrace, before the exile-that-wasn't-quite-exile, before eleven years of barbs we both hid behind. "Yara. I need you to come with me. Right now. Out of the valley. North."

"North." I laughed, and it came out wrong, thin and crackling. "Sef, the valley is the world. The wards are the edges of everything. There *is* no north, there's just—"

"There's just a kingdom full of people who are about to start forgetting things." His voice cracked on it. "Starting with names. Starting with faces. Working up to why they loved them. I've seen the first village. I've seen what's coming here." He gripped my wrist again, gentle, urgent. "The ember is the last living spark of dragon-fire in existence, and it has bound itself to you, and I don't know why, and I don't care. It's the only thing the Regent fears. It's the only thing that can unbind the king. And it's in the hand of a food-taster who talks to dangerous objects, which — honestly — might be exactly why it picked her."

The ember pulsed against my palm. Warm. Patient. Certain.

Outside, far off across the valley, a bell began to ring — not the ward-bell, not any bell I knew. It rang wrong, arrhythmic, like a heart stumbling. And beneath it, faint but getting closer, came a sound I will hear until I die: hoofbeats. Many. Even. *Smooth.* As if the horses had forgotten how to gallop and remembered only marching.

Sef's face went white. "They're early."

I looked down at the small burning weight in my hand — the last star in a starless kingdom, sitting in the palm of a woman who'd spent her life tasting death so others wouldn't have to swallow it.

"Well," I said, closing my fingers around it, and the warmth climbed my arm and settled behind my ribs like it had always lived there. "I suppose I'm coming north."

The kitchen doors blew open behind us, and the night poured in.
