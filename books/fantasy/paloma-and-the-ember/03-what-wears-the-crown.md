## 3. What Wears the Crown

They took my staff at the gate, which was fair enough, and my dignity somewhere between the second and third checkpoint, which was less so.

"Name," said the guard, a boy who couldn't have been older than the wards themselves.

"Sef. Court mage, formerly of the Third Circle." I let the *formerly* sit there like a stone in the road. "Formerly of the palace entirely, if we're being precise, which I assume is why you're being thorough."

He wrote it down anyway. Bureaucracy is the one magic that never fails.

The throne room had not improved in my years of exile. It had, if anything, committed further to its own grandeur — the vaulted ceiling painted with the old constellation map, gold leaf flaking off the stars like dandruff from a god. The banners of House Aldric hung stiff as corpses on either side of the dais. And there, at the top of seven steps that seemed designed to make every petitioner's knees consider treason, sat the king.

I had loved Aldric once, in the way you love a man who taught you chess and lost to you on purpose and pretended you didn't notice. He'd been broad-shouldered then, loud, a laugh that could be heard three corridors away. The man on the throne was thinner. Grayer. His hands rested on the arms of the chair with the careful stillness of someone who has forgotten what hands are for.

And above his brow — the crown.

Now, I should explain something about crowns. A real crown is just metal. Expensive metal, yes, worked by artists, but inert. What I saw sitting on Aldric's head was not inert. It *coiled*. That's the only word for it. Like smoke poured into a mold and told to hold still, like a serpent of shadow threaded through the filigree, its tail disappearing down behind his ear and into — I swallowed — into him. Where the crown touched his temple, the skin was pale and smooth as riverstone, and I understood, with the particular horror available only to people trained to see such things, that the boundary between ornament and skull had stopped being a line and become a negotiation.

*Hollow Regent.*

Old stories. Nursemaid stories, mostly, the kind you tell children so they'll sleep with their windows shut: a hunger older than the kingdom, that eats remembering, that wears kings the way lice wear hair. Every mage of my generation had been taught to dismiss them. We were modern. We had wards. We had procedures.

The thing beneath the crown turned its attention toward me, and I felt it the way you feel a draft under a door — a coldness looking for the gap in me where my memories lived.

*Not today,* I thought, and built walls out of everything Sef-the-disgraced still had: spite, mostly. Spite is excellent masonry.

"You are the taster's mage," the king said.

His voice was wrong too. Aldric had always spoken like a man filling a room. This came out thin, rationed, each word costing something. "Sef. You delivered the tribute dishes tonight."

"I did." I bowed, because the knees demanded it. "Your Majesty honors Emberfall Valley with his appetite."

"The valley." Something flickered across his face — a man surfacing briefly through ice. "Yara Underhill. She tastes for me. She has tasted for me since—" He stopped. Frowned. I watched him reach for the memory and find the shelf empty, dusted, reorganized by something that hadn't asked permission. "Since," he finished, quietly.

My stomach dropped. Not because of the forgetting. Because of the *since* — the way he'd papered over the hole rather than admit it was there. I'd seen that before, in my grandmother, in her last year. The polite fictions the mind builds over its own erosion.

"Your Majesty," I said carefully, "how long have you worn the crown?"

"Always." Then, softer, almost puzzled: "Has it always been this heavy?"

* * *

They left us alone. That was the first truly alarming part. A king does not clear his guards from a disgraced mage without reason — unless the king, or whatever steers the king, wants something. I stood at the foot of the dais steps doing sums in my head: the wards failing tonight, the stars going out, an ember falling out of a starless sky into the kitchens of all places, and now this audience. Coincidence is a currency I stopped accepting years ago.

"Do you know what I dreamed last night?" Aldric said.

"Your Majesty—"

"A garden." He was looking at his hands. "A garden in the valley. There was a woman teaching me the names of herbs. Marjoram. Lovage. She laughed when I called the basil 'the small fierce one.' I woke up and I could not remember her face, only that she laughed, and that losing it felt like—" His jaw tightened. "Like a debt unpaid."

"That sounds like a good dream," I said, because I am a coward.

"It is the only one I have left." He looked up at me then, and for one terrible moment his eyes were fully his own — brown, sharp, amused, the eyes of the man who'd once thrown a chessboard at my head over a gambit I still maintain was legal. "Sef. How bad is it?"

I could have lied. I want that noted for the record. I opened my mouth to lie, and the Hollow Regent shifted beneath the crown — a slow, muscular ripple of dark, like something turning over in sleep — and I understood that lying would be worse than useless. It fed on secrets the way it fed on everything else. Truth was the only food it couldn't digest.

"There is a parasite in your crown," I said. "It eats memories. It has been eating yours for years, and tonight it broke the wards of Emberfall Valley to get at something, and I believe that something fell into our kitchens wearing fire."

Silence. Somewhere far off, a bell rang the hour, though the hour had no name anymore; the stars were gone and half the court's clocks had stopped agreeing with each other.

"I know," Aldric said.

"You—"

"I've known for two years." He smiled, and it was the old smile, crooked and self-deprecating, and it hurt worse than anything else that night. "You think I don't notice pieces going missing? I'm the board, Sef. I feel every square it takes." He pressed a hand to his temple, below the smooth pale skin. "It started small. My mother's voice. The smell of the stables. Then names. Then faces. Yesterday I called my daughter by my sister's name, and she cried, and I could not remember why that mattered, and *that* frightened me more than anything — that the caring itself might go. That I might stand here one morning perfectly content, hollowed and happy, and never know there had ever been an Aldric inside this Aldric."

"Why haven't you told anyone? Why not cast it off? Burn the crown, abdicate, run to the mountains—"

"And leave the kingdom to it?" For a moment the old commander surfaced. "It doesn't live in the crown, Sef. The crown is just where it perches. Cast it off and it goes hunting elsewhere — into the court, the city, the wards. At least up here I can feel what it takes. At least I keep count." He exhaled. "Besides. There's a reason it hasn't finished me."

"The ember."

His head came up sharply. "You've seen it?"

"It landed in the palace kitchens tonight. Seared itself to Yara Underhill's palm. She's—" I hesitated over how to describe it. "She's carrying it. It answers her heartbeat. Not mine. I tried."

Something moved through the king's face — hope, but hope with fear wrapped around it, the way a sword is wrapped around a hilt. "Then listen to me, because I don't know how many lucid minutes I have left, and I can feel it circling right now, patient as winter." He leaned forward, gripping the armrests. "The Regent isn't just hungry. It's *homesick*. Whatever it is, whatever it was — it lost something, and it's been trying to fill the hole ever since, and memories are the only thing that fits badly enough to keep it eating. The ember is dragon-fire. The last of it. If the stories hold any truth, the fire and the Regent are two halves of one grief."

"Your Majesty, with respect, you're speaking in riddles and I'm standing in a room with a monster wearing your head—"

"*Find it.*" The word cracked out of him with the full weight of the throne, and then immediately he shrank, wincing, as if the crown had punished him for the volume. When he spoke again it was barely a whisper. "Find the ember. Find what it truly is. Before it gutters out, before the Regent grows strong enough to stop pretending, before—" He stopped. Swallowed. "Before I forget my own name. Because I can feel it, Sef. *Aldric* is getting thin. Some mornings I wake and the name sits strangely on me, like a coat cut for a bigger man. And when the name goes, the rest goes fast. A king is mostly name. Take away the name and you've got a shape on a throne, and shapes on thrones are exactly what things like that underneath my crown were made to steer."

I stood there, forty-one years old, disgraced, unarmed, staffless, and terrified, and I heard myself say: "I'll need help."

"You'll have it. There's a woman in the kitchens with a dragon's heart burning in her hand and the strongest will I've ever tasted in a tribute dish." He almost smiled. "Start there."

* * *

The guards returned. The moment they did, the light went out of Aldric's eyes like a lamp shuttered — not gradually, but all at once, the lucidity folding away, and the thing beneath the crown settled deeper, satisfied, and the king looked at me with polite, distant incomprehension and said, "Was there something else, master mage?"

"No, Your Majesty," I said. "Nothing else."

I walked backward down the seven steps, because you don't turn your back on a throne, especially not that one. At the doors I glanced back once. Aldric sat straight and gray and still, hands folded, a portrait of kingship — and beneath the crown, coiled in the gold like a snake in tall grass, the darkness was watching me leave.

It didn't stop me. That was the part I kept turning over as they escorted me through the starless corridors, past guards whose faces were beginning to look very slightly too smooth. It *wanted* me to go to the ember. It wanted the ember found.

Which meant either I was walking into a trap —

— or the trap was already sprung, and I was the part of it that moved.

I found Yara in the kitchen passage, awake, cradling her bandaged hand against her chest like something precious and cursed, glaring at me with the exact expression she wore whenever I arrived anywhere.

"Well?" she said. "You look like a man who's been talking to ghosts."

"Worse," I said. "I've been talking to the king. Pack light, Yara. We're leaving tonight, and I suspect the whole kingdom is about to forget we were ever allowed to stay."
