## 4. A Kingdom Gone Dim

*Yara*

The first thing I learned about fleeing a city was that nobody tells you which way the exits are.

You'd think a food-taster would know. I'd walked every corridor of Aldric's palace a hundred times, memorized the kitchens down to the scorch mark where Cook's temper had once met a flambé gone wrong. But corridors look different when you're running through them with a piece of burning sky clutched to your chest and a man behind you shouting things like *left, LEFT, unless you want to die, in which case carry on.*

"Which way is left?" I shouted back.

"Your other other left!"

"That's not how lefts work!"

"It is tonight!" Sef grabbed my wrist and hauled me down a servant's stair so narrow our shoulders scraped both walls. Above us, something heavy went through a window — glass, then armor, then a sound like a bell being murdered. The palace bells had been ringing since midnight, but wrong. Not alarm-rhythm. Something slower. Hungrier. Like the bells themselves had forgotten what they were for and were just making noise because noise was all they had left.

The ember pulsed against my sternum where I'd wrapped it in my apron. It had been warm before. Now it was *rhythmic*, and it took me three flights of stairs to realize why.

It was beating with me.

I stopped on the landing. Sef crashed into my back, swore in what I sincerely hoped wasn't spell-language, because if it was, we were both about to grow feathers or something.

"It's doing something," I said.

"Everything in this city is doing something. Most of it is trying to kill us. Move."

"No — listen." I pressed my palm flat over the apron. Under the cloth, light seeped between my fingers like water through a fist. "It's keeping time. With my heart."

Sef went very still behind me. In the dimness his face did the thing it always did when he was recalculating the universe — jaw tight, eyes going somewhere over my shoulder as if the answer might be standing there. It never was. I'd watched him do it for years across the tribute table while pretending not to notice.

"That shouldn't be possible," he said.

"You say that a lot."

"Because the world keeps insisting on it." He reached toward the ember and I caught his hand.

"Don't."

"I'm not going to hurt it."

"You're going to *poke* it. You poke everything." I said it without thinking, the way you say old habits, and then heard what I'd said. His hand stopped midair. For a moment neither of us moved, and somewhere above us the bells tolled their empty toll, and the ember beat its small stubborn beat against my ribs like a second heart that had decided mine was worth copying.

"Sef," I said carefully. "When you tried your spells on it. Back in the kitchen."

"They didn't take."

"They didn't take," I agreed. "But it's been answering me since the moment it landed in my hands. So maybe stop treating it like a problem to solve and start treating it like—" I didn't have a word. "Like something that chose."

He lowered his hand slowly. "Chose you."

"Apparently I'm very charming when someone's dying."

"That has historically been true, yes."

And there it was again — that flicker under the sarcasm, the thing we'd spent six years burying under trade barbs and tribute schedules. Six years since the trial. Since they'd stripped his robes and put him on the road and I'd stood in the gallery and said nothing, because saying something would have cost me the only life I had, and I had been a coward about it, and we had both pretended otherwise ever since.

"We're not doing this now," I said.

"No," he agreed. "We're doing this *running*."

We ran.

* * *

*Sef*

Here is what nobody tells you about disgrace: it teaches you the back ways.

Six years of exile will map a city onto you like scar tissue. I knew which gutters ran beneath the nobles' quarter, which wine merchants kept cellar doors that opened onto streets nobody patrolled, which walls had been mortared by masons who cut corners and could be climbed by anyone desperate enough. I had spent those years telling myself the knowledge was bitterness. Turns out it was provision. The gods have a sense of humor; it's just a very slow one.

We came up out of the cellars into the tanners' district, and I made the mistake of looking back at the palace.

The Hollow Court had lit itself. That was the only word for it — the citadel glowed from within like a lantern made of skin, pale gold light leaking from every window, and around it the sky bent inward, starless, drinking. And below it, moving through the streets in perfect silence, were the knights.

They swept the avenues in ranks. No torches. They didn't need them; their armor gave off a faint shineless gleam, like fish underwater. And their faces —

Their faces were smooth.

Not masked. Masked faces have edges, straps, intention. These were simply *absent*, the way an egg is absent of anything, curved and blank and complete. I had seen one up close in the throne room, had watched it turn its head toward King Aldric with the patience of something that had been hungry for a very long time and intended to keep being hungry long after everything else was eaten.

"Yara." My voice came out lower than I meant it to. "Don't run for the north gate."

"The north gate is the way out of the city."

"The north gate is where they *want* us to go out of the city." I pulled her into the shadow of a drying shed, into air thick enough to chew. Tanneries. She'd thank me later, or more likely insult me later, which amounted to the same thing with her. "Aldric taught me that much, back when he still remembered teaching me anything. A trap wants you to move. So we move wrong."

"Wrong how?"

"Through the river district. Through the places with too many smells and too many turns." I looked at her. In the dark she was mostly eyes and the faint gold glow leaking through her apron, which she'd knotted tighter without seeming to notice. "Can you make it do that less?"

"It does it when I'm scared."

"Are you scared?"

"I'm *fleeing a city full of faceless knights* with *you*, so yes, Sef, I'm having a wonderful calm evening."

The glow brightened. I watched it happen — watched the light swell with her pulse, quick and stubborn, and felt something cold slide down my spine that had nothing to do with knights.

Every text I'd ever studied said dragon-fire answered bloodlines. Royal lines. Old pacts sealed in centuries I'd only read about. It should have answered *me* — I was the mage, I was the one who'd spent ten years learning the grammar of fire, and it sat in her apron beating time to her heartbeat like she was the only song it knew.

"Yara," I said. "When the wards fell. When it landed. Did it burn you?"

She held up her palm.

In the dark I couldn't see what I'd seen in the kitchen — the mark like a brand of a closed eye. But I remembered it. I remembered thinking it looked less like a wound than like a *signature*. Something signing its name.

"No," I said, before she could speak. "Whatever you're about to ask — no. We're not stopping to theorize. Theorizing is how people die in cities being eaten by memory."

"You're theorizing right now."

"I'm allowed. I'm trained."

"You're *exiled*."

"Exiled *trained*." I took her arm and turned us south, toward the river, toward the long ugly road north that nobody took because nobody wanted to go anywhere near the caldera. Which was exactly why we were taking it. "Come on. If we reach the ferry before dawn, we can be past the flood-locks by morning."

"And after that?"

"After that," I said, "we find out why a piece of the sky picked a food-taster."

Behind us, faint and patient, the bells went on ringing their hollow count. And beneath them — so faint I almost missed it, and would spend the next week wishing I had — something else began to ring. A second bell. Farther off. North.

Where we were going.

* * *

*Yara*

By the third day on the north road, I had learned three things.

One: Sef could sleep sitting upright against a tree and wake instantly at any sound heavier than a squirrel, which suggested his exile had been considerably worse than the official record admitted.

Two: the ember liked music. Or maybe it liked *me liking* music — I'd caught myself humming on the second evening, some valley tune my mother used to sing, and the light had swelled in time until the whole hollow we were hiding in glowed like a lamp. Sef had watched with the expression of a man watching his entire profession become obsolete in real time.

Three: trust is a strange thing to build out of shared terror, but it builds fast.

We were walking the old drover's road through country that got quieter the farther north we went. Not peaceful-quiet. Subtracted-quiet. On the second day we'd passed a farmstead where a woman stood in her field pulling weeds, over and over, the same row, and when Sef asked her the way to the ford she'd looked at him with polite blank eyes and said, "I'm sorry. What is a ford *for*?" She'd known how to weed. She'd known the motion, the rhythm, her hands sure in the dirt. She just no longer knew why she was doing it, or what the field would become, or that she'd ever known anything else.

I hadn't slept well after that. Neither had Sef, though he pretended otherwise, which was its own kind of answer.

Now, dusk of the third day, we sat on a fallen wall above a valley that should have been familiar-country to me — hills like Emberfall's hills, orchards like Emberfall's orchards — except that the lights in the farmhouse windows burned steady and joyless, and nobody anywhere was singing.

"Do you think it's already been here," I said, "the thing that did that? The Regent?"

Sef was cleaning his one surviving knife, which he did when he was thinking, which was constantly. "Or it's ahead of us. Hunger moves faster than grief."

"Grief?"

"It was a keeper once. Before it was a crown." He said it quietly, not quite to me. "Things that guard things, when they lose the things — I don't think they get better."

I looked down at my palm. The mark had deepened over three days, the closed-eye shape now fine-lined and permanent, and when I pressed it the ember answered — a pulse of warmth up the wrist, almost companionable. Almost *fond*.

"It talks to me," I said. "Not words. More like... pressure. When the hunters were close in the city, it went cold before I heard anything. Last night it warmed up an hour before dawn like it knew I'd need convincing to get up." I hesitated. "Sef. Why me? You've never once asked why me."

He set the knife down. For a long moment he watched the joyless windows across the valley, and I watched him decide whether to lie, and then watched him decide not to, and understood that this — this small, expensive honesty — was worth more than anything he'd given me in six years of barbed tribute-table conversation.

"Because I don't know," he said. "And because whatever the reason is, I'm afraid it's going to cost you. Fire like that doesn't choose vessels for convenience, Yara. It chooses them for *capacity*." He finally looked at me. "And you've spent your whole life training your body to hold things that should kill you."

Poison-immunity. Twelve years of measured doses, building a body that could carry death without dying. I'd thought of it as a skill. A trade. A way to matter in a kingdom that had never needed me to matter.

The ember beat against my chest, warm and patient, like something that had read the same recipe I had and liked the ingredients.

Down in the valley, one by one, the farmhouse lights began to go out — not extinguished, I realized. *Dimmed.* Drawn toward something. And far to the north, past the dark hills, a village that should have had lights had none at all, and the road bent toward it whether we wanted it to or not.

"Sef," I said. "How far to that town?"

He followed my gaze. I felt him go still beside me.

"Half a day," he said. "Why?"

"Because I don't think we're the ones choosing the road anymore."
