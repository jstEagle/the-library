## 5. The Village That Forgot Its Name

The town announced itself by smell before we ever saw it: bread baking, woodsmoke, the green tang of a millpond. After three days of empty road and Sef's increasingly inventive curses at the starless sky, it smelled like salvation.

"Brindlemere," Sef said, reading a leaning signpost at the crossroads. "Population, apparently, everyone."

"Everyone's still here," I said. "That's more than I can say for Hallowford." We hadn't spoken about Hallowford. The village we'd passed through yesterday had been empty in the way that made your skin crawl — doors open, meals half-eaten, a doll in the road with no footprints around it. Whatever the Regent's hunters did, they did thoroughly.

Brindlemere was not empty. Brindlemere was worse.

We came down the hill into late afternoon light that had no stars waiting behind it, and the town went about its business like a music box wound by someone who'd forgotten the song. A woman swept her step. She swept it well — brisk strokes, corners attended — and when she reached the end of the porch she turned around and began again, her face as blank as fresh paper. A man split firewood with the easy rhythm of thirty years' practice, stacking each piece precisely, and if you asked him what winter he was preparing for, I'd have bet my tongue he couldn't tell you.

"They're not hurt," Sef said quietly.

"No," I said. "They're *hollowed*."

I knew hollow. Hollow was my trade, in a way. You can't taste poison for a living without learning what emptiness looks like on a face — the moment before the pupil widens, the slackness where a person used to be deciding things. Every face in Brindlemere wore it. Not dead. Worse than dead: *maintained*. Bodies running on habit the way a clock runs on weights, ticking away beautifully with no one inside to read the time.

A baker pulled loaves from his oven and set them on the windowsill to cool. Perfect loaves. Golden, scored in a pattern I recognized from the capital's finest quarter — the same pattern, in fact, our kitchen apprentices spent years failing to master. He set them down, stood a moment with flour on his hands, and then turned back to his bench and began shaping dough that would never be eaten, because there was no one left in him who knew why bread mattered.

"He remembers *how*," Sef said. His voice had gone strange. "Not *why*. The Regent eats meaning first. Skill is muscle memory — too cheap, too deep in the hands. But the reasons, the names, the—" He stopped. He was looking at a girl of maybe eight who sat on a fence rail, braiding grass into rings with perfect competence, humming a tuneless note over and over because she could not remember how the rest of the song went.

My hand closed around the ember through my shirt. It pulsed against my palm — warm, eager, answering my heartbeat the way it always did now, quickening when I was afraid. It had been quickening a lot since Hallowford.

"Sef," I said. "Could it fix them?"

He didn't answer fast enough, which was an answer.

"Don't do that," I said. "Don't do the court-mage thing where you weigh words before you spend them. Could it?"

"It could," he said finally. "It's dragon-fire. Dragon-fire is *memory* — the oldest kind, the kind the world keeps so it doesn't forget itself. If Yara's ember touched one of them—"

"*Our* ember," I said. "*Yara*? Since when does the last living spark of dragon-fire have a name?"

"Since it chose a food-taster over a mage, apparently." He said it lightly, but his eyes weren't light. They were doing arithmetic. I'd seen that look on him before, back when we were young and stupid and he was showing me ward-sigils scratched into a tavern table, explaining why some sums only come out one way no matter how much you wish otherwise.

"And the cost," I said.

"The cost," he agreed, "is everything."

* * *

We took shelter in the inn, because the innkeeper let rooms to anyone who walked in and asked — asking was apparently optional, but it felt better. The common room was full at supper, every table occupied, and the silence in it was the loudest thing I'd ever heard. Forty people eating stew they'd made from recipes their hands remembered, tasting nothing, saying nothing, looking at nothing. A woman fed a man across from her, lifting the spoon to his mouth, and neither of them knew the other's name. Their hands moved together easily. Someone had taught those hands, once. Someone had loved them, probably. The love was gone; the choreography remained, like dancers performing at a funeral.

I couldn't eat.

Sef watched me not eat for the length of one entire bowl going cold, and then said, carefully, "Yara."

"You're going to talk me out of something," I said. "I can hear it coming. Save your breath."

"I'm going to *try*," he said. "Because if I don't, you'll do whatever reckless thing is currently glowing behind your ribs, and I'll have failed you twice in one lifetime."

"That's dramatic, even for you."

"I'm a disgraced court mage hiding in a haunted town with the last fire in the world strapped to the chest of a woman who tastes poison for fun. Drama is the honest register." He leaned forward. "Here's the sum, then, plainly. The ember holds enough fire to unbind the king — barely. Vessk said so, and Vessk has forgotten more about dragons than either of us will ever learn, which in his case is literal. Every spark we give away is a spark we don't have when we reach Aldric. Feed one villager, the flame dims. Feed forty—"

"—and the king forgets his daughter's name forever, and the Regent wins, and the whole kingdom ends up sweeping porches until the world runs down," I finished. "Yes. I did the arithmetic too. My sums are smaller but I have them."

"Then you understand why I'm talking you out of it."

"Do I?" I looked at him. "Because here's mine, Sef, and you're going to hate it. I'm a food-taster. Do you know what my whole life *is*? I put things in my mouth that might kill me so someone else doesn't have to. That's the job. That's been the job since I was sixteen, and I've never once resented it, because it's the only honest work I know: take the risk into your own body so it stays out of someone else's." I spread my hands. "And now the risk is sitting under my shirt, warm as a hearthstone, and there are forty people in this room who will wake up tomorrow having lost another day of their lives to a thing that ate them, and I'm supposed to walk past them because of *budgeting*?"

"Yes," Sef said. "If the budget is the sky."

We stared at each other. Around us the silent room ate its tasteless stew.

"The ember answers your heartbeat," he said, softer. "Not my spells. Mine just bounce off it — I told you, on the road, why do you think that is? It didn't choose me. It chose someone whose whole life is *taking harm so others don't*. It picked you the way a lock picks a key. And a key that spends itself opening every door on the road arrives at the last door hollowed out." His voice cracked, just slightly, on the word. "You'd be them, Yara. Sweeping a porch somewhere with beautiful hands and nobody home."

I wanted to argue. That was the terrible part — I wanted it the way the hungry want bread. Because he was right, and being right is the argument that never loses, and I hated him for it in the old familiar way, the way you hate someone who loved you enough to leave.

Instead I stood up, and crossed the room to the woman who had been feeding the man, and asked her — knowing she wouldn't answer — "What's his name?"

She looked up at me with mild, pleasant nothing. "I set his place," she said. "Every night. I don't recall learning to."

I walked back to our table and sat down and put my face in my hands.

"There might be a middle way," Sef said after a while. He said it the way you offer a rope to someone drowning, already apologizing for the knots. "Vessk said the ember regenerates — slowly. Dragon-fire isn't fuel, it's a *living thing*. If you gave one spark, one small touch, to a single person—"

"One," I said. "Out of forty."

"One is not nothing."

One is not nothing. I thought about my father, dead eleven years, and how I'd have burned the whole valley for one more hour of him. I thought about King Aldric, whom I'd never met, tasting dishes in his name every day of my adult life — my duty, distant as a star, and both of us now starless.

"One person," I said. "The girl. The one braiding grass. She gets the song back or she gets nothing, and I decide which, and I'll live with it."

Sef exhaled. "You realize I'm going to be the one who carries you out of here if it goes wrong."

"You've carried worse," I said. "You carried yourself for fifteen years. That took real strength."

* * *

The girl was still on the fence rail at dusk, braiding grass in the blue dark, humming her broken note. Up close she had flour on her sleeve — the baker's flour — and a smudge of it on her cheek, and I thought: somebody taught her that braid. A mother, probably. A mother whose name she no longer kept.

I knelt. The ember came to my hand without being called, sliding warm out of my collar like a cat that knows dinner time. In its light her face was small and serious and utterly vacant, and my heart broke cleanly, the way bones break — one sharp sound and then a long ache.

"Hey," I said. "This might feel like breakfast."

I pressed my palm, and the ember, gently as I have ever felt anything move, to her forehead.

Fire went *into* her. Not burning — filling, the way water fills a dry cup, and for one heartbeat the ember dimmed in my hand so badly that terror shot through me cold as well-water — and then it steadied, banked, alive, a candle instead of a bonfire. And the girl blinked. Blinked again. Her brow furrowed, and her lips parted, and the hum in her throat changed key, found its next note, its next —

"—*da-da-da-daaa*," she sang, startled at herself, and then she looked at me with eyes that had someone behind them, and said, "Where's Mama? Mama's got the loaves, she'll burn them, she always burns the ends—"

"She's fine," I lied, because her mother was three houses down remembering nothing, and the ember had given this child herself and not one soul besides. One spark. One person. The math held, and the math was monstrous, and I knelt in the road holding a dying fire while a little girl ran toward a house singing a song she didn't know she'd lost.

Behind me, Sef caught my arm as I swayed. "How much?" he demanded. "How much did it cost?"

"A candle's worth," I whispered. "Maybe less. Maybe—"

But even as I spoke, the ember flickered against my palm, and in its guttering light I saw — clear as a painted miniature, vivid as yesterday — my father's kitchen, the copper pots, his laugh, the exact smell of the valley in autumn.

And then it was simply *gone*, smooth and painless, like a page turned by someone else's hand.

I stood very still.

"Sef," I said. "What did I just lose?"

His face, in the emberlight, told me everything before his mouth could lie about any of it.

"We need to reach the caldera," he said. "Tonight."
