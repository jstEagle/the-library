## 6. Ashes of the First Dragon

I had told myself the climb would kill us before the Regent's hunters could, which I'd decided counted as optimism.

The caldera of Ashenreach did not look like a ruin so much as a wound that had never learned to close. The mountain's crown had been torn out centuries ago, leaving a bowl of black glass and bone-white ash, and the air tasted the way a forge smells — like something enormous was still being made, or unmade, somewhere beneath our feet. Yara climbed ahead of me with the ember cupped against her sternum, wrapped in three layers of wool that glowed through them anyway, like a lantern someone had tried to smother in a blanket.

"Tell me again why we're walking into the one place on the map marked *do not*," she said. Her breath came out in little silver flags.

"Because Vessk is the only creature alive who remembers what the Regent is."

"And you trust it?"

"I trust its memory," I said. "The rest of it can go hang."

She laughed — a short, startled sound, the kind people make when they've been frightened too long to do anything else. Three days north from the capital, sleeping in ditches while smooth-faced knights rode past close enough to count their buttons, and she still found things funny. I had loved her for that once. I was trying very hard not to love her for it now, because love was a liability with a heartbeat, and everything with a heartbeat in this kingdom was being hunted.

The ember pulsed against her chest. It had been doing that more often — keeping time with her heart, or perhaps conducting it. Sef's spells slid off it like rain off oiled leather. Yara breathed near it and it flared. I had stopped pretending this meant nothing.

* * *

We found Vessk at the bottom of the caldera, coiled in the ash like a mountain that had lain down and decided to stay there.

I want to be precise about this, because precision is all I have left of my old life: dragons are supposed to be gone. Every text in the royal library said so. Every mage who ever burned at the stake for heresy said otherwise, quietly, and we were right. Vessk was vast beyond sense — grey-scaled, ash-caked, wings folded into humps along her spine like eroded hills. Her eyes were white as boiled milk. Blind. Ancient. She smelled us coming from the ridge, because of course she did.

"A taster," she said. Her voice was gravel poured slowly. "And a mage who smells of burnt books and worse decisions. And between you—" her nostrils flared, and the whole caldera seemed to inhale with her— "*my heart.*"

Yara went very still. "Your what?"

Vessk turned her blind face toward us. It should have been terrifying. It was, a little. But mostly it was tired in a way that made my bones ache to look at.

"Come down, small ones," she said. "If I wanted you dead, the ash would already have taken you, and I would not have bothered to speak first. Dragons are rude that way. We skip the courtesies."

We descended. The ash held our weight like snow. Up close, Vessk's scales were scarred everywhere — not battle scars, though there were those too, but long deliberate lines, ritual cuts filled with something dark that had long since gone inert. Brands. Old brands, in patterns I recognized from forbidden texts: keeper's marks.

"You were bound," I said. "Once. To serve."

"To keep," Vessk corrected. "There is a difference, and the difference is the entire history of your kingdom, mage, though no one alive has the manners to know it." She lowered her head until it hung over us like a storm front. "Sit. Ash makes an honest chair. And I will tell you about the Hollow Regent, since you have dragged its stolen heart across half a starless country to hear it."

* * *

"Before your king," Vessk said, "before the wards, before Emberfall had a name, there were dragons, and where there are dragons there must be a keeper. Dragon-fire does not govern itself. Left alone it either dies of loneliness or burns the world out of sheer boredom. The First Races understood this. So they made a keeper — not a dragon, not a mortal. Something between. Something patient. They gave it our hearts to hold."

"The ember," Yara whispered.

"One ember. The last. But once there were hundreds, and the keeper held them all in a crown of fire, and the dragons slept easy under its watch, and the world was warm." Vessk's tail shifted; a wave of ash rolled off it. "And then your ancestors — clever, frightened, ambitious creatures — decided warmth was better owned than shared. There was a war. You will not find it in your histories; the victors wrote none, they simply burned the libraries. The keeper fought for us. It lost. And when it lost, they did not kill it, because it could not be killed. Instead they took its crown of hearts away and hid them, one by one, until a single spark remained. Then they bound the keeper into something new. A shape that could walk among mortals. A shape that could serve."

"Serving the crown," I said. My mouth had gone dry.

"Serving the crown," Vessk agreed. "They hollowed it out and filled the hollow with obedience, and they set it to guard the kings of men, generation upon generation, wearing whatever regalia each age preferred. Your Regent has worn rings, scepters, thrones. For the last two hundred years it has worn a circlet, and sat coiled beneath it, and done exactly as it was compelled."

"But it's eating Aldric," Yara said. "His memories. His—"

"It is starving." The word cracked out of her like a whip. The ash around us shivered. "Forgive me. It is starving, child. Do you understand what they made it? A keeper without a single heart to keep. They left it one spark — one — and then they bound it so deep it cannot even touch that. It sits beside its own stolen heart for centuries and feels nothing but hunger and duty, hunger and duty, and every few generations the binding frays a little, and the hunger leaks. Into whoever wears the crown. It drinks memories because memories are the nearest thing to fire a soul can burn. That is all your monster is. That is all it has ever been. A betrayed servant, starving in plain sight, blamed for the wound it was dealt."

The silence after that was long. Somewhere above, wind moved through the caldera rim with a sound like distant singing.

Yara looked down at the bundle against her chest. Through the wool, the ember pulsed — steady, warm, patient. Like something waiting a very long time to come home.

"So the wards," I said. My voice sounded far away to me. "When they fell—"

"They did not fall," Vessk said. "They were let fall. The binding on the Regent is woven through the ward-lattice of your valley — the same hands built both, the same age, the same purpose. When the binding weakens past mending, the wards weaken with it. Your king's parasite did not break into your world, taster. Your world's cage finally grew too rotten to hold its prisoner, and everything the cage was holding fell out together." Her blind eyes found me, or seemed to. "You have spent your lives behind walls built to keep magic out. No one thought to ask what else the walls kept in."

* * *

I walked away from the dragon to the caldera's edge, because my legs wanted to move and my thoughts wanted to argue, and the two activities had always helped each other.

Here is what I knew as a court mage, disgraced twice over, exiled to delivering tribute dishes up a mountain: the Hollow Regent was a horror. It wore knights with egg-smooth faces. It ate an old friend of mine one memory at a time while he still smiled and called it wisdom. Whatever Vessk said about grievance, I had watched its work up close, and grief does not excuse appetite.

Here is what I knew now, standing in a dead dragon's cradle with the truth sitting on me like a second spine: the monster had been wronged first. Profoundly, systematically, by the same crown Yara and I were trying to save. If we unbound King Aldric — if we freed him from the thing coiled beneath his crown — we would not be destroying a parasite. We would be taking its heart away a second time. Leaving it starving in the dark forever, with its hunger loose in the world and no binding left to leash it.

Or.

Or we gave it back.

Give the Regent its heart, and the binding breaks — that part Vessk had confirmed, in words so old they predated grammar. The keeper, whole, would release its grip on the king. Aldric freed. Kingdom saved. And the keeper — fed, restored, remembering what it was before men hollowed it — might remember mercy too. Might become what it was made to be: a guardian of fire instead of a devourer of memory.

*Might.* The whole plan balanced on *might*, and underneath *might* lay a floorless dark full of every person in the capital whose face had gone smooth.

"You're going to tell me we should give it back," Yara said behind me. I hadn't heard her follow. The ember lit her from below, throwing her features upward into something older than her years.

I looked at her a long time. "Aren't you going to argue?"

"With a blind dragon's version of events?" She shrugged. "I taste things for a living, Sef. I know poison when I hear it, and that wasn't poison. It was just bitter. Bitter things are usually true." She paused. "But giving it back means the ember goes to the thing hunting us. The thing that emptied a whole village of its own children's names. What if it takes its heart and stays hungry anyway?"

"Then we'll have armed the end of the world," I said. "Cheerfully. On a dragon's recommendation."

"Wonderful." She smiled without any humor in it, and then, terribly, with some. "You realize what it needs to reignite, don't you? Vessk said it. Memories. Fire for a keeper is memory — that's why it eats them. If we hand it the ember, it won't just need the spark. It'll need fuel."

The cold that went through me then had nothing to do with altitude.

"No," I said.

"You don't even know what I was going to say."

"You were going to volunteer something," I said. "You're always going to volunteer something. It's your worst quality. Well. Second worst. The first is liking my jokes."

She didn't laugh. She held the ember up between us, and it pulsed, and I swear on every burned book of my ruined career it pulsed *toward her*, leaning like a plant toward light.

"Vessk," I called, not turning around. "The keeper. If it got its heart back — if it got enough fire to be whole — what would it want?"

The great head lifted. Ash sheeted off her neck in slow waterfalls.

"What it always wanted," Vessk said. "What any keeper wants. Something to tend. Something to keep warm. Someone to guard who is not yet ash." She turned her blind face to the sky — to the starless sky, the wound in the world that had opened the night the wards fell. "Look up, mage. Your kingdom's stars did not die. They were swallowed. The keeper drank them in its starving, the way a starving man eats his own coat. Give it back its heart, and it will give the sky back its light. That is the bargain your ancestors were too frightened to make, and it is the only bargain left."

Above us, the darkness was absolute. Not one star. Not one. And I thought of Aldric's face the last time I saw him lucid, one moment of clarity in a fog of theft, gripping my wrist: *find the ember before I forget my own name.*

And I thought: the price keeps changing shape. First it was a journey. Then a war. Now it was going to be something with a heartbeat and a name, and I knew — I knew the way I know the taste of arsenic in wine, instantly, absolutely — that Yara had already decided.

"We leave at dawn," I said, because someone had to say something practical, and practicality is how cowards pray. "And Yara? Whatever you're planning—"

She was already walking back toward the dragon, ember glowing, asking Vessk in a low voice how much fire a sky needed.

"—don't," I finished, to nobody.
