## 7. The Price of Immunity

The hunters found us at the ford.

I want to say we fought them off with cunning and valor, but the truth is we were arguing about breakfast when the smooth-faced knights came out of the reeds like a bad memory surfacing, and all our cunning went into running.

"Left!" Sef shouted.

I went right, because the left bank was crawling with egg-faced death and I have always believed in choosing my own way to die. The ember flared against my chest — it lived in a sling of oiled silk now, tucked under my shirt, and it had taken to warming when danger came the way a cat takes to hiding under furniture: uselessly, but with great conviction.

We made the treeline. We lost them in the birches. And then, three miles on, my legs gave out and I sat down hard against a fallen log and laughed until Sef looked at me like I'd gone mad, which, fair.

"You're laughing," he said.

"I'm alive," I said. "It's a recent development and I'm celebrating."

He crouched in front of me, checking me for wounds with those quick mage's hands, and that's when he saw it. My palm. The one the ember had seared itself into on the night the stars died — the mark had faded weeks ago to a pale silver whorl, barely there. Tonight it was glowing. Bright as a coin at the bottom of a well.

Sef went very still. Sef going still is never good news. Stillness is what he does instead of screaming.

"Yara," he said carefully, "when did you last feed it?"

"Feed it? It's not a hamster, Sef."

"It's been dimming for days. You've noticed. Don't lie to me, you notice everything." He took my hand, turned it over, and his thumb traced the whorl. "It's supposed to gutter without a hearth. Without a dragon. It should be ash by now. So why isn't it?"

"Because I'm an excellent host?"

"Yara."

"Sef."

He didn't laugh. He did the other thing, the thing where he pulled out every book he'd memorized and none he'd written down, and I watched him arrive somewhere terrible in real time.

"The immunity," he said quietly. "Your whole career. Twenty years of drinking poison in milligram doses. Arsenic in your morning tea. Wolfsbane tincture. Serpent venom, drip by drip by drip. You built yourself into something no toxin can touch."

"I built myself into someone who can eat the king's dinner without dying. It's less glamorous than it sounds. Mostly it's stomach cramps."

"No." His grip tightened on my hand. "You built yourself into a vessel. Do you understand? The ember is fire, yes, but it's also the last living thing of its kind, and living things choose. It needed a body that could hold it without being burned away. Not a mage — we're full of channels, it would have poured through us like water through a sieve. Not a knight, too much iron in the blood. It needed someone *proofed*. Someone who'd spent a lifetime making herself immune to everything that kills."

The forest was very quiet. Somewhere behind us, water moved over stones at the ford, indifferent.

"So it picked me," I said. "Out of everyone in a kingdom of millions. Lucky me."

"It didn't pick you at random." Sef's voice had gone strange. "Think about where it fell, Yara. Of all the kitchens, all the cities, all the people under that collapsing sky — it fell into *your* hands. Into the kitchen you were standing in. That wasn't chance. That was aim."

I wanted to argue. I opened my mouth to argue. But the ember pulsed against my sternum, warm and unmistakable, the way it always did when I spoke near it — and I realized it had been pulsing every time I said its name, every time I thought about it, every time my heart rate climbed. It answered my heartbeat. Sef had told me that himself, back on the road north, back when it was a curiosity and not a diagnosis.

Living things choose. And this one had chosen me before I ever knew it existed.

"What's the cost?" I asked. Because there is always a cost; I am a food-taster, cost is my entire profession. Nothing in this world gets eaten without something paying for it.

Sef closed his eyes. "Vessk said the Regent eats memories. That's what it became after losing its heart — a hunger wearing a crown. The ember is the opposite of the hunger. It's everything the Regent gave up. But fire needs fuel, Yara, and it's been burning on nothing for centuries, and now—"

"Now it has me."

"Now it has twenty years of poisons metabolized into something it can drink. Your body doesn't just resist toxins. It *transmutes* them. That's the real trick of tasters, the part they don't put in the ballads — we turn poison into tolerance, harm into armor. The ember can burn that. It can burn what you are." He opened his eyes, and they were wet, which frightened me more than the hunters had. "And memories aren't stored in some little jar in your head. They're chemistry. They're you. If you let it draw deep enough—"

"How deep?"

"Don't."

"*How deep*, Sef?"

"Deep enough that you might forget Emberfall," he said. "The valley. The wards. The taste of river water in summer. Me." His voice cracked on the last word and he hated it, I could see him hating it. "Deep enough that you could stand in the place you grew up and feel nothing at all."

* * *

We argued for two hours. I won, which is to say I decided, which is to say I lost everything except the argument.

Here is how it went, roughly: Sef said no. Sef said there had to be another way, another vessel, another road, and when I pointed out there wasn't, he said we'd find one anyway because that's what people do, they find ways, and I said people also die waiting for ways that don't exist. He said the king wasn't worth it. Then he immediately retracted it, because Aldric had been his friend since they were boys stealing apples from the orchard below the west tower, and Sef is incapable of sustaining a lie even to win an argument.

"He's worth it," Sef admitted finally, to the ground. "That's the problem. Everything's worth it to you. The king, the villagers at Hollowmere whose names came back for one bright hour because you spent a sliver of the flame on them — you'd spend the whole thing on strangers if it came to that."

"They remembered their children, Sef. A woman hugged a daughter she'd walked past for a week. You think I regret it?"

"I think you'll regret it when there's nothing left of you to do the regretting!"

And then, quieter, in the voice he almost never used — the unarmored one, the one from before his disgrace, from before whatever happened between us that I only knew in fragments:

"I'm not asking you to save yourself. I'm asking you to let me be selfish for once. Let me keep you. The kingdom got twenty years of your tongue keeping Aldric alive. Can't I have — can't there be something after? Something that's just ours?" He laughed, hollow. "I had a speech prepared. For when this was over. I was going to tell you about the night before I was exiled, why I really did it, what I was protecting. I was going to ask you—" He stopped. Started over. "Don't make the ending of that speech a eulogy."

I looked at him for a long time. Firelight — emberlight — moved across his face, and I thought about how I had spent twenty years tasting death in small doses so that a man I'd met perhaps six times could eat dinner safely. Duty was the shape of my whole life. I knew its weight the way you know a stone you carry daily: precisely, intimately, without thinking.

But duty had never once asked me what I wanted. That was the thing about duty nobody tells you — it doesn't love you back. It accepts. It consumes. It is, when you get down to it, a kind of poison too, and I had spent my whole life building immunity to that as well, and maybe that was the real reason the ember chose me. Not the arsenic. The other thing.

"Sef," I said. "Give me your hand."

He did. Of course he did.

I pressed his palm flat against my chest, over the sling, over the warmth. "Feel that?"

"It's beating with your heart."

"It's been beating with my heart since the first night. Every mile north, it's gotten stronger, and every mile north, I've felt — lighter. Clearer. I told myself it was purpose. It wasn't. It's been feeding already, gently, on things I haven't missed yet. Small things. The name of my first teacher. What my mother's lullaby sounded like. I noticed the holes this morning and I didn't tell you because I was afraid you'd do exactly this." I swallowed. "It's already begun. The only question is whether it happens slowly, in the dark, taking pieces while I sleep — or whether I hold it *whole*, like Vessk said, and choose what it takes."

"You can't know it would stop at the valley."

"No. But I can choose to point it." I smiled, and it hurt, and I did it anyway. "Take the valley. Take the wards and the kitchens and the tribute dishes and every gray morning I ever loved. Take all of it. Because here's the thing you keep missing, Sef Whitlock: I am offering my past to buy everyone else's future, and that trade is so far above fair that the ember should be ashamed to accept it."

"That's not — you can't frame suicide as generosity and expect me to agree—"

"I'm not dying. I'm being *spent*." I leaned my forehead against his. "There's a difference. Tasters know the difference. It's the whole job."

He was silent for a long moment. When he spoke again, his voice was rough and low and absolutely steady, the voice of a man who has stopped bargaining and started planning.

"Then we do it my way. At the throne, not before. You hold it whole until the last possible moment, and the moment you offer it — the moment it starts to drink — I will be standing next to you, and afterward I will find you, wherever you end up inside your own head, and I will sit down across from you and I will not leave. Do you hear me? Whatever it takes. However long. I will tell you our story until you believe it."

"That sounds exhausting."

"I'm a disgraced court mage. Exhausting is my natural habitat."

I laughed, and the ember flared, and far off through the trees, at the edge of the dark, the smooth-faced hunters had found our trail again — I could see their lanterns now, or rather the absence of light where lanterns should be, moving toward us through the birches like holes cut out of the world.

Sef stood and pulled me up. North, past the ford, past the last villages with names, lay the starless citadel and a king who had maybe days of himself left.

"One condition," I said, drawing the ember out into the open, letting its light spill gold across both our faces. "When you tell me the story — start with the apples. I want to know what kind of fool steals apples with a prince."

His mouth twitched. "Green ones. Sour as sin. He insisted they were ripe."

"Perfect," I said, and turned north, into the dark, carrying the last fire in the world against my heart — and for the first time since the stars went out, I wasn't afraid of what it would take from me.

I was afraid of whether we'd reach the throne in time to give it.
