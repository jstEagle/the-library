## 8. Storming the Hollow Court

### Sef

Vessk had told us she could carry us one more time, and only one more time, and that after that her heart would stop mid-beat like a clock someone forgot to wind. She said it the way old dragons say everything — as a fact about the world, not a complaint about it.

"Then we'd best not waste the trip," Yara said, and climbed onto a dying dragon's back like it was something she did on Tuesdays.

I want it recorded — somewhere, anywhere, in whatever ledger the stars keep now that they're gone — that I objected. That I laid out the odds with all the rigor of my training: three of us against a citadel full of faceless knights, an ember barely holding its glow, and a king who might already be too hollow to save. I want it recorded that my objection lasted four sentences before Yara looked at me over Vessk's shoulder and said, "Sef. You can do the math on the ground."

So we flew.

The Hollow Court didn't used to be called that. It was the Starhold once, white-walled and ridiculous, built by Aldric's grandfather to look like a crown set down on the mountains. Now it was the same shape wearing a different expression. The walls had gone the color of old teeth. Where the glass of the great windows should have caught light, there was nothing to catch — no moon, no stars, not even the gray suggestion of clouds. Just black, and the citadel drinking it.

And below us, the valley where I'd grown up, where I'd loved a girl who threw bread at my head when I was insufferable, lay under the same starless dark. The Regent's hunger had spread this far. I could feel it from the air, a silence with weight, like standing in a room where someone has just stopped breathing.

"Hold," Vessk rumbled, and her voice came up through her bones into ours. "When I land, I will burn what remains of me through their gates. It will be brief. Do not waste it arguing with each other. You are very bad at it."

"She's right," Yara said.

"I'm aware," I said.

We hit the causeway at speed. Vessk's fire went ahead of us — not the roaring torrent of storybooks but a thin, white, terrible line, the last of the first dragon's flame spent in a single stroke. The gates didn't melt so much as *agree* to be open. And then she folded down onto the stone, vast and still, her blind head turning toward me one last time.

"Mage," she said. "The keeper's grief is real. Remember that when you stand in front of it. Grief is the only weapon it cannot recognize."

"That's either very wise or very ominous."

"It is both. Most true things are." Her eyes — those milky, ruined eyes — found Yara instead. "Little vessel. Your heartbeat is the loudest thing in this kingdom. Keep it steady."

Then she closed them, and the heat went out of her the way heat goes out of a forge at dawn, gradually and then all at once, and there was no time to mourn because the knights were already coming.

* * *

### Yara

Here is what nobody tells you about fear: past a certain point, it stops being loud. Mine had gone quiet somewhere over the valley, the way a hand goes numb when you've gripped something too long. What was left was strangely practical. *Left foot. Right foot. Ember warm. Don't fall.*

The knights poured out of the gatehouse like water finding a crack. Smooth-faced, every one — no eyes, no mouths, just that awful eggshell blankness — and they moved without a sound, which was somehow worse than screaming. Sef's hand found mine for half a second, squeezed once, and let go.

"Throne room's up the central stair," he said. "I'll hold the hall."

"You'll *what*?"

"There are maybe forty of them and one of me, and I'm going to make that math embarrassing for them." He was already pulling sigils off his belt — little iron tags he'd been carving every night on the road, muttering, refusing to explain. "Yara. Look at me. The ember answers your heartbeat. So whatever happens — however bad it gets in there — don't stop being brave. Bravery has a rhythm. Keep it."

"That's the worst advice anyone's ever given me."

"It's the only kind I have." And he smiled — that crooked, infuriating smile I apparently used to throw bread at — and turned to face the smooth faces coming out of the dark.

I ran.

The ember sat against my chest inside its wrapping, and it beat with me, a second pulse slightly ahead of mine, like a drummer who knew the song better than I did. The stairwell was lit by nothing; I navigated by warmth, the ember flaring hotter whenever I drifted toward a wall. Somewhere below, iron tags began detonating in sequence — Sef's voice chanting words I couldn't hear, and then a sound like the sky clearing its throat.

Don't think about him. Climb.

The doors to the throne room were open. Of course they were. The Regent wanted me here; it had wanted me here since the night the wards fell, since the ember chose my palm over every other palm in a burning kitchen. Walking through those doors felt less like breaking in and more like arriving at an appointment I'd forgotten making.

Aldric sat on the throne.

Or — the body that had been Aldric sat there. He was thinner than the coins made him look, grayer, and the crown on his head had grown. That was the detail that stopped my breath: it had *grown*, filaments of pale metal threading down into his hair, his temples, the skin behind his ears, rootlike. His eyes tracked me as I crossed the floor, and there was something in them — a man looking out of a window of a house that was being sold around him.

"You're the taster," he said. His voice was papery. "From Emberfall. You taste the tribute dishes." A flicker, almost a smile. "You always send back the honeyed pears. You say they're cloying."

"They are cloying, Your Majesty."

"Do you—" He stopped. His hands gripped the armrests, knuckles whitening, and for a moment the filaments of the crown pulsed faintly, feeding. When he spoke again the words came faster, a man running downhill ahead of an avalanche. "There was a girl. My daughter. Elira. She had — she had a laugh like—"

He faltered. His brow furrowed, genuinely searching, and I watched a king reach for his own child's face and find a shelf where it used to be.

"Like bells falling downstairs," I said quietly. Because Sef had told me, on the road north, in one of the hours when neither of us slept. "She laughed like bells falling downstairs, and she bit her nails, and she hid in the kitchens when the tutors came, and you covered for her because you remembered what hiding felt like."

His eyes filled. "Yes," he whispered. "Yes. That's — who told you that?"

"A friend. Who loved your daughter almost as much as you did." I walked closer, up the dais steps, and the crown's filaments flexed toward me like something smelling food. "Your Majesty, I need you to hold on. One more hour. Can you hold on?"

"I don't know how much of me is left to hold on *with*." His gaze dropped to the wrapped bundle at my chest, and something ancient moved behind his eyes — not Aldric anymore, something older, hungrier, watching through the window of its own house. "You brought my heart," it said, in a voice layered under his like sediment under topsoil. "After all this time. After everything. You carried it *here*."

"I did."

"Why?"

And the honest answer rose in me before any clever one could: "Because it shouldn't belong to anyone who'd keep it in a box."

The thing beneath the crown went very still. Somewhere far below, another of Sef's iron tags went off, and the floor shivered, and dust sifted down between us like gray snow.

"Then come closer," the Regent said, through the mouth of a king who was mouthing *no, no, run* — and the great doors boomed shut behind me.

* * *

### Sef

Forty knights. I'd guessed forty. It was thirty-one, which I chose to take as a compliment to my reputation.

They didn't fight like men; they fought like a tide, patient and identical, absorbing everything I threw at them and closing the gap behind it. My iron tags bought me space in installments — a ring of fire here, a wall of force there — and each purchase cost me something I wasn't sure I had. Magic in a starless kingdom was like drawing water from a well that was actively being filled with sand. Every sigil burned dimmer than the last.

But they had one flaw, and it was the flaw of everything the Regent made: they were smooth. No eyes. Which meant they saw the way the Regent saw — through hunger, through memory, through the trails people left in the world. And I had spent ten years in exile learning to leave no trail at all. Disgrace, it turns out, is excellent training for guerrilla warfare.

I fought the hall sideways, backwards, in the dark, letting them chase the ghost of where I'd been while the real me circled toward the high knight.

He stood apart from the others, near the base of the throne stair, and he was worse than the rest. Taller. And where theirs were blank, his face had *one feature*: a single seam across it, like a mouth that had been sewn shut and then thought better of it. When I came at him he turned, unhurried, and the seam opened.

"Sefryn Vale," it said, in a voice like my mother's, like my teachers', like every voice I'd ever disappointed. "The court remembers you. The court remembers everyone. Would you like to know what it remembers about you?"

"No," I said, and hit him with everything I had left.

The duel, if you could call it that, was short and deeply unfair in both directions. He was stronger — impossibly strong, each blow ringing through my arms like being struck by a falling door. But he was also *curious*, and curiosity is a slow poison in a fight. He kept talking. Kept offering me my failures, my disgraces, the precise texture of the day they took my staff and my name and put me on the road. And every time he talked, he paused, and every pause was a door, and I have never in my life been too proud to walk through an open door.

"The court remembers," he intoned, catching my blade in one gauntleted fist, "that you loved her. The taster. And that you were too much a coward to—"

"Yeah," I said, and drove the last iron tag — the one I'd been saving, the one carved with Vessk's name in the old script, the one that held a single scale's worth of first-fire — into the seam of his face.

The high knight came apart like morning fog. Not dead — I don't think the Regent's things die — but *unmade*, unraveled back into whatever smooth nothing they're woven from, and I stood in the settling ash of him with bleeding hands and exactly zero magic left, and started up the stairs at a dead run.

Because the throne room doors had slammed, and through them I could hear Yara's heartbeat — the ember's echo of it, that double pulse I'd learned to listen for on the road — and it was stuttering.

Not slowing. *Stuttering.* Like a heart trying to remember which memory comes next.

"YARA!" I hit the doors with my shoulder, with everything, and they gave way—

—and I saw her on the dais, the wrapping torn open, the ember bare in her cupped palms and blazing brighter than it had since the night it fell, and Aldric rising from the throne with the crown's filaments straining toward her like roots toward water, and the whole vast dark of the room bending inward around her as if the darkness itself had leaned in to watch.

She turned her head and looked at me, and she was smiling, and it was the saddest smile I have ever seen on a living face.

"Sef," she said. "Tell me again. Tell me about the valley. All of it. Quickly — I want to carry it with me when I go in."

And the ember opened like an eye.
