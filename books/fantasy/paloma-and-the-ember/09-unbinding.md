## 9. Unbinding

The throne room of the Hollow Court was the largest empty space I had ever stood in, and I include in that assessment the inside of my own head after three days without sleep.

Everything in it was white. Not clean white — *drained* white, the white of a page someone had erased and erased again until the paper thinned. The pillars went up into a dark so complete it might have been painted on. No stars through the windows, because there were no windows, because what kind of monster needs a view? The floor made no sound under our boots. Even Vessk's great clawed feet, even Sef's staff striking stone — nothing echoed. The room ate sound the way its master ate everything else.

And at the far end, on a dais of pale steps, sat the throne.

On the throne sat King Aldric — or what was left of him, which was a man-shaped absence wearing a crown. His eyes were open. They were the worst part. They were open and aware and looking at me with the desperate clarity of a drowning man watching a rope being lowered to somebody else.

"Yara." His voice came out cracked, like a door in a house nobody lived in. "You're — I know you. I know your face. I don't know why I know your face."

"You taste-tested my wedding feast," I said, walking forward. My legs felt borrowed. "You complained about the wine. Loudly. In front of the ambassador from Corvane."

"I did?" Something flickered behind his eyes — not memory exactly, but the *shape* where a memory had been, the way a burned field keeps the outline of the house. "I think I did. I think I complain about things. That sounds like me."

"It's very you," I said, and kept walking, because if I stopped I would look at Sef, and if I looked at Sef I would see him begging me with his whole stupid face, and I had already decided, and decisions die easily when you let people love at you.

The Regent rose.

I want to be honest: every plan I'd made fell apart in that moment. We had a strategy. Sef had drawn it in the dirt outside the caldera with a stick, talking fast the way he does when he's terrified, and Vessk had rumbled corrections through her ruined throat, and I had nodded along as though a diagram in the dirt could prepare you for *this*.

It uncoiled up out of Aldric like smoke poured backward. It had been wearing the king the way a glove wears a hand, and now it stood to its full height, and its full height was the ceiling, and the ceiling was very high. It had no face. It had a place where a face should be, smooth as an egg, and around that place swirled everything it had ever eaten — I could see them, little drifting lights, thousands of them, memories it had swallowed whole and never digested. A woman laughing in a kitchen. A boy running through wheat. A dragon's wing against a sunset. A valley humming with wards.

It looked at me. A thing with no face looked directly at me, and I felt it *open* me like a book.

"Little vessel," it said. Its voice wasn't a voice; it arrived already inside my skull, wearing my own tone. "You carry my heart. You walked it across a dead kingdom. Why?"

Here is what I should have said, according to the plan: I should have said nothing. I should have held the ember up and let Vessk's last strength anchor the binding-circle Sef had chalked into the dais, and we should have forced the thing back into its heart like stuffing a goose, and everyone would have lived unhappily ever after.

But the ember was beating against my palm. It had been beating since the caldera, faster now, urgent, and I finally understood — standing there, book opened by a god-shaped hunger — that it wasn't afraid.

It was *homesick*.

So I said the true thing instead of the planned thing.

"Because it's yours," I said. "Not a weapon. Not a bargaining chip. Your heart. Somebody stole it out of you and you've spent — how long? Centuries? — trying to fill the hole with other people's lives. And it hasn't worked. Has it?"

The Regent went very still. Stillness, in something that size, is louder than roaring.

"They betrayed you," I went on, and my voice only shook a little, which I count as a professional triumph. "The dragons' keeper. They gave you a crown of service and then they took your heart to make their wards, their protections, their pretty starlit kingdom — and they called *you* the monster. And you believed them eventually. That's the cruelest part. They stole from you until you became the thief."

Behind me, Sef made a small wounded sound. He had figured it out one step ahead of me, or maybe he'd known all along and hoped I wouldn't say it, because saying it meant there was no enemy anymore. There was only grief with an army.

"The wards are down," I said. "Your heart is here. Everything they built on top of you is ash. So take it back. Take *all* of it back. And then give the rest away."

The Regent drifted closer. The eaten lights swirled faster around its hollow face, and among them I saw the valley — our valley — Emberfall, the kitchens, the ward-hum, the market bells, all of it spinning in that hungry dark like a fly in amber.

"You offer me mercy," it said, in my voice, in Sef's voice, in a dozen voices it had stolen. "Why would I accept? I have taken kingdoms. I have taken *sky*. What is one mortal's kindness against that?"

"One mortal's kindness," I repeated, and laughed, and it came out wet. "Gods, you really don't remember anything real, do you? Kindness isn't a currency. It doesn't weigh against anything. It's just — what you do. Here." 

I climbed the dais steps. Aldric watched me come with those drowning-man eyes, and when I passed him I touched his shoulder once, briefly, the way you steady a friend at a funeral.

Then I knelt in front of the Regent and opened my hand.

The ember rose off my palm. Up close it was beautiful in the way fires are beautiful right before they kill you — a seed of gold light, pulsing, and every pulse matched my heartbeat, and I understood suddenly, completely, why it had chosen me. Years of tasting poison. Years of teaching my body to hold things meant to destroy it. I was the only vessel in the kingdom built to carry fire without breaking.

Which meant I was also the only vessel that could do this next part.

"It needs fuel," I said to the Regent, conversationally, as though we were two traders haggling over grain. "A heart doesn't beat on its own. Yours has been starving for centuries. If you just swallow it back you'll gutter out in a year and everything dies anyway — you, the king, the sky, all of it. It needs kindling. Real kindling. Memories with weight. Love in them." I swallowed. "I have some of those."

"*No.*"

Sef's voice cracked across the throne room like a whip. I heard his boots on the steps, heard the binding-chalk scrape as he moved to break the circle, and Vessk's tail came down in front of him — gently, for a dragon, which is to say it still knocked him flat.

"He cannot stop you," Vessk rumbled. "Nor can I. This is the bargain the world has waited for, little taster. But hear me—" her blind head swung toward me, nostrils flaring over my hair, breathing me in like a last meal, "—the fire will take what burns brightest first. It will not ask twice. Are you certain?"

Nobody asks a food-taster that question enough, in my experience. Everyone assumes we're certain. We spend our lives swallowing things on faith.

"No," I said honestly. "But certainty is for people with better options. Do it."

"Yara, *please*—" Sef, from the floor, and his voice was wrecked, and I made myself not turn around, because I knew if I saw his face I would become a coward, and cowardice was a luxury the whole starless sky couldn't afford.

"Sef," I said, still facing the Regent, still kneeling, "when this is done, you're going to sit me down and tell me everything. Every terrible joke I ever made at you. Every dish I poisoned-test that made us both gag. Don't leave any of it out. Promise me."

Silence. Then, raw: "I promise."

"Good. Because I intend to be extremely annoyed about all of it secondhand."

The Regent bent over me. Its hollow face filled my whole sky, and the stolen lights whirled, and I felt it hesitate — actually hesitate — vengeance and grief grinding against each other like millstones.

"Why," it said quietly, in a voice that was suddenly, terribly its own — old, cracked, tired, a keeper's voice. "Why give what you cannot spare?"

"Because you're not the only one who lost things to that crown," I said. "And because somebody has to go first."

I reached up — into the dark, into the hunger — and pressed my hand flat against the place where its face should have been.

"Take the valley," I whispered. "Take all of it. The wards and the bells and the kitchen and the hills. Take the mornings. Take him." My voice broke on that one and I let it. "Take every single thing I love. Burn it bright. And give them back their sky."

The ember struck home.

There was no pain at first. There was *light* — gold pouring up through the Regent's hollow, filling it, and the thousand stolen memories whirled faster and faster and began, one by one, to fly free, streaming out of that hungry dark like sparks up a chimney. Somewhere below, Aldric gasped. Somewhere below, Sef was shouting my name. Somewhere above, impossibly, impossibly, there was a crack in the ceiling, and through the crack —

— a star.

And then the fire found my memories, and there was pain, and there was the valley, going.

I held onto it as long as I could, the way you hold a door shut against a flood. The kitchen, warm with bread. The ward-hum at dusk, that low contented note under everything. The market bells. The green hills I'd never once thought to be grateful for, because who thanks the ground for being under them?

Sef. Younger, furious, brilliant, delivering tribute dishes with insults attached. Sef older, bloodied at my door the night the stars died. Sef drawing plans in the dirt. Sef promising, just now, to tell me everything.

*I'll wait,* I thought at the fire, at the whole burning universe. *Tell him to make it good.*

The valley went out like a lamp.

And the sky came back like a sunrise.

* * *

I woke on cold marble with a warmth in my chest and a stranger's coat draped over me, and the first thing I saw was stars — hundreds of them, shameless, glittering through a shattered ceiling like the sky was showing off.

A man was kneeling beside me. Dark-haired, singed, crying without any attempt to hide it, holding my hand in both of his like it was the only object in the world.

"Hi," he said, when my eyes focused. His voice was wrecked and hopeful and terrified, all at once. "Don't panic. My name is Sef. And I promised you a story."
