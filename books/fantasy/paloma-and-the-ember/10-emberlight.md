## 10. Emberlight

I have seen many impossible things in my life. I was trained by the College of the Ninefold Flame to expect them, catalog them, and file them neatly under "further research required." But nothing in nine years of study prepared me for watching a king kneel to a kitchen girl who had no idea who he was.

The throne room was still smoking when it happened. The Hollow Regent's citadel — a fortress built of stolen memories and black stone that drank light — was coming apart around us in slow, glittering pieces as the ember did its work. Every fragment that burned away released something back into the world: a name here, a face there, whole decades pouring out of the dissolving architecture like water from a cracked jug. Vessk's last strength had carried us through its gates, and the old dragon had paid for it. She lay now in the ruined courtyard below, vast and still, her blind eyes turned toward a sky that was, at long last, filling up with stars.

One star first. Then ten. Then a thousand, spilling back into their places like beads returning to a snapped string.

And King Aldric — hollow-eyed, threadbare Aldric, who thirty minutes ago could not remember his daughter's name — stood up from his throne for the first time in years without the crown whispering to him. He looked at Yara Underhill, standing on his dais with ash in her hair and an emptiness behind her eyes where a valley used to be, and he did not know what she had done. He would never know, not fully. The Regent was gone; the debt had been paid by someone else.

He knelt anyway.

I watched from the base of the dais, my hands still shaking from the duel — more on that later, or possibly never; some things a man keeps — as the most powerful man in the kingdom went down on both knees before a girl who blinked at him with the polite confusion of someone encountering a stranger behaving strangely.

"You should get up," she said. Her voice was steady. It was her voice; that much the fire hadn't taken. "You're a king, aren't you? I can tell because of the crown. And the kneeling."

"Child," Aldric said, and his voice broke on it like ice on a pond, "you have no idea what you are to me."

"No," Yara agreed. "I really don't." She looked down at her own palm, the one that wasn't seared anymore, the one where the ember had lived for a season. There was a mark there now, faint as a watermark: a small spiral of gold beneath the skin, warm to the touch. I knew because I'd touched it. Twice. Don't read anything into that.

She looked at him with no fear and no awe, only a taster's frank appraisal, and said, "You look like a man who hasn't eaten properly in years. Has anyone been tasting your food?"

And that was when I understood that whatever the fire had drunk, it had left the important parts of her. The spine. The wit. The stubbornness. It had only taken what she'd offered — the valley, the river, the taste of home — and left the girl herself, intact, like a book with its first chapter torn out.

Aldric wept. Kings are not supposed to weep, but he had spent six years forgetting how to be a king, so perhaps he'd forgotten that rule too.

* * *

The weeks after were administrative, which is the least poetic word I know, and I use it deliberately, because nobody tells you that saving a kingdom is ninety percent paperwork.

There were wards to rebuild — real ones this time, honest ones, warded against hunger rather than magic, since we had all learned which of those was more dangerous. There were villages to re-teach their own names, ledgers of the forgotten to be compiled, and a dragon's body to honor. We burned Vessk on a pyre the size of a cathedral, and her ashes went up into the newly returned sky, and I choose to believe some of them became stars. I'm allowed one belief like that per lifetime. I've spent mine.

Princess Maeryn — who remembered her father perfectly, having been too young and too stubborn for the Regent to digest — took over the reconstruction while Aldric recovered what remained. He got perhaps half of himself back. The other half lives in the sky now, in every star Yara lit, and I think he finds that an acceptable trade, though he never says so. He sends letters to Emberfall Valley. Care of me. They are always the same letter: *Is she well? Does she remember? Tell her nothing unless she asks.* I answer them faithfully. I am, apparently, a man who follows instructions now. Age changes us all.

Yara stayed in the palace a month. Long enough for the healers to confirm what I already knew: there was nothing wrong with her. No wound, no curse, no lingering shadow of the Regent. Just a girl with a golden spiral in her palm and twenty-some years of memory missing, replaced by a warmth in her chest that the physicians kept calling "anomalous" until she told them, quite sweetly, that they were anomalous.

Then she said she wanted to go home.

"I don't know where that is," she explained, apologetic, "but apparently it's a valley, and everyone says I loved it, so I'd like to see it. Even if it doesn't feel like mine."

Everyone advised against it. Everyone except me.

So I packed two bags — hers and mine, and yes, I noticed what that meant, and no, I didn't say it aloud, I'm wry, not brave — and we traveled north together, through a kingdom learning its own name again, to the place where her story had started and ended and started again.

* * *

Emberfall Valley was rebuilt by the time we arrived. That was Maeryn's doing — she sent masons and timber and a royal decree that the valley kitchens be restored exactly, down to the copper pots, because a princess who had nearly lost her father to forgetting had developed strong opinions about memory and its vessels.

Yara walked through the door of her own kitchen and stopped.

"It's beautiful," she said slowly. "It feels like—" She pressed her hand flat against her sternum, against the warmth. "Like a song I almost know the words to."

"That's more than most people get back," I said.

She turned and looked at me then — really looked, the way she used to, with that appraising taster's stare that made grown courtiers check their cups — and said, "You keep looking at me like I'm a dish you're afraid is poisoned."

"The irony has occurred to me," I admitted.

We fell into a rhythm, that first month. She cooked — muscle memory, mercifully, survives everything — and I sat across the table from her every evening and told her our story. All of it. The barbs and the tribute dishes. The night the stars went out. The ember searing itself to her palm while she swore creatively enough to make a dockworker blush. The road north. The village that forgot its name, and how she cried about it for an hour and then made the choice that saved it, feeding the fire a sliver of her past so that strangers could remember their children. The caldera. Vessk. The hunters with faces smooth as eggs, whom she once disabled with a pot of boiling oil and a stream of profanity I transcribed for the royal archives.

I told her everything, every night, like a man paying off a debt in installments.

And every night she listened with her chin in her hands, and at the end she would say, "Tell me the part about your spells not working," because that was her favorite — the part where the disgraced mage's careful magic slid off the ember like rain off oiled leather, and the fire answered only her heartbeat, quickening when hers did.

"It liked you better than me," I said, the first time.

"It sounds like it had good taste," she said, and smiled, and I had to look very hard at my tea for a while.

Here is what I learned, in that valley, in that kitchen: memory is not the same as love. The fire took her memories, but love isn't stored in memories — or if it is, it's stored somewhere deeper, in the marrow, in the pulse, in places even a hungry god can't reach. Because she laughed at my jokes before I finished them. Because she reached for my hand on the third night, absent, automatic, like her fingers remembered what her mind could not. Because sometimes she'd stop mid-chore and stare at me with an expression of furious puzzlement, as if I were a word on the tip of her tongue, and say, "Who *were* you to me?"

"A nuisance," I always answered. "An exile. A coward who ran from the court and hid in a valley delivering soup."

"And?"

And I never had an answer ready that was equal to the question.

Until tonight.

Tonight the valley smelled like woodsmoke and the first frost, and she set two bowls of stew on the table between us — the same stew, I realized halfway through the first bite, that she used to make for the king's tribute, the recipe she'd guarded with her life and her tongue. She sat down across from me and folded her hands.

"Tell me the end," she said. "You've told me everything else a hundred times. You always stop before the end. What happened on the dais?"

I put down my spoon.

"You offered it your memories," I said. "Not as fuel. As kindling. You said—" and my voice did something embarrassing, so I steadied it— "you said, 'Take the valley. Take the river and the orchards and the smell of bread. Take all of it, and give them back their names.' And it took them. And the sky came back."

She was quiet for a long time. Outside, the stars were thick as spilled salt.

"And you?" she asked finally. "What did you lose?"

"Nothing," I said. "That's the unbearable part. I lost nothing and you lost everything, and I've spent every day since trying to figure out how to carry that."

She reached across the table and took my hand — the scarred one, the one with the burn from the night the world ended — and turned it over, studying the palm like she used to study the king's dishes.

"Sef," she said. "Look at me."

I looked at her.

"I don't remember loving you," she said, plainly, the way she said everything. "But my chest does this thing, every time you walk into the room. Like an ember answering a heartbeat. So either the fire left something behind that it shouldn't have, or—" she exhaled, frustrated with herself— "or I loved you so thoroughly that forgetting couldn't finish the job."

"There's a third option," I managed.

"Oh?"

"That we start over. Properly. Tonight. Chapter one." I swallowed. "I'll tell you the whole story again tomorrow. And the day after. For as long as it takes. I'm determined, Yara Underhill, to give you every single memory back — and if the fire wants to argue about it, it knows where to find me."

She looked at our joined hands for a long moment. Then she lifted my palm and pressed it, gently, against the warmth in her chest, and beneath her skin and mine both, faint as a heartbeat, something gold pulsed twice — like recognition, like welcome, like a promise being renewed rather than made.

"Well," she said, and her eyes were bright, and she was smiling, and the whole valley smelled like bread. "Then you'd better start talking, mage. And this time—"

The fire in the hearth flared, gold and glad, though neither of us had fed it.

"—don't skip the part where you fall for me. I want to hear it in your own words."

I opened my mouth to protest — on principle, because some traditions must be preserved — and found, to my complete lack of surprise, that I had no argument worth making.

Outside, every star in the sky was burning.
