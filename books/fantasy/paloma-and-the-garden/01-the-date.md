## 1. The Date

The rain had been coming down on Thornfell for three days, and by the time I reached the Waystone Tavern it had turned from rain to something finer and meaner — a mist that got under cloaks and into boots and settled in the bones like an unpaid debt. The mountains wore it like grey wool. Somewhere above me, past the tree line, the smuggler roads I'd spent my life learning were slick and treacherous as eel skin, and every instinct I owned was telling me to be watching them instead of walking into a tavern with clean hands and my hair tied back.

But I had a date. That was the whole ridiculous truth of it. A date, arranged eleven days ago by letter, confirmed three days ago by a second letter that Paloma Idris had sent up through the pass with a mule driver she'd apparently bribed with a hand-drawn sketch of his own mule. *Thursday, sundown, the Waystone,* she'd written. *I'll be the one pretending to read.*

I pushed open the door, and there she was.

She hadn't been lying about the reading. She sat at the corner table beneath the antlered chandelier with a book open in front of her, a half-drunk cup of something steaming at her elbow, and she was so absorbed in whatever was on the page that she didn't see me come in. That gave me a moment — an unfair one, a stolen one — just to look.

Paloma Idris was not what most people pictured when they thought *cartographer*. Most people pictured a fussy old man with ink-stained cuffs and a squint. She was none of that. She was maybe thirty, dark-haired, with the kind of face that looked like it was always mid-decision: quick eyes going from point to point across a room the way they must have gone across a landscape, measuring, weighing, deciding what mattered. Her coat was good wool but travel-worn, patched at the right shoulder where a pack strap would sit, and her hands — even from across the room I noticed her hands — were freckled and stained faintly brown at the fingertips, the mark of someone who mixed her own inks.

I stood there in the doorway dripping mountain mist onto the floorboards like some kind of ghost, and I realized I was nervous. Me. Beatrix Ravensworth, who had stared down the Vetch brothers' entire crew on the Widow's Stair without blinking, who had talked a knife out of a man's ribs twice last winter, who did not get nervous. My palms were sweating against the straps of my pack.

Because this was different, wasn't it. This wasn't a knife or a cliff or a smuggler with bad intentions. This was a woman I'd met exactly once, six weeks ago, at the lowland market where she'd asked me directions to the Thornfell survey station and I'd given her directions that were wrong on purpose because I didn't want the conversation to end too fast. And she'd caught the lie — of course she had, she was a cartographer, lying about geography to a cartographer is like lying about scripture to a priest — and instead of being annoyed she'd laughed, this sudden bright bark of a laugh, and said, "You know, if you wanted more time to talk, you could have just said so."

And then we'd talked until the market closed around us like water closing over a stone.

* * *

"You're late," she said now, without looking up from her book. "Or I'm early. One of us has committed a temporal crime."

"I'm not late." I crossed the room and hung my soaked cloak on the peg by the hearth. "Sundown was at the quarter bell. It's the half."

"Ah." She closed the book on one finger, keeping her place, and finally looked up at me, and I felt that look land somewhere under my sternum like a thrown knife sticking quivering into wood. Her eyes were brown, ordinary brown, except they weren't ordinary at all when they were aimed at you. "Then I'm early. Which means I've been sitting here rehearsing greetings for twenty minutes, which is humiliating, so let's agree never to speak of it."

"We won't." I sat down across from her. Up close she smelled of ink and woodsmoke and something green, juniper maybe, or crushed pine needles. "What are you reading?"

She hesitated. There was a flicker — quick as a fish turning — of something behind her eyes, and then she smiled and angled the cover toward me. A surveyor's manual. Dry as old toast. *Principles of Elevation and Contour.*

"Thrilling," I said.

"Riveting," she agreed. "Chapter four is called 'On the Proper Use of the Alidade.' I've read it four times tonight. I could recite it. I've never been less interested in anything in my life."

"Why keep reading it?"

"Because the alternative," she said, "was staring at the door like a fool waiting for someone to walk through it, and I have a reputation."

The serving girl came and I ordered stew and the black beer they brewed up at Hollowmere, and Paloma said she'd have the same, and for a little while we just — talked. That was the strange miracle of it. Talking with Paloma Idris was not like talking with other people. With most people, conversation is a trade: you give a story, they give a story, everyone counts their change. With her it was more like walking a road together. She'd say something about how the contour lines on her new map of the eastern passes had come out looking like fingerprints, like the mountains themselves had touched the page and left their mark, and I'd find myself telling her about how my grandmother used to swear the Thornfell peaks were the knuckles of a buried giant, and how smugglers still tapped the high stones twice before crossing, an apology to whoever slept underneath. And she'd lean forward with her chin on her hand and her whole face lit like a lamp and say, *say that again, slower, I'm putting it on the map.* Not the giant — she was too sensible for the giant — but the tapping. The custom. The human geography of a place.

"That's the thing nobody understands about maps," she said, somewhere in the second hour, gesturing with her beer. "Everyone thinks a map is about land. It isn't. Land doesn't care. A map is about *use*. Where people go, what they avoid, what they name and what they leave nameless. Show me any two maps of the same country drawn fifty years apart and I'll tell you what broke between them."

"What breaks?"

"Everything." She said it lightly, but something moved underneath it. "Roads close. Rivers move. People forget why a village is where it is, and then the village dies, and then even the forgetting dies." She caught herself, laughed a little, waved her hand. "Listen to me. You invite a beautiful stranger to dinner and I lecture her about cartographic epistemology."

"I invited *you*," I pointed out, and then heard what I'd said, and felt heat climb my neck.

Her eyebrows went up, delighted. "Beautiful stranger."

"The word beautiful was doing a lot of work in that sentence," I said, staring hard into my stew. "Mostly grammatical."

"Mm-hm."

"It was purely structural."

"Beatrix." She waited until I looked up. She was smiling, but softly now, no edge on it. "For the record, I also think you're a beautiful stranger. Structurally speaking."

The fire popped in the hearth. Outside, the mist pressed against the windows like something listening. I thought: *careful.* I thought: *you don't do this, you don't let anyone in, you live alone on a mountain with a sword and your grandmother's rhymes and that's how you survive.* But her knee was almost touching mine under the table, and the beer was warm in my blood, and there's only so long a person can hold a door shut against spring.

"So tell me about the smuggling," she said.

"Not the romantic version," I warned. "Everyone wants the romantic version. Cloaks and moonlight and daring."

"No?"

"I spend most of my time doing arithmetic," I said. "Weights and tolls and which customs officer takes bribes and which takes heads. I know the exact load a mule can carry over the Widow's Stair before its legs go, and I know it because I once watched a mule carry too much, and I still think about that mule more than I'd like to." I turned my cup on the table. "It's a job. It's watching and counting and knowing which shadows are safe to stand in. The romance is mostly weather."

"Good," she said. "I hate romance. In the literary sense. Give me weather."

So I gave her weather. I told her about the ice-fog that comes off the high tarns in autumn and swallows a whole valley so completely that men have walked off cliffs inside it; about the way sound travels on cold nights up there, so that you can hear a conversation happening two ridges away and learn things you weren't meant to know; about the particular quality of silence in the deep passes, a held-breath silence, like the mountains were listening for something. She listened the way she mapped — completely, greedily, asking questions that went sideways and deeper than I expected, and I kept giving her answers I hadn't known I was carrying.

At some point I realized I'd told her about my mother. Just a piece — the oath, the swordspeaking, the way it had hollowed her out year by year until the oath was all that was left standing in her clothes. I hadn't told anyone that in years. I watched the words leave my mouth with a distant feeling, like watching coins roll toward a drain.

Paloma didn't flinch and didn't pry. She just reached across the table and put her ink-stained hand over mine, briefly, and took it away, and said, "Thank you for telling me," and changed nothing else.

That was the moment, if I'm honest. That was when the guarded thing in my chest — the thing I'd inherited along with the sword-marks on my palms, the family talent for keeping doors shut — quietly, stupidly, without consulting me, opened.

* * *

The tavern had emptied around us. The serving girl had gone from refilling our cups to pointedly stacking chairs nearby, and the innkeeper had begun banking the fire, and neither of us had moved. The rain had stopped at some point; the windows showed stars coming out raggedly over the black shoulder of the mountain.

Paloma looked at the stacked chairs. Looked at me. Looked at the stairs leading up to the rooms.

"I should—" I started.

"Yes," she said. "Probably. Although—"

"Although."

"Although I bought you something," she said, "and I've been carrying it around for eleven days like an idiot, and if I don't give it to you now I'm going to lose my nerve, and I would rather lose several other things first."

"You bought me something?" We'd known each other six weeks. "You didn't have to—"

"I know I didn't have to. That's rather the point." She was already digging in her satchel, the worn leather one she carried everywhere, and I noticed her hands had gone slightly unsteady, which surprised me, because everything else about her was steady as a plumb line. "It's a gift. An actual gift. Which means you can't refuse it, that's the law, I checked."

She set it on the table between us.

A book. Old — genuinely old, not merchant-old with artificially cracked spines, but old the way bones are old. It was small enough to fit in one hand, bound in leather gone dark and smooth with handling, its corners rounded like river stones. There was no title on the cover. No tooling, no gilding, nothing except a single design stamped near the bottom: a sword crossed with a flowering branch, so worn you had to tilt it toward the candlelight to see it at all.

Something cold walked up the back of my neck.

I knew that stamp. Every Ravensworth knew that stamp. It was pressed into the leather of my grandmother's old satchel. It was branded into the beam above the door of the house I'd grown up in, painted over twice and always bleeding back through. Sword and blooming branch. The arms of the Oathbound. The sign of the swordspeakers.

"Where did you get this?" I said. My voice came out flat, careful, the voice I used on men holding knives.

"At an estate auction, months ago. Down in Corrow Vale. The whole library of some dead scholar, sold off by weight — can you imagine, books by the *weight*, like turnips." She was talking fast now, nervous, filling space. "Nobody wanted it. Too damaged, too obscure, no title. But it's a prophecy cycle, Beatrix, a real one, a century old at least, and it mentions — well. It mentions the Thornfells. And I thought, she's from the Thornfells, and she has those marks on her hands that she never explains, and maybe—" She stopped herself. Took a breath. "Maybe I was showing off. Maybe I wanted to impress you with a clever gift. That's all it is. It's just supposed to be a gift."

The candle between us guttered, though the door was shut and there was no draft.

I reached out and touched the cover. The leather was warm. That was the detail my mind snagged on afterward, the one I'd keep returning to in the long years after: not the sigil, not the age of the thing, but the simple animal fact that the book was warm, warmer than the table, warmer than the air, warm as something recently alive.

"Open it," Paloma whispered.

I opened it.

The pages were heavy and cream-colored, soft at the edges, and the handwriting was a fine brown ink, elegant, unhurried. Verse. Old-fashioned verse, the kind with formal repetitions, the kind meant to be spoken aloud. And as my eyes went down the first page, the candle flame leaned sideways, though there was no draft, and the letters seemed to shift very slightly, the way a reflection shifts on moving water—

—and I read words that had no business being there.

Words about a line of speakers whose oaths would fade. Words about a garden below the crown of the world. Words about a king who was not a king. And then, in fresh-looking ink, darker than the rest, as though written yesterday:

*two shall come to the waystone inn*
*when rain has fallen three days*
*a guide of roads and a drawer of maps*
*the sixth night of the reaping moon*

The sixth night of the reaping moon was tonight.

The rain had fallen three days.

My name was on the page. Not spelled out — the verse was older than my name — but there, unmistakable, in the shape of it: *a guide of roads.* And beside it, *a drawer of maps,* and Paloma made a small sound next to me, a sound like a stitch tearing, because she'd seen it too.

"Beatrix," she said. "Beatrix, the ink is moving."

"I see it."

The candle guttered again and this time it didn't recover. The light shrank to a blue coin, and the coin shrank to a spark, and in that failing light the tavern began to change. Not dramatically — that was the horror of it. No wind, no thunder, no tearing of the world's fabric. Just the corners of the room drawing inward, gently, the way paper draws in when it burns, the walls curving toward us like the edges of a map folding shut, and the hearth-fire stretching into a thin gold thread, and the smell of stew and woodsmoke giving way to something older, green and loamy, like a garden after rain.

Paloma's hand found mine across the table. Her grip was fierce. Her fingers were cold.

"Is this—" she started.

"Hold on," I said, which was such a stupid thing to say, such a useless smuggler-road thing to say, hold on, mind the gap, watch your footing — but it was all I had, so I said it again. "Hold on to me."

The last thing I saw before the dark took us both was the book, lying open on a table that was no longer there, its pages turning by themselves, patiently, like something that had been waiting a hundred years to be read — and had just, finally, begun.
