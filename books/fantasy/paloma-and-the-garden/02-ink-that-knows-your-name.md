## 2. Ink That Knows Your Name

Paloma Idris had faced down cliff-edge survey work in a gale, argued price with a Kessarin harbor-master who kept a knife on the table for punctuation, and once spent three days lost in the Mirefen with nothing but a compass that lied to her and her own stubbornness. None of it had made her hands shake.

Her hands were shaking now, because of a book wrapped in oilcloth inside her satchel, and because Beatrix Ravensworth was watching her across a tavern table with an expression Paloma could not quite read — something between amusement and wariness, like a hawk deciding whether the thing moving in the grass was prey or weather.

She had rehearsed this. She had rehearsed it on the road up from Lowmark, muttering to herself at the ferry crossing until a fisherman gave her a wide berth. She had rehearsed it while her boots dried by the waystation fire. And yet every version she'd practiced had assumed she would be calm, and calm had apparently stayed behind in Lowmark along with her dignity.

"You're staring," Beatrix said.

"I'm *observing*," Paloma corrected. "It's a professional habit. I observe terrain."

"And what's the terrain say?"

"That you haven't touched your cider, which means either you don't like it or you're nervous." Paloma lifted her own mug as evidence. "I've drunk half of mine, which means I'm nervous enough to need it but not so nervous I've forgotten to taste it. Cartographers are very precise about these things."

The corner of Beatrix's mouth moved. It wasn't quite a smile, but it was heading in that direction the way a river heads toward the sea — slowly, against considerable resistance. The tavern around them was doing its evening work: a fire snapping in the great hearth, the smell of tallow and woodsmoke and onion stew, a pair of wool-traders arguing amiably about tariffs, the innkeeper's daughter hauling a tray of mugs with the practiced indifference of someone who had seen every kind of traveler Thornfell roads could produce. Rain ticked against the shutters. Somewhere upstairs a floorboard creaked under somebody's restless pacing.

It was, Paloma thought, a good room. She had chosen it deliberately — the Drowned Bell sat at the junction of three smuggler paths and one honest one, close enough to Beatrix's patrol routes that accepting the invitation cost her little pride, far enough from anything that the evening couldn't turn into an ambush of obligation. Paloma planned things. That was the whole architecture of her life: measure first, then move. Draw the coast before you name it. Learn where the deep water lies before you promise anyone safe passage.

And yet here she was, about to do the one unplanned thing she'd done in years, because she had met a woman on a bridge in Lowmark who looked at maps the way other people looked at old lovers — with recognition, and regret, and a hunger she didn't bother to hide — and Paloma had thought, with a certainty that alarmed her: *I want to be worth looking at like that.*

"So," Beatrix said. "A cartographer walks into a smuggler's tavern. Sounds like the start of a bad joke."

"The punchline is me," Paloma said. "But I brought a gift, so you have to be polite about it."

Beatrix's eyebrows rose. "You didn't have to—"

"I know. That's rather the point of gifts." Paloma reached into her satchel, and her fingers found the oilcloth, and the shaking got worse, and she ignored it the way she ignored weather. "I found it in a bookbinder's cellar in Lowmark. He said it came out of an estate sale — some family clearing out generations of clutter. I paid too much for it. I'd like to tell you I haggled him down out of professional pride, but honestly, the moment I opened it, I forgot how money works."

She set it on the table between them and unwrapped the cloth.

The book was not beautiful, exactly. It was beautiful the way mountains are beautiful — heavy, dark, unapologetic about its own mass. Leather gone the color of wet slate, corners worn round, a spine cracked in two places from centuries of being opened at particular pages. No title on the cover. Just a stamped device near the bottom edge: a sword crossed over a flowering branch.

Beatrix went very still.

Paloma had spent enough time around soldiers and guides to know stillness when she saw it — the difference between a person resting and a person listening. Beatrix had stopped resting.

"Where," Beatrix said quietly, "did you say this came from?"

"Lowmark. An estate sale. Why?"

"What estate?"

"The bookbinder didn't know. Some mountain family, he thought. Old name." Paloma hesitated. "Ravensworth, actually. Which is why I bought it. I thought—" She heard her own voice speed up, the way it did when she was nervous, and forced it back down to surveying pace. "I thought you might like it. A piece of your family's history. I know you don't talk much about them, but everyone has a past, and most people's pasts aren't for sale in a cellar, and if this is intrusive, tell me now and I'll put it away and we'll drink our cider and discuss drainage instead, I have *opinions* about drainage—"

"Open it," Beatrix said.

* * *

The pages were thick and soft-edged with age, the color of old cream, and they smelled of dust and something else — something green, almost, like crushed stems, like a garden after rain. Paloma noticed that and filed it away as odd and moved on, because the handwriting had caught her.

It was a fine hand, confident, slanting slightly left. Not a scribe's copybook script but a working hand, a hand used to writing fast in bad light. The opening page read:

*Herein is set down the Verse of the Ravensworth Oath, as spoken at the binding of the crown, and the manner of its keeping, and the manner of its ending, should ending come.*

Below that, in different ink, darker, more hurried:

*God forgive me for writing it down.*

"Oh," Paloma said softly.

"It's a prophecy book." Beatrix's voice had gone flat and careful, the voice of someone walking on ice they trusted but did not like. "My grandmother used to talk about — no. No, she never talked. That was the whole point. We were raised *not* to talk about it. Swordspeaking, the oath, all of it. My mother wouldn't even say the word *oath* indoors."

"Swordspeaking?" Paloma turned the page. "Is that real? I always thought it was a story. Sword that answers when you speak to it, that kind of thing."

"It's real." Beatrix said it the way people say *the fever is real*, or *the flood is coming*. "Was. There's only ever one at a time. The oath passes down the line, mother to daughter, and each one swears it or breaks it, and either way it costs." Her eyes flicked up. "Why are you showing me this? Truly."

"Because I wanted to impress you," Paloma said, and then, since honesty seemed to be the order of the evening, kept going before courage failed: "Because you told me you guide smugglers' roads through the Thornfells and I asked why someone who could do literally anything else chooses to live in the cold guarding other people's contraband, and you said *family business*, and changed the subject so completely that I spent two weeks afterward trying to find out what the family business actually was. This is what I found. I'm a mapmaker, Beatrix. I can't stand unmapped territory. Especially not in a person."

For a long moment Beatrix said nothing. The fire popped. The wool-traders laughed at something. Rain worked patiently at the shutters.

"My mother broke the oath," Beatrix said finally. "Renounced it. That's why we lived low, in the valleys, why she flinched whenever anyone mentioned the mountains. And I—" She exhaled. "I took the training. The forms, the discipline. Then I walked away from it too. So whatever your book says my family did a hundred years ago, we're a long way from it now. It's just paper."

"Just paper," Paloma agreed, and turned another page, because she was helpless before an unmapped thing, and because the green smell was stronger here, and because the ink—

The ink moved.

She would spend the rest of her life trying to describe that moment and never quite manage it. The words on the page were settled, dry, fixed — and then they weren't. It was subtle at first, like heat-shimmer over summer stone, the letters trembling at their edges. Then a line of text began to crawl, rearranging itself letter by letter with a sound Paloma felt in her teeth rather than heard, a faint dry whispering, like sand running through an hourglass, like a pen scratching somewhere very close to her ear.

"Beatrix."

"I see it."

The line resolved. It had been some verse about the crown and the keeping of it; now it read, in that same fine left-slanting hand, as though it had always said this:

*Two shall come to the Bell by the drowned road,*
*when the rain rides the wind off the western fells,*
*the mapmaker and the last-sworn blade,*
*and the hour shall be the hour of the guttering candle.*

Paloma became aware, distantly, absurdly, that the candle on their table had begun to drown in its own wax, flame shrinking, guttering exactly as described. She became aware that rain rode the wind outside, and that it had come, all evening, off the western fells. She became aware that her heart was going hard enough that she could see her own collar moving.

"This is tonight," she said. Her voice sounded strange to her, thin, as though heard through water. "This is — Beatrix, this is *us*. The date. I invited you on the — the verse knows about the *date*—"

"Don't read it aloud again." Beatrix's hand had gone to the sword at her hip — an old blade, plain-hilted, the kind of weapon that gets carried for decades — and Paloma saw, with the part of her mind that never stopped taking measurements, that Beatrix's knuckles were white. "Some things take being spoken as permission. Don't give it permission."

"I'm not reading aloud. I'm barely breathing." But Paloma's eyes were already moving down the page, because she was who she was, and the page was turning itself anyway, gently, the way a leaf turns in wind, presenting the next passage:

*The mapmaker shall draw what cannot exist,*
*and love what she draws, and be drawn.*
*The last-sworn blade shall unsay her silence,*
*and the garden below shall open for them both,*
*and the crown shall come down or the world shall wear it forever.*

"Crown," Beatrix repeated. "What crown? There hasn't been a king in Thornfell in a hundred years. The line ended. Everyone knows the line ended."

"Maybe everyone knows wrong," Paloma whispered. "Maybe that's the point of a prophecy. It's a map of somewhere that isn't supposed to exist anymore." Even as she said it, her professional soul rebelled and marveled at once: a map of a place that had been erased, that persisted anyway, waiting for someone with her particular affliction — the inability to leave a blank space blank — to come and chart it. The book hadn't fallen into her hands by accident. Books that knew your name didn't fall. They *arrived*.

The candle flame shrank to a blue bead.

Around them, the tavern continued — that was the horrible part, the ordinary part. The innkeeper's daughter set down two bowls of stew at the next table. The wool-traders argued on. A log shifted in the hearth, shedding sparks. Nobody else looked up. Nobody else saw the ink crawling, or perhaps nobody else existed in quite the same way they had a moment ago, and Paloma could not decide which possibility frightened her more.

"Close it," Beatrix said.

"I'm trying to find out how it ends."

"It doesn't end. Prophecies don't end, they *wait*—" Beatrix reached across the table, and her hand closed over the edge of the page, and Paloma felt the touch of her fingers, warm and steady, and thought with strange clarity: *of all the ways I imagined this evening going, holding hands over a cursed book was not among them, and yet.*

The book did not want to close. She felt that now plainly, physically — a resistance in the binding like a living thing bracing its shoulders, like a door held open against wind. The green smell flooded up out of the pages, impossibly strong now, wet stems and turned earth and something flowering, something blooming in the dead of a rainy autumn night in a smoky tavern room where nothing had ever grown.

"Paloma." Beatrix's voice, low. "Look at the walls."

Paloma looked.

The tavern was folding. There was no better word for it, and she was a woman who prided herself on finding the right word. The corners of the room were drawing inward, slow and smooth, the way a map folds closed — crease by crease, wall meeting ceiling meeting floor, the hearth compressing to a bright seam, the wool-traders flattening into painted figures, into suggestion, into ink. The rain on the shutters stretched into long vertical lines, like hatching on a hillside, like contour marks, like the shorthand a cartographer uses for cliffs.

*It's folding like a closing map,* she thought, and the thought was the last ordinary thought she ever had in that century.

"Beatrix." She grabbed for her hand properly now, both of hers around it, anchor and anchor-line. "Whatever happens — I didn't buy this to hurt you. I bought it because I liked you. I want that on the record. Whatever record survives."

Something that was almost a laugh escaped Beatrix, tight and terrified and real. "Noted. For the record: I'm glad you paid too much for it."

The candle went out.

In the dark, the book lay open between their joined hands, and its pages glowed faintly green, like moss in deep woods, like a garden seen from above at midnight, and the last thing Paloma heard before the world finished folding was the whisper of the ink, still writing, still setting down their names in a hand that had been dead a hundred years and was somehow, terribly, still taking notes.

Then the fold closed over them like water, and the Drowned Bell was gone, and they fell — together, hand in hand, down through the paper and the years — into the century that had been waiting for them all along.
