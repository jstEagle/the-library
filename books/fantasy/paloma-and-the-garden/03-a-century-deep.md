## 3. A Century Deep

The first thing Beatrix felt was stone under her cheek, and the second was cold — not the damp, forgiving cold of a tavern floor but the dry, ancient cold of high mountain rock that had never known a roof. She opened her eyes to a sky she did not know.

That was the wrongness of it, she thought later, when she had words for it again: not that the stars were strange, but that they were *arranged* wrong. She knew the night sky over Thornfell the way she knew the scar on her own palm. She had navigated by it through whiteouts and war and one memorable winter when the passes closed and she'd eaten pine bark for eleven days. The Shepherd's Yoke hung in its proper season over the eastern crags; the Seven Sisters trailed their long silver braid down toward the southern horizon like a woman letting down her hair. Every smuggler, every guide, every lost child in those mountains learned the sky before they learned their letters.

This sky had no Yoke. No Sisters. The constellations up there were strangers — bright, indifferent, arranged in patterns that made her eyes water if she tried to follow them, as though the heavens themselves had been redrawn by someone working from memory.

"Paloma." Her voice came out cracked. She pushed herself upright on her elbows, and the world tilted with a lurching slowness, as if she were drunk or the mountain were breathing. "Paloma, wake up."

Beside her, sprawled across a stretch of road that gleamed pale in the starlight, Paloma Idris lay on her side with one arm flung out and her satchel still strapped across her body — she'd slept with that thing since Beatrix had known her, maps and ink and instruments clinking softly when she moved. Now Beatrix could hear the glass vials inside it chiming faintly, though Paloma wasn't moving at all.

Beatrix got a hand under her shoulder. "Come on. Up. Whatever this is, we don't lie down in it."

Paloma's eyes fluttered open. For a moment they were unfocused, swimming, and then they fixed on Beatrix's face and went wide with a terror so pure it looked almost like recognition.

"The book," Paloma whispered. "The candle went out and the walls — Beatrix, the walls *folded*—"

"I know." She hauled her upright. "I was there."

They knelt together on the road then, both of them, gripping each other's forearms like two climbers who'd woken to find themselves on a ledge neither remembered reaching. The road itself was the next wrongness. It was paved — actually paved, fitted stones worn smooth and crowned in the old style, the kind of work that hadn't been done in the Thornfells in living memory. The modern tracks up there were dirt and scree and the occasional plank bridge some smuggler had thrown together and sworn would last. This road ran straight and sure along the mountainside, curving away into darkness on either side, and it was *clean*, swept, maintained.

Roads like this didn't exist anymore. Roads like this hadn't existed in a hundred years.

"Okay," Paloma said, in the voice she used when she was talking herself down from a height — Beatrix had heard it once, on the Widow's Stair, when the rope had frayed. Steady. Professional. Lying through her teeth about how fine everything was. "Okay. Assessment. We were in the Waystone Tavern, we were — I gave you a gift, you were reading it, the ink moved, and then—" She stopped. Swallowed. "And then the room folded up like a map being closed. Which is not a sentence I ever expected to say aloud."

"You said it very calmly."

"I'm having a crisis very calmly. There's a difference." But her hand found Beatrix's and held on, and Beatrix held back, and for a moment that was the whole world: two people on an impossible road, anchored to each other.

Then Beatrix looked up at the mountains, and forgot how to breathe.

She knew them.

Not the way you know any range — the general grammar of peaks and shoulders — but specifically, intimately, the way you know a face from a portrait painted before the sitter's death. The skyline against those stranger-stars was the Thornfell massif, unmistakably, the great broken crown of it silhouetted against the horizon. But it was *whole*. The Sundered Gap — the vast collapsed notch where the eastern face had come down in her grandmother's grandmother's time, taking three villages and half the old high road with it — was not sundered. The peak stood unbroken, proud, wearing its full crown of stone against the stars. And below it, where in Beatrix's world there was nothing but the Grey Scree fields and the bones of the old slide, terraces caught the starlight in neat green lines. Cultivated land. Vineyards, maybe. Orchards.

"That's not possible," she heard herself say. "That gap fell ninety years ago. I've walked the slide field. I've pulled bodies out of it."

"Beatrix." Paloma's voice had gone very quiet. "Look at the road markings."

There, on the crown of the paving stones at intervals, cut deep and filled with something that still caught light after all this time: the mark of the Ravensworth line. A sword crossed over a thorn branch. Beatrix had grown up seeing it — on her grandmother's ring, burned into the lintel of the family house, tattooed in faded blue-green ink across the backs of her mother's hands where the oath-marks sat. She had spent fifteen years avoiding mirrors that might show her the same marks rising on her own skin.

Her family's road. Her family's mark. On a road that should have been rubble for a century.

"No," Beatrix said. It came out flat and final, the word she used to end arguments with bandits and landlords. "No. Whatever this is — dream, trap, poison in the wine — I'm not going to stand here and—"

The wind came down off the peaks then, and it smelled wrong. Not wrong-bad. Wrong-*right*. It smelled of woodsmoke from hearths that shouldn't be lit, of bread baking somewhere below in the dark, of pine resin and cut hay and the green, living breath of forests that in Beatrix's time had been ash and stump. It smelled like her grandmother's stories. Like the stories her grandmother had told her by the fire when she was small enough to still believe that stories were safe: tales of the high roads busy with honest traffic, of waystation lanterns strung along the passes like a necklace of stars, of the Ravensworth swordspeakers walking their oaths from pass to pass and the mountains *safe*, Beatrix, safe all the way to the sea.

The Thornfell before the fall.

"Oh," Paloma said softly. She had the book out now — of course she did, she must have been holding it even as they fell — and it lay open across her palms, and even in the starlight Beatrix could see the pages glowing faintly, the ink shifting like fish under ice. "Oh, Beatrix. I think I know where we are. When we are."

"Don't say it."

"In the year it was written." Paloma turned the book toward her. The verse on the open page had rearranged itself again; Beatrix could feel the words rearranging something behind her ribs too, some load-bearing wall of her understanding giving way. "A hundred years ago. Maybe more. The book didn't just tell us a prophecy, love. It *is* the prophecy. It's happening. We're standing in it."

Beatrix looked at her hands. In the starlight, the backs of them were shadowed, but she could feel it — a warmth in the old scar tissue, a prickling along the lines of her palms like the moment before a storm, when the air fills with the smell of lightning that hasn't struck yet. The oath-marks. Waking.

She stood up. Walked to the edge of the road, where the mountain fell away into a valley full of lights — dozens of them, hundreds, waystations and farmhouses and a town strung along a river that glittered like spilled jewelry. A living valley. In her lifetime, that valley had been called the Hollow Reach, and nothing lived there but goats and ghosts.

Behind her, Paloma rose too, the book hugged to her chest, and came to stand at her shoulder. Neither of them spoke for a long moment. Below, somewhere, a dog barked. A cart creaked along a road, its lantern swinging. Ordinary sounds, a century out of place, and somehow those were what broke something loose in Beatrix's chest.

"My grandmother used to sing me a rhyme," she said finally. Her voice sounded strange to her own ears, scraped raw. "*High road low road, thorn and fell, keep the watch and keep it well.* Smuggler's counting rhyme. Every kid in the Thornfells knows it. I always thought it was just — nonsense. Old words." She laughed, and it hurt. "It's a *map*. Isn't it? All those stories, all those songs — they're not memories of this place. They're instructions. My family kept the knowledge alive for a hundred years like seeds in a drawer, waiting for—" She gestured helplessly at the impossible, blooming valley. "Waiting for whatever this is."

"For us," Paloma said quietly.

"For me." Beatrix turned to look at her, and the fear in her own chest had begun, slowly, painfully, to change shape — to harden into something more useful. "The book named both of us, but the marks are mine, Paloma. The sword-marks. The Ravensworth line. Whatever my family swore an oath about, they swore it *here*, in this year, on this road. And whatever's coming—" she touched the hilt of the sword at her hip, an old habit, an old comfort, "—it started here too."

Paloma studied her in the starlight. Then, deliberately, she closed the book and tucked it into her satchel, and squared her shoulders the way she did before surveying dangerous ground.

"All right," she said. "Then we do what we're good at. You know these mountains — a version of them. I make maps. Between your stories and my instruments, we'll figure out where we are, what's true, and what wants killing." A pause. The ghost of her usual crooked smile. "And Beatrix? If anyone asks, we're travelers. Married, ideally. Married travelers ask fewer questions."

Despite everything — the wrong stars, the dead-alive valley, the warmth crawling up her wrists like sunrise under the skin — Beatrix felt her mouth twitch. "Married."

"It's a professional recommendation."

"We met three weeks ago."

"And I already trust you with my life, which is more than I can say for most people I've known a decade. So." Paloma held out her elbow, formal as a courtier, absurd as anything on a midnight mountain road. "Shall we, wife?"

Beatrix looked at her — this sharp, brave, ridiculous woman who had bought a cursed book to impress her and then fallen through a century holding her hand — and took her arm.

"Keep the watch," she murmured, half to herself, half to whatever grandmother-shaped ghost might be listening, "and keep it well."

They walked down the old road together, two small figures under stranger-stars, toward the lights of a valley that had been dead for a hundred years and was, tonight, impossibly, gloriously awake. Behind them the Ravensworth mark glowed faintly on the paving stones and dimmed as they passed, like a door closing gently behind travelers it had waited a long time to welcome home.

They had gone perhaps half a mile when Paloma stopped so abruptly that Beatrix nearly overbalanced.

"What?"

"Listen."

Beatrix listened. Wind in pines. Water over stone. And beneath it, threading down the mountainside from the heights above — from the direction of the Sundered Peak, whole and crowned and *wrong* — a sound she had no name for. Not bells. Not singing. Something between a hum and a hunger, a vibration she felt in her teeth more than her ears, in the oath-marks most of all, which had begun to burn like brands.

Paloma's face had gone pale. "What is that?"

Beatrix stared up at the perfect, unbroken peak, and her grandmother's voice surfaced from the depths of memory, from a story told late and low, the kind of story grandmothers only tell once:

*And above it all sits the crown of the mountain, little bird, and something wears it.*

"I don't know," Beatrix lied, because the truth was worse: some buried, inherited part of her knew exactly what it was, and it had already begun to call her name.
