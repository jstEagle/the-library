## 4. The Garden Below

Paloma had drawn a thousand valleys in her life, and she knew the grammar of them the way she knew her own handwriting: the way a river wrote itself into the low ground, the way roads clung to contours like ivy to a wall, the way villages always, *always*, huddled near water and called it coincidence. She had spent eleven years learning to read landscape the way priests read scripture — patiently, hungrily, with the assumption that everything meant something.

The valley below them obeyed none of it.

"There," Beatrix said, and her voice had gone strange, flat as a blade laid on a table. "That's not on any map I've ever seen. And I've seen maps of Thornfell that were never printed."

They stood on a shoulder of granite where the mountain road — the road that should not exist, that had unspooled beneath their feet for two days like thread from a spindle — bent around a crag furred with grey lichen. The air smelled of snowmelt and pine resin and something else beneath it, something green and sweet and old, like opening a chest of linens in a house long empty. Below them, past a lip of scree, the land folded inward on itself, and in the fold lay green.

Not summer-green. Not the tentative green of high-altitude spring. It was the deep, confident, almost insolent green of midsummer in a sheltered garden, blooming here between walls of stone under a sky still wearing the ragged grey of late winter. Fruit trees stood in rows that no orchardist would have chosen — too close together, angled wrong against the sun — and they were heavy. Paloma could see the weight of it from here: apples or something like apples bending boughs, roses gone fat and blowsy over a trellis that had rotted everywhere except exactly where the roses needed it most.

"It's the middle of Thawmonth," Paloma said. Her voice came out thin. "Nothing blooms in Thawmonth above three thousand feet. Nothing blooms at all."

"The book says otherwise." Beatrix had the prophecy open across her forearm, holding it the way soldiers hold things — braced, ready to drop it if it turned out to be a weapon. The pages had stopped behaving like paper since the tavern. They moved when the light moved, ink swimming up to meet the eye like fish rising to feed. "Verse nine. *Where the mountain folds its hands, the garden keeps what grief commands.*" She paused. "I hate this book."

"You've said that four times since we woke up a century ago."

"And I stand by every one." But Beatrix was already moving down the switchback, sure-footed on scree that shifted like water, and Paloma scrambled after her, one hand on her satchel strap, feeling the maps inside shift against her hip like sleeping animals turning over.

They had been walking for two days in the year the book was written, and Paloma had spent both of them doing the only thing she knew how to do with terror, which was to measure it. She had sketched the impossible road. She had charted the stars — wrong stars, constellations rotated a hand's width off true, as though the whole sky were a drawing someone had nudged while the ink was wet. She had noted the way Beatrix went quiet whenever they passed a boundary stone carved with a mark like a sword laid over a rose, and how Beatrix touched each one without seeming to decide to, the way you touch a doorframe of a house you grew up in.

Her own hands, she noticed now, were steady. That frightened her more than shaking would have.

* * *

The garden received them the way a host receives expected guests.

There was no gate. There was simply a place where the scree gave way to soft dark soil, and the soil was warm through Paloma's boot soles, warm as a living body, and the smell hit her all at once — rosemary, crushed thyme, apple blossom overripe, wet earth, woodsmoke that had no source. Her eyes stung. Somewhere a bee was working, the low contented drone of it impossibly loud in the silence, because there were no other bees, no birdsong, no wind. Just the one bee, patient as a clock.

"Stay behind me," Beatrix murmured, and her sword was already half-drawn, the steel catching a light that didn't quite match the sky. "Gardens don't grow themselves."

"This one appears to."

"Then it's lying about something, and I want to know what before it tells us."

But Paloma had stopped listening, because she had seen the beds.

They were laid out in rows, but the rows made no horticultural sense — herbs beside nightshade, roses twined with bean-vines, a plum tree growing straight out of what looked like an herb spiral. And yet nothing choked anything else. Everything coexisted the way instruments coexist in a song, each taking its own space. She knelt by the nearest bed without meaning to, pressing her palms into the soil the way she'd done since she was six years old, kneeling in her mother's survey plots, learning that ground told the truth even when people didn't.

The soil was warm. And underneath the smell of loam there was another smell, fainter, one she couldn't name for a moment, and then could, and wished she couldn't: old paper. Old ink. Like a library that had been buried and had decided, quietly, to become a garden instead.

"Paloma." Beatrix's voice, careful. "Your hands."

Paloma looked down. Where her fingers pressed the earth, small pale shoots were rising — fast, unnaturally fast, unfurling leaves like fists opening. She snatched her hands back and the growth stopped, the seedlings trembling at the height of her fingertips, waiting.

"They're following me," she whispered.

"Or leading you." Beatrix crouched beside her, sword across her knees now, and studied the seedlings with the narrowed look she gave smugglers' trails. "Look at the pattern. They're not random."

They weren't. The shoots had come up in lines, and the lines had angles, and the angles repeated — a short stroke, then a longer one, then two crossing strokes. Paloma knew the shape. Every cartographer did. It was a surveyor's benchmark symbol, the mark used to anchor a measured point, the first thing drawn on any map worth trusting.

"It's marking itself," Paloma said slowly. "It's telling me where to start measuring."

"Is that good?"

"I have absolutely no idea." She laughed, and the laugh came out wobbly. "Beatrix, I have surveyed salt flats and war zones and a swamp that ate my entire expedition's boots, and none of it ever *answered* me. This—" she gestured helplessly at the beds, the bee, the impossible fruit— "this is looking back."

* * *

They walked the rows because there was nothing else sensible to do, and because Beatrix refused to leave without understanding the ground — *never sleep somewhere you haven't read twice,* her grandmother's rule, apparently, though her grandmother had been dead thirty years and somehow her rules had crossed a century with her intact.

It was Paloma who worked out what the plants were.

She was sketching again — she always sketched, it was how she thought, pencil moving so her fear had somewhere to go — and she had drawn the fourth bed three times before she admitted what she was seeing. The plants there were forget-me-nots, but grown enormous, waist-high, each flower the size of a saucer, and they were arranged in a spiral that spiraled *inward*, tighter and tighter toward a center where the soil was darker than anywhere else, dark as spilled ink.

"Memory," she said aloud, testing it. "The verse said it. *Every root drinks what was forgotten.*" She looked up at Beatrix, who had gone very still among the roses. "These aren't plants. Or — they are, but that's not what they're *for*. Each bed is a memory. Grown from a memory. Buried here by someone, or left here, or—" she stopped, recalculating, the way she did when a map refused to fit the territory. "Or *shed*."

"Shed how?"

"I don't know. But look." She led Beatrix along the row, pointing. "This bed smells of woodsmoke and there's chrysanthemum — mourning flowers. This one's all lavender and something burnt underneath. That one—" she hesitated at a plot of white lilies so tall they arched overhead like a chapel, and her throat closed briefly, because her mother had been buried under lilies, and she hadn't thought of that in years, and here the scent was, exact, unbearable. "That one is somebody's worst day. Or best. With memories I think you can't always tell."

Beatrix had stopped at the lilies too, and her face had changed — not softened, exactly; Beatrix's face didn't soften so much as lose an argument with itself. "My grandmother told stories about a garden," she said quietly. "I thought they were just stories. Smugglers' nonsense to make children behave — *the garden below remembers, little bird, so mind what you bury.*" She exhaled through her nose. "I thought it was a threat. It's an address."

Something in Paloma's satchel moved.

Not shifted — *moved*, deliberately, the flap straining, and she yanked it open with fingers that had finally begun to shake, and pulled out her folio, and felt the world tilt gently sideways.

Her maps were redrawing themselves.

The valley survey she'd made yesterday — careful, professional, annotated in her own cramped hand — was rewriting its own margins. Fine new lines crept across the paper like frost spreading across a window, tracing paths she had never walked, doors in hillsides she had never found, and in the corner, in ink that was definitely not hers, a small precise legend was assembling itself letter by letter:

*THE GARDEN KEEPS. THE GARDEN KEEPS. THE GARDEN KEEPS.*

"Beatrix," she said, very calmly, because panic was for people whose equipment wasn't writing to them. "I need you to look at this, and then I need you to tell me whether we run."

Beatrix took the folio. Read it. Her jaw tightened in a way Paloma was already learning to read as *fury held in reserve for a more convenient hour.*

"It knows your name," Beatrix said. "The tavern knew our names. Now the dirt knows your profession." She handed the folio back with exaggerated care, as if returning a loaded weapon. "Verse eleven. *The hand that draws the hidden ways shall draw them home.* I thought that meant a mapmaker would guide us. I'm starting to think it means the garden has been expecting a mapmaker the way a lock expects a key."

"That's not comforting."

"No," Beatrix agreed. "It isn't."

* * *

They made camp at dusk in the lee of the garden wall — a wall Paloma was certain hadn't been there that morning, dry-stacked stone furred with moss, enclosing the far side of the valley as though the garden had grown it overnight the way a snail grows shell. The fire caught on the first strike despite damp wood, which Beatrix pronounced "suspiciously helpful," and they ate hard bread and the apples Beatrix had finally consented to pick, after a ten-minute argument about whether eating the garden's fruit constituted accepting a contract.

Paloma won the argument by pointing out that refusing offered hospitality was its own kind of oath, and Beatrix had grumbled but bitten, and then gone quiet, and said, "Tastes like my grandmother's orchard," in a voice that ended the conversation better than any argument could have.

Now Paloma sat with the folio on her knees, working by firelight, doing the thing that steadied her: making order. She had started a fresh sheet, titled it *The Garden Below — First Survey*, and begun to map what she'd measured. Beds, rows, the spiral of the forget-me-nots, the wall, the spring that fed a stone basin no one drank from.

The pencil kept drifting.

Not her hand — the pencil. Or the paper pulling the graphite, she couldn't say which, but the lines she drew kept extending beyond her intention, adding detail she hadn't observed: a path behind the plum trees, a second terrace above the first, a shape in the upper garden that her pencil rendered before her eyes had ever seen it. A door. A small door, set into the hillside itself, framed in living roots.

And around the little drawn door, her own hand — her actual hand, she watched it happen, watched the muscles obey something that was not entirely her — wrote a caption:

*Here grows what Beatrix Ravensworth will not say.*

Paloma put the pencil down.

She looked across the fire. Beatrix sat with the prophecy book open, though Paloma had noticed she hadn't turned a page in an hour; she just held it, the way you hold a sleeping animal that has bitten you before. Firelight moved on her face and showed the exhaustion under the competence, the guardedness that Paloma had mistaken, on their first evening — their real first evening, in the tavern, a hundred years from here — for aloofness, until the moment Beatrix had laughed at Paloma's terrible joke about projection errors and had looked so surprised at herself that Paloma had fallen a little bit in love right there between the cheese board and the candle.

That woman was sitting forty feet away in a garden made of other people's buried grief, and the garden was already drawing maps of what she refused to say.

"Paloma." Beatrix hadn't looked up. "Your pencil stopped. You've been going like a millwheel all evening. What did it write?"

Paloma considered lying. She was, she realized, quite good at it — cartographers learned diplomacy young; you didn't survive border country by telling villagers the truth about their neighbors' claims. And then she looked at the line again, at the small neat door her hand had drawn without permission, and understood that in this place, lies might have weight in a very literal sense. Things buried here grew. She did not want to find out what a buried lie became.

"A door," she said. "In the upper garden. My pencil drew it before I saw it, so either it exists and I'll find it tomorrow, or it's asking to exist, and I honestly don't know which is worse." She paused. "And a caption."

"What kind of caption?"

"*Here grows what Beatrix Ravensworth will not say.*"

The fire popped. Beatrix closed the book, slowly, deliberately, the way you close a door on a room where something is crying. For a long moment she looked at the flames, and Paloma watched her decide — watched the decision move through her like weather — whether to be angry, or afraid, or honest.

"Huh," Beatrix said at last, which was none of those, and was somehow all of them.

"You don't have to tell me."

"No. I don't." Beatrix looked up, and her eyes were firelit and level and, beneath everything, tired in a century-deep way that had nothing to do with how long they'd walked. "But the garden knows, doesn't it? That's the trick of this whole valley. It's not growing flowers. It's growing *everything people wouldn't say out loud*, and it's been doing it for a hundred years, and we're standing in the compost of an entire kingdom's silences." She smiled, and it was a terrible, wonderful smile. "Sooner or later, Paloma Idris, this place is going to make me say it. Whatever 'it' turns out to be."

Paloma's heart did something complicated and unsurveyable.

"Well," she managed, "for what it's worth, I'll be taking extremely accurate notes."

"Don't you dare map me."

"I'm a cartographer. I map everything I love." The words were out before she could weigh them, hanging in the smoke between them, and Paloma froze — but Beatrix didn't flinch. Beatrix went very still, the way she went still before drawing steel, and looked at Paloma across the fire with an expression that had no name in any language Paloma spoke.

Above them, in the dark upper garden where the pencil had drawn the door that neither of them had yet seen, something flowered all at once — a sound like a thousand buds opening in the same second, soft and vast and deliberate.

Both of them turned to look. The hillside above the last terrace had changed. Pale blossoms covered the slope where bare earth had been at sundown, and in their midst, unmistakable now, framed in living root:

A door. Standing open.

And from somewhere behind it, faint as breath, someone was humming a smuggler's rhyme — one Beatrix's grandmother used to sing — and the garden's single tireless bee had gone silent, as if it, too, were listening.
