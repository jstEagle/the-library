## 5. What the Crown Wears

The village sat at the valley's lip like a stone dropped at the edge of a dream — Thornfell's edge, and yet not. Beatrix smelled it before she saw it: woodsmoke, tallow, wet wool, the green rot of a garden running riot beyond any wall meant to hold it. Smoke rose from chimneys in thin, obedient columns. Laundry hung on lines, white as teeth. Somewhere a dog barked twice and stopped, as if it had forgotten why.

"Too quiet," Paloma murmured beside her.

Beatrix said nothing. She had grown up on smuggler roads where silence meant soldiers or worse, and her body had learned to read it the way other people read weather. But this was a different species of quiet. This was the silence of a village that had nothing left to gossip about. Nothing new happened here. Nothing had happened here for a long time.

They came down the last switchback with the sun behind them, two travelers on a road that shouldn't exist, wearing dust and a century of distance. The book rode in Paloma's satchel against her hip, and Beatrix could feel it there without looking — a warmth like a sleeping animal, like something breathing slow and patient in the dark of the leather.

*The verse had led them here.* The pages had turned themselves twice on the mountain road, ink crawling into new arrangements while Paloma swore and held it flat and tried to pin it down like a specimen. What it showed them now was simple enough: *where the crown keeps its house, go and see what it wears.*

So they went to see.

* * *

The first person they met was a woman drawing water from the village well, and she smiled at them, and the smile was wrong.

Not hostile-wrong. Not suspicious. Wrong the way a portrait is wrong — painted by someone who had been told what a smile was but had never quite seen one happen. Her eyes crinkled at the proper places. Her mouth curved the proper amount. And none of it reached anywhere. It sat on her face like paint on glass.

"Travelers," the woman said, delighted. "Travelers! It's been so long."

"How long?" Paloma asked, easy, pleasant, her cartographer's voice — the one she used when she wanted strangers to underestimate how carefully she was watching them. Beatrix watched too. She watched the woman's hands, which were steady on the bucket rope, and her feet, which did not shift their weight, and her shadow, which lay long across the cobbles in the late light.

"It must be years," the woman said. "Since anyone came from outside." She said *outside* the way you'd say *the moon*, or *the bottom of the sea*. "You'll stay for supper. Everyone stays for supper."

"We're bound through," Beatrix said. "Just need directions and provisions."

The woman's smile stayed exactly where it was. "Everyone stays for supper," she repeated, and Beatrix felt the words settle over the street like frost.

And then Beatrix saw it. The sun was lowering behind the western ridge, throwing every shadow eastward, long and sharp-edged — and the woman's shadow moved a half-second after she did. She lifted the bucket; the shadow lifted its bucket a heartbeat later. She turned toward her door; the shadow began turning while she was still mid-motion, catching up, arriving just in time, like a dancer who knew the steps but kept losing the count.

Beatrix's hand went to her sword hilt without her permission. She forced it back down. *Don't draw steel in a quiet village,* her grandmother's voice said in her skull. *Draw steel only when the quiet breaks.*

Paloma had seen it too. Beatrix knew because Paloma had gone very still in that particular way of hers — the stillness of a woman memorizing terrain. Their eyes met for half a breath. *Later,* the look said. *Say nothing. Learn everything.*

"Supper would be welcome," Paloma said warmly. "Thank you."

* * *

The village was called Aldermoor, and it had perhaps forty houses, one inn, one shrine, and one king.

That last fact was written on the shrine. Beatrix stood before it while Paloma negotiated beds and bread inside the inn, and read the carved inscription three times to be sure her eyes weren't lying:

**UNDER THE CROWN OF ALDRIC THE GARDENER-KING, THE VALLEY KEEPS ITS PEACE.**

*Aldric.* The name snagged in her like a fishhook. She knew that name. Every child raised on Ravensworth stories knew it — the lost king, the last king of the old line, who vanished into the Thornfell wars a hundred years ago and was mourned in songs so old they'd worn smooth as river stones. Her grandmother used to hum one while mending nets. *Aldric under the hill, Aldric under the snow, wait for the swordspeaker's hand, wait for the garden's glow.*

She had thought it a lullaby. A nonsense rhyme, the way most of her grandmother's rhymes were nonsense until suddenly, horribly, they weren't.

But here the verse said he reigned. Here, a hundred years ago, King Aldric was not lost. He was *here* — young, enthroned, keeping his peace in a valley where no one smiled properly and shadows lagged half a beat behind their owners.

And the carving beneath the king's name showed him crowned, and the crown was wrong. The stonemason had rendered it as a circlet of leaves and thorns, and where the leaves met, they had carved small open mouths.

Beatrix stepped back. The shrine's little alcove was cool and dim, and it smelled of old incense and newer rot, and she realized the smell was coming from the offerings at the king's carved feet: flowers. Fresh ones, laid in careful rows, all of them cut from plants she recognized — memory-plants. Foxglove that bloomed in colors foxglove had no business knowing. A sprig of something silver-leafed that her grandmother had named *yesterday's yew* and refused ever to explain.

Someone in this village was cutting flowers from the buried memories of the dead, or of the living, or of whatever grew in that impossible garden below — and laying them at the feet of a king made of stone whose crown had mouths.

"You shouldn't touch those."

Beatrix turned. An old man stood at the shrine's edge, hat in his hands, wringing it slowly. He had a farmer's face, seamed and brown, and the same painted-on warmth as the well-woman, except that his had cracked. Through the cracks she could see something else. Something tired. Something afraid.

"I wasn't going to," Beatrix said.

"No." He nodded too many times. "No, of course. But folk get ideas, seeing them. Folk think, these are only flowers. Folk take one home, put it in water on the sill." His hands twisted the hat tighter. "And then they remember things they set down. Things they gave up. And then—" He stopped. Swallowed. Started again, quieter. "Then the king sends for them. To ask them about it. Kindly. He's always kindly."

"And then?" Beatrix asked.

The man looked at her, and for one unguarded moment the painted smile fell off him entirely, and what was underneath was so hollow that Beatrix felt her stomach drop as if she'd missed a stair.

"Then they're kindly too," he said. "Forever after. Very kind. Very agreeable. My wife is very agreeable now." He put his hat back on. "Enjoy your supper, traveler. Everyone enjoys supper here."

He walked away, and his shadow followed him faithfully, promptly, exactly on time — and somehow that was worse than the lagging ones. As though whatever watched this valley checked each villager's shadow the way a shepherd counts sheep, and this man's had been counted, and found correct.

* * *

Supper was stew, and it was good, and everyone at the inn's common table was friendly in the way of dolls arranged around a toy table. Paloma ate with her map-callused hands and asked questions with the artless charm of a traveling surveyor — harvests, trade roads, the lie of the land northward — and got answers that were smooth and useless, like stones from a riverbed. Everyone farmed. Everyone traded a little. Everyone was content. Nobody remembered a hard winter, a fever, a wedding, a funeral. When Beatrix asked an old shepherd what the valley had been like in his father's day, he beamed at her and said, "Much as it is now. The king sees to that."

"The king sees to it," his neighbor agreed.

"The king sees to it," said the innkeeper's daughter, who looked sixteen and had eyes like the well-water — clear, cold, and showing nothing at the bottom.

Under the table, Paloma's knee pressed against Beatrix's. *Steady.* Beatrix breathed and kept eating and let her grandmother's oldest lesson run through her head like a rope hand over hand: *when you're among wolves, eat like a wolf, walk like a wolf, and save your howling for when it counts.*

It was the innkeeper's daughter who finally broke pattern. As the bowls were cleared, she collected them with her gaze fixed somewhere past the middle distance, and she said, softly, in a voice pitched for no one:

"The hedge-wife is dying. If you're the ones the verse speaks of, you should see her tonight. Before the king does."

Then she smiled her painted smile, curtsied to the room, and carried the bowls into the kitchen humming a lullaby Beatrix's grandmother used to sing.

* * *

The hedge-wife lived at the village's ragged hem, where the cobbles gave up and became mud and the garden's green spilled over every fence like a tide that never finished coming in. Her cottage leaned into a bank, roofed with living turf, chimney smoking a thread.

"She knows we're coming," Paloma whispered.

"They all know we're coming," Beatrix said. "That's what frightens me."

The door opened before they knocked.

The woman inside was dying — no mistaking it, no polite fiction of it. She was small and shrunk into her bed, hands like paper over bone, skin gone the yellow-gray of old candle wax. But her eyes — her eyes were the first whole, honest, human things Beatrix had seen since they came down the mountain. They were fierce and wet and entirely present, and when they fixed on Beatrix, they widened with something that looked terribly like recognition and terribly like pity.

"Ravensworth," the hedge-wife rasped. "I knew your great-grandmother's hands. Yours are the same. Come closer, girl. Let me see the marks before my eyes quit me."

Beatrix didn't move. "How do you know that name?"

"Because I've been waiting a hundred years to say it to someone." The old woman laughed, and the laugh turned into a cough that bent her nearly double, and Paloma lunged forward with the water jug, holding it to lips that had gone blue-tinged. When the fit passed, the hedge-wife sagged back and studied them both with terrible patience. "Sit. Both of you. Death and I have come to an arrangement — he waits while I talk. He's the only thing in this valley that keeps appointments honestly."

They sat. The cottage was crowded with hanging bundles of herbs, jars of dark preserves, charms of woven straw — and, Beatrix noticed, not one flower. Not one bloom from the garden. In a village drowning in out-of-season green, this woman's windowsill held only a pot of plain, ordinary thyme, browned at the edges, stubbornly mortal.

"You felt it," the hedge-wife said to Beatrix. "In the street today. The shadows."

"Their shadows move late," Beatrix said. "Half a second behind. Some don't."

"Some are still being eaten," the old woman said simply. "Some are finished." She turned her head on the pillow with effort. "You've come from the garden. Don't deny it — it's written on your friend's satchel. Look."

Paloma looked. So did Beatrix, and her blood went cold. The canvas of the satchel, where it had brushed the valley grasses on the road down, had sprouted. Tiny roots, fine as spider silk, had worked their way into the weave, and from them rose a single leaf, unfurling even as they watched, green and glossy and alive. Growing toward Paloma, the way — Beatrix remembered the verses now, remembered Paloma reading them aloud in a shaking voice — *it grows toward truths people refuse to say.*

"That's not possible," Paloma said. "Plants don't—"

"In this valley, everything grows toward truth," the hedge-wife said. "Or toward its absence. That's what the garden *is*, child. It's the king's larder."

Silence. Outside, the evening birds had gone quiet, all of them, everywhere, at once.

"Tell us about the king," Beatrix said. Her voice came out low and steady, and she was grateful to it, because everything under her ribs was moving like floodwater.

"Aldric." The hedge-wife pronounced the name with care, the way you'd handle a blade by the flat. "A good man, once. Better than most kings — I remember, I was old even then, old enough to be old when he was crowned. Kind to his tenants. Fair in judgment. He loved this valley the way a gardener loves a plot — not because it was his, but because he tended it." Her papery hands worked at the blanket. "And then the wars came to Thornfell, the wars that broke the old kingdom, and Aldric rode out to hold the passes. And he found, in a barrow above the high snow, a crown older than any kingdom. A crown with no maker's mark. A crown that fit him perfectly, though it had waited a thousand years for a head."

"The crown-spirit," Beatrix said. The words came out of her like something pulled on a hook. "The prophecy says—"

"The prophecy says a great deal, and half of it is bait." The old woman's eyes glittered. "Listen to me, swordspeaker, because I will only say this once and death is drumming his fingers. You think the enemy wears the crown. You think you'll find a monster under it — some demon, some wraith, something you can name and fight and kill. That's what every Ravensworth believed, and it's why your line has bled for a hundred years without winning." She drew a breath that whistled. "The crown is not an object. It is not a trap. It is not a curse laid on a king."

"Then what is it?" Paloma asked, barely above a whisper.

"*It is a tenant.*" The hedge-wife said it with finality, the way you'd pronounce a sentence of law. "It moved into Aldric the way a squatter moves into a great house. Slowly. Politely. Room by room. First it wore his face in public and left him his nights. Then it took his anger — he hadn't needed anger in so long. Then his grief. Then his doubts, his appetites, his private jokes, the song he hummed when he thought no one listened. Each thing it took, it fed to the ground, and the ground grew the garden. Every plant down there, every impossible bloom — that's a piece of a king. That's Aldric's laughter growing wild. That's his childhood, flowering out of season. The spirit doesn't devour flesh, girls. Flesh is cheap. It devours the *self*. It eats a person the way winter eats a hearth — from the edges inward, quietly, while everyone praises the warmth."

Beatrix's mouth had gone dry. "The villagers."

"Are his leavings, in a manner of speaking. The spirit needs keepers, and it makes them the only way it knows — it takes a little self from each of them, year by year, a tithe. Enough to leave them agreeable. Enough to leave them smiling." The hedge-wife's lip curled with what strength remained. "Kindness without a self behind it. That's the flavor of this whole valley. You've tasted it since you crossed the ridge."

"The king," Beatrix said. "Is anything left? Is Aldric still—"

"Deep. Far down. Chained in his own smallest rooms." For the first time, the old woman's voice gentled. "Sometimes, on certain nights, he surfaces. Scratches messages on his own palm in a hand no one can read but him. The spirit doesn't notice anymore. Why would it? It stopped watching the cellar years ago." Her eyes moved between them, and Beatrix saw her tallying them — the sword-hand and the map-hand — and arriving, reluctantly, at hope. "The prophecy names you both. The swordspeaker to speak, the cartographer to chart. But hear the shape of it, Ravensworth. You cannot kill a tenant by burning the house. You cannot cut it out — it has grown through him like roots through soil. The only thing that evicts a tenant—"

"—is ending the lease," Paloma said softly. Beatrix looked at her. Paloma's face was pale but level, her cartographer's mind visibly turning the problem, measuring it. "Something has to choose to go. Or something has to make the house uninhabitable."

"Or the deed has to change hands," the hedge-wife whispered. "There are verses for it. There's always a verse. But the price—" A cough took her again, longer this time, deeper, rattling in the hollow of her chest. When it released her she lay still a moment, breathing in small sips, and when she spoke again her voice had dropped to almost nothing. "There's a reason the last swordspeaker failed. There's a reason your grandmother never taught you the fourth part of the oath. Ask yourself, girl, why a family of oathskepters raised you oathless."

Cold walked up Beatrix's spine, vertebra by vertebra. *Her mother. Her mother who had spoken the oath, once, and paid. Her sister, walking into danger alone while Beatrix stood in the doorway and chose the door instead of the blade.* The garden had grown toward truths people refused to say, and Beatrix had a lifetime of them packed down like river stones.

Outside, somewhere up the darkening valley, a horn sounded. Once. Long and clear and patient.

The hedge-wife's eyes went to the window, and Beatrix watched terror move across a dying woman's face like weather.

"Heard you arrived," the old woman breathed. "The king always hears. Go — out the back, down the herb-row, into the garden paths where his riders won't follow. And Ravensworth —" her hand shot out, shockingly quick, and gripped Beatrix's wrist with the strength of the nearly-dead, "—when you stand before him, look at his *shadow*. Not his face. The face will beguile you. The shadow tells you how much of him is left. And if you see him scratch at his palm — if you see it, even once —"

The grip tightened.

"—then there's still a man in there worth saving. Now *run*."

The horn sounded again, nearer, and this time a second horn answered it from the ridgeline they had descended only hours before — behind them, closing the road home like a book.

Beatrix took Paloma's hand, and they ran.
