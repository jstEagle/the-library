## 6. Oathbound

The hedge-witch's cottage smelled of tansy and old smoke, and it leaned against the hillside the way a tired animal leans against its pen — not built so much as settled there, generations ago, and never quite gotten up again. Its walls were wattle and river clay, patched with what looked like strips of woven reed and, in one place, the flattened lid of a traveling trunk. Dried herbs hung from every rafter in bundles gone gray with age. A single window let in the last copper light of evening, and through it Beatrix could see the village below: low roofs, woodsmoke rising straight up in the windless air, and beyond them all, at the valley's far edge, the pale gleam of a keep she had no name for yet.

She stood just inside the doorway, dripping snowmelt onto a floor of packed earth, and tried to make her hands stop shaking.

They hadn't stopped since the witch had grabbed her wrist.

* * *

It had happened on the road into the village, at the little shrine where the cart-track forked — a stone basin crusted with offerings gone to rot, apples shriveled to leather, candles burned down to puddles of wax and wick. An old woman had been kneeling there when they passed, wrapped in a cloak the color of wet bark, and as Beatrix drew level with her the woman's head snapped up like a hound catching scent.

"Your hands," the witch had said. Not a question. A demand.

Beatrix had kept walking. Paloma, a half-step behind with her satchel clutched to her chest and the book inside it humming faintly the way it had hummed ever since the garden, had hissed, *"Beatrix—"*

But the witch was faster than she had any right to be. She'd crossed the frozen mud in three strides and seized Beatrix's right hand, turning it palm-up in the failing light, and Beatrix — who had broken the grip of mountain bandits and crown soldiers both without breaking stride — had simply... let her. Because the touch was gentle. Because the old fingers tracing the lines of her palm were trembling.

"There," the witch whispered. "There it is. I thought the line was dead. I *prayed* the line was dead."

And Beatrix had looked down at her own hand as if seeing it for the first time in years: the fine white scarring that laced her palm and curled around the base of each finger, branching like frost ferns or river deltas or — she'd never let herself think it before, not once in fifteen years — like the veins of a leaf. The marks every Ravensworth carried. The marks her mother had carried to the grave, and her grandmother before her, earned not by wound but by word, burned into the skin the first time a swordspeaker spoke the oath aloud and meant it.

The sword-marks. She'd spent half her life telling people they were rope burns from a childhood spent hauling cargo over the passes. Most of them believed her. She'd almost believed herself.

"The cottage," the witch had said now, still holding her hand, looking up with eyes gone milk-pale at the edges but fierce in the center. "Both of you. Now, before full dark. There are things said after dark in this valley that shouldn't be heard by anyone carrying what you're carrying." Her gaze slid to Paloma's satchel, and something in her face went old and frightened and certain all at once. "Especially that."

* * *

Now the witch — Maren, she said her name was, though she offered it the way one offers a coin already bitten — moved about the single room lighting candles with a taper, muttering to each flame as she woke it, as if asking its pardon. Paloma sat on a stool near the hearth with the book open across her knees, ostensibly studying it, actually watching Beatrix with the particular careful attention she'd developed over the past two days: the look of someone mapping a person the way she mapped terrain, noting the fault lines.

"You should eat," Maren said, setting down bread and hard cheese and a crock of honey. "You'll need your strength, and not for the reason you think."

"We need to find the king," Beatrix said. "The young one. Aldric. We've seen the soldiers, we've seen the way this whole valley bends toward him like grass toward sun, and we've read enough of that book to know he's the center of it. So if you know the roads to the keep—"

"I know the roads." Maren lowered herself into a chair by the fire with the care of someone whose joints had opinions. "I've known them sixty years. And I'm telling you no road leads anywhere worth going until you understand what you are."

"I know what I am. I'm a guide. I take caravans over Thornfell. I avoid soldiers, weather, and prophecy, generally in that order."

"You're a swordspeaker."

The word landed in the room like a dropped blade. Even the fire seemed to lean away from it.

"No," Beatrix said.

Maren's pale eyes didn't blink. "Show me your hands."

"They're rope burns. Cargo work. You can ask anyone on the—"

"*Girl.*" It wasn't loud, but it cracked through the cottage like green wood in a hot fire, and Beatrix heard beneath it — beneath the age and the authority — something rawer. Grief, maybe. Or hope worn down to the quick. "I knelt at that shrine every dusk for forty years asking the old powers to send the line back. Do you know what I asked for? Not a hero. Not a savior. Just *someone*. Someone whose blood remembers, because mine has forgotten everything except how to wait." She held out her own hand then, palm up, and in the candlelight Beatrix saw the ghost of similar markings on it — faded nearly to nothing, stretched thin and pale, like scars that had given up. "I was hedge-taught. Half an oath, spoken by a half-trained girl to a sword that had already gone quiet. It marked me and left me. That's what happens when the words aren't meant whole." Her voice dropped. "Yours were meant whole. Someone in your line spoke the full oath, recently enough that the marks haven't dulled. Your mother?"

Beatrix's jaw tightened. Outside, the light through the window had gone from copper to bruise.

"My mother is dead," she said.

"I know. They always are, in your line. That's rather the point."

* * *

Paloma spoke for the first time in minutes, quietly, from her stool. "Beatrix. Maybe you should tell her."

"Tell her what? That my grandmother dragged me out of bed at six years old and made me memorize verses until I cried? That she called it 'the family music'? That when my mother took the oath she came back from the speaking with white streaks in her hair and a look in her eyes like she'd seen the bottom of a well?" Beatrix heard her own voice climbing and made herself stop, breathe, start again lower. "I know the oath, if that's what you want. I could recite it drunk, asleep, dying. My grandmother made sure of that. She said the words would keep me safe even if I never spoke them."

"And did they?" Maren asked.

Beatrix didn't answer.

Because here was the truth she kept folded small and buried deep, the truth she hadn't even shown Paloma yet — not wholly, not with the edges showing: the words *had* kept her safe. All her life. Every fall on the ice, every rockslide, every knife in a smuggler's den that should have found her ribs and somehow found her belt instead. The Ravensworth words worked whether or not you spoke them; that was the family curse dressed up as a blessing. Her grandmother had called it standing watch. Her mother had called it a debt. Beatrix had called it nothing at all, because naming it would mean admitting she'd spent thirty years being guarded by a promise she refused to keep, living on interest from an inheritance she'd disowned.

And it had cost her mother everything anyway.

"She spoke it," Beatrix said finally, and the words came out flat and cold, quarried rather than spoken. "The full oath, at the speaking-stone above Highfell, when she was nineteen. You know what a swordspeaker does, hedge-witch? You know what the oath actually *is*?" She laughed, and there was no humor in it. "Everyone thinks it's a battle-magic. Sword sings, enemies fall, glorious songs get written. Nobody sings the rest of it. The oath binds the speaker to every wrong within the sound of their voice. Every injustice the sword can reach, the speaker has to answer. Has to. It's not a choice after the words are said — the words *are* the choice, and after that you're just... spent. Spent answering. My mother answered every call for twenty-two years. Bandits, feuds, a flood at Wrenmouth, a fire at the salt-works. She gave and gave and the oath took and took, and do you know how she died?"

Maren's face had gone very still.

"Tired," Beatrix said. "She died tired. In her own bed, at fifty-one, worn through like a rope over a cliff-edge, and the last thing she said to me was *don't speak it, Bea. Whatever happens, whatever calls you — don't. Let the line end clean.*" Her hands had curled into fists without her noticing, the white marks standing out sharp against her knuckles. "So don't sit there in your leaning house and tell me what I am. I know exactly what I am. I'm the last of a line of women who were used up by their own promises, and I am the one who ended it. That's not a shame I carry. That's the one thing I've ever done right."

The fire popped. Somewhere in the walls, a mouse moved through the dried herbs with a sound like falling sand.

Maren looked at her for a long moment — and then, unexpectedly, she nodded.

"Good," the old woman said. "That's honest. Most who carry the marks either boast of the oath or pretend it isn't there. You've done neither." She leaned forward, and the candlelight caught the ruined ghost-marks on her palm. "But honesty doesn't change what's coming, child. You've felt the book pulling. You've walked in a garden that grows out of graves. And you've seen the king."

A silence. Paloma's head came up sharply. "We haven't told anyone we saw him."

"You didn't have to. This valley sees *him* every day, and everything in it leans." Maren rose, joints cracking like kindling, and shuffled to the window. Beyond the glass, the first stars were surfacing over the eastern ridge — and Beatrix saw again what she'd been trying not to see since they arrived: the wrongness of them, constellations rearranged like furniture in a house she'd only known from stories. "He rode through the village at midwinter," Maren said softly. "Young. Beautiful. Smiling at everyone. And his shadow came around the corner a half-second after he did. Every year the gap widens. Every year there's less of him left inside." She turned. "That thing wearing the crown ate a gardener-king once, and it has been eating kings ever since. And there is exactly one force under sky and stone that it fears. One voice it cannot digest. Guess whose grandmother's grandmother bound it the first time."

Beatrix's mouth had gone dry. "The oath."

"The oath," Maren agreed. "Spoken whole. Spoken by a Ravensworth with the marks and the meaning both. That's the lock. That's always been the lock." She crossed the room, slow and inevitable, and stopped in front of Beatrix, close enough that Beatrix could smell the tansy in her clothes and see her own reflection doubled in those milking eyes. "Speak it. Here, now, tonight, while there's still time to teach you what it costs before it takes its price. Speak the old words and wake what sleeps in your blood, and we'll have a fighting chance when the crown comes hunting the two named in the verse — because it *will* come, girl, it's already coming, I've watched the crows fly wrong for three days."

"No."

The word came out of Beatrix before she'd decided anything at all, and it came out iron-hard.

Maren sighed, unsurprised. "Child—"

"No." Beatrix stepped back, putting the width of the hearth between them. "You didn't hear me. I didn't refuse out of fear. I refused out of *memory*. I watched what that oath did to the strongest woman I will ever know, and I promised her grave I'd let it die with me, and I have kept that promise for fifteen years through every temptation this rotten world has offered. You think the crown-spirit is the monster here? The oath is a monster too. A patient one. It wears a family like a crown wears a king." Her voice shook and she let it. "I won't. Ask me tomorrow, ask me dying, ask me at the gates of whatever hell that thing crawled out of — the answer is no."

For a moment the cottage was silent enough that Beatrix could hear Paloma breathing.

Then Maren said, gently, terribly: "I know. I hoped you'd say it."

"What?"

"Because the oath was never meant to be spoken to please an old woman, or a prophecy, or a book." The witch's voice had gone soft as ash settling. "It was meant to be spoken true. And now we know yours would be." She turned back to the fire. "Get some sleep. Both of you. Tomorrow I'll teach you what I remember of the binding-verse, and you can decide what to do with—"

The sword sang.

There was no other word for it. The blade at Beatrix's hip — her plain, honest, unremarkable pass-guarding sword, bought secondhand in Lowmark fifteen years ago, never once having done anything more remarkable than cut rope — *sang*. A single clear note, low and resonant, rising out of the steel like breath through a bottle, and the candle flames all bent toward it at once, and the white marks on Beatrix's palms lit up with a heat like sunburn and a light like dawn.

She looked down at her own hand and watched the frost-fern scars glow gold.

"Oh," said Maren, in a voice like a prayer finally answered. "*Oh*, there it is."

"Make it stop." Beatrix's hand hovered over the hilt without touching it. The note swelled, sweet and terrible, harmonizing with something — with the book, she realized, with the low hum Paloma's satchel had carried for two days, the two sounds finding each other across the room like long-parted voices. "Make it stop, I didn't say anything, I *refused*—"

"That's how it wakes," Maren breathed. She had both hands pressed to her mouth, and tears were running freely down the old seams of her face. "Not to obedience. To refusal. Forty years I waited, and it answers a *no*—" She laughed, wet and wondering. "Your mother's oath didn't die with her, girl. It went down the line and waited. It waited for someone strong enough to say no first — because the binding needs a speaker who chooses, and a choice means nothing unless refusing was real."

The note climbed. The sword-shape of it pressed against Beatrix's hip like a heartbeat not her own, and along her palms the golden light traced the old marks and began — she felt it, unmistakably, the way you feel rain starting — began to *change* them. Deepening. Branching further. Writing.

"Beatrix." Paloma was beside her now, one hand hovering near her shoulder, not quite touching, her eyes wide and bright and afraid. "What's it writing?"

Beatrix looked down.

In light, along the lines of her palm, in a script older than the kingdom, older than the valley, the sword was writing the first words of the oath she had sworn never to speak.

Outside, far off across the dark fields, a horn sounded from the direction of the keep — one long note, then another, then a third — and every dog in the village began to howl.
