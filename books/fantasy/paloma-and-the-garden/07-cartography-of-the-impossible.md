## 7. Cartography of the Impossible

Paloma had always believed that maps were the most honest form of lying.

A map told you where the road went, but not why it bent. It showed you the river's course but never its temper. Every cartographer she'd ever trained under — old Master Fenwick with his ink-stained cuffs, the sour woman at the Guildhall who could smell an inaccurate contour from across a room — had taught her the same catechism: *the map is not the territory; the map is a promise about the territory.* You drew what was true, and you accepted that truth would always be partial, and you slept well knowing the difference between representation and reality.

The garden had no interest in any of that.

Paloma sat cross-legged on a flat stone in the middle of the hidden valley, her satchel open beside her, and watched her own work betray her. The map of Thornfell she'd drawn three years ago — commissioned by a mining concern, rendered in careful sepia, every ridge line surveyed twice — had begun to change. Not smudge, not fade. *Change.* The ridgelines were rearranging themselves like sleeping animals turning over. A river she had never charted was threading itself through the western quarter in fine blue ink that glistened wetly, as though it had been drawn moments ago and hadn't yet dried.

And in the center of the map, where the mining concern's surveyors had marked nothing but empty scrubland, a valley was blooming.

She recognized its shape. She had walked its length yesterday morning, or a hundred years ago this morning, depending on how one counted. The garden's contours were appearing on her map stroke by stroke, as if some invisible hand were copying the land into her cartography — or as if her cartography had finally decided to stop pretending it didn't know this place.

"Beatrix," she said, without looking up. "You should see this."

A pause in the rhythm of sword-maintenance behind her. Beatrix Ravensworth had spent the last hour sharpening a blade that did not need sharpening, which Paloma had learned was what Beatrix did instead of saying things like *I'm frightened* or *I don't understand any of this either.*

"I've seen enough impossible things since we fell through that book," Beatrix said. Her voice came closer. "But show me anyway."

Paloma turned the map toward her. Beatrix crouched, studied it, and Paloma watched her face do the thing it did — the quick flicker of calculation, the jaw tightening, the way her eyes moved like a scout's, checking the treeline even while reading paper.

"That's the valley," Beatrix said slowly. "That's *this* valley. On your map."

"On my map. Which I drew before either of us knew this valley existed." Paloma ran her thumb along the new river. Still damp. "Which means either my map is drawing itself—"

"—or something is using your map to draw." Beatrix finished it for her, because that was what they did now, had done since the tavern, since the book: finished each other's sentences when the sentences were too strange to say alone.

* * *

The discovery came later that day, and it came, as all the worst discoveries did, from the book.

They had made camp in the lee of a stone wall so ancient its mortar had become soil, so overgrown with moss that it seemed less built than grown. The wall marked the garden's northern boundary, and beyond it the mountain rose in dark pines, and somewhere up there, according to the hedge-witch's dying directions, stood the village where a young king reigned with his shadow half a heartbeat behind him. But that was tomorrow's problem. Tonight there was a fire, hardtack softened in melted snow, and the book open on Paloma's knees.

It was a beautiful object, which she kept forgetting because it terrified her. Calfskin gone soft as suede with age, corners worn round by a century of hands. The pages were heavy rag paper, and the ink — she'd examined this closely, professionally, the first night — was iron gall, properly made, the kind of ink a careful scribe mixed fresh and used within the month. Someone had loved this book once. Someone had written it with the same devotion Paloma brought to her finest commissions.

Tonight she turned past the verses they already knew — the ones naming Beatrix's line, the swordspeakers, the oath — looking for anything about the crown, the garden, the way home. And she found, tucked between two chapters of prophecy like a pressed flower between pages of scripture, a folio of drawings.

Her breath stopped.

Not drawings exactly. *Maps.* Hand-inked, exquisitely detailed, unmistakably professional. She knew the conventions — she'd spent ten years learning them, arguing about them, dreaming in them. Hachured relief shading. A compass rose with a fleur-de-lis north point, slightly idiosyncratic, the kind of flourish a confident hand added against convention. Scale bars in chains and furlongs. And the subject—

The garden. This garden. Drawn from above with the loving precision of someone who had walked every path, measured every bed, mapped every tree. There was the spring-fed pool where Paloma had washed her face that morning. There was the row of white-flowering trees that bloomed out of season. There, in the southeast corner, a small structure marked with a symbol Paloma knew from guild notation: *observatory,* or sometimes *workshop.*

In the lower corner of the final plate, in a small, proud, careful hand:

*Surveyed and drawn by Ysolt Idris, Cartographer, in the year the verse was written.*

Paloma read the name four times. Five. The fire crackled. Somewhere in the dark, the garden breathed — she had started thinking of it as breathing, the whole valley inhaling and exhaling in slow vegetal rhythm — and she sat absolutely still with her ancestor's name in her lap.

"Paloma?" Beatrix's voice, quiet. "Your face just did something."

"My name," Paloma said. Her voice sounded far away to her own ears. "My family name. There's a cartographer in here. Ysolt Idris. She drew these maps — drew *this*, the whole garden, a hundred years before I was born." She looked up. "Beatrix, the book didn't choose you by accident. It chose both of us. It chose our *bloodlines*. Your grandmother's oaths, my—" She stopped, laughed, a small unsteady sound. "I bought this book to impress you. I paid six silver marks for it from a dealer in Lowmarket who said it came out of an estate sale, no provenance, probably worthless."

"And instead it was worth everything," Beatrix said, "and cost everything, and here we are." She shifted around the fire to sit shoulder to shoulder with Paloma, close enough that Paloma could feel the warmth of her, and looked down at the plates. "Ysolt Idris. Any relation?"

"Has to be. The name passes down the female line in my family — my mother's mother's mother. I used to think it was just an heirloom name, like the good silver." Paloma traced the signature with one fingertip, not quite touching the ink. "There's more, isn't there. There's always more with this book."

There was. She turned the page and found the last of Ysolt's plates — unfinished, the linework trailing off into nothing, the southern third of the garden left blank — and beneath it, a passage in the same careful hand, but hurried now, the letters leaning forward as if the writer were being pulled:

*I have learned the garden's law and I set it down here so that whoever follows me will not have to learn it as I did, in grief. The garden does not grow toward sun or water. It grows toward truths refused. Every plant in this valley is rooted in something someone would not say. The pool is fed by a confession no one made. The white trees flower from a promise broken and never acknowledged. When I began mapping this place I believed it was merely strange. Now I understand it is a ledger. It keeps accounts of silence.*

*I am going to walk into the southern quarter, which I cannot map, because the southern quarter grows from my own refusals, and I find I cannot look at them straight enough to draw them. If I do not return, let my successor know: the way to map the unmappable is not better instruments. It is honesty. Bring your whole self or bring nothing.*

*— Y.I.*

The page ended there. No date of death, no closing flourish. Just the lean of those last letters, hurrying toward something.

Paloma closed the book with hands that were not entirely steady.

"She vanished," she said. "Into her own map. Into the part she couldn't draw." She stared into the fire. "I have her hands, Beatrix. Did you know that? My mother used to say I had Ysolt's hands. Long fingers, the same callus on the middle finger from the pen. I thought it was a joke. Family resemblance, nothing more." She flexed her fingers, looked at them as if they belonged to a stranger. "I'm standing inside her life's work. I'm sitting in the margin of her map."

Beatrix was quiet for a long moment. When she spoke, her voice had the low gravity it carried when she talked about her grandmother, about the oaths, about inheritance — the voice of someone who knew exactly what it meant to be handed a legacy you never asked for.

"The prophecy named the Ravensworth line for the sword," she said. "Maybe it named yours for the map. Maybe it needed both. A blade to speak and a hand to chart." She paused. "Or maybe that's what I want it to be, because if the book chose us, then none of this is random, and if none of this is random, then at least it isn't *cruelty without design*."

Paloma huffed a laugh despite herself. "You have a very grim comfort style."

"It's a mountain upbringing."

* * *

She began the next morning, and she began the way Ysolt's note had instructed, though she didn't realize it until evening: with honesty.

Her instruments were useless here, she discovered within the hour. Her brass compass needle spun lazily, pointing at whatever it pleased — once, memorably, at Beatrix. Her chain measure gave different lengths on different passes of the same path. The garden would not be reduced to bearings and distances. So she improvised. She drew freehand, working large, pinning sheets of paper to a board across her knees. She sketched the shapes of things rather than their measurements, and found, to her astonishment, that the shapes held. The garden permitted likeness. It simply refused arithmetic.

By midday she had noticed the second rule.

She was mapping the eastern beds, a tangle of flowering vines she'd taken for ornamental, when Beatrix came to stand beside her and said, abruptly, "I haven't told you about my sister."

Paloma's pen stopped.

"Not now," Beatrix said, and her voice had shut like a door. "Forget I said it."

But the vines had moved.

Paloma saw it happen — actually saw it, the way one sees a star move only by attending to its absence of movement elsewhere. The nearest vine uncoiled a finger's width toward Beatrix, toward the place where she stood, its pale flowers turning like faces. And where it grew, a new tendril emerged that had not existed seconds before, and its shape was — Paloma's cartographer's eye catalogued it helplessly, automatically — *topographical.* It looked like a ridge line. Like a scar in the land. Like a long straight thing that might have been a road, or a wound.

"You said 'my sister,'" Paloma whispered, "and it grew."

Beatrix looked at the vine. Paloma watched her go very still, the stillness of prey, of someone who has heard the predator's step in dry leaves. Then Beatrix said, flatly, "Don't map that."

"I have to."

"*Don't.*"

"Beatrix." Paloma set down her pen and turned to face her fully, and made herself hold the grey-green gaze, though it cost something; Beatrix's attention had weight, like weather. "Ysolt wrote it down. The garden grows toward truths people refuse to say. Which means—" She took a breath. "Which means the parts of this garden we can't map, the blank spaces, the places where the paths tangle into knots — those aren't confusion. Those are *secrets.* Yours. Mine. Everyone who ever walked here. The garden is a map of everything we won't tell each other."

"And you want to read it."

"I want to *chart* it," Paloma said. "There's a difference. Reading is taking. Charting is—" She searched for the word and found it, surprising herself. "Charting is listening. I listen to the land and write down what it says. That's the whole craft. That's all any of us ever do." She gestured at the valley, the impossible blooming abundance of it. "This place has been listening to people for a hundred years. It's full of what they couldn't say. And somewhere in it — I'd stake my guild membership on this — somewhere in it is what we need. The crown's weakness. The way through. The way *home*. But we'll only find it if we're willing to be heard too."

Beatrix looked at her for a long time. The wind moved the white trees at the valley's center, and their blossoms made a sound like distant applause, or like rain beginning.

"You're different here," Beatrix said at last. "In the tavern you were — I liked you, I was nervous, you were nervous, it was all very human and small. Here you're—" She shook her head. "Here you're certain. I've watched you pick up an impossible task the way other people pick up a cup of tea."

"It's the first task anyone's ever given me that matters," Paloma said, and heard the truth of it land in her own chest like a struck bell. "Do you know what I did before this? I mapped mining claims. Drainage ditches. I drew the honest borders of dishonest parcels of land for men who wanted to underpay farmers. I was *good* at it, and it was eating me alive, and I told myself craft was enough, that it didn't matter what the craft served." She looked down at Ysolt's unfinished plate, the trailing lines, the vanishing. "She came here and she got lost in her own refusals. I keep thinking — I can finish her map. Not the way she couldn't. I can finish it *because* of what she wrote. She left me the answer in her own handwriting. That's more inheritance than most people ever get."

Something shifted in Beatrix's expression — recognition, maybe, or envy gently disguised, or perhaps just the particular tenderness of watching someone else claim what you yourself were still refusing. "Then map," Beatrix said quietly. "And when the garden starts growing toward things I refuse to say—" Her jaw worked. "I'm not promising anything. But I won't stop you."

From Beatrix Ravensworth, Paloma understood, that was practically a love letter.

* * *

She worked for three days, and the garden taught her, and she filled eleven sheets.

She learned that the garden answered questions asked aloud, but only honest ones — ask it *which way to the village* and the paths stayed stubbornly mute; ask it *what does the crown fear* and the white trees shed a single petal that drifted, deliberately, impossibly, against the wind, westward, toward the mountains and the pale court beyond.

She learned that the pool showed reflections of conversations that had happened near it decades ago, ghost-light flickering on the water: a woman's voice saying *I never loved him, I never told him,* and a bed of reeds rising where the words fell.

She learned that her own satchel had changed — the leather grown faintly greener, the stitching tighter, as if the garden had decided she was a plant of a kind too, and was cultivating her.

And on the third evening, alone at the map-board while Beatrix scouted the northern wall, Paloma laid Ysolt's unfinished plate beside her own work and understood, with a clarity that felt like cold water, what she had to do next.

The blank space on Ysolt's map — the southern quarter, the region her ancestor had walked into and never returned from — was no longer blank. Over the past three days, as Paloma's honesty had watered the ground, the garden had been growing into that emptiness. New paths. New beds. New shapes rising out of the unmappable south like islands surfacing from fog.

And at the center of the newly risen territory, in a clearing that had not existed when they arrived, stood a single tree unlike any other in the valley. Dark-barked, heavy-branched, hung with fruit that glowed faintly, like lanterns seen through fog. Paloma had not mapped it yet. She had been avoiding it, she realized — avoiding it the way Ysolt must have avoided the south, the way one avoids the room in the house where the grief lives.

Because she knew, with her ancestor's certainty in her blood, what grew on that tree.

Truths refused. And she knew whose.

The fruit was shaped, unmistakably, like the memory of a girl standing at a crossroads in the rain, watching her sister walk away alone.

Paloma picked up her pen. Her hand, Ysolt's hand, was steady.

Tomorrow, she thought, she would have to decide whether to show Beatrix what the garden had grown from her — whether truth or tenderness served them better. And tonight, far off toward the mountain pass, she heard the first horns of the king's hunt, thin and cold on the wind, coming down into the valley looking for the two named in the verse.
