## 8. The King's Hunt

The horns began at dawn.

Beatrix heard them before she was fully awake — three notes, a pause, three notes again — and her body knew the language before her mind did. She had grown up on that signal. Her grandmother had hummed it while kneading bread, while mending nets, while telling stories by the fire: *three-and-pause means they're looking; three-and-close means they've found.* The hunt-horn of the Pale Court, older than the crown that wore the king, older maybe than the mountains themselves.

She was on her feet with her hand on the sword hilt before Paloma even stirred in the hollow beneath the alder roots where they'd slept.

"Up," Beatrix said quietly. "We're being hunted."

Paloma blinked up at her, hair full of leaf-rot and sleep, and for one absurd heartbeat Beatrix wanted to kiss her — here, under an alder, in a century that wasn't theirs, with horns singing death through the mist. Then Paloma's eyes sharpened, the cartographer's mind clicking into place like a well-oiled lock, and she was rolling up their meager bundle and asking the right questions all at once.

"How many? From which direction? Do they have dogs?"

"Three-and-pause," Beatrix said, tilting her head as the horn sounded again across the valley. "That's a sweep pattern, not a pursuit. They don't know exactly where we are yet. And no dogs this time of year — the valley's too high, the nights still kill them." She paused, listening to the second horn answer the first from the far ridge. "Two parties, moving toward each other. They mean to comb the whole valley floor and push anything alive up against the scree."

"Then we go down?" Paloma frowned. "Into them?"

"We go *through* them." Beatrix felt something shift in her chest — not the old cold weight of the oath, but something lighter, stranger. "There are roads here that aren't on any map. Not because they're secret. Because they only exist if you know the stories."

* * *

They moved out as the light came gray through the fog, along a streambed that babbled with water too cold for the season. Behind them, somewhere above the tree line, the first soldiers would be finding their cold camp and reading its ashes. Beatrix didn't look back. Looking back was for people who hadn't been raised on smuggler rhymes.

Her grandmother's voice lived in her head like a second heartbeat, and it had never been louder than now. *When the king's men walk the vale, walk where the water tells no tale.* She stopped at the stream's edge, knelt, and pressed her palm flat against the current. Freezing. Clean. Singing over stones.

"What are you doing?" Paloma crouched beside her.

"Listening." Beatrix closed her eyes. "My grandmother smuggled salt and secrets through these mountains before the fall — before this valley became a memory. She taught me her routes as songs, because songs survive when maps burn. Every verse is a road." She opened her eyes. "This stream feeds a sinkhole two miles down. Under the sinkhole there's a limestone throat the water carved, and it comes up again on the other side of the Hunter's Spine. The soldiers won't take it. There's a rhyme about it — about what lives in the dark part — and soldiers believe rhymes."

"And does something live in the dark part?"

"No idea." Beatrix grinned, and was surprised to find the grin was real. "But my grandmother swore the rhyme worked better than a locked door ever did. Fear keeps people honest."

Paloma looked at her for a long moment, and Beatrix saw the thought move behind her eyes like a fish under ice. "You've never walked these routes. Not once. You learned them from stories."

"I learned everything from stories." The words came out rougher than Beatrix intended. "The sword-marks, the oaths, the smuggling roads — my family kept it all alive in verses after Thornfell fell, waiting for someone to need it again. I used to think that made me a keeper of nothing. A librarian of a dead country." She stood, wiping her hand on her coat. "Turns out the country isn't dead. It's just a hundred years upstream."

* * *

The sinkhole opened in the hillside like a mouth, ringed with ferns that grew pale and preternaturally lush — garden-spill, Paloma called it, the valley's buried memories leaking into the soil. Beatrix went down first, testing each foothold, the darkness rising around her like cold water. Inside, the limestone throat ran narrow and wet, and their candle-flame bent sideways in a wind that shouldn't exist underground.

Somewhere in the deepest bend, where the passage narrowed to a crawl, the wind carried sound.

Beatrix froze. Paloma bumped into her back. "What—"

"Listen."

Faint, distorted, but unmistakable: voices. Boots on stone. The jingle of mail. The soldiers were *in* the tunnels — or in tunnels connected to these — moving parallel to them through some other vein of the mountain. And among the muffled words, one phrase surfaced again and again, passed from voice to voice like a coin:

*"—the two named in the verse—"*

"They're reading us off the book," Beatrix breathed. "Somebody up there has a copy. Or the original found its way to the court." Cold crawled up her spine that had nothing to do with the cave. If the prophecy existed here, in this century, then the crown-spirit might already know how it ended. Might be trying to read ahead of them, tear out pages before they were written.

Or worse: the spirit had written it.

"Keep moving," she said, and her voice came out steadier than she felt. "Crawl section's ahead. Stay low, breathe shallow, and whatever you hear in the walls — it's just stone settling. It's always just stone settling."

It wasn't stone settling. Something long and patient brushed past the candlelight, or seemed to, and Paloma's hand found hers in the dark and gripped hard, and they crawled together through the mountain's throat until the passage spat them out into daylight on the far side of the Hunter's Spine, blinking, soaked, alive.

Behind them, faint through the rock, the horns sounded three-and-close.

They'd missed the sweep by less than an hour.

* * *

The far side of the Spine was country Beatrix knew only as story: the Hanging Meadows, where her grandmother's sister had once hidden a wagonload of contraband wool under a flock of very confused sheep. The Sheep-Walk rhyme was nonsense on the surface — *count the bells and mind the bell that doesn't ring* — but Beatrix had spent her childhood pulling those verses apart, mapping them in her head the way other children mapped treasure islands. Now she walked the real ground and felt the stories settle onto it like clothing onto a body.

"There." She pointed to a fold in the hillside where the grass grew in a suspiciously rectangular shadow. "Wool-cache. My great-aunt hid smuggled goods right there, a hundred years ago, and the rhyme says so: *where the hill wears a hem, the hem hides a hand.*"

"You can read the landscape like text," Paloma said softly. She'd unslung her satchel and was sketching as they walked, quick confident strokes, and Beatrix noticed — with a small shock — that the maps inside were redrawing themselves again, ink crawling into new coastlines, new paths. Paths that matched the ones Beatrix was calling up from memory.

"Stop," Beatrix said suddenly.

Paloma stopped. Ahead, the meadow sloped down toward a birch stand, pretty and open and utterly wrong. Beatrix stood very still, letting her grandmother's voice run the verses. *Where the birches stand in ranks like soldiers, turn aside — the king plants his eyes in rows.*

"It's too neat," she murmured. "Birch grow wild, tangled. These are planted. Straight lines." She picked up a stone and threw it into the stand. Nothing happened — no snap of wire, no whistle of alarm — but the birds that should have flushed stayed silent, and when she looked closer she saw the thin glint of a signal-line strung knee-high between the trunks, camouflaged with strips of bark.

"A picket line," she said grimly. "They've seeded the whole valley with them. Anyone who walks that way trips a bell-chain two miles off." She turned to Paloma, and the confidence in her own chest surprised her — warm, unfamiliar, growing. "We go around, down through the Sheep-Walk, and we cross the river at the Fiddler's Ford. There's a verse about why the ford is safe: *the fiddler plays where the water's shallow, and the king's men never learn the tune.*"

"How do you hold all of this?" Paloma asked, genuinely wondering. "A hundred years of routes you've never walked, in *rhyme*?"

Beatrix shrugged, embarrassed. "It's how Ravensworths keep things. We can't write it down — writing gets found, gets burned, gets read by the wrong eyes. So we put it in songs and give the songs to our children like inheritance. I used to resent it. All those evenings drilling verses while other kids played." She looked out over the planted birches, the hunted valley, the smoke of soldier-fires rising thin and gray against the peaks. "Now every song is a door, and I'm the only one carrying the keys."

Paloma took her hand. Her fingers were ink-stained and cold and certain. "You know," she said, "when I met you, I thought the interesting thing about you was the sword. The last swordspeaker. Very dramatic." Her smile went crooked. "I'm revising my notes. The most dangerous thing about you is that you're a library with legs."

"Flatterer."

"Cartographer. I only record what's true."

* * *

They crossed the Fiddler's Ford at dusk, boots in hand, trousers rolled, the water biting at their shins. On the far bank Beatrix allowed them a fire — small, shielded, in the lee of a fallen oak — because the verses said the king's men feared this stretch of river after dark. *The fiddler plays at night,* the rhyme went, *and the king's men will not follow the tune.* She didn't ask what the fiddler was. Some inheritances were better taken on faith.

Over the tiny fire, they ate hard cheese and the last of the valley-bread, and Paloma spread her maps across a flat stone, weighting the corners with pebbles. In the firelight Beatrix watched the ink finish its slow work: the garden drawn in the center now, precise and blooming, and around it the valley's hidden anatomy surfacing line by line — the sinkhole throat, the Sheep-Walk caches, the ford. Paloma's maps were learning Beatrix's songs.

"Look at this," Paloma whispered. She turned one sheet toward the light. At its edge, in ink that hadn't been there an hour ago, a new feature was drawing itself: a building. Tall, many-towered, crowned — the map rendering it with a precision that felt less like cartography and more like confession.

"The Pale Court," Beatrix said. Her mouth had gone dry. "The palace. Why would your map draw the palace? We're nowhere near it."

"Because the map isn't drawing where things are." Paloma traced the towers with one fingertip, and her voice dropped low. "It's drawing where things *are going*. It did this before, remember — the garden, before we found it. It grows toward truths nobody's saying." She looked up, firelight caught in her eyes. "Beatrix. The book burned a page last night. Did you feel it? Time is running out on whatever we're meant to do here. And the map thinks we're going to the palace."

Beatrix stared at the inked towers, and her grandmother's voice rose unbidden with a verse she'd never understood, one the old woman had sung only twice, soft and unwilling:

*Kings keep crowns and crowns keep kings,*
*and both of them keep hungering.*
*If ever you must seek the throne,*
*go knowing what you'll bring back home —*
*or leave the part of you that goes.*

The fire popped. Somewhere across the dark river, faint and patient, a horn sounded three-and-pause — and then, for the first time, a fourth note. Long. Low. New.

Paloma's head came up sharply. "What's that one?"

Beatrix listened, and the warmth in her chest curdled. "I don't know," she said. "There's no verse for that one. They've invented a new call." She reached for her sword without deciding to. "Which means they've stopped hunting blind. Which means somebody told them exactly where we're going."

The map lay between them, the palace towers gleaming wetly in the firelight, still drawing itself — tower by tower by tower — like something patiently remembering them.
