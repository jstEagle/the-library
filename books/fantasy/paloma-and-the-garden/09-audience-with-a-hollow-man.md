## 9. Audience with a Hollow Man

The trap we walked into was of my own drafting, and I want that written plainly somewhere, in some hand steadier than mine: it was my plan.

Beatrix had argued against it the whole night before, pacing the smuggler's loft above the tannery where Serah had hidden us, her boots wearing a groove into floorboards older than either of us. "There's a difference between brave and stupid," she'd said, "and I've spent thirty years learning exactly where the line sits."

"And yet you're coming with me," I'd said.

"I'm not letting you walk into a crown's mouth alone." She had stopped pacing then, and something in her face went soft and terrible at once. "Besides. You're the only one who can read what's in his palm. If there's anything left in him to read."

So here is how it was done: we let ourselves be found.

We walked down the valley road at midmorning, no swords drawn — Beatrix had even surrendered hers at Serah's insistence, which cost her more than she let show; her hand kept drifting to her empty hip like a tongue to a missing tooth — and we made no effort at concealment. We carried the book openly in my satchel. We sang, quietly, the first line of a Ravensworth smuggling rhyme as we passed the first checkpoint, because Beatrix said the soldiers would need to *hear* us be dangerous before they could believe us caught.

They took us at the second checkpoint anyway, eight men in livery so pale it looked bleached, their spears leveled with hands that shook only slightly. Their faces were ordinary faces. That was the worst part, at first — how ordinary they were, these men serving a thing that ate kings. One of them had chapped lips and kind eyes, and when he bound my wrists he did it gently, murmuring an apology that might have been habit or might have been conscience.

"The verse-named ones," his captain said, looking at us the way a man looks at weather he doesn't understand. "The crown wants them brought living."

"Does the crown say why?" Beatrix asked, mild as milk.

"No one asks the crown why," the captain said, and crossed himself in a gesture I would come to understand was less piety than reflex — the way you flinch from a draft, or a door opening on its own.

* * *

The Pale Court earned its name honestly.

We were taken up through terraced orchards gone strange — every tree bearing blossom and fruit and bare branch simultaneously, seasons layered like pages pressed together — and then through gates of white stone worn smooth by hands, and into a palace that seemed built by someone who had once known joy and was now merely maintaining the memory of it. The halls were beautiful the way a portrait is beautiful: accurate, composed, and utterly still. No dust, but no warmth either. Servants moved along the walls with their eyes down, and none of them spoke, and when one dropped a tray of cups near us the crash rang down the corridor like a gunshot, and six heads turned toward it in perfect unison, and then all six turned back, in perfect unison, and I felt Beatrix's arm press against mine.

*Don't*, her elbow said. *Not yet.*

The throne room doors were oak and iron, tall enough for grief to walk through standing upright. Inside, light fell through high windows in long dusty columns, and the court assembled along the walls — courtiers, officers, a few clerics in grey — all arranged with the careful spacing of people who had learned, over generations, exactly how close one could stand to the throne without being noticed by it.

And on the throne, King Aldric.

I had imagined a monster. I want to confess that plainly too: I had pictured something twisted, crowned in shadow, a body bent around the thing inside it. Instead he was a man perhaps fifty years old, broad-shouldered, dressed simply for a king in dark wool, and he sat with his hands open on the arms of the throne like a man listening to music. He was handsome in a faded way, the way a river-carved cliff is handsome — shaped by long forces. His crown was iron, old work, plain, without a single jewel, and it sat on his head not like ornament but like a lid.

His eyes were wrong. Not black, not red, nothing so theatrical. They were *patient*. They were the eyes of something that had been looking out of that face for a very long time and expected to look out of it for a great deal longer. When they moved, they moved smoothly, without any of the small hesitations living eyes have — the flickers of thought, of distraction, of a person deciding where to look next. These eyes already knew where everything was.

"Paloma Idris," the king said, and his voice was warm, resonant, a voice made for coronations. "And Beatrix Ravensworth. The last swordspeaker." He smiled, and the smile reached every muscle it should have, and reached nothing behind them. "You honor me. Sit. You must be weary from the road, and from running."

"We weren't running," Beatrix said. "We came to see you."

"Then you are braver than the last Ravensworth who sat where you stand." The head tilted, birdlike, and the patient eyes moved over Beatrix's hands, her empty hip, her face, cataloguing. "Your grandmother stood there once. Did she ever tell you? No. Of course not. She ran well. Your whole line runs well. It is almost a kind of loyalty, how faithfully you flee me."

Something in Beatrix went rigid beside me, and I put my hand over her bound wrists, and felt the tremor in her run down through the rope and into my palm.

"And you," Aldric said to me, "the mapmaker. Show me your maps. I am told they redraw themselves in my garden."

"They do," I said. My voice held. I was proud of that later, though pride seems obscene now. "Because your garden is made of memories, and memories move."

The smile widened a fraction. "Everything moves," the king said, "except me."

* * *

It happened during the questions. That is how these things go — salvation arrives sideways, while everyone is looking somewhere else.

Serah's testimony had prepared me for the mechanics: the crown-spirit speaks through him, wears him, spends him slowly across the centuries. But nothing had prepared me for the seams. We were kept kneeling before the throne while the spirit questioned us — about the book, about the garden, about the final smudged verse, probing each answer with the gentle relentless curiosity of something that has all the time in the world — and it was in a pause between questions, a silence of perhaps three heartbeats while the spirit considered whether I was lying about the burning pages (I was), that the king's right hand moved.

No one else saw it. The courtiers studied the walls. The guards studied the middle distance. Even the spirit's attention, I think, was turned inward, turning my lie over like a coin.

But I was watching his hands, because I am a cartographer, and a mapmaker learns early that the truth of a place is rarely in its monuments. It is in what drifts at the edges. Litter in gutters. Footpaths cutting across formal lawns. Hands, moving when no one commands them.

The right hand rose from the throne's arm, slow, effortful, like something swimming upstream against its own current, and turned palm-upward, and the fingers curled slightly inward, toward himself, toward the throne, hiding the palm from the room — from the spirit — offering it instead to the two of us kneeling below.

I leaned forward as if bowing deeper. Beatrix understood at once; I felt her shift her weight to shield the angle of his arm from the dais guards. And in the shadow our bodies made, I read his palm.

The writing was small. Childlike. The letters wobbled and crowded, drawn in ink that had gone brown with age — old ink, years-old ink, refreshed here and there in darker strokes, renewed again and again across some span of time I did not dare estimate. A message written by the last free hand of a drowning man, and rewritten whenever he could steal a moment of his own body, the way you'd retrace a fading map you couldn't bear to lose:

**END IT. PLEASE. I AM STILL IN HERE. THE GARDEN REMEMBERS ME. FIND WHAT IT TOOK FIRST.**

Below it, smaller, newer, almost illegible, pressed in with desperate fine strokes:

**MY NAME WAS ALDRIC.**

That was all. That was the whole testament of a king: four lines in a child's handwriting, hidden in the one place in the kingdom the tenant never bothered to read. Why would it? Who checks the walls of the house they own?

My eyes must have changed, because the hand closed, slowly, and returned to the arm of the throne, and the spirit's attention came back up out of its consideration like a fish rising, and the king's face resumed its warm attentive stillness.

"You have seen something," the crown said pleasantly.

"A fly," I lied, "on His Majesty's hand."

"There are no flies in the Pale Court." The patient eyes settled on me, and I felt them begin, very gently, to *look through* — sorting through me the way you'd sort through a drawer, looking for the shape of me, testing whether I would fit somewhere. "You are wondering if he can hear you," the spirit said, softer now, intimate, the voice dropping into something almost tender. "You are wondering if there is a man in here, trapped, screaming. Everyone wonders that, eventually. The priests wondered it. The queens wondered it — oh, especially the queens." Something moved behind the face, some expression assembling itself from borrowed parts. "Let me spare you the suspense, little mapmaker. There is nothing in here. Whatever he was, I have been wearing it so long it has become *me*. What you saw just now was an old habit. A scar twitching. Do you understand? Scars twitch. It does not mean the wound still lives."

"Yes," I said. "Of course."

I had learned lying from Beatrix, and Beatrix had learned it from smugglers, and the first rule is: agree quickly, agree boringly. Give the listener nothing interesting to keep.

But the spirit was not finished with me. It leaned forward on the throne, and the iron crown caught the window-light, and for one moment I saw past the composed surface of that face into something vast and starved underneath, something like a cellar seen through a keyhole — and it said, in Aldric's coronation-voice, with genuine warmth:

"You will stay a while. Both of you. As guests." A pause, delicate as a blade laid flat on silk. "Guests of the crown may not leave. It is an old law. I wrote it myself, some centuries ago, when I was younger." The smile returned, full and gracious and empty. "Take them to the west rooms. The ones with the good light."

* * *

The west rooms did have good light, and bars beyond the glass, and a door that locked with a sound like a knuckle cracking. When the guards had gone — the kind-eyed one last, lingering a half-second longer than protocol allowed, his gaze flicking to Beatrix's empty sword-belt and away — I sat down on the narrow bed and discovered my hands were shaking so badly I had to press them between my knees.

Beatrix crouched in front of me. Close, in the way she had begun to allow herself lately — knee touching knee, her forehead nearly resting on mine, a geography we had been mapping in stolen inches for days. "Tell me," she said quietly.

So I told her. All four lines. I had memorized them instantly, completely, the way I memorize coastlines — because some things you take into yourself whole or you lose them.

"*End it. Please. I am still in here.*"

Beatrix closed her eyes.

"*The garden remembers me. Find what it took first.*"

Her breath went ragged on that one. "*Find what it took first.*" She opened her eyes, and they were wet and furious and alive, more alive than anything in that palace, and I loved her so much in that moment it frightened me more than the throne room had.

"It lied," she said. "About him. About there being nothing left. It lied because—" She stopped. Started again, slower, working it aloud the way I work a difficult survey. "Because if it says he's gone, no one looks for him. But if he can still write — after all this time, Paloma, *how long* — then he isn't a husk. He's a prisoner. And prisoners can be freed."

"And freeing him kills him," I said. Because I had done the arithmetic already, in the throne room, with my heart hammering. Serah had been clear about what unbinding meant. "The spirit is woven through everything that's left of him. Pull the weed and the garden bed comes up with it."

"Maybe." Beatrix sat back on her heels. "Or maybe that's what it wants us to believe, too. Every story it tells serves it. 'He's gone.' 'Freeing him is death.' Convenient, both." She looked toward the barred window, at the strange orchards below, blossom and fruit and bone-bare branch all at once. "The garden remembers him, he wrote. Find what it took first."

"What did it take first?"

"That's your question," she said, and turned back to me, and there was something fierce and certain in her face that I wanted to fold up small and carry against my heart forever. "You're the one who reads gardens. You're the one whose maps grow. Somewhere under this palace, or in that valley, or in whatever passes for its roots — there's a first memory. The first thing it ate. The seed of the whole rotten harvest. And I think—" her voice dropped, roughened— "I think that's what he's begging us to find. Not the crown. The beginning of it. What the gardener-king buried before he became the thing that buries."

Outside, far off across the valley, a horn sounded — one note, then another, then a third, a pattern I didn't know but Beatrix clearly did, because she went still as a hare.

"That's the hunt call," she said softly. "They're sweeping the valley again. Looking for us. Except we're here." A thin, sharp smile. "Which means they're not hunting us at all. They're hunting something else."

We looked at each other, and the same thought landed in both of us at the same instant — I saw it arrive in her face the way weather arrives on a horizon.

The garden. Our garden. Our maps, our verses, our half-finished survey of the impossible. Left unattended in a valley that grew toward truths, while the thing that fed on memories sent its hounds into it.

"They'll burn it," I whispered.

"No," Beatrix said, rising, and though her sword was gone there was something in the way she straightened that made the empty room feel armed. "It won't burn what feeds it. But it will *harvest* it. Everything we've mapped. Every truth those plants grew from — ours included." She pulled me up off the bed, her grip hard and warm. "So we don't have days, Paloma. We have tonight. Maybe less."

Through the wall, faint as breath, I heard it: singing. Far below, deep beneath the foundations of the Pale Court, threading up through stone like water through limestone — a voice, or many voices, thin and sweet and terribly sad.

And among them, barely distinguishable, a single voice singing low and steady, keeping time with the rest, holding the melody like a man holding a rope:

King Aldric, still in there, still singing in the dark.
