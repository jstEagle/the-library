## 10. Escape by Old Roads

The cell smelled of wet stone and old incense, and Beatrix had been awake long enough to count every crack in the ceiling twice.

*Forty-one. Forty-two. The forty-third runs crooked, like a river that changed its mind.*

Paloma slept sitting up against the wall, her satchel clutched to her chest like a child holds a cat it refuses to let go of. Even in sleep her fingers twitched, tracing lines — maps, always maps, drawn on the inside of her own eyelids. Beatrix had watched her do it for hours and found it steadying in a way she couldn't explain. In a palace where the king's shadow moved a half-second behind his body, where soldiers wore faces borrowed from the dead, Paloma's small compulsions were the most honest thing in the building.

The torch in the corridor guttered.

Beatrix went still. Torches guttered all the time — draft, damp, nothing. But this one guttered *rhythmically*. Three short, two long. Three short, two long.

Her grandmother's rhyme surfaced before she could stop it, rising from wherever old songs live when they're not being sung:

*Three short, two long, the watch is wrong,*
*the pale court sleeps but not for long.*

She was on her feet before she'd decided to stand.

"Paloma." Her voice came out low and rough. "Wake up. Someone's at the door."

Paloma's eyes opened instantly — no grogginess, no confusion, just that cartographer's clarity, as if she'd never fully left the surface of things. She took in Beatrix's posture, the torchlight, the angle of the door, and said nothing, which was its own kind of fluency.

The lock turned. Not forced. *Turned*, with the patient precision of someone who knew exactly what kind of lock it was.

Beatrix put herself between the door and Paloma without thinking about it, hand closing on empty air where her sword should have been. They'd taken it in the audience chamber. She still felt the absence like a missing tooth, her tongue going to the gap again and again.

The door swung open on a woman in palace livery, grey-cloaked, tall, with a shield-sigil worked into the shoulder of her cloak in thread so dark it was nearly invisible. Her face was the kind that gave away nothing for free. She carried no lantern. She didn't need one; she moved through the dark like it was furniture she'd arranged herself.

"You're the two named in the verse," the woman said. It wasn't a question.

"And you're the one who put us in it," Beatrix answered. "Serah. Aldric's sworn shield."

Something flickered across Serah's face at the name — not surprise. Recognition of being *known*, and the cost of it.

"Then you know I don't have time to be insulted," Serah said. "The crown knows you're awake. It's deciding whether to wear you or break you. I'd rather we weren't here when it finishes deciding." She stepped back from the doorway. "Come. Or stay. But choose quickly."

* * *

They went up through the palace the way water goes through stone — finding the cracks, following them down and sideways, never up when up was watched.

Serah led. Beatrix mapped. That was the division of labor within the first hundred steps, unspoken: Serah knew the palace as a soldier knows a fortress, all guard rotations and sightlines, but Beatrix knew it as her grandmother had known it, sixty years before the stones were laid in their current arrangement — because palaces are built on the bones of older buildings, and smugglers build their roads in the bones.

At the first junction, where the corridor split toward the servants' stair and the great gallery, Serah turned right. Beatrix caught her sleeve.

"No. Left."

"The gallery is faster."

"The gallery has eyes in the tapestries. My grandmother's rhymes say—" and here Beatrix heard herself, heard how mad it sounded, singing smuggler verses in a living king's palace, "—*where the weaver hung her lie, walk beneath and wave goodbye.* There's a passage under the gallery floor. There has to be. This wing was built over the old Thornfell keep."

Serah looked at her for three full seconds. Then she knelt, pressed her palm flat against the flagstone, and slid it until the stone clicked — a seam so fine Beatrix hadn't seen it, and she'd been looking.

"Aldric showed me that when we were young," Serah said quietly. "He found it the same way you did. From stories." She stood. "Left, then."

They descended beneath the gallery through a passage barely shoulder-width, air tasting of dust and extinguished centuries. Above them, muffled by stone, they could hear the gallery guards walking their rounds — and once, horribly, a voice humming a tune Beatrix almost recognized, sung in a register no human throat should hold.

Paloma's hand found hers in the dark. Neither of them commented on it. Both of them held on.

* * *

The rhymes came easier the deeper they went, as if the palace itself remembered being smuggled through.

Beatrix sang them under her breath — barely more than breath, really, a thread of sound to walk by:

*Under the hall where the banners hang dry,*
*kiss the cold stone and count to five,*
*the road remembers what the roof forgets,*
*and the roof forgets everything alive.*

And each time she sang, something answered. A seam would show itself. A draft would shift, pointing like a finger. The old roads of Thornfell had been sung into existence, her grandmother used to say — cut by smugglers who marked their ways in verse because verse lives in the mouth, and mouths can't be confiscated.

At a collapsed archway, blocked by rubble that looked impassable, Beatrix stopped and sang the third verse of the *Hollow Stair*:

*When the way is shut, ask the wall what it swallowed.*
*Everything a palace tears down, it keeps.*
*Crawl where the grief went in, and come out where the grief comes out.*

Paloma, holding the book — the prophecy, its pages now burned back past the middle, the remaining verses flickering like a dying fire — traced the rubble with her fingers and said, "There. Behind the fallen keystone. The mortar's newer than the stone. Someone bricked this up from the other side, recently."

"Recently?" Serah's voice sharpened. "How recently?"

"Within the last year, I'd judge. The lime hasn't cured." Paloma paused. "Someone else has been using these passages. Recently enough to seal them behind themselves."

Beatrix looked at Serah. Serah looked at the wall.

"It wasn't me," Serah said. "I've known about the old roads for years. I've walked maybe six of them. But I sealed none."

"Then who?" Paloma asked.

And Beatrix, with a cold certainty settling into her stomach like swallowed river-stone, said: "Aldric. Before he was too far gone. He was sealing them *behind himself* — keeping something out. Or keeping something in."

No one answered that. Serah broke the mortar with her dagger, quietly, and they crawled through where the grief had gone in.

* * *

They lost the sword in the armory passage, though "lost" was generous. It was taken from them again — or rather, it left.

They had crossed a long low vault stacked with ceremonial arms, pikes and halberds racked like winter trees, when Beatrix felt it: a hum against her hip that wasn't there, because the sword wasn't there, and yet she felt it anyway, the way an amputee feels weather in a missing limb.

She stopped. "It's here."

"What's here?" Serah asked, hand going to her own blade.

"My sword. The one they took." Beatrix turned slowly. "It's in this room."

They found it in the deepest rack, set apart from the others, wrapped in oilcloth — and unwrapped, lying on a stone shelf as if placed there deliberately rather than stored. Around it, the air was wrong. Still. The dust on the shelf lay undisturbed in a perfect circle around the blade, as if even the dust declined to touch it.

"It woke," Beatrix whispered. "In chapter— in the hedge-witch's hut, it woke when I refused the oath. And they took it anyway. And it's been *waiting*."

"Don't touch it," Serah said.

"I have to. It's mine."

"It's *marked*. Look at the shelf."

Beatrix looked. Beneath the sword, scratched into the stone in a spidery, hurried hand, was writing. Words. A message, or a warning, or both:

*It sings to whoever holds it. It sang to me for thirty years. Leave it for the ones the verse names. They'll need it more than I ever did. — A.*

Aldric. Aldric's handwriting. Aldric, years ago, hiding the sword for them — for two people who wouldn't be born for another century, or who had simply not yet arrived, depending on how you counted.

"He knew," Beatrix breathed. "Some part of him knew, all along. He's been leaving us supplies like a man provisioning a rescue he won't live to see."

She reached for the sword.

The moment her fingers closed on the grip, the hum became a wordless chord, and the chord became — almost — language. Not speech. *Recognition.* The feeling of a door opening in a house you thought was empty.

And then the vault doors slammed open above them, and light poured down, and the crown had finished deciding.

* * *

What poured down the stairs was not soldiers. It wore soldiers' bodies, moved them competently enough, but the faces were wrong in the specific way Beatrix had learned to dread in the audience chamber: they were faces she knew. Her mother's face, on a man twice her mother's age. Her sister's face, younger than her sister had ever been allowed to become. Paloma's face, on a woman who smiled at Paloma with Paloma's own smile, slightly out of sync, like an echo arriving before the shout.

"They're wearing the loved," Beatrix snarled, drawing the sword at last. It came alive in her grip like a struck bell. "Don't look at the faces. Look at the hands. The hands don't know how to be the people."

She fought the way her grandmother's stories had taught her to fight — not like a duelist, like a *door*. Every stroke closed a line of approach. Every step gave ground only where ground was cheap. And under it all, under the clang and the blood-hot effort, she sang, because the sword wanted the songs, and the songs made the old roads answer:

*Run, run, the pale court wakes,*
*it wears your mother's face for sake*
*of one more mile, one more breath —*
*sing the road ahead of death.*

A section of shelving toppled at her verse. A hidden door she hadn't known was there sighed open behind Paloma. The old roads were listening. The old roads were *helping*.

But there were too many, and the faces kept coming, and Beatrix felt the moment turn — felt the fight tip from fierce to fatal —

— and then Serah moved.

Not against Beatrix. Against the soldiers. Serah drew her own blade and cut down the one wearing Beatrix's sister's face with a single economical stroke, and then planted herself in the doorway, shield up — the actual shield, the sworn-shield's shield, bearing the arms of House Aldric — and said, in a voice that carried the weight of twenty years of standing next to a hollowing man:

"*I am the shield of the king. I decide what reaches him. And I decide what leaves.*"

The soldiers stopped. All of them. At once. As if a hand had lifted from a puppet-strings and hesitated.

Because she was right. However wrong the crown was, however deep its rot, the oath Serah carried was older than its tenancy — and oaths, Beatrix was learning, are the one currency the crown cannot counterfeit.

For three heartbeats, the corridor hung balanced on Serah's vow.

Then the nearest soldier — wearing the face of a boy Serah had clearly once trained, a boy whose name she said aloud, half-swallowed, "*Tomas*" — lunged, and the balance broke, and they ran.

* * *

They ran through the bowels of the palace while Serah covered the rear, and Beatrix learned two things about the sworn shield in the space of a hundred stairs.

First: Serah fought like a woman who had been rehearsing betrayal for years and had finally been given permission. There was no hesitation in her, no grief spent mid-motion. Grief could wait. Grief could wait forever if it had to.

Second: Serah was crying. Silently, steadily, without changing her breathing, tears cutting clean lines through the grime on her face while her blade did its work. She wept the way Beatrix's grandmother had described the mountain weeping snowmelt — not because it wanted to, but because the alternative was worse.

They burst out through a postern gate into the night side of the mountain, into wind and starlight and the smell of pine, and Serah barred the gate behind them with an iron bar she'd prepared — *prepared*, meaning she'd planned this escape for years too, meaning she'd stood in that palace beside a disappearing king and built exits the whole time, hoping, hoping, hoping.

Then she leaned against the wall and let herself shake.

Beatrix stood with the sword humming gently in her hand, watching the strongest person she'd met in either century come apart, and understood suddenly, completely, what it had cost Serah to fight men wearing the faces of children she'd trained. What it had cost her to spend twenty years watching a good man get eaten slowly, publicly, politely, while everyone called it kingship.

"He asked me once," Serah said, to the wall, to the wind, to no one. "Years ago. Early. He still had most of himself then. He took my hands and said, 'If I ever stop asking questions, Serah — if I ever stop being curious about anything, even the weather — find whatever's left and help it out.' And I told him I would. And then for fifteen years I watched him stop asking questions, one at a time, like lamps going out in a house." She wiped her face with the heel of her hand, efficient, furious. "Tonight he stopped asking about the weather."

Paloma crouched beside her. She didn't offer comfort in words. She offered it the way she offered everything — precisely. She opened her satchel and showed Serah the map she'd been drafting since the audience chamber: the garden below, the valley, the palace, and now, in fresh ink, the route they'd just escaped by, rendered in Paloma's sure hand.

"We need somewhere the crown can't follow," Paloma said. "Somewhere off its maps."

"There's nowhere off its maps," Serah said. "It *made* the maps."

"Not mine," Paloma said, and there was something in her voice Beatrix had heard only once before — in the garden, when the memory-plants had turned toward her like flowers toward sun. "Mine redraw themselves."

Serah stared at the map. At the ink, which was, even now, shifting — the escape route glowing faintly, the garden's borders creeping outward like something growing in fast-forward, and at the garden's heart, a new mark appearing letter by letter, in handwriting none of them recognized:

*Aldric was here. Aldric is still here. Come to the root.*

Beatrix felt the sword hum against her palm, felt the old roads humming under the mountain, felt her grandmother's rhymes stirring in her throat like birds before a storm.

Above them, far up the palace rock, a bell began to ring — not the alarm bell, but the *other* one, the deep one, the one her grandmother's songs said was rung only when the pale court wanted something back.

*Three short, two long,* Beatrix thought. *The watch is wrong. The pale court sleeps but not for long.*

She looked at Serah. "Can you keep up?"

Serah straightened, shouldered her shield, and for the first time all night, smiled — a small, hard, sunrise-through-cracks smile.

"I've been waiting twenty years for someone to ask me that," she said. "Lead on, swordspeaker. Sing us the road."
