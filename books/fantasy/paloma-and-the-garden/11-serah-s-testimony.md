## 11. Serah's Testimony

The safehouse was a smuggler's cache dressed up as a shepherd's hut, and Paloma loved it immediately in the way she loved all places that lied well about themselves. From the outside: a low stone roof furred with moss, a door that hung crooked on its hinges, a smell of wet wool and old smoke. Inside, the walls were lined with false stones that swung open onto shelves of tinned peaches, wax-sealed maps, and — Beatrix had explained with something like reverence — the best tobacco between here and the coast. The single window looked out over a fold of the valley where the sheep were not sheep but charms, grazing on the hillside grass to make the land itself forget there was anything worth hiding.

They had been in hiding for two days. Serah had brought them here through the dark with the sure-footed silence of a woman who had spent twenty years learning exactly which floorboards in a palace sang.

Now Serah sat across the hearth from them, her armor off for the first time since they'd met her. Without it she looked younger and more tired, a big-boned woman with grey coming in at her temples and a sword-callus on her right palm that matched Beatrix's own. She was cleaning her gauntlets with an oiled rag, slow circular motions, and she had been silent so long that Paloma had begun counting the ticks of the kettle against the crackle of the fire just to keep from screaming.

It was Beatrix who finally broke. "You said you suspected. You said *years*. So say it now. All of it."

Serah set down the gauntlet. She looked at it a moment as if it had asked her something.

"You want the history," she said. "Fine. But I'll tell it the way it happened, not the way the courtiers tell it. The way the courtiers tell it, every king is chosen by the crown because he is worthy. That's backwards." She fed a split log to the fire. "The crown chooses first. Then it makes him worthy."

Paloma felt the hair rise along her arms. "Makes him how?"

"Kind," Serah said, and the word came out like something spat. "That's the horror of it, cartographer. It makes him kind."

* * *

She told it in pieces, the way people tell things they have carried too long — setting each one down carefully, afraid of what noise it would make.

The crown had come to Thornfell four generations ago, in the reign of King Aldric's great-grandfather, a man named Osric who had been, by every account Serah had dug out of sealed archives and dying servants' memories, a tyrant of the small and petty sort. A tax-fixer. A grudge-keeper. A king who had his brother's lands seized over a card game.

"The winter Osric took the crown," Serah said, "he pardoned nine hundred debtors. Opened the granaries. Wept publicly at the execution of a man he'd signed the order for himself, three days prior. The ballads say Thornfell wept with him." Her mouth twisted. "The ballads don't say that by spring, Osric couldn't remember his brother's face. Not the grief of forgetting — the *fact* of it. He'd look at portraits and ask who the man was. By summer he couldn't remember why he'd ever hated anyone, because he couldn't remember hating. He'd sit through council meetings smiling like a saint and afterward he couldn't tell you one thing that had been said. He died gentle, beloved, and hollow as a gourd, and the crown passed to his son, who was already kinder than his father had ever been, and so the crown had less work to do."

"And it went on," Beatrix said quietly. "Each reign."

"Each reign. Kinder in public. Emptier in private. Aldric's father used to lock himself in his rooms for days. The servants thought it was melancholy. It wasn't melancholy. It was *hunger* — no, worse, it was the absence of hunger, the absence of everything, and him sitting in the middle of it trying to remember what his own voice sounded like." Serah's hands had gone still on the rag. "I served his son. I've served Aldric since he was nine years old. I watched it happen to him the way you watch a tide come in. Slow enough to doubt. Fast enough to drown."

"What does it take?" Paloma asked. "Exactly. What does it eat?"

Serah looked at her then, really looked, and Paloma understood that the question was the right one and the wrong one both.

"Everything that makes a self," Serah said. "Grudges. Loves. Fears. The memory of your mother's hands. The reason you flinch at thunder. It takes the sharp edges first — the things that make you difficult, particular, *yours* — because those are the densest food. What's left is smoothness. Agreeableness. A king who never refuses anyone, because refusing requires wanting something, and wanting has been eaten." She paused. "He signs whatever's put in front of him now. Did you wonder why a kingdom this old has no bad laws? No cruelties on the books? It isn't wisdom. It's that the man under the crown can't hold an opinion long enough to have one."

The fire popped. Somewhere outside, one of the charm-sheep bleated, a sound like a hinge.

"He fights it," Serah said, softer now. "I need you to understand that. Whatever else you think of him — he fights it. There are mornings I find him with his fists pressed into his eyes, saying his own name over and over like a prayer, trying to hold on to it. He keeps lists. Little things. *My dog was called Bristle. I hate the taste of cloves. My mother sang flat.* He hides the lists in his boots. And every few weeks a list goes missing, and a week after that he's forgotten the thing on it, and he writes me these letters—" her voice caught, once, and steadied— "these letters apologizing in advance for the man he's going to become."

Paloma found she could not speak. Beside her, Beatrix had gone very still, the stillness of someone holding a blade flat so it doesn't sing.

* * *

Later — after the kettle had boiled and gone cold untouched — Paloma sat by the window with her satchel open on her knees, and it was there, in the ordinary ritual of checking her maps, that the true shape of it came down on her.

Her maps had been redrawing themselves for days. She had stopped fighting it sometime during the flight from the palace; the garden insisted on being mapped, and her ink obeyed. But tonight, unrolling the newest sheet by candlelight, she saw it plainly for the first time.

Every plant in the garden was marked now. Every one. And beside each mark, in her own handwriting though she had never written it, was a name.

Not the plants' names. The names of *people*.

There was a row of white flowers that bloomed in spirals, and beside them: *Osric, who forgot his brother.* A stand of blue-flushed grass near the stream: *Aldric's father, who sat in the dark.* A climbing vine heavy with pale fruit: *the queen who smiled at festivals and could not recall her children's birthdays.*

And everywhere, threaded through all of it, marks without names yet — dozens of them, hundreds, little blank spaces in her own hand waiting to be filled.

"Oh," Paloma said. It came out very small.

Beatrix looked up from sharpening her knife. "What?"

"It eats identity." Paloma's finger traced the garden's outline on the map, the whole impossible valley of it, blooming out of season below the mountain's crown. "You said it yourself — it takes the sharp edges, the particular things, the *self*. And it has to put them somewhere. Nothing is destroyed, Beatrix. Nothing is ever destroyed. It's *stored*."

She looked up, and met Beatrix's eyes, and saw the same understanding arrive in her like a cold wind.

"The garden," Paloma whispered. "The garden grows toward truths people refuse to say. It grows from buried memory. I thought it was some old magic, some sanctuary. It's not a sanctuary." Her hand was shaking now, and she pressed it flat against the map to steady it. "It's a *larder*. Every plant in that valley is a meal the crown finished eating. Every flower is somebody's self, planted where it couldn't be reached. That's why it knows me. That's why my ancestor vanished into it — she didn't get lost, she *saw* it, she saw what it was, and—" 

She stopped. Because another thing had arrived, arriving the way the worst things do, quietly and all at once.

The blank spaces. The hundreds of unnamed marks.

"Beatrix. The garden is bigger than four kings' worth of meals." Her voice sounded far away to her own ears. "Four reigns, even long ones — that's decades of eating. But the garden goes back further. The verses go back further. Which means the crown was feeding before it ever reached the throne room. Or—" and here her cartographer's mind laid the lines down, cool and precise even as her stomach turned— "or the gardener-king in the oldest verse, the one who wouldn't die — he wasn't the crown's first victim. He was its first *keeper*. He's been planting for centuries. Tending the larder. Growing the garden out of everyone the spirit has ever eaten, and the spirit has been eating since before your family ever swore an oath."

The hut was very quiet. Outside, the false stars wheeled in their wrong arrangements, and somewhere down the valley the garden went on growing in the dark, patient as appetite.

"So it's not a haunting," Beatrix said at last. Her voice was rough. "It's a *farm*."

"And we're standing in it," Paloma said. "And it already knows our names."

She looked back down at the map, at the newest marks that had appeared while she slept — two fresh sprigs sketched at the garden's western edge, drawn in her own hand, their labels left deliberately, mercifully, blank.

But she knew. Looking at the shape of the leaves, the angle of the stems, she knew whose selves those seedlings were grown from. The garden had started them the night she and Beatrix fell through the book.

It was saving their names for last.

* * *

Serah stood at the hearth, watching them, and when she spoke her voice had the flat calm of a woman who has decided something irrevocable.

"There's one more thing. About the lists Aldric hides in his boots." She turned the gauntlet over in her hands. "Last month I found one he didn't write. A child's scrawl, cramped into the margin — *it watches when I sleep, it wears my smile, dig the garden, dig it up, burn it* — and beneath that, in the same hand: *if anyone comes who can map what cannot be mapped, show them the west wall.*"

Paloma's blood went cold and hot at once. "The west wall of the garden?"

"The west wall of the *palace*," Serah said. "Where the oldest foundations are. Where the masons' records stop four hundred years ago and start again with no explanation." She looked at Paloma directly. "Aldric wrote that before he could have known you existed. Before either of you fell through any book. He wrote it years ago, in the last corner of himself the crown hadn't eaten, and he hid it where only I would find it."

She set the gauntlet down and straightened, and the firelight made her shadow tall against the stone.

"So. Cartographer. Swordspeaker. The king under the crown is asking for you — has been asking for years. The question is whether we move tonight, or whether we spend one more day learning what that garden is growing from *you*."

Outside, the charm-sheep had gone silent, all together, as if listening.
