## 12. The Verse That Wasn't Written

The safehouse was an old charcoal-burner's hut wedged into the shoulder of the mountain, the kind of place that smelled of cold ash and resin and had been forgotten by everyone except the smugglers who still used its chimney as a landmark. Beatrix had slept in it twice before, both times alone, both times with her boots on and her sword across her knees.

Tonight she sat with a book in her lap that was older than the mountain's grief, and she could not make herself open it again.

The fire was small — Serah had taught them to build fires that lied about their size, all heat and no light — and it threw just enough orange across the pages to show her what she'd already seen once and wished she hadn't: the final verse, the one that should have told them how to end this, smudged into a brown-gray blur like a bruise on the vellum.

Not water damage. She'd checked. Water damage ran in streaks; it feathered at the edges; it faded with the grain of the paper. This was different. This was *deliberate*. Someone had wet a thumb — or something worse than a thumb — and worked the ink while it was still fresh, grinding the words back into pulp before they could dry into meaning. And they had done it with care, because the surrounding text was pristine. Every other verse on the page stood sharp as cut slate. Only the last one had been murdered.

"Someone didn't want it read," she said aloud, mostly to hear a voice that wasn't the wind worrying the chimney.

Across the hut, Paloma looked up from her maps. Candlelight caught the ink stains on her fingers, the ones that never quite washed out anymore, as if the garden had gotten under her skin and decided to stay. "You've said that three times."

"It keeps being true."

Paloma set down her pen and came over, folding herself onto the bench beside Beatrix with the easy economy of motion she brought to everything — measuring, mapping, sitting close enough that their shoulders touched. Beatrix felt the touch like a struck match. Even now. Even with the world ending on a schedule.

"Show me again," Paloma said.

Beatrix turned the book toward her. The prophecy filled the last spread of the bound pages, verses marching down the left column in a hand that shifted subtly from line to line, as though several people had written it across years — or as though one person had written it across a lifetime and worn herself into different hands. The Ravensworth marks were there in the margins, tiny sigils drawn beside certain lines: a sword crossed with a root, a crown crossed out, a door standing open.

And beneath them all, the final verse. Or the grave of one.

*When the crown is named and the oath is spoken,* the legible part began, and then dissolved into that deliberate smear, and then, near the bottom, a few surviving words surfaced like corpses in a flooded river:

*—but know that breaking is not the same as keeping, and the price of the unbound—*

Nothing after. Just the smear, and below it, pressed so lightly it was almost not there at all, a single word in a different hand, smaller, shakier:

*Forgive.*

Paloma read it twice. Beatrix watched her lips move.

"That's not the author's writing," Paloma said quietly. "Is it?"

"No." Beatrix's mouth had gone dry somewhere around the third reading. "It's my grandmother's."

* * *

She had the letters with her. Of course she had the letters. She had carried them for eleven years, wrapped in oilcloth at the bottom of her pack, through every pass and every winter and every night she'd told herself she wasn't running from anything. Nine letters, tied with a bootlace because ribbon seemed too tender for what they contained. Her grandmother Maren Ravensworth had written them in the last two years of her life, when her hands had begun to fail and her mind had begun, in Beatrix's private opinion, to sharpen — the way old blades do, thinning down to the truth of themselves.

She untied the bootlace now, and Paloma watched her do it without asking questions, which was one of the ten thousand things Beatrix loved about her and would admit to approximately none of.

The fourth letter was the one. She knew before she opened it. She always knew, and she had never once let herself finish reading it since the night Maren died.

*Little wren,* it began, in handwriting that started strong and deteriorated by degrees, as if the woman writing it were walking downhill and picking up speed.

*I have not told you the whole of our inheritance, and I will not pretend that silence was kindness. It was cowardice wearing kindness's coat, and I am too old now to tell the difference even in myself.*

*You asked me once why there are no more swordspeakers. You were seven. I told you the oaths died out, that the world grew too small for them. That was a lie, and you deserved better, and here is the truth while my hand still obeys me.*

*There was one after me. My sister. Your great-aunt Elowen.*

*Elowen believed the oath itself was the trap. Not the crown, not the spirit — the oath. She said a binding strong enough to hold a monster is also strong enough to hold the one who speaks it, and that we had been feeding ourselves to the work for nine generations and calling it honor. She said the final verse showed another way. A way to break the binding instead of keep it. To speak the crown's name *unbound* — to say it as a free woman says a thing, without the oath's chains in her mouth.*

*She went to the garden to learn how.*

*I have written and burned six letters trying to describe what came back. I will try once more and then I will stop.*

*What came back wore Elowen's face. It walked like her. It answered to her name. But it spoke in the crown-spirit's cadence, little wren, and when it smiled, its shadow moved first. The spirit did not fight her at the throne. It let her carry its voice out of the valley in her own mouth. She thought she had broken the oath. What she had done was loosen the knot at the wrong end of the rope.*

*It took four swordspeakers — four of us, sworn and singing — to put it back in the crown, and the cost was such that the survivors agreed the order should end rather than risk another Elowen. I was the last who chose to walk away instead of die. Do not confuse the two. Walking away was its own kind of failure, and I have paid for it every year since in dreams I will not describe to a child.*

*The book you will someday find has a final verse. I made sure of what happened to it. I ground the words out with my own thumb while the ink was wet, because I could not destroy the page without destroying the prophecy entire, and the prophecy must survive — but the verse must never be read by someone desperate enough to try it.*

*If you are reading this, little wren, then you have found the book, and you are angry with me, and you are right to be. Here is all I can give you: the verse was not censored because it was forbidden. It was censored because it was unfinished. Elowen died — truly died, the second time, the real death — before she could write down what she learned in the garden about speaking free. What remains in that smear is not wisdom. It is a wound.*

*Do not go looking for the missing words in the garden. The garden remembers everything, and some things remember back.*

*Burn this letter if it frightens you. I will love you either way.*

*Maren*

Beatrix finished it. For the first time in eleven years, she read it all the way to the end, including the parts she had always skipped — the last paragraph, where the handwriting collapsed entirely into a scrawl, and beneath the signature, in letters barely controlled enough to be called writing:

*P.S. — She begged me to stop her, at the end. When it was wearing her. She was still in there, somewhere underneath, and she found enough of herself to beg. I have never told anyone that. I am telling you because you will need to know it someday: whatever the crown takes, it does not take everything. There is always a room inside the person that stays theirs. Find the room.*

Beatrix set the letter down very carefully, the way you set down something that might go off.

The fire had burned low. Outside, the wind had changed direction, coming up from the valley now instead of down from the peaks, and it carried a smell that did not belong to any season — green and sweet and faintly mineral, like water moving over roots in the dark. The garden, breathing. The garden, which remembered everything, and some things remembered back.

Paloma had read over her shoulder. Beatrix hadn't heard her move closer, but she was there, warm along Beatrix's whole arm, and her hand came to rest flat on the letter as if to hold it — or hold Beatrix — steady against the table.

"Your grandmother erased it," Paloma said. "To protect whoever came next."

"She erased it to protect *me.*" Beatrix's voice came out flatter than she intended. "There was only ever going to be a next. She knew it. The prophecy names the Ravensworth line, Paloma. It's been waiting a hundred years for a Ravensworth stupid enough to open the cover." She laughed, and hated the sound of it. "And here I am. With my girlfriend. On what was supposed to be a date."

"The date was going well," Paloma said. "Before the book."

"The date was going *very* well."

"I know. I was there." But there was no smile in it. Paloma's eyes had gone distant, cartographer's eyes, the look she got when she was holding a landscape in her head and turning it over, looking for the route through. "Elowen tried to speak the name unbound. Free. Without the oath."

"And it hollowed her out and wore her home like a coat."

"But your grandmother said the verse was *unfinished.* Not wrong. Unfinished." Paloma tapped the letter, once, gently. "Maren censored a wound, not a lie. Which means the idea might still be sound. An oath is a chain, Beatrix. You've said so yourself. A hundred times. You renounced yours because it asked you to feed yourself to the work."

"Don't." Beatrix pulled her arm back. The loss of the warmth was immediate and stupid. "Don't take my anger and make it a battle plan."

"I'm not—"

"You are. Everyone does. Mother did. The hedge-witch did. The *sword* did." She stood, because sitting still had become impossible, and paced the length of the hut — four steps, turn, four steps — the way she had paced every cell and campsite of her adult life. "Elowen was right about one thing, maybe. The oath *is* a trap. But the answer isn't to slip the chain. Look what slipping it cost. Four dead swordspeakers. A great-aunt turned into a puppet. My grandmother's whole life spent grinding ink out of a page with her thumb." She stopped at the window, at the black square of it, at the smell of the garden rising. "And the answer isn't to keep the chain either, because the chain leads to the throne room and the throne room leads to Aldric's empty face and Serah bleeding in the dirt. So what's left? What's the third thing, Paloma? Because I have read every letter that woman wrote me and there is no third thing in any of them."

Paloma was quiet for a long moment. When she spoke, her voice was careful, the way it was careful when she was charting a river she couldn't see the bottom of.

"There's one person who might know what Elowen learned. Someone who was there."

Beatrix turned. "Serah's not old enough—"

"Not Serah." Paloma's gaze dropped to the book, to the smear, to the word *forgive* in Maren's shaking hand. "The garden. Your grandmother said it remembers everything. Elowen went to the garden to learn the verse. If the garden grows from buried memory—" She hesitated, and Beatrix saw her weigh something, saw her decide to say it anyway, which was the most frightening thing Paloma ever did. "Then somewhere in it, there may be a plant grown from *Elowen's* last night. From what she understood, right at the end, in the room inside her that stayed hers."

The wind gusted against the shutters. Down in the valley, impossibly far below, something bloomed.

Beatrix looked at her hands. At the old sword-marks on her palms, pale as scars, the inheritance she'd spent eleven years pretending was a birthmark. Her mother had refused the oath and died for the refusal — not by the crown's hand, but by the slow rot of a life unlived, a woman who locked her doors against her own bloodline until the bloodline starved. Elowen had seized the oath and shattered it and been shattered in return. Maren had walked away and paid in dreams.

Three generations. Three ways to lose.

"And if the garden shows me Elowen's ending," Beatrix said slowly, "and it's as bad as the letter says — what then? We'll have days. Maybe hours. The book is burning from the back forward, Paloma. Time is eating our map."

"Then we'll know the shape of the mistake," Paloma said. "I'm a cartographer, Beatrix. Half my trade is knowing where other people fell in." She rose and crossed to her, and took her hands — took the marked palms, held them open between them like a map neither of them had finished drawing. "But I'm not asking you to decide tonight. I'm telling you there's a way to find the missing words, and it doesn't require you to break anything, or keep anything, or become anyone you're not."

Beatrix looked down at their joined hands. The candle guttered. Somewhere below them, patient as winter, the garden waited with everything it remembered — including, she understood suddenly, with a cold that started in her spine and spread outward, things about *her* that she had buried deeper than any verse.

Things she had never said aloud. Things the garden grew toward.

"Tomorrow," she said. "We go down at first light."

Paloma nodded. But as they banked the fire and lay down side by side on the charcoal-burner's narrow cot, Beatrix lay awake listening to the wind, and to the letter rustling on the table where the draft kept stirring it, turning it, as if the dead woman's hand were still trying to point at something.

And in the last hour before dawn, half-dreaming, she heard it — faint, beneath the wind, in a cadence she recognized from a dying witch's stories and a hollow king's court and her own grandmother's worst nights:

Someone, far below in the flowering dark, practicing how to speak free.
