## 13. Roots and Reckonings

The garden had changed its mind about the season again.

Paloma noticed it first as a sound — a soft, wet rustling high overhead, like paper turning in a room where no wind reached. She looked up from her drafting board, which was really a flat stone worn smooth by a century of hands she would never meet, and saw that the rowan trees along the eastern wall had begun to blossom while their leaves still burned orange. Blossom and autumn together. The garden did this when it was thinking. She had learned its moods the way she learned coastlines: patiently, by returning to them until they confessed their shapes.

She had been mapping for eleven days now, though time moved strangely here and she could not have sworn to it. Her satchel lay open beside her, and inside it her maps were redrawing themselves in slow, deliberate strokes — ink crawling across vellum like something waking. The original maps, her great-great-aunt Odile's maps, had done the same thing once, if the marginalia were to be believed. *The garden grows toward truths people refuse to say.* Paloma had written that sentence at the top of her working sheet three days ago and had not yet understood it. She thought she might understand it tonight.

Because tonight the garden had grown something new.

It stood at the edge of the memory-plots, where the soil went dark and soft as turned cake, apart from the rows of ordinary impossible things — the lavender that bloomed with someone's wedding day, the fig tree heavy with a dead man's childhood summers. This plant was young, barely knee-high, with a single stem the color of old iron and leaves like folded letters. And it was turned toward her. Not toward the sun — there was no sun here, only a sky like frosted glass — but toward her, the way a dog turns toward the person holding its lead.

Paloma set down her pen.

"No," she said aloud, to the garden in general. "I know what you're doing."

The stem swayed, gently, as if in disagreement.

She had been avoiding this plot for two days. She told herself it was because the light was bad for surveying there, which was a lie so transparent that even her ink seemed embarrassed by it. The truth was simpler. Two evenings ago, walking the perimeter with her compass, she had felt the ground beneath that spot pull at her attention like a hand tugging a sleeve, and she had looked down and seen the first pale shoot breaking the soil, and she had known — the way cartographers know when a river is about to change course, before any visible sign — whose memory was rising there.

Beatrix's.

* * *

She found Beatrix at the far side of the valley, at the boundary stones, running drills with the sword that had woken without her permission. That was how Beatrix dealt with things she couldn't face: she faced them with steel instead. She moved through the forms her grandmother's rhymes described — *heel and hollow, wrist and wall* — and the sword hummed along her palm-lines in answer, and her mouth stayed set in the hard line of a woman refusing conversation with herself.

Paloma watched her for a long moment from the path. There was a version of tenderness that would have let this continue. There was a version of love — if it was love; eleven days and one impossible prophecy seemed a thin foundation for the word, and yet the word sat in her chest like a settled fact, patient and immovable as bedrock — that wouldn't.

"Beatrix."

The sword stilled. Beatrix didn't turn around. "If Serah's sent you to tell me we're moving camp, tell her I'll—"

"It's not Serah." Paloma came down the slope, boots sinking into moss that gave slightly, like flesh. "There's something growing in the east plot. It's yours."

That got her to turn. Beatrix's eyes went first to Paloma's face, then past her shoulder, toward the dark line of trees, and Paloma watched the color leave her cheeks in stages, like water draining.

"You don't know that," Beatrix said.

"I know what the garden does. It grows toward truths people refuse to say. You've spent—" Paloma counted, not to be cruel but because precision was the only kindness she trusted herself to offer, "—eleven days not saying anything about yourself. Not your mother. Not the oath. Not why you left Thornfell's roads for a life of guarding smugglers who never thank you. The garden has been very patient. Gardens are."

"Don't." Beatrix's voice cracked on the single syllable, and she hated it, Paloma could see her hating it, jaw tightening against the fracture. "Whatever's growing down there, leave it buried. Some roots should stay under the ground."

"The crown-spirit doesn't think so," Paloma said quietly. "It's made a meal of buried things for a hundred years. Every person it emptied — everything they refused to say, everything they couldn't bear to remember — that's what fed it. That's what these plants *are*, Beatrix. Leftovers. Scraps of self that something else swallowed." She gestured back toward the east plot. "I think the garden grew yours because it wants us to understand the enemy. Or because it wants to give you back something that was taken. Or both. I don't know. But I do know that we march on the Pale Court soon, and you're going to stand in front of a thing that eats identity, and speak an oath you've spent your whole life running from. And I'd rather you met whatever's in that plot here, with me, than let it ambush you in front of the throne."

Beatrix said nothing for a long time. Somewhere above them, the rowans turned their blossoms like faces.

"My sister," Beatrix finally said, and stopped, as though the word were a door she'd opened onto a long drop. She closed it again. "Not here. Not standing up."

* * *

They went at dusk, or what passed for dusk — the frosted sky dimming to the color of pewter, the memory-plots glowing faintly with their own borrowed light, each plant lit from within by whatever moment it held. Paloma walked ahead, and Beatrix followed like a woman being led to a scaffold she had chosen herself, chin up, hands loose at her sides, all her competence arranged around her like armor that no longer fit.

The plant had grown while they walked. It stood waist-high now, iron-stemmed, and where its flowers should have been there hung buds shaped exactly like sealed envelopes, wax-pale, trembling faintly.

"How does it work?" Beatrix asked. Her voice was flat. Cartographer-flat, Paloma thought; the voice people used when they needed terrain to hold still.

"I'm not certain. With the others, I touch a leaf and I see — I *feel* — the memory it grew from. Not my own. The gardener's. Whoever planted it, whoever buried the truth here first." Paloma hesitated. "This one is different. This one grew overnight, out of season, pointed at me. I think—" She made herself say it. "I think it grew from *your* refusal. The truth you refuse to say. And I think it's showing itself to me because I'm the one who has to decide whether it gets spoken."

"Decided," Beatrix said. "No."

"Then it stays, and grows, and feeds nothing, and teaches us nothing, and you walk into that throne room carrying it like shrapnel."

"Paloma."

"Beatrix." Paloma stepped closer, close enough to see that Beatrix's hands had gone white-knuckled at her sides. "I bought you a book to impress you. Did I ever tell you why? Because you talked about Thornfell the way other people talk about a person they've lost. You knew every pass and every rhyme and every grave, and you never once said *why*. I fell for a map of you that you'd drawn to hide the territory. I'm a cartographer. I hate incomplete maps." Her voice gentled. "And I love you, which means I want the whole chart. Even the parts marked 'here be dragons.' Especially those."

The buds trembled harder. One of them split along its seam with a sound like a sigh.

Beatrix stared at it. Then, without looking away, she said, "Her name was Wren."

* * *

The plant opened for her voice the way a lock opens for the right key.

Paloma touched a leaf — iron-cool, faintly warm at the heart, like a blade that had been held too long — and the garden fell away, and she was *there*.

Rain. A mountain road she recognized from Beatrix's grandmother's rhymes, the Switchback above Hollowmere, slick black stone and fog like wet wool. Two figures: a younger Beatrix, twenty maybe, sword at her hip and oath-marks still bright on her hands, silver as new coins. And beside her a slighter girl with rain-frizzed dark hair and Beatrix's stubborn chin — Wren — laughing, actually laughing, pulling a hood up against the weather.

"They'll be waiting at the ford," Wren was saying. "Uncle Tomas's boys, running salt wine through the low pass. Bea, come *on*. One last run together before you go be solemn forever."

"I can't." Young Beatrix's voice was raw. "I spoke the renunciation this morning, Wren. In front of the whole chapter-house. I'm not a swordspeaker anymore. If I walk that road with you and something happens, I can't — the oath was the only thing that ever kept anyone safe on those roads. My word used to *bend the mountain*. Now I'm just a girl with a sword."

"You're just my sister," Wren said, "with a sword, and I've never needed more than that." She grinned. "Stay at the waystation. Have a drink with Mother's old friends. I'll be back by midnight with wine and stories."

Paloma felt it then — felt it through young Beatrix's skin, because the memory was hers and Paloma was only the guest inside it — the hesitation. The terrible, ordinary hesitation of a tired person at the end of a long argument, wanting peace more than wisdom. The rain. The warmth of the waystation window. The little lie she told herself: *the roads are safe enough; they've always been safe enough.*

"Midnight," young Beatrix said. "Don't be late."

Wren laughed again and went down into the fog, and the memory did something memories do not usually do — it *waited*. It waited the way the real night must have waited: the fire burning low, the drink untouched, the clock dragging its feet. Midnight. Half past. The waystation door opening at last, not to Wren's laughter but to a stranger's hat held solemnly in a stranger's hands, and the words that came with it, and the sound young Beatrix made — not a scream, worse than a scream, a small punched-out *no*, the sound of a person discovering the exact weight of a choice already made.

The ford had been washed. Bandits, the stranger said — the uncle's boys scattered, the cargo lost, the girl—

They never found her body. Paloma felt that fact like a splinter driven under the nail: *they never found her body,* and so for years Beatrix had been able to feed the wound with hope, checking every missing-persons list, every unmarked grave, every rumor of a dark-haired woman seen alive somewhere, hope as a form of punishment, hope as a way of never forgiving herself, because forgiveness would require deciding what had happened, and not knowing meant not having to say it—

The memory ended. Paloma came back to herself on her knees in the dark soil, gasping, tears freezing on her lashes, and Beatrix was standing over her with her arms wrapped around herself like a woman holding a wound closed by force.

"Now you know," Beatrix said. Her voice was almost steady. Almost. "My sister walked into the fog alone because I wanted a drink and an evening off from being responsible for everyone. They never found her. And every year after, I guarded those roads twice as hard, and took no dates, and no rest, and told myself it was duty, when really it was just penance wearing duty's clothes. There. Truth spoken. Is the garden satisfied?"

The plant had bloomed. Where the envelope-buds had been, six flowers now stood open — pale, star-shaped, luminous — and the light of them fell across Beatrix's face, and Paloma saw that she was crying without appearing to notice, the way people cry in cold weather, as though the tears belonged to someone else.

"Beatrix—"

"Don't." Beatrix turned away. "You wanted honesty, you have it. Don't dress it up. Don't tell me it wasn't my fault, because it *was*, I chose the drink, I chose the door, I chose to believe the mountain owed me a night's peace after everything I'd given it—"

"Did you choose the flood?" Paloma asked quietly.

Beatrix stopped.

"The bandits. The washed ford. The fog. Did you choose those?" Paloma rose slowly, mud on her knees, the flower-light making her shadow long. "Because I've just lived the worst hour of your life, and I can tell you what I saw. I saw a tired twenty-year-old trust her sister to be as capable as her sister clearly was. I saw Wren *laughing*, Beatrix. She wasn't walking into danger. She was walking home the way she'd walked home a hundred times. You didn't send her to her death. You let her take a road she'd taken her whole life. Those are different things, and you have spent years nailing them together so the guilt would have nowhere to escape."

"You don't understand what the oath meant. What it *cost*—"

"No," Paloma admitted. "I don't. But I understand maps, and yours is wrong. You've drawn the blame lines in the wrong places, and you've done it so long you think the error is the terrain." She stepped closer, close enough that Beatrix could not pretend to look anywhere else. "Here is my decision, since the garden apparently left it to me. I choose truth. But truth isn't a knife, Beatrix. Truth is a *survey*. It says: here is the ridge, here is the river, here is where the ground actually falls. And the actual ground says you were young, and tired, and grieving your own surrendered life, and you loved your sister, and she died on a road that killed better-guarded travelers than her every winter. The oath couldn't have saved her. Your grandmother's rhymes say the Switchback flooded that same night and drowned two sworn swordspeakers at the lower ford. Sworn, Beatrix. Oathed. The mountain took them anyway."

Beatrix's breath came ragged. "Then what was any of it for?"

"That," Paloma said softly, "is the question underneath the question, isn't it? Not *did you fail her* — but *if the oath couldn't save her, what did you give your life to?*" She reached out, slowly, giving Beatrix every chance to step back, and Beatrix did not, and Paloma laid her palm flat against the blooming plant's iron stem, between Beatrix's grief and the garden. "You get to answer that differently now. That's what all of this is. The crown eats people who refuse their own stories. The garden gives them back. You don't have to forgive yourself tonight. You just have to stop lying about where the fault lines run."

For a long moment Beatrix stood utterly still, the star-flowers lighting her from below like a woman standing in shallow water full of moonlight. Then she sank down beside the plant — not gracefully, not like acceptance, but like a bridge cable finally letting tension go — and put her hand next to Paloma's on the stem.

"She used to steal my boots," Beatrix whispered. "Every market day. Just to make me chase her. I'd give anything to chase her one more time."

The garden rustled around them, soft as turning pages. And then, from deep beneath the eastern plots — deeper than any root Paloma had mapped, deeper than the buried heart of the valley itself — something answered.

Not a sound. A pressure. A slow, vast attention turning toward them through the soil like an eye opening underground, and every memory-plant in the garden bent at once, all ten thousand of them, bowing or flinching, Paloma couldn't tell which, toward the place where the two of them knelt.

Beatrix's head snapped up. "What was that?"

Paloma stared at the trembling field, at the maps in her open satchel redrawing themselves in frantic strokes, and felt the truth arrive with the cold certainty of a compass needle settling north.

"It knows we're here," she said. "Beatrix — the gardener. Whatever's been tending this place for a hundred years. It just woke up. And it's coming to see what we've planted."
