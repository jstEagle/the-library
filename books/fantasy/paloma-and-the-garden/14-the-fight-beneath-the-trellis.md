## 14. The Fight Beneath the Trellis

The memory-plant stood between them like an accusation.

It was small — that was the first thing that made Beatrix angry, later, when she had the distance to be angry at all. All the ruin of that night, all the weight she'd carried for eleven years, and it came up out of the garden soil no taller than her knee: a twisted sapling with bark the grey of old scars, leaves like struck flint, and a single fruit hanging low, heavy, dark as a clenched fist. It did not sway in the garden's breathing wind. Everything else in this valley moved — leaned toward Paloma when she passed, turned its flowers like faces toward the sun that shouldn't have been there. But this one plant held itself rigid, the way Beatrix herself had held herself rigid on a mountain road while her sister walked away from her into the dark.

*Say nothing,* she told herself. *Don't give it the satisfaction.*

But the garden didn't work that way. Paloma had learned its rules and taught them back to her in the low, urgent voice she used for corrections on maps — *it grows toward truths people refuse to say, Bea, it's not malicious, it's just honest* — and honesty was exactly what Beatrix couldn't afford. The fruit was ripening as they watched. She could see it happening, the dark skin going glossy, going taut, swelling with something that was not juice.

"It's yours," Paloma said quietly. "I didn't ask for it. I swear it, I only walked past the east beds and it was already coming up."

"I know." Her voice came out flat as slate. "That's what makes it worse."

The trellis arched over them both, ancient white wood gone silver with age, strung with vines that hadn't yet decided whether to flower. Above them the false stars wheeled in their wrong constellations, and somewhere far off down the valley the crown soldiers' watchfires made a smudge of orange against the black, patient as a held breath. They had perhaps until dawn before the hunt resumed. Perhaps less. There was no time for this.

There was never time for this. That had been the whole trick of her life.

"Bea." Paloma stepped closer, and even now — even here, even with a memory-plant growing out of Beatrix's worst night — she moved like a woman approaching a skittish horse, hands open, weight careful. "You don't have to. I want you to hear that first. Whatever the garden wants, whatever the book wants, you don't owe it anything."

"Don't I?" Beatrix laughed, and hated the sound. "My grandmother owed it everything. My mother owed it everything. The whole rotten line of us, kneeling in gardens saying words some dead king needed said—"

"This isn't about your grandmother."

"No," Beatrix said. "It isn't."

She looked at the fruit. And the fruit, being made of memory, looked back.

---

It happened the way it always happened now, since the garden had gotten into her blood: not remembering but *arriving*. The valley dissolved. The trellis dissolved. Paloma's hand, reaching for hers, dissolved last, fingertips brushing her knuckles like the final page of a letter —

— and Beatrix was twenty-three years old, standing on the Widow's Stair with sleet needling her face and her mother's oath-sword heavy on her hip and her sister Wren three steps away, walking toward the smuggler's tunnel where the Vane brothers were waiting with their knives and their debts and their lies about one last job, one last score, enough to buy Wren out of Thornfell forever.

"Come with me or don't," Wren had said. That was the thing about Wren; she never begged. She'd learned early that begging got you nothing from the mountains. "But if you stay, stay *there*, Bea. Don't stand there holding the door open like a coward who wants credit for it."

"I can't leave the pass." The words had come out of Beatrix like stones coughed up from a well. "The oath—"

"The oath is a *song*, Bea. You've told me yourself, half a hundred times. It's just words Grandmother made you memorize. Words don't hold passes. People do." Wren had turned then, and her face in the lantern light had been so tired, so young, so certain her older sister would finally choose her over the dead. "Unless you actually believe it. Unless you actually believe the sword matters more than me."

And Beatrix had said nothing.

That was the truth beneath the trellis. Not that she'd chosen the oath — she'd been too proud to admit she could choose anything, so she'd let the silence choose for her, and called it duty afterward. Wren had waited four heartbeats. Beatrix counted them still, some nights, lying awake under strange roofs: *one, two, three, four.* Then Wren had pulled her hood up and gone down into the tunnel, and the Vane brothers' job had gone wrong the way jobs went wrong, and the pass road had taken two days to dig clear after the slide, and by the time Beatrix reached the low country Wren was a name on a cairn and a coat returned to her folded, smelling of someone else's smoke.

She had renounced the oath that same night. Kneeling on frozen ground with the folded coat in her lap, she had spoken the unbinding backwards, every word reversed, and driven the point of the sword into the earth, and told the sky — told whatever grandmother-ghosts were listening — *never again. Take your inheritance and bury it.*

The sword had gone quiet. It had stayed quiet for eleven years. She had thought that meant it had accepted her refusal.

Standing in the memory now, watching her younger self stand mute on the Widow's Stair, Beatrix understood that quiet was not acceptance. Quiet was a wound deciding not to bleed where anyone could see.

---

She came back to herself on her knees in the garden soil, and the first thing she felt was rage.

It came up through her like fire through a dry field. Eleven years of it, banked and buried, feeding on itself in the dark the way coals feed in a shut stove. She surged upright and drove her boot into the memory-plant's trunk and heard wood crack, and the fruit didn't fall, and that was somehow the worst part — the tree *held on*, stubborn as she was, refusing to drop it, refusing to let her break it loose.

"You don't get to have this!" she shouted at it, at the garden, at the silver trellis, at the whole patient listening valley. "You don't get to dig her up and make a *harvest* out of her!"

The garden didn't flinch. Gardens didn't. It just kept breathing around her, warm loam and green rot and that impossible honeyed light, and the trellis vines stirred, and somewhere behind her Paloma said her name, soft, once, and didn't touch her, and Beatrix loved her so much in that moment it frightened her more than the crown ever had.

"I renounced it." Her voice cracked down the middle. "I *unbound* myself. I did it right, I did it by the book, every word backwards, and it should have ended — the prophecy should have died with me in that snow, why does it keep *reaching*—"

"Because you never finished the sentence," Paloma said.

Beatrix turned on her. "What?"

Paloma stood at the edge of the trellis shadow with her satchel clutched against her chest, ink-stained fingers white-knuckled on the strap, and her eyes were wet but steady, steady as contour lines, steady as north. "The hedge-witch told me how the old oaths worked, before your family changed them. A swordspeaker doesn't just swear the vow, Bea. She swears it *whole*. Every verse, including the ones about what the sword costs. Your grandmother's version — the one you memorized — it ends at the promise. But the original oath has a second half. A reckoning half. You speak what the blade has taken from you, aloud, in full, and only then does the binding take root instead of closing like a trap." She swallowed. "Your mother spoke the first half and never the second. Neither did her mother. That's why it eats Ravensworths, Bea. You've all been swearing half-truths for three generations, and the garden grows toward truths people refuse to say, and you—" her voice caught, steadied, "—you are the most refused truth I have ever mapped."

The wind moved through the valley. The memory-tree shivered, and the fruit swelled another inch, and Beatrix stood in the middle of her own buried night with her fists bleeding where her nails had cut crescents, and understood.

It wasn't the prophecy that wouldn't let go of her.

It was her.

---

"I'm going to say it," she said, "and then I never want to talk about it again."

"That's fair," Paloma said. "I'll be right here."

Beatrix laid her hand on the sword at her hip. It had woken in the hedge-witch's hut weeks ago — a lifetime ago, another century ago — and it had hummed against her palm ever since, patient, insistent, like a dog waiting at a door. Now, under her fingers, it went very still. Listening.

She knelt. Not because the garden demanded it. Because her grandmother's stories had always described this part as kneeling, and if she was going to do it, she was going to do it properly, on her own terms, in front of the only witness she'd have chosen.

"Wren," she said. Just the name. It came out ragged.

The fruit trembled on its branch.

"My name is Beatrix Ravensworth. Last of the oath-line. And I am going to tell the truth about my sister." Her voice shook. She let it. For the first time in her adult life, she let it shake and did not clamp down on it, did not turn it into stone, did not make it useful. "Her name was Wren. She was nineteen. She was funny and reckless and she saved my life twice before she was fifteen, and I loved her more than the pass, more than the oath, more than the whole stinking mountain range, and I let her die."

"Not let," Paloma whispered. "You didn't—"

"The truth," Beatrix said, and her eyes were burning and she did not wipe them. "*Let* is the true word. I stood on the Widow's Stair and she asked me — she *asked* me, straight out, gave me the choice naked in my hands, and I chose silence. Because choosing her meant admitting the oath was a story we told ourselves, and I was too much a coward to live in a world without the story. So I hid behind duty. I dressed my fear up in Grandmother's words and I sent my baby sister down into the dark alone, and she died believing I loved a song more than her." The words tore coming out, each one, like roots ripping free of shale. "And then I renounced the oath and I told everyone — told *myself* — that it was grief. That I'd put the sword down out of mourning. But that was a lie too. I put it down because I couldn't bear to carry the thing that had cost me her, and I called the cowardice principle, and I built a whole life on top of it like building a house over a sinkhole. Eleven years guarding roads I didn't care about, saving strangers I didn't love, because if I was good enough, useful enough, *dutiful* enough, maybe it balanced out. Maybe it paid the debt. It doesn't balance. It will never balance. She's dead and I did that, with four heartbeats of silence, and no number of smuggler routes guided and no number of travelers saved will ever make it otherwise, and I am *tired*, do you hear me, I am so tired of carrying her like contraband—"

Her voice broke completely. She let it break.

And the garden bloomed.

Not gently. Not gradually. The whole valley went off like a green explosion — the trellis above her bursting into flower all at once, white blossoms opening with audible cracks like distant ice, vines racing along the silvered wood, the memory-tree's flint leaves flushing living green, and the fruit splitting softly, harmlessly, spilling not rot but light, a smell like rain on a grave in springtime, like bread, like Wren's laugh, which Beatrix had not heard in eleven years and which rose up out of the broken fruit and filled the garden and filled her, and she was crying openly, both hands pressed to the soil, and the soil pressed back.

The oath came then. Not summoned. *Settling.*

She felt it move into her the way roots move into ground — seeking out every crack she'd confessed to, filling them, anchoring through them. It found the wound where Wren lived and it rooted around it tenderly, did not fill it, did not erase it, left the hollow hollow because the hollow was hers and holy, and grew its strength around the edges of it instead. It threaded down through her rage, her guilt, her eleven years of frozen silence, and gripped bedrock, and Beatrix understood suddenly, bodily, what three generations of Ravensworth women had never known:

An oath spoken whole cannot be a chain. A chain needs a lie to cinch around. Hers had nothing left to catch on. It simply *grew*, and she simply held it, and the difference between those two things was the difference between drowning and learning the shape of the water.

The sword sang. One clear note, low, under hearing, under the soil, and every blossom on the trellis turned toward her at once, and Paloma made a sound that was half sob and half laugh and dropped to her knees beside her, and Beatrix pulled her in and held her among the flowering ruins of her worst night and said, into her hair, "I'm sorry. About all of it. About making you carry the map of me."

"You're the best country I've ever charted," Paloma said into her shoulder, wetly, fiercely. "Every inch worth the survey."

They stayed like that while the garden blazed around them, while the false stars seemed almost to soften, while the light of the impossible sun began, far down the eastern peaks, to change.

Because it was changing. Beatrix saw it over Paloma's shoulder and felt the new-rooted oath tighten like a hand closing around hers — not chains, roots, *her* roots — and the joy curdled fast into alertness.

The eastern sky was turning the wrong color. Not dawn-gold. A bruised violet, spreading, deliberate, rolling down from the direction of the Pale Court like spilled ink across the face of the morning. And beneath it, faint but unmistakable, carried on a wind that smelled of cold iron and old hunger, came a sound Beatrix knew from a hundred border skirmishes and had hoped never to hear again in any century:

Horns. Many horns. And under them, thin and rising, a chorus of voices calling out greetings — warm, familiar, loving — in voices that belonged to people long dead, and to people standing right beside her, and to her, calling her own name from the dark.

The crown-spirit had stopped hunting.

It had started *imitating*.

Paloma pulled back, and her face had gone cartographer-pale, and her hand went to the satchel where the burning book slept. "Bea," she said. "The pages. It's burning faster."

"How fast?"

"Fast enough that we march tonight."

Beatrix rose. The oath held her like earth holds a tree, and the sword came off her hip singing, and down the valley the false-loved voices of the enemy drew nearer through the blooming dark.

"Then we give the gardener his answer," she said.
