## 15. March on the Pale Court

The safehouse was a smuggler's cellar beneath a collapsed mill, and Serah had made it defensible in ways Paloma only half understood — sight lines through the broken floorboards, three exits, a kettle of water that never seemed to cool. She had also made tea, which felt absurdly domestic given that the world was ending on a schedule.

Paloma sat with her maps spread across two upturned barrels, and for once they were not redrawing themselves. That frightened her more than the moving ink ever had. The garden's plants had gone still, as if holding their breath.

"The Pale Court," Serah said. She stood with her back to the wall, arms crossed, the posture of a woman who had spent years learning to look like furniture beside a king. "You want to walk into it. I want to be certain you understand what you're asking."

"We've been over this," Beatrix said from the doorway, where she'd been sharpening a knife that didn't need sharpening. Paloma recognized it now as what Beatrix did instead of pacing. "Twice."

"Then we'll go over it a third time," Serah said, "because the first two times you were brave, and bravery is a poor cartographer."

Paloma looked up despite herself. "That's — actually a line I could steal."

"Steal whatever you like. I stole most of mine from Aldric." Something moved behind Serah's face when she said his name — grief compressed into a shape small enough to carry. "Listen to me. The throne room sits at the heart of the palace like a seed in a fruit. Every corridor spirals toward it. The walls listen. The doors open where the crown wants them and close where it doesn't. For six years I have walked those halls knowing that the man I swore to protect is a larder, and something inside him is eating."

"Serah—" Beatrix began.

"No. You'll hear all of it." Serah pushed off the wall and came to the barrels, laying one gauntleted hand flat on Paloma's map of the valley. "Here is what I know of the crown-spirit's habits. It does not sleep, but it dreams, and when it dreams, Aldric surfaces — briefly, at dawn, before the household wakes. That is when he wrote on his palm. That is when he is most himself. If there is a moment to reach the man rather than the monster, it is dawn."

"And the rest of the day?" Beatrix asked quietly.

"The rest of the day, you are walking through a mind wearing a body." Serah tapped the map where the palace rose above the village, drawn in Paloma's hand but already older than yesterday. "It knows everything that happens under its roof. It tasted my suspicion years ago and chose not to act on it — I have wondered since whether that was mercy or farming. Do not assume it doesn't know you're coming. Assume it wants you to come, and plan for the trap inside the invitation."

Paloma's pen had stopped moving. She made herself start again, sketching the palace's bones from memory of Serah's descriptions, because drawing helped her think and she needed very badly to think. "Then we don't come through the doors at all. We come through the garden."

* * *

They argued about it until the candle burned down to its collar.

The garden lay beneath the palace — Paloma was sure of it now, sure in the way you are sure of your own heartbeat. Every memory-plant in the hidden valley grew from something the crown-spirit had eaten and discarded, and the roots went down and down, past soil, past stone, into the deep places where the Pale Court had been built on top of them. The garden was not near the throne. The garden was *underneath* it, the way a grave underlies a house no one admits is haunted.

"If Paloma can finish the true map down there," Beatrix said, "she can read the crown's name straight out of it."

"Not read it." Paloma turned her notebook so they could both see: her sketches of the garden's oldest bed, where the plants grew densest, their leaves veined with silver like handwriting. "Names aren't words here. They're shapes. The spirit was a gardener once — a gardener-king who refused to die. Its name will be written in what it planted. In what it *grew itself from*. I have to map the whole root system, every truth it fed on, and find the shape underneath. Then—"

"Then I say it out loud," Beatrix finished. Her voice was steady. Her knuckles weren't; Paloma saw them, white around the knife hilt, and reached across the barrels without thinking to cover them with her own hand.

Beatrix looked at the touch. Didn't pull away.

"You'll be speaking it unbound," Serah said. "Not the oath. Not the prophecy. Your own voice, your own choice. That's the only version of the name that has power to unmake — anything spoken by obligation, it can digest."

"How do you know that?" Beatrix asked sharply.

"Because it told me." Serah's mouth twisted. "Not in words. In confidence. Six years of watching it grow fatter every time someone in that palace did their duty out of fear. Fear is just obligation with the skin off. It eats both."

The cellar was silent except for the wind worrying the collapsed mill above them. Somewhere water dripped in a rhythm almost like a clock.

"So we have three tasks," Paloma said, because someone had to make the shape of the thing visible or it would stay a fog. "I map the name. Beatrix speaks it. And Serah—"

"Hold the door." Serah said it simply, the way soldiers say things they have already died a little while deciding. "The moment Beatrix begins speaking, the spirit will know, and it will bring everything it has. The court, the guard, the things wearing faces you love. My job is to stand in the doorway of the throne room and be the reason none of that arrives on time."

"For how long?" Paloma asked, though she dreaded the answer.

"As long as it takes." Serah smiled then, thin and crooked, and for a second Paloma saw the woman Aldric must have chosen, decades ago, to stand at his shoulder. "I've held worse doors for worse reasons."

* * *

It was Beatrix who noticed the smell.

Paloma was deep in the maps — tracing the root-shapes, testing which truths grew where — when Beatrix went rigid by the candle and said, "Do you smell smoke?"

There was no smoke. There was something older than smoke: a scent like sun-hot parchment, like libraries in summer, like the inside of the book itself. They turned together to the prophecy, lying closed on its shelf of stones, and watched a thread of gray rise from between its covers.

Paloma opened it with hands that shook more than she wanted them to.

The last page was burning. Not with flame — with a slow, deliberate charring, as though an invisible thumb were smudging it into ash line by line, exactly the way the final verse had been censored a century ago. But this was not censorship. This was consumption. As she watched, the closing words of the prophecy darkened, curled, and flaked away, leaving blank vellum where a future had been written.

"It's eating itself from the end," she whispered. "Backward."

Beatrix took the book gently, as if it were a wounded bird. "How much time?"

Paloma did the arithmetic and hated every number of it. The book had perhaps forty pages left. The burning had started tonight. Whatever pace the fire kept — and it would keep a pace, prophecies being nothing if not punctual — they had days. Maybe fewer.

"It's a deadline," she said aloud, because saying it made it real, and real things could be mapped. "The prophecy is finishing itself. Everything after the last page stops existing — maybe just the story ends, maybe..." She couldn't finish. Maybe the valley. Maybe the century. Maybe the two of them, pulled out of their own time like splinters from wood.

"Then we march tomorrow," Beatrix said, "before dawn. We hit the palace at first light, when Aldric surfaces, and we give the man inside a chance to help us before we kill the thing wearing him."

"That isn't how I'd phrase our odds," Serah said dryly.

"It's how I'd phrase our purpose." Beatrix set the book down and looked at Paloma, and there it was again — that guarded hope from the tavern, from the mountain road, from every terrible mile between, except now the guards were down and the hope was bare. "Paloma. Once we start, there's no fold in the road home until it's done. Are you certain?"

Paloma thought of the garden growing toward unsaid truths. She thought of the night she'd shown Beatrix the memory-plant grown from her sister, and the fight beneath the trellis, and the way Beatrix had finally spoken and the whole valley had bloomed. She thought of a date that had begun with nervous hands and a gift book and ended a century deep.

"I've spent my whole life mapping places other people decided were important," she said. "This is the first one I chose myself. Yes. I'm certain."

* * *

They prepared in the blue hour before dawn, and the preparation was mostly silence.

Serah oiled her armor and said nothing, which was its own kind of speech. Beatrix wrapped her hands the old way, the way her grandmother's rhymes described, murmuring smuggler verses under her breath like rosaries. Paloma packed her satchel with the care of a woman packing for the moon: pens, inks, the redrawn maps, the book with its burning tail, and — at the bottom, wrapped in oilcloth — a handful of seeds taken from the garden's stubbornest plant, the one grown from a truth nobody had ever dared to say aloud until Beatrix did.

Just in case, she told herself. Just in case there's an afterward.

They climbed out of the cellar into a sky the color of unpolished steel, stars going pale at the edges. The Thornfell before the fall rose black against them, and somewhere beyond it, the Pale Court waited with its listening walls and its hollow king.

"One correction to the plan," Serah said at the treeline, pausing with her hand on the trunk of a pine. "When you speak the name — whatever it turns out to be — don't say it *at* the spirit. Say it *for* Aldric. He has to hear it as a door opening, not a verdict. Otherwise he'll cling harder, and a drowning man who clings drowns his rescuers too."

Beatrix nodded slowly. "Say it like an invitation home."

"Yes." Serah's voice roughened. "He was a good man. Remember that in there, when you see what's left of him. He was a good man who was afraid, and something patient found him."

They moved out as the sky cracked along the east, gray light bleeding down the mountain like ink finding water. Paloma walked between them — the swordswoman on her left, the shield-sworn on her right — and felt the book against her spine growing warm, another page giving itself up to ash with every hundred steps.

She did not look back at the garden. But she felt it, below and behind, all those buried memories holding their breath, waiting to see if anyone would finally speak the truth that set them free.

Ahead, the towers of the Pale Court caught the first light and threw it back wrong — pale, bloodless, like bone remembering flesh.

Paloma opened her notebook to a fresh page and wrote the date at the top, because cartographers mark the beginning of journeys, even — especially — the ones that might not come home.

Then the wind shifted, and from the palace, faint and clear across the valley, came the sound of bells ringing for an execution that hadn't happened yet.

Two names on the proclamation, she would learn later. Hers and Beatrix's.

The crown knew they were coming.
