## 16. The Burning Book

The book began to burn at moonrise.

Beatrix felt it before she saw it — a warmth against her hip where the volume rode in its oilcloth wrapping, as though she carried a sleeping bird whose heart had begun to race. She pulled it out by the light of their hidden campfire, there in the hollow beneath the old smuggler's arch, and watched the last pages curl at their edges like leaves touched by frost in reverse.

"Paloma," she said, and her voice came out steadier than she felt. "It's starting."

Paloma looked up from her maps, ink to the second knuckle, hair pinned back with a quill that had seen better decades. Her eyes went to the book and something in her face went carefully, professionally still — the stillness of a woman measuring a flood by the height of a mark on a wall.

"How much is left?" she asked.

Beatrix turned the pages with two fingers, gentle as handling a wound. The verses were vanishing from the back forward, each one burning away not into ash but into nothing — the words simply lifting off the vellum like heat-shimmer and dissolving, leaving blank parchment that was already browning at its margins. Verse after verse after verse, gone. What remained was a thin sheaf, perhaps a dozen leaves, and the final page — the smudged one, the censored one, the verse no Ravensworth had wanted read — glowing faintly, stubbornly, like a coal refusing the dark.

"Twelve," Beatrix said. "Maybe ten by now."

"Ten verses." Paloma set down her pen. Her hands were not quite steady. "How long does a verse take to burn?"

"I don't know." Beatrix closed the oilcloth over it, and even through the waxed fabric she could feel the heat, patient and incremental, a clock made of fire. "My grandmother never told me what happens when the prophecy finishes. Only that it mustn't finish before we're ready."

"And are we ready?"

Beatrix looked at her — really looked — at this woman who had walked into a tavern three weeks and a century ago carrying a gift and a grin and no idea what either would cost. Paloma Idris, who mapped the impossible because someone in her bloodline once failed to come home from it. Who had spent days in the garden below learning its rules, who had traced the crown's true name in lines so fine they could only be read by candlelight, who had slept badly every night since and risen every morning anyway.

"No," Beatrix said honestly. "But we're closer than the crown wants us to be. That's why it's striking early."

* * *

They heard the army before they saw it.

Not drums — Thornfell's passes ate sound, bent it, returned it wrong. What they heard was absence: the sudden silence of birds along the eastern ridge, the way the wind through the pines went flat and held its breath. Beatrix had grown up on these mountains in her grandmother's stories, had memorized their moods the way other children memorized prayers, and every instinct she possessed stood up and screamed.

She climbed to the mouth of the hollow and looked down the long throat of the valley pass, and her stomach dropped.

Torches. Hundreds of them, snaking down from the Pale Court road in columns that did not march so much as *pour*, filling the pass like water fills a channel. And the soldiers —

Beatrix had fought soldiers. She had fought bandits, mercenaries, the desperate and the cruel. She knew what an army looked like: a gray mass, faces indistinct, individual men swallowed by the machine of them.

These soldiers all had faces she knew.

The nearest column wore the face of her mother. Not a likeness — the face itself, young again, unlined, wearing the expression Beatrix had last seen across a kitchen table the night everything broke. Behind her marched a man with Beatrix's father's crooked smile. Behind him, Serah — but Serah was miles away holding the door at the garden's edge, and this Serah smiled too widely, and her shadow on the torchlit rock moved half a heartbeat late.

"They're wearing them," Paloma breathed beside her. She had come up silently; Beatrix hadn't heard her, which frightened her more than the army did. "The faces. The people the crown —"

"The meals," Beatrix said. Her mouth was dry. "Every self it ever ate. Every person some worn-out king loved and lost to it. It kept them." She understood then, with a lurching clarity, what she was looking at: not an army but a *pantry*, opened and armed. A century of stolen faces given swords and orders.

And at the head of the foremost column, walking with a young king's easy stride and an old thing's patience, a figure in Aldric's colors turned its face upward toward the hollow, and the face it wore was Beatrix's own.

Her breath stopped. It was exact. The scar through her left eyebrow. The way her hair fell when she hadn't cut it in months. It smiled with her smile, and behind the smile something ancient and hungry looked out through her features like a tenant looking out a window it intended to keep.

"*There you are,*" it said, and the voice was her voice, and the pass carried it up to her perfectly. "*Little swordspeaker. Come down. Come home. I have your whole family here.*"

Beside her, Paloma's hand found hers and gripped hard enough to hurt, and Beatrix was grateful for the pain, because without it she thought she might have simply stepped off the ledge into that sea of beloved faces and drowned.

"It lies," Paloma whispered. "You know it lies."

"I know." But knowing and believing were different countries, and the road between them was lined with her mother's face.

* * *

The plan had been simple. That was the problem with plans; simplicity only held until the enemy declined to cooperate. Serah held the garden gate. Paloma mapped the true name in the garden's heart. Beatrix carried the name to the throne room and spoke it unbound. Three parts, clean as a blade.

The crown-spirit had struck early precisely to break the middle part — to flood the valley, to pin them here, to keep Paloma from the garden while the book burned itself to nothing above their heads. If Beatrix stayed in the hollow, they both died waiting. If they ran for the garden together, the army followed and swallowed them both.

So there was really only one thing left, and it was the thing her grandmother's stories had always circled and never named: the pass. The narrow place. The place where one swordspeaker stood against many, because the mountain made the numbers lie.

"You can't," Paloma said. They stood at the fork in the road, the downward path to the garden on one side, the upward throat of the pass on the other. Torches filled the valley floor below like spilled stars. "Beatrix. There are hundreds."

"There are hundreds in open ground." Beatrix drew the sword. It came out of its sheath with a sound she felt in her teeth — not the ring of steel but something deeper, a note like a struck root, like the mountain clearing its throat. The blade was faintly luminous, the way the garden's plants were faintly luminous, and along its fuller ran lettering that shifted when she wasn't looking directly at it. "But the Narrows above the old toll-arch fit four abreast, and the drop is sixty feet onto rock, and I know every foothold my grandmother ever sang about. They can't flank me. They can't rush me. All they can do is come up the throat and die."

"And you'll be alone up there."

"I've been alone worse places." She meant it as comfort. It came out as truth, which was sometimes the same thing between them.

Paloma grabbed her by the collar and kissed her — hard, brief, furious, the kiss of a woman who refused to make it a farewell. When she pulled back her eyes were wet and blazing. "You speak that oath," she said. "You hear me? You don't hold back. You don't ration yourself. Whatever your mother taught you about what that oath costs — you spend all of it, and you come back down that mountain and we argue about it later."

"That sounds like a mapmaker's plan. Very precise. No allowance for weather."

"Tamsin Vasquez's granddaughter will manage." Paloma's voice cracked on the joke, and she laughed despite herself, a wet broken sound, and pushed Beatrix toward the pass. "Go. I'll be in the garden. Find me when it's done — I'll leave the candle lit."

Beatrix climbed.

* * *

The Narrows were exactly as her grandmother's rhymes described them: a stone gullet above the ruined toll-arch, walled in granite older than any kingdom, floored in scree that sang underfoot if you knew how to listen for the warning-notes. Beatrix took her position where the walls pinched to a spear's width of sky overhead, planted her feet in the two worn hollows — *worn by Ravensworth feet*, she realized, generations of them, standing exactly here, saying exactly what she was about to say — and waited.

The army reached the base of the pass as the book in her mind counted down another vanished verse. Nine. Eight. She could feel it burning against her ribs like a second heartbeat.

They came up the throat of the pass singing.

That was the worst of it. The crown-spirit had given its stolen faces voices, and they sang marching songs in voices Beatrix knew — her mother's low alto, her father's tuneless hum, the village children from the valley-edge, Serah's barracks ballads. They sang as they climbed, hundreds of beloved dead-and-stolen mouths, and the first rank that rounded the bend into the Narrows was led by a woman with her mother's face carrying her mother's old hunting spear.

"Put it down," Beatrix said. Her voice shook. "Whatever you are — put it down. Those faces aren't yours."

The thing wearing her mother smiled her mother's smile. "Neither are those hands, oathbreaker," it said, and charged.

Beatrix had trained for war her whole life and fought almost none of it, and in the first terrible exchange she learned the difference. Training was geometry; this was weather. The stolen soldier was fast and utterly fearless and fought like someone who had nothing left to lose because nothing it had was real. Beatrix gave ground, gave more ground, caught the hunting spear on her crossguard and felt the shock of it in her wrists, and behind the first rank came the second, and behind them the third, an endless tide of familiar faces pouring up the stone throat with love's own features twisted to the crown's purpose.

She could not do this. The thought arrived cold and complete. She could not cut down her mother's face, her father's, the neighbors of her childhood — her body would fail her, her heart would fail her first, she would stand here and be buried under a wave of everyone she had ever —

The sword hummed against her palms.

Not sharply. Gently. The way a tuning fork hums when its twin has been struck somewhere out of sight. And through the hum, wordless, she felt it say what it had been trying to say since the night she'd renounced her oath and walked away: *these are not them. These were never them. The people you loved are gone, and grief for them belongs to you — but these things wearing their shapes are the thief, not the stolen.*

The distinction landed in her like a keystone dropping into an arch.

They were not her family. Refusing to fight them would not protect her family. It would only feed the thief another generation — hers.

Beatrix set her feet in the worn hollows, lifted the singing blade, and spoke.

Not the fragments. Not the careful, rationed pieces she'd allowed herself in the garden, testing the water. She spoke the whole oath, every line, beginning to end, the full weight of it, the inheritance her mother had paid for and she had refused — and the mountain took it the way dry earth takes rain.

Words she had never memorized rose in her and she knew them anyway, the way you know your own name:

"*By root and ridge and river's claim, by every road the smugglers know, I bind my voice to blade and stone. Let what I speak be spoken true. Let what I shield stay shielded. Let what I name fall named.*"

Power went through her like dawn through a valley.

It was not what she had feared. All her life she had imagined the oath as a chain — had watched it eat her mother by degrees, had fled it the way you flee a house where someone died. But standing in her ancestors' footprints with the truth finally loose in her chest, the oath came into her like roots coming into soil: not binding her *down* but binding her *in*, holding her fast to the mountain, to the moment, to herself. She was not less free. She was, for the first time she could remember, entirely located. Every scattered piece of Beatrix Ravensworth gathered home.

The lead soldier struck at her and she moved — not faster, exactly, but *truer*, each motion arriving exactly where it belonged, and the stolen spear shattered against a parry that was also a sentence, because that was what swordspeaking was, she understood now: speech made edge. Every word of the oath had a shape in steel, and she knew all of them, and the Narrows rang like a bell being played.

"*Let what I name fall named,*" she said to the next rank, and named them — named the shadows inside the stolen faces, named the crown-spirit's hunger in the old tongue of her line, and where her voice touched them the false faces flickered like candles in wind. Some collapsed into smoke. Some faltered, wept with borrowed eyes, turned and fled down the mountain. Others pressed on, and those she met with steel and sorrow in equal measure.

Terror was still there. She would not pretend otherwise — terror sat in her chest like a swallowed stone, cold and permanent. But around the stone, impossibly, joy had taken root. This was the fierce, singing, furious joy of finally being exactly what she was, in exactly the place she was needed, doing the thing she had been made for and had chosen — both, both at once, inheritance and election braided into a single cord. Her grandmother's rhymes poured through her. Her mother's sacrifice made sense at last, not as a warning but as a payment on a debt Beatrix was honored to settle. The sword danced. The mountain sang. The pass held.

Verse by verse behind her sternum, the book burned down. Seven. Six. Five. She fought through them.

Four. Three. Two.

And at two verses remaining, with the Narrows choked in smoke and the tide momentarily broken, the thing wearing her own face climbed the last stretch of rock into the pass and stood before her, and it was no longer smiling.

"You cannot win," it said with her mouth. "The book ends. The garden closes. Everything returns to me."

"Then it's a good thing," Beatrix said, leveling the blade, her voice roughened and blazing, "that we're not trying to win. We're trying to *end* you. There's a difference. My friend is drawing it right now."

Below and behind, far down the valley where the hidden garden bloomed out of season, a light kindled — small, steady, candle-colored, exactly where a certain cartographer had promised to leave it lit.

One verse left.

Beatrix Ravensworth, last oath-bound swordspeaker and first of the new kind, turned to face the final descent of the stolen host — and saw, breaking over the eastern ridge behind them, a rider on a lathered horse with a wounded shoulder and a sworn shield's colors flying, Serah come at last with news she could not wait to give.
