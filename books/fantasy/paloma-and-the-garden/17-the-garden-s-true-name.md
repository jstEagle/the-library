## 17. The Garden's True Name

The heart of the garden was not where Paloma expected it to be.

She had mapped the valley for six days now — six days of ink that behaved like a living thing, of lines that crawled across her parchment when she wasn't looking, of plants that leaned toward her pencil like flowers toward sun. She had learned the garden's grammar: it grew toward truths refused, bloomed where someone had buried something too heavy to carry any farther. She had charted grief-rows and shame-thickets, a whole orchard of Beatrix's unspoken things, a hedgerow of Serah's doubts pruned neat as a soldier's beard.

But the center eluded her. Every map she drew placed it somewhere different. North by one measure, below by another, *inside* according to a third — until she understood, on the sixth evening, with the burning book warm against her ribs, that the garden did not have a center so much as it had a *door*, and doors did not care about compasses.

It opened for her at moonrise, when she stopped trying to find it and simply said aloud, to the dark and the leaves and whatever listened: "I'm not here to take anything. I'm here to finish what you started."

The brambles parted like a curtain drawn by a careful hand.

* * *

The path beyond was white gravel that crunched like old bone, though when Paloma knelt and touched it, it was only stone worn smooth by centuries of feet — or by centuries of one pair of feet, going over and over the same ground. The walls on either side were yew, clipped into shapes: a stag, a woman kneeling, a crown.

Always a crown. She had stopped flinching at them days ago; now she only counted them. Forty-one between the door and where she was going. She wondered if anyone had ever counted before her, or if the number mattered, or if the yew simply liked repeating itself the way grief likes repeating itself, saying the same name over and over into a room where no one answers.

The air changed as she walked. In the outer valley the garden had smelled of wet earth and green growing things, loamy and alive. Here the scent thinned into something older — dried roses, dust in a closed room, the ghost of sweetness rather than sweetness itself. Preserved. Pickled. A memory of a garden instead of a garden, kept under glass by something that could not bear to let even smell change.

Paloma's satchel bumped against her hip, and inside it the book — the prophecy, three-quarters burned now from the back forward, its remaining pages hot as fresh bread — gave a single pulse, like a heartbeat heard through a wall.

"I know," she murmured. "I know. I'm going."

She had left Beatrix at the trellis camp with Serah, both of them arguing strategy in low voices that kept softening into touches and then hardening again into strategy. Beatrix had wanted to come. Of course Beatrix had wanted to come; Beatrix wanted to stand between Paloma and every danger in every world, which was noble and infuriating and would have ruined everything. The garden answered to Paloma. It had answered to her bloodline for a hundred years, waiting, and if Beatrix walked into the heart trailing iron and oaths, the garden would close around her like a fist.

"Come back," Beatrix had said, holding her wrist, not quite meeting her eyes. "That's an order from your smuggler guide."

"That's a request from my—" and Paloma had kissed her rather than finish the sentence, because finishing sentences about what they were to each other still felt like pressing thumbprints onto wet clay: permanent, and easy to smudge.

Now, alone in the yew corridor, she finished it anyway, quietly, to no one. *From my Beatrix.* Saying it made the dark feel less like a throat.

* * *

The corridor ended at a gate that was not locked but *tired* — iron hinges bowed outward as if the gate had been pushing itself open for a century and had finally given up halfway. Beyond it lay the true garden, and Paloma stood on the threshold for a long moment, forgetting entirely how to be a cartographer.

It was beautiful the way ruins are beautiful, the way a face is beautiful after crying. Terraces descended in wide arcs toward a pool of black water, and every terrace was planted — but wrong. Roses grew from stones. Apple trees bore fruit shaped like hands. A bed of lavender hummed faintly, and when Paloma leaned close she realized the humming was voices, dozens of them, murmuring fragments: a lullaby in a dead language, a list of debts, someone saying *I never told him I forgave him* over and over in a voice cracked with age.

Memory-plants. But these were different from the ones in the outer valley. Those had grown from ordinary people's discarded truths — small griefs, small shames. These had been *cultivated*. Rows straight as soldiers. Labels on stakes driven into the soil, written in a spidery hand:

*Queen Maren — her laughter (the real kind). Kept: 61 years.*
*Aldric — his mother's voice. Kept: 9 years. Spoiling.*
*Aldric — the shape of his own name in his mouth. Kept: 3 years.*

Paloma read the labels until her eyes stung. This was not a garden of memories. This was a *larder*.

And at the bottom of the last terrace, beside the black pool, stood a tree unlike the others. It was ancient and silver-barked, and its fruit was neither apple nor hand nor anything grown — its fruit was *faces*, pale and closed-eyed, hanging among leaves that whispered with all the voices of the labeled beds at once.

Beneath the tree, half-sunk into the earth as if the garden had grown up around it, lay a slab of stone. And on the stone, in the same spidery hand as the labels, words had been cut deep and then, at the bottom, scratched out — violently, desperately, by someone who had changed their mind about being remembered.

Paloma knelt. She took out her pencils, her inks, the last clean sheet of vellum she owned. And she began, with hands that shook only a little, to do the thing she had been born — or chosen, or condemned — to do.

She mapped the impossible.

* * *

It took her until dawn, and she would remember almost none of the work itself, only the sensation: that the map was drawing her as much as she drew it. Her lines found their places without measuring. Her shading fell where shadow actually fell. When she wrote the word *terrace*, the terrace seemed to become more terraced, more itself, as if naming had been the one thing missing from this place all along — as if everything here had gone unlabeled for so long that it had begun to forget what it was.

Which, she understood suddenly, sitting back on her heels with cold-stiff fingers, was exactly the point.

She looked down at her finished map, and the map looked back, and she saw it: the whole valley rendered true at last, and at its heart — where the tree stood, where the stone lay — a name. Not written by her. Surfacing through the vellum from beneath, the way drowned things surface, in handwriting that was not the spidery gardener's hand at all but something rounder, younger, a king's educated script going gradually to ruin down the length of the letters:

**AURELIAN THE GARDENER-KING. FIRST OF THE CROWN. LAST OF HIMSELF.**

And beneath the name, surfacing slower, the story — because the garden grew toward truths refused, and this truth had been refused longest of all.

* * *

Paloma read it the way you read anything unbearable: fast, then slow, then fast again, hoping speed would change it.

There had been a kingdom here before Thornfell, before Aldric's line, before the mountains wore their present names. Its king was called Aurelian, and he was — the garden insisted on this, pressed it into the vellum in letters that deepened as she watched — he was a good man, or good enough, the way kings are measured, which is poorly. He loved two things without reservation: his gardens, and the idea that he was not yet finished. He planted trees whose fruit he knew he would never taste and told his courtiers this proved his humility. He was lying. It proved the opposite. Every tree was a bet that the future would need him in it.

He grew old the way everyone does: by surprise. And on his deathbed, with his son waiting outside the door and the winter roses dying unpruned in the frost, Aurelian did the thing the histories had scrubbed from every record, the thing that required a century of prophecy and two stolen cartographers and a swordspeaker's broken oath to undo.

He refused.

Not dramatically. That was the horror of it, the thing that made Paloma set down the vellum and press her palms flat against the cold ground as if to steady herself against the turning of the world. He refused *quietly*. He lay in his bed and felt the ending coming, felt it the way a gardener feels frost coming — inevitable, indifferent, arriving on schedule — and he reached for the only tools he had ever mastered, and he *gardenered himself*. He took hold of his own ending the way he'd taken hold of unruly vines, and he pruned. He cut away the parts of himself that could die — the body first, that went easily, gratefully — and kept the part that wanted, and the want had nowhere to go, so it went into the nearest vessel: the crown on the bedpost, gold and hollow, waiting for a head.

His son wore it next. And the thing in the crown — no longer Aurelian, because Aurelian had cut away the parts of himself that remembered being loved, and what remained was appetite wearing his patience like a borrowed coat — the thing fed. Gently at first, the way the garden watered things. A memory here. A preference there. The way his successor laughed, transplanted. The way he prayed, uprooted. Each king grew kinder in public because kindness is a performance, and performances can be worn like coats; and emptier in private because private is where the feeding happened.

A hundred years. Four kings. The last of them, Aldric, had lasted longer than most because he had been stubborn — had hidden pieces of himself in odd corners, scrawled truths on his own palm in a child's handwriting because a child's handwriting was the one disguise the thing in the crown never thought to check.

And the faces in the tree — Paloma made herself look at them, made herself count — those were not only kings. Those were everyone the crown had eaten to stay sharp. Chamberlains who noticed. Queens who resisted. Gardeners who had recognized, in the palace rows, the planting style of a dead man's hand.

One of the faces, low on the trunk where the bark was newest, was a young woman with a hedge-witch's crooked smile.

Paloma sat down on the cold stone, quite suddenly, because her legs had decided the matter for her.

* * *

Here is the thing she had expected to feel, standing in the monster's pantry with its true name in her hands: triumph. Maybe hatred. Certainly the clean bright anger that makes swordspeaking possible, the anger Beatrix carried like a whetstone.

What she felt instead was pity, enormous and awful, rising in her like floodwater.

Because she had read the labels. She had seen the rows. And underneath the hunger, underneath a century of stolen selves, she had seen the gardener's hand in everything — patient, meticulous, *tending*. The thing in the crown did not gloat. It did not rage. It weeded. It watered. It kept its victims' memories alive in labeled beds, pruned and thriving, and there was something in that care worse than cruelty: it meant the thing still thought of itself as a gardener. After everything, after eating four kings and a kingdom's worth of souls, some splinter of Aurelian remained, kneeling forever in the rows, telling itself the lie it had died telling: *I am not finished. Nothing that is tended truly ends.*

He had been afraid. That was the whole of it, the seed the whole terrible orchard grew from. He had been afraid of ending, and he had been a gardener, and gardeners know better than anyone that everything ends — that knowing it is the price of the work, that you plant for hands that will never be yours, and the planting is the love and the ending is the honesty and refusing either is refusing both.

He had refused both. And a hundred years later, a king wore his fear like a crown, and a valley full of buried people bloomed out of season in the dark.

"He should have let the frost come," Paloma said aloud. Her voice sounded very small under the whispering tree. "He should have pruned for winter and trusted the spring to someone else."

The garden went still. All of it — the humming lavender, the whispering leaves, the murmur of the black pool — every voice in every bed stopped at once, the silence of a room where someone has overheard themselves described.

Then, from the deepest bed, from soil that had not been turned in a hundred years, a single shoot broke the surface. Pale as bone, trembling. Growing toward her.

Toward the truth she had just said.

Paloma watched it rise, and understood, with a cartographer's cold clarity, that she had just done the one thing no one in a hundred years had managed to do in this place: she had named him honestly, in his own house, and the garden — which grew toward truths refused — had no choice but to grow toward her.

*He knows,* she thought, and the thought arrived already cold. *Some remnant of him knows I'm here, and knows I have his name.*

The faces on the tree began, very softly, to open their eyes.

* * *

She gathered the map with shaking hands and ran — up the terraces, through the tired gate, down the yew corridor past forty-one crowned topiaries, while behind her the garden woke the way a body wakes from a nightmare, in stages, with gathering panic. By the time she burst through the bramble-door into the grey dawn of the outer valley, the book against her ribs was burning hot enough to sting through cloth, its final pages curling, and far off across the valley, from the direction of the Pale Court, horns had begun to sound.

Beatrix was already running toward her through the dew, sword drawn, terror and relief fighting openly on her face. Behind her, Serah limped and swore. Behind *them*, on the horizon, the sky above the palace had gone the color of a bruise.

"Paloma—"

"I have it." She grabbed Beatrix's arms, felt her own voice come out steadier than she had any right to expect. "Beatrix, I have his name. Aurelian. He was a gardener, and he was afraid, and he's been pruning us for a hundred years." She swallowed. "But he knows I know. And the book — there are maybe ten pages left."

Beatrix looked at the smoke threading from Paloma's satchel. Then at the bruised sky. Then at Paloma, and something settled in her face — the look of a woman stepping onto ground she has decided to trust.

"Then we're out of time," she said. "Tell me everything on the way."

They ran for the Pale Court together, and the garden behind them bloomed — wildly, terribly, furiously — after.
