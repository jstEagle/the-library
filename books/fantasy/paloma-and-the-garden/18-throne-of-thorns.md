## 18. Throne of Thorns

The Pale Court had no doors anymore. Serah had seen to that.

She stood in the shattered archway with her shield raised and her sword drawn, and behind her the corridor beyond was already filling with soldiers — not the hollow-eyed palace guard Beatrix had expected, but men and women weeping as they advanced, wearing the faces of Serah's dead comrades, of her father, of a boy she must have loved once, because when he called her name her whole body flinched like a struck bell.

"Go," Serah said, without turning around. "Whatever you hear behind you — whatever *I* say — keep walking."

Beatrix gripped her shoulder once, hard, the way you gripped a comrade before a pass you might not both walk out of. "Hold the door."

"There is no door." Serah smiled, and it was the first true smile Beatrix had seen from her, crooked and fierce and entirely alive. "So I'll be the door."

Then they were through, and the throne room swallowed them.

* * *

It was larger than a room had any right to be. The ceiling vanished into a darkness that was not shadow but absence, a place where light went to be forgotten. The walls were living thorn — black briar grown through marble, through gold, through the bones of the building itself, and every branch was hung with small pale blossoms that opened as they passed, turning to follow them like eyes.

Paloma's breath came fast beside her. She had the map out, the impossible map, ink still drying on vellum that should have been full a hundred years ago. In its center, where the garden's heart should have been, she'd written a single word in letters that shifted when Beatrix tried to read them directly: the crown-spirit's true name, mapped the way Paloma mapped everything — patiently, precisely, with love for the shape of a thing.

"Don't look at it yet," Paloma murmured. "Not until you're ready to speak it. The moment you read it aloud, it knows."

"And until then?"

"Until then it only suspects." Paloma's hand found hers, brief and grounding, calluses against calluses. "Beatrix. Whatever it shows you in there — I'll be saying your name. Listen for my voice. Promise me."

"I promise."

The thorns parted.

* * *

King Aldric sat upon a throne that had grown around him rather than been built beneath him, briar fused to flesh at wrist and throat and temple, pale blossoms crowning his head in mockery of gold. He was beautiful the way winter is beautiful, the way a frozen lake is beautiful — all surface, all stillness, and underneath, something drowning.

His eyes were wrong. They had always been wrong, Beatrix realized now — not empty, but *occupied*, lit from behind by a patience so old it had forgotten how to want anything except continuation.

"Well," said the thing wearing the king, and its voice was Aldric's voice layered over something vast and dry, like wind through a dead orchard. "The swordspeaker comes home at last. And the little mapmaker, too. I've been tasting you both since you crossed the valley line."

Beatrix drew breath to speak the name.

The spirit spoke first.

"You were four years old," it said gently, "the first time you held your mother's sword. Do you remember? Of course you don't. But I do. I remember everything this valley has ever refused to remember. Shall I show you?"

The throne room folded.

* * *

Suddenly Beatrix was small. The kitchen of the Ravensworth hold smelled of bread and woodsmoke, and her mother was laughing — actually laughing, an unguarded sound Beatrix had heard perhaps five times in her life before the oath took her — and her mother was setting the sword in her small hands and saying, *someday this will be yours, if you choose it, and only if you choose it.*

"That never happened," Beatrix whispered.

"It happened exactly so," the spirit said, everywhere now, in the thorn-blossoms, in the stones, in her own heartbeat. "And here is what came after."

Her mother on the mountain road. Her mother turning back for the last survivor of the caravan raid, because the oath demanded it, because a Ravensworth does not count the cost. Her mother's blood on the snow, and seven-year-old Beatrix holding her hand while it went cold, and the oath settling onto her shoulders like a yoke fitted for a woman twice her size.

"You could have it differently," the spirit murmured, and the vision rippled, and her mother turned back one road sooner, and lived, and grew old, and taught Beatrix songs instead of sword-forms, and the kitchen filled with grandchildren—

"Stop."

"Or this." The vision swirled again. Her sister. Wren, walking into the pass alone because Beatrix had renounced the oath and someone had to carry the mail-run through, and Wren coming back — *coming back*, alive, waving, calling her name — because in this version Beatrix had gone with her, had never let her go alone, and Wren was laughing and whole and there was no scar on Beatrix's conscience shaped exactly like her sister—

"*Stop.*"

"Everything you ever wanted," the spirit said, soft as snowfall, "and all it costs is the name in the mapmaker's hand. Forget it. Unlearn it. Sit on my throne beside me and be loved, Beatrix Ravensworth, be *known*, be part of something that never ends—"

That was the trap, and Beatrix saw it with terrible clarity even as her feet took one step toward the throne without her permission. It didn't want to kill her. Killing was waste. It wanted her to *stay* — to set down the self that hurt and accept a curated one, the way it had curated Aldric for a century, kinder in public, emptier in private, until nothing remained but a smiling shell with a crown grown through it.

One more step. Two. The blossoms were singing now, her mother's laugh, Wren's voice, a hundred warm rooms—

"*BEATRIX.*"

Paloma's voice cut through it like a blade through silk.

* * *

Paloma stood at the edge of the folding visions with the map held up between both hands, and she was reading aloud — not the spirit's name, not yet, but coordinates. Anchors. *The waystation tavern, the third candle from the left, the wine Beatrix called ambitious and secretly liked. The mountain road under stars arranged wrong. The garden, blooming out of season, growing toward truths people refuse to say.*

"The truth," Paloma was saying, steady and clear, "is that your mother died and it broke you. The truth is that Wren almost died and it broke you worse. The truth is that you ran from the oath for fifteen years and you were right to be afraid—"

Each sentence landed like a stone dropped into the spirit's warm water, and the visions shuddered.

"—and you chose to come back anyway. Not because fate made you. Because *I asked you to*, and you said yes, and nobody wrote that in any book."

Beatrix stopped walking toward the throne.

She stood in the ruined kitchen-vision with her mother's ghost-laugh dying around her, and she looked at the warmth being offered — endless, effortless, painless — and she understood at last what her grandmother's letters had meant. The last swordspeaker hadn't failed to break the oath. She had failed to break it *honestly*. She had tried to trade it away, and the spirit had eaten her wanting.

Beatrix turned her back on the throne.

"No," she said, and her voice came out rough and real and hers. "You can keep the version where she lives. It isn't worth what it costs. Nothing warm is, if I have to stop being me to hold it."

The throne room screamed.

* * *

The thorn-blossoms all closed at once, and the false dark rushed in, and the spirit dropped its gentleness like a mask. The pressure of it was physical — a century of hoarded selves pressing against Beatrix's mind, prying at the seams of her, hunting for the loose thread of her identity to pull. She felt it find the place where the oath lived, the root-system her grandmother had described, and it *gripped*.

"Then I'll take the name out of your mouth," it said, and began to wear her.

For one vertiginous moment Beatrix was looking at herself from outside — a tired woman with sword-marks on her hands, standing in a garden of thorns, small and mortal and about to fail — and the spirit poured itself into the shape of her, trying on her voice, her memories, her grief, sorting through her like a thief through a house.

But a thief searches for valuables, and Beatrix's house was full of things the spirit couldn't price. The smell of Paloma's ink. The weight of her grandmother's rhymes. The specific, unglamorous courage of getting up afraid, every day, for fifteen years, without prophecy or purpose, just because the roads needed keeping.

It couldn't wear her. There was nothing in her that wanted to stop being herself.

"Paloma," Beatrix gasped. "Now. Give it to me."

Paloma was already moving, map held high, walking straight through the collapsing visions as though they were weather. Ink crawled across the vellum, redrawing itself in frantic spirals, converging on the center word. She reached Beatrix and pressed the map into her hands, and their fingers overlapped on the vellum, and Paloma looked at her — not at the spirit, not at the throne, just at her, ordinary and certain.

"Read it," Paloma said. "I've got you. Read it, and I'll hold the thread."

Beatrix read the name.

* * *

It was not a word so much as a wound — a name that tasted of topsoil and grave-dirt and the first green shoot through ash, a gardener-king's name spoken backward through a century of stolen lives. She felt it take hold of her tongue like a hook.

And the spirit *heard*.

The throne room came apart. Briar lashed from the walls; the blossoms opened all at once and shrieked with a hundred stolen voices; the dark above descended like a lid. And through it all the spirit threw everything it had left at the woman speaking its ending — every face it had ever eaten, every king it had worn, a century of the beloved dead crying out in voices Beatrix knew from stories, from Serah's grief, from her own mother's memory—

She kept reading. Her voice cracked and she kept reading. Blood ran from her nose and she kept reading. And through the chaos, steady as a lighthouse, Paloma's voice wove around her own:

*"Thornfell tavern, third candle. Your hand on mine. The road home. The road home. The road home—"*

The name reached its final syllable.

The spirit lunged — not at Beatrix, but past her, at Aldric, pouring itself back into its last vessel, into the man on the throne, flooding him, drowning him one final time, because if it could not persist in the world it would at least persist in *him*, would seal itself inside the last body it had worn and go down holding on—

Aldric's eyes cleared.

For one instant — one naked, human instant — the king looked out from behind his own face, and his mouth moved, shaping words with no air behind them, and Beatrix read his lips across the ruined hall:

*Let go. Let me go. Please.*

* * *

The name finished.

Light did not fall so much as *return* — rising up out of the floor, out of the roots, out of the buried memories the spirit had fed on for a hundred years, and the briar throne ignited in green fire that did not burn. The spirit came apart like smoke in wind, like frost in sun, and in its last moment Beatrix felt it reach for her one final time — not to take, but in the simple animal terror of a thing that has existed too long to remember how to end.

*I only wanted to keep going,* it seemed to say, in a voice suddenly small, suddenly a gardener's voice, suddenly a man's. *Was that so much?*

"Yes," Beatrix said, gently. "It was."

And it was gone.

* * *

The silence afterward was total. The thorns crumbled to black dust. The false dark lifted, and through the broken ceiling the stars of a century ago looked down, arranged wrong and beautiful.

Aldric slid from the ruins of the throne.

Beatrix caught him before he hit the ground — she was moving before she knew she was moving, sword-forgotten, oath-forgotten, catching a dying king like catching any falling man. He weighed almost nothing. A century of being worn hollow.

"Is she safe?" he whispered. His eyes were his own now, and they were already going dim. "Serah. Is she—"

"She held the door," Beatrix said. "She's alive. You're free, Aldric. You're free."

Something like a smile. His hand fumbled at his palm — the child's handwriting, the last honest act — and he pressed it flat against his chest as though holding a door shut from the inside.

"Tell her... tell her the boy who..." His breath caught. "Tell her I remembered. At the end. Tell her I..."

He didn't finish. He didn't need to. The light in his eyes went out the way a candle goes out when a hand cups it — gently, deliberately, *chosen* — and King Aldric, freed at last after a hundred years, exhaled once and was no one's vessel ever again.

Behind them, far off down the corridor, Beatrix heard the clash of steel stop, and then footsteps running — Serah, alive, coming fast, coming too late for her king but in time for whatever came next.

Paloma knelt beside her in the green-lit ruin, and took her hand, and neither of them said anything at all.

Above the broken throne, where the briar had burned away, something new was growing — pale shoots pushing up through royal stone, reaching for the honest stars.

And in Beatrix's satchel, the book turned another page to ash.
