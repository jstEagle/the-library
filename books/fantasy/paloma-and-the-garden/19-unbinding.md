## 19. Unbinding

The throne room smelled of roses and rot.

Paloma noticed it first — that wrongness, that sweet decay threaded through the perfume of a hundred cut blooms arranged in towering vases around the dais. Fresh flowers for a dead man's court. The petals were white as bone and just as cold, and where they had begun to wilt, no one had thought to replace them. No one here thought of anything anymore except the thing on the throne.

Aldric sat beneath the crown like a hand pressed over a candle flame.

He had been handsome once — Serah had told them so in the hiding dark, her voice cracking on the word *once* — broad-shouldered and quick to laugh, a king who walked his own walls. What remained was the shape of him. His skin had gone the grey of old parchment, stretched fine across cheekbones that might have belonged to a saint or a starving man. His eyes were open. They were not looking at anything. They were looking *through* everything, through Paloma, through Beatrix beside her with the sword bare and singing low in her grip, through the banners and the braziers and the long fall of years, into some country only the crown could see.

And his shadow — Paloma made herself look at it, though every instinct screamed not to — his shadow moved a half-second behind him.

It moved *wrong*. When Aldric's fingers twitched against the armrest, the shadow's fingers were still. When the shadow finally caught up, it lingered, savoring, the way a tongue lingers over the last taste of something.

"Stay behind me," Beatrix murmured. Her voice was steady, but Paloma could feel the tremor underneath it, felt it through their joined hands before Beatrix pulled free to hold the sword two-handed. "Whatever happens. Whatever you see me do or say or *become* — you hold onto the map. You anchor me. Promise me."

"I promise."

"Even if I forget your name. Even if I forget mine."

Paloma's throat closed. She nodded instead of speaking, because speaking would have broken something she needed whole.

Serah lay by the door they had come through, one hand pressed to the wound in her side, her sword across her knees. She had bought them this far with blood — hers, freely given, the shield's oldest vow — and now she watched the throne with the expression of a woman who had loved two kings: the boy who had been, and the thing that wore him, and did not know which grave to weep at.

The doors boomed shut behind them. The sound did not echo. In this room, sound simply *ended*, as if the walls drank it.

Then the crown-spirit spoke.

It came from everywhere and nowhere — from the roses, from the stones, from the king's grey lips without moving them. It was a voice like roots growing through a coffin, patient and green and hungry.

*Two names written in a burning book. One who speaks swords. One who maps the unmappable. You have come far to bring me such gifts.*

"We didn't bring them," Beatrix said, and her voice rang true, oath-tempered, and the air itself seemed to lean toward her. "We came to take something back."

*Take.* The spirit tasted the word. *Everything in this kingdom is already taken. Every self, every secret, every summer afternoon some child spent laughing under a tree that has since forgotten the child's name. I have eaten a century of this valley, little speaker. Your grandmother's stories — I ate those too. Her mother's oaths. The garden grew fat on what I discarded, and now its fruit walks into my hall and offers itself.*

"It doesn't offer itself," Paloma said.

Her voice surprised her — small in that vast ending-place, but clear. She stepped forward, past the point where Beatrix would have stopped her if Beatrix hadn't been holding the line with her whole body, and she held up the map.

She had finished it in the garden's heart while the world burned at its edges, ink still wet in places, drawn in the old cartographer's script that lived in her hands deeper than memory. It showed Thornfell entire — the mountain, the valley, the pale court, and beneath all of it, threaded through every root and stone, the garden. And at the center of the map, where a cartographer would place a capital or a compass rose, she had drawn the crown.

Not the gold circlet on Aldric's brow. The *true* crown. The name beneath the name.

*I know that mark,* the spirit said, and for the first time there was something in its voice besides hunger. Something thin. Old. Almost — almost — afraid.

"Yes," Paloma said. "You do."

She had learned the garden's rule well: *it grows toward truths people refuse to say.* For a hundred years the spirit had refused to say its own. It had buried its beginning so deep beneath a century of stolen selves that even it, perhaps, no longer remembered what it was. But the garden remembered. The garden kept everything the crown threw away, and in its deepest bed, grown from the oldest discarded memory of all, a single plant had bloomed for Paloma alone — a nightshade-white flower whose petals unfolded to show her the truth in letters of pollen and shadow:

*A gardener. A gardener-king who loved his gardens more than his life, and when death came for him as it comes for everyone, he refused. He poured himself into his crown of office and told his successors it was blessing, and he stayed, and stayed, and stayed, until staying became eating, and eating became everything he was.*

"You began as a man," Paloma said to the throne, to the shadow, to the thing between them. "You began as a man who was afraid to end."

The silence that followed was the loudest thing Paloma had ever heard.

Then the room *moved* — not the walls but the light, the very air curdling — and the spirit's patience broke like ice over deep water. The braziers flared green. The roses turned their faces toward Paloma all at once, a hundred white eyes. The king rose from the throne, or the shadow rose wearing the king, and his voice when it came used Aldric's mouth and it was terrible because it was gentle:

*Little mapper. I can give you every map never drawn. I can show you coastlines that drowned before your grandmother's grandmother drew breath. I will make you the greatest cartographer in the history of paper, and you will never be lost again — never lost, never uncertain, never small. Is that not what you have wanted your whole careful life? To matter? To be found?*

Paloma felt it slide against her like cold silk. It knew. Of course it knew; it had eaten a century of wanting. It knew about the girl who had copied other people's maps because her own were never good enough, who had bought an antique book to impress a woman she liked, who had spent her whole life charting places in the hope that somewhere, someone would chart *her*.

For one shining, poisonous second, she saw it: her name in every atlas ever bound. Her lines on every coast.

Then she felt the weight in her satchel. Not the map — the seeds. The handful she had taken from the garden's ordinary beds, plain thyme and marigold and moss, the stubborn unglamorous things that grew without prophecy. Beatrix's laugh, rough with sleep. The way Beatrix had looked at her across a tavern table an hour before the book tore them out of the world — nervous, hopeful, *choosing*.

"No," Paloma said. "I want to be found by *people*, not by power. And I'm already found."

The spirit screamed without sound, and the king lunged —

— and stopped.

Stopped mid-stride, one grey hand outstretched, and his face changed.

It was like watching frost reverse. The wrongness drained out of his eyes and something flooded in behind it, something exhausted and young and drowning, and Aldric — the real Aldric, the boy Serah had sworn to, buried ninety years beneath the tenant in his own skull — surfaced like a man breaking water after a long time under.

His gaze found Paloma. Focused. Wept without changing expression, tears cutting clean tracks down the parchment skin.

"*Palm*," he rasped. "My — my palm —"

Beatrix moved toward him, sword rising. Paloma caught her arm.

"He's asking," she breathed. "*Look.*"

She crossed the last few feet to the dais alone. Up close, the king smelled of winter and old paper. His hand trembled against the armrest, curled half-shut, and Paloma took it as gently as taking a bird, and turned it over.

There, scrawled across the lifeline in shaky, childish letters, in ink gone brown with age — ink that had to be ninety years old, ink that had survived everything the crown had done to him because he had written it in the one place his captor never thought to look, inside his own skin, inside the self it fed on —

*REMEMBER ME. I AM STILL HERE. END IT KINDLY IF YOU CAN.*

His last honest act. A boy-king, in his first year of captivity, writing a message to whoever might someday stand close enough to read it. Ninety years of patience. Ninety years of hoping.

"Oh," Paloma said. Softly. The way you speak to something small. "Oh, we remember. We're here. We see you."

His fingers closed weakly around hers.

"*Kindly*," he whispered. "If — if you can. Tell Serah — tell her the boy she — the boy is sorry. Tell her he tried. Tell her—" His breath hitched, and for a moment the shadow at his feet went utterly still, listening, and Paloma understood with a lurch that the spirit was listening too, that this was dangerous, that every word cost him. "Tell her I chose. At the end. Tell her I *chose*."

"You'll tell her yourself," Paloma said, and hated the lie even as she spoke it, because kindness sometimes wears a lie's face. "But yes. We'll tell her you chose."

Behind her, Beatrix raised the sword.

The blade had been waking since the pass, humming its low oath-note, and now it sang outright — a sound like dawn breaking over stone, like her grandmother's rhymes, like every Ravensworth who had ever stood between the innocent and the devouring. Green fire crawled the fuller of the blade, garden-light, memory-light, and Paloma saw her wife-to-be — no, she corrected herself, grinning wildly even now, her *almost* — saw Beatrix standing straighter than Paloma had ever seen anyone stand, rooted, chosen, *herself*.

"Spirit of the crown," Beatrix said, and her voice filled the room the way the garden filled the valley. "Once a gardener. Once a man. Once *afraid*. I know your name now, and the garden knows it, and I speak it back to the dark where you've hidden it all these hungry years."

She drew breath. Paloma spread the map wide between them, anchoring, grounding, holding the present like a rope thrown across a chasm, and met Beatrix's eyes, and nodded.

"*Witherroot*," Beatrix said. "*First of the name. Gardener of the Pale Court. Man who would not end. WITHERROOT — be ended.*"

The name struck the room like the first day of spring striking a frozen river.

The spirit rose out of the king all at once — a great green-dark shape like a tree grown backwards, roots reaching, branches grasping, a century of stolen faces flickering across its bark — and it *screamed*, and the scream was a thousand voices, all the kings and queens and servants and children it had worn and emptied, crying out as their fragments tore loose and remembered themselves.

*NO — I am the kingdom — I AM the spring — without me you are only — only —*

"Only people," Beatrix said. "Ending. Like everyone should get to."

And Paloma held the map, and the map held the truth, and the truth held Beatrix steady while she spoke the name again, and again, each repetition quieter and more certain, a door opening rather than a wall falling — until the spirit could not hold its shape against being *known*, against being *named*, against the unbearable mercy of being understood and released anyway.

It looked at Paloma, in the last moment. Through the tearing, through the dissolution, something ancient and exhausted regarded her with a gardener's eyes.

*The beds,* it whispered. *Someone must tend the beds.*

And then it came apart — not destroyed but *dispersed*, unraveling into motes of green-gold light that poured out through the high windows and down over the valley, down into the earth, into the garden it had starved for a century, going home to the roots at last. The roses in their vases flushed from bone-white to living pink as the light passed through them. Somewhere far below, Paloma knew, every memory-plant in the hidden valley was blooming at once, fed by a century of returned selves.

The crown fell from Aldric's brow and rang on the stone like a bell rung once for a funeral.

The king collapsed, and Beatrix caught him before he hit the ground, and they lowered him together, and Paloma knelt and held his hand — the one with the writing — and his shadow lay still beneath him for the first time in ninety years, moving exactly when he moved, belonging to him, only him, at the end.

"Serah," he said. His voice was a thread now, human, small. "Is she — did we win? Did the boy —"

"She's here." Paloma's vision blurred. "She's right here."

They carried him to the doors, and Serah dragged herself up on her sword to meet him, and the old shield took her king's hand in both of hers, and whatever passed between them passed in silence — ninety years of it, and forgiveness, and grief, arriving all together like weather.

He died at sunset, they learned later, in the rose garden, asking for the sun on his face and getting it. He died *empty* in the best sense: nothing left in him but Aldric, and Aldric was enough. He smiled at the sky. He said something to Serah that made her laugh and cry at once, and then he was done, kindly, as he had asked.

But that was later.

In the throne room, in the ringing quiet after the crown fell, Paloma sat on the cold stone with the most important map of her life spread useless beneath her hands, and Beatrix knelt and gathered her in, sword still humming, and neither of them said anything for a long time. There was nothing to fix. There was nothing to fight. There was only the strange, raw openness of a future with no verse in it at all.

"The book," Beatrix said at last, hoarse.

Paloma reached for her satchel — and stopped.

Through the leather, faint but unmistakable, came a smell of smoke. And when she drew the old prophecy out and opened it, the final pages were curling, browning, burning backward from the last leaf inward — the prophecy consuming itself now that its work was done, erasing the road behind them page by page.

Except — the fire paused. Hovered. On the very last page, above the smudged verse, new ink was surfacing, slow and deliberate, letters rising like something written underwater coming up for air:

*What the garden gave, the garden may ask back. One seed remains unplanted. Choose before the fold closes.*

Paloma read it twice. Then she looked at Beatrix, and Beatrix looked at the page, and outside the high windows the first honest stars in a century were coming out over Thornfell.

"There's one more choice," Paloma said quietly. "And I think it's ours."
