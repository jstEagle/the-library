## 21. Two Roads Home

The fold between centuries did not look like anything.

That was the first thing Paloma noticed, and the thing she would remember longest: after all of it — the burning book, the throne of thorns, the king who had finally been allowed to die — the door home was not a door at all. It was a place where the world simply stopped insisting on itself. The mountain path they stood on continued for another dozen paces and then grew uncertain, its stones fading like a sketch the artist had abandoned mid-line. Beyond it, the air shimmered with a quality Paloma had no word for, though her whole life had been an education in naming things. It was not light. It was not mist. It was the visual equivalent of a held breath.

She had seen it before, from the other side. A hundred years ago — or one evening ago, depending on how you counted, and Paloma had given up counting — this same fold had opened beneath them in a tavern called the Waystation Rest, and the room had folded inward like a closing map, and they had fallen into a century that was never supposed to hold them.

Now it waited again. And nothing pulled.

"Do you feel that?" Beatrix asked quietly.

Paloma turned to look at her. Beatrix Ravensworth stood with her weight on her back foot, the way she always did when she wasn't sure whether she wanted to stay or run — except that now there was no sword humming at her hip, because the sword had gone to ash along with everything else, dissolved into the garden it had spent a hundred years defending. Her hands were empty. Paloma had never seen them empty. They looked strange and beautiful that way, scarred and open, hanging at her sides like something finally unclenched.

"I feel it," Paloma said. "Or rather — I don't."

"That's what I mean." Beatrix's voice had a raw edge to it, the voice of someone who had screamed a true name into the face of an immortal hunger only hours ago and hadn't yet found her way back to ordinary speech. "There's no pull. Before, I could feel it — like a hook behind my sternum. Like the book knew where we were supposed to be and was reeling us in. Now—" She lifted one hand, turned it over, studied the palm as if expecting verse to bloom there. "Nothing. It's just air."

"Just air," Paloma repeated, and heard the wonder in her own voice.

Because that was the thing. That was the impossible, terrifying, magnificent thing. Whatever happened next was unwritten.

She was a cartographer. She had spent her entire adult life believing — no, *knowing* — that every place existed in relation to other places, that a map was a web of obligations, that you could not stand anywhere without standing somewhere's north of somewhere else. Her whole craft was the science of consequence: this river feeds that valley, this pass determines that war, this road exists because someone, once, refused to go around. She had thought fate was just cartography at scale. Lines drawn by something older and less careful than she was, connecting her to Beatrix across a hundred years of ink.

And now the lines were gone. The page was blank. She stood at the edge of a fold between centuries with no pull in either direction, and the sensation was so close to grief that she had to breathe through it slowly, hand pressed flat below her ribs.

"Paloma." Beatrix had come closer without her noticing; she did that, moved like smoke when she wanted to. "Talk to me. You've got your mapping face on."

"My mapping face?"

"The one where you're redrawing the world in your head and don't like what the world is doing to interrupt you."

Paloma laughed — a small, cracked sound, but real. "I'm trying to find my bearings. Professionally speaking. There are none. Do you understand what that means? There are *no bearings*. No fixed points. The book burned. The prophecy closed. Aldric is free and dead and mourned, and the garden is going to sleep for another century, and Serah is down there holding a kingdom together with both hands and a sworn shield's stubbornness, and none of it — none of it — needs us anymore." She spread her arms, gesturing at the shimmering nothing ahead of them, the unfinished path, the stars above Thornfell that were still arranged wrong, still the stars of a year that had ended long ago. "We're off the map, Beatrix. Completely. For the first time in either of our lives, nobody wrote us here."

Beatrix was quiet for a long moment. Wind came up the mountainside, smelling of pine and cold stone and, faintly, impossibly, of roses — the garden's scent clinging to them still, woven into their hair and clothes like a blessing neither of them had asked for.

"When I was small," Beatrix said at last, "my grandmother used to tell me about the roads. The smuggler roads. She'd sing the rhymes and say, *these paths were made by people who weren't allowed to walk the proper ones.* Everything official belonged to the crown and the lords and the tax collectors. So people who had nothing made their own ways. Over scree. Through ravines. Under the roots of things." She looked down at her empty hands. "I used to think the oath was the opposite of that. The oath was the proper road, the one written down, the one you were bound to whether you chose it or not. That's why I hated it. That's why I ran from it for twenty years." She looked up, and her eyes were wet and fierce all at once. "But tonight I spoke it freely. Nobody made me. The book was ash before the last word left my mouth. And it still worked. It worked *better*."

"Because it was yours," Paloma said softly.

"Because it was mine." Beatrix exhaled, long and shaking. "So here's what I know about unwritten places, Paloma Idris. People who aren't allowed to walk the proper ones make their own. And we—" her voice caught, steadied— "we're standing in the biggest unwritten place there's ever been."

* * *

They sat down at the edge of the fold, because some conversations should not be had standing, and because Paloma's legs had finally informed her that she had been running, fighting, grieving, and mapping for what felt like several lifetimes and was, technically, three days.

From her satchel she took out the maps. Most of them were ruined — scorched at the edges where the book's fire had leapt, soaked with the mud of the valley, annotated in her own increasingly frantic hand as the garden had redrawn itself faster than she could follow. But the last one, the true map, the map of the garden's heart with the crown-spirit's name inscribed across it in letters that had already begun to fade now that their work was done — that one she smoothed carefully across her knees, weighting the corners with stones.

It was beautiful. She could admit that without vanity. Every line of it had been earned.

"What will you do with it?" Beatrix asked.

"Burn it, probably. Eventually." Paloma traced the fading name with one fingertip. "Some maps are weapons. This one's a weapon pointed at something that doesn't exist anymore. But not yet. Not until I've copied out the parts worth keeping. The garden's rules. The way memory grows toward truth. Serah will want those, if she's going to rebuild. If anyone plants anything in that valley again, they should know what they're planting into."

"And the seeds?"

Paloma reached into the inner pocket of her coat, where she'd sewn a little pouch from a strip of her spare shirt lining. Inside: two dozen seeds, small and dark and unremarkable, gathered from the garden's outer rows in the last hour before they climbed. Memory-plants, grown from buried truths. She had no idea what they would become in the present day, in real soil, under right-arranged stars. Perhaps nothing. Perhaps ordinary flowers. Perhaps something that would make whoever planted them suddenly, unbearably honest.

"They're not for me," she said. "I don't think I get to decide what they grow into. I just — couldn't leave them all behind. It felt like letting the garden die twice."

Beatrix nodded slowly. Then she reached into her own pocket and brought out her contribution to the future: a scrap of parchment, creased and soft with handling. Paloma recognized it even before she saw the childlike scrawl — the words King Aldric had written on his own palm, his last honest act, the message he'd hidden inside himself for years hoping someone would read it: *end it. please. i am so tired. i remember my mother's garden.*

"You kept it," Paloma said.

"Somebody should." Beatrix's jaw tightened. "He was a person. Everyone keeps saying *the king died* like the man was just a coat the spirit wore. He wasn't. He held on for sixty years inside that thing. Sixty years, writing notes to himself in the dark." She folded the scrap away again, gently, the way you'd fold away a letter from someone you loved and lost. "Serah's going to put it in the histories. Not the official ones. The real ones. The ones smugglers keep."

"Speaking of Serah." Paloma glanced back down the mountain, toward the valley where torchlight had begun to gather — the first lights of a kingdom waking up from a hundred-year dream, confused and grieving and, for the first time in living memory, *itself*. "She made us promise."

"She made *me* promise. You she asked nicely."

"She asked me to draw her a new map of the valley. One that shows where the garden sleeps, so nobody plows it up by accident." Paloma smiled despite the ache in her chest. "I told her I would. I meant it. Which means—"

"Which means we can't just walk through the fold and pretend this century never happened."

"No. It means we owe a debt to a woman who took a mace to the shoulder for us." Paloma's throat tightened. Serah was alive — the healers said she would live — but changed, the way everyone was changed, carrying the memory of a king she'd served and failed and finally freed. "We go back down tomorrow. We finish what we promised. And then—"

"And then the fold."

"And then the fold."

They let the silence sit between them for a while. It was a good silence, the kind Paloma had learned to recognize over three days and one impossible prophecy as the particular silence of being understood. Below them the valley lights multiplied. Above them the wrong stars wheeled, indifferent and ancient. Somewhere behind them, in a hollow beneath a hillside, a garden full of other people's discarded selves slept on, dreaming the dreams its eaters never got to finish.

"Can I say something selfish?" Beatrix asked.

"You've earned at least three selfish things."

"In the throne room. When the spirit had me. When it was offering—" she stopped, swallowed. Paloma remembered. The spirit had worn Beatrix's own longing back at her: belonging, family, a sister alive again, a mother proud instead of broken, a life where the oath had never cost anything. It had offered her everything she'd ever wanted, gift-wrapped in her own voice. "When it offered me my sister back. Alive. Real. It felt real. It *was* real, in the way the garden is real — grown from a memory, watered with a truth. And for one moment I wanted it more than I wanted to breathe." She shook her head. "What got me out wasn't courage. It was you. Your hand on the map, your voice saying my name — my actual name, not the one the spirit was using. You anchored me to a present tense. To a self that actually exists." She turned, and in the starlight her face was bare in a way Paloma had rarely seen it, all the guarded competence stripped down to the person underneath. "I don't want to go home to a life where you're not in it. Whatever unwritten thing comes next. I want it to have you in it."

Paloma's heart did something complicated and painful and wonderful.

"Beatrix Ravensworth," she said, "are you proposing we date? Again? As in, continue the date that started this entire catastrophe?"

A laugh broke out of Beatrix like water through a dam — helpless, hiccupping, half-sob. "The worst first date in recorded history. Yes. That's — yes. Exactly that."

"For the record, I bought you a book. A *gift*. To impress you."

"It worked."

"It named us both and dragged us a hundred years into the past!"

"It worked," Beatrix repeated, grinning through tears now, and reached over and took Paloma's hand — her ink-stained, thorn-scratched, mapmaker's hand — and laced their fingers together with the deliberate care of someone fastening a buckle on armor, or tying off a rope, or making a knot designed to hold. "Paloma. I need to say this properly, because if the last three days taught me anything, it's that unsaid truths grow into monsters. I choose you. Not because a book told me to. Not because a prophecy arranged us like furniture. The book is ash. The prophecy is closed. There is no destiny left with any claim on either of us — and I am standing in the middle of all that freedom, and every direction I look, I want the one with you in it. That's the whole speech. That's all of it. Freely given, freely meant."

Paloma discovered she was crying, which was fine, because so was Beatrix, and because the wind coming off the fold was cold enough to excuse it anyway.

"Then here's mine," she said. "I spent my whole life drawing other people's worlds. Places I'd never see, for patrons I'd never meet, according to rules I didn't write. I thought that was humility. It turns out it was just fear with good penmanship." She squeezed Beatrix's hand. "You asked me once why I agreed to a first date with a smuggler's guide I'd met exactly twice. I told you it was because you had interesting scars and better stories. That was true. But the real reason is that when you talked about the mountains, you talked about them like they were *worth knowing*. Not territory. Not terrain. Worth knowing. And I thought — I want to be known like that. By someone like that." She laughed wetly. "I got my wish in the most catastrophic possible way. But Beatrix — freely, with no book, no verse, no pull in any direction — I choose you too. Whichever road. Both roads. The one home and whatever comes after."

Above them, the wrong stars kept wheeling. Ahead of them, the fold breathed its held breath. And between them, for the first time since a candle guttered in a tavern called the Waystation Rest, there was nothing pulling them anywhere at all — which meant, Paloma realized with a joy so large it frightened her, that wherever they went next, they would be going there *on purpose*.

* * *

They went down to the valley at dawn.

The kingdom of Thornfell-as-it-was woke around them like a patient surfacing from anesthesia: doors opening, voices rising, people stepping outside and staring at the sky as if seeing it for the first time — which, in a sense, they were. A baker stood in her doorway with flour to her elbows, weeping without knowing why. An old soldier polished a breastplate he'd forgotten he owned. Children ran through streets their grandparents' grandparents had walked, shouting names that had come back overnight, names that had been eaten and were now returned.

At the foot of the palace steps, wrapped in a sling with her shield-arm bound, Serah waited for them. She looked ten years older than she had two days ago and twenty years lighter.

"You're leaving," she said. Not a question. She'd seen them looking at the mountain, at the place where the air went uncertain.

"Tomorrow, at moonrise," Beatrix said. "Tonight we finish your maps."

Serah nodded. Then, awkwardly, with her good arm, she embraced them both at once — a crushing, sworn-shield embrace that smelled of steel and woodsmoke and grief. "The histories will remember you," she said into their shoulders. "The real ones. The smugglers' rhymes will have verses about you within a year, mark me. Two women and a burning book."

"Make sure they get the details right," Paloma managed, muffled. "Especially the part where I did all the difficult intellectual work."

"And I hit things," Beatrix added.

"And she hit things," Serah agreed solemnly, and then her voice cracked, and she held on a moment longer. "He'd have liked you both. The man underneath. He liked gardens."

That night, Paloma drew the last map of the old century by lantern-light while Beatrix sat beside her sharpening a borrowed knife against a borrowed stone, because some habits survive the death of prophecies. She marked the sleeping garden. She marked the fold. She marked, in a corner where only cartographers would ever think to look, a tiny pair of crossed lines and a single word: *chosen.*

At moonrise they climbed.

The fold waited where it had always waited, patient as weather. The unfinished path faded into held breath. Behind them lay a valley full of returning names and a grave with a king in it, finally, truly, at rest. Ahead lay a tavern, a candle stub, a century of distance, and absolutely nothing telling them what to do.

Beatrix stopped at the edge and looked back once. Paloma watched her take in the whole of it — the wrong stars, the sleeping garden, the kingdom remembering itself — and then turn away from it, deliberately, the way you close a door on a room you love because the house you're building is elsewhere.

"Together?" Beatrix asked.

"Together," Paloma said, and stepped through.

The fold closed behind them like a book whose story was finished — and somewhere far below, in soil that had never known them, the seeds in Paloma's pocket stirred, and began, very quietly, to wake.
