## 22. Paloma and the Garden

*Beatrix*

The seeds came home in a tin that had once held Paloma's ink nibs, rattling against the metal like small impatient bones.

Beatrix carried it up the last stretch of the Thornfell pass herself, though Paloma had offered twice and Serah's promise weighed nothing in her pack at all. The morning was ordinary — that was the miracle of it. Mist off the lower slopes, a kestrel hanging on the wind above the scree, her boots finding the smuggler road by habit rather than prophecy. No verse named this hour. No book burned in her satchel. The mountain simply was, gray and green and indifferent, and Beatrix found she loved it more for the indifference.

The plot she'd chosen sat above the old waystation tavern, on a shelf of south-facing ground where her grandmother used to say the snow melted first. It was poor soil, stony, stubborn — good Thornfell dirt that made everything fight for its living. She'd cleared it over three evenings with a mattock older than she was, turning up stones and one rusted buckle from some smuggler's pack a century dead. Nothing about the work had felt sacred. That was how she knew it was right.

"Ready?" Paloma called from behind her.

Beatrix turned. Paloma stood at the edge of the plot with the tin cupped in both hands, hair escaping its braid in the wind, ink still ghosted along the side of her right hand no matter how often she scrubbed. She looked tired in the deep way they were both still tired, the way that lived under sleep rather than in it. But her eyes were clear. They'd been clear since the fold between centuries closed behind them and left them standing in the tavern with cold tea and an empty tin and no explanation anyone would ever believe.

"Ready," Beatrix said.

Paloma knelt and began to plant.

They worked without speaking much. There wasn't a ritual to it — Beatrix had half expected there to be, some echo of the garden below, some shiver of the old magic recognizing itself. But the seeds went into the dirt the way any seeds do: a finger's depth, a palm's width apart, pressed down with a thumb. Paloma's hands moved with a cartographer's precision even here, spacing rows like survey lines. Beatrix just dug and covered and moved on, letting her body remember that it was built for labor as much as for the sword.

Halfway down the third row, Paloma laughed.

"What?"

"They're so *small*." She held one up between finger and thumb — a brown fleck, unremarkable, the kind of thing you'd sweep off a windowsill. "I mapped a garden where every flower grew out of a buried memory. Where the hedges rearranged themselves when I lied to them. And the seed looks like — like gravel."

"Good," Beatrix said. "Let it be gravel."

She hadn't meant to say it aloud, but once said, she meant it completely. Let the garden come back ordinary. Let it grow toward truths people said easily instead of truths they starved on. Let it be theirs — small, stony, stubborn, unhaunted.

They finished as the mist burned off. Paloma watered the rows from the spring cistern, carefully, and Beatrix sat back on her heels and looked at what they'd made: six neat furrows of dark-turned earth doing absolutely nothing, promising absolutely nothing, asking only time.

It was the first thing she had planted in her life. Her mother had planted oaths. Her grandmother had planted stories. Beatrix had spent thirty years planting herself firmly in front of doors, guarding other people's passages through. This — dirt under her nails, sun on her neck, a woman beside her wiping mud on her trousers without a second thought — this was different. This was hers.

"Your hands," Paloma said quietly.

Beatrix looked down. The sword-marks on her palms — the old oath-lines her grandmother's grandmother had carried, the ones the hedge-witch had read like scripture a lifetime ago and a world away — had faded. Not gone. They were still there if she looked, faint as watermarks, the way a scar stays after the wound stops mattering. But they didn't burn anymore. They didn't pull.

"They're just hands now," Beatrix said.

"They're your hands now," Paloma corrected, and reached over, and laced her ink-stained fingers through Beatrix's marked ones, and the two kinds of inheritance sat together in the sun without arguing.

Below them, faintly, the tavern door banged open. Voices — the first students arriving early, three days ahead of schedule, because young people never believed roads took as long as they did. Beatrix heard their laughter bounce up the switchbacks and felt the old reflexive dread, the flinch she'd carried since the night she renounced her oath: *they'll want something from me. They'll want the sword, the words, the story.*

But the feeling passed through her and out the other side, and what remained was simpler. They wanted to learn. That was all. And learning could be given freely or not at all.

"Come on," she said, standing, pulling Paloma up with her. "If we're late to our own first lesson, I'll never live it down."

* * *

*Paloma*

The maps sold faster than Paloma could draw them, which was its own strange problem.

She'd expected indifference. She'd braced for it — the long slow failure of a cartographer whose masterwork depicted a valley that officially did not exist, drawn from a year that officially had never happened. She'd planned to publish under a pseudonym, sell maybe a dozen copies to collectors of the fanciful, and keep the true plates locked in her flat like love letters.

Instead, *A True and Particular Map of the Garden Below Thornfell* sold out its first printing in nine days.

"It's the detail," her printer said, mystified, counting coins. "People say it feels *remembered*. Like they've been there. Old Marta at the chandlery wept over the trellis row — said it looked like her mother's garden before the blight."

Paloma didn't tell him why. Some things belonged to the map and not to the talk around it. But she understood: the garden had grown from memories, and memory recognized itself wherever it lived. Every person who unfolded her map found some corner of it that was *theirs*, some hedge or well or flowering tree that matched a thing they'd lost or loved and never said aloud. The garden grew toward truths people refused to speak. Apparently it still did, even rendered in ink at eight pennies a sheet.

So she drew. She drew at the table by the window of the room she was slowly, without ever discussing it, sharing with Beatrix — because Beatrix's spare sword oil had migrated to her shelf, and Paloma's drafting pens had colonized every jar in the kitchen, and neither of them had remarked on it, and remarking would have broken the spell of how easy it was.

That was the thing Paloma kept returning to, the thing she turned over at night like a coin she couldn't stop testing for counterfeit: how easy it was. Before, everything had been arranged — the date planned to the minute, the gift chosen to impress, both of them performing competence at each other across a tavern table while fate sharpened its pen nearby. Now nothing was arranged. Now they chose each other daily, in small unremarkable ways, with no verse requiring it. Beatrix brought her tea without being asked. Paloma saved her the window seat. When Beatrix woke gasping from dreams of soldiers wearing familiar faces, Paloma didn't ask what she dreamed; she just put her hand flat on Beatrix's back until breathing evened out, and in the morning Beatrix made breakfast and told one story from the bad years, voluntarily, a little at a time, the way you turn soil.

Truths, said freely, in their own season. The garden's whole law, practiced badly and honestly in a rented room above a chandler's shop.

Today's commission spread across the table: the new training yard Beatrix was building above the pass. Not a fortress — a school, though Beatrix refused the word. A place with a straw practice dummy, a rack of blunt blades, a fire ring, and a sign Paloma was lettering that read, in Beatrix's own dictated phrasing:

**SWORDSPEAKING TAUGHT HERE. BY INVITATION ONLY. NO OATHS SPOKEN ON THE PREMISES.**

The last line had taken an argument to settle. Beatrix had wanted to leave it off — *it'll scare them off, it sounds bitter* — but Paloma had held firm, pen poised.

"It's not bitter," she'd said. "It's the whole point. Your family bound swordspeaking to oaths for four hundred years and it ate them alive. You're the first one offering it freely. Say so. Put it on the door."

And Beatrix had looked at her for a long moment — that look Paloma was still learning to read, the guarded one cracking open like ice in spring — and said, "You always did know how to label a map," and then kissed her, which settled the argument more efficiently than any rebuttal.

Now Paloma lettered the final line in careful capitals, blew the ink dry, and thought about her own inheritance, laid out beside Beatrix's on the same table.

Her great-great-grandmother had drawn the original garden maps and vanished into them, swallowed by a truth too large to carry back. For a hundred years the Idris family had treated cartography like a curse — precise, professional, deliberately dull, generation after generation mapping coastlines and trade routes and never once drawing anything that frightened them. Paloma had inherited the fear without knowing its name. She'd bought a cursed book to impress a beautiful stranger precisely because some buried part of her was starving for the work her bloodline had abandoned.

And now? Now she drew impossible gardens and published them, and people wept over trellises, and somewhere in the archive of her family's careful dullness there was a shelf waiting for the maps that would finally finish what her ancestor started — not by vanishing into the garden, but by bringing it back, piece by piece, into the light of the ordinary world.

She signed the plate in the bottom corner, small and plain: *P. Idris.* No prophecy had dictated a single line of it.

Outside, the evening bell rang from the tavern, and she heard Beatrix coming up the stairs two at a time, smelling of woodsmoke and horse and the particular iron tang the sword gave off these days — not a demand anymore, just a hum, like a struck tuning fork remembering its note.

"You'll never guess," Beatrix said from the doorway, breathless, grinning, "who showed up at the yard today asking to learn."

* * *

*Beatrix*

The girl's name was Wren Halloway, she was sixteen, and she had walked forty miles on the strength of a rumor and a printed map.

"I saw the garden," she said, standing in the yard with her cap in her hands, refusing the stool Beatrix offered. "On the map. The one everyone's buying. And there's a tree in it — a white one, by the east wall — and my gran died last winter, and she had a tree like that, and when I look at the map I can *smell the blossoms*, ma'am, I swear it, and I don't know what that means except—" She stopped, steadied herself. "Except I think I'm supposed to be here. And I know you said invitation only, so I'm asking. Will you invite me?"

Beatrix studied her the way her grandmother had taught her to study travelers on the pass: weight distribution, hands, eyes. The girl's hands were calloused from farm work, not sword work. Her eyes were red-rimmed and absolutely steady. And there — faint, so faint most people would miss it — the ghost of a mark on her right palm, not a Ravensworth mark, not an oath-line at all, something older and stranger, the kind of thing that surfaced in a bloodline once a century and waited patiently to be noticed.

The sword hummed at Beatrix's hip. It hummed these days whenever someone honest came near. It had never done that before the garden; it had only ever *demanded*. Now it was content to suggest, the way a good dog suggests a walk.

"Where's your family?" Beatrix asked.

"Wren's an orphan," said a voice from the gate, and Serah ducked under the lintel, gray at the temples now, walking with the careful grace of a body that had been broken and rebuilt. She wore no uniform anymore; the pale court had dissolved into a council, and Serah had resigned from the council in a letter Beatrix would have framed if it weren't so profane. "She's mine now. Or I'm hers. We're still negotiating the terms." Serah's mouth did the thing that was almost a smile. "I told her about you. I may have oversold it."

"You undersold it," Wren said fiercely, and then went pink.

Beatrix looked at the girl, and at Serah, and at the yard — the straw dummy, the fire ring, the sign Paloma had lettered drying on the fence — and felt the whole shape of her life settle around her like a coat that finally fit.

Once, an oath had been a chain: spoken before she understood it, binding her to a prophecy, a bloodline, a war. Once, she had thrown the chain off so violently she'd nearly destroyed herself and let her sister walk into danger alone, and it had taken a garden growing out of her worst memory to teach her the difference between renouncing a chain and choosing a purpose. The oath hadn't died with the book. It had just stopped being an order and become, at last, an offer.

"Wren Halloway," she said formally, because some things deserved ceremony even when freely given. "You are invited. Not bound. If you ever want to walk away, you walk away, and the road will be open behind you, and no word of mine will follow you. Do you understand the difference?"

The girl frowned, working at it. "An invitation can be refused."

"An invitation can be *renewed*," Beatrix said. "Every day. That's the whole craft. Everything else is just footwork."

Wren grinned then, sudden and bright, and something in Beatrix's chest — the part that had been clenched since a tavern table and a burning book — finally, fully, unclenched.

* * *

*Paloma*

Autumn came early to the pass, as it always did, and on the first gold morning of it the garden came up.

Paloma spotted it from the window while grinding ink: a line of green where six bare furrows had slept all summer. She was down the hill before Beatrix finished lacing her boots, and they arrived together at the plot to find it — not miraculous, not vast, not glowing. Just growing. Six rows of ordinary vegetables and flowers and one absurd, thriving apple sapling, exactly where the seeds from the tin had gone, doing exactly what seeds do.

Except.

"The rows are wrong," Beatrix said slowly.

They were. Paloma had planted straight lines — survey lines, cartographer's pride. The garden had other ideas. The plants had drifted, gently, unmistakably, into a shape: a curve of path, a suggestion of wall, a hollow where a bench might go. Anyone else would see a messy kitchen garden. Paloma saw a map. She saw *her* map — the first one, the true one, the garden below rendered in ink — translated into living green, in miniature, on a mountainside.

"It's not repeating itself," she breathed. "Look — the trellis row is where I *said* it should be, out loud, that day in the valley. The part I described to Beatrix on the road. It grew toward what we spoke. What we *chose* to say."

The garden's oldest law, still keeping itself: it grows toward truths people refuse to say — and flourishes best on truths freely given.

Beatrix crouched and touched an apple leaf, and the sword at her hip hummed one low, contented note, and the leaf trembled as if answering. Above them the kestrel wheeled. Below them the tavern smoke rose, and the school bell — Paloma had insisted on a bell — rang the hour for Wren and the two other students who had trickled in over the summer, each invited, none bound.

"Well?" Beatrix said, looking up at her with dirt already on her knees and no intention whatsoever of pretending otherwise. "Cartographer. Is it worth mapping?"

Paloma knelt beside her in the warm dirt and opened her satchel and took out a fresh sheet, and began, for the first time in her life, to draw a map of a place that existed in no prophecy, served no destiny, answered to no book — a small stubborn garden on a gray mountain, growing toward everything two women had finally learned to say out loud.

"It's worth everything," she said. "Hold still. You're in the frame."

And Beatrix laughed — easy, unguarded, hers — and stayed exactly where she was, and the pencil moved, and the morning went on being ordinary, which was, they had both learned, another word for miraculous.
