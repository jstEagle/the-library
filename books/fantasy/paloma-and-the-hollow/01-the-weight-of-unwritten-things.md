## 1. The Weight of Unwritten Things

There were three rules Odile Brightwater had written into the front page of her own mind, in a hand steadier than her heart: *Count everything. Own nothing. Be no one.*

Six years in the Gilded Hollow had taught her that the first rule was the only one worth keeping. The other two were consequences.

She sat now in the counting room beneath the guildhouse, where the light came down through amber glass set into the ceiling like the eyes of some patient god, and she catalogued the impossible. It was what she did. It was, in fact, the entire reason Corvane Ashgrave had pulled her out of a ditch outside Vell's Crossing seven years ago, dried her off, fed her stew, and said, "You have an honest face and dishonest weather. Come count for me." The honest face had been a lie even then—her face was as guilty as any face ever made—but the counting was true, and truth was a currency the Hollow dealt in more reliably than gold.

Item forty-one. She lifted it from its velvet cradle with both hands, though it weighed nothing at all. A bottle of grey glass, stoppered with wax the color of old teeth, and inside it—not smoke, not mist—an echo. You could see it pressed against the glass like a moth against a window: the shape of a sound, a last word someone had said so desperately that it refused to die when the voice did. The label, in Corvane's slanted script, read: *A mother's calling-name, spoken to a drowned son. Purchased from the river at dusk. Do not open indoors.*

Odile turned the bottle slowly. The echo stirred, pressing its small dark weight against whichever side faced her palm, as if it knew a keeper's touch from a buyer's. She had catalogued it three years ago and every year since, because echoes changed hands in this place the way grief changed hands everywhere: reluctantly, and never entirely.

*One bottled echo,* she wrote in the great ledger, in her small precise hand. *Condition: restless. Provenance: sorrowful. Value: not for sale.*

The last entry was hers alone. Corvane would have written *priceless* or *unpriced*, and either word would have been a kind of lie. Odile preferred the truth that things existed which could not be sold, even here—even in the Gilded Hollow, which traded in everything the world had misplaced.

She set the bottle back among its velvet and reached for item forty-two.

This one lived in a drawer that hummed faintly against her fingertips, a low note like a struck bell that never finished ringing. Inside lay a contract, folded eight times, each fold sealed with a different wax. A debt payable in years—the seller had sold eleven years of his own life to buy his daughter out of a plague-town, and now the years sat in the paper like coins in a purse, waiting for whoever held the debt to spend them. Odile had counted them once, carefully, the way you might count another person's heartbeats while they slept. Eleven years. They were warm through the paper. They smelled, faintly, of the seller's garden, though he had sold the memory of the garden along with everything else and would not remember why the smell made him want to weep.

*Eleven years, unspent,* she wrote. *Holder: the Hollow. Debtor: Tomas Wren, deceased two winters past. The years remain payable regardless.* Her pen paused over the page. *Note: they are still warm.*

Some nights she dreamed about those years. Not spent, not wasted—simply sitting in their drawer, going soft and golden as stored apples, belonging to no one, wanted by no one. There was something obscene in it, and something holy, and she had long ago stopped trying to say which.

* * *

At midday she climbed the spiral stair to the gallery above the trading floor, carrying the day's tallies, and stood where she always stood: half in shadow, behind the rail, visible to exactly no one. Below her the Hollow conducted its morning business, and it looked, from up here, like any merchant house in any city—a long room, good light, clerks at their desks. It was only when you came close that you saw what was being sold.

A woman in traveling grays was bargaining with Master Corvane himself over a jar containing, according to its label, *the last summer of a village since drowned by the king's engineers*. Beside them, a collector from the coast examined a cage of moths whose wingbeats spelled out, in slow rotation, a message their dead owner had never dared send. And near the door, a young man was trying to sell something he kept hidden in his sleeve, glancing at it between sentences the way you glance at a wound.

Odile watched Corvane work. He was a broad man gone silver at the temples, with the particular stillness of someone who had learned that moving too fast frightened the merchandise. He listened more than he spoke. When the woman in grays named her price, he laughed—not cruelly—and shook his head, and offered her something better than coin, and Odile saw the exact moment the woman decided to trust him, the moment her shoulders came down from around her ears. He was very good at that. He had done it to Odile once, in a ditch, in the rain.

*He bought my silence without ever asking what it cost,* she thought, and then, as she did most days, she pushed the thought away before it could finish becoming a feeling.

Because underneath the tallying, underneath the careful invisible life, there was the storm.

She did not let herself think of it in full. She had practiced not thinking of it the way musicians practice scales—daily, mechanically, until the not-thinking ran smooth and automatic and cost her almost nothing. But it was always there, the way the sea is there under a ship. Six years ago—no, seven; time went soft when she tried to measure it—she had stood on the bluffs above Vell's Crossing with rain in her teeth and something ancient waking in her blood, and she had called the storm. Not summoned it. Called it, the way you call a dog you believe is yours.

It had come. It had killed.

And the man it killed was not the man she had meant.

That was the whole of it. That was the sentence the tribunes had read aloud while she knelt on wet stone: *the storm was called by Odile Brightwater, and it took Aldous Ren, magistrate's clerk, innocent of all charges.* Innocent. The word had gone through her like a nail. Because the man she'd meant—the grain-tithe officer who had burned a widow's fields and laughed about it—had walked out of the storm untouched, and Aldous Ren, who owed her nothing, who had once returned her dropped shawl on a ferry without a word, had died in her place, face-down in the flooded road with his ledger floating open beside him.

She had fled before the tribunal finished. Exile was not a sentence passed on her; it was a sentence she had passed on herself, and the Gilded Hollow was simply where the sentence was being served. Here she counted impossible things and touched none of them and signed her name as *O. Brightwater, keeper* in ledgers no official would ever read, and every year on the anniversary of the storm she climbed to the roof of the guildhouse and stood in whatever weather came and let it rain on her without calling anything at all.

Wary contentment. That was the honest name for her life, if she was forced to give one. Contentment like a bird in a hand—alive, warm, and one hard squeeze from being over.

* * *

Evening came early that day, the way it did in autumn, pooling in the corners of the counting room while Odile worked by lamplight. Corvane found her there. She heard him on the stair—that deliberate tread, each step placed rather than taken—and she did not look up until his shadow fell across her ledger.

"You've been at the strange shelf again," he said.

"Everything here is the strange shelf."

"True." He lowered himself onto the stool across from her, groaning theatrically, an old merchant's habit that Odile suspected was itself merchandise—a thing he sold to make people underestimate him. "What's tonight's treasure?"

"Item forty-three." She turned the tray toward him. On it lay a single key made of black iron, cold enough that frost crept across the metal even in the warmth of the room. *A key to a door that has not been built yet,* the label read, in handwriting Odile did not recognize and never had. *Purchased from a dreamer who insisted it would be needed within the year. That was nine years ago.*

Corvane picked it up and turned it in the lamplight, and for a moment—just a moment—his merchant's ease fell away and something else looked out of his face. Something tired. Something that had been waiting a long time for a door.

"Nine years," he murmured.

"That's what the label says."

"Mm." He set the key down with exaggerated care, as if it might hear him, and when he looked up at her the ease was back, seamless, and Odile hated how relieved she was to see it. "You do good work, Brightwater. Best keeper I've had. Better than I deserve, probably." He rose, joints cracking. "Lock up when you're done. And Odile—" he paused at the top of the stair, silhouetted against the amber light, "—if anyone comes asking after strange inventory tonight, you're closed. Whatever they offer. Whatever they say. We're closed."

"We're always closed after dark."

"Not like this." He smiled, and it did not reach anywhere near his eyes. "Not tonight."

His footsteps faded up the stair. Odile sat a long while with her pen dry against the page, listening to the guildhouse settle around her—the creak of old timber, the hum of the debt-drawer, the faint, muffled flutter of the bottled echo against its glass, restless, restless, always restless.

She should have written the key into the ledger. Instead she found herself staring at the last line she'd written, *note: they are still warm*, and thinking about doors that hadn't been built yet, and about Corvane's face in the lamplight, and about the particular quality of a warning given without explanation—which is that it tells you exactly how much trouble the giver believes is coming, and not one word about why.

Outside, the night had gone strangely, perfectly still. No wind. No rain. The lamps in the yard below burned without flickering, as if the air itself were holding its breath.

Odile blew out her lamp and sat in the dark, and that was when she heard it: a knock at the guildhouse door. Small. Twice. Then again—small, twice—patient as a heartbeat, from a visitor who clearly had nowhere else in the world to knock.

We're closed, Corvane had said. Whatever they offer. Whatever they say.

Odile stood, took the lamp, and went to see anyway.
