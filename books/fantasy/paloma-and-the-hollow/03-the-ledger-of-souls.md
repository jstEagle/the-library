## 3. The Ledger of Souls

The child slept in the linen closet off the counting room, curled among bolts of grey cloth that smelled of cedar and old ink. Odile had told herself it was temporary—a night's charity, nothing more—but she had also told herself, once, that the storm was an accident, and she had learned to distrust her own gentle lies.

She worked by lamplight with the door to the closet cracked open, listening to the small, even breathing while she spread her materials across the counting table: three sheets of vellum rubbed smooth, a pot of lampblack thinned with tallow, and the notes she'd been keeping since the child arrived two nights past.

The words would not leave her alone.

*Vess. Kaelen. Vess-ith-ara.*

She had written them phonetically at first, then in the trade-glyphs every guild clerk learned, then in the older script—the one used for contracts of binding, the one Odile had been forced to study during her first year at the Hollow because Corvane said a ledger-keeper who couldn't read a binding clause was a ledger-keeper who could be cheated. She had resented those lessons. She resented them no longer.

Because the words were not nonsense. They were not even properly First Tongue, though they carried its bones. They were *trade-tongue*—the pidgin that had grown up in the mountain markets four centuries ago, when the courts above and below still traded openly, before the wars, before the sealing. A dead language preserved only in the oldest documents, the ones kept under lock in the Hollow's archive because their clauses still had teeth.

And the child spoke it fluently. Fluently, and desperately, repeating the same phrases over and over into Odile's hands, into her hair, against her collarbone in the dark hours when the nightmares came—phrases Odile now understood well enough to make her hands shake.

*I am here. I am not finished. Do not let them count me.*

*Do not let them count me.*

Odile set down her pen and pressed her palms flat against the table until the trembling stopped. Counting. The word *counting*, in a language built for commerce, meant something very specific: to enter into a ledger. To take possession by record. Somewhere beneath the mountain, something considered this child an entry not yet written—or an entry written and awaiting collection—and the child knew it.

She thought of the iron ring the child clutched even in sleep, fingers hooked through it like a drowning sailor through a lifeline. Hollow iron. Not forged-hollow but *made*-hollow, the way a bell is made hollow, the way a soul-vessel is made hollow. Odile had catalogued enough impossible things in six years at the Gilded Hollow to know what she was holding, and she had told herself she didn't know, because knowing would require action, and action would require her to stop being invisible.

The lamp guttered. In the closet, the child murmured: *vess-ith-ara*. *I am not finished.*

"No," Odile whispered. "You're not."

She rose, took the lamp, and went down into the archive.

* * *

The Gilded Hollow's founding ledger lived in a case of blackened oak at the far end of the archive, behind three locks for which Odile held two keys and Corvane held the third. It was the guild's holiest object and its most boring: two hundred pages of charter terms, membership rolls, fee schedules, and the famous first entry—"Let all things be tradeable, and all trades be written"—in Corvane's young hand, sharp and confident as a blade.

Odile had read it cover to cover twice in her early years, out of the same dutiful thoroughness that made her invaluable. There was nothing hidden in it. She was certain of that. Certain the way she had been certain of her own safety for six years, which was to say: certain because certainty was comfortable.

The third lock opened to Corvane's key, which hung on his belt, which meant she could not open the case honestly. But the case was old, and the oak had dried and shifted, and Odile had spent six years learning where every joint in this building flexed. She braced the lamp, worked the thin blade of her letter-opener along the back panel, and felt the pegs give with a sound like a held breath released.

The ledger came out heavy as a sleeping animal. She laid it on the reading table, and the archive's silence pressed close around her, full of the small ticking sounds of old paper settling.

Page one. Page forty. Page one hundred and twelve, where the membership roll ended mid-signature because the final member had died before the ink dried—an entry Odile had always found oddly poignant.

She turned pages slowly, looking for what she could not have named: a wrongness in the margins, a quire stitched unevenly, a shadow where the lamplight caught differently. The Hollow traded in impossible things; its founder would hardly have left his own impossibilities lying flat on a page.

It was the *smell* that found it. Between pages sixty-one and sixty-two, where the binding swelled just slightly, there was a breath of air that did not match the rest—colder, mineral, like the air off a deep cellar. Mountain air.

Odile slid the letter-opener into the gutter of the binding and cut the stitching.

The hidden page came free like a splinter from a wound.

It was smaller than the others, folded twice, and the paper was wrong—not guild stock but something finer, veined faintly grey, the kind of paper made, if the stories were true, from fibers grown in the dark. The writing on it was in two hands. The first was Corvane's; she knew his script the way she knew her own reflection, every descender sharp, every numeral closed. The second hand was not a hand at all. It looked less written than *impressed*, as though the words had been pressed up through the paper from underneath, each letter slightly sunken, slightly cold.

She read the Corvane half first, because it was easier, because it was human:

*"Received of the Undercourt, one charter, sealed and irrevocable, granting the Gilded Hollow license to trade in all things above and below, in perpetuity. In consideration whereof, the undersigned conveys—"*

And then the second hand took over, and the trade-tongue she had spent two nights decoding rose up off the page like water finding its level:

*"One soul, whole and unspent, voice included, name included, future included. To be counted at the fire's failing."*

*To be counted at the fire's failing.*

Odile read it again. And again. Her eyes kept sliding off the last line the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth, because the last line was signed, and the signature was not Corvane's alone. Beneath his name, in that pressed-and-sunken script, was a counter-mark: a ring. A hollow circle, drawn with a single unbroken stroke.

The same ring the child carried. The same ring the child had pressed into Odile's palm on the first night, over and over, saying *kaelen, kaelen*—and Odile had finally decoded that word too, yesterday, in the worst hour before dawn. *Kaelen*: pledge. Token. *The thing given to prove the bargain true.*

A receipt. The child was carrying a receipt.

No. Worse. The child was carrying the *proof of sale*, and the sale was—

"Voice included," Odile said aloud, and her voice cracked on the second word. "Name included."

The child was mute. The child had no name it could say. The child spoke only in a dead tongue, begging not to be counted.

The sold soul was not some stranger's, some merchant's enemy, some debtor dragged down into the dark. The soul conveyed in the founding bargain of the Gilded Hollow—the bargain that bought the charter, that built everything, that sheltered *her*—belonged to the child asleep in her linen closet.

Corvane had sold a child's voice, name, and future to buy his guild. And he had done it believing, the contract's phrasing suggested, that souls sold whole and unspent to the Undercourt went mindless—that whatever remained would never know enough to grieve. But this one knew. This one had walked out of the dark and up the mountain and across the city to the only place its receipt still existed, carrying the token of its own sale, asking the one person paid to keep records to please, please, not write it down.

*Do not let them count me.*

Odile became aware, distantly, that she was crying. She let it happen without wiping her face, the way you let rain fall when there's no point in sheltering. Six years of careful invisibility, of ledgers balanced and opinions unwritten, of gratitude toward the man who had taken in a disgraced storm-caller when every other door was shut—all of it stood in her chest like a building whose foundations had just been pulled out.

He had sheltered her. That was true, and it did not stop being true. He had fed her and paid her and taught her the binding-scripts and never once asked about the storm beyond what the records required. And he had built the floor she stood on out of a child's stolen future, and the roof over her head out of a charter signed in a dead tongue by something that pressed its words up through paper from below.

Both things were true. Both things could not stand.

She looked down at the hidden page, at the phrase she hadn't yet let herself translate fully: *at the fire's failing.* Fire. What fire? The Undercourt's fires were legend—the dragon-fire, the old stories said, the flame that had burned since before the sealing, that warmed both courts, that both realms drew life from like rooms on either side of a single hearth. Stories. Fires in stories didn't fail.

But contracts weren't stories. Contracts had dates. And this one said *to be counted at the fire's failing*, which meant the seller had always known there would come a day when the fire guttered low enough for collection—and Corvane had signed anyway, betting, perhaps, that the fire would outlast him.

Somewhere above her, through floors of stone and timber, Odile heard the front bell of the Gilded Hollow ring once, deep and wrong, the way it rang when something entered that was not a customer. Then again. Then a third time, and stopped.

The lamp flame leaned sideways, though there was no draft, and shrank to the size of a seed pearl, and in that shrunken light Odile saw her own shadow thrown huge against the archive shelves, and understood with terrible clarity that the fire's failing had already begun, and that the counting had started.

She folded the page along its old creases, tucked it into her bodice against her heart, and climbed the stairs toward the ringing bell, toward the man who had sheltered her, rehearsing as she went the only question that mattered:

*What did you sell, Corvane? And what do we owe now?*
