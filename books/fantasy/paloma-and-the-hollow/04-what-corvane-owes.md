## 4. What Corvane Owes

Odile had hidden the child in the counting room, behind the shelves of unclaimed goods where the candlelight didn't reach, and now she stood in Corvane's study with the founding ledger open between them like a wound neither of them could stop looking at.

He hadn't touched it. That was the first thing she noticed. Corvane Ashgrave, who signed everything—who initialed receipts for bottled echoes and debts payable in years, whose hand was on every page of the Hollow's history—had not once laid a finger on that ledger since she'd laid it open before him. He sat with his hands folded on his knees, an old man's posture he never wore in front of the others, and looked at the signature at the bottom of the hidden page as though it belonged to a stranger.

*Corvane Ashgrave, by my own hand and will, one soul entire, to the Undercourt beneath the mountain, in exchange for charter, protection, and perpetual license of trade.*

The ink was forty years old. It still looked wet.

"You knew," Odile said. Not a question. She'd known the moment she saw him not touch it.

"I knew there was a price." His voice was quiet, level, the voice he used for negotiating with things that could not be negotiated with. "I did not know what it was until the broker came down from the mountain with the contract already written. They do not show you the merchandise, Brightwater. They show you a jar."

"A jar."

"A soul-jar." He finally reached out—not for the ledger, but for the decanter beside it, pouring two fingers of plum brandy he did not drink. "Small thing. Clay-colored glass, sealed with wax the color of dried blood. The broker said it was mindless. A husk. Something scraped out of a body that was never going to live anyway." He turned the glass in his fingers without drinking. "I believed him. Do you understand? I *chose* to believe him, because the alternative was walking away from the only trade agreement that would have us, and the Hollow was eleven people sleeping in a flooded cellar, and I was thirty years old and certain that what I built would be worth what it cost."

"And was it?"

He looked up at her then, and Odile saw something she had never seen in nine years of keeping his books: Corvane Ashgrave afraid.

"The fire is guttering," he said. "You've felt it. The stillness last night—the lamps burning low, the river running silent under the ice. That's not weather. That's the dragon-fire going out. And when it goes out, everything it sustains goes with it. Both courts. The mountain. Us." He set the brandy down untouched. "And three days ago, a summons came up through the mountain's roots. The Undercourt is calling in all debts. Every contract. Every bargain ever struck beneath its roof."

Odile's mouth went dry. "Including—"

"Including mine." He said it flatly, the way a man says *the water is rising*. "The charter was bought with that soul. If the debt is called, the charter dissolves, and the Hollow with it. Unless—" his eyes went, despite himself, toward the door, toward the counting room beyond it, toward the small shape hiding behind the unclaimed goods, "—unless the collateral is returned."

"She's a child," Odile said. "She's mute. She speaks the First Tongue in her sleep and doesn't know she's doing it, and she carries an iron ring with a hole in it where something used to be, and you are not giving her back to whatever came out of that mountain."

"I said *returned*, Brightwater. Not surrendered." He rose, crossed to the window, and stood looking out at the frozen street where the lanterns burned low and blue. "There is a difference. There has to be a difference. Because if there isn't, then I sold a child forty years ago, and everything I've built since is standing on top of her."

The words hung in the cold air of the study. Odile found she couldn't answer them. She knew about building on top of people. She had been doing it for nine years herself—a careful, invisible life stacked neatly over the memory of a storm and a man who died in it because she'd called the lightning wrong.

"Tell me about the fire," she said instead, because it was easier than telling him what she thought of him, and because some part of her—some part she distrusted deeply—still wanted to believe there was a way through this that didn't end with either of them destroyed.

* * *

Corvane had learned, over forty years of trade, that the worst bargains were the ones you made honestly.

He sat across from Odile now and told her the truth in the order he'd kept it buried: first the fire, then the court, then the sale. It was the order of his own cowardice, and he knew it, and he let her see that he knew.

"The dragon-fire predates both courts," he said. "It burns at the mountain's heart, and it doesn't burn wood or coal or anything you can carry to it. It burns on *keeping*. Old vows. Sworn service. The Undercourt feeds it with the debts owed to it—every promise extracted, every bargain enforced, all of it rendered down into flame. The Sunward court above feeds it too, in their way, with oaths of fealty and law. Two courts, one fire, each convinced they alone keep it lit."

"And now?"

"Now the ledgers are full of debts no one intends to honor. Oaths sworn with fingers crossed. Contracts written to be broken. The fire has been starving for a century, and the courts have been papering over the hunger with clever accounting." He smiled, and it was a terrible smile. "I would know. Clever accounting is my religion."

"Whose soul did you sell?" Odile asked. "Do you know?"

"No." The word cost him; she watched it cost him. "The broker never said. But the ring the child carries—that's a soul-anchor. Hollow iron. Whatever was in that jar was bound to that ring before it was bound to me, and if the child carries it, then the child and the soul have been circling each other for forty years, waiting for someone foolish enough to bring them into the same room."

"Me," Odile said. "You mean me."

"I mean you." He turned from the window. "You decoded a dead trade-tongue in one night. You found a page I hid from myself for four decades. You are the best ledger-keeper I have ever employed, and I employed you because you were running from something, and running people make careful bookkeepers." He came around the desk and stood before her, and for the first time since she'd known him, Corvane Ashgrave looked old. "Three days ago the summons came. Tomorrow, the collectors come to read the terms aloud. And I am asking you—as the man who sheltered you when no one else would take a storm-caller with blood on her hands—to help me find a way to pay this debt that does not put that child back in a jar."

"That's not a request," Odile said slowly. "That's a confession."

"Yes."

"And you're making it to me because—"

"Because you are the only person in this building who has never lied to me in a ledger." He held her gaze. "And because I think you already know what the fire eats, Brightwater. I think you worked it out before I finished talking. And I think you're trying very hard not to say it, because saying it will change what you have to do next."

Odile said nothing. Outside, the frozen street creaked under a silence so complete she could hear her own heart. Somewhere behind the shelves of unclaimed goods, the child slept, breathing the First Tongue into the dark.

*Vow-fire*, the old texts called it. Kept promises, burned. And a debt called in by a starving court—

A debt paid willingly. A debt *offered*.

"Oh," she said quietly. "Oh, no."

"I'm sorry," Corvane said, and meant it, which somehow made it worse. "I truly am."

* * *

They talked until the candles drowned in their own wax. What emerged from the wreckage of the night was not a plan—it was less than a plan, a splinter of one—but it was a direction, and direction was more than either of them had possessed at sundown.

The collectors would come at dawn to read the terms. Terms could be contested, if contested from within the court itself. No one contested a debt from above the mountain; petitions had to be delivered below, in person, by a party named in the contract. Corvane was named. So, technically, was the collateral.

So, as of tonight, was Odile Brightwater—because she had opened the ledger, and in the Hollow's oldest law, the reader of a hidden page became witness to the contract it contained. She had felt it happen. A cold thread through the chest, like a signature written somewhere inside her.

"You should have left the book closed," Corvane said, and there was real grief in it.

"You should have told me what was in it," she answered, "forty years ago."

They were both right. Neither said so again.

Near morning, Odile went to the counting room and knelt beside the child, who woke instantly and silently, dark eyes finding hers in the gloom with the unerring accuracy of the hunted. The iron ring lay in the small palm, dull and lightless.

"We're going underground," Odile whispered. "Both of us. Maybe all three of us, if he keeps his word." She did not know if she believed he would. Loyalty, she was learning, was not a thing you felt or didn't feel; it was a thing you weighed, hourly, against the truth. "Can you walk?"

The child nodded. Then, in a voice like a bell heard through deep water—words no living tongue should know, and yet Odile understood them as though they'd been carved into her long ago—the child spoke for the second time since arriving:

*"The fire knows your name."*

Odile's blood went cold. "What did you say?"

But the child only closed her small hand around the hollow ring and looked toward the eastern wall—toward the mountain—toward the place where, faint as a dying pulse beneath the ice and stone, something vast was guttering.

And far above them, at the top of the house, the first grey light of dawn brought with it the sound of iron-shod boots on the front steps, and the knock of the Undercourt's collectors, come to read the terms.
