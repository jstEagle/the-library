## 7. Doors of Bone and Bargain

The antechamber of the Undercourt was not a room so much as a throat.

Odile felt it the moment Sef's lantern-light spilled across the threshold—a vast descending space, ribbed with pillars of pale stone that might once have been something living. Bone, she thought, and then corrected herself: *bone-colored*. The Undercourt was old enough that even its architecture had forgotten what it was made of. The walls sweated a faint warmth, like the flank of a sleeping animal, and the air tasted of iron and candle-smoke and something sweeter beneath it all—overripe fruit, perhaps, or funeral lilies left too long in their water.

Paloma's small hand tightened around hers.

The child had not spoken since the tunnels—not in the First Tongue, not at all—and Odile had stopped trying to coax words from her somewhere past the third collapsed gallery. Silence, Odile understood, was its own language. She had spoken it fluently for seven years.

Sef held the lantern high. Its light did not so much illuminate the chamber as negotiate with it, pushing back a few arm-spans of darkness and no more. Somewhere below them—far below, down stairs that spiraled into the mountain's marrow—the dragon-fire breathed. Odile could feel it now that they were close: a slow, labored pulse against her skin, like a heartbeat heard through a wall. It stuttered. Caught. Stuttered again.

*It is guttering,* Corvane had said, and for once he had not lied to her.

"Someone's coming," Sef murmured. The lantern-keeper had gone gray around the mouth; his hands, steady enough on the road, had begun to tremble. "Stay behind me. Both of you."

"You can't fight it," Odile said quietly. "Whatever it is."

"I know." He almost smiled. "But I can stand in front of it badly, which is more than nothing."

The thing that came up out of the dark was not walking so much as *arriving*, in the way that weather arrives—one moment the passage was empty, and the next there was a figure standing ten paces away as though it had always been there and they had only just noticed.

It wore the shape of a person the way a glove wears the shape of a hand. Tall, narrow, draped in robes the color of wet slate, its face smooth and featureless except where features were required: two eyes when it wished to be seen looking, a mouth when it wished to be heard. When it spoke, the voice came from everywhere at once, patient as dripping water.

"Three petitioners," it said. "One child. One lantern. One ledger-keeper." The smooth face turned toward Odile, and though it had no expression, she felt herself being *read*—leafed through like an account book, every entry weighed. "I am the Broker of this door. You have come to reclaim what was sold."

"We've come," Odile said, and was surprised at how level her voice came out, "to return what was stolen."

"Those are different words." The Broker tilted its head. "In this court, they are priced differently too. But come. All who reach the antechamber may bargain. That is the law older than the law." It gestured, and a table rose from the floor between them—or perhaps it had always been there, waiting. Upon it lay a single sheet of vellum, blank, and beside it a pen whose nib was carved from something yellowed and curved. A tooth. A claw. Odile did not let herself wonder.

"The terms are simple," the Broker said. "The soul held in trust beneath this mountain belongs, by contract, to the Undercourt. Contracts are not broken here. They are *balanced*. If you wish the debt voided, you must pay an equivalent weight." The featureless face inclined toward Paloma. "A soul is heavy, little one. Heavier than you know. Heavier than your keeper knows." The eyes—if they were eyes—found Odile again. "You would take this debt onto yourself?"

"Yes."

"Then you must pay in kind. Not blood—we have no use for blood, it spoils. Not gold—it is only a memory of value, and value is all we trade." The Broker laid one long finger upon the blank vellum. "A memory of equal weight. We will weigh it. If it balances, the contract dissolves. If it does not—" the mouth appeared, and curved, "—you may try again. We are patient. We are very, very patient."

"And if I have nothing of equal weight?" Odile asked.

"Then the child walks out of here and the soul remains. Or the soul walks out of here and the child remains. Either way, the scales close." The Broker spread its hands. "Choose what you will surrender. Choose carefully. Once weighed, it cannot be unweighed. You will not remember what you paid, but you will feel the shape of its absence forever—a tooth-socket in the mind, aching where something used to live."

Odile looked down at Paloma.

The child was watching her with those enormous dark eyes, hollowed by the long road, by the silence that was not hers by nature but by theft. Seven years ago, Odile had stood in a courtroom while Magistrate Hale read out the charges in his dry, unhurried voice, and she had thought: *this is what it costs to be seen.* She had built a life in the spaces where no one looked. Ledgers balanced. Doors locked. An invisibility so complete it had begun to feel like safety.

And then a mute child had arrived at the Gilded Hollow speaking words no living tongue should know, and Odile had recognized her instantly—not the face, not the name, but the *condition*. Two exiles. One who had lost her voice to a bargain signed before she could walk, and one who had lost her world to a storm she had called with open eyes.

*What would you give,* Odile thought, *to have it back?*

She knew exactly what she would give. She had known since the tunnels, since the moment she first felt the dragon-fire's failing pulse and understood that everything—both courts, both worlds, this child's whole unwritten future—hung on whether someone was willing to put something irreplaceable on the scale.

There was only one memory in her that carried true weight.

"The storm," she said.

Sef turned sharply. "Odile—"

"The night I called it." Her voice stayed steady. It cost her more than the words themselves. "Seven years ago. Every detail. The harbor. The wind rising under my hands like a living thing. The man who died." She swallowed. "All of it. Weigh it."

The Broker went very still, in the way of things that do not breathe and therefore never need to pretend to.

"A storm-calling," it said softly. "A death. A judgment. An exile." The smooth face seemed to sharpen. "Yes. That has weight. That has *considerable* weight." A pause, long as a held breath. "But weighers must know what they weigh. Tell me the truth of that night, ledger-keeper. All of it. The court beneath the mountain trades in truth above all things, and false weights are punished severely."

Odile closed her eyes.

She had never told anyone. Not Corvane, who had taken her in without questions because questions were bad for business. Not Sef, on the cold road, in the thin grey dawns. Not the magistrate, though he had sentenced her for it. The truth had lived inside her for seven years like a swallowed coal, and now she opened her mouth and let it out.

"The man the storm killed," she said, "was not the man I called it against."

Sef made a small sound, as if struck.

"I was twenty-three. There was a shipping magnate named Aldous Vane who had burned out three fishing families on the coast—burned their boats, bought their debts, drowned one of them in a 'boating accident' the courts declined to investigate. I had proof. No one wanted it. So I did what Brightwaters do. I went to the sea cliff above his estate and I called a storm to break his fleet, to ruin him the honest way storms ruin people—fear and loss, not death." Her hands had begun to shake; she watched them as though they belonged to someone else. "But storms are not precise instruments. They are *weather*. And Vane was not on his flagship that night. He had traded berths with a guest—a young courier, some nobody carrying letters, a man I never learned the name of. The flagship went down with all hands, and the courts needed someone to blame, and there I was, standing on the cliff, soaked in rain, guilty of exactly what they said and guilty of something far worse that they never knew."

She opened her eyes.

"That is my memory. That is its weight. A storm, a dead man whose name I never knew, and the truth that I have spent seven years calling myself a murderer of the wrong man—as if that were the crime. As if killing the *right* man would have been clean." Her voice finally cracked, and she let it. "Take it. Take all of it. Weigh it against a child's soul."

The Broker regarded her for a long moment. Then it dipped one finger into the air above the vellum, and the pen moved by itself, writing in characters that squirmed and settled like eels:

*One storm, called in grief and wrath.*
*One death, unintended and unnamed.*
*One truth, kept seven years in silence.*
*One exile, self-appointed.*

"Yes," the Broker said. "That will balance. Barely. Souls and truths weigh nearly the same, in the end—it is why we prefer trading in souls. They hold still." It slid the vellum across the table. "Sign. And understand what you sign away: not merely the images. The *meaning*. You will know that a storm happened, that you survived it, that you were exiled for it—but the heart of it, the guilt, the grief, the nameless dead man, the reason you flinch at rain—all of that goes into our keeping. You will be lighter. Lightness is not the same as peace."

"I know," Odile said. "I've been light before. It's how I got here."

She took the pen.

It was warm. That was the worst part—she had expected it to be cold, funereal, but it was warm as a living hand, and the nib left lines of silver rather than black. She wrote her name the way she wrote it in a thousand ledgers: *O. Brightwater*, small and precise, a signature designed to take up as little room as possible.

For one absurd moment she wanted to write it larger. To claim the whole page.

Then the memory began to leave.

It did not hurt. That was somehow worse. It lifted out of her like heat off summer stone—first the cliff, the salt wind, the taste of lightning on her back teeth; then Vane's lit windows far below; then the flagship listing, going, gone; then the faceless courier in his borrowed berth; then the courtroom, Hale's dry voice, the word *exile* falling like a stone into still water. She felt each piece detach and drift toward the Broker, who received them the way a miser receives coins, and she felt the terrible spaciousness opening where they had been.

"No," Sef said, and caught her elbow as she swayed. "Odile, look at me—you don't have to—"

"It's done," she said.

Her own voice sounded strange to her. Distant. She knew she had been exiled—knew it the way one knows a fact from a ledger, an entry in another person's book. *Exiled for a storm.* What storm? Why? Who had—there had been a man. Someone had died. She reached for the grief that should have been there and found only smooth walls, a tooth-socket in the mind, aching.

*So this is what I paid,* she thought, and could not remember why it had seemed like a price at all.

The Broker folded the vellum, and the vellum became a sliver of light, and the light sank through the floor toward the fire below. Far beneath them, the dragon-fire's stuttering pulse quickened—just slightly, just for a moment—as if something had been fed to it. Or freed for it.

"The debt is balanced," the Broker said. "The contract may be dissolved before the throne. Proceed down. The Court will receive you." It paused at the edge of the dark, and the mouth reappeared one last time. "Ledger-keeper. A courtesy, since you paid honestly: the keeper of the fire will want to speak with you especially. Storm-callers are rare. Willing ones are rarer." The eyes glittered. "And the fire is hungrier than any scale."

Then it was gone, and the dark where it had stood was simply dark.

Sef's hand was still on her arm. "What did you give?" he asked quietly. "What did it take?"

Odile searched herself and found the absence shaped like everything and named like nothing.

"I don't remember," she said, and discovered she was crying, and did not know for whom.

Paloma tugged her hand. The child pointed down the spiraling stair, toward the pulse of the dying fire, and for the first time since the Hollow's door, her lips moved—and though no sound came out, Odile read the shape of the words perfectly, the way one reads a language written in the body:

*Come. Before it goes out.*

They descended together into the mountain's beating heart.
