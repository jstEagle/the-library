## 9. Stormfire

The fire was smaller than Odile had imagined.

She had carried the image of dragon-fire all her life the way other people carry images of gods—vast, devouring, a wall of gold that could unmake cities. What burned before her in the heart-chamber of the mountain was a candle-flame cupped in a basin of black iron no wider than her two hands. It guttered as she watched, leaning left, leaning right, like a drunkard trying to remember the way home. Each time it dipped, something far above them shuddered—the Undercourt's vaulted ceiling of fused bone, the hanging gardens of pale fungus, the thousand lamps that were not lamps but eggs, sleeping and waiting.

"It has been dying for eleven years," said the keeper.

The keeper had been old when mountains were young; that was the only way Odile could describe what stood beside her, robed in ash-grey, its face folded like paper that had been wet and dried too many times. Its voice came from everywhere and nowhere, from the stone itself.

"The last true dragon gave this flame freely before it went into the deep dark to die," the keeper continued. "It cannot be replaced by another dragon. There are no others. It can only be fed." The folded face turned toward her. "And it eats one thing only."

"Storms," Odile said. Her mouth was dry. "It eats storms."

"Willing storms. Called by a willing caller, given without reservation, held back by nothing." The keeper's voice gentled, which was somehow worse. "You swore an oath once, Brightwater. I can taste it on you. Oaths have flavor. Yours is copper and rain."

Behind her, Sef made a small sound—not a word, just a shape of protest in the throat. The lantern-keeper had gone grey as their own light during the descent, and now they gripped the child's shoulder as if the mountain might take Paloma bodily away. The child stood very still, watching the guttering flame with those hollow eyes, the eyes that had looked at Odile since the night on the guild steps with something worse than fear: recognition. A soul looking out of the wrong window.

Odile knelt. The stone was warm under her knees, blood-warm, the whole mountain alive with borrowed heat.

"Paloma," she said—and the name still caught in her chest, because it was not the child's name yet, it was the name the ledger had whispered to her in Corvane's cramped hand, *the vessel shall be called nothing until the debt is paid*—"I need you to listen. I'm going to do something loud."

The child tilted their head. Their lips moved around soundless shapes. Eleven years mute, eleven years of carrying someone else's voice in the hollow where their own should have been, and now—

Now the broker's price had been paid. Odile had laid her memory of the storm on the bone table like a coin, and the broker had taken it, and she had felt it go—a whole season of her life lifted out like a splinter—and she did not remember what the storm had been. She remembered that there had been one. She remembered a man dead in the wreckage of the harbor road, and a judge's sentence, and exile, but the storm itself was a door painted on a wall. She had paid not knowing what she'd lose, and this was what she'd lost: the thing she'd built her whole punishment around.

And it didn't matter. That was the terrible joke of it. The fire didn't need her memory. It needed her gift, and gifts lived deeper than memory, in the marrow, in the blood's oldest rooms. She could feel it there now, coiled and sulking, the thing she had sworn never to call again, whatever *again* meant when she couldn't remember the first time.

"Stand back," she told Sef. "All the way back. Take the child."

Sef didn't move. "Odile. Whatever this costs—"

"It costs everything. That's how you know it counts." She managed something that was almost a smile. "Go on. You kept me alive through the tunnels. Return the favor by living through this yourself."

Sef took the child's hand and retreated to the chamber's edge, beneath an arch of ribs grown into the rock like roots. The keeper withdrew too, folding backward into shadow, leaving Odile alone with the little flame and the enormous silence.

She closed her eyes.

Calling a storm, she discovered, was not like calling a dog or a debt. It was like calling yourself. Somewhere above the mountain, above the border city, above Magistrate Hale's marble courtroom and the drowned harbor and the whole gray country that had exiled her—somewhere up there the sky was heavy and undecided, full of water and lightning with nowhere to be. All she had to do was open the door she had nailed shut inside herself and say *come down.*

"I'm sorry," she said—to whom, she wasn't sure. To the sky. To the man in the wreckage whose face she could no longer see. To Corvane, wherever he was, riding down through the dark with whatever terrible price he meant to pay. "I'm sorry, and I'm asking anyway."

She opened the door.

The storm answered the way storms do—not politely, not gradually, but *all at once*, a pressure drop so sudden her ears popped, a green-black bruise blooming across the underside of her eyelids. Far overhead, muffled by a mile of stone, thunder began. Not the polite thunder of weather. The thunder of something enormous changing its mind.

"Here!" she cried, arms flung wide, palms up, the posture of surrender and of catching. "Here! Through me!"

Lightning does not travel through stone. But it travels through *storm-callers*, because a storm-caller is a door, and doors are made to be used. It came down through the mountain like a spear seeking its mark, and it found her, and it entered.

There was no describing it afterward and she would never try. It was every color and none. It was the taste of the oath she couldn't remember making, copper and rain. For one endless instant she was transparent, she was weather, she was the whole argument between earth and sky compressed into a girl kneeling in the dark—and then she turned it, the way a millwheel turns a flood into flour, and poured.

Into the flame.

The little fire drank. Gods, how it drank. It rose off the iron basin in a column of white-gold, and the chamber filled with roaring, and the sleeping eggs on the ceiling began—faintly, one by one—to glow. Odile knelt in the center of it, hair lifting, skin luminous, giving and giving, and somewhere in the middle of the giving she understood why the old dragons had done it freely. Because this—this was the opposite of exile. This was being *of* something, wholly, without remainder.

Then the door at the far end of the chamber banged open, and Corvane came through it burning his own paper.

He was holding the charter. The Gilded Hollow's founding charter, the one bought with a stolen soul, the document that had sheltered Odile for six years and damned a child for eleven—he held it over his head and it was already on fire, and he was shouting words in the First Tongue, the trade-tongue's grandfather, the language of binding and loosing:

*"I void! I void my signature, I void my seal, I return what was purchased and accept the forfeiture—"*

The price of voiding a contract with the Undercourt was written nowhere, because no one had ever been foolish enough or honest enough to do it. Now everyone in the chamber watched the price collect itself. Corvane aged as they watched—not smoothly, but in lurches, like a candle burning fast, years sloughing off him with each syllable. His black hair went salt. His straight back curved. His voice cracked and climbed into registers no merchant's voice should hold, and still he shouted, because he was Corvane Sterling-of-nowhere, master of the Gilded Hollow, and he had never once in his life let a debt stand unpaid, even when the debtor was himself.

"*—and the vessel is released! The soul returns to its body! So signed, so sealed, SO VOIDED!*"

The charter burned to ash in his ancient hands. And the child—

The child screamed.

Not silently. For the first time in eleven years, *loudly*—a raw, rusted, magnificent sound, the sound of a voice being born, as the soul came home through corridors of bone and bargain and slammed back into the small body like a bird returning to a window it remembered. The child doubled over. Light poured out of their mouth, their eyes, the seams of their skin, and Sef caught them, and the light gathered, and gathered—

And spoke.

"*Paloma.*"

The word rang off the rib-arches, off the glowing eggs, off the pillar of stormfire, and the mountain repeated it back, *paloma, paloma,* and the keeper unfolded from the shadows and bowed, low, to a child of perhaps nine years.

"That is your name," the keeper said. "It always was. Welcome back to yourself."

Paloma raised their head. The hollow eyes were not hollow anymore. They were brown and furious and alive and brimming, and they found Odile across the blazing chamber—found the woman kneeling in the heart of the fire she had called, translucent with light, spent past standing, smiling like a fool.

"You're the storm lady," Paloma said. Voice rusty, cracking, *theirs*. "You talked in your sleep. In the tunnels. About rain."

Odile laughed, and the laugh came out as sparks.

* * *

Afterward there was quiet, the particular velvet quiet that follows either an ending or a beginning. The fire burned tall and steady in its basin, fed now, patient now, a flame that would outlast them all if they were careful. The eggs glowed softly overhead like a ceiling of dawn. Corvane lay against the far wall, old and breathing, staring at the ash of his charter in his cupped palms as though reading something written there.

Sef sat with Paloma wrapped in their coat. The lantern-keeper's hands hadn't stopped shaking, but their voice, when it came, was steady enough:

"So what happens now? We're a long way underground, the collectors want us dead, and the guild master is ninety years old."

"Ninety-one," Corvale rasped, correcting them on principle even now.

Paloma slipped free of the coat and crossed the chamber to Odile, who had not yet found the strength to rise from her knees. The child studied her with those newly-lit eyes, then reached out and touched the basin's rim, where the fire burned without heat.

"It says thank you," Paloma reported gravely. "In the First Tongue. It says it hasn't eaten a real storm in eleven years, and yours was—" the child paused, hunting for the word, and found it, and delivered it with devastating accuracy— "*angry.*"

"It had reason to be," Odile said.

"And it says—" Paloma cocked their head, listening to something in the fire that no one else could hear, and their face changed, brightening and troubling at once. "It says the storm isn't finished. The one you called. It's still up there. It's been circling the mountain since you opened the door, and it won't leave until—"

Until what?

Above them, faint through a mile of stone, thunder rolled again. Patient. Expectant. A storm that had come all this way down for her and found only half of her waiting.

Paloma looked up at Odile and translated, simply:

"Until you come up and meet it."
