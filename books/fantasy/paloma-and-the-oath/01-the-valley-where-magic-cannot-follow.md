## 1. The Valley Where Magic Cannot Follow

The boundary stones woke before Isandro did.

He felt them the way he always felt them at first light — a faint hum through the soles of his boots, like a plucked string still trembling somewhere far below the earth. Twenty-two stones, one for each year of his service, set in a broken arc along the valley's northern rim where the pines gave way to bare granite and the air itself seemed to forget how to move. He had walked this line so many times that his feet knew the path better than his eyes did, and on mornings like this one, gray and windless and cold enough to bite, he let them.

*Left foot past the split boulder. Right foot over the root-bridge of the dead oak. Pause at the seventh stone. Breathe.*

Habit. All of it habit.

He paused at the seventh stone anyway, because the oath demanded it, and pressed two fingers to its surface. The stone was warm. It was always warm — the only warm thing up here besides his own blood — and it was always silent, which was the whole point, or so the elders said. The Silent God lived in silence the way fish lived in water. His shrine down in the valley held no idol, no altar-cloth, no offerings but the swept floor and a single unlit lamp that no one was permitted to light. You served Him by carrying nothing into His presence. No noise. No wonder. No magic.

Isandro had been carrying nothing for twenty-two years.

He was thirty-four years old and he had never once seen anything impossible. That was not an accident. That was the work. Every mercenary sworn to the valley's covenant bore the oath-marks — thin silver lines that traced the inside of the left forearm like veins seen through skin — and every morning those marks drank whatever strangeness tried to cross the boundary stones and gave it back to the mountain as silence. Wraiths unraveled against them. Charms curdled. Spells aimed at the valley died six feet out from Isandro's body like moths hitting a closed window. He was, in the elders' careful phrasing, *a living extension of the boundary*, and they said it with the pride of men describing a wall.

Walls were not asked what they wanted.

He checked the stone's warmth out of ritual rather than need, noted it in the little wax tablet he carried — *seventh stone, steady* — and moved on. Behind him the valley opened up in terraces of barley and apple orchard, smoke rising straight from a hundred chimneys, the whole human world folded small and safe between the mountain's shoulders. Ahead of him the world simply stopped being reliable. Beyond the last stone the ground rose toward the high passes, and everything there had a wrongness to it that ordinary eyes learned to skip over: shadows that pooled uphill, birds that flew in perfect straight lines without ever landing, a light that came from everywhere and belonged nowhere. Magic country. The court's country, though nobody in the valley used that word anymore. They just called it *beyond*, the way you'd call a grave *the other side of the door*.

His marks were quiet. They were always quiet. In twenty-two years they had flared exactly once, the day he swore, and he could no longer remember the feeling except as a memory of a memory, the way you remember a fever.

He was halfway between the ninth and tenth stones when he heard the singing.

It came threading down from the high pass, thin and sweet and utterly wrong — a melody that bent in the middle the way light bends through water. Isandro stopped. His hand found the hatchet at his belt without any decision being made about it, the way his heart found its rhythm, the way his breath found its pace. Everything about him that was still a man stepped back and let everything about him that was a wall do its work.

A traveler was coming down the pass.

She — he could see her now, a dark smudge resolving into a young woman with a pack half her size and a walking staff — was following the song. Of course she was. They always followed the song. The boundary wraiths had learned long ago that they couldn't reach into the valley, so they stood just outside it and sang, and waited for something foolish or lost or grieving to walk to them. The wraiths didn't eat flesh. They ate the part of you that remembered why you'd been walking anywhere at all, and what was left afterward usually sat down in the scree and stayed until weather took it.

Isandro broke from the line of stones and ran.

The ground beyond the boundary was soft in a way ground shouldn't be, absorbing sound, giving back nothing. His boots made no noise. His own breathing sounded distant, secondhand. This was the deadness the elders meant when they said the valley was protected: not that magic couldn't come in, but that within arm's reach of the stones, everything — wraith-song included — went hoarse and weak. Three steps further out and the song would have him too. He knew his range the way a swimmer knows the cold.

The traveler had reached the edge of it. She stood perhaps forty feet beyond the tenth stone, swaying gently, her staff dangling from two fingers, her face turned up toward a crag where nothing was. Up close the song resolved into words in no language at all, and underneath the words, if you listened with the part of yourself you weren't supposed to use, you could hear the hunger: a vast patience, a mouth the size of the sky.

"Hey." His voice came out flat and dead in the hush. "Hey. Look at me."

She didn't. Her eyes were fixed on the crag, and tears were running freely down her face, though her expression was one of unbearable happiness. Whatever the wraith was showing her, it was better than anything she'd ever had. That was how it worked. That was why the elders forbade anyone to go past the stones even to pull people back — because the wraith showed each rescuer their own best thing, and some of them, over the centuries, had stayed.

Isandro had never once looked at the crag. Not in twenty-two years. He kept his eyes on the traveler's face, on the tears, on the living proof that she was still in there, and he walked forward into the song.

It hit him like warm water. *Come home,* it said, in the voice of everyone he had ever failed to become. *You are tired. You have been a wall for so long. Here is the place where walls can rest.* And beneath it, sweetly, patiently, the image began to assemble itself at the edge of his vision — a hearth he almost recognized, a table set for more than one, a life with weight and warmth in it instead of patrol rotations and wax tablets and the swept floor of an empty shrine.

He did not look. He had trained himself not to look the way other men trained themselves not to bleed, and the effort cost him what it always cost, a white-hot pressure behind the eyes and a taste like coins.

"Your name," he said to the traveler, loud and flat, a voice like a hammer breaking crockery. "Give me your name."

Her lips moved. Nothing came out.

"Say your name. Names don't grow here. Nothing grows here. Say it and it'll be yours and it will weigh more than that song."

Her mouth worked. A thread of sound escaped, half-drowned: "*Marisol—*" and then the wraith's melody surged, furious, trying to bury the word, and the crag above them rippled like the surface of a pond with something enormous turning over beneath it.

Isandro reached her, caught her wrist, and pulled.

The moment his oath-marks crossed into range of the tenth stone, the world snapped. The song cut off mid-note — not faded, *cut*, like a throat gripped shut — and the traveler staggered against him, suddenly heavy, suddenly real, suddenly weeping in great ugly gulps against his shoulder while the crag behind them settled back into being merely rock. Something in the hush around them exhaled. Somewhere upslope, in a register below hearing, Isandro felt the wraith withdraw, unhurried, unoffended. It could wait. It had waited longer than his family line had existed.

"It showed me my mother," the woman gasped. "She's been dead nine years and it showed me her kitchen, it showed me her hands, it—" She stopped, and looked at him properly for the first time, and whatever she saw in his face made her go quiet. "You heard it too."

"I hear it every day." He steadied her, stepped back, and became again what the valley required: courteous, complete, and closed. "Isandro Villalobos. Oath-keeper, third watch. You're past the boundary stones, which means you're either very lost or very determined, and I've learned not to bet against determined. Come down to the valley. There's bread, and there's an elder who decides what happens to people who cross where they shouldn't."

"My brother went ahead of me," she said. "Three weeks ago. Through the pass. I'm looking for him."

Isandro looked at the high passes, at the light that came from everywhere and belonged nowhere, and said nothing for a moment. Then: "Then you'd better come and tell the elders everything. And Marisol—" he hesitated, because the next part was habit too, worn smooth as the shrine floor, "—whatever it showed you. Don't go back for it. It wasn't yours. It was bait."

They walked down together toward the stones, and as they crossed the tenth one Isandro felt the familiar faint hum rise through his boots, the boundary acknowledging its wall, and behind them the high passes lay silent and patient under their impossible light.

At the bottom of the path, where the first terrace of barley began, a girl was standing by the shrine gate with a leather satchel of maps and ink-stained fingers, arguing cheerfully with old Tomás about whether the valley's eastern ridge could possibly exist, because it wasn't on any chart she'd ever drawn.

Isandro slowed. He did not know yet why he slowed.

The girl looked up, caught him watching, and smiled like first light coming over the boundary stones.

* * *

That evening, after the elders had questioned Marisol and quartered her in the guest house, Isandro climbed to the shrine as he did every night, swept the floor, and sat a while in the dark beside the unlit lamp.

"You should know," he told the silence, because twenty-two years of habit deserved at least conversation, "I saved one today. Out past the stones. I didn't look at the crag this time either."

The silence said nothing. The silence always said nothing.

But when he rose to leave, his oath-marks ached — a deep, bone-old ache, like a chain shifting in its sleep — and for the first time in all his years of service, Isandro Villalobos stood in the doorway of the empty shrine and wondered, quietly and terribly, who exactly he had been keeping all this silence for.
