## 2. A Date in the Ordinary World

Isandro had dressed for this the way he did everything: twice, and with suspicion.

The first shirt had been too formal — the kind of collar that announced intention like a drawn blade. The second was too casual, the gray one with the frayed cuff that said he hadn't tried at all. He'd settled on a third, a deep blue woven thing his sister had sent from the coast two years ago and which he had never once found an occasion for, because occasions were not something the valley offered in abundance.

Now, standing outside the archive-house as the evening light went amber over the rooftops of Solmara, he suspected the shirt was a mistake anyway. It made him look like a man who expected something. And expectation, in his experience, was just another way of handing someone a knife and showing them where you kept your soft parts.

*You're forty minutes early,* he told himself. *Go walk. Come back.*

He walked. He always walked when his hands didn't know what to do — along the low wall by the millrace, past the shrine where the Silent God's stone face watched the water with its empty patience. The shrine had no offerings tonight. It never did, really; the valley's devotion had long ago thinned into habit, into the oath-marks on his forearm and the boundary stones and not much else. Isandro touched two fingers to the god's cold brow anyway, the way his mother had taught him before she died, and felt nothing but stone.

*One night,* he thought. *Let me have one ordinary night.*

Because that was what Paloma Reyes had asked him for. Not a patrol, not a favor, not a consultation about the strange deadness at the valley's edge that she kept circling in her notes like a hawk over a field. A *date*. She'd used the word deliberately, watching him over her tea with that direct dark gaze of hers, and he had heard himself say yes before the part of him that managed risk could get a word in.

He came back to the archive-house with six minutes still to spare and found her already there, sitting on the steps with a leather satchel across her knees, sketching.

She looked up, and the whole careful architecture of his composure developed a crack straight down the middle.

Paloma Reyes drew maps the way other people prayed — total, unselfconscious, her whole attention poured into the page. She had ink on her fingers already, a smudge high on her left cheekbone where she must have pushed back her hair, and when she saw him she smiled the crooked smile that started on one side and took its time arriving at the other.

"You're early," she said.

"So are you."

"I'm always early. Maps reward it." She closed the sketchbook and stood, brushing off her skirt. "You look—" Her eyes moved over him once, quick and honest, the way she read terrain. "Tall," she finished, and then laughed at herself, pressing the ink-stained hand to her mouth. "That was terrible. I'm sorry. I've spent three weeks talking to surveyors and goats. My conversation has atrophied."

"Goats are honest company," Isandro said gravely. "Better than most."

"See, that's why I like you." She fell into step beside him, close enough that their shoulders nearly brushed, and he became acutely aware of every inch of the space between them, the way he was aware of wind direction, of the weight of a blade, of the exact distance between himself and anything that might do harm. Except Paloma wasn't a threat. That was the disorienting part. She was the opposite of a threat, and he had no training whatsoever for the opposite of a threat.

"I have a confession," she said as they turned down toward the market square, where the lantern-sellers were making their rounds and the smell of roasting peppers drifted from the corner house. "I've been looking forward to tonight since Tuesday."

"It's Friday."

"I know how calendars work, Isandro." She glanced up at him, and there was something underneath the humor, something almost shy. "I'm telling you I've been looking forward to it. Out loud. Because I've noticed you don't believe good things until they're stated plainly, and I'd rather you believed this one."

He stopped walking.

It was such a small thing, what she'd said. But it landed in him like an arrow finding the gap in armor nobody else had ever bothered to find. Three years of patrols. Ten years of oaths. A lifetime of being the man who watched the edges so others could live safely in the middle, unseen, unnamed, useful and alone. And here was this woman, four weeks into knowing him, saying *I see you, and I'm glad.*

"We should eat," he managed. "Before I say something foolish."

"Foolish is my favorite genre," Paloma said. "But all right. Feed me first. I'm told mercenary types fight better on a full stomach, and if this goes badly I may need to defend your honor against the entire market square."

* * *

They ate at Doña Inés's table under the strung lanterns, elbow to elbow at the long communal bench, and Isandro learned that Paloma Reyes ate the way she did everything — with appetite and attention, commenting on the saffron in the rice, interrogating Doña Inés about where the peppers were grown, drawing a little map in spilled salt of the trade routes she thought the peppers must have traveled.

"You can't help it, can you?" he said.

"Help what?"

"That. Mapping everything. You mapped the salt."

She looked down at the tiny etched coastline she'd been absently tracing and laughed. "No. I can't help it. It's a disease." She wiped the salt away with her palm, gently, like erasing something sacred. "My grandmother says I was born holding a pencil. She says when I was small I drew maps of places that didn't exist, and when she scolded me for wasting paper, I told her they weren't wasted because someday I'd find those places and already know the way."

"Do you believe that?"

"I believe the maps were better than I knew." Something flickered across her face — quickly, gone before he could name it. "Why do you think I came here, Isandro? To catalog a valley? There are a hundred valleys. This one has geography that argues with itself. The river runs uphill for half a mile above the millrace. The boundary stones cast shadows at noon even on cloudless days. Your mountains appear on no survey made beyond the pass, and the ones that do show them disagree about how many peaks there are." She ticked each impossibility off on ink-stained fingers. "I've been mapping this valley for three weeks and it keeps *changing under my pencil*. Not erosion. Not season. Changing. Like it doesn't want to be known."

"The valley keeps its own counsel," Isandro said. It was the official answer, the one drilled into every oath-keeper, and he heard how hollow it sounded the moment it left his mouth.

Paloma studied him. "And what does the unofficial answer say?"

He turned his cup slowly on the wood. Around them the market noise went on — laughter, haggling, a fiddle starting up somewhere near the fountain — and none of it touched the small quiet space that had opened between them.

"The unofficial answer," he said finally, "is that I've guarded the boundary for ten years, and I've stopped asking questions the elders won't answer. Magic doesn't cross the stones. Nothing crosses the stones. Things that try don't come back the same, if they come back at all. We're told it's protection. We're told the Silent God set the border Himself, out of love for us." He met her eyes. "We're not told much else."

"And you believe that?"

"I believe in the oath. Belief is optional. Oath isn't." He heard the bitterness in it and was surprised by it — surprised that she could pull things out of him he hadn't known were there, the way water finds cracks in stone. "I'm sorry. This is supposed to be a date. I'm supposed to be charming."

"You are charming," Paloma said. "In a grim, heavily armed sort of way. Besides—" she leaned closer, lowering her voice conspiratorially, "—you just told me more truth in five minutes than the entire archive staff has given me in three weeks. If that's not seduction, I don't know what is."

The laugh escaped him before he could stop it — a real one, rusty, startling a nearby child into staring. Isandro Villalobos did not laugh often. He'd forgotten what it cost, and what it bought.

"There it is," Paloma said softly. "I was starting to worry it was extinct."

* * *

Afterward they walked, because neither of them wanted the evening to end and neither would say so. The moon had come up fat and white over the eastern ridge, laying silver along the rooftops, and the fiddle music followed them for two streets before fading behind the old tannery wall.

Paloma talked. Isandro discovered he liked listening — liked the way her mind moved, leaping from the migration patterns of valley swallows to the economics of ink to a story about the worst innkeeper in the southern provinces, delivered with such relish he laughed twice more. And then, without quite deciding to, he began talking too. About growing up in the shadow of the boundary stones. About his mother teaching him the oath-words before she taught him letters. About the traveler he'd pulled back from a wraith at the north marker last spring, and how the man had wept and thanked him and never once asked his name.

"You should be asked your name," Paloma said quietly. "Everyone should be."

"It's Isandro."

"I know that, Isandro. I mean by the valley. By everyone. You give them everything and they don't even—" She stopped herself, pressed her lips together. "Forgive me. I'm lecturing a man about his own life."

"No one's ever lectured me about my own life," he said. "It's novel. Continue."

She elbowed him, and he caught her elbow — lightly, reflexively, the mercenary reflex — and then didn't let go, and she didn't pull away, and for a moment they stood in the middle of the empty street with the moon on the cobbles and her hand in his, both of them pretending this was an accident.

"Show me something," she said. "Something only you know about this valley. Not the archive version. Yours."

So he took her up the switchback path behind the shrine, the one that climbed to the overlook where the boundary stones marched away north and south like a spine of grey teeth. It was the place he went when the silence got too heavy, though he'd never told anyone that. From up here the whole valley lay below — lamplit, small, beloved — and beyond the stones the land changed. Not visibly. Visibly it was just hills and dark forest. But Isandro had looked at that line for ten years and knew it the way you know the edge of a wound.

Paloma stood beside him at the rail and went very still.

Then she opened her satchel, took out her sketchbook, and began to draw.

He watched over her shoulder. Her pencil moved fast now, faster than it had all evening, sure and hungry, and what came onto the page made the hair rise on his arms. It was the view before them — the valley, the stones, the dark beyond — but rendered with a precision no eye could supply. Contour lines flowing through the forest beyond the boundary as if she'd surveyed it. A second river beneath the first, dashed and speculative. And in the heart of the mountain itself, where no cartographer had ever been, a shape like a great coiled root-system descending and descending, converging on some central chamber she shaded darker and darker until the pencil tore slightly at the paper.

"Paloma," he said carefully. "Have you been beyond the stones?"

"No." Her voice had gone distant, absent, the voice of someone reading aloud from somewhere far away. "Never. No one has. But I keep dreaming about it, Isandro. Every night since I arrived. Stairs going down. Roots. A door that breathes." The pencil paused. "And tonight I dreamed about wine. Red wine, spilling on my sleeve, and someone reading my name out loud in a room full of—"

She stopped.

The pencil had snapped in her fingers.

For a long moment neither of them moved. Below them the valley glowed, innocent and warm, and beyond the boundary stones the dark land lay exactly as it always lay — patient, silent, waiting. On Isandro's forearm, beneath his sleeve, the oath-marks ached suddenly, deeply, the way they had not ached in years — a slow cold pulse, like something turning over in its sleep.

"Isandro?" Paloma's voice was very small. "Why did the air just get colder?"

He looked up. The moon still hung over the ridge. The lamps still burned below. But along the line of the boundary stones, faint as breath on glass, something shimmered — a seam of darkness opening in the open air, like a page turning in a book no one was holding.

"Get behind me," Isandro said, and reached for a sword he hadn't brought to a date.
