## 3. The Book That Knew Her Name

The archive of the valley smelled of cold stone and older paper, a smell like the inside of a sealed letter. Paloma Reyes had been working there for eleven days, and she had already learned to move through it the way she moved through any landscape: reading first, touching second, trusting nothing until she'd traced its edges with her own hands.

"Careful with the west shelves," Old Tomás had told her that morning, shuffling past with his lantern and his perpetual air of a man who had outlived everyone he'd ever argued with. "They lean. Like everything else in this valley."

She'd smiled at that. She was smiling at most things this week, which was unlike her. Her smile kept arriving unbidden — on the stairs down into the archive, at breakfast, once in the middle of sketching a contour line so badly she'd had to erase it twice.

Isandro Villalobos.

She wrote his name in her field notebook the way she wrote coordinates: small, precise, in the margin where marginal things belonged. Except it wasn't staying in the margin. It kept bleeding toward the center of the page.

Their date had been three nights ago. Dinner at the tavern under the crooked eaves, where the lamplight did her the favor of softening every awkward silence before Isandro filled it with something better. He'd talked about the boundary stones with the reverence other men reserved for lovers or battles. She'd talked about maps, about how the valley's rivers refused to behave, how every survey she ran came back a half-degree wrong, as if the land itself were holding its breath. He'd listened. Actually listened — leaning forward, elbows on the table, wine forgotten beside his hand until he knocked the glass with his knuckle and red spilled across the linen and soaked up her sleeve where her wrist rested on the table.

"I'm sorry," he'd said, mortified, dabbing at it with a napkin.

"It's fine," she'd said. "It's just wine."

It wasn't just wine. It was the first date she'd had in two years that she hadn't wanted to end, and now her sleeve carried a faint rust-colored ghost of it, and she had not washed the shirt because she was, apparently, the kind of woman who kept stained shirts like relics.

Focus, Reyes. You're here to map an impossible valley, not to catalog a man.

She turned back to the shelf.

Her commission was straightforward enough on paper: the Royal Cartographic Society wanted a full survey of the valley and its boundaries, mostly, she suspected, because no one had ever managed one. The valley resisted being measured. Compasses wandered. Distances disagreed with themselves depending on the hour. And along the eastern rim, where the boundary stones stood in their ancient patient row, her instruments simply went quiet — needles drooping, pendulums stalling, as though the world beyond those stones had been subtracted from arithmetic itself.

She'd come down into the archive hoping for old surveys, old measurements, anything that might explain why this place sat in the world like a hole cut from cloth.

What she found instead, on the fourth shelf of the west stacks, wedged behind a water-damaged registry of goat sales, was a book bound in grey leather with no title at all.

It was heavy in a way that had nothing to do with its size. She felt the weight settle into her wrists before she'd even lifted it clear of the shelf, and a strange pressure followed, like standing too near a cliff edge in the dark.

"No title," she murmured. "No colophon. No date."

But the leather was old. A century old, maybe more, by the crackle of it under her fingers. She carried it to the reading table beneath the archive's single high window, where afternoon light fell in a dusty column, and opened it.

The handwriting inside was small and cramped and utterly certain of itself.

*Entries,* she realized. Dated entries. Someone had kept a record here — not of goats, not of harvests, but of *events*, each one written down after the fact with the dry economy of an accountant:

*Third of Thaw-Month. The miller's second son will lose two fingers to the saw. He will keep them wrapped in linen and will not weep.*

Paloma frowned. *Will*. Not *did*. The entry was dated a hundred years ago, and yet it read like a prophecy, written in past tense.

She flipped forward.

*Midsummer. A fire in the tannery, begun by lightning, ending in rain. No deaths. The tanner's daughter will carry the scar on her left arm and be ashamed of it until she is forty-one, when she will stop being ashamed all at once, for reasons she will never explain to anyone.*

Paloma's skin prickled. She set the book flat on the table, pressed both palms against the pages as if to hold them still, and looked around the empty archive. Lanterns. Dust. The tick of cooling stone.

Just a curiosity, she told herself. Some mad monk's exercise. A hundred years of invented futures, most of them unverifiable, all of them unfalsifiable—

Except.

She remembered the tanner's daughter. Well — she remembered *a* tanner's daughter, the old woman who sold leather goods at the market square, who wore long sleeves in summer and pulled them down over her left forearm whenever children stared. Everyone knew the story of the burn, how lightning had struck the tannery a lifetime ago and the rain had saved the town.

Paloma's mouth had gone dry.

She began turning pages faster, scanning dates, hunting for something recent, something checkable, something that would let her dismiss the whole book with a laugh. Entries slid past: floods, weddings, a landslide, a hanging reprieved at the last moment by a pardon arriving on a lame horse. Each written in the same cramped hand — then, somewhere past the midpoint, a second hand joined it, and later a third, as though the book had been passed down like an inheritance nobody wanted but nobody dared refuse.

The most recent entries were decades old. Then the writing stopped mid-sentence:

*The year the cartographer comes—*

And nothing more.

Palomo sat very still.

*The year the cartographer comes.* She was a cartographer. She had come. Eleven days ago, with her instruments and her notebooks and her society seal, into a valley that didn't want to be measured.

"That's coincidence," she said aloud, to hear a voice in the room. Her voice sounded thin against the stone.

She turned the page. Blank. Turned another. Blank. The rest of the codex was empty paper waiting for entries that had never been written — except that the final leaf wasn't blank at all. It held one last entry, in the third hand, fresher than the others, the ink barely browned with age:

*In the year the cartographer comes, she will dine beneath the crooked eaves with the keeper of the stones. Wine will spill. It will fall across her left sleeve, at the wrist, and she will keep the shirt unwashed.*

The archive went very quiet. Or perhaps Paloma did; perhaps the sound draining out of the world was entirely hers, her own heartbeat suddenly loud in her ears, her own breath shallow and careful, as though too much movement might shake loose what she was seeing.

She looked down at her left sleeve. At the faint rust-colored stain at the wrist. At the shirt she had not washed because she was keeping it, like a relic, like a flower pressed between pages.

"No," she whispered.

Her hands were shaking. She flattened one against the page anyway and read on, because her whole life she had been the sort of person who walked toward the thing she feared rather than away from it — that was why she mapped borders, why she charted coastlines that swallowed ships, why she had taken a commission into a valley that made compass needles lie down and sleep.

Below the entry about the wine, in smaller script, almost apologetic, the third hand continued:

*She will find this book. This is also written.*

A sound escaped her — half laugh, half sob. Of course. Of course it would say that. Any clever forger could construct the trap she'd just stepped in: write that the reader would find the book, and the reader's finding becomes proof. It was the oldest trick in prophecy, self-sealing, self-crowning.

Except the wine. The wine wasn't a trick. The wine had happened three nights ago, before she ever touched this book, described in ink that had dried before her grandmother learned to walk.

Paloma pushed back from the table. Stood. Paced the length of the reading room and back, her boots loud on the stone. Through the high window, the valley lay gold and ordinary in the late light — rooftops, terraced fields, the smoke of supper fires beginning to rise, the dark line of boundary stones along the east rim where her instruments went quiet.

An ordinary valley. An ordinary evening. Somewhere out there Isandro was walking his patrol, probably thinking about her, hopefully thinking about her, in the uncomplicated way men thought about women they'd shared bread with.

She sat back down. She made herself turn back to the last written page, because there was more of it — lines she'd seen and refused to finish reading, the way you refuse to look directly at a wound.

*On the seventh night after the cartographer comes, the court beneath the mountain will send for what it is owed.*

*The debt is old. Older than the valley. Older than the stones.*

*Her name was entered in the ledger four generations ago, by her own blood, in exchange for safe passage and green fields. The valley was bought. She is part of the price.*

*Her soul is due beneath the mountain.*

Paloma read it three times. The words did not soften with repetition. They got heavier, the way stones get heavier the longer you carry them.

"My soul," she said stupidly. As if saying it slowly might reveal it to be a different word.

Four generations ago. Her family had lived in the lowlands for as long as anyone could remember — farmers, then tradesmen, then her father, who'd sold everything to put her through the cartographers' guild. There was no valley in her family history. No mountain. No debt she'd ever heard named.

But families lied. Families forgot. Families buried things so deep the graves themselves were lost, and passed down only the shape of the silence.

*By her own blood.*

She thought of her grandmother, who had gone rigid every time Paloma asked about the family's origins. Who had once, drunk on festival wine, gripped Paloma's wrist hard enough to bruise and said, *Never sign anything you don't understand, niña. Never sign.* Then laughed it off, next morning, as festival nonsense.

Never sign anything you don't understand.

Someone had signed. Four generations ago, someone with her blood in their veins had signed away — what? Everything downstream? Every descendant? Had her grandmother known, all those years, gripping wrists and repeating warnings, that the bill was still coming due?

Paloma became aware, gradually, that she was crying. Not dramatically — just tears arriving without permission, sliding down her face while she stared at the page. She wiped them with the heel of her hand and laughed at herself, a cracked little sound.

This was madness. This was a book of tricks and she was a trained professional with instruments in her room that could measure the angle of the sun to a fraction of a degree, and the rational response was to close the codex, return it to the shelf behind the goat registry, and go find Isandro and tell him — tell him what?

That a dead man's handwriting had described their date?

*Seventh night.* Tonight was the fifth. Two days.

Or — she counted again, checking her notebook, checking the dates against her survey log — tonight was the fifth night since her arrival. The dinner had been the second night. Which meant the seventh night was tomorrow.

Tomorrow.

* * *

She did not sleep. That surprised her less than it should have. She spent the evening doing what she always did with fear: she worked.

She copied the entire final entry into her notebook, word for word, in her own precise cartographer's hand, as though transcribing it might convert it from omen into data. She cross-referenced the codex's verifiable prophecies against parish records in the archive — the tannery fire, yes; the miller's son, yes, the man was sixty now and wore gloves to hide the missing fingers; the lame horse and the pardon, recorded in the magistrate's rolls to the day. Entry after entry, prediction after fulfillment, a century of futures written before they happened, accurate as survey work.

There was no rational explanation left. She kept looking for one anyway, because that was who she was, right up until the moment she couldn't.

Near midnight, she found herself back at the reading table, the codex open to the last written page, and she noticed something she'd missed. In the margin beside the final entry, in ink so pale it was nearly invisible, someone had added a single line — not prophecy, but commentary, the writer breaking form for the only time in a hundred years of entries:

*We tried to warn them above. They do not read. If you are reading this, cartographer: the ledger can be read. What is written in a book may be struck from it. Find the ledger.*

Paloma read the words until they blurred.

*Find the ledger.*

The candle guttered. Outside, the valley slept under a fat indifferent moon, and far off along the eastern rim she could see — or imagined she could see — the boundary stones, pale as teeth in the dark, keeping whatever watch they had kept for centuries. Keeping watch, or keeping something in. Or keeping something *down*.

*Beneath the mountain.* The court beneath the mountain. Every culture she'd studied had its underworld stories, its courts of the dead, its debts collected in darkness. Stories, she'd always believed. Comfortable fictions shaped like fears.

But the wine was on her sleeve.

Paloma closed the codex gently, the way you close the eyes of the dead, and pressed both hands flat on its grey cover. Her heart was hammering but her mind had gone strangely calm, the way it did in the field when a storm rolled in — panic useless, action required, plan first, terror later.

Tomorrow. Seventh night. Whatever was owed, whatever court waited below, whatever her blood had signed away four generations ago — it would come for her tomorrow, and the only person in this valley who might believe her, who had looked at the boundary stones like they were scripture and the Silent God like a friend who'd stepped out of the room, was a mercenary with oath-marks on his arms and wine-spill guilt in his eyes.

She would go to him at dawn. She would show him the book. And if he thought she was mad, well —

She glanced at the stain on her sleeve, faint as an old wound.

Well, then she would be mad alone, but she'd been mad alone before.

She blew out the candle. And in the sudden dark of the archive, from the direction of the western wall — from the deep foundations, from below, from underneath — she heard, distinctly, unmistakably, the sound of a great door opening somewhere far underground.

Then, faint and patient and horribly courteous, as if carried up through a century of stone:

A knock.
