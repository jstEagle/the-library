## 4. Taken Beneath

The wine went first.

Isandro saw it happen the way he saw everything that mattered — a half-second too late to stop it. Paloma laughed at something he'd said about the shrine's crooked door, lifted her glass to punctuate her own rebuttal, and her elbow caught the edge of the candle-holder, and the whole dark red weight of it tipped across the table and into her lap.

"Oh no." She looked down at herself. The wine had bloomed across her sleeve in a spreading stain shaped like nothing and everything — a wing, a river delta, a country that didn't exist yet. "Oh *no*, this is your good tablecloth."

"It's the tavern's tablecloth," Isandro said. "And it's had worse done to it."

"That is not comforting."

"It wasn't meant to be. It was meant to make you stop looking like the world ended."

She looked up at him then, and the lamplight caught the stain and the smile fighting its way through her embarrassment, and Isandro thought — with the particular clarity that comes only once or twice in a life — *I would like to remember exactly this.* The smell of stewed lamb and tallow smoke. The rain just beginning against the window of the Drowned Lantern. Her sleeve ruined. Her eyes bright.

"I'll pay for it," she said.

"You will not. I'm the one who put the candle too close."

"You're the one who made me laugh." She said it simply, without artifice, the way she said most things, and it landed in his chest like a stone dropped into deep water.

He reached for the cloth napkin, dabbing uselessly at her sleeve while she held her arm out with the patient resignation of someone used to being mended by other people's inadequate efforts. Her skin was warm under his fingers. His oath-marks lay quiet beneath his own sleeves, as they always did, as they had for eleven years — old scars in the shape of words he'd never been allowed to read, inked into him at nineteen when he'd knelt before the boundary stone and sworn himself to the valley and the Silent God who kept it.

*Keep what cannot be kept,* the oath began. He'd never known what the rest of it meant. None of them did. They spoke the words; the words did their work; magic stayed out of the valley and the valley stayed alive, and that was the whole of the bargain.

"Your hands are shaking," Paloma said quietly.

"They're not."

"They are. Here—" She covered his hand with hers. "Cold night. Bad circulation. Mercenary's occupational hazard?"

"Something like that."

It wasn't cold in the tavern. It wasn't anything in him except a low, rising wrongness, like the pressure change before a storm front crosses a ridge line. He'd felt it once before, years ago, the night the boundary wraith came out of the fog and he'd learned how fast a man could move when the alternative was dying stupid. But that had been fear. This was something older. Something underneath the fear, underneath the floorboards, underneath the mountain itself.

Paloma was still talking — something about the survey, about how the eastern ridges didn't close properly on any map she drew, about how she'd started to think the valley was *hiding* from her instruments — when the lamps guttered.

All of them. At once.

The tavern didn't go dark. That would have been simple. Instead the light bent, leaned toward the door as if every flame in the room were suddenly facing the same direction, the way grass leans before wind, though there was no wind. The fire in the hearth flattened to blue. The rain on the windows stopped mid-fall — Isandro would swear to his grave he saw the drops hang there, suspended, each one holding its own small distorted lamp-flame — and the silence that followed was so complete he could hear the blood moving in his own ears.

Every patron in the Drowned Lantern had gone still. Not frozen. Still. The way animals go still when they smell wolf.

"Isandro," Paloma whispered. "What is—"

"Don't finish the sentence."

"Why not?"

"Because I don't know what it does yet."

But she was already finishing it. He could see it building behind her teeth — some ordinary, lovely thing, *what is happening* or *are we safe* or *stay with me tonight*, and he would never learn which it was, because the floor opened.

Not exploded. Not cracked. *Opened*, the way a hand opens, the boards peeling apart along a seam that had always been there waiting beneath the grain, and out of it came the smell of deep stone and old sweetness, like honey left too long in a sealed jar, and out of it came the hounds.

They were not dogs. They were the idea of dogs, rendered in shadow and hunger — shapes that moved wrong, legs bending where legs shouldn't, bodies flowing like poured ink and yet striking with the precision of drawn blades. Their eyes were coins. Old coins, green with verdigris, and they did not blink because there was nothing behind them that needed to.

The tavern erupted. Chairs went over. Someone screamed. The innkeeper's daughter bolted for the kitchen. And Isandro moved.

He moved the way the valley had trained him to move, eleven years of patrol-drill and wraith-fighting compressed into a single fluid violence — off his chair, between Paloma and the seam, one arm sweeping her behind him, the other hand already finding the knife at his back. Steel wouldn't matter. He knew steel wouldn't matter even as he drew it. But a man defends with what his hands know, and his hands knew the blade.

"Hounds," he heard himself say, calm and far away, the voice he used in reports. "Court-hounds. Stay behind me. Don't look into their eyes."

"What court?" Paloma's voice, pressed against his shoulder blade. "Isandro, what court—"

"The mountain's. There's a court beneath the mountain." He had never said those words aloud in his life. He hadn't known he knew them. They came out of him smooth and certain, like water finding a channel cut long ago. "*Everyone down!*"

The nearest hound lunged. He cut at it and the blade passed through shadow and met something dense beneath, a resistance like cutting frozen river-water, and the hound shrieked — a sound with no throat in it, a sound that arrived directly inside the skull — and recoiled, and Isandro understood with cold clarity that he had hurt it and that hurting it had changed nothing.

There were four of them. They spread with terrible patience, flanking, the way wolves flank, the way soldiers flank, and he realized they weren't hunting the room. They weren't hunting anyone else. Every coin-eye was fixed past his shoulder, past his knife, on the woman crouched behind him.

"No," he said. Out loud, absurdly, to creatures that did not bargain. "No. You're not taking her."

One of them tilted its head. Almost politely.

Then three of them hit him at once.

He fought. He wanted, later, to believe he had fought well, and in truth there was a stretch of seconds — he would count them forever, five, maybe six — where he was magnificent, where the knife found the dense heart-shadow of one hound and *tore*, where he got an arm around another's impossible neck and slammed it into the table hard enough to crack the board, where he was everything the valley had made him and more than it had ever needed him to be. A lamp shattered. Stew went across the floor. Somewhere a woman was praying in the old dead tongue the elders still mouthed at funerals.

And then a weight of cold closed over him like a lid, like the mountain itself setting a hand on his chest, and he went down onto the broken boards with all four shadows pressing him flat, and he watched — pinned, breathless, *useless* — as two of them turned to Paloma Reyes.

She didn't scream. He remembered that afterward, clung to it in the black hours that followed: she did not scream. She stood her ground with her stained sleeve and her wide dark eyes and she looked at the hounds the way she looked at impossible geography — with fury and fascination in equal measure, a cartographer confronting a place that refused to be mapped.

"You can't have me," she said. "Whatever this is. Whoever sent you. My name is mine."

The lead hound's coin-eyes reflected her face, small and upside-down.

"It was never yours," it said. Its voice was the sound a ledger makes when a page turns. "It was signed away before your grandmother's grandmother drew breath. We have come only to collect what is owed."

And Isandro, flat on the floor with the mountain's cold in his bones, felt the words land somewhere deeper than his ears. Because he knew contracts. Oaths were contracts. He had lived inside one for half his life, and he knew the tone of a clause executing exactly as written, and that was what he heard in the hound's voice — not malice. *Procedure.*

"Paloma," he gasped. "Run. Don't—"

She ran. Of course she ran; she was quick and clever and she'd grown up on streets harder than this valley's lanes. She made it four steps. The remaining hounds flowed around Isandro's body like water around a stone, unhurried, and the seam in the floor widened, yawning now, showing stairs that had not been visible before — worn stone descending into warm dark, lit from below by a gold light that had no source and no mercy in it.

They took her arms. She fought — gods, she fought, twisting, driving her heel into shadow and connecting with something solid enough to elicit that skull-sound again — but they were many and she was one and the seam pulled, the whole tavern leaning subtly toward it now, chairs sliding, plates ringing across the floor into the dark.

"ISANDRO!"

Her hand caught his. Their fingers locked. For one instant he felt her whole weight hanging on his grip, felt her pulse hammering against his palm, felt the absolute totality of what he stood to lose, and every muscle in his body tore against the cold pinning him down and gained — nothing. An inch. Half an inch. The hounds' cold was not strength; it was *law*, and law does not strain against muscle.

"Let go," she said, and her voice broke clean through her fear into something fierce and clear. "Isandro. *Let go*, you'll tear—"

"Never."

"*Never*" — and here, in the last second, she almost smiled, tears cutting tracks through the lamplight on her face — "is the stupidest word in any language."

Then the seam took her.

It happened between one heartbeat and the next — her fingers sliding from his grip with the terrible slow inevitability of something already decided, already recorded, already *filed*. He saw her fall backward into the gold light. He saw her reach for him even as she fell. He saw her mouth shape his name one final time, and then the hounds poured in after her like ink returning to a bottle, and the seam closed, and the boards were whole, and the lamps stood upright, and the rain resumed against the windows as if it had never stopped.

The Drowned Lantern was silent. Fifteen people staring at a floor that held nothing but spilled wine and a cracked table and one man kneeling with an empty hand outstretched.

Isandro knelt there. He did not move. Somewhere very far away, the innkeeper was saying his name, and the innkeeper's daughter was crying, and none of it reached him, because everything in him had narrowed to a single point of absence: the warmth fading out of his right palm, the ghost-grip of her fingers, gone.

*It was signed away before your grandmother's grandmother drew breath.*

*We have come only to collect what is owed.*

* * *

The pain started in his forearms.

At first he thought he'd been cut in the fight — hounds had touched him, after all, had pinned him, and shadow-wounds were a known evil along the boundary. He turned his arm over, expecting blood.

The oath-marks were burning.

Eleven years, they had lain dormant — brown-black lines spiraling from wrist to elbow, the script of his vow, inert as any scar. Now they glowed. Not red, not angry — *gold*, the same gold as the light below the seam, and they moved. He watched, with a horror that bypassed fear entirely and went somewhere colder, as the letters crawled. Slowly, laboriously, like insects waking from a long winter, the script rotated along his skin, rearranging, and one word surfaced above the rest, larger than the others, brighter:

*OWED.*

"No," Isandro said aloud, to his own arms, to the god whose marks they were. "No. She is not part of my oath. She has nothing to do with my oath."

The marks pulsed. Once. Twice. And then — unmistakably, impossibly — they *answered*. A sensation moved up through them into his chest, and it was not pain and it was not comfort; it was attention. Vast and buried and straining, like something enormous turning over in a sleep too deep to wake from, and for one vertiginous moment Isandro Villalobos knelt in a puddle of spilled wine and understood that the Silent God had felt Paloma Reyes taken. Had felt it the way a sleeper feels a door slam three rooms away. Was feeling it *now*, pressing upward against something that held Him down.

The marks dimmed. The presence receded — not gone, never gone, but folded back beneath whatever weighted it, and the silence that followed was somehow worse, because now he knew it was not emptiness. It was gagging.

He got to his feet. His knees were wet. The innkeeper was still talking at him — *the guard, send for the guard, the elders, blessed stones, what in the God's name* — and Isandro looked at the old man and heard himself speak in the flat report-voice that had carried him through every crisis of his adult life:

"Seal the tavern. No one leaves until the elders arrive. No one touches that floorboard seam." He was buckling his knife back on. He was checking his kit with hands that shook and did not miss a single strap. "I'm going to the boundary stone."

"The *stone*? Isandro, the girl—"

"I know about the girl." His voice cracked on it, and he let it. Let them see. Because the lie of calm was finished now, burned through in a single evening, and what remained was simpler and hotter and far more dangerous. "She was taken beneath the mountain. By the court. And every oath I've ever sworn says I stand on this side of the boundary stones and let it happen."

He shouldered past the innkeeper, out into the rain.

The streets of the lower town blurred past him — lamplit windows, faces turning, someone calling his name and someone else calling for the watch. He barely registered any of it. His mind was running the same circuit, over and over, a hound chasing its own tail: *her hand in mine, the ledger-voice, OWED, the god stirring, her hand slipping, procedure, procedure, procedure.*

By the time he reached the boundary path his lungs were raw and the rain had soaked him to the skin, and the first boundary stone rose out of the dark ahead of him, ancient and moss-eaten, humming its faint dead hum — the null-feeling he had lived beside all his life, the place where magic went to stop.

He stopped three paces from it. Breath steaming. Marks still faintly gold beneath his soaked sleeves.

Beyond the stone, up the black shoulders of the mountain, lightning flickered inside the cloud — and stayed. Flickered again. Held. As if something up there were signaling. Or counting.

*We have come only to collect what is owed.*

His hand closed over the marks on his forearm, and beneath his palm they pulsed — once, twice — in a rhythm he slowly, dreadfully recognized.

A heartbeat. Hers. Carried in the god's old script like a name carried in a stranger's mouth.

Isandro stood at the boundary stone with the rain coming down and the elders already hurrying up the path behind him, lanterns bobbing, voices raised in the tones of men arriving to forbid something — and for the first time in eleven years of faithful service, Isandro Villalobos did not turn to kneel.

He turned to face the mountain.
