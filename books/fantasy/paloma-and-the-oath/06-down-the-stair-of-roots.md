## 6. Down the Stair of Roots

The elders' hall smelled of tallow and old decisions.

Isandro stood in the center of it with his fists at his sides and the hound's dying words still ringing in his skull like a struck bell: *Sold long ago. Paid for in full. She was always meant to be collected.*

Elder Sabina Vásquez sat at the head of the crescent table, her hands folded over a ledger older than any of them, her face arranged into the particular patience of a woman who had spent forty years telling young men no. Behind her, the boundary stones' map hung on the wall — the valley rendered in ink and thread, every warding stone marked with a small black X, the whole shape of it like a cupped hand holding nothing.

"The answer is no," she said, before he could even open his mouth. "Sit down, Isandro."

"I'll stand."

"You always do." She sighed. "Then stand there and listen. The oath is not yours to break. It belongs to everyone who has ever slept safely inside these stones. Three hundred years, Villalobos. Three hundred years since the last time magic crossed our border, and do you know what happened the time before that? Neither do I. That's the point. We don't remember, because there was nothing left to remember *with*."

"Paloma is out there." His voice came out low and even, which surprised him. He'd expected shouting. "She was taken through the seam at the third stone. You all felt it. Don't tell me you didn't."

"We felt it," said Elder Tomás from the left end of the crescent, an old man whose oath-marks had faded to gray ghosts on his forearms. "And we mourned it. As we have mourned others."

"*Others?*"

Tomás's eyes flicked away. Sabina's did not.

"There have been four," she said quietly. "In my lifetime. A shepherd's daughter. Two brothers cutting peat near the north seam. A child, once." Her folded hands tightened. "We wrote their names in the book of the lost and we sealed the stone behind them and we went on. Because that is what the oath costs, Isandro. It has always cost exactly this. You knew that when you swore it."

He hadn't known. Or he had known the way you know the sun will set — abstractly, without ever believing it would take anything you loved.

"She has a name," he said. "Paloma Reyes. She draws maps. She puts too much sugar in her coffee and apologizes to furniture when she bumps into it. She isn't a line in your book of the lost. She's—" His throat closed. He forced it open. "I saved a traveler from a wraith two weeks ago because the oath says the valley keeps its people. Where was the oath for her?"

"The oath keeps those *inside* the stones." Sabina's voice gentled, which was worse than if she'd shouted. "She was taken from inside them, yes. But the debt was not contracted here. The hound told you as much. Whatever holds her was signed generations ago, beyond our reach, beyond our law. Chasing it means carrying magic into the valley or yourself into its teeth, and both mean breaking faith with every living soul in this room."

"Then break mine first," Isandro said. "I'm the one going down."

Silence. The tallow flame on the nearest candle bent, though there was no draft.

Sabina looked at him for a long moment, and something moved behind her eyes — not anger. Recognition. Pity, maybe, or memory.

"No," she said again, softer. "Go home, Isandro. Grieve. In a year, perhaps less, you'll thank us."

He turned and walked out. No one stopped him. No one ever did; that was the whole architecture of the valley, obedience built so deep into the walls that rebellion never needed locking doors.

But his feet didn't carry him home. They carried him east, toward the third boundary stone, where the grass still lay flattened in a fan-shape where Paloma had stood, and the earth bore a seam like a healed scar that wasn't healing at all.

* * *

The third stone rose out of the hillside like a broken tooth, taller than a man, older than the valley's oldest house. Its surface was covered in oath-marks — thousands of them, carved and re-carved by generations of keepers, until the original script was buried under centuries of devotion like a fresco painted over a fresco.

Isandro had polished this stone a thousand times. He had pressed his palm to it a thousand times and felt nothing, because the valley was dead to magic and he was a creature of the valley, and that was the bargain.

Now he put his palm against it and felt — faintly, horribly, wonderfully — a pulse.

Not from the stone. From beneath it.

His own marks answered. They woke along his forearms like embers breathed upon, heat crawling up under the skin, and he snatched his hand back with a curse. In thirty years of service they had never done more than ache in cold weather. Now they burned like brands, and beneath the burn was something else, something that felt disgracefully like *hunger*.

"Don't," he told himself aloud. "You're a keeper, not a—"

A keeper of what? An empty shrine. A god who never spoke, never came, never answered — whom he had served his whole life on the strength of other men's certainty. And what had that certainty bought? A shepherd's daughter. Two brothers. A child. Paloma.

He thought about Sabina's folded hands. About Tomás looking away. About how many times, over three hundred years, someone must have stood exactly where he was standing, palm against the stone, feeling that pulse, hearing that argument — and walked away home to grieve.

He thought about Paloma's laugh, startled out of her when he'd knocked over the sugar dish. About the way she drew coastlines like she was remembering them rather than inventing them.

"Forgive me," he said. Not to the elders. To whatever was under the stone, or above the sky, or nowhere at all. "If You're listening. If You were ever listening. Forgive me."

He broke the seal.

It was easier than it should have been — the wax plug at the stone's base crumbled at a touch, as if it had been waiting years for exactly this hand. Behind it, where every map in the archive showed solid bedrock, a stair wound downward into the dark. Roots grew through its walls, pale and thick as arms, descending alongside the steps like banisters grown rather than carved. Warm air rose out of it, smelling of rain and loam and something electric, something that made his teeth ache.

Magic. He could smell magic. He hadn't known it had a smell.

Isandro took off his patrol cloak and folded it neatly on the grass — habit, absurd, unbreakable — and left his knife beside it. Then, before his courage could curdle into sense, he stepped onto the first stair.

The world ended.

That was the only way to describe it. One step, and the flat silence he had lived inside for thirty-one years tore open like a dam failing, and everything the valley had kept out came through him instead.

It did not feel like power. It felt like drowning in reverse.

Light poured into him through the marks on his arms — he could see them glowing now, gold-white, the old carvings lighting up one by one like windows in a dark house — and with the light came everything else. Sound arrived first: he could hear the roots growing, a slow creak like ships in ice, could hear water moving leagues below, could hear his own heart stuttering and stumbling and then, appallingly, keeping perfect time with something vast and slow far beneath the mountain. Then sensation: the stone under his boots became a language, each grain distinct, the whole stair a single thought he was standing inside. Then memory that wasn't his — flashes, images, a green country under a kind sun, a voice like wind through wheat—

He fell to his knees on the stairs and vomited into the dark.

For a long time he knelt there, shaking, half-blind with afterimages, his body trying to reject what had been poured into it. Every nerve screamed that this was wrong, that he was a valley-man, a dead-zone man, that magic was for elsewhere. Thirty years of trained emptiness fought thirty seconds of flood.

And underneath the screaming, quiet as a second heartbeat, was the exhilaration.

That was the terrifying part. Not the pain — pain he knew, pain was just information. What terrified him was how *good* it felt. How much of him had been hollow without his knowing. How eagerly the hollow filled. He had spent his life believing devotion had made him whole, and now, flooded past bursting, he understood with sick clarity that he had been a cup carried empty, told the emptiness was virtue.

"Oh," he whispered, and his voice echoed strangely, doubled, as if the stair repeated it back with interest. "Oh, that's what it's supposed to feel like."

He got up. He climbed.

* * *

The stair descended farther than the valley was deep. He understood that eventually, the way you understand things underground, where distance stops behaving. Hours passed, or seemed to. The roots thickened around him until he walked through a corridor of pale wood, and the air grew wet, and somewhere below he began to hear water — not dripping but moving, a broad slow current, the sound of an underground river shouldering through the dark.

His marks had settled into a steady warmth, no longer burning but humming, and he had learned — quickly, clumsily, the way a man learns to swim by nearly drowning — to let the magic sit in him rather than fight it. When he flinched from it, it scalded. When he accepted it, it warmed. Everything down here ran on that same terrible simplicity: don't fight, don't cling, just *hold*.

He was practicing holding when he heard the splash.

Not the river's patient churn — something smaller, frantic, human-sized. He quickened his descent, taking the slick steps two at a time, light gathering along his arms without his quite deciding it, and burst out of the root-corridor onto a shelf of black rock above an underground lake.

The lake was enormous and utterly still, a sheet of obsidian under a ceiling of hanging roots, and near the shore something was struggling in the water — a figure, hooded, weighed down, going under and hauling itself up and going under again.

Isandro didn't think. He was moving before thought caught up, sliding down the rock, wading in to his thighs — the cold hit like a fist, river-cold, bone-cold — and grabbing a fistful of cloak as the figure sank a third time.

She fought him. Of course she fought him; he registered that distantly, admiringly, as her elbow found his ribs and her nails raked his wrist. She fought like someone who had learned that reaching hands meant capture. He took the blows, got an arm around her chest, and hauled backward, dragging them both up the shelf until they collapsed on dry stone, her coughing water and fury, him gasping steam — because his breath steamed now, visibly, magic bleeding heat off him into the freezing air.

"Stop — stop, I'm not them—" he managed.

"They say that too." Her voice was hoarse, cracked, furious. She twisted in his grip, and her hood fell back, and he saw a woman perhaps his own age, brown skin gone gray-blue with cold, black hair plastered to her cheeks, and eyes that glowed faintly green — actually glowed, witch-light rising from her irises like foxfire. "They being shadow-hounds, in case you're wondering. If you're one of theirs, do your worst. I'm done running."

"I'm not theirs. I'm from the valley. Above." He held up his hands, palms out, the way you'd calm a spooked horse. The marks on his arms were still glowing. He watched her see them, watch her expression shift from murder to confusion to something sharper. "Oath-keeper. Third stone. My name is Isandro Villalobos."

Her breath caught. "Villalobos." She said the name like she was tasting something spoiled. "An oath-keeper. Down *here*." She laughed, a raw, disbelieving sound, and pushed wet hair from her face. "Do you have any idea what you've done? Do you have any idea what you people are?"

"People?" He braced himself on his knees, still fighting for breath. "I just pulled you out of a lake."

"And I'm grateful," she snapped, "insofar as gratitude survives context. Marisol." She didn't offer a hand; she offered the name grudgingly, like coin counted out. "Marisol Ochoa. Runaway. Former property of the court beneath this mountain, currently between owners, and the only person you've met down here who can keep you alive — which, oath-keeper, is a service you are about to need very badly."

"Why?"

She pointed past him, up the stair he'd descended, and her glowing eyes narrowed.

"Because you came down the root-stair with your marks lit like festival lanterns," she said, "and the court felt it. The Regent felt it. Nothing has come down that stair in three hundred years, Isandro Villalobos, and the moment you touched the bottom step, every soul in that gilded rotting palace turned toward the sound of you like wolves turning toward a whistle." She got to her feet, swaying, wrung out her cloak with shaking hands. "So. Are you coming or not? Because standing still is dying slowly, and I've done enough of both."

He should ask questions. Who she was, what the court was, why an escaped servant of the Regent would help an oath-keeper of the valley that had abandoned her. He filed all of it away for later, because later was a luxury, and already — distantly, through the stone, through the water, through the new-opened doors of his own senses — he could feel it too.

Attention. Vast and cold and curious, turning toward him from far below, the way a sleeping thing turns toward warmth.

Paloma was down there. In the middle of that attention. In the middle of that hunger.

"Yes," Isandro said, and stood, and the light along his arms threw their shadows huge against the root-hung dark. "Lead the way."

Marisol looked at him a moment longer — at the glow, at the steam of his breath, at the trembling in his hands that might have been fear or might have been the opposite — and something almost like pity crossed her face.

"Gods help me," she muttered, turning toward the water's edge and a narrow passage beyond it, "they've sent a believer down the stair. This way, oath-keeper. And whatever you hear singing in the galleries ahead — don't answer it."
