## 7. The Court Beneath the Mountain

Paloma woke to the smell of honey and rot.

It was a strange combination — sweetness layered over something deep and fungal, like fruit left too long in a sealed room. She lay on a bed that should have been luxurious: sheets of some fabric too smooth for linen, a coverlet embroidered with silver thread, pillows stuffed with something softer than down. Everything was beautiful. Everything was slightly wrong. The embroidery depicted vines, but the vines had teeth. The silver thread was tarnished in patterns that looked deliberate, like veins beneath skin.

She sat up slowly. Her head ached in the way of someone who has been crying hard and then struck silent — a pressure behind the eyes, a rawness in the throat. The last thing she remembered was Isandro's face, lit by candlelight through the archive window, his mouth open around a word he never finished saying. *Paloma—* And then the floor had opened like a mouth.

Her sleeve still bore the wine stain. A dark bloom across her forearm, dried now to the color of old rust. She stared at it for a long moment. In the codex, the entry had described this exact stain, this exact evening, down to the hour. *The cartographer's daughter drinks red at the ninth bell.* Except she wasn't a cartographer's daughter; she'd made that detail up herself years ago when people asked about her family, because the truth — an orphan raised by the valley's charity, no name worth recording — had seemed too small and sad to offer strangers.

And yet the book had known. The book had known everything.

"Good," said a voice from the doorway. "You're awake. They told me you might sleep until the turning of the moon, and I confess I hoped they were wrong. Guests who sleep through their own welcome are such a disappointment."

A woman stood framed in the archway, holding a candelabrum whose candles burned without flickering, as if the flames had been painted there. She was tall and narrow, dressed in layers of gray silk that moved like smoke, and her face was beautiful the way a mask is beautiful — symmetrical, serene, and utterly fixed. Only her eyes moved, and they moved too much, tracking Paloma with the quick darting attention of birds deciding whether something is food or threat.

"I'm Vesperine," the woman said. "Keeper of Guests. You may think of me as your hostess." She glided into the room without seeming to walk, her feet making no sound on the stone. "You'll want to know where you are. Everyone does, at first. It's very tedious, the asking. So: you are beneath the mountain. Below the valley. Below, below, below — three descents past the place where maps stop pretending to be honest."

"The valley," Paloma said. Her voice came out cracked. "There's a way back up."

"There is always a way back up," Vesperine agreed pleasantly. "That's what stairs are for. Whether one is permitted to use them is another matter entirely, and matters of permission are not mine. I only keep guests comfortable." She set the candelabrum on a table carved from a single block of black stone, its surface polished to a mirror. "Come. Bathe, dress, eat. The Regent receives honored property at the tenth bell, and it would be a cruelty to present you looking like — well. Like someone who was dragged here by hounds."

The word landed like a stone dropped into still water. *Honored property.*

"I'm not property," Paloma said.

Vesperine turned, and for the first time her mask-face showed expression — a small, genuine smile, almost tender. "No," she said softly. "Of course you aren't. That's what makes it so sad."

* * *

They bathed her in water that smelled of crushed flowers and something older underneath, mineral and cold, like snowmelt from a glacier that had never seen sun. Attendants with downcast eyes and hands too smooth — no calluses, no scars, no history written on them at all — washed her hair and dressed her in a gown of deep green silk that fit as though it had been sewn for her body specifically, which, Paloma thought with a chill, perhaps it had. Perhaps it had been waiting for her for a hundred years.

They fed her at a small table set before a window that wasn't a window. It looked out onto a garden of white trees with silver leaves, and the light that fell through it was warm and golden and came from nowhere. When Paloma pressed her palm against the glass, it was cold stone on the other side. A painting. An impossibly detailed painting of a garden that might have existed once, somewhere far above, before the mountain closed over it.

The food was exquisite and tasted of nothing. Each course arrived perfect — pears glazed in honey, bread still steaming, a soup the color of sunset — and each bite dissolved on her tongue leaving only the memory of flavor, like eating photographs of food. By the third course she understood: this was sustenance without substance. Everything here was surface. Everything here was a copy of something real, made by things that could no longer touch the real.

"You're wondering why it doesn't taste right," Vesperine said, watching her from across the table. The Keeper of Guests had not eaten. "Nothing does, here. Not for us." She touched her own sternum lightly, a gesture so brief Paloma might have imagined it. "But for you, dear, it will taste better every day. That's what happens when a soul settles into new soil. It takes root. It learns to love what feeds it."

"I want to go home."

"Yes," Vesperine said, with the patience of someone who has heard this many times. "They all say that first. Then they say other things." She rose, smoothing her gray silk. "Come. The Regent is ready. Better to meet him fed than fasting — hunger makes people agreeable, and agreement, in this place, is forever."

* * *

The court beneath the mountain was gilded the way a corpse is embalmed.

Paloma walked beside Vesperine through corridors that took her breath away and then made her sick with looking. Every surface gleamed: walls of veined marble shot through with gold, ceilings frescoed with gods and heroes whose faces had been carefully repainted so many times the features had gone soft and anonymous, floors of mosaic depicting the valley above — she recognized the boundary stones, the shrine, the river's bend — rendered in tiles of lapis and pearl. It was magnificent. It was also dying. Gold leaf curled away from the marble in long peeling strips. The frescoes' paint flaked and drifted down like colored snow. Whole sections of mosaic had collapsed into rubble that no one had cleared, just swept to the edges of the halls in neat, hopeless piles.

And everywhere, everywhere, there were people. Courtiers in silks of every color, standing in alcoves and along the walls, watching her pass. They were beautiful and terrible — faces too perfect, movements too fluid, eyes that caught the light wrong, reflecting it flatly like a cat's. None of them spoke. None of them blinked enough. As she passed, heads turned in unison, dozens of faces tracking her with identical hungry attention, and Paloma understood with cold certainty that she was looking at souls. Souls that had been taken, worn, kept. Some of them, she realized with a lurch, wore faces she knew. The miller's son who'd drowned two winters ago. Old Tomás, who had wandered off the boundary path a decade back and been mourned with a cairn and a song. He stood in an alcove wearing courtier's silk, and when their eyes met, something behind his borrowed beauty flickered — recognition, or grief, or a scream held so long it had become silence.

Paloma's legs nearly gave out. Vesperine's hand closed on her arm, cool and strong.

"Don't look at them," the Keeper murmured. "Or rather — look, but don't *see*. It's kinder that way. For both of you."

"They're from the valley." Paloma's voice shook. "They're *people* from the valley."

"They were. Once. Now they are the court's." Vesperine resumed walking, drawing Paloma along. "You'll find we are excellent stewards. Nothing here is wasted. Nothing here is lost. Everything is *kept*."

The throne room opened before them like the inside of a jewel.

It was vast beyond reason — a cavern hollowed and shaped until it resembled a cathedral, its vaulted ceiling lost in shadow above, its walls cascading with gold. Torches burned in brackets of black iron, and their light did not flicker either. At the far end, atop a dais of seven steps, stood the throne: a massive chair of black stone and gold, and upon it—

Paloma had expected a monster. Horns, scales, a voice like grinding rock. What she saw instead was worse.

The Regent Below looked like a tired, elegant man. He was seated — no, arranged — on the throne with the weary grace of someone who has attended ten thousand tedious meetings and expects ten thousand more. His robes were the color of dusk. His face was handsome in a faded way, like a portrait left too long in sunlight, and his eyes were the color of coins at the bottom of a well. When he saw Paloma, he smiled, and the smile was warm, and it reached all the way to his eyes, and none of it was real.

"Paloma Reyes," he said, and her name in his mouth sounded like a signature being signed. "At last. Do come closer. I have waited a hundred years to say hello."

She didn't move. Vesperine pressed gently at her back, and her feet carried her forward anyway, across the mosaic floor, up the seven steps, until she stood close enough to see that the Regent's skin had the same faint translucence as the courtiers' — light passing through it and coming out changed.

"Do you know why you're here?" the Regent asked. He did not rise. Rising, she understood suddenly, was for people who feared other people. He feared nothing in this room, least of all her.

"A book," Paloma said. "A prophecy. It said my name."

"Mm. Books." He waved a hand dismissively. "Books are how it looks from your side of the ledger. From mine, it is simpler." He gestured, and a servant — or something shaped like a servant — stepped from the shadows bearing a great book bound in green leather, its pages edged in gold. "Your great-great-grandmother's grandmother. Reyes — yes, the name survives, names are stubborn things. She came to the valley in the famine year, with nothing. Hungry children. Winter coming. And she made a bargain."

The Regent opened the book. Paloma's stomach dropped, because she knew that binding, knew that green leather, had spent the last week with her fingers in its twin upstairs in the valley archive. This was the ledger. The codex was its echo, its shadow cast upward through a century of stone.

"She signed," the Regent continued, turning pages with a lover's care. "Not with blood — blood is vulgar, and besides, signatures in blood can be contested. She signed in ink, properly witnessed, fully informed. We do not trick anyone, Miss Reyes. That is a slander spread by people who regret their choices. She read every clause. She understood that the payment would come due generations hence, when the debt had ripened. And in exchange—" he paused, savoring it, "—her children ate that winter. And the winter after. And every winter since, your line has prospered in small, unremarkable ways. Luck, we call it, when it arrives in installments."

"That was her choice," Paloma whispered. "Not mine."

"No," the Regent agreed, with terrible gentleness. "It was hers. But debts do not ask permission of the debtor's descendants any more than roots ask permission of the tree. You inherited her name, her blood, and her signature. All three are legally binding." He turned the book toward her. "Look. Find yourself. I know you're a mapmaker. You of all people will appreciate accurate records."

Paloma looked.

The page was dense with entries in a dozen different hands, centuries of ink layered over centuries. And there, in the column of names, written in fresh black ink that gleamed wetly as though written moments ago:

**Reyes, Paloma. Cartographer. Soul: one (1), whole and undivided, sold in trust by Micaela Reyes, famine year, due upon ripening. Status: COLLECTED.**

Below it, a date. Tonight's date.

The letters seemed to move as she read them, rearranging themselves into truer shapes. *Collected.* Not *due*, not *promised*, not *owed*. Collected. Past tense. Done. As if she had already ceased to be a person and become a possession, filed and finished, a transaction completed while she slept.

Something in Paloma went very quiet and very cold. It was not despair — despair was hot, despair screamed and thrashed. This was colder than that. This was the clarity that comes on high ridgelines, when the air thins and every detail stands out sharp and meaningless and true.

She was a mapmaker. Her whole life, she had drawn borders — lines on paper that decided where one thing ended and another began, where safety ended and wilderness began, where a person's world ended and the unknown began. And she knew, professionally, intimately, what borders really were: agreements. Fictions everyone consented to pretend were walls. Change the agreement, and the border moved. Refuse the agreement entirely, and the border *dissolved*.

This creature — this Regent, this court, this ledger — wanted her to believe the ink was the wall. Wanted her to stand here drowning in gilded splendor, wearing a dress sewn for her a century ago, eating food that tasted of nothing, and accept that a dead woman's signature defined the borders of her soul.

Fine. Let him believe it. Let them all believe it.

Because Paloma Reyes had redrawn stubborn borders before. And she had never yet met a line on a page she couldn't erase.

"It's a great deal to absorb," the Regent was saying, watching her with those coin-colored eyes. "I don't expect gratitude — not yet. Gratitude ripens later, like everything else here. For now, simply know this: you are safe, you are valued, and you are *home*. The valley above is a drafty little hallway. This is the house." He closed the ledger with a soft, final sound. "Vesperine will show you your rooms. Rest. Feast tomorrow — the court will want to welcome you properly. And Paloma." He smiled again, that warm, false, patient smile. "Do try to be happy. Property that struggles only bruises itself, and I do hate waste."

She bowed her head — a small, defeated-looking motion that cost her nothing, because it promised nothing — and let Vesperine lead her away.

* * *

Her rooms were beautiful. Of course they were. A bed hung with green silk, a writing desk of pale wood, shelves stocked with books — real ones, she noticed, books from above, from the valley archive, even a few she herself had annotated. They had taken her life and rebuilt it here, brick by stolen brick, a cage furnished with everything she loved.

She waited until the door closed and the footsteps faded. Then she sat at the desk, and found — as she had known she would — paper, ink, and pens. Honored property was allowed its tools.

She did not write. Not yet. Instead she pulled the lamp close and began, methodically, to examine her cage. The window-paintings. The seams in the silk wallpaper. The way the corridor outside ran — she had counted her steps from the throne room, mapped the turns in her head the way she mapped everything, because a map was not a picture of a place. A map was a weapon you carried folded in your mind.

Three turns. Forty-one steps. A stair descending where it should ascend. And behind the throne dais, when the Regent had gestured with the ledger — for half a heartbeat, the wall had shimmered, and she had seen darkness behind the gold. A passage. Hidden, but *there*.

Paloma dipped her pen and began to draw, in light strokes she could lick away if anyone entered: the throne room, the corridors, the alcove where old Tomás stood wearing silk and silence. And at the edge of the page, where a mapmaker writes the things that don't fit anywhere else, she added a single line of text:

*Every border is an agreement. Find who else wants out.*

Somewhere far above, she thought — in a valley where magic couldn't follow, in a house where wine was spilled and words went unfinished — Isandro was breaking. She couldn't know that. She couldn't count on it. But she drew his face anyway, quickly, from memory, in the margin: the scarred brow, the stubborn jaw, the look he got when duty and desire pulled him apart like taffy.

Then, beneath it, she wrote the thing she would not let herself feel until it was safe to feel it:

*Come find me. Or better — I'll come find you.*

She blotted the page, folded it small, and hid it in the lining of her gown, over her heart. Outside her door, the court murmured and glittered and rotted, keeping its thousand stolen souls like coins in a miser's fist.

And Paloma Reyes, honored property, whole and undivided and collected, picked up her pen again and began to draw the way out.
