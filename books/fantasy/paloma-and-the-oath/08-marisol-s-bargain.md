## 8. Marisol's Bargain

The river down here had no name, and Marisol liked it that way.

Names were handles. Names were how the Regent Below gripped a thing and turned it over in His long pale hands until it gave up whatever was inside. She had spent eleven years beneath the mountain learning that lesson the way you learn anything in that place — slowly, painfully, and with witnesses who applauded at the wrong moments.

So she had named nothing since she fled. Not the river she'd swum down, not the root-stair she'd climbed, not the half-drowned mercenary she'd fished out of the dark because he was too stubborn to die and too loud to ignore.

She called him *the oath-keeper* in her head, and even that felt like too much of a gift.

He was sitting against the boundary stone now, three levels up from where she'd pulled him gasping from the water, wringing river out of his sleeve and pretending he wasn't shaking. Magic had gone into him like a flood into a dry well — she'd watched it happen, watched him convulse on the wet stone while a lifetime of nothing became suddenly everything. Most people screamed. He had only gritted his teeth and said something to a god who never answered.

That was the part Marisol couldn't stop thinking about.

"You're staring," he said, without looking up.

"I'm assessing." She crouched across from him, keeping the fire between them. It was a small fire, fed on dead root-wood that burned blue-green down here, and it threw her shadow long against the tunnel wall — a crooked, hunched shape she didn't recognize as herself anymore. "You're going to ask me what I know. You've been working up to it for an hour. Ask."

Isandro Villalobos finally looked at her. He had the kind of face that had been arranged for endurance rather than beauty — heavy brow, jaw like a doorframe, eyes the color of old bronze that had been polished so many times they'd gone thin. The oath-marks crawled up his forearms where he'd pushed back his sleeves: silver filigree, faded almost to memory, still faintly warm. She could feel them from across the fire, the way you can feel a banked stove through a wall.

"Who are you?" he asked.

"Marisol Vane." She let the name sit there between them, tasting its weight. She hadn't said it aloud in years. "Witch. Formerly of the court beneath this mountain. Currently of nowhere, which is a promotion, if you're wondering."

"Formerly." His voice was flat, careful. "Serving the Regent."

"Serving the Regent." She laughed, and it came out wrong, rusty, like a hinge in a house no one lived in. "That's one word for it. *Servant* is what we called ourselves. *Property* is what we were. But sure. Serving."

The fire popped. Somewhere far below, water moved through channels it had been carving since before the valley had a name, and the sound was so much like breathing that Marisol had to look away for a moment.

"The hound," Isandro said. "Before it died, it told me—"

"Hounds don't tell. Hounds leak." She held up a hand before he could bristle. "I know what it leaked. I was close enough to hear it die, if you'll recall. You were busy being dramatic about your oath-marks." She leaned forward. "It told you her soul was sold. Paloma Reyes. Sold generations ago by someone with her blood in their veins, entered in a ledger older than the valley elders' grandmothers. Yes?"

His jaw tightened. That was answer enough.

"Then you already know the shape of it," Marisol said. "What you don't know is why. And the why is the only thing worth knowing down here, oath-keeper, because the why is the only thing that will keep you alive long enough to get her back."

* * *

She made him walk first. Not out of cruelty — though she would have admitted to some, if pressed, just to see how he took it — but because the tunnels remembered footsteps, and hers were known. If anything down here was going to wake and listen, better it listened to a stranger's tread than to a signature it recognized.

They moved along the unnamed river's course, through galleries where the roots of the mountain hung down like the ribs of some drowned cathedral. The blue-green light of the root-wood torches turned everything the color of deep water. Isandro walked like a soldier even now, weight low, hand never far from the knife at his hip, and Marisol catalogued his habits the way she used to catalogue the court's — because habits were leverage, and leverage was the only currency that kept its value below.

"Talk," he said, after an hour of dripping silence.

"I'm deciding where to start."

"Start with the god."

Of course. Of course that was where he'd start. She should have known; the man wore his faith like a scar he kept touching.

Marisol stopped walking. Ahead, the tunnel opened into a vaulted chamber where the river spread thin and glassy over black stone, and their reflections stared up at them from below like drowned twins. She looked at his reflection instead of his face. It was easier.

"You have a shrine up there," she said. "In the valley. Empty."

"It isn't empty. It's—" He stopped himself. She watched him wrestle with the sentence, watched him lose. "It's quiet. It has always been quiet. The Silent God doesn't speak. That's not abandonment. That's His nature. We built the oaths around His silence. We guard the valley because He cannot, and He—"

"He what?" Marisol let it hang. "Finish the theology, oath-keeper. I want to hear how it ends."

The silence stretched. Down in the glassy water, his reflection's mouth pressed into a line.

"He keeps us," Isandro said finally. "Even silent. Even absent. He keeps us."

"No," Marisol said. "He doesn't. Because He can't. Because He isn't absent, and He isn't silent by nature, and He isn't keeping anyone." She turned to face him fully, and let him see all of it in her face — every year, every rot, every night she'd stood in the gilded dark listening to the thing beneath the throne eat. "He's *caged*, Isandro Villalobos. He has been caged for longer than your valley has existed. Your shrine isn't empty because He left. It's empty because He was taken, and everything your oaths protect, everything your order has bled for across ten generations, was built by the ones who took Him — to keep anyone from ever coming to look."

The words went into him like the river had. She saw it hit — the small collapse behind his eyes, the way his hand found the wall without his permission.

"That's not possible," he said. It was barely a whisper. "The boundary stones. The deadness. Magic can't cross into the valley. We've always known that. It's His protection. His—"

"His prison walls." She crouched, touched the black stone, and let a thread of her own craft rise through her fingers — a small light, moth-wing pale, trembling in the air between them. In the valley above, this would be impossible. Here, it was as easy as breathing. "You felt it yourself, coming down. The moment you crossed under the boundary stone, magic flooded into you like water into a cracked jar. Why do you think that is? Why do you think the valley is the only place in the world where the craft dies?" She closed her fist and the light died with it. "Someone built that. Someone reached up from below and hollowed out a country-sized hole in the world's magic, and everyone up there decided it was a blessing. A gift from a silent god who loved you so much He made you safe."

"And you're telling me it was a cage."

"I'm telling you it was a *hiding place*." Her voice dropped. The chamber drank the sound. "There is a difference, oath-keeper, and the difference is everything. A protection says *no one may harm you*. A hiding place says *no one may find Him*. The Regent Below did not build the valley to keep you safe. He built it to keep you *looking the wrong way*."

* * *

They walked again, because standing still felt dangerous now, like standing in a room with a lie that was starting to smell.

Marisol told him the rest in pieces, rationed out across miles, the way you feed a fire you're afraid will either go out or burn the house down.

She told him about the court: the gilded rot of it, the feast-halls where shadows ate at long tables, the Regent Below seated on a throne grown out of the mountain's own bone. She told him about the ledger — the great book of souls, bound in something she refused to name, its pages recording every bargain struck across centuries. Debts of blood. Debts of names. Debts signed by desperate ancestors who wanted rain, or sons, or revenge, or simply one more winter, and never read to the bottom of the page where the interest was written.

"The court doesn't eat flesh," she said. "Flesh is cheap. Flesh is everywhere. The court eats *souls* — slowly, over generations, the way a landlord bleeds a tenant. Every soul in that ledger is a tap the Regent can open whenever He's hungry. And He is always hungry, because of what He did to become the Regent, and what He has to keep doing to stay it."

"What did He do?"

Marisol stopped at the mouth of a side gallery — a narrow throat of stone breathing cold air — and put her back against the wall, and looked at him for a long time before answering.

"He ate a god," she said.

The word fell into the chamber and did not echo. Some places down here swallowed sound whole; she'd learned which ones, and she chose them deliberately when the truth needed saying.

"There was a god under this mountain before there was a Regent. Old. Quiet. Quiet the way the sea floor is quiet — not empty, just *deep*. The first oath-takers found Him. Or He found them; the stories disagree, and the stories that survive are the ones the Regent allows." She wrapped her arms around herself, though the chill was not entirely the gallery's. "They made a bargain with Him, and then they broke it, and then they did worse than break it. They bound Him. They built the cage out of His own silence — took the voice He'd never used and turned it into walls. And then the one who led them sat down on the throne above the cage and began to *feed*."

"On Him."

"On everything He was. Power, presence, worship — all of it drained upward, century after century, into the thing on the throne. That's what the Regent is, underneath the gold and the courtesy. He's a man-shaped debt that ate a god and has been starving ever since, because divinity doesn't sit well. It runs through Him like water through a net. So He needs more. Always more. Souls. Contracts. Whole bloodlines signed away in ledgers." She met Isandro's eyes. "Your Silent God never abandoned the valley, oath-keeper. He's *below it*. He's been below it the whole time, listening to ten generations of the faithful swear oaths in His name to the very order that buried Him. And He can't say a single word about it, because His silence *is* the prison."

Isandro had gone very still. It was the stillness of a man watching his entire life rearrange itself around a new center of gravity — every patrol, every prayer at the empty shrine, every year of devotion reordering itself into evidence.

"The oath-marks," he said hoarsely. "When the hounds took her. They flared. First time in years. I thought—"

"You thought He was waking." Marisol nodded slowly. "Maybe He was. Maybe He does, sometimes, when something happens near the cage that matters enough. Or maybe the marks aren't His at all. Maybe they're the Regent's leash, and they flared because the contract activated." She shrugged, and hated how casual she made it sound. "Down here, oath-keeper, even your blessings might be chains. Assume nothing is what it feels like. Especially what it feels like."

* * *

They camped in the breathing gallery, because the cold air moving through it masked sound and scent alike, and because Marisol knew — from eleven years of service and two of flight — exactly which places the hounds' noses failed.

Isandro sat sharpening a knife that did not need sharpening. Marisol watched him do it for a while, and then decided the hour had come, because hours like this one did not come twice.

"We need to talk about price," she said.

The whetstone stopped. "I don't have money."

"I don't want money. Money's worthless below and nearly worthless above, if you know the right markets." She shifted so the blue-green firelight caught her face, and made herself hold still under his gaze. This was the part where she'd lost people before. Best to do it clean. "I want a place in the valley."

He frowned. "A place."

"A house. A name on a roll. A door that locks from the inside and neighbors who know my face and don't cross the street." She heard her own voice go quieter than she intended. "I fled the court two years ago. Do you know what I've been since? Nothing. Nowhere. The outlands don't take witches, the towns don't take runaways, and the valley—" She laughed, short and bitter. "The valley especially doesn't take me. Magic-dead ground. My craft would starve there. I'd be less than nobody. And I want it anyway. Do you understand? I want the place where my craft dies, because it's the only place in the world where *His* reach dies too. I want to sleep somewhere the Regent Below cannot follow, and I am willing to pay any price you name to get it."

Isandro set the knife down. For a long moment he said nothing, and Marisol watched him weigh her — the witch, the runaway, the self-declared servant of the thing that had stolen the woman he loved — and she let him. Trust that came free was trust that came cheap, and neither of them could afford cheap.

"You served Him," he said at last. "Eleven years."

"Eleven years, four months, and some days I don't count."

"Why should I believe anything you say?"

"You shouldn't." She smiled, and knew it was a bad smile, all edges. "Believe nothing I say. Watch what I do. I'm leading you toward the court, aren't I? Toward the thing that frightens me more than anything alive. I could have taken your purse and left you at the river. Instead I'm walking you into the wolf's den because you have something I need and I have something you need, and that, oath-keeper, is the only honest kind of alliance there is. The kind where betrayal costs both sides too much."

"And if I refuse? If I say the valley council decides who lives there, not me?"

"Then I walk away, and you find the court on your own, and you die in the second corridor, because you don't know that the third corridor is the one that counts." She held his gaze. "And Paloma Reyes stays in a gilded cage until the Regent finishes opening her soul like a letter, reading it, and filing it away. Your choice. I'm not sentimental about it. Sentiment is a luxury the court taught me to stop affording."

The fire burned down a notch. Somewhere far above, muffled by a hundred yards of stone, the valley slept on — the elders in their hall, the shrine in its silence, the whole beautiful lie holding its shape for one more night.

Isandro looked into the fire for a long time. When he spoke, his voice was rough, but steady.

"If we get her back — if we get *out* — I'll stand in front of the elders myself and vouch for you. My name, my record, my oath. Whatever's left of any of it." He extended his hand across the fire. "A place in the valley. That's the bargain."

Marisol looked at the hand. Big, scarred, river-cold. An oath-keeper's hand, the kind that had shaken on a thousand vows to a god who couldn't answer — and here was one more vow, offered to a witch who might yet betray him, sealed over a dying fire in the dark under the world.

It was the most honest thing she'd been offered in thirteen years.

She shook it.

"Now," she said, rising, pulling her pack onto her shoulder, "we should talk about the part I haven't told you yet. The reason the Regent wanted Paloma Reyes collected *now*, after a century of waiting. The prophecy your archive has been sitting on." She glanced back at him, and let the fear show in her face, because he needed to carry some of it too. "Her soul isn't just payment, oath-keeper. The ledger says it's the *key*. And the Regent isn't feeding her to Himself."

Ahead, down the breathing gallery, the dark opened toward the court — and from somewhere deep in that dark, faint as a heartbeat, came the sound of many voices singing a feast-song with no joy in it.

"They're preparing a wedding," Marisol said. "We have two days."
