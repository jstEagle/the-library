## 9. Ledger of Souls

They dressed her in gold.

Paloma sat on a stool of pale bone while three attendants — if that was the word for creatures whose faces were smooth as river stones and whose hands moved with the patience of clockwork — worked pins through her hair and clasped a collar of hammered gold at her throat. It was beautiful. That was the worst part. Everything in the court beneath the mountain was beautiful the way a spider's web is beautiful: engineered for one purpose, and not hers.

"You honor us," said the tallest attendant, in a voice like water running over coins.

"I'm honored," Paloma said, and let her eyes go soft and wondering, the way she'd seen lost travelers look at maps they didn't understand. *Docile. Grateful. Stupid.* She had spent two days learning this face in the polished obsidian of her cell wall, practicing until the fear underneath it stopped leaking through.

The Regent Below had been very clear about what she was now. Not a prisoner. Property, yes, but property of distinction — an heirloom coming home after a century in circulation. She was to be fed, clothed, admired. She was to be *kept*. And kept things, the Regent had said with a smile like a key turning, were given the run of their cage.

So when the attendants finished and gestured toward the doors, Paloma rose, smoothed her sleeves, and walked where they led — past colonnades of black marble veined with something that pulsed faintly, like veins; past gardens where the flowers were made of shadow so dense it had weight; past windows that looked out onto nothing at all, only the warm dark of the mountain's interior, pressing against the glass like a face.

The library was at the heart of it all.

She had asked for it herself, on the first day, in her softest voice. *I'm a cartographer,* she'd told the Regent, folding her hands like a schoolgirl. *If I'm to be yours, I should like to know your country. Every good map begins in a library.*

The Regent had liked that. The Regent liked cleverness in its possessions the way a collector likes provenance — it raised the value. So here she was, standing in a chamber vast enough to swallow the valley archive whole, shelves climbing into darkness overhead, ladders on brass rails, and lamps that burned without flame, giving off light the color of old honey.

And there, on a lectern of black stone in the center of the room, open under glass, lay the Ledger of Souls.

*Don't run to it.* She made herself drift along the nearest shelves first, trailing her fingers over spines written in scripts she half-recognized from her work above — dead languages, living ones, some that seemed to shift when she wasn't looking directly at them. She pulled down a volume on the rivers of the deep places and carried it to a reading chair positioned, by careful accident, with a clear line of sight to the lectern.

Then she read, and waited, and watched.

* * *

The court kept hours the way the surface world kept seasons. There was no sun, but there were tides — Paloma had begun to feel them in her teeth, a slow pressure that swelled and ebbed, and she'd learned that during the ebb, when the whole court grew quiet and heavy, the attendants went still as furniture. The Regent called it the Deep Rest. The servants called it nothing, because they could not speak unless spoken to.

It was during the third ebb that Paloma set down her book, crossed the library floor on silent feet, and lifted the glass from the lectern.

The Ledger of Souls was bound in leather gone soft as cloth, its pages thin and faintly warm, like the hide of something recently alive. The script inside was elegant, meticulous, and organized by year. By *sale*.

She found her own name within minutes. Reyes, Paloma. Entry dated ninety-seven years before her birth. *Soul tendered in payment by Tomás Reyes, cartographer, in consideration of debts forgiven. To be collected upon the fulfillment of the Prophecy of Return.* Beneath it, in different ink, added later: *Collected. Held in trust.*

Held in trust. As if her soul were a coat checked at a door.

Her hands wanted to shake. She held them flat against the page and breathed, and made herself keep reading, because the entry above hers caught her eye — Reyes, Tomás — and above that, Reyes, Inés. Three generations of her family, each entry in the same patient hand. Her ancestor hadn't just sold her soul. He'd sold his own first, and his mother's before him, a chain of transactions stretching back into the ledger's early pages like a rope lowered into a well.

But it wasn't only Reyeses.

She turned back. And back. And back. The names came faster now, hundreds of them, thousands, and the surnames began to repeat in patterns she knew — not from genealogy but from the valley itself. Ochoa. Ferreira. The name of the baker on the corner of the plaza where Isandro had bought her coffee on their first date. The name of the ferryman who ran the slow brown river at the valley's southern edge. The name of a boy called Nico who had drowned in the reservoir two summers ago, eight years old, and whose mother still left marigolds on the boundary stone every Sunday.

*Nico Vidal. Age 8. Soul tendered by guardian in consideration of a cure. Collected.*

Paloma stood very still in the honey-colored light, and something in her chest went cold and hard and terribly clear.

The children. The sick children of the valley, the ones the doctors couldn't save, the ones whose desperate parents had gone to the old women who remembered the old ways — the whispered bargains, the offerings left at the roots of the boundary stones. She had grown up hearing those stories and filed them away as folklore, as superstition, as the charming sad inventions of a small place.

They were invoices. Every miracle the valley had ever known had been a purchase, and every purchase had been paid for with a soul, and every soul had come *here*, into this warm dark, to be held in trust by a court that gilded its cages and called theft inheritance.

She thought of the shrine in the valley, the empty one, the Silent God whom no one had heard from in a hundred years. She thought of Isandro's oath-marks, the faded silver lines on his forearms he never talked about, and how he'd once said, half-asleep against her shoulder, *We keep the valley safe. That's what the oath is for. We keep it safe and quiet.*

Safe and quiet. A valley drained of magic, drained of wonder, its people paying invisible debts generation after generation — and calling the silence peace.

Her fingers had stopped trembling. That was the strange thing. Somewhere between Nico Vidal's entry and the page she was touching now, the fear had transmuted into something with edges. She was a cartographer. Her entire life had been training for this: the patient accumulation of detail, the love of systems, the understanding that any territory, however labyrinthine, could be mastered by anyone willing to map it.

The Regent had made a mistake. It had given the mapmaker the atlas.

She needed proof. Above, the elders would demand proof — Isandro had warned her about the elders, their caution, their devotion to the letter of things. Words would not be enough. She needed a page out of this book, and she needed it to survive being taken.

She studied the binding. The pages were sewn in quires, and the quires were stitched to cords visible in the spine's hollow — and one cord, near the middle of the current century's section, had been repaired at some point with newer thread, slightly loose. Someone had removed and replaced a gathering here before. Which meant it could be done again.

Paloma worked with a cartographer's patience. She loosened the stitching with a pin pulled from her own hair — the attendants had left her hairpins, considering ornament harmless — and eased free a single leaf: the page bearing the entries for the reservoir district, forty years of them, including Nico Vidal and eleven other children whose funerals she suddenly remembered hearing about, distant bells, her grandmother crossing herself.

The moment the leaf left the binding, it grew cold in her hand. Then warmer. Then it shivered, once, like a bird settling, and went still — and she felt it settle against her skin with a faint sense of *recognition*, as though the page knew what she intended and approved.

She folded it small and slid it into the lining of her collar, behind the gold, where flesh met metal. Then she re-stitched the cord, replaced the glass, returned to her chair, and picked up her book on the rivers of the deep places, and turned a page, and was docile again when the tide swelled and the attendants woke like clocks resuming.

"Does my lady find the library agreeable?" the tall one asked.

"Deeply," Paloma said, and smiled her practiced smile. "I should like to see more of the court. The Regent said I might."

* * *

They gave her the tour she asked for, because asking was permitted and wanting was encouraged, so long as the wants stayed inside the walls. She saw the feast hall where the long table was laid nightly with food that cast no shadows. She saw the aviary, where birds made of smoke beat against a ceiling of crystal. She saw the throne room.

That was the point of the tour, though the attendants didn't know it. She had steered them there with the gentlest of suggestions — *surely the throne is the heart of the court; I should hate to leave without seeing it* — and pride had done the rest.

The throne room was a cavern dressed in conquest. Banners hung from walls of living rock, trophies from courts and kingdoms Paloma had only read about, their heraldry faded to ghosts. And at the center, on a dais of layered stone — she counted the strata automatically, professionally, and her stomach tightened, because the strata were wrong, too regular, cut rather than grown — stood the Regent's seat: a chair of black iron and gold filigree, tall as three men, its back carved with a tree whose branches became chains.

The Regent was not present. Even better.

"The throne is ancient," the tall attendant said reverently. "Older than the court. Older than the oaths."

"May I approach?" Paloma asked, wide-eyed. "Just to look?"

Permission granted, she climbed the dais steps slowly, letting her gaze travel over the carvings, the filigree, the armrests worn smooth by centuries of hands. Behind the throne, the cavern wall rose sheer and unremarkable, seamed with quartz that caught the lamplight.

Except.

Cartographers learned to distrust unremarkable things. A blank space on a map was never truly blank; it was a confession of ignorance, and ignorance had shape. The wall behind the throne was *too* even. The quartz seams ran in parallel courses — natural quartz never did that — and the courses bent, subtly, around a region roughly man-shaped, slightly left of center, where the stone's grain flowed differently, the way water flows around a submerged rock.

She let her hand trail along the wall as she turned, admiring, and felt it: a seam. Hairline-fine, cool, breathing the faintest draft against her fingertips. Air was moving somewhere beyond the stone. There was a passage behind the throne, and someone used it often enough to wear the air a path.

She did not press. She did not linger. She stepped back, thanked the attendant warmly, and remarked on the beauty of the chain-tree carving all the way out of the room.

But walking back through the colonnades with her escort, Paloma felt the folded page against her throat and the knowledge of the passage behind her ribs, and understood that something fundamental had shifted since the hounds had dragged her screaming through the seam in the earth.

She had arrived as prey. She was becoming something else. The Regent thought it owned a frightened girl; it had instead acquired a spy with a perfect memory, a thief's patience, and a growing list of everything it could not afford for her to learn.

* * *

That night — night being a courtesy term, the dimming of the lamps — Paloma sat in her gilded chamber and drew.

She had asked for paper and ink, pleading boredom, and been indulged. Now she filled sheet after sheet with what she had gathered: the library's position relative to the throne room; the route of the tour; the direction of the draft behind the wall; the layout of corridors as she'd memorized them in passing, every turn logged without appearing to look. Around it all she sketched the larger shape of the court as she'd come to feel it through her teeth, the tides, the Deep Rest, the rhythm of the place rendered as contour lines.

A map was forming. Not of the court alone — of its *weaknesses*. The still hours. The loose stitching. The breathing wall. The fact that a court built on contracts must, by its own nature, obey rules, and rules could be read, and anything that could be read could be mapped, and anything that could be mapped could be beaten.

She thought of Isandro — his stubborn jaw, the way he'd stepped between her and the hounds with nothing but a patrol knife and fury, the flare of silver along his arms as the earth swallowed her. He would come. She knew it the way she knew north: absolutely, without evidence, in her bones. He would break whatever he had to break to come down here, and he would arrive with courage and no plan whatsoever, and the court would eat him alive.

Unless she left him a map.

She pressed her palm flat over the hidden page at her throat and allowed herself, for the first time since the taking, a real smile — small, fierce, entirely private.

Somewhere far above, the valley slept under its false peace, its people dreaming dreams that were not entirely their own. Somewhere below, a god sat caged and silent while thieves sang hymns to their own theft. And somewhere between, in a gilded room lit by honey-colored lamps, a woman the prophecy had named as sacrifice bent over her inkwell and began, line by careful line, to draw the way out.

On the fourth sheet, in the margin, she wrote a single word and underlined it twice:

*Soon.*
