## 10. Reunion in Chains

The feast of shadows began at the hour when no hour existed.

Isandro learned this from Marisol, who pressed herself against the cold wall of a service corridor beneath the court and spoke with her mouth nearly touching his ear. "They feast when the mountain forgets what time is," she whispered. "Every soul in that hall will be watching the Regent's table. Every eye but the guards', and the guards drink shadow-wine that makes them slow. If you're going to do something stupid, Isandro Villalobos, do it now."

"I'm going to do something stupid," he agreed.

"Good. At least you're honest about it." She pulled a scrap of gray cloth from her satchel — a hood, woven from something that wasn't wool and didn't move when it should have. "Drape this over your shoulders. It won't make you invisible. Nothing down here makes you invisible. But it will make you *uninteresting*, which in that hall is worth more than gold."

He tied it on. The cloth settled against him like a hand he didn't trust.

Marisol caught his wrist before he turned away. Her fingers were thin and ink-stained, and they trembled — not from fear, he realized, but from something she was holding back. "Listen to me. You find her, you see her, you confirm she breathes. Then you come back. Do you understand? The contracts in this place are older than the stone. They don't need your consent. They only need your touch."

"I know."

"You don't. You've lived your whole life in a valley where nothing magical can reach you. You think you understand hunger because you've been hungry. This place doesn't get hungry, Isandro. It gets *owed*."

He looked at her for a long moment. In the dark of the corridor, lit only by the sickly glow of veins of luminous fungus running along the ceiling, her face seemed carved from the same stuff as the walls — pale, patient, worn by long residence in the dark.

"You came down here running from them," he said quietly. "Why help me at all?"

Her jaw tightened. "Because I owe a debt I can't pay any other way. Go. And if you die in there, haunt someone useful."

* * *

The great hall of the court beneath the mountain had been built by people who wanted to be remembered as magnificent and had succeeded only in being remembered as enormous.

Isandro slipped through a servant's arch meant for bodies carrying platters, not questions, and stopped just inside the shadow of a pillar the width of a chapel. Above him arched a ceiling so high it vanished into darkness, hung with chandeliers that burned without flame — spheres of captured gloom that gave off light the way a wound gives off heat. The floor was black marble veined with silver, polished to a mirror, and every step of every guest rang off it like a struck bell.

There were hundreds of them.

Courtiers in robes of layered dusk, their faces beautiful in the way of things grown in the dark — too smooth, too symmetrical, eyes that caught the gloom-light and held it. They drifted between tables laden with food that steamed in colors steam should not come in: violet, green, a bruised orange. Musicians played instruments made of bone and wire, and the music was lovely the way a landslide is lovely, all momentum and no mercy.

And at the far end of the hall, on a dais of white stone, sat the Regent Below.

Isandro had expected a monster. What he saw was worse: a figure of dignified, almost gentle aspect, robed in silver-gray, its face hidden behind a mask of polished obsidian that reflected the whole hall upside down. It did not eat. It did not drink. It simply received, with small tilts of its masked head, the homage of its court, and Isandro felt — even across that vast distance — a pressure like deep water. Like standing at the bottom of something.

*She's here somewhere.*

He moved along the wall, one uninteresting shape among many, and searched.

It took him longer than he liked. The courtiers blurred together, faces like coins struck from the same mold. Servants glided with trays. Hounds of shadow lay beneath the tables like sleeping dogs, and every one of them made his oath-marks ache — those old faded lines on his forearms that had burned alive the night Paloma was taken and hadn't fully cooled since.

Then he saw the table of honor.

It stood apart, nearer the dais, set for one. And at it sat Paloma.

His breath went out of him slowly, the way air leaves a room when a door closes.

She wore white — some courtier's idea of ceremony, a gown that seemed spun from frost and spiderwork — and her hair had been arranged by hands that were not hers. But she sat straight-backed, chin lifted, and even at this distance he could see the mapmaker in her posture: the woman who catalogued impossible geography, who sketched borders that shouldn't exist, who had laughed at his terrible joke about boundary stones and then, embarrassed by her own laugh, covered her mouth with a napkin.

She was alive. She was unharmed. She was scanning the hall with quick, precise flicks of her eyes — counting exits, he realized. Counting guards. Mapping the room the way she mapped everything, because mapping was how she kept from drowning.

*Oh, my love,* he thought, and the word arrived unbidden and true. *You're already fighting.*

A servant leaned over her table, pouring wine the color of dusk into her cup. She waited until the servant withdrew, and then — so smoothly that only someone who knew her would catch it — she tipped the cup against the tablecloth's edge, letting it drain away into the marble's seams.

Isandro almost smiled. Almost.

Then a hound beneath her table lifted its head.

* * *

The creature had no eyes, but Isandro felt its attention settle on him like a physical weight, the way a cat finds the one still thing in a swaying field. Beneath the tables, other heads began to turn — not toward him, exactly, but toward the place where he stood, as though the shadows themselves had noticed an absence where a presence should be.

*Uninteresting,* Marisol had said. *Not invisible.*

The hound rose. It flowed out from under the table like spilled ink deciding to be a dog, and it did not growl or bare teeth. It simply walked toward him through the crowd, and the courtiers parted around it without looking, the way water parts around a stone it has long since accepted.

Isandro weighed his options and found them all terrible.

Run, and be chased through a hall of enemies. Stand, and be examined by a thing whose examination might itself be a trap. He chose a third path: he picked up a tray from a passing servant's hands — the servant blinked at him, confused, then forgot him mid-blink, the hood doing its strange work — and carried it toward the honored table as though he belonged to it.

The hound stopped three paces away. Its head tilted. Where a face should have been there was only smooth blackness, but Isandro had the distinct sense of being *read* — turned over like a page, weighed like a coin.

He set the tray down on the edge of Paloma's table.

And Paloma Reyes looked up at him.

For one heartbeat, neither of them moved. Her eyes went wide — not with fear but with a ferocious, disbelieving joy that she strangled instantly, smoothing her face into courtly blankness a half-second before any watching eye could read it. But her hands, folded on the table, trembled once, hard.

"You may go," she said to him, in a voice pitched for servants, cool and distant.

But her eyes said everything else. They said *you came*. They said *you absolute fool, you came*. They said *I've counted eleven exits and forty guards and I was going to need more time*.

"The wine is not to your liking?" Isandro asked, keeping his own voice flat, servile, his heart slamming against his ribs so hard he feared it would echo off the marble.

"It is not to anyone's liking," she said. "That is rather the point of it."

And then, because he was weak — because he had crossed a hundred miles of dark and broken his life's single law to stand in this rotten gilded hall, and because she was *right there*, close enough to touch, close enough that he could see the wine stain still ghosting her sleeve from a night that felt like another century — he reached out and brushed a stray lock of hair from her face.

His fingertips touched her skin.

The world screamed.

Not aloud. Nothing in the hall made a sound — the musicians faltered, the chatter died, the very gloom-lights guttered — but through the point of contact between his hand and her cheek, something *tore*. Isandro felt it happen to him, felt a contract older than his grandmother's grandmother wake up and take notice, felt words written in a language of debt and descent wrap around his wrist like a manacle closing.

Silver light flared along his forearm — tracing the old oath-marks, and then *beyond* them, writing new lines beside the old ones, a second script interweaving with the first. He looked down and watched the mark of the mountain court brand itself into his skin, elegant and fine as engraving, and he understood with perfect clarity what it meant:

*This one is now also owed.*

"No," Paloma breathed. She had risen, her chair scraping back, all her careful disguise abandoned. "No, no — you *idiot*, wonderful, terrible man—"

"Paloma Reyes." The voice came from the dais, and it was the Regent Below, and the hall had gone utterly silent around them. The masked head had turned. Though the obsidian reflected everything upside down, Isandro had the horrible certainty that behind it, something was smiling. "You have brought a guest to my table. How thoughtful of you."

"He's no one," Paloma said quickly. "A servant. He touched me without leave—"

"He is *marked*," the Regent said, and the word rolled through the hall like a stone dropped down a well. "Marked by contract, marked by blood, marked by the old god's fading favor. Come forward, oath-keeper. Let us look at what the valley sent me."

Guards materialized from the crowd — tall figures in lacquered dark armor, their faces hidden, their spears tipped with shards of gloom-light. Four of them. Six. They closed around the honored table with the unhurried confidence of those who have never once needed to hurry.

Isandro stepped in front of Paloma. It was a stupid gesture, tactically meaningless, six spears against one unarmed man. He did it anyway.

"Isandro." Her voice cracked, and now she wasn't playing a role anymore; she was just a woman in a stolen gown, gripping his sleeve above the burning new mark. "Don't fight them. Please. There are things in this court you haven't seen yet. I have. If you die here, everything I've endured to stay sane — to stay *useful* — is for nothing. Live. Whatever happens, live, and get out, and come back with—" her voice broke again, and steadied, "—with everyone. With the valley. I'll leave you a trail. I'm good at trails. I'm a *cartographer*, damn you."

The spears lowered.

"Take the oath-keeper to the lower cells," the Regent said. Its tone never changed, never rose, and that was the most frightening thing about it — the sound of a ledger being balanced. "Gently. He is owed to us now, and we do not damage what we own." A pause, and the masked head tilted toward Paloma, almost tenderly. "As for you, my dear... finish your supper. We have such plans for tomorrow."

The guards' gauntlets closed on Isandro's arms. He did not resist. Instead, in the last moment before they pulled him away, he twisted against their grip — not to escape, but to press his forehead against Paloma's, one fierce instant, her breath shaking against his lips.

"Eleven exits," he murmured. "Forty guards. Leave me a better trail than that."

"I'll leave you a *map*," she whispered, and then the guards wrenched him back, and the last thing he saw as they dragged him toward the descending stair was Paloma Reyes standing alone at the Regent's table in her gown of frost and spiderwork — spine straight, chin high, tears standing unshed in her eyes like water held behind a dam —

— and her right hand, low against her side, fingers moving in small, deliberate shapes.

Tracing, against her thigh, the first lines of a map.

* * *

The cells below the feast hall were cut from living rock, and the rock was warm.

Isandro noticed that first, hanging in the dark after they chained him — not cold, as a cell should be, but warm as a sleeping animal, and faintly pulsing. The mountain's heartbeat, he thought. Or something wearing the mountain's heartbeat like a borrowed coat.

They had taken the hood from him, stripped him to his shirt, and locked his wrists in manacles of black iron that hummed faintly against the new mark on his arm. Every time he flexed his hands, the two scripts — the old oath-marks of the Silent God and the fresh engraving of the court — slid against each other like mismatched gears, and the friction sent sparks of pain up into his shoulder.

He welcomed the pain. Pain meant the marks were still fighting each other. Pain meant whatever the court thought it owned, it did not yet own cleanly.

Hours passed, or what passed for hours underground. He counted his breaths the way the shrine had taught him — the Silent God asks nothing, the Silent God needs nothing, breathe and be present — and found, to his surprise, that the practice still held. Even here. Even caged. Especially caged.

Somewhere above him, muffled through fathoms of stone, the feast continued. Once, distantly, he heard singing — a sound so wrong and lovely that he pressed his palms against his ears and breathed until it passed.

Then footsteps. Not the metronome tread of guards. Something lighter, quicker, familiar in its rhythm.

A key turned. His cell door swung open, spilling fungus-glow into the dark, and a slight silhouette stood framed in it — ink-stained fingers, a satchel slung crooked, eyes that had seen too much of this place and survived by becoming part of its shadows.

"Marisol," Isandro rasped.

"Don't thank me yet." She knelt beside him, working a bent pin into his manacles, her hands steady now that they had a task. "I got your message to the servant who wasn't a servant. Clever, using the tray. Sloppy, touching her face." She glanced up, and despite everything, one corner of her mouth twitched. "Though I suppose if I'd crossed the world for someone, I might have risked a fingertip too."

"She's alive. She's fighting. She says she'll leave a trail."

"A trail." Marisol snorted softly, freeing his left wrist, moving to the right. "This whole court is a maze built to eat trails. Whatever she leaves, we'll need more than a cartographer's chalk to follow it." The pin clicked; the second manacle fell open. She sat back on her heels and studied him in the fungoid light, and her expression turned serious. "The mark on your arm. Show me."

He pushed back his sleeve. In the dark, the new engraving gleamed faintly silver, woven through the older faded lines like ivy through a fence.

Marisol went very still.

"That's not a brand," she said quietly. "That's a *claim*. Full contract, witnessed and sealed. Isandro — do you understand what you've done? Before tonight, the court had one claim on this valley: her soul, sold a century ago. Now they hold paper on *you*. They can spend you. Trade you. Burn you for fuel the way they burn everything else they own."

"Then we move fast," Isandro said, rubbing feeling back into his wrists, "before they read the fine print."

Marisol stared at him. And then she laughed — a short, startled, genuine sound, quickly smothered against her sleeve, the first honest laugh he'd heard from her. "Gods help me. You sound like her."

"I'll take that as a compliment."

"Don't. It's a warning." She rose, pulling him up with her, and pressed something into his palm: a stub of chalk-white stone, cool and dense. "Bone-chalk. From the galleries below. Mark the walls as we move — doors, turns, dead ends. If we get separated, follow your own marks backward, and whatever you hear calling your name in the dark, *don't answer it*."

They slipped out of the cell into the corridor beyond, two shadows among shadows, moving deeper into the warm and breathing dark.

And as they went — past cell after cell, most empty, some not, past doors behind which things stirred and breathed and sometimes, horribly, *hummed* — Isandro felt the twin marks on his arm pulse in slow alternation, like two hearts arguing in one body.

Old oath. New claim. The god who asked nothing. The court that took everything.

Somewhere above, Paloma was tracing maps against her thigh, building a trail out of memory and defiance. Somewhere below, in a cathedral of silence he could feel through the soles of his feet like a missing tooth, something vast and patient waited behind a cage of quiet.

Isandro flexed his marked hand, and followed Marisol into the dark.

*Two days,* he told himself. *However long it takes. However much it costs.*

*Hold on, Paloma. I'm coming back with a map of my own.*
