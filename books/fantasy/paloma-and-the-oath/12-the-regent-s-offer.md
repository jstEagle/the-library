## 12. The Regent's Offer

They dressed her in gold.

Paloma had expected chains when the handmaidens came at what she guessed was morning — though morning meant nothing down here, where the light came from veins of luminous stone and never changed its mind. Instead they brought silk the color of candle flame, a gown heavy with embroidery that moved like water when she walked, and jewelry set with stones she refused to name because naming them would mean admitting how beautiful they were.

"You honor me," she said to the eldest handmaiden, a woman with eyes like tarnished silver who had not spoken once in three days.

The woman only smiled and fastened the last clasp at Paloma's throat — a pendant shaped like an open hand, palm upward, as if offering something or asking for it. Paloma had learned by now that everything in the court beneath the mountain was both.

*Honored property.* That was the phrase the Regent's steward had used, and he had not been cruel about it. He had said it the way a librarian describes a rare edition: with genuine reverence, and no awareness at all that books might prefer to stay unshelved.

She ate breakfast alone in her gilded rooms — honeyed bread, fruit from orchards that could not possibly exist this far underground, wine dark as garnets. The food was perfect. That was the point of it. Every meal was a small argument that life here would be sweet if only she stopped struggling against the shape of it.

Paloma tore the bread and thought about maps.

Three days of captivity had taught her the court's rhythms the way she'd once taught herself the contours of the valley above: by attention, by repetition, by loving a place enough to memorize its lies. The guards changed at intervals marked by bells that rang soundlessly — she felt them in her teeth. The steward came twice daily. The handmaidens never spoke but heard everything. And every corridor she'd glimpsed, every archway and stair, she had drawn afterward in her mind, over and over, until the court was a map she carried under her tongue.

She had stolen one page of the ledger. It sat folded now inside her bodice, against her skin, warm as a second heartbeat. *Reyes, Paloma. Sold by Tomás Reyes, 1847. Payment deferred. Payment deferred. Payment due.*

Somewhere above her, Isandro was either dead or coming for her. She had decided, on the first night, that she would not let herself believe the first thing. Belief was a resource. She spent it carefully.

* * *

The Regent sent for her at the hour the bells did not ring.

Two guards flanked her through corridors she catalogued even as fear crawled up her spine — left at the arch of weeping stone, past the gallery where portraits hung of people whose faces were all subtly wrong, down the stair whose steps were worn in the middle as if by centuries of feet, though the court claimed to be younger than that. Lies stacked on lies. She gathered them all.

The throne room opened around her like the inside of a geode, vast and faceted, its walls glittering with crystal that grew in spirals toward a ceiling lost in shadow. Torches burned without smoke. And at the center, on a seat carved from a single block of black stone, sat the Regent Below.

She had seen him twice before. He was beautiful the way rot is beautiful when you find it in amber — preserved, perfect, and profoundly wrong. His face was ageless in a way that suggested not youth but *arrest*, a moment caught and held so long it had forgotten how to move on. When he smiled, which he did now, the smile arrived a half-second before his eyes agreed to it.

"Paloma Reyes." Her name in his mouth sounded like a signature being traced. "Cartographer. Great-great-great-granddaughter of Tomás, who signed in ink and fear. You've been reading my ledgers."

It wasn't a question. She kept her face still. "I've been reading your bookkeeping," she said. "There's a difference. A ledger tells you what someone owns. Bookkeeping tells you what they're afraid of."

Something flickered behind the Regent's beauty — interest, or its imitation. He rose from the throne and descended the three steps, and up close she saw that his robes were embroidered with thousands of tiny names, stitched in thread that seemed to shift when she wasn't looking directly at it.

"Your ancestor was afraid," the Regent said. "That winter killed half the valley. Crops failed. Children starved. And a stranger came down from the mountain with an offer, as strangers always do." He spread his hands, palms up — the same gesture as the pendant at her throat. "Tomás sold what he did not know he possessed, to save what he loved. I have never considered that a crime. Have you?"

"I consider it theft," Paloma said. "He stole from people who weren't born yet. Me. My mother. Her mother. A debt chain isn't a gift."

"No," the Regent agreed softly. "It's an inheritance. And here is what no one above ever understood, cartographer: inheritances can be refused. But only by the one who holds them. Only willingly." He began to walk, slow, circling her the way she'd circle an unfamiliar landmark before committing it to paper. "You have been very busy in my court. Reading. Listening. Stealing." He said the last word without heat, almost with approval. "I admire industry. So let us stop pretending, you and I. Let us speak plainly, as two people who both understand that everything is a transaction."

"Everything is a transaction to you," Paloma said. "That's not understanding. That's a cage you've decorated."

The Regent laughed — a real laugh, startlingly warm, and that warmth frightened her more than any threat had. "Oh, you are *better* than the prophecies promised. Yes, there are prophecies about you too, in case you wondered why the hounds knew your name before you did. Books below as well as above. Everyone writes about the girl with the sold soul. None of them asked what *you* want." He stopped before her, close enough that she could see the crystal light refracting in eyes that held no reflection at all. "So. What do you want, Paloma Reyes?"

And that was the trap, she realized. Not the question itself — the honesty of it. Because she did want things. She wanted the valley mapped truly, every impossible contour explained. She wanted her mother's name remembered without grief attached. She wanted to stand at a boundary stone with a mercenary who looked at her like she was weather arriving after a long drought, and she wanted that wanting to be *enough*, to be allowed, to be hers.

The Regent watched her want, and smiled his delayed smile, and she understood that he was counting.

* * *

"Here is my offer," he said, when the silence between them had ripened. "Renounce the oath-keeper."

The words landed in her chest like dropped stones. "Isandro."

"He carries an oath older than your family's debt. An oath sworn to a god who has never answered him — not once, not in thirty years of devotion. Do you know what that is, Paloma? That is a man who has given his whole life to a silence." The Regent's voice softened into something almost tender, and the tenderness was the sharpest blade in the room. "You above all should understand what it does to a person, to be owed rather than chosen. His god owes him nothing because his god *is* nothing — a hollow name, an empty shrine. But I am here. I am listening. I see you, exactly as you are, and I am making you an offer no one in your bloodline has ever been offered: freedom, freely given."

"And the price."

"The price is a renunciation. Words, nothing more. Say that you release him from any claim, that you will not wait for him, that you choose your own path — and I tear the page from my ledger. Your soul returns to you, whole, unencumbered. No debt. No prophecy. No destiny written by other hands." He gestured, and the air above his palm filled with images: the valley above, rendered in light — but *changed*. Orchards heavy with fruit in the dead soil. Rivers running full through the dry season. The boundary stones glowing soft and safe, the wraiths gone, the travelers walking freely. Children — she recognized some of their faces from the ledger pages, the souls sold generations ago — laughing in a village square that had known only hardship. "This is what I offer the valley. Prosperity bought with one word from your mouth. One word, and everyone you have ever met eats well for the rest of their lives."

Paloma's breath went shallow. She hated that it did. She hated that some traitorous part of her *leaned*.

"That's not prosperity," she managed. "That's a leash made of abundance. You feed them so they never ask where the food comes from."

"Perhaps." The Regent shrugged, elegant. "Or perhaps I am simply generous, and the world above has taught you to distrust generosity because it has never once received any. Consider, cartographer, the shape of your life. Your valley keeps oaths to a silent god and calls the silence holiness. Your elders keep secrets from their children and call it protection. Your mercenary—" and here his voice curved, delicate as a hook, "—your mercenary broke a lifetime of discipline the moment he saw you taken. Do you think that ends well? Men like him are built to hold lines, not to cross them. When he comes for you — and he will come, the fool, the dear fool — he will come into my court carrying an oath I can snap like thread. And then I will own him too, and it will be *your* doing, your face, your name that led him down. Unless you say the word first. Unless you free yourself, and spare him, and spare the valley, all with a single act of will."

"There it is," Paloma whispered.

"There what is?"

"The trap wearing the shape of mercy. You're not offering me freedom. You're offering me the chance to abandon someone *voluntarily* — because a soul given up freely tastes different than one taken. Isn't that right? That's the whole architecture of this place, isn't it? You don't steal. You *arrange*. You make people sell themselves and call it consent." Her hands were shaking; she pressed them flat against her skirts. "Tomás didn't know what he was signing. You'll make sure I know exactly what I'm refusing. That's the difference. That's the vintage."

For a long moment the Regent said nothing. The torches burned. Somewhere far off, water dripped in a rhythm like a patient heart.

"You see clearly," he said at last, and there was no warmth in it now, only a kind of scholarly respect that chilled her more than rage would have. "Yes. Freely surrendered souls sustain me as harvested grain sustains no one else. Taken souls are ash. Given souls are wine. Your ancestor gave in ignorance, which is a lesser vintage, but a century of patience improves any cellar." He turned away, robes whispering with their thousand shifting names, and gazed up at the crystal vault. "But do not mistake clarity for immunity, Paloma. Seeing the trap does not spring it. You may refuse me today. Refusal has a cost as well — everything does; that is the first law beneath the mountain. The feast approaches. The court grows hungry. And hungry courts do not wait forever on the whims of one cartographer, however well-mapped her mind may be."

* * *

He dismissed her. The guards walked her back through corridors she mapped, mapped, mapped — and it was only in her gilded rooms, alone, the door sealed behind her, that she let herself feel the true weight of what he had done.

Because the offer had not been the trap. The offer had been the *gift*. He had shown her the exit, painted it in orchard-light and children's laughter, and then he had shown her the toll. And now the exit would never leave her. Every hour from this one, she would carry the knowledge that salvation existed, simple as a sentence, and that speaking it would save everyone except the man who was probably, at this very moment, breaking himself open to reach her.

*Being chosen,* she thought, staring at the false dawn of the luminous walls. *That's what he offered. Not freedom — the feeling of it. Someone finally saying: you are worth this much.*

Her whole life she had been a debt. Named in a book before she drew breath, priced by a stranger's desperation, walking around in a body that belonged, in some cosmic ledger, to another party. And the Regent had looked at all of that and said: *I see you. I value you. Choose me back.*

It would have worked on her, she admitted, pressing her forehead to the cool crystal wall. If she had met him in another life — if she had never found the codex, never spilled wine on her sleeve while a quiet mercenary laughed across the table — it might have worked. Seduction was not always flesh. Sometimes it was simply being *seen* by the one thing in the world large enough to hold you.

But she had been seen already. Truly. By a man who read her maps upside down just to be near her, who saved strangers in the dark because no one had ever told him he was allowed to want anything for himself, who was at this moment somewhere above or below, coming for her with an oath he'd have to break and a god he'd never heard answer.

Isandro had never once asked her to choose him. That was the difference. That was the whole difference, and it outweighed every orchard in every lie.

Paloma reached into her bodice and drew out the folded ledger page. In the crystal light, the ink of her own name seemed to pulse faintly, as if the paper remembered being part of something larger. She smoothed it flat against her knee and studied it again — not the entry this time, but the watermark pressed into the vellum's corner: a spiral, faint as a fingerprint, matching the spirals of crystal in the throne room walls. Matching, she realized with a slow cold thrill, the spiral she had sketched three days ago in the archive above, without knowing why, her pencil moving as if the map were drawing itself.

The page was a key. She had felt it since she stole it, and now she knew the lock.

Outside her door, the soundless bells tolled the change of guard. Paloma folded the page along its old creases and tucked it away, and began, methodically, to take apart the gown of gold — because underneath its embroidery, the silk was strong, and strong silk had uses, and whatever the Regent believed about hungry courts waiting, she intended to make sure that when the feast came, it would find its honored property gone.

Far beneath her, deeper than the throne room, deeper than anything mapped, something ancient shifted in its sleep — and for one impossible instant, Paloma felt it notice her. Not the Regent. Something older. Something *silent*.

It felt, she thought wildly, like being read by a map that had been waiting a hundred years for someone to finally look at it.
