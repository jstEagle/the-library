## 13. Breaking the Oath

They came for him at the hour the court called morning, though no sun had ever reached this deep.

Isandro knew it was morning because the shadows thinned. In the court beneath the mountain, shadow was a substance — it pooled in corners like standing water, clung to walls like moss, and breathed. When the Regent Below willed it, the shadows drew back from the torches, and that was dawn. He had been counting dawns for three days. Three dawns, two interrogations, one feast of shadows he'd been dragged through in chains to be displayed like a trophy boar.

Now the door of his cell opened without a sound, which was somehow worse than a scream.

Two guards entered first — tall figures whose armor was grown rather than forged, plates of black chitin weeping slow beads of oil. Between them walked a herald in robes of gray silk, its face smooth as an egg, features suggested rather than present, the way a face appears in fog. It carried a scroll sealed with wax the color of dried blood.

"Isandro Villalobos," the herald said, and its voice was many voices braided together, none of them its own. "The Court of the Regent Below has weighed your crimes. You entered these halls uninvited. You touched what was contracted. You bear the marks of a god the court has already consumed." The egg-face tilted. "The sentence is unmaking."

"Unmaking," Isandro repeated. His throat was raw. He hadn't spoken much in three days; there had been no one worth speaking to, and nothing worth lying about. "Not death."

"Death is for things that live," the herald said. "You will be unwritten. The court will remember you never came down the stair. The valley will remember you never stood watch. Even your mother's milk will forget it ever fed you." A pause, and something almost like pleasure moved through the braided voice. "It is a mercy. Most beg for it, by the end."

Isandro looked past the herald at the guards, at the corridor beyond, at the faint golden seam in the far wall where Paloma had once told him — laughing, wine on her sleeve, alive — that every map has a flaw if you look long enough.

"Then I have until the end," he said. "Good. I've been wanting a word with your Regent."

* * *

They did not grant him the word. They marched him instead through galleries of bone and gilded rot toward the Court Proper, where justice beneath the mountain was performed rather than decided. Isandro catalogued everything as he went — the way a man dying catalogs, fiercely, uselessly, wonderfully. Forty paces between the cell block and the first junction. A left turn where the air grew colder. A staircase spiraling down past niches where faces were set into the walls like coins, souls pressed flat and gleaming, their mouths open in expressions that might have been screams or might have been song.

He looked for Marisol and did not see her. After the feast, after they'd pulled him off Paloma and the contract-marks had flared black along his arms, they had taken her somewhere else. He chose to believe she was alive because the alternative was a weight he could not carry and still walk straight.

And Paloma. He had touched her hand for four seconds. Four seconds was all the contract needed. He had felt it happen — felt something reach through her skin into his, cold and patient as a root finding water, and write itself along his bones. *Claimed by association. Collateral of the debt.* The Regent had smiled with too many teeth and said, "How convenient. Two debts, one ledger."

So this was how it ended. Not on the boundary stones under open sky, not old and gray with forty years of quiet service behind him, but here, in a cathedral of theft, chained to a lie he'd only just learned was a lie.

The Court Proper opened around him like the inside of a bell.

Pillars of veined marble rose into darkness. The floor was obsidian polished to a mirror, and everywhere he looked his reflection looked back — dozens of Isandros, chained, bleeding from the brow where the hounds had struck him, each one watching him with an expression he didn't recognize until he understood it was hope. The gallery seats were filled with the court's nobility: things that had once been people, perhaps, or things that had worn people so long they'd forgotten the difference. They rustled when he entered, a sound like dry leaves applauding.

And above it all, on a throne carved from a single tooth of the mountain itself, sat the Regent Below.

Isandro had seen it at the feast, but seeing was different when you were kneeling. The Regent wore the shape of a tall man in robes of woven contracts — actual parchment, thousands of pages, stitched into cloth, each page covered in names. Its face was handsome the way a portrait is handsome: correct, composed, and utterly without life behind it. When it spoke, the whole court leaned in.

"The oath-keeper," the Regent said, and its voice made the obsidian hum. "Do you know why I have kept you alive these three days? Not mercy. Curiosity. I wanted to see the marks."

It gestured, lazily, and Isandro's chains tightened, dragging his arms forward, baring them to the court. Along both forearms, from wrist to elbow, the oath-marks burned their familiar silver-gray: the interlocking rings of the Silent God's covenant, the pattern every sworn keeper of the valley carried, the blessing he had received at seventeen kneeling before an empty shrine, promising his life to a god who had never once answered.

"They are beautiful," the Regent mused. "Do you know what they are, Isandro Villalobos? Truly?"

"Blessings," Isandro said. "Marks of service."

"Chains," the Regent said, and the court laughed, a sound like cutlery dropped down a well. "Beautiful chains. I should know. I helped forge them." It rose from the throne, and the parchment robes whispered with ten thousand names. "Your Silent God bound His keepers with vows disguised as gifts. Every promise you swore fed Him strength; every year you kept faith, the binding deepened. He built Himself a wall of loyal hearts and called it a valley. And when I took Him — when I caged Him beneath this mountain and drank His silence — do you know what happened to His precious keepers?" The Regent descended the steps of the throne, unhurried. "Nothing. Their marks stayed. Their oaths held. Because an oath outlives its god, little soldier. A chain does not need the hand that closed it. Only the lock."

"And you hold the lock," Isandro said.

"I hold everything here." The Regent stopped before him, close enough that Isandro could smell old paper and something sweeter underneath, like fruit rotting in a locked room. "Which brings us to the sentence. Unmaking is delicate work with a marked man. First the marks must come off. And there is only one way to remove an oath-mark, keeper." The handsome dead face bent close. "You must break it yourself. Willingly. Loudly. With your whole heart turned against the vow. Then the chain slips free — and the man who breaks his god's oath is emptied. Hollowed. A vessel with nothing left, ready to be filled with whatever the court chooses." It straightened. "I will give you a choice, because I am generous and because it amuses me. Die whole, and be unwritten. Or break your oath, live hollow, and serve. Many of my finest guards began exactly where you kneel."

The court waited. The reflections in the obsidian floor waited. Somewhere far above, Isandro thought he could feel the valley — the boundary stones, the empty shrine, the wind coming down off the ridge with the smell of pine and rain. He thought of thirty years of patrols. Of the traveler he'd pulled from the wraith's reach. Of the elders' faces when they forbade him the descent. Of Paloma's voice saying *your maps feel familiar* and meaning, without knowing it, that she had always been coming home to him.

His whole heart turned against the vow.

But not the way the Regent meant.

* * *

"No," Isandro said.

The Regent's head tilted, a portrait adjusting its frame.

"You want me to break my oath to make me hollow. To break it cursing the god who never answered, spitting on thirty years, so the breaking leaves nothing behind." Isandro got his feet under him, chains screaming against the obsidian, and stood — swaying, bleeding, upright. "So I'll break it differently. I'll break it telling the truth."

"Keeper—"

"You want truth? Here is truth." His voice cracked across the court like a whip, and the nobility flinched, because it was a sound this place had not heard in a century: a man speaking plainly, with nothing bought and nothing sold. "I swore an oath to a god I thought had abandoned me. I kept it anyway. Thirty years, I kept faith with silence. I thought that was devotion." He laughed, and it came out ragged. "It wasn't devotion. It was obedience. And obedience to a lie isn't virtue — it's just a longer way of being owned."

The Regent's expression did not change, but the parchment robes stirred, pages turning of their own accord, searching for something.

"So hear me, court beneath the mountain. Hear me, whatever is left of my god in your cage. Hear me, Paloma, if any part of you can hear anything in this rotten place." He filled his lungs with air that tasted of dust and stolen souls. "I, Isandro Villalobos, break the oath. Not because the god failed me — but because the vow was never the god's. It was yours. Yours, Regent. Your contract dressed in silver. Your chain painted as a blessing. You wrote the words the shrine made us swear, and you fed on every year we gave you, and you called our loyalty your harvest."

The marks on his forearms flared white-hot.

"But an oath broken in truth belongs to the breaker," Isandro said, and now he was shouting, and the obsidian floor was ringing like a struck bell, and his reflections were all standing with him, dozens of Isandros, fists raised. "So I take it back. All of it. Every patrol, every vigil, every silent year at the boundary stones — I take it back, and I give it to no one. Not to the valley's elders who chose comfort over courage. Not to the court that eats children's souls. Not even to the god, if the god wants worship. My life is mine. My faith is mine. And I swear — freely, loudly, with my whole heart — that I will spend both tearing this place down to its roots!"

He broke the oath aloud.

The world broke with him.

* * *

There was no pain. That was the first impossible thing. He had braced for agony — for the holy fire the elders had promised would consume an oath-breaker, for the hollowing the Regent had described. Instead there was a sound, low and vast, like a glacier deciding to move, and the silver-gray rings on his arms *unlocked*.

He saw it clearly, in the half-second before the light: each ring was a link, and each link had a seam, and the seams had always been there, hidden in the pattern, invisible unless you were looking for locks. Chain-work. Masterful chain-work, wound around his bones since he was seventeen, tightening a fraction each year, each kept vow another turn of the key. The blessing had never blessed. It had *measured*. It had held him at exactly the height a servant should stand, and called the ceiling heaven.

The links shattered outward in a corona of white fire.

The shockwave took the nearest guards off their feet. The obsidian floor crazed beneath him in a spiderweb of cracks, and every reflection in it — every Isandro — blazed with the same light, and then the reflections were not reflections anymore. Something rose out of the floor and into him, or out of him and into the floor; he could not have said which, only that the wall between was gone. Magic flooded back into him the way it had on the root-stair, but a hundredfold, a thousandfold — not the painful trickle of a man drinking after a lifetime of thirst but a river remembering its bed. Thirty years of withheld power, dammed and stored in the marrow of a faithful man, released all at once.

He could see everything. The seams in the court's glamour, gold thread over rot. The souls pressed into the walls, flickering as his light washed over them, mouths opening wider — and now he could hear them, faintly, a chorus humming under the stone. He could see the contract written on his own chest where Paloma's touch had marked him, a black root-system of clauses — and he watched, with fierce joy, as the white fire of his freedom crawled along those roots and *burned them back*, burning only the parts that bound, leaving untouched the single thread at the center that was not a clause at all but a memory: her hand in his, four seconds, warm.

The court screamed. The nobility shrank back from the light like frost from a window. The Regent alone held its ground, and for the first time the portrait-face showed something alive behind it, and the thing it showed was calculation.

"Impossible," the Regent said. "The oath-breaker is always hollowed. That is the design. *I* designed it."

"You designed a cage," Isandro said. Fire ran along his arms now where the marks had been — not chains but script, new writing, in a hand that was entirely his own. "You never imagined someone would break it loving the wrong thing. You counted on hatred. Hatred hollows." He raised his arms, and the light gathered between his hands like a held breath. "Turns out love doesn't. Turns out it fills you all the way up."

He brought his hands together and the corona detonated.

The chains did not shatter — chains, he understood now, were the wrong word for what he could do. Chains implied effort, resistance, a contest of strengths. What happened was simpler: the chains forgot they had ever been chains. The iron remembered being ore, the ore remembered being stone, and the stone remembered being mountain, and Isandro Villalobos, oath-breaker, walked out of the wreckage of his bindings into a court full of screaming shadows with the power of a lifetime of stolen devotion burning in his chest.

The guards rushed him. He did not fight them the way he'd fought all his life, blade and angle and economy. He simply *insisted*, and reality agreed. The first guard's grown armor unbuilt itself into a drift of black sand. The second found the floor beneath it suddenly honest about being liquid. The herald's scroll burst into flame in its hands, and the herald looked down at its burning sentence with its egg-face and made a sound like a library sighing.

Above the throne, alarm bells Isandro had never noticed began to toll — deep, subterranean, felt in the teeth more than heard. Doors he hadn't known existed slammed open along the gallery. From somewhere far below, rising through the mountain's bones, came a second sound underneath the bells: a rhythmic, grinding thunder, like something enormous turning over in its sleep.

The Regent had not fled. It stood at the foot of its throne, robes of contracts whipping in a wind that had no source, and it looked at Isandro with the expression of a merchant watching his ledger catch fire.

"Do you know what you've done?" it said. "That power was *stored*. Decades of it, banked in a faithful heart, earmarked for harvest. You haven't freed yourself, keeper. You've spent yourself. There is perhaps an hour of that fire in you, and then you will be emptier than any hollow I ever made — and I will be here, and I do not tire, and I do not run out."

"An hour," Isandro said. The fire in his chest roared. His blood sang with it. Somewhere in this labyrinth, Marisol was locked in a dark cell; somewhere above, a war band of outcasts was waiting on his signal; somewhere in the walls, a hundred pressed-flat souls were humming louder now, and the sound had words in it, and the words were *soon*. "An hour is more than I need."

He turned for the doors — and stopped.

Because the far wall of the Court Proper, the one threaded with that faint golden seam he'd memorized three days ago, had begun to glow. Not with his fire. With a softer light, warm and steady, and through the widening crack came a smell that did not belong in this place at all: pine, and rain, and woodsmoke.

And a voice, low and furious and unmistakable, calling his name.

"Isandro! Hold them off — we're coming through!"

Paloma had found the flaw in the map.
