## 14. Escape Through the Bone Galleries

The page was warm in Paloma's hand.

She had carried it for two days folded against her ribs, tucked into the lining of the gown the court had dressed her in — silk the color of drowned things, beautiful and wrong. The stolen ledger page had not yellowed or cracked as paper should. It had grown heavier, the way a stone grows heavy when you hold it too long, and sometimes, when the court's music drifted through the corridors like smoke looking for lungs to fill, she could feel it pulse against her skin.

A heartbeat that was not her own.

*Good*, she thought now, standing in the dark of the servants' passage with her ear pressed to the cold stone. *Stay awake. We need you tonight.*

Beyond the wall, the feast of shadows was reaching its crescendo. She could hear the Regent's voice threading above it all — that voice like honey poured over a blade — and beneath it the laughter of courtiers who had been dead so long they had forgotten laughter was supposed to mean something. Two hundred souls in that hall, give or take, wearing bodies of shadow and borrowed light, feasting on the residue of everything the mountain had stolen.

Paloma closed her eyes and let herself be a cartographer again.

It was the only way she had survived. When fear came — and it came constantly, arriving in waves, in the Regent's smile, in the way the hounds' eyes caught no light at all — she did what she had always done with terrain that frightened her. She mapped it.

Four days in the court beneath the mountain, and she knew its bones better than the Regent suspected. She had learned it the way she learned everything: by pretending to be less than she was. Docile. Dazed. A girl overwhelmed by gilded halls, grateful for her velvet cage. She had walked where they told her to walk, dined where they told her to dine, and all the while her eyes had been working — measuring corridors, counting turns, noting which stairwells echoed hollow and which swallowed sound entirely. She had filled three pages of her own memory with a map no one would ever find, because it lived in the one place the court could not confiscate.

And the map had shown her something the Regent would very much have preferred she never learn: this place had been built, not grown. Every corridor obeyed an architecture, and every architecture had a logic, and every logic could be broken by someone stubborn enough to understand it.

The bone galleries were the proof.

They ran beneath the feast-hall like roots beneath a tree — corridors ribbed with femurs and stacked skulls, the remains of whoever had built this place before the Regent wore its face. The courtiers avoided them. The hounds patrolled them, but poorly, lazily, because nothing down here had ever had anywhere to go.

Nothing until now.

Paloma pressed the ledger page flat against the lock of the cell door and felt it heat, then shiver, then dissolve partway through itself — ink crawling off the parchment like ants, pouring into the iron. The contract-script that bound Isandro and Marisol inside recognized the page. Of course it did. It was written in the same hand, drawn from the same ledger, powered by the same stolen centuries. One piece of the Regent's machinery unlocking another. He had built his whole dominion out of debts, and debts, it turned out, cut both ways.

The lock sighed open.

Isandro stood on the other side of the bars, and even in the gloom Paloma saw what the soul-contract's mark had done to him — the black sigil burned across his chest like a brand, spreading tendrils toward his throat. Four days since the guards had torn them apart, and the mark had grown. Her stomach dropped.

"You're late," he said. His voice was rough as gravel, but his eyes — his eyes were the same steady brown they'd been across a restaurant table in the ordinary world, a lifetime ago, when the worst thing either of them faced was whether to order dessert.

"I was building you an exit," Paloma said. "It takes time."

Marisol rose from the cell's corner, witch-thin and sharp-eyed, her stolen protection burned away to nothing. "How much time do we have?"

"The Regent retires at the third bell. The hounds change patrol at the half." Paloma pulled the ruined page from the lock — it was blank now, spent, its power poured out. She felt its absence immediately, like losing a limb. "So we have until the half-bell. Maybe forty minutes."

"Forty minutes to cross the bone galleries, climb the root-stair, and reach the boundary stone." Marisol smiled without any humor at all. "I've seen people try to cross those galleries in a year."

"They didn't have me," Paloma said.

* * *

The galleries were worse than she remembered from the glimpses she'd stolen.

Bones everywhere — not scattered but *arranged*, mortared into the walls and ceiling in patterns that hurt to look at, spirals within spirals, a geometry that seemed to shift when she wasn't watching directly. The air tasted of dust and old grief. Their footsteps made no echo; the bone drank sound the way the valley above drank magic.

Isandro moved beside her like a weapon sheathed in a man — silent, watchful, one hand always near the guard's knife he'd taken off a sleeping sentry. Marisol drifted behind, muttering ward-fragments under her breath, small protections that fizzled and died almost as fast as she cast them. The court's hunger soaked through everything down here.

"Left here," Paloma whispered, and took it.

The corridor branched seven ways ahead of them, each passage identical, ribbed white and vanishing into dark. Marisol made a small despairing sound. Isandro just looked at her.

"How?" he asked.

"Skulls." She touched one set into the wall at eye height. Its cranium had a hairline fracture running left to right. "Every junction has one marker skull. Fractured means the passage loops back toward the feast-hall. Intact means forward." She had spent two nights confirming the pattern while her escorts believed her admiring the craftsmanship. "Whoever built this place wanted their servants to find their way home. Then the Regent moved in and stopped caring about anyone finding anything."

"And the hounds?"

"Hounds don't read maps." She allowed herself one fierce smile. "That's the point."

They went forward. Twice more she chose correctly at junctions, reading fractures in the dark by touch, and twice more the gallery bent away from the feast-hall's distant glow. For eleven minutes — she counted — it worked.

Then the howling started.

It came from behind and below, a sound like a wolf crying through a cathedral organ, and it was answered from ahead and above. Paloma's blood went cold. The patrols hadn't changed on schedule. Something had spooked them — the empty cells, maybe, discovered early, or the dead sentry, or simply the ledger page's final flicker of power ringing through the court's nerves like a struck bell.

"They know," Marisol breathed.

"Then we stop sneaking." Isandro drew the knife. "Paloma. Fastest route. Doesn't matter how loud."

Loud. Right. She shut her eyes and rebuilt her map behind them — the whole subterranean country of it, every corridor she'd charted, every junction, every fracture-marked skull — and overlaid it with the one thing she'd noticed and filed away and told no one: the way certain passages hummed faintly underfoot, hollow, load-bearing in ways that suggested older architecture buried beneath the Regent's additions.

"This way." She broke into a run.

The hounds found them at the fourth junction — three of them, unfolding out of a side gallery like shadows given joints and teeth, their eyes twin absences. Isandro met the first one head-on, knife low, and Paloma had no time to be afraid because Marisol grabbed her arm and *pulled*, shouting directions of her own now, witch-sight guiding them around the fight. Behind them Isandro shouted — actual pain in it — and then a wet sound, and then silence except for panting.

He caught up at the next bend, bleeding from the forearm, knife gone, holding a hound's tooth the length of her finger.

"Trophy," he gasped. "For the elders. Proof I keep my promises."

"We'll frame it," Paloma said, and grabbed his hand, and ran.

* * *

The humming passage saved them.

She'd guessed right about the hollow sound — it was a service tunnel, older than everything around it, built by the bone-masons whose skeletons lined the walls. It sloped upward at a punishing grade, narrow enough that they climbed nearly single file, hands on slick stone. And it had one more gift, the one Paloma was gambling everything on: at its midpoint it passed *beneath* the great pillared hall where the Regent held court, separated from it by a ceiling thin enough that they could hear the Regent's voice come through the stone.

"—the thief is found. Let the galleries run red. And bring me the cartographer *alive*. Her soul is owed, and I do not forgive debts."

Paloma's blood turned to ice water. Not because of the threat. Because of the word *cartographer*.

He knew what she was. He'd known all along — known her the way you know a tool, the way you know a key. Everything since her arrival, the honored treatment, the velvet cage, had been a farmer's patience with the crop.

Isandro's hand found hers in the dark. His grip said everything his voice couldn't risk: *not yet, not today, keep moving.*

They kept moving.

The hounds were smarter than she'd credited. They didn't chase them into the tunnel — they went around, cutting to the far end, and when Paloma, Isandro, and Marisol emerged onto a ledge overlooking a vast bone-floored cavern, six of the creatures were already there, spread in a crescent, herding them toward the cavern's far wall.

Where there was no wall. Just a drop, black and breathing cold.

"Well," Marisol said. "It was a good escape. Very well mapped."

Paloma's mind was screaming, flipping through her mental atlas at desperate speed — and then it snagged, the way a boot snags on a root, on a detail she'd catalogued without understanding: the cavern floor wasn't solid bone. It was a lattice. Grated, interlocked femurs laid over open space, and the pattern of the lattice —

— was a spiral.

The same spiral as the fracture-marks. The same spiral as the masons' work everywhere in these galleries. Which meant the masons had built this floor too, and masons built floors with weight limits, and load-bearing lattices failed along their seams —

"Hounds can't read maps," she said aloud. "But they weigh something."

"What?" Isandro said.

"Run *diagonally*." She seized his sleeve. "Across the seams. Break the lattice behind us. Trust me!"

She didn't wait for agreement. She bolted onto the bone floor, choosing her steps by instinct and geometry, striking each stride along the weakest seam — and the lattice sang, cracked, gave way behind her in a cascade like a glacier calving. The first hound plunged through with a shriek into the dark below. The second leapt, cleared the gap, landed on a seam she'd already weakened, and dropped. Isandro thundered behind her, heavy, trusting her absolutely, and she screamed route corrections at him — *left, hard left, jump* — reading the floor faster than she'd ever read any landscape, the whole cavern suddenly, gloriously legible.

Marisol came last, light-footed, cackling like something unhinged.

They hit the far ledge together as half the floor collapsed into the abyss behind them, hound-shrieks fading down into whatever waited at the bottom of the world. Paloma stood heaving, shaking, alive, and felt Isandro's hand close over her shoulder and turn her around.

He was grinning. Actually grinning, blood on his teeth, the first true grin she'd ever seen from him.

"You broke their floor," he said.

"I broke their *floor*," she agreed, breathless, and laughed — wild, unlovely, alive.

* * *

The root-stair was close now. She could feel it — a wrongness in the air, a thinning of the court's pressure, the way the atmosphere changed before a storm breaks. Above them, somewhere past miles of stone, was the valley. The boundary stone. Home, if home still existed, if the elders hadn't already declared them dead traitors, if—

Marisol stopped so abruptly Paloma walked into her back.

Ahead, where the passage should have opened onto the stair, stood the Regent.

Not the whole of him — a projection, a presence, shadow poured into the shape of a tall courteous man, gold-eyed, smiling. But real enough. Close enough that Paloma could smell him: cold stone and old coins.

"Cartographer," the Regent said warmly. "You continue to surprise me. Do you know, in four hundred years, no one has ever broken my floor?"

"Someone should have," Paloma said.

"Oh, undoubtedly." The golden eyes moved past her to Isandro, to Marisol, cataloguing, appraising. "But here is the difficulty, little mapmaker. You are standing in my house, on my stairs, holding my page's ashes in your bodice, and the debt your grandmother's grandmother signed is *still outstanding*. You cannot outrun a contract. It follows. It always follows."

"Then I'll amend it," Paloma said, and heard her own voice come out steady, and marveled at it. "Contracts have loopholes. You taught me that — your ledger, your language, your law. I've been reading nothing else for four days." She stepped forward, into his shadow, and lifted her chin. "You want my soul? Come collect it yourself. Or send your hounds down a staircase that's about to become very interesting."

The Regent's smile did not waver. But his eyes, for one flickering instant, went *upward* — toward the deep places of the mountain, toward the cathedral of silence Marisol had described in whispers — and Paloma saw, unmistakably, fear move through them like a fish under ice.

Something down there had stirred. Something had opened one eye.

The Regent looked back at her, and for the first time, the warmth in his voice was real.

"Then let us see," he said softly, "what you've awakened."

And the mountain began to shake.
