## 15. The Price of the Page

Marisol felt the page die before she saw it burn.

It happened in the space between one heartbeat and the next, somewhere in the bone galleries where the walls were stacked with the femurs of people whose names no one above ground remembered. One moment she was running — Isandro's broad back ahead of her, Paloma's voice calling turns in a cartographer's clipped cadence — and the next, a coldness opened in her chest like a door swinging wide.

She stumbled. Caught herself against a rib-cage archway that had once belonged to something enormous, human-shaped or not, she didn't want to know.

The page.

She'd sewn it into the lining of her coat three days ago, pressed flat against her left side, over her heart, because that was where protection lived in the old grammar of things. The Regent's court ran on ledgers and signatures and names written in ink that drank light, and a page torn from the ledger of souls was a page torn from the Regent's own skin. While it had been whole, the court's hounds had smelled her as *property* — as something already claimed, already catalogued, already owned down to the marrow. They had parted around her like water around a stone.

Now the stone was sinking.

The coldness spread. She could feel the page unraveling against her ribs, thread by invisible thread, its stolen letters going gray, then blank, then nothing at all. And with every letter that died, she felt the court's attention swing toward her like a lighthouse beam.

*There you are,* said the attention. *There you've been all along.*

"Marisol." Isandro had stopped, turned back. Of course he had. He was the kind of man who turned back; it was his whole tragedy, worn like a second set of oath-marks. "Keep moving. The hounds will circle behind us."

"They're not behind us," she said. Her voice came out flat and strange, and she heard in it the sound of a woman reading her own death warrant. "They're coming for me."

Paloma appeared at Isandro's shoulder, ledger page clutched in one hand — her page, the proof, the key that had opened their chains. In the blue-white glow of the gallery's veined stone, her face was sharp and pale and utterly unfooled. She was, Marisol thought not for the first time, far too clever for anyone's comfort, including her own.

"The page," Paloma said. Not a question.

"Burning." Marisol peeled open her coat. Against the lining, where the stolen page had lain, there was now only a scorch-shadow in the shape of columns of text — the ghost of a hundred names, burned into cloth like a brand burned into hide. "I can feel it going. Every name on it was an anchor. Every name was a claim the court couldn't break without breaking itself." She laughed, and hated the sound. "I stole a hundred souls' worth of leverage, and I've spent it all in four days. That's probably a record. Even for me."

Isandro's jaw tightened. She watched him do the arithmetic she'd been doing since the first coldness opened — watched him arrive at the sum and flinch from it.

"How long?"

"Long enough to be out of the galleries." She pushed off the bone arch and started walking, because standing still was dying politely and she had never once in her life done anything politely. "Not long enough to reach the root-stair. So we'd best find another way up, mapmaker, or you're about to learn what I look like when I'm being dragged back beneath the mountain."

Paloma was already unfolding her own sketch-map against her forearm, pencil moving. But her eyes flicked up once, quick and dark, and Marisol met them and knew: Paloma Reyes had already guessed there was more to this than a burning page.

The hounds announced themselves thirty seconds later.

Not with howls. The shadow-hounds of the court did not howl; they *named*. Their voices were the voices of clerks, dry and precise, reciting the crimes of the hunted in the language of debt. It came echoing down the galleries behind them, multiplied by stone:

*"Marisol Vane. Servant of the Regent Below. Keeper of the third antechamber. Thief of the record. Traitor to the contract. Debt outstanding. Debt outstanding. Debt outstanding—"*

"Charming," Marisol breathed, and ran.

* * *

They lost the hounds twice, and both times it was Paloma who saved them — once by collapsing a gallery of loose vertebrae behind them with a leveraged shove of a support column, once by reading the flow of cold air through the bones the way she read rivers, finding a current that ran where the hounds' flat clerk-voices could not follow. Marisol filed both tricks away with the grim admiration of a professional watching an amateur outperform her. The girl had been underground for less than two weeks and she moved through the mountain's guts like she'd drawn them.

Which, Marisol supposed, in a way she had.

The third time, they didn't lose them.

It happened at a junction where three galleries met, and Paloma stood frozen over her map with a look Marisol recognized too well — the look of a navigator whose chart has lied to her. "This passage should climb," Paloma said. "It climbed on my way down. It has to—"

"It doesn't have to do anything." Marisol's legs were shaking. She told them to stop and they ignored her. "The mountain rearranges for the court. You mapped it while we were still thieves. We're prey now. Prey gets different architecture."

The clerk-voices swelled in the dark behind them, closer, unhurried. *Debt outstanding. Debt outstanding.* They didn't need to hurry. The mountain was on their side of the ledger.

"There." Isandro pointed to a crack in the wall, barely wide enough for a child, breathing warm air. "Root-smell. That goes toward the living wood. If it reaches the stair—"

"It won't reach the stair," Marisol said. "But it might buy time. Go. All of you. I'll hold the junction."

"You'll *what*?" Paloma's head snapped up.

"I'm the one they want. I'm the one they can track. Every minute I stand here being loudly guilty is a minute you spend getting farther away." Marisol drew the thin witch-knife from her boot — the one tool of her old life she'd kept, because sentiment was just another kind of debt and she paid all of hers. "Don't argue with me, mercenary. You of all people know how this works. Someone holds the door."

"No," Isandro said.

"Yes," Marisol said, "and here's the part you're going to hate—"

The hounds came around the corner before she could say it.

There were six of them, which meant the court considered her worth six, which was almost flattering. They poured out of the dark like spilled ink given ambition, and their faces — the court had given them faces, once, and the cruelty of that had never dulled — were smooth and eyeless except for the mouth, and the mouth was full of teeth arranged like type-set letters.

Marisol raised her knife and spoke the only ward she had left, the small one, the selfish one, the one her grandmother had taught her for keeping wolves out of the henhouse. It bought her perhaps ten seconds. She spent them badly and gloriously, screaming at Isandro and Paloma to *run*, and then the first hound hit her like a falling wall and the world became teeth and cold and the taste of her own blood, iron-bright, familiar, almost homey.

What saved her was not courage. What saved her was that the hound wanted her alive — *debt outstanding*, debts must be collected from the debtor, dead men pay nothing — and so its teeth closed on her shoulder instead of her throat, and its weight pinned her, and through the white roar of pain she heard Isandro roaring too, heard steel ring on shadow, felt the hound shudder as his blade found whatever passed for its spine.

Then hands — Isandro's hands, huge and sure — hauling her up, throwing her arm across his shoulders, running. Paloma ahead, knife out now, holding the crack in the wall open, screaming at them both in a voice cracked clean through with fear.

The last thing Marisol saw before the dark swallowed the junction was the lead hound, sitting motionless amid the scattered bones, its lettered mouth opening one final time.

*"Debt outstanding,"* it said, gently, almost kindly. *"We will wait."*

* * *

The crack became a crawl, the crawl became a chimney, the chimney became a hollow like the inside of a cupped hand, walled in living rootwood that bled a faint amber light and smelled, overwhelmingly, of sap and rain. Somewhere above, muffled by a hundred feet of mountain, water was moving. Marisol lay on her back in the amber gloom and concentrated very hard on not dying, which after forty years of practice she was ordinarily quite good at.

Her shoulder was a ruin. The hound's teeth had gone deep, and worse than deep — the wounds were *cold*, rimed at the edges with a frost that had no temperature, only meaning. A branded wound. The court marked its traitors so the whole mountain would know them, so every stone and shadow and borrowed servant would know to bar the door or open it, whichever served the Regent. There was no healing a branded wound above ground. There was certainly none down here.

"So," Paloma said.

Marisol opened her eyes. The cartographer sat cross-legged beside her, tearing strips from her own hem with the methodical calm of someone who had decided panic was inefficient. Isandro crouched a few feet away, cleaning his blade, and he was not looking at Marisol, which from Isandro was louder than shouting.

"So," Marisol agreed.

"You knew the page was burning. Back in the galleries. Before it finished." Paloma tied off the first bandage, snug, competent. "You felt it start and you didn't say anything until it forced your hand."

"I was hoping I was wrong."

"Were you?"

Marisol looked at the root-lit ceiling. Amber light crawled along the grain like slow honey. She thought about lying. She was extremely good at lying; it had kept her alive through seven years in the Regent's service and three years running from it. But the branded wound was spreading its cold up her neck now, and cold, she had discovered, made liars honest, the way heat made them sleepy.

"No," she said. "I wasn't wrong. I knew exactly what spending that page would cost. I spent it anyway." She pushed herself up on her good arm, because she refused to deliver this lying down like an invalid or a penitent. "And since we're apparently doing confessions tonight, here's mine, and then you can decide what to do with me."

Isandro stopped cleaning the blade.

"When I found you on the root-stair," Marisol said, "drowning in magic, half-mad, swearing you'd tear the mountain apart for a woman you'd known a month — I didn't help you out of kindness. Kindness wasn't in the building." The words came out level, which surprised her; she'd expected them to cost more. "I helped you because I needed a way back inside the court. Seven years I served the Regent, and in seven years I learned one thing above all others: the ledger room is never guarded well enough, and the ledger is where the souls are."

Paloma went very still.

"A soul," Marisol continued, "can be taken from the ledger. Not freed — taken. Moved to a new name. I've seen it done. I watched the Regent do it for a favorite, once, as a reward." Her hand drifted, without her permission, to her branded shoulder. "My sister died owing the court. Died in their cells, with her name in that book, and everything she was got fed into the mountain's hunger a spoonful at a time for sixty years. I went down the stair with you, mercenary, because I meant to steal a soul out of that ledger and put it somewhere safe. Hers. I meant to use you, and your oath-breaking, and your girl's prophecy, as the loudest distraction in the history of the court, and while everyone watched you, I was going to commit the theft of the century."

The amber light ticked along the roots. Water moved overhead.

"And the page?" Paloma asked quietly. "The hundred names?"

"That was real." Marisol met her eyes. "That part I won't apologize for. I took the page because a hundred stolen souls make a better weapon than one, and because every name on it was a person the court had no right to. But I'd be lying if I said I didn't also take it because a page from the ledger is the only thing in creation strong enough to anchor a soul-transfer." She smiled, and it felt like a wound reopening. "Everything I did that helped you also helped me. That's not heroism. That's bookkeeping."

Silence. Isandro rose, slowly, and crossed to her, and stood over her with the mountain's weight in his face, and Marisol braced for the judgment — the flat mercenary verdict, duty's cold arithmetic, the sentence she'd been dodging her whole life.

He knelt instead. Set the blade down between them, within her reach, which from a man like Isandro Villalobos was either madness or a message.

"My sister," he said.

Marisol blinked. "You don't have a—"

"Hadn't. For eleven years. Sold, we thought. Taken. My mother signed something she didn't understand when the fever took our village, and the collectors came, and Ana was nine." His voice didn't shake, but it went somewhere below shaking, into the bedrock register where men keep the things that broke them. "I took the oath to the Silent God because the shrine was the only place in the valley that didn't look at me like I should have saved her. And I stayed at that boundary for fifteen years because standing guard felt like paying a debt." He looked at her, and his eyes were wet and furious and, horribly, understanding. "So don't tell me about bookkeeping, Marisol Vane. I invented it."

The cold in her shoulder pulsed. She let out a breath she felt she'd been holding for seven years.

"I didn't find her name," Marisol said. It came out smaller than she intended. "In the ledger. I looked. Three nights I looked, while Paloma played docile and the court feasted. Ana Villalobos. Ana, daughter of Serafina. Any variant. She's not there." Her throat worked. "Either she was fed whole, long ago, or—" She stopped. Couldn't finish it. *Or she was never in the court's book at all,* whispered the thought she'd been strangling for days. *Or the Silent God's shrine kept some records of its own.*

"Then we look elsewhere," Isandro said simply, as if the matter were settled, as if eleven years of grief could be settled by a woman he'd known a week, a liar, a thief, a traitor with a brand eating up her neck.

"Why?" The word tore out of her before she could dress it up. "Why are you like this? I used you. I'd have left you in those cells if the escape hadn't suited me. Say it — say what I am —"

"You're wounded," Isandro said, "and you came back for us at the junction when you could have run, and you're telling the truth now when a lie would be easier, and I've broken every oath I ever swore in the last month, so I'm hardly in a position to cast stones." He picked up the blade and offered it back to her, hilt-first. "Besides. You held the door. Nobody holds the door for people they're only using."

Marisol took the knife. Her hands, she noticed distantly, were trembling.

Above them, through a hundred feet of root and stone, the water-sound changed — deepened, quickened, took on a rhythm that was almost, almost a pulse. And far away, down through the galleries they'd fled, faint as a memory of a memory, the clerk-voices began again, patient as interest accruing:

*"Debt outstanding. Debt outstanding. The mountain remembers. The mountain waits."*

Paloma was already unrolling her map, pencil flying, lips moving — counting passages, hunting the pattern in the mountain's rearrangements. Isandro watched her with that look he wore like a wound he'd chosen.

Marisol flexed her branded shoulder, tested the cold, and made herself a promise in the old grammar of things, the kind that cost blood whether you kept it or not.

One more theft, then. The last one. Not a soul this time.

A god.
