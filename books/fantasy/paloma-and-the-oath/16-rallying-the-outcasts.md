## 16. Rallying the Outcasts

The valley elders met him at the boundary stone with lanterns and folded arms, and Isandro understood, before a single word was spoken, that he had already lost.

There were seven of them, as there had always been seven — one for each of the old families whose names no one remembered anymore except as titles: Keeper of Stones, Keeper of Wells, Keeper of the Ledger of Births. They stood in the gray hour before dawn, when the mist came down off the ridgeline like something poured, and their faces were the faces of people who had rehearsed their refusal on the walk over.

Isandro set the stolen page from the ledger of souls on the flat top of the boundary stone and weighted its corners with four pebbles so the wind could not take it.

"Read it," he said. His voice came out rougher than he intended. He had not slept. None of them had slept, probably, but he had spent his night climbing out of the earth with a wounded witch on his back and a name burning on his skin where Paloma's contract had marked him, and roughness was all he had left.

Elder Serafín, oldest of the seven, keeper of the shrine keys, leaned forward without touching the page. His eyes moved across the columns of names. Isandro watched him read — watched the way his jaw tightened at the third column, the one that listed dates, the one that went back further than any living memory.

The wind moved the mist. Somewhere behind them, a rooster in the village called once and thought better of it.

"These are old debts," Serafín said at last.

"They're children." Isandro's hand closed on the edge of the stone until his knuckles went white. "Tomas Ibarra, taken eleven years ago. The miller's daughter, Luz, taken before she could walk. There are two hundred and six names on this page alone, and Marisol says there are nine more pages like it beneath the mountain. Two thousand souls, Elder. Sold by our ancestors. Collected while we stood patrol and called ourselves protectors."

"We protect the valley," said Elder Constanza, and her voice had the flat certainty of a woman reciting scripture. "That is what the oath is for. That is what *you* swore."

"I broke it." He let them look at him. Let them see the oath-marks, which should have been black scars by now, dead ink on dead skin, and were instead faintly luminous, like embers under ash. "I walked into magic. I carried it back up the root-stair in my own blood. And the valley is still here. The stones still stand. Whatever we were told the oath protected us from —" he tapped the ledger page — "it wasn't this. This is what the oath was *for*. We weren't guarding the border. We were guarding the door of a larder."

The silence that followed had weight. Mist beaded on the lantern glass. Serafín's gaze dropped again to the names, and for one heartbeat Isandro thought he saw something move behind the old man's eyes — grief, maybe, or shame — before it was shuttered away behind seventy years of careful nothing.

"The court beneath the mountain is older than the valley," Serafín said quietly. "Older than the oath. Our grandmothers' grandmothers made their peace with it, as we make ours. You went down carrying a sword, Isandro Villalobos, and you came back up carrying a war. We did not ask you for either."

"You didn't ask me for anything. That's rather the point."

"The council's answer is no." Constanza again, iron under the words. "No pursuit. No mustering. No word of this to the villages. The page will be returned to the mountain with our apology, and the matter will be —"

"*Apology.*" The word cracked out of him like a whip. A bird startled off the boundary stone and beat away into the fog. Isandro made himself breathe. Made himself lower his voice, because shouting at seven old men and women who had spent their whole lives kneeling to a fear they never named would accomplish nothing except making him feel briefly magnificent. "They have Paloma Reyes. She has been down there for three days. Every hour we spend standing here being *reasonable*, they are feeding on her."

"Then it is her debt," said the youngest elder, a man named Bruno whom Isandro had once drunk with at the harvest fair, "and her family's, and it was paid long before she was born. I'm sorry, Isandro. Truly. But the valley cannot fight a god-eater with pitchforks."

"It can't," Isandro agreed. "But it isn't going to have to."

He took the page off the stone, folded it with exaggerated care, and put it inside his coat, over his heart.

"But you should know," he said, "that some of us are going to try anyway. And when we come back — if we come back — I'd think hard about which side of that boundary stone you want to be remembered standing on."

He turned and walked into the mist, and not one of the seven called after him. He hadn't expected them to. That was the thing about oaths, he was learning: the ones imposed from outside hold longest right up until the moment they shatter, and then they hold not at all.

* * *

Marisol was waiting for him at the shrine of the Silent God, sitting on the broken altar steps with her bandaged arm cradled against her chest and an expression that managed to be both sardonic and hopeful, which Isandro was beginning to understand was simply her face.

"Well?" she said. "Did the wise and venerable council of cowards leap to arms?"

"They offered to apologize to the Regent on my behalf."

"Mm. Generous." She pushed herself up. Her wound had been dressed with valley herbs and witch-craft together, an ugly compromise that was holding, mostly. "So it's just us, then. A disgraced oath-keeper and a traitor witch with one working arm. The Regent should surrender by lunchtime."

"It's not just us." Isandro looked past her, into the shrine.

It was the first time he had stood inside it since coming back up from below, and it looked different now. Smaller. The empty alcove where the god's statue should have been — removed, everyone said, centuries ago, or perhaps there had never been one — did not look empty anymore. It looked like a cage with the door painted on. He could feel it now, the way he could feel the faint warmth of the marks on his forearm: a pressure, a presence, patient as stone. *He's still here,* Isandro thought. *He's been here the whole time, listening to us apologize.*

"There are others," he said aloud. "People the valley threw away. People the oath failed. If the elders won't come, we find the ones with nothing left to lose."

Marisol tilted her head. "You're describing a war band."

"I'm describing a family," Isandro said. "The kind you get to choose. It just happens to be armed."

* * *

The first of them was easy, because Isandro had already saved his life.

Teo the traveler — though he had stopped traveling, these days, and lived in a lean-to half a mile beyond the eastern boundary stones because the villages would not have him — was mending a fishing net when they found him, and he took one look at Isandro's face and put the net down.

"You're the one who pulled me off the boundary path," Teo said. "Three years back. The wraith night."

"You remember."

"I remember everything about that night." Teo's hands, Isandro noticed, had a fine tremor that had nothing to do with age; he couldn't have been more than thirty. "I remember what the wraith felt like. Cold that gets into the part of you that decides things. And I remember that you stepped between me and it like it was nothing. Like it was Tuesday." He looked from Isandro to Marisol and back. "You've got the look of men about to do something stupid and necessary. What do you need?"

"Can you use a bow?"

"I can use anything," Teo said. "That's why they exiled me. I can use things I shouldn't be able to. Things I find." He reached into the wreck of his lean-to and came out with a longbow of pale wood that Isandro had never seen and yet somehow recognized, the way Paloma's maps had been familiar to him. "Found this at the bottom of a dry well. It doesn't miss. I don't know why. I've stopped asking."

"Good," said Marisol. "Stop asking is practically the valley motto. Welcome aboard."

* * *

The smiths were harder.

They lived in the slag hills north of the village, three brothers and a sister, exiled a generation ago for forging with mountain-iron — ore that came from the wrong side of the boundary, metal that held a whisper of magic the way a bell holds a ring after it's struck. The elders had called it contamination. The smiths had called it their livelihood, and lost the argument, and kept the forge lit anyway out of spite.

Their name was Ferrán. The eldest, Aurelio, was built like his own anvil, and when Isandro and Marisol crested the hill and he saw them coming, he picked up a hammer the size of a fence post and did not put it down.

"Oath-keeper," he rumbled. "Come to finish what the council started? Confiscate my tongs? Arrest my fire?"

"I'm not an oath-keeper anymore," Isandro said. "I broke it. Formally. Underneath the mountain, in front of witnesses."

Aurelio's hammer lowered an inch. Behind him, in the shadow of the forge mouth, his siblings had drifted closer, and Isandro saw that the youngest — a wiry woman with burn-scarred forearms — had a blade half-drawn.

"Why would you break it?"

"Because it was a lie." Isandro walked forward slowly, hands open, until he stood close enough to feel the forge heat breathing against his face. "Because the Silent God never left us. He was taken. Caged beneath the court, and the oath was the lock on His cage, and every generation of keepers — me included — turned the key a little tighter and called it devotion." He drew out the ledger page and held it up. "And because they've been eating our people for two hundred years, and the council knows, and the council said no."

The forge was very quiet. Coals ticked. Somewhere in the hills a hawk cried.

It was the burned sister who spoke first. "Two hundred years," she said. "My grandmother used to sing a song about the mountain taking the pretty ones. We thought it was a nursery rhyme."

"It's a bill of sale," said Marisol dryly.

Aurelio looked at the page a long time. Then at Isandro's marks, glowing faintly through his sleeve. Then at his own hands, scarred and strong, hands that had been told for thirty years that what they made was unclean.

"What do you need forged?" he asked.

"Everything," Isandro said. "Cold iron that remembers fire. Blades that can cut a shadow-hound. And if you can manage it — something that can break a chain built to hold a god."

Aurelio smiled, and it was not a comforting smile. It was the smile of a man who had been waiting thirty years for permission.

"My grandmother," he said, turning back toward the forge, "left me her pattern-book. The council never knew. Come inside, oath-breaker. Let's see what your god wants made."

* * *

By the third evening, the lean-to beyond the eastern stones had become a camp, and the camp had become something Isandro had no clean word for.

Teo had shot a wild boar at four hundred paces in failing light, and they ate it around a fire that Marisol fed with small green flames that gave light without smoke, because hounds hunted by scent and the court had long ears. The Ferráns worked at a portable forge through the nights, and the ringing of hammer on steel became the camp's heartbeat. Word traveled the way word does in a valley — down well-chains and across garden fences, in the pauses of conversations — and people began to come.

Not many. Not the strong, not the settled, not the ones with something to lose. They came the way water finds low ground.

A widow whose son had been taken forty years ago, who arrived carrying nothing but his christening spoon and asked, in a voice like a snapped wire, whether the ledger might have his name. It did. She stayed. An old shepherd who claimed he could read weather in the behavior of moths and wanted, before he died, to see the inside of the mountain that had frightened him his whole life. A girl of sixteen named Inés who had been born with silver eyes and spent those years being called cursed, and who could, it turned out, see the seams in the world — the thin places, the doors — and who looked at Isandro's marks and said, wonderingly, "You're full of holes. Good holes. Like a window someone's about to climb through."

Paloma would have loved them, Isandro thought, and the thought hurt in the specific way that thoughts of her always hurt now: not dull but precise, like a paper cut on the heart. She would have sat by the fire sketching all of them, naming them, putting each crooked piece on the map. *Here lives the widow with the spoon. Here lives the girl who sees doors.* She had a gift for making scattered things into a territory. Into a home.

He missed her the way you miss a limb — not as an absence but as a constant, maddening presence of the place where it used to be.

On the fourth night, the whole strange company gathered around the fire, twenty-three souls, and Isandro stood up because it was time, and because no one else was going to.

"I'm not good at speeches," he began, and Marisol muttered "understatement of the century," and someone laughed, and that was better, actually, because laughter loosened the knot of dread in the circle. "So I'll say it plain. Beneath the mountain there is a court that eats souls. It has eaten ours for two hundred years, sold to it by our own ancestors, and the elders have decided that's a fair arrangement. The woman I love is down there right now. So is a god who never abandoned us — we abandoned Him, or worse, we guarded His prison and called it faith." He held up his marked arm, and the embers under his skin brightened, and he saw Inés's silver eyes go wide. "I can't promise you victory. I can't promise most of you will live. What I can promise is that no one down there will ever be able to say again that we didn't come for our own. That we'll stop being a valley that apologizes."

Silence. Firelight. The mountains black against the stars.

Then the widow with the spoon rose, stiff and slow, and held the little silver spoon up to the light.

"For Mateo," she said. "Whatever's left of him. We go."

"For Luz," said Teo. "For the miller's daughter."

"For my grandmother's song," said the burned Ferrán sister.

One by one, raggedly, like a fire catching, they said the names. All of them. It took a long time — two hundred and six names on the first page alone, and the widow seemed to know half of them by heart — and Isandro stood in the middle of it and let the names wash over him, and understood that this was the thing the elders had never grasped: that a list of the dead, spoken aloud by the living, was not a debt.

It was an army.

When the last name faded, Marisol limped to the fire and dropped something into the coals: a scrap of parchment that flared green and rose as a thread of witchlight, pointing south, toward the dark shoulder of the mountain.

"That's the seam Inés found," she said. "The back way in. It won't stay open long — the court is pulling itself tight, getting ready for something. I felt it in my bones today, and my bones are rarely wrong." She looked around the circle, at the smiths and the widow and the girl with the silver eyes, and finally at Isandro. "So. War band. Do we march tonight?"

Isandro looked at the thread of light bending toward the mountain, and at the faces around the fire — his family, chosen and armed and afraid, every one of them — and felt the marks on his arm pulse once, twice, like a second heartbeat answering his own.

Somewhere far below, something vast shifted in its sleep. Or woke.

"Yes," Isandro said, and picked up his sword. "Tonight. Before the mountain knows what's coming for it."

But even as he said it, the ground beneath the camp trembled — gently, once, like a great throat clearing — and every moth within a mile rose into the air at the same instant, streaming south toward the peak, and Inés went white as bone-milk and whispered:

"It already knows."
