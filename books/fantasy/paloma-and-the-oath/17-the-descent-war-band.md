## 17. The Descent War-Band

The boundary stone had never looked like a door before.

Paloma had walked past it a hundred times in the weeks since her escape — sketching it, measuring it, mapping every crack and lichen-scar as if understanding its surface could explain what waited beneath. It was a squat granite pillar, older than the valley's fences, older than its roads, wearing moss like a shawl. Ordinary. Deliberately ordinary, she understood now. The elders had spent generations teaching themselves not to see it.

Tonight she saw it for what it was: a threshold, and they were going to cross it with fire in their hands.

The war-band gathered around it in the blue hour before dawn, eleven souls and one god's attention. Isandro stood at the head of them, and Paloma watched him the way she watched weather change — reading the set of his shoulders, the way his transformed oath-marks caught the moonlight like veins of silver ore running up his forearms. He had been a wall of a man when she'd met him; now he was something else. A door that had learned to open.

"Say it again," he said quietly. "All of it."

She unrolled her map across the flat top of the boundary stone, weighting the corners with four river stones Marisol produced from somewhere inside her coat. The parchment glowed faintly where her own blood had been mixed into the ink — Marisol's idea, and Paloma had hated it and done it anyway, because the court could smell lies but not truth written in sacrifice.

"The root-stair descends ninety-one steps to the first gallery," she said. "We bypass it entirely." Her finger traced a line west of the stair, through blank parchment that should have been solid rock. "There's a fissure here. Water-cut, old, maybe older than the court itself. It lets out into the bone galleries two levels above the throne room — close enough to hear the feasting, far enough that the hounds won't catch our scent on the air current."

"And you know this how?" asked Tomás Herrera, one of the exiled smiths, arms crossed over a breastplate he'd hammered himself from plow-iron. He wasn't challenging her. He was making her say it, so the others would believe it when it mattered.

"Because I mapped our escape through those galleries with hounds at my heels," Paloma said. "I counted every turn because counting kept me from screaming. And because—" she tapped the fissure line again, "—the court doesn't guard what it has forgotten. This passage predates the Regent. It isn't in *his* maps. I checked, while I was playing the honored guest. I checked everything."

Marisol smiled thinly from the shadows of her hood. "She checked everything," the witch echoed. "I taught her that much, at least. Before I got us both nearly killed."

"You got yourself nearly killed," Paloma said. "I just failed to stop you."

"That's called leadership, cariña. You'll get used to it."

Isandro cleared his throat, and the banter died the way a candle dies — gently, completely. He looked down the line of them. The traveler Paloma had only heard stories about, Wren, who had once been saved from a boundary wraith by Isandro's blade and who now carried a spear blessed in the old way. The three Herrera siblings, smiths cast out of the valley proper for forging iron with the dead traditions still folded into it — quenched in rainwater collected from grave-roofs, struck nine times while naming ancestors. Two of the boundary-witches who had sheltered Marisol during her years of running. Old Ferro, who was seventy if he was a day and who insisted on coming because, as he put it, "the mountain ate my brother in 'forty-three and I've been meaning to have words."

And weapons. Paloma looked at the weapons and felt something tighten in her chest that was not fear.

They were strange things, these arms blessed by the valley's dead traditions. Not swords of state, not ceremonial blades. Farming implements reforged. Scythe-blades ground to a killing edge and inscribed with names of the dead — because the dead of the valley remembered what the living had been made to forget, and iron that carried a name carried that name's anger too. Marisol had led the blessings herself, standing in the shrine of the Silent God with her hands bleeding into the soil, calling out lineage after lineage until the empty shrine had felt, for one impossible moment, less empty.

"The hounds are shadow given teeth," Marisol had told them afterward, wrapping her burned palms. "Shadow fears nothing living. But it remembers being alive. Name the dead at them, and they flinch. They can't help it. Every hound in that court is made from someone who was stolen."

Paloma had not slept well after that. None of them had. But they had come anyway.

* * *

The fissure swallowed them single file, and the world changed.

Paloma went third, behind Isandro and Wren, with the map rolled tight against her spine in oilcloth. Behind her came the Herreras, then Marisol, then the witches, then old Ferro, whose breathing was steady as a metronome and who muttered ancestor-names under his breath like a rosary. Eleven people carrying light they couldn't afford to show — cold-lanterns shuttered to slits, enough glow to find handholds and no more.

The rock wept around them. Water threaded down the walls in dark braids, and the air tasted of minerals and deep time. Paloma had mapped this passage from memory and instinct, from the way water sounds travel and the way her feet had remembered turns her mind hadn't catalogued — but memory is a cartographer who lies. Twice she stopped them at junctions that shouldn't exist, and twice she stood with her palm against wet stone, eyes closed, listening for the deep bass hum of the court's furnaces, feeling for the draft that meant open space ahead.

First junction: draft from the left, cold and moving. Correct. She signaled left.

Second junction: nothing. Dead air both ways. She stood there long enough that Isandro's hand found her shoulder in the dark — not guiding, just present — and she breathed, and listened harder, and heard it: a thread of moving air so faint it might have been imagination, from a crack barely wide enough for Wren's shoulders. They went through sideways, one by one, Ferro last, laughing silently at the indignity of it, tears cutting tracks through the grime on his face that he didn't bother wiping away.

Then the passage opened, and they were in the bone galleries.

Paloma had described them to the others on the surface, and had watched their faces go pale, and had known her description had failed. Nothing prepared a person for walls built of femurs and ribs laid in courses like masonry, mortared with something pale and glassy. Nothing prepared them for the way sound behaved here — flattened, intimate, every footfall arriving at the ear already softened, as if the gallery itself were listening politely.

Wren gagged. One of the witches, Sefora, pressed her lips to her spear-shaft and whispered a name into the wood, over and over, like a prayer.

"We move along the upper tier," Paloma breathed. "The outer guard rotates through the lower gallery every quarter hour. We let the first rotation pass. We take the second."

"Take," repeated Tomás, tasting the word.

"Ambush," Paloma said. "Six hounds, walking in file. They'll be between rotations — relaxed, if hounds relax. Marisol says they're blind in the galleries unless they hear heartbeats. So we control the heartbeat." She looked at Marisol.

The witch stepped forward and began unpacking things from her coat: candles of black tallow, a length of red thread, a hollow bone flute that made Paloma's skin crawl just to look at. "A lure," Marisol said. "A false heartbeat, big and frightened, planted at the far end of the lower gallery. The hounds will go to it in file, because fear draws them like flies to a wound. And while they're gathered around a lie—" her smile was all teeth, "—you drop the gallery's bone-chute."

"Chute?" said Ferro.

Paloma pointed up. In the ceiling of the lower gallery, half-hidden among the femur-courses, was a circular plug of fitted bone — a trapdoor, ancient, its mechanism a counterweight of skulls on a chain gone green with age. She'd found it during her escape, lying flat in a side alcove with hounds' voices echoing below, and she'd memorized its position the way she memorized everything: because a map was a weapon you carried in your skull, and no one could take it from you.

"When it opens," she said, "everything below it falls into the sump-channel. It's flooded, waist-deep, slow water. Hounds hate running water — it scatters the shadow, makes them stupid. They'll be trapped in the channel, half-dissolved, and we kill them from above with named iron before they re-form."

Silence. Then Tomás said, "You've thought about this a great deal."

"I had a great deal of time," Paloma said, "and a great deal of rage, and nothing to do with either but count."

Isandro touched her elbow, light as a moth landing. When she looked at him, he didn't say *are you sure* or *we can turn back*. He said, "Show me the counterweight release. I'll want my hands on it when it matters."

That was why she loved him, she thought, absurdly, in a bone gallery above a court of stolen souls. Because he never asked if she was strong enough. He asked what she needed him to hold.

* * *

They waited two hours in the dark, and the waiting was its own country.

Paloma crouched at the alcove beside the chute mechanism, Isandro a arm's length away with his hands on the counterweight chain, and through the slit between femur-courses she watched the lower gallery the way she'd once watched the valley archive's door for signs of the archivist. Below, the bone floor stretched pale and vast. At its far end, Marisol — invisible, warded, wrapped in silence-magic that cost her visibly, sweat shining on her brow even in the shuttered lantern-light — set the black candles in a triangle and lit them with a word.

The false heartbeat started as a feeling more than a sound. A slow, panicked *thud-thud... thud-thud...* that seemed to come from everywhere and nowhere, the rhythm of a thing small and terrified and alone. Paloma's own heart tried to sync to it, and she clenched her jaw until it hurt to break the sympathy.

"They'll feel it from three galleries away," Marisol had promised. "It's cruel magic. I'm good at cruel magic. It's the kind magic I need practice with."

The first rotation passed below them at what Palomo estimated was the forty-minute mark — six hounds in file, moving with that horrible liquid smoothness, shapes like dogs assembled from smoke and malice, their eyeless heads swiveling as they patrolled. She held her breath without needing to; the silence-wards did their work. The hounds passed beneath the chute without pausing, and Paloma exhaled slowly, and old Ferro, crouched beside her, patted her knee once with a hand like tree bark.

The second rotation came forty minutes later.

They came faster than the first — drawn, already, by the distant throb of the false heart. Paloma watched them quicken as they entered the gallery, watched the file loosen into a lope, watched all six converge on the triangle of black candles with the eagerness of starving things. For one moment they clustered there, snouts down, circling the lie—

"Now," Isandro breathed, and threw his whole weight onto the chain.

The counterweight dropped. The skull-mechanism screamed — there was no other word for it, a shriek of bone on bone that the gallery's strange acoustics made enormous — and the plug swung open, and six hounds fell into the dark.

The sound they made falling was bad. The sound they made landing was worse. Then the water took them, and the shrieking became a hissing, boiling fury, shadows thrashing in the sump-channel, scattering, fraying at the edges like cloth coming apart.

"Names!" Marisol's voice cracked across the gallery like a whip. "Give them their names!"

And the war-band leaned over the edge and gave the dead back to themselves.

"Ferro Ondiz, drowned in the mine year forty-three! Your brother remembers you!"

"Sela Herrera, taken in the winter of the red fever! Your loom still stands!"

"Wren of the north road, lost and never mourned! You are mourned now!"

Each name fell into the churning water like a hot coal into snow. The hounds convulsed. Where the names struck, the shadow tore open and showed glimpses of what was underneath — faces, briefly, mouths open in expressions that were not quite agony and not quite relief. Paloma shouted names too, names she'd read in the ledger page she'd stolen, names she'd memorized on the long climb back to daylight precisely for this moment, and her voice broke on the fourth one and she kept shouting anyway.

Isandro went down the chute first, oath-marks blazing silver, into the waist-deep water with a scythe-blade inscribed with his grandfather's name. What happened in the next ninety seconds, Paloma watched from above with her heart in her throat and her map-keeper's mind taking notes even through the terror — angles, timings, which strikes landed, how the hounds recoiled from named iron the way flesh recoils from fire. He fought like a man who had spent his whole life practicing restraint and had finally been given permission to stop. The marks on his arms threw light across the water. Six times the blade fell. Six shadows came apart and did not re-form.

When it was done, he stood in the channel breathing hard, surrounded by drifting motes of darkness that were dissolving into ordinary, harmless gloom. He looked up at her through the open chute, water streaming off him, and there was blood on his cheek that she was almost certain wasn't his, and he grinned — actually grinned, wild and young, a boy's grin on a scarred man's face.

"One rotation short," he called up. "Next patrol comes in thirty minutes. We've bought ourselves a staircase."

* * *

They climbed down into the aftermath because leaving bodies — even dissolved ones — was waste, and Marisol was ruthless about waste. From the sump-water she gathered what remained: six small stones of hardened shadow, dense as obsidian, cold as grief.

"Hound-hearts," she said, pocketing them. "Reagents. Also proof. Also—" she hesitated, and for once the witch looked uncertain, "—when the god wakes, these might be people again. Or what's left of them. Someone should carry them gently."

Paloma volunteered. She didn't examine why. Some weights you choose because choosing them is the only comfort available.

They moved fast now, momentum doing what courage alone could not. That was the thing nobody told you about bravery, Paloma thought as she led them down the service stair toward the court's outer ring — that it compounded. The ambush had worked. The plan had survived contact with reality. Eleven farmers and smiths and witches and a cartographer had killed six nightmares of the mountain court and lost nothing but Ferro's hat, which had floated away in the sump and been declared, by Ferro himself, a worthy sacrifice. They walked differently than they had on the surface. Shoulders back. Weapons loose and ready instead of clutched. Even Sefora had stopped whispering names to her spear and started whispering them *at* the dark ahead, preemptively, like a woman throwing punches before the fight.

The outer ring of the court announced itself by warmth. The air grew thick, gilded, rotten-sweet — incense over decay, the smell Paloma had woken to in her gilded cage. Torchlight bled orange around corners. Somewhere below, faintly, music: the court feasting, always feasting, on shadows and stolen years.

Paloma pressed her back to the wall at the final junction and unrolled the map one last time, right there against the stone, trusting her people to watch the dark behind her.

"Here's the shape of it," she whispered. "The feast won't end for hours — the Regent drags them out deliberately, makes them wait hungry. The outer guard is thinner than it's ever been because we just thinned it. This corridor runs to the servants' gallery, and the servants' gallery runs directly beneath the throne dais." She looked up and found Isandro's eyes. "Directly beneath the place where the Regent keeps the ledger."

"The ledger," Marisol said softly. "Your page's mother-page."

"The source. If we burn the ledger, every contract in it burns. Every soul comes home." Paloma rolled the map closed. Her hands were steady. She noticed that they were steady, and marveled at it, and decided to trust them. "One problem. The throne dais sits on the cage. The Silent God's prison is the foundation the whole court is built on. Which means to reach the ledger, we have to walk across the roof of a god."

Old Ferro chuckled, low and dry, in the dark.

"Well," he said, cracking his knuckles one by one, "my brother always did say I'd end up walking on holy ground. Took him forty years to be right."

Above them, far off, the music stopped.

Every head came up. Paloma's stomach dropped through the floor — because the feast did not stop early, the feast *never* stopped early, the Regent's hungers ran on schedules older than kingdoms —

And then a voice filled the corridors. Not loud. It didn't need to be loud. It arrived inside the ear, inside the chest, inside the marrow, the way an earthquake arrives inside the bones of a house before anything visible moves.

*"Little thieves,"* it said, warm as honey poured over a wound. *"You've taken my hounds. Come, then. Take the rest. I've kept your seats at table."*

The torches along the corridor guttered out, one by one, from both ends at once — snuffing toward them like a closing fist.

In the sudden dark, Paloma reached out and found Isandro's hand, and gripped it, and drew her knife with the other.

"He knows," she whispered.

"No," Isandro said, and the silver of his oath-marks was the only light left in the world, and it was enough. "He suspects. There's a difference — and it's the only one we need. Move."
