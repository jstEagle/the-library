## 19. The Regent Unmasked

The throne room had stopped pretending to be beautiful.

Paloma saw it now with the eyes of a cartographer who had spent three weeks learning this place's true geography — the way the gilt flaked from the pillars like scabs, the way the "marble" floors were bone pressed flat by centuries of weight, the way the light that poured from the chandeliers was not light at all but the slow leak of stolen souls, golden and grieving. The court beneath the mountain had dressed itself in splendor the way a corpse is dressed for a funeral: carefully, lovingly, by hands that knew exactly what lay underneath.

She walked toward the Regent Below with her stolen ledger page folded against her heart, hidden in the lining of her gown, and she made her face into a mask of surrender.

It was not hard. She had been practicing surrender her whole life — surrendering to her mother's silences, to the valley's smallness, to the sense that she had been born waiting for something she could not name. Now she understood why. The something had a name, and it sat on a throne at the end of a hall of bones, wearing her great-great-grandmother's debt like a crown.

"You came willingly." The Regent's voice filled the cathedral of rot the way water fills a drowned bell. It did not come from His mouth so much as from the walls themselves, from the contracts carved into every stone. "I confess, I wondered. The prophecies are rarely wrong about such things, but souls are stubborn cargo. They resist their own delivery."

"I read the book," Paloma said. Her voice held. She was almost proud of it. "The codex in the archive. I read my own name."

"And still you walk to me. Good. Willingness sweetens everything." The Regent rose.

Paloma had expected a monster. Every story the valley told about the mountain court had prepared her for a monster — horned, or eyeless, or made of smoke and teeth. What stood before her instead was worse: a tall figure in robes of woven shadow, whose face was *almost* human, worn smooth as a river stone, as if a thousand years of smiling had eroded everything else away. Only the eyes remained sharp, and they were not eyes at all but two small doors, and behind them Paloma could see — faintly, horribly — the flicker of other lives burning as fuel.

"Before we proceed," the Regent said, spreading hands that cast no shadow, "there are forms. There are always forms. Your ancestor signed; I honor signatures. Come, stand upon the seal."

The floor before the throne bore a circle of script older than any alphabet Paloma knew, glowing faintly, hungry. She stepped onto its edge and felt it taste her — a cold, probing attention, like a finger drawn along her spine.

"The contract," the Regent continued, circling her slowly, savoring, "was struck one hundred and seven years ago by one Reyes, a woman of your blood, in exchange for passage through my mountain during the famine winter. She could not pay what was asked. So she signed instead for what would come after — a soul unborn, a descendant unnamed. A debt deferred across generations." The Regent smiled, and the smile went all the way to the back of the hall. "You were born owing, Paloma Reyes. Everything you have ever loved, you have loved on borrowed time. Does that wound you? It should not. Most souls in my ledger arrived here the same way. Very few of them ever learn they were sold. You are among the privileged."

*The children.* Paloma kept her breathing even. In the days since she'd stolen the page she had memorized names — Tomás Herrera, aged nine, taken last spring. The Vidal twins, marked for collection next midwinter. Whole columns of them, sold by grandparents, by desperate parents, by oath-keepers who thought they were only signing away bad luck. "And what happens to me now?" she asked. "You've collected me. Is that the end of it?"

"That depends entirely on you." The Regent drifted closer, and where He passed, the guttering soul-light bent toward Him like plants toward sun. "A collected soul is fuel, yes. But a *willing* soul — ah. That is a different vintage altogether. Which brings me to my offer."

Here it was. Marisol had warned her: *He will offer you a door out. It will be real. That's what makes it a trap.*

"I know you have allies in my galleries," the Regent said mildly. "Do not insult us both by pretending otherwise. The mercenary who broke his oath to follow you. The little witch who ran from me twice. I let them come, Paloma. Do you understand? A court must eat, and fear seasons the meat, but *hope* — hope is the rarest dish. I let them crawl down here carrying hope like a lantern, because hope makes the final surrender so much sweeter when it breaks."

Paloma's nails bit her palms inside her sleeves. Somewhere above, in the bone galleries, Isandro was fighting his way toward her with half the court's guard between them, and the mountain had begun, very faintly, to tremble. She could feel it through the soles of her feet. Something vast was stirring under the Regent's pretty rotting floor, and the Regent felt it too — Paloma saw the flicker of it behind those door-eyes, the first crack in the eroded calm.

"So," the Regent said. "My offer. Renounce him. Renounce the mercenary, the witch, the valley, the whole ragged constellation of people who love you. Kneel upon the seal of your own free will, accept your inheritance, and take your place at my right hand as mistress of my ledgers. You read scripts even my scribes find archaic. You see patterns where others see noise. You were *made* for this court, Paloma — your ancestor ensured it. In return: the valley prospers. No more collections. The names in my ledger belonging to your neighbors will be struck clean. The famine never touches you again. One soul, freely given, buys four hundred forever spared." He tilted the smooth face. "Or refuse, and I take everything anyway — slowly, and starting with the ones who came to save you. Choose being chosen, Paloma. It is a far gentler thing than being owed."

*There it is.* The seduction of surrender, laid out on a silver tray. And the terrible thing — the thing Paloma would carry for the rest of her life, however long that proved to be — was that for three full heartbeats, she wanted it.

Not the power. Not the gilded cage. But *being chosen*. All her life she had felt like a placeholder, a name in someone else's margin, a debt passed hand to hand without anyone asking if she minded. And here, at last, was a voice offering to make her central. Essential. Chosen.

Three heartbeats. Then she looked up past the Regent's shoulder, to the far wall of the throne room — where, during the feast of shadows, she had seen the guards drag Isandro in chains, where she had touched him once and watched the contract-light leap between them like a spark off flint, marking him, claiming him, because the contract did not merely own her; it owned anything that dared to love her.

That was the moment the wanting died. Because the Regent had made one error, the same error every empire makes: He believed that what people want most is safety.

What they want most is each other.

"I have a counteroffer," Paloma said.

The Regent stilled. Even the soul-light seemed to lean in.

"You want a willing soul," she said. "Freely given. Not collected, not dragged, not inherited — *given*. Because a willing soul burns brighter and binds deeper, and because the original contract has a flaw you've papered over for a century." She took a breath. This was the part Marisol had helped her find, the clause buried in the ledger's frontispiece like a thorn in a rose stem: *That which is given may be returned; that which is returned cannot be reclaimed.*

"You're bluffing," the Regent said softly.

"I'm a mapmaker," Paloma answered. "Bluffing is drawing roads that don't exist. I don't do that. I show people what's actually there." She lifted her chin. "You offered me freedom in exchange for renouncing Isandro. I offer you something better. My soul — not the one in your ledger. That one isn't mine to give. My ancestor sold something she never owned. A debt contracted by fraud voids at revelation; even your own law says so; I found it in your statutes, carved on the forty-first pillar, and your scribes have been walking past it for a hundred years hoping no one would read it."

For the first time, the doors behind the Regent's eyes flickered — genuinely flickered — and somewhere deep beneath the floor, the trembling grew.

"But this soul," Paloma went on, pressing her hand flat against her chest, over the stolen page, over the beating muscle beneath, "*this* one is mine. Earned. Lived-in. Loved-in. And I give it back — freely, willingly, upon your seal — not to you, but to its rightful owner."

The Regent's voice dropped an octave, and the walls groaned with it. "To *whom*?"

"To the one you caged," Paloma said. "To the Silent God. To the prisoner under your pretty floor, whose silence you stole and called divine, whose jailers you disguised as oath-keepers, whose worshipers you fed on for a thousand years while He slept beneath your throne. You were the first oath-taker, weren't you? The first to kneel at His shrine and swear protection — and then sell Him. That's how a mortal became the Regent Below. That's how you got the court. You didn't conquer it. You *inherited* it, the same way I inherited my debt. From a signature."

The hall erupted.

Not with sound — with *absence* of sound, a pressure-drop, every candle of soul-light guttering black for an instant. When the light returned, the Regent's face had changed. The erosion was gone. Underneath the river-smoothed calm was something raw and old and furious, a face like cracked slate, and Paloma understood that she had just done the one thing no one in a thousand years had dared:

She had told Him the truth about Himself, to His face.

"Clever girl," the Regent said, and each word fell like a stone into deep water. "Clever, clever dead girl. Do you imagine a loophole matters? I *wrote* the loopholes. I wrote the law, the ledgers, the language itself down here. You think your gift-back will reach Him? He cannot receive. He cannot *hear*. I took His hearing with His voice. I took—"

"You took His name," Paloma said quietly. "But names come back. That's in your statutes too. Pillar sixty-three. You really should have burned the pillars, Your Honor. But you couldn't, could you? Because they're load-bearing. The whole court stands on the law that condemns it."

The Regent moved then — fast, faster than anything that size should move — and the shadow-robes spread like wings, like a net, like the closing of a book. Paloma did not run. Running was for people without leverage. Instead she pulled the stolen ledger page from her gown, held it high, and spoke the words Marisol had drilled into her until she dreamed them: the words of return, of restitution, of *that which is given may be returned* —

—and drove her own open palm down onto the glowing seal.

The contract woke.

Light exploded upward through the floor in veins, gold and white and furious, and Paloma felt her soul answer the call — rise up through her like water finding its level, like a bird that has spent a lifetime forgetting it could fly suddenly remembering all at once. It hurt. It hurt like birth, like grief, like every homecoming compressed into a single tearing instant. For one endless second she stood at the center of a hundred years of stolen names, and she felt all of them — the children, the elders, the sold and the surrendered — feel *her*, and turn toward her, and begin, all together, to pull.

"No!" The Regent's roar cracked pillars. "The contract is MINE—"

"Was," Paloma gasped, on her knees now, blazing, tears streaming down a face that would not stop smiling. "A contract built on fraud is void at revelation. *Your* law. *Your* pillars. I didn't break your rules, Regent. I finally enforced them."

The seal beneath her turned from gold to white.

And beneath the throne — beneath the bone, beneath the rot, beneath a thousand years of careful lies — something that had been silent since before the valley had a name drew its first breath.

The mountain shook. Dust fell from ceilings in the galleries above; Paloma knew, without knowing how she knew, that Isandro was running, that Marisol was shouting, that the war band had reached the cathedral of silence and found it silent no longer. A sound was rising through the rock, through the roots, through the boundary stones of the valley above — not a voice, not yet, but the *place where a voice would be*, a pressure like the moment before thunder, like the inhale before a name long unspoken.

The Regent staggered backward from the blazing seal, robes shredding into smoke, and for the first time in a thousand years, the Regent Below looked afraid.

"What have you done," He whispered.

Paloma rose. Her body felt translucent, emptied and enormous, a lantern with the flame removed and the glass left glowing. Her soul was gone from her — given back, given *through*, already traveling down through the dark toward the cage where it was needed, where every returned soul was gathering like rain against a dam.

"It was never mine to sell," she said. "So it was never yours to keep. And now, Your Honor — now He knows the way out. I just drew Him the map."

Far below, behind a door forged of oaths and silence, something opened one eye.

* * *

The Regent screamed — not in rage, Paloma realized, but in command, summoning every hound, every guard, every chain in the court — and the throne room doors burst inward off their hinges, and through the smoke and falling stone came a silhouette she would have known anywhere, sword alight with borrowed fire, oath-marks burning black and new across his arms.

Isandro skidded to a halt beside her, took in the blazing seal, the crumbling throne, the shaking mountain — and said, slightly breathless, "I leave you alone for one hour."

"Had some paperwork," Paloma said, and took his hand.

Above them, the ceiling began, gently and irrevocably, to sing.
