## 20. When the Mountain Sings

The mountain began to sing before the god was even free of His cage.

Isandro felt it through the soles of his boots — a vibration like a struck bell that never finished ringing, rising out of the stone and into his teeth, his ribs, the hollow places in his chest where an oath had lived for twenty years. The silence he had known all his life was peeling away, layer by layer, like frost surrendering to sun.

"Move!" Marisol's voice cracked across the cathedral of silence — except it wasn't silent anymore. Sound poured back into the world as the unbinding spread: the drip of water somewhere deep, the groan of stressed stone, his own ragged breathing loud as a war drum. "The whole gallery's coming apart!"

Behind them, the prison of the Silent God — that vast hollow where sound had gone to die — was unfolding. The walls that had been smooth as held breath were splitting along seams no one had ever seen, and through those seams came light. Not torchlight. Not the gilded rot of the Regent's court. Something older and colder and cleaner, the light of a star remembered rather than seen.

And from within it, one eye.

Isandro had spent a lifetime serving a god who never answered. He had knelt at an empty shrine until his knees knew the stone better than his mother's hands. He had told himself, ten thousand times, that faith without reply was still faith — that the Silent God heard even if He did not speak. And now the god looked at him, one immense eye opening in the breaking dark, and Isandro understood that every prayer had been heard. Every single one. They had simply been received by something bound too tightly to answer, gathering like water against a dam.

The eye did not speak. It didn't need to. It *looked* at him, and Isandro felt his broken oath — spoken backward in this place, unmade syllable by unmaking syllable — settle into his bones not as absence but as space. Room. An invitation.

*Carry me,* said the mountain, in a voice that was not sound at all but pressure, meaning pressed directly into marrow. *I have been still for so long.*

"Yes," Isandro whispered, and the word fell upward.

* * *

They ran.

The bone galleries — Paloma had named them well, corridors ribbed with pale strata like the inside of some titanic chest — shuddered around them as the god began to rise. Dust fell in gray sheets. Cracks raced overhead like lightning frozen mid-strike. Behind them, deeper in the mountain, came a sound Isandro would carry to his grave: the Regent Below, screaming in a voice made entirely of contracts.

Paloma had done that. Paloma, with her mapmaker's mind and her stubborn, luminous courage, had stood before the thing that wore a crown of ledgers and offered her own soul back freely — a soul that had never been hers to sell, sold again, and the contract had turned on its author like a blade reflected in a mirror. Isandro had seen only the beginning of it: the golden chains of obligation whipping off Paloma's shoulders and coiling instead around the Regent, page after page of signed and sealed theft unraveling into the very hands that wrote them.

Then the floor had tilted, and there had been no time to watch justice finish its work.

"The stair!" Marisol shouted, pointing ahead to where the root-stair descended — no, *had* descended; now it climbed, because everything was climbing now, the whole geography of the underground lurching toward the surface as if the mountain itself were trying to be born. The stair they'd come down weeks ago — or days, time moved strangely beneath the boundary stone — was fracturing step by step, great slabs of stone and petrified root shearing away into blackness.

Isandro counted heads as they ran. Marisol, limping but upright, her witch-marks flaring silver with borrowed power she was spending like a woman burning furniture to heat a house. Two of the smiths from the war band, Oren and little Tam, hauling each other over the broken ground. The traveler — Aldric, the man Isandro had pulled from a boundary wraith what felt like another lifetime ago — carrying a wounded outcast across his back.

And Paloma, three steps ahead of him, her stolen ledger page clutched in her fist, blazing like a coal.

The stair gave.

It happened all at once. The landing below them folded inward with a boom that swallowed itself, and the entire flight above — their way out, the only way out, two hundred steps of root and stone between them and the sky — groaned, listed, and began to come down.

"No," Paloma breathed.

The collapse started at the top and worked downward. Slabs the size of cottages broke loose and thundered past them into the abyss below, close enough that Isandro felt their wind. In thirty seconds, maybe less, the stair would be gone, and they would be entombed in a mountain that was busy tearing itself open around an ascending god.

Isandro looked at the stair. Then at his people. Then he did the arithmetic he had been doing his whole life, the mercenary's cold accounting of lives against costs, except this time the numbers meant something different, because this time he was not spending strangers.

He could hold it. Maybe. His oath-marks were gone — burned away when he broke his oath, replaced by something rawer, wider, a channel instead of a chain. The Silent God's power moved through him now like blood through a reopened vein, and he was only beginning to learn what it could do. But he had carried weight all his life. Weight was the one language he spoke fluently.

"Everyone under the arch," he said. "Now."

Paloma turned. She knew. Of course she knew — she'd watched him make this exact calculation with his face since the day she'd met him, the day he'd stepped between her and a boundary wraith without hesitation, without drama, the way a man steps between his family and weather.

"Don't," she said.

"I'm going to hold the landing," he said. "The arch will take the load if I can keep the keystone from shearing. Go up. All of you. Don't stop, don't look back—"

"*Don't you dare tell me not to look back.*"

The mountain screamed. A crack split the wall beside them, wide as a door, and through it Isandro glimpsed the god rising — a shoulder of darkness moving through living rock, vast beyond sense, wrapped in the light of old stars. The pressure of Him passed through the corridor and everyone staggered except Isandro, who leaned into it like leaning into wind.

He pressed his palms flat against the keystone of the arch.

The power answered. It came up through the stone and into him and he let it, opened everything, spent himself like a man pouring out a jug. The stair above them shuddered — and held. The falling slabs slowed, caught, hung. He was holding two hundred tons of collapsing mountain with his bare hands and his whole heart, and it was the easiest thing he had ever done, because every year of standing watch, every night of patrol, every hollow prayer at the empty shrine had been rehearsal for exactly this: *hold the line. Hold it. Hold.*

"GO," he roared.

They went. Marisol first, dragging Tam, shouting at the others to climb. Aldric with his burden. The smiths. Their footsteps hammered up the suspended stair, and above them the mountain shook and sang — the song growing louder now, a chord of stone and starlight, the Silent God ascending through His own tomb toward the court above, toward the gilded rot, toward the end of a captivity older than the valley.

Paloma did not go.

* * *

"Hold," Isandro gritted, and held.

Sweat ran into his eyes. Blood, too — his nose had begun bleeding somewhere in the last minute, and he could feel smaller vessels going, warm threads down his throat, his ears. The power was not infinite. It was a river and he was a channel, and channels erode.

"You're killing yourself." Paloma stood at the base of the stair, looking up at him, her face streaked with dust and tears that cut clean lines through the grime. "Isandro. Look at me. You're *dying*."

"Then climb faster."

"Not without you."

"Paloma—" The word tore out of him. The stair bucked; a slab the size of a wagon tore loose forty feet up and he caught it, snarling, muscles that weren't muscles locking around its fall, and hurled it aside into the dark. "You have to go. You're the prophecy. You're the one who ends it. Everything we did — the ledger, the loophole, all of it — it means nothing if you die in a hole in the ground."

"And it means nothing if YOU do!" Her voice cracked across the singing dark. "You think I don't know what you're doing? You've been doing it since I met you! Standing between everyone and the weather! Guarding the valley, guarding the shrine, guarding me — holding everything, always, and never once asking anyone to hold YOU!"

The stair groaned. He felt a fissure crawl through the keystone under his palms, hairline, then less than hairline. Below them the abyss exhaled cold air that smelled of deep time.

"Someone has to hold," he said.

"YES! And someone has to be held! That's the whole point, Isandro — that's what I learned down here, in that beautiful rotten court — they built a whole kingdom on people being held *against their will*, souls as property, oaths as chains! And you — your oath, your marks, your whole life — you thought devotion meant disappearing!" She was crying openly now, furious, magnificent. "I am not leaving you. Write it in your ledger. Carve it in your stone. I am NOT leaving you, so you can either hold this stair AND live, or you can watch me stand right here while the mountain comes down on both of us."

Something in him broke — or mended; at this altitude of feeling the two were indistinguishable.

"Marisol!" he bellowed up the stair.

Her face appeared over the edge of the landing above, silver-lit, furious. "MOVE FASTER, BOTH OF YOU!"

"Come up here! Both of us — I can't hold it alone anymore!"

She understood instantly, because she was Marisol, transactional to her marrow and loyal beneath it like ore under stone. "Link hands! Give her your strength and give him yours — a circuit! Three points, a triangle, DO IT NOW!"

Paloma scrambled up onto the lowest intact step and seized his left wrist. Above, Marisol leaned down and gripped his right hand, her witch-marks flaring white-hot. And Isandro, half-blind with his own blood, stopped trying to be a pillar and became instead a bridge — let the power flow through him in a circle, out through one hand and in through the other, his strength feeding Marisol's working and Marisol's craft steadying his grip and Paloma—

Paloma fed him something no spell had a name for. Not magic. Just her. Her stubbornness, her terror, her love, pouring through his wrist like sunlight through a window, and the fissure in the keystone stopped spreading, and the stair held, and held, and held.

Above them, the mountain sang.

* * *

Later — much later, though how much later Isandro would never be able to say — the last of them crested the final step and the sky took them back.

They emerged through a wound in the mountainside into night air and starlight, tumbling onto a slope of scree above the valley's high meadows. Isandro collapsed face-first and lay there, shaking, emptied, alive. His hands were ruined — skin split, nails blackened — and when he tried to make a fist he found he could not, and he laughed, a cracked and disbelieving sound, because he was alive to fail at making a fist.

Below and behind them, the peak of the mountain was opening.

Not exploding — opening, the way a fist opens, the way a hand that has been clenched for a thousand years finally remembers it can let go. Light poured from the summit in slow ropes. And rising out of it, unfolding joint by impossible joint, came the Silent God.

He was not what any of them expected. He was not fire or terror or a figure out of the old carvings. He was *vastness* — a shape like the shadow of the valley itself given form, wearing the stars like a mantle, and where He passed, the dead air of a century came alive. Isandro felt magic wash down the slopes ahead of the god like the first warm rain after drought, sinking into the boundary stones, the meadows, the shrine, the graves. Somewhere below, in the valley, he knew — knew it in his marrow — that the empty shrine was not empty anymore.

And the Regent.

The Regent came apart as the god rose. From the shattered mouth of the court far below, Isandro heard it — the last sounds of the thing that had worn a crown of ledgers: not a scream but a recitation, thousands of contracts reading themselves aloud in a chorus of stolen voices, every clause turning on its author, every debt called due at once. The gilded voice that had promised prosperity in exchange for surrender rose to a crescendo of its own signatures — and then, gently, almost politely, it was unmade. Unwritten. The way a word is unwritten when the page burns: still shaped for a moment in the smoke, and then not.

The silence afterward was the good kind. The kind that follows a held breath released.

Isandro rolled onto his back. Stars. Real ones, not the memory of stars trapped in god-light. His whole body hurt in ways he would be cataloguing for months. Beside him, Paloma lay in the scree, dust-gray and tear-tracked and laughing softly at the sky.

"You didn't leave," he said.

"Never going to." She turned her head to look at him. In the god-light washing down the slope her eyes were bright and steady. "You held the stair."

"We held the stair."

"We held the stair," she agreed, and reached over and laced her ruined fingers through his ruined fingers, and neither of them let go.

Down in the valley, lights were appearing — lanterns, dozens of them, moving up the meadow paths. The elders, the families, the whole waking valley coming to see what had risen from their mountain. And among the lights, Isandro could just make out figures running ahead of the rest, small and fast: children, drawn uphill by something they couldn't name, the way birds rise before an earthquake.

Marisol sat a little apart, arms around her knees, watching the god with an expression Isandro had never seen on her face — wonder, naked and unpriced, the first thing she had ever seemed to want that wasn't a bargain.

"He's going to speak," she whispered. "After all this. After everything. He's actually going to—"

The god turned His gaze upon the slope, upon the small battered band of outcasts and mercenaries and mapmakers lying in the scree. The mountain held its breath. The stars leaned closer.

And the Silent God opened His mouth, and spoke His first words in a thousand years —

and what He said made Isandro sit bolt upright, heart slamming, because it was not a blessing, and it was not a judgment.

It was a question. And it was asked in *his* name.
