## 1. The Ledger of Impossible Things

The candle on Ulyana's desk had burned down to the width of a thumbnail, and she did not replace it. The Salt Guild kept its accounts by candlelight out of tradition, not necessity — tradition and the fact that certain goods in the vault below behaved badly under lamplight. Borrowed years, in particular, were said to dim beside any flame that had not itself been traded. Ulyana had never tested the claim. She had learned, in the eleven years since she came to the Guild with a dead order behind her and an oath still warm in her mouth, that the safest way to handle impossible things was to assume every warning was true.

She turned the page of the master ledger with two fingers, the way one touches a sleeping animal.

*Entry the four hundred and eleventh, month of Ashfall.* A cooper in the Lowtown ward had traded six months of his daughter's laughter to settle a debt to the harbor tax-farmers, and had come back three nights running to ask the price of buying it home. Three nights running, Ulyana had quoted him the rate and watched him leave without speaking. Tonight, the entry remained open. The ink of his name had dried pale, the way ink did when the thing it named was thinning.

She wrote nothing in the margin. The Guild's rules were simple, and she had copied them onto the first page of her own hand so many times they had worn grooves in her memory:

*All things may be traded. Nothing may be returned for free. Nothing may be returned at all, save at price. The ledger does not judge. The ledger does not forget.*

There had been a fourth rule once, in another book, in another life. *A swordspeaker does not sell the covenant.* But that book had burned with the rest of her order, and the rule had burned with it, and only she remained to remember the shape of its ashes.

The bell above the counting-room door rang at half past the ninth hour, which was late even for a Guild that kept no honest hours, and Ulyana rose to admit the customer because that was her work. Not guildmistress. Not clerk. Keeper of the ledger, and keeper, too, of the small back room where the traded things waited in their salt and their glass, humming to themselves like wasps in winter.

The widow's name was Maren Ostwick, and Ulyana knew it before the woman spoke, because the appointment had been made a month ago and entered in the ledger in her own careful hand. *M. Ostwick, of Candlemaker's Row, to redeem one (1) hour of voice, belonging to T. Ostwick, deceased, held in bond since the month of Ember.* The Guild did not deal in the dead — not in bodies, not in souls, whatever the street preachers said — but a voice was not a soul. A voice was a thing a man could set down on a table in his last winter and trade for the medicine that failed to save him anyway, and the Guild would hold it, salted and stoppered, until someone came to claim it or until the holding fee lapsed and it passed into the general stock, to be bought by strangers who wanted a voice that was not their own.

"Keeper Fairweather." Maren Ostwick stood just inside the door, dripping rain, her hands working at each other like two birds trying to escape one cage. She was perhaps fifty, with the raw knuckles of a woman who had washed other people's linen for thirty years. "I've brought it. All of it. I counted twice."

She set a leather purse on the counter, and Ulyana did not open it. Weighing a customer's coin in front of them was a discourtesy the old guildmaster had practiced and the new one had made law, and Ulyana broke small laws where she could, quietly, the way a man might steal bread crumbs from his own table.

"Then we'll do it properly," she said. "Sign here. And read the terms first, mistress — I'm required to say that, and I mean it besides."

The terms were the terms. They always were. *The Guild guarantees the integrity of the goods. The Guild does not guarantee the effect. One hour of voice, redeemed, will sound as it sounded. It will say what it said. The buyer is advised that the dead do not know they are being listened to.* That last clause had been added forty years ago, after a man in the Uplands bought back his drowned son's last words and heard, in the boy's voice, an argument with a brother he had never known existed. The Guild had refunded nothing. The Guild never refunded anything. But the clause was there, and Ulyana read it aloud in her flat, clear voice, and watched Maren Ostwick's face do the thing that faces did — the flinch, the steadying, the terrible arithmetic of a grief that had finally found its price.

"I know what he said," the widow said. "He told me before he traded it. He said, *you'll want to hear it, and you'll wish you hadn't, and you'll want it anyway.*" Her mouth twisted. "He was right about most things. It was his worst habit and his best quality, being right."

Ulyana went down into the vault.

The stairs were salt-white and worn hollow in the middle, and the cold down there was not the cold of stone but the cold of held breath. The shelves ran back into the dark farther than the cellar had any right to be deep — the Guild had bought that, too, decades before Ulyana's time: forty feet of borrowed depth from a dying architect who had more space in his head than his house could hold. Jars and stoppered flasks and small lead coffers lined the shelves, each tagged in ledger-hand. *Six months of laughter, a cooper's daughter, Lowtown. One summer, unlabeled, provenance disputed. A name, not yet earned, surrendered by a boy of nineteen who decided he would rather be no one than be what he was becoming. One storm, bottled, small, coastal — do not shake.* And on the shelf reserved for voices, in its jar of grey glass, an hour of a cooper's speech, the words inside it coiled like smoke that had been taught to hold its shape.

Ulyana carried it up with both hands, though it weighed no more than an apple. Some things you carried carefully because they were heavy. Others because they were not.

At the counter, she unstopped the jar, and the hour came out.

It did not fill the room. That was the first cruelty of traded voices — they were only an hour, and an hour is a small territory. It was a winter kitchen, the sound of a kettle, the scrape of a chair, and then T. Ostwick's voice, low and roughened, saying things to no one: that the medicine had been bought, that the cooper's yard owed him a week's wages, that the rain would be good for the cabbages. And then, near the end of the hour, quieter, in the tone of a man speaking to a door he thinks is shut: *Tell her the hour I sold was the hour I complained about the rain. Not the good ones. She should have the complaining. The good ones she already has.*

Maren Ostwick made no sound at all. She stood with her hand pressed flat against her mouth, and her shoulders shook the way a house shakes in wind — from somewhere deep in the frame — and when the hour ended, in mid-sentence, in the ordinary nothing of a kettle coming to boil, she wept the way people weep at funerals in countries where it is shameful: silently, furiously, as if the weeping were an enemy to be outlasted.

Ulyana looked away and gave her the dignity of the counter between them, and pretended to check entries that needed no checking.

"The Guild thanks you for your custom," she said, when the widow's breathing had evened. "The goods are yours. The Guild holds no further claim." She hesitated, and then broke another small law. "Mistress Ostwick. The last minute of the hour — it repeats, if you buy the extension. The Guild doesn't advertise it. It's called a reprise, and it costs a tenth of the original price, and it is yours at half that, because the ledger has an error in your entry that I am not permitted to correct but am permitted to compensate." There was no error. There never was. But Ulyana had been making the same error, in the same careful hand, for eleven years, and the guildmaster had never once caught it, because guildmasters did not read; they counted.

Maren Ostwick looked at her for a long moment, rain-wet and wrecked and suddenly, sharply, seeing her. "You hate this place," she said. It was not a question, and it was not an accusation. It was the tone of a woman who had spent thirty years reading other people's linen and knew every stain for what it was.

"The ledger does not judge," Ulyana said, which was the rule, and was also, she had found, the closest thing to an answer that did not cost anything.

The widow gathered her jar in both arms like an infant and went out into the rain. The bell rang behind her, a small bright sound, and then the counting room was quiet, and Ulyana sat back down at her desk, and the candle had gone out while she was below, and she did not relight it.

The moon through the high window was enough to write by, if she let her eyes remember what they had been trained to do in the dark — another inheritance from the burned order, from the years of night-marches and night-vigils, when swordspeakers learned to read the world without asking it for light. She opened the master ledger to the day's page and began to close the entries. The cooper's daughter's laughter: still unredeemed. She wrote *holding fee paid through Ember* in the margin, though no fee had come, and knew she would pay it herself from the purse she kept beneath the floorboard, the one that was slowly emptying, the one that was the whole of her rebellion and the whole of her hypocrisy and the whole, if she were honest, of her life.

That was the thing no one understood about oaths, she thought, dipping her pen. People imagined they were iron — that you swore once, loudly, and the iron held you forever after, and you were done. But an oath was not iron. It was a fire you carried, and it needed feeding, and it fed on you, and the question was never *will I keep it* but *what is left of me to burn.* Eleven years, and she was down to embers and margins and small illegal mercies. Some nights she wondered what the oath would ask of her when there was nothing left but the shape of her, hollow as a jar, waiting on a shelf in the dark for someone to come and claim what was inside.

She was still writing when the bell rang again.

She did not look up. "The Guild is closed," she said. "Trades resume at the ninth hour. Emergencies are heard by the guildmaster, at the house on Vellum Street, and I will thank you not to—"

The bell rang again. Twice. Then a third time, and then it did not stop — it went on and on, a small bright frantic sound, as though the bell itself had been traded something terrible and wanted it back. And beneath it, faintly, from the vault below, Ulyana heard the impossible things begin to hum.

All of them. The laughter, the summer, the unearned name, the bottled storm. Eleven years she had kept that vault, and it had slept like the dead, and now every shelf was singing, one low rising note, like a struck glass, like a held breath, like a word about to be spoken in a tongue no living mouth remembered.

Ulyana stood. Her hand went, out of eleven years of habit, to her hip — where no sword had hung since the night her order burned — and someone knocked, past closing, at the Guild door, small and slow, the way a child knocks.
