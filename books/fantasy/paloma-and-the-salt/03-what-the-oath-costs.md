## 3. What the Oath Costs

The boy slept in the vault.

Not in a cell — the Salt Guild did not keep cells; imprisonment implied the prisoner had value one wished to reclaim later — but among the shelved impossibilities, on a pallet of sacking between a jar of borrowed thunder and a cask of last words, because it was the only room whose locks Ulyana trusted. She had told herself it was practicality. She had told herself it was containment. She had not told herself the truth, which was that when she had laid him down she had brushed the frost from his hair with her thumb, and her hand had shaken afterward for reasons she declined to examine.

Now dawn came grey through the high slit of a window, and Ulyana sat across from him with an unlit lamp and a ledger open on her knees, and did not read a word of it.

He slept the way children sleep in war-torn places, if they sleep at all: curled small, fists closed, as though sleep were something that might be taken back. His breathing made a sound like tide through shingle. And every so often — this was the thing she could not stop watching for — every impossible thing on the shelves around him *answered* him. The jar of thunder rolled gently against its glass. The cask of last words seeped a smell of beeswax and grief. The hourglass of a hanged man's final minute, sealed behind leaded glass on the third shelf, ticked backward once, softly, like a heart remembering.

He spoke in his sleep. Not the pre-kingdom tongue this time. Fragments. A woman's voice saying a name. The smell of smoke. He said *no* twice, in plain trade-common, with such old grief in it that Ulyana's chest hurt to hear.

She had been sixteen the last time she had heard someone say *no* like that — with their whole life inside the word.

* * *

The Order of the Sundered Blade had kept its hall above the salt marshes at Cael Verren, and in the spring of Ulyana's sixteenth year there were still forty-one swordspeakers alive to walk its corridors. She remembered the number because Master Ellery Vane made them count themselves each morning, standing in ranks in the cold yard, answering to their names in the old cadence: *here, and sworn.* Forty-one voices saying *here, and sworn* while gulls screamed over the walls and the marsh breathed its slow iron smell.

She remembered Vane's hands most of all. A swordspeaker's hands were ruined things — scarred at the knuckles where the oath ran closest to the skin, split along old lines of light that showed only when they spoke. When Vane gripped your shoulder to correct your stance, you felt the warmth of the covenant come off him like heat from a hearthstone. *The blade is only the shape of the promise,* he used to say. *The promise is the thing itself.*

The guild had come in autumn, with ledgers instead of soldiers, which was how the guild always came first. Guildmaster Corvane Sault himself had crossed the yard under a white parasol against the drizzle, and drunk Vane's tea, and admired the sword-racks, and then opened his ledger on the table between them and said, pleasantly, as if discussing the price of wool:

"The covenant is an asset, Master Vane. An unmonetized asset, sitting on worthless marshland, guarded by forty-one people who can be replaced by thirty clerks. I am offering you partnership."

Vane had looked at the ledger for a long time. Then he had said no — said it the way Paloma had said it in his sleep, whole-life-inside-the-word — and Sault had closed the book without rancor, thanked him for the tea, and gone back down the causeway.

The soldiers came nine days later. They wore no colors. That was the guild's elegance; the guild never spent its own blood if a king's could be spent instead, and kings were cheap, kings were *buyable*. Ulyana survived because Vane broke his own deepest teaching in the last hour of her life-as-it-was: he put his scarred hand flat against her sternum and *unspoke* her. He took her name out of the air and folded it into the oath, so that when the soldiers went through the burning hall counting corpses they found no Ulyana Thackeray anywhere in the world. A girl without a name cannot be mustered, taxed, tried, or sold.

She had crawled out through the reed beds with her ears still full of the sound of forty voices saying *here, and sworn* to the end, and she had knelt in black water at dawn and spoken the swordspeaker's oath alone, with no order left to swear it into, and the light had run up her arms like fire finding a wick, and she had understood that Vane had given her the only inheritance left: the burden.

*We keep the terms,* the oath went. *We do not weigh them.*

For nineteen years she had kept the terms inside the very institution that had murdered her order, telling herself it was penance, telling herself it was strategy, telling herself that a spy inside the guild's walls was worth more than a martyr in a mass grave. Telling herself, above all, that she did not enjoy the work — the appraising, the pricing, the quiet arithmetic of souls — because enjoyment would make her what they were.

And now there was a child in the vault who made impossible things sing, and Guildmaster Sault knew he existed, and Ulyana had watched Sault's eyes go bright with exactly the hunger she remembered from a white parasol and a ledger opening on a tea-stained table.

History, she thought, watching the boy sleep, does not knock politely the second time. It kicks the door in wearing your own face.

* * *

Paloma dreamed in pieces, and the pieces were older than stone.

*A tower of white teeth — no, tusks, no, pillars — and a woman walking between them with a lantern that burned without fuel. Her voice was the language, or the language was her voice; there was no difference yet, because nothing had names yet and everything did. "Little covenant," she said — but she said it in the tongue-before-tongues, which meant something more like: little-bridge-between-what-is-and-what-must-be. "When the fire gutters, they will send you. Do not be afraid of the water. Do not be afraid of the salt. Be afraid only of the ones who count."*

*Then snow, and cold, and a door, and a woman with tired eyes kneeling in it — and the woman's fear tasted like iron and old grief, and the dream-Paloma reached toward it because grief was a word he knew in every language there had ever been.*

*Then fire, far away, guttering. A coal wrapped in coils of sleeping dark. And something vast turning one ancient eye toward him through the dream, patient as geology, and a thought that was not a word but felt like: soon.*

He woke with the taste of brine in his mouth and the woman watching him from across the room, and for a moment neither of them moved. Then she held up three fingers, and said, carefully, in trade-common: "Three. Can you say three? We start with numbers. Numbers are safe."

He looked at her fingers. He looked at her face. And he said — not *three*, but a word in the old tongue that meant, roughly, *the-number-of-stars-you-can-trust*, which was four — and then, seeing her confusion, he laughed, a small cracked sound, and tried again. "Fwee."

"Fwee," Ulyana repeated gravely, and something in her chest that had been locked for nineteen years turned over in its sleep.

* * *

Sault summoned her at midmorning, which meant he sent no summons at all, merely arranged to be watering the ferns outside her office when she arrived, because Sault believed that ambushes conducted among houseplants seemed like accidents.

"Thackeray." He smiled his dry smile. He was smaller than men expected — neat, grey, precise, a man shaped like a filing error — and his hands were soft, and Ulyana had spent nineteen years making sure she never forgot what those soft hands signed off on. "Strange inventory discrepancy. One item entered the building last night that appears in no ledger."

"Strays come to all doors in winter."

"Mm." He touched a fern frond with one fingertip, as if checking it for dust. "This one resonated with the vault. Every shelf, Thackeray. The Marrowtide chalice sang. Do you understand what that means?"

Ulyana kept her face still, which was easy, because keeping her face still was the one discipline her order had taught better than any other. "Children are loud. Impossibilities are sensitive. Coincidence."

"Coincidence." He savored the word. "Nineteen years you've worked for me, and you've learned to lie beautifully, and yet you always choose the same tell. You go still. Stillness, Thackeray, is a confession with better posture." He stepped closer, and lowered his voice to the register he used for valuations. "Here is what my sources tell me. A child, mute until now, speaking the pre-kingdom tongue. Arriving at *your* door, past closing, when you were the last one in the building. A child who makes traded things resonate — which, if you know your history better than you pretend to, means he isn't carrying an impossibility." His eyes were pale and patient. "He *is* one."

The silence stretched. Somewhere below them, the guild's morning trade murmured — a man selling his talent for dancing, a woman buying courage by the dram — and Ulyana stood in it, stiller than the ferns.

"I don't know what you mean," she said.

"No," Sault agreed pleasantly. "But you'll decide to know soon, and I'd rather you decided in my favor. So let me make the arithmetic simple." He straightened his cuffs. "A living covenant, Thackeray. Do you know what the dragon-fire has been worth to us? Everything. Every borrowed year, every bottled storm, every name not yet earned — all of it is drawn from the flame, and the flame has been drawing down for decades, and when it dies, the guild dies, and everything we've ever sold becomes simply *gone*. But a covenant renewed — a covenant made flesh, delivered to the flame —" He spread his soft hands, a merchant displaying cloth. "That is a monopoly extended for another age. There are buyers already asking. Kings. Things older than kings. I intend to be paid *enormously*."

"And if I refuse to hand him over?"

Sault looked genuinely puzzled, the way a knife might look puzzled by the concept of mercy. "Refuse? Thackeray. I'm offering you a choice between survival and irrelevance. I know about Cael Verren. Of course I know. I've known since the day I hired you — why do you think I hired you? Guilt is the cheapest loyalty there is. It works for free." He patted her shoulder, and his hand was warm, horribly warm, warm as a hearthstone. "Bring me the boy tonight. Unharmed — mostly. And perhaps I'll let the dead of your order rest in peace instead of in evidence. I have their names, you know. All forty-one. I've had them for nineteen years, waiting to see what you'd do."

He walked away down the corridor, soft-footed among the ferns.

Ulyana stood a long time. Then she went down to the vault, past the shelves of priced miracles, to the pallet of sacking where a boy sat counting on his fingers — *one, two, fwee* — to no one, patiently, the way the tide counts against the shore.

He looked up at her. In the old tongue that no kingdom remembered, in the voice that made impossible things sing, he said a single word.

Later, when she had time to translate, she would understand it. What it meant was: *I-know-what-he-offered-you. I-know-which-way-your-heart-broke-the-first-time.*

What she heard, standing there with her hand already moving toward the door bar, was simpler, and worse.

It sounded like *here, and sworn*.
