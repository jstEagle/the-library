## 4. Flight Across the Grey Marshes

*Ulyana*

They left Ashfall before the third bell, when the fog still owned the streets and the guild's night-porters were too busy counting to watch the doors.

Ulyana carried Paloma on her back, wrapped in a fishmonger's oilcloth she'd bought with the last honest coin in her purse. The boy weighed less than a ledger. That frightened her more than the hunters would have — that something so slight could hold a language older than kingdoms, could make every bottled thing in the vault sing against its glass.

She did not take the coast road. The coast road had tollhouses, and tollhouses had guild eyes. Instead she turned west, toward the Grey Marshes, where the Salt Guild sent nothing and wanted nothing, because the marshes were where traded things went to die.

Every guild has a graveyard. The marshes were theirs.

"Hold on," she said, not knowing if he understood, and felt his small arms tighten anyway. He understood *that*, at least. Some languages needed no translation.

The ground began to fail beneath them within the hour. First the road went soft, then it went strange, then it stopped pretending to be a road at all and became a suggestion of dry land winding between black pools. Reeds stood taller than Ulyana's head, silver-grey and whispering even when there was no wind. The air tasted of iron and old rain.

Paloma made a sound against her shoulder — not his pre-kingdom tongue this time, but something smaller. A whimper.

"I know," she said. "I don't like it either."

That was a lie. She liked it fine. She had been born in country like this, before the order took her, before the oath, before everything. Marsh country was honest country. It showed you exactly what it was: mud and hunger and things that had outlived their owners.

The first ghost-lantern appeared at dusk.

It hung over the water ahead of them, a soft gold light with no lamp beneath it, bobbing gently as if someone invisible were walking ahead carrying it. Ulyana knew what it was. Somewhere, decades ago, a woman had sold her evening walks — one hour of golden light along a canal every dusk for twenty years — and when she died unpaid-for years still remained, unowned, drifting until they found their way here.

The lantern drifted closer. It had no face, but Ulyana felt it looking.

"Don't follow it," she told Paloma quietly. "It isn't lost. It's just waiting for someone to be lost enough."

He watched it over her shoulder as they waded past, his breath warm against her neck, and the lantern dimmed slowly, like an eye closing, as though disappointed.

* * *

*Paloma*

The world was wrong here, and Paloma did not know how to say so.

In his head, words moved like fish under ice — the old language, the deep one, the one he had spoken before he could speak anything else. But the marsh was full of other voices. Things murmured under the water. Things whispered in the reeds. Once, passing a pool perfectly still and black as ink, he saw shapes standing at the bottom — tall figures made of paper and wax seals, debts given flesh, their faces blank ledgers. They did not rise. But they noticed him. He felt it like cold fingers between his shoulder blades.

The woman — Ulyana, she had said, pressing her own name into his hands like something precious — talked to him constantly. Most of her words slid off him. But some stuck.

*"Water."* She pointed at a pool.

*"Food,"* holding up dried fish from her pack.

*"Run,"* said with a grip on his hand and fear in her voice, and he learned that word fastest of all.

He tried to give something back. That was the law of things, even before kingdoms: you do not receive without returning. So when they stopped that first night in the hollow of a dead willow, its roots making a roof above black sand, he took her hand and traced on her palm the sign for the word he knew meant *shelter* — the oldest word he had, older than walls, the word his people had used before they built any.

Ulyana went very still.

"What was that?"

He did it again. *Shelter. Safety. This place holds.*

"That's not writing," she whispered. "That's covenant-script. I've only ever seen it in the restricted vault, behind three locks. The founding documents are written in—" She stopped herself. Looked at him differently then, the way you look at a locked door you suddenly realize you've been sleeping beside. "You wrote that without being taught."

He shrugged, because how could he explain that he hadn't written anything? He had simply *said* it, the way she simply breathed.

She pulled her cloak around them both and, after a long moment, rested her hand briefly on his head. It was the first time she had touched him without necessity. He slept better for it.

* * *

*Ulyana*

On the second day she heard the hounds.

Not dogs — the Guild didn't use dogs, dogs couldn't smell a stolen covenant. These were *retrieval-hounds*, bred from ledger-paper and grievance: pale, hairless things shaped vaguely like wolves, whose bodies were covered in script. Every debt ever forgiven appeared on their skin as it was called due again. They tracked by contract-scent. They would find Paloma because Paloma was owed, and they would find Ulyana because she was in breach.

She heard them at midmorning, a sound like wet pages turning, far behind but gaining.

"Time to run," she said, and lifted him, and ran.

The marsh fought them both ways. Water sucked at her boots; reeds whipped her face; twice she sank to her hip in peat and had to claw out while Paloma clung to her collar. But the boy helped in ways she couldn't explain. When she chose a path, he sometimes tugged her sleeve and pointed elsewhere, and always — always — the path he chose avoided the worst of it, avoided the pools where the paper-debts stood watching, avoided a stretch of reeds that she later saw was dense with sleeping ghost-lanterns, hundreds of them, a harvest of abandoned hours.

"You can read this place," she panted once, hauling them both onto a hummock of firmer ground. "Can't you? You know what the land is saying."

He looked at her with those dark, serious eyes and pressed his hand flat against the earth. Then, deliberately, he took her hand and traced on her palm again: *listen.*

She listened. Beneath the wind and the turning-pages sound of pursuit, she heard it — faint, rhythmic, almost like breathing. The whole marsh, breathing.

"It's alive," she said stupidly. "Of course it's alive. Everything the guild ever traded came from somewhere living."

And then the hounds came over the ridge of dead reeds behind them, six pale shapes flowing down the slope like spilled milk, script crawling across their skins, and there was no more time for listening or lessons or anything but running.

* * *

*Paloma*

The hound-voices hurt him. Not their teeth — though he saw those, yellow and endless, as the woman ran with him through the drowning country — but their *words*. Their skins were covered in broken promises, and broken promises screamed in a register only he could hear.

So he did the only thing he knew. He reached back into the oldest part of himself, the part that spoke before kingdoms, and he *answered*.

He didn't understand what he did. He only opened his mouth and let the deep words come out, and the marsh itself seemed to lean in to listen.

A ghost-lantern rose from the pool beside them — the same one, he thought, the one that had looked at them the first night — and drifted into the hounds' path. Then another. Then a dozen, dozens, all the unclaimed hours and unsold evenings rising out of the black water like a congregation, gold lights crowding the pale beasts, wrapping them in light they had never earned and could not collect.

The hounds stopped. Confused. Their contract-scent drowned in a flood of unowned time.

Ulyana stared. Then she laughed — a short, disbelieving bark of a laugh — and ran harder than ever.

*You,* he thought at her, not knowing how to say it aloud yet. *You gave me a name. You fed me. This is my payment.* The law of things. Even before kingdoms.

* * *

*Ulyana*

They lost the hounds by nightfall, and Ulyana allowed them both to collapse on a shelf of dry ground between two meres, shaking, soaked, alive.

She got a fire going — small, screened by stones, all a fugitive could risk — and roasted the last of the fish. Paloma ate like a small wolf, then sat watching her with an expression she was beginning to recognize as his version of questions.

"You want to know why they're hunting us," she said. "Why I'm running instead of handing you over and going back to my warm room and my clean ledgers."

He nodded once, gravely.

She should have lied. A good liar would have lied. But exhaustion strips away craft, and the truth came out plain:

"Because thirty years ago, men came to my order's hall and asked us to sell something that wasn't ours to sell. And we said no. And they killed us for it — all but me. I survived because I swore an oath to the guild that day: service, silence, obedience. An oath bought with my life." She turned her hands over, palms up, scarred from sword-drill and ledger-work alike. "I told myself keeping the oath was honoring the dead. But tonight I watched a child raise the dead hours of strangers to save us both, and I think the dead would rather I kept *them*."

Paloma crawled across the space between them and took her scarred hand. He turned it palm-up, studying it in the firelight. Then, carefully, with one finger, he traced new signs — slow ones, teaching-signs, the way she had been teaching him all journey.

*Oath,* he wrote. Then: *broken.* Then, after a pause long enough that she understood he was choosing the next word with great care: *free.*

Her throat closed. "Is that what your people believed? That an oath broken for love isn't broken at all?"

He didn't answer. But he didn't let go of her hand either, and somewhere in that silence she found her answer.

She taught him two more words that night — *fire* and *stay* — and he taught her one in return, tracing it into her palm until she could shape it blind: a spiral with a tail, meaning something her tongue had no true word for. *The thread that remains when everything else is cut.*

"What is it called?" she asked. "Your language. Does it have a name?"

He shook his head. Names came later than it did.

She banked the fire and lay back with the boy tucked against her side, listening to the marsh breathe, and might even have slept, except—

Except that across the water, far off, a light appeared that was no ghost-lantern. It burned white and steady and wrong, and beneath it she could hear — very faint, very patient — the turning of wet pages.

Not hounds this time. Hounds were for retrieval.

This was a Reader. The guildmaster's own kind, who could find anything ever written down — and Ulyana had just realized, with a cold sliding horror, that she had written her confession aloud in firelight, and that oaths, once broken, are always legible.

She rose, woke Paloma with a hand over his mouth before he could cry out, and pointed east, where the marsh ended and the drowned crater waited.

"We run for the dragon," she whispered. "And pray the old fire still wants us."
