## 10. Salt in the Morning Air

A year had taught Paloma the shape of mornings.

Before, in the time before Ulyana — a time he kept folded away like cloth too fine to wear every day — mornings had been only cold and the grey hush of the marshes, the taste of salt on everything, even his own skin. He had not known then that morning could be a *thing*, could have weight and warmth and a name people gave it gladly. Now he woke in the narrow room above the guildhall with light coming through shutters he had learned to open himself, and somewhere below, bread was baking, and someone was laughing.

He lay still a moment and listened to the building breathe.

The Salt Guild had changed its bones. That was what Vessarion had said, drowsy and vast in the crater's dark, when the new flame first steadied: *You cannot mend a thing like this without changing what it is made of.* The old guild had been built on taking — years from the desperate, storms from the sky, names from children too young to know they'd been robbed. The new one was being built, plank by plank and law by law, on giving back. It was slower work than burning had been. Some days it felt like carrying water in cupped hands across a desert. But the ledgers were honest now, or as honest as human hands could keep them, and every entry was written twice: once for what was traded, once for what was owed back.

Paloma had learned to read those ledgers before he had learned much else of letters. The pre-kingdom tongue did not trouble itself with ink; it lived in breath and bone. But Ulyana had sat with him through whole winters of evenings, her finger moving down columns of figures, saying *this is how you know a thing is true — this is how you count what cannot be counted* — and he had understood that she was teaching him more than arithmetic. She was teaching him the shape of trust, set down where anyone could see it.

He got up. The floorboards were warm where the sun had already found them.

* * *

The schoolroom had once been the vault.

That had been Merrin Stave's idea, and everyone had agreed it was either the most fitting or the most blasphemous thing any of them had ever done, depending on who you asked. The vault where impossible things had slept in their rows — bottled storms ranked like soldiers, borrowed years humming behind leaded glass, names coiled in their little boxes waiting to be worn — that room now held desks. Chalk. A slate board. Twenty-three children from the harbour quarter whose parents could not pay, which was to say, all of them, because under the new law no one paid, because knowledge was the one impossibility the guild had finally decided should be free.

Ulyana stood at the front of the room, and Paloma watched her from the doorway the way he always did for the first few minutes, the way you watch a fire you helped relight, just to be sure it holds.

She looked smaller than she had in the marshes. Not weaker — never that — but *contained*, the way a blade is contained by its scabbard. Her swordspeech was gone. Everyone knew it; no one spoke of it in the way no one speaks of a missing limb in a house that loves the person missing it. Where her oath had burned there was now only an ordinary voice, a teacher's voice, a little rough at the edges, and when she raised her hand the air did not split along bright seams the way it once had. It simply moved, because hands move air, and that was all.

And yet.

Paloma had seen her stop a fight between two boys last autumn without raising that hand at all — just by looking at them the way she must once have looked down the length of a drawn sword, and something in her stillness said everything the magic used to say. He had seen her sit up all night with a widow whose husband's voice had come back wrong from the ledger-burning, hoarse and halting, and hold the woman's hands while she wept, and say nothing, because nothing was the right thing to say. Kindness, Vessarion had told him once, in the long slow language of the deep places, *is the only magic that survives its own spending.*

"Paloma." Ulyana had noticed him, of course. She always noticed him first, before any of the children, before the chalk dust even settled. "You're late."

"The bridge council ran long," he said. "The ferryman wanted to know if the river would forgive him for the tolls he took during the siege. I told him yes, but he has to carry widows free until spring."

"And?"

"And he swore it on his mother's name, so now it's true."

Ulyana's mouth did the thing it did instead of smiling fully — a small dry tilt, one corner, like a door left ajar. "Sit down, ambassador. You can help little Sef with his letters. He keeps writing his name backwards."

"It isn't backwards," Sef protested, six years old and ferocious. "It's *mirrored*. My da says mirrored things are worth double."

"Your da," Ulyana said, "sold a storm to the old guild in '41 and has been insufferable about value ever since. Sit."

Paloma sat. The morning went on around them, ordinary and enormous.

* * *

At midday he walked out to the crater, as he did most days now, though the walk had grown shorter — or rather, the distance had grown shorter around him, the way distances do when a road learns you. The covenant-flame burned steady in Vessarion's coils, gold-white, no longer guttering, and the dragon slept easier than the old stories said dragons ever slept. His breathing moved the whole basin of the drowned volcano, a tide of it, slow as seasons.

"You're thinking loudly again," Vessarion rumbled, without opening his eyes. One vast lid slid aside anyway, revealing an eye like a well of evening. "Sit. Tell me what the small ones did today."

"Sef wrote his name mirrored. The ferryman swore an oath on his mother. And—" Paloma hesitated, folding himself down onto the warm stone near the flame, close enough that its light lay on his hands like water. "And I signed to Ulyana. At the door. Before I left."

Vessarion waited. Dragons were good at waiting; it was most of what they were.

"I signed *home*," Paloma said. "And she signed it back."

The dragon's breath went out of him in a long slow roll, stirring the flame, making it lean and steady itself. "A year ago," Vessarion said, "you could not say your own name in any tongue. Now you are a bridge between realms, and you spend your mornings worrying whether a swordspeaker without a sword knows she is loved."

"She knows," Paloma said. Then, honestly: "I want her to *know* she knows."

"That," said Vessarion, "is the whole of every covenant ever made. Everything else is bookkeeping." The eye closed again, but the voice went on, softer, sinking toward sleep. "You will be called east soon. The other realm stirs. They have felt the flame change hands, and they will want to look at the boy who carries their oldest words in a mouth barely older than a decade. When they come, remember—"

"Remember what?"

But the dragon had gone down into his rest, and only the flame answered, crackling gently, keeping its own counsel.

* * *

Evening found him back at the guildhall, on the steps, where Ulyana sat with the day's last ledger open on her knees and the sunset doing unremarkable, miraculous things to the harbour water. She didn't write in ledgers the way the old guildmasters had — hungry, grasping, each figure a hook. She wrote the way she did everything now: carefully, plainly, leaving room in every margin.

Paloma sat down beside her. For a while neither of them spoke. Below them the reformed guild went about its evening business — Merrin Stave arguing cheerfully with a storm-seller about fair rates, two former hunters stacking crates of returned years for delivery to the families they'd been taken from, a girl of maybe ten carefully carrying a bottled breeze to the window shelf where the guild kept the things too small to matter and too lovely to refuse.

"Vessarion says the east will call me soon," Paloma said at last.

"I know. I felt it too, this morning. In my teeth." She almost smiled. "Old habits. The oath may be spent, but the body remembers standing watch."

"Are you afraid?"

Ulyana considered this with the seriousness she gave to everything, which was the thing he loved best in her, though he had no word for love yet in any language, pre-kingdom or otherwise. He was working on it.

"No," she said finally. "I spent a year afraid of the wrong things — that losing my gift would make me less. It turns out I was mostly the gift's all along, not the other way round." She closed the ledger. "What I am is curious. I'd like to see the east. I'd like to see you stand between two worlds and translate, and watch them understand each other for the first time in a thousand years." She paused. "I'd like to be there when they realize the bridge is a child who once couldn't say his own name."

"They'll weep," Paloma said. "The hired blades wept. These are scholars."

"Scholars weep worse."

The sun went down. The first lamp was lit inside, and its light spilled yellow across the steps and over the two of them, and the smell of salt came in off the water the way it always did, but gentler now, like something that had finished grieving.

Paloma lifted his hands into the lamplight. He did not need to sign — he had words enough now, in three tongues and a fourth that belonged to no kingdom — but some things were older than speech, and this was one of them, and he wanted it to be old.

*Home*, he signed.

Ulyana watched his hands, and then she set the ledger aside entirely, and answered him in the same silent grammar, her movements a little stiff, learned late, learned thoroughly, the way she learned everything that mattered:

*Home* — and then, after a moment, adding the sign they had invented together, the one that existed in no language anywhere except between the two of them: *always*.

Far to the east, beyond the mountains, beyond the maps, something ancient turned over in its sleep and began, slowly, to wake — and in the crater below the city, a gold-white flame leaned toward the sound of it, steady and unafraid, waiting for its bridge.
