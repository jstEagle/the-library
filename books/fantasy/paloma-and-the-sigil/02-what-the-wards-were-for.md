## 2. What the Wards Were For

The morning after the stars died, the valley pretended it had not noticed.

Felix saw it in the way the baker still opened his shutters at the same hour, in the way the miller's boys drove their cart down the same ruts, in the way the temple bell rang terce as though the sky above Veyra's Reach were still sewn with its thousand familiar lights instead of hanging black and blank as a closed eye. The valley went about its business the way a man walks on a broken leg—carefully, deliberately, refusing to look down.

Felix could not refuse to look down. He had spent the whole night looking up.

He stood now in the ward-house yard, where the border stones had been carried after the shattering, laid out on trestle tables like casualties of some battle no one would admit to having fought. There were forty-one of them once, set at intervals along the valley's rim, each carved with sigils so old that the grooves had gone soft-edged with centuries of weather. Now there were thirty-three whole stones and a hundredweight of fragments, and the fragments were what troubled him most. Stone did not shatter like that. Stone cracked, stone crumbled, stone wore away grain by patient grain. These had burst—as if something inside them had finally stopped holding its breath.

He picked one up. It was lighter than it should have been, the way a thing is light when everything true has been burned out of it. Along its broken face he could see the cross-section of the sigil-work: not one carving but many, layered like the rings of a tree, generation pressed upon generation. Someone had renewed this ward over and over for longer than the valley had kept records. And every layer, when he tilted it toward the grey light, held traces of the same dark stain soaked deep into the stone's heart.

It looked like blood. It looked very much like blood.

"Put that down."

Felix turned. Elder Cassian stood at the edge of the yard wrapped in his formal grey, the wool heavy even in summer, because elders were never permitted to dress for weather—only for office. Behind him stood two of the temple guard, and behind them, at a distance that was itself a kind of statement, half the patrol had gathered to watch.

Felix set the fragment down. "Elder."

"You were on the wall last night." It was not a question. Everyone knew who had been on the wall; everyone knew whose patrol had been closest when the wards came apart. "You felt something."

Felix considered lying. He was good at lying—it was a mercenary's craft, honed alongside swordwork, and in twenty years of service he had lied to bandits and lords and dying men with equal fluency. But Cassian's eyes were pale and quick, and they had the particular patience of a man who already knew the shape of the answer.

"I felt the wards break," Felix said. "Like glass going out of a window. All at once."

"And before that?"

Felix looked at the tables of dead stone. Somewhere under his breastbone, in the place where his god had lived silent for twenty years, something shifted again—a slow turn, like a sleeper rolling over. He had told no one. He had barely admitted it to himself in the small hours, standing watch over a border that no longer existed, feeling that vast and patient attention press against the inside of his ribs like a hand against a door.

"Before that," Felix said carefully, "it was quiet. As always."

Cassian studied him for three breaths too long. Then: "The council convenes at midday. Your patrol is confined to the inner quarter until then. No one speaks to the townsfolk of what happened on the wall. The official word is a tremor in the ley-lines. Seasonal. Correctable."

"Seasonal," Felix repeated. The word tasted like ash. "Elder—the stars are gone. Not dimmed. Gone. Every one of them, all at once, in the space of a heartbeat. I've served this valley twenty years and I've never seen the wards so much as flicker. What were they for?"

For a moment—one moment, no longer—something moved behind Cassian's composure. Not fear exactly. Recognition. The look of a man hearing a question he had hoped never to be asked by someone who might understand the answer.

"The wards protect us," Cassian said. "That is all you need know. That is all any soldier needs know."

"With respect, Elder, my oath says otherwise. I swore to defend this valley against whatever crosses its borders. If I don't know what the borders are *for*—"

"Your oath," Cassian said, and his voice dropped into something almost gentle, which was worse than anger, "is to obey. It has kept you well, Vasquez. Don't make it a burden now, at the end of things."

*At the end of things.* Felix held the elder's gaze and did not ask what that meant, though the words rang in him like a struck bell long after Cassian swept from the yard, the guards trailing, the watching patrol dispersing into murmurs.

At the end of things. As if the breaking of the wards were not an accident but an appointment.

* * *

The temple of the Silent God occupied the highest terrace of the town, and Felix had climbed its steps more times than he could count—first as a boy-soldier with a fresh brand on his forearm, later as a man keeping the only vow that had never wavered. The god had no name anyone used aloud. The god had no voice, no visions, no miracles. The god had silence, and the silence was called holy, and Felix had built his entire life inside it the way a barnacle builds inside its shell.

He went there at dusk, ostensibly to report. Really he went because the alternative was sitting alone with the turning thing beneath his ribs, and because twenty years of habit said that when the world broke, you went to the temple.

The sanctuary was nearly empty. A handful of old women knelt in the side pews; a novice trimmed lamps that gave off more smoke than light. High Priest Ambrose stood at the altar with his back to the doors, and Felix knew at once that something was wrong, because Ambrose always heard the doors. Ambrose heard everything. That was his gift and his terror—he missed nothing, forgave selectively, and remembered forever.

"Ambrose."

The high priest did not turn. "You should not have come tonight, Felix."

"The wards are broken. The stars are gone. Where else should I come?"

"Nowhere," Ambrose said quietly. "Nowhere at all. That is rather the point."

Felix crossed the sanctuary floor, boots loud on stone worn smooth by generations of kneeling. Up close he could see what the high priest held: a book. An old one, its boards swollen with damp, its spine stitched twice with different thread in different centuries. Ambrose held it the way a man holds a snake—firmly, at the neck, ready for it to twist.

"What is that?" Felix asked. "I've catalogued the reliquary twice. That's not in it."

"No," Ambrose agreed. "It isn't."

And then, while Felix watched, the high priest carried the book to the brazier beside the altar and fed it to the coals.

Felix had killed men. He had watched villages burn and done nothing, because doing nothing was sometimes the contract. But something in him lurched forward before thought could stop it—his hand closing on Ambrose's wrist, hard enough to bruise, hard enough that the novice by the door dropped her lamp-trimmer with a clatter that rang through the whole silent sanctuary.

"What are you doing?"

"My office," Ambrose said. His voice did not shake. His eyes, when they found Felix's, were terrible in their calm. "Which you swore to uphold, along with every other soul in this valley who wears the brand. Release me, soldier."

Felix released him. The book curled and blackened. He caught one glimpse of the open page as it went—dense script in an old hand, and among the lines a diagram, a sigil of concentric rings with spokes radiating outward like a wheel or a star—and then the fire took it, and the diagram became a red ghost, and the ghost became nothing.

"Why?" Felix's own voice sounded strange to him. Rough. Young. "Ambrose. Twenty years I've kept faith. Tell me why."

The high priest watched the last of the cover collapse into glowing lace. When he spoke, it was barely above a whisper, pitched for Felix alone.

"Because the covenant is ended, and what it bound is waking, and there is nothing left in these pages that can help us now. Only things that can hurt us. Names, mostly." He brushed ash from his fingers with terrible tidiness. "Go home, Felix. Pray as you always pray. Be grateful, for perhaps the first time in your life, that your god does not answer."

Then he walked past Felix and out of the sanctuary, and the two temple guards flanking the doors fell in behind him, and Felix stood alone in the lamplight listening to a book finish becoming smoke.

* * *

He did not go home.

He told himself he would. He made it as far as the barracks courtyard, sat on the cold lip of the well, and listened to the turning thing beneath his ribs, and understood that home was on the other side of a question he could no longer leave unasked.

The temple's archive sat below the sanctuary, down a stair so narrow two men could not pass abreast. Felix had entered it four times in twenty years—twice to fetch ceremonial records, twice to escort elders who wished to consult the founding charters. He knew the lock, and he knew the guard rotation, and he knew that tonight the guard would be doubled and distracted, because half the temple's strength had gone to the walls where the wards had fallen, staring at a border that no longer glowed.

He also knew, with the flat certainty of a man reading his own sentence, that what he was about to do would end the life he had built. You did not break into the temple of your own god. Whatever else happened tonight, the man who walked down those stairs would not be the man who climbed back up.

He went anyway. That was the thing about faith, he thought, easing the archive door with a knife-blade and a prayer he wasn't sure anyone heard: nobody warned you that it worked both ways. They told you belief would hold you up. Nobody told you that once it cracked, you'd fall through fast, and the falling would feel like flying right up until the landing.

Inside, the archive breathed dust and tallow-smoke. Felix lit no lamp. Twenty years of night patrols had given him eyes that drank the dark, and besides, he knew what he was looking for—or near enough. Ambrose had burned one book. Men who burned books owned more than one. And men who burned books in haste, on the very night of catastrophe, always missed something.

The shelves stood in ranks like a buried army. Felix moved down them by touch, reading spines with his fingertips: ledgers of tithe, rolls of the sworn, annals copied and recopied until the originals were legend. Near the back, in a section that smelled older—colder, somehow, as if the air itself had been waiting—he found a shelf of volumes with no titles at all. Unmarked boards. The kind of anonymity that is itself a mark.

The third one was warm.

Not metaphorically warm. Warm, faintly, like a stone left near a hearth, though the archive held no fire. Felix drew it out and felt the weight of it settle into his hands with a rightness that unsettled him more than any omen. Beneath his breastbone, the sleeping thing turned again—and this time it did not merely shift. This time it *leaned*, the way a hound leans toward a door it knows.

He opened the book where it fell open, because the binding had been broken and mended so many times that it chose its own pages now.

Most of the text was in the old ecclesiastical hand, dense and looping, and he could read perhaps a third of it—the valley's soldiers learned letters, but not deep ones. He gathered fragments. *...the binding holds so long as the payment holds...* *...each generation owes...* *...the silence must not be mistaken for absence...* Words that raised more questions than they answered, arranged around that same diagram he had seen burning: concentric rings, radiating spokes, and at the center a mark he did not recognize except that his body recognized it, his scarred forearm prickling under the old brand as though the skin remembered being written on.

The page opposite the diagram had been torn out. Long ago, from the stain of it—but torn carelessly, in haste or grief, and a corner remained, ragged, clinging to the gutter of the spine.

Felix worked it loose with the tip of his knife.

It was smaller than his palm, that scrap, scorched brown at one edge as if it had already survived one fire and been tucked back into hiding by someone desperate. Most of the writing on it had gone to char. But ink in those days had been cut with iron and oak-gall, and iron keeps its temper, and two things survived the scorching clearly enough to read.

The first was a single word, written large across the top of the fragment in a formal, ceremonial script: **SIGIL**. Below it, smaller, the rest of the phrase had burned away—only the first letters of the next word remaining, enough to guess at *sigilwright* or *sigil-cutter*, some maker's title from a craft Felix had never heard named in twenty years of service.

The second was a name.

*Ibarra.*

He said it silently, testing it, and felt nothing—no recognition, no resonance. Just a name, foreign to the valley's rolls, to its tithe-ledgers, to every family he'd drunk with and bled beside. A name from outside. A name that belonged, by every clue the fragment offered, to whoever had cut the sigil at the center of the burning book—to whoever had built the wards that had guarded Felix's whole life and shattered in a single night.

Someone outside the valley had made the cage. Which meant the thing caged—

Beneath his ribs, the silence *pressed*. Not pain. Worse than pain: attention. The sensation of a vast ear held close, listening through him, to him, for him. Twenty years Felix had prayed into that silence and received nothing, and he had made his peace with nothing, had built a life on nothing the way other men built on stone. Now the nothing leaned against the inside of his chest like a prisoner at a door, and for the first time Felix understood that the silence had never been empty.

It had been *held*. Carefully. The way you hold your breath.

The brand on his forearm burned. He looked down at it in the dark—the small ring-and-spoke mark every sworn soldier carried, the mark he'd assumed was a blessing—and saw it suddenly for what the fragment suggested it might be. A countersignature. A line in a ledger he had never been shown.

Somewhere above him, a floorboard creaked.

Felix closed the book, slid it back into its place among the untitled dead, and folded the scorched fragment into the lining of his coat, over his heart, where the pressing silence seemed to gather thickest. Then he climbed the narrow stair past the creaking board, past the distracted guard, out into a night with no stars, carrying the first crack in his lifelong faith like a splinter working toward it.

A name. A sigil. A family that owed the valley something, or the valley that owed them.

He didn't know yet which. But as he walked the empty streets under the blind sky, one thing settled in him with the weight of certainty: somewhere beyond the broken wards, someone named Ibarra was going to come looking for what had been taken from them.

And Felix, gods help him, was already certain he would be the one to open the gate.
