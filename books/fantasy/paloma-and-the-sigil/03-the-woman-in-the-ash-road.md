## 3. The Woman in the Ash Road

The wards had been falling for three days before they finally broke, and Paloma Ibarra had spent all of them running.

She felt it the way a blind woman feels weather—through her skin, through the fine hairs on her arms, through the ache behind her eyes that was not quite pain and not quite memory. Sigilcraft lived in her blood the way other people carried their mothers' names, and what she felt now was the unraveling of the greatest working her family had ever cut: thread by thread, knot by knot, the covenant coming apart like a hem picked at by patient fingers.

On the first night, the stars at the valley's edge had dimmed to embers. On the second, they had gone out entirely, swallowed in a darkness that did not behave like night. And on the third—the third had brought the things.

They walked without light. That was the only way she could describe them, though *walked* was generous and *without light* was worse than it sounded. They were not shadows; shadows needed something to be cast from. They were absences that had learned ambition, holes in the world shaped vaguely like men, and where they passed, the ash road went gray and dead beneath their feet, and the crickets stopped singing as if they had never known how.

Paloma ran because she knew what they were. That was the cruelty of it. Her guildmasters had spent years teaching her every horror the old books contained, and then, when she asked the one question that mattered—*what do we do when the binding fails?*—they had put her out of the tower with nothing but her mother's notes sewn into her coat lining and a warning never to come back.

So she ran, and she knew exactly what chased her, and knowing made no difference at all.

* * *

The ash road wound down through hills that should have been green with late summer. Instead everything wore the color of old bone. The starless dark pressed close, thicker than fog, and Paloma's lantern—a stub of tallow in a tin case, hoarded like a relic—threw a circle of light barely wider than her shoulders. She had learned in the last two nights to stop looking past that circle. Looking past it meant seeing the shapes at its edge, standing very still, waiting for the flame to gutter.

Her breath came ragged. Three days of running, two of them without sleep worth counting, and her body had begun sending her the kind of quiet, reasonable messages that bodies send when they are preparing to betray you. *Rest here. Just a moment. Just until your hands stop shaking.* She knew better. The things behind her did not rest, because they were not alive enough to tire.

In her coat, against her ribs, the notes pressed warm. Her mother's handwriting, cramped and urgent, ink gone brown with age:

*If the stars fail, go east. Go to the valley. Find the ward-line and follow it to the oldest stone. The covenant remembers its makers, even if the makers are forgotten.*

*Even if,* her mother had written, and underlined it twice, as if she had known exactly how forgotten an Ibarra could become.

Paloma's family had kept the covenant for nine generations. Nine generations of blood and ink and silence, and at the end of it, her own guild had looked at her across a table and told her that the rite required a death, and that hers would do nicely, being both willing and expendable. She had run from that table straight into a world that was now, thanks to whatever failure had broken the binding, coming apart around her.

She should have hated them. Some nights, huddled in ditches with the absences drifting past close enough to touch, she managed it. But mostly what she felt was smaller and more shameful: she missed them. She missed the tower and the smell of hot iron and the sound of the apprentices chanting the foundation-strokes. She missed belonging to something, even something that wanted her dead.

Ahead, the land dropped away into a long shallow bowl, and at the bottom of it, faint as breath on glass, she saw the line.

It took her a moment to understand what she was seeing, because it was wrong in a way that made her stomach clench. The ward-line of the valley should have been visible for miles—a shimmering wall of maintained power, the pride of the covenant, fed by blood and sigil for two hundred years. Instead there was only a scar. A seam of dead ground running north to south as far as she could see, and along it, scattered like teeth knocked from a jaw, the shattered stumps of wardstones.

Broken. All of them broken.

Paloma stood at the lip of the bowl and felt something in her chest crack quietly, the way ice cracks on a pond—not all at once, but enough to know the water underneath is coming for you.

"Nine generations," she said aloud, to no one, to the dark. Her voice came out cracked too. "Nine generations, and you couldn't keep one more winter."

The dark did not answer. But somewhere behind her, up the slope of the ash road, something shifted its weight, and the crickets died again.

She ran.

* * *

She crossed the ward-line at the place where an old gate-stone lay split down its middle, its sigils burned black. Stepping over it felt like stepping through a curtain of cold water that wasn't there—and then wasn't cold either, just a pressure, a moment of resistance, like the whole valley drawing a breath to decide whether to let her in.

Then she was inside, and the difference hit her like a change in altitude.

Light. Not much—but some. A few stars overhead, dim and trembling, as if they weren't sure they'd been invited. The air tasted different, cleaner, threaded with woodsmoke and turned earth and livestock. Somewhere downslope a dog barked, steady and stupid and alive, and Paloma had to stop walking for a moment because the sound of it nearly undid her.

Inside the wards, the world still worked. Outside them, it was dying. And the wards themselves were broken—which meant the valley was a lit room with the door hanging open, and everything she had outrun for three days was going to walk through that door eventually.

Unless someone mended it.

*Find the ward-line,* her mother had written. *Follow it to the oldest stone.* The oldest stone would be at the heart of the valley, in the old sanctuary, where the first Ibarra had cut the first stroke of the first sigil. If anything in this world could tell her why the binding failed—and how to reforge it—it would be there.

She got maybe half a mile along the dead seam before the lantern betrayed her.

It wasn't the flame's fault. It was a good flame, faithful little thing, burning down its last inch of tallow. But the wind came off the broken wards in a gust that smelled of ozone and old iron, and the flame ducked, and in the half-second of darkness that followed, hands closed around her arms from both sides.

Not the absences. Hands. Human hands, calloused, strong, smelling of leather and lamp oil.

Paloma did what any sensible sigilwright would do: she screamed, drove her heel down onto the nearest instep, and began cutting a defensive glyph in the air with her finger, tracing the opening strokes of a ward that would blister the skin of anyone holding her—

And stopped. Because the man gripping her left arm had seen the gesture, and instead of flinching he had done something impossible.

He had completed it.

His hand moved over hers, quick and sure, closing the third stroke she hadn't finished and adding a fourth she didn't recognize—a modification, a dampener, turning her blistering ward into something harmless, a sigil that would do nothing but glow faintly and fade. He knew the grammar. He shouldn't have. No one outside the guilds knew the grammar; that was the entire point of the guilds.

The lantern relit itself—or rather, someone else relit it, a second figure, younger, nervous, holding the match with shaking fingers. In the sudden light Paloma saw she was surrounded: four men, maybe five, dressed in the plain dark gear of border soldiers, spears and short swords, faces grim with exhaustion. And the one holding her arm, the one who had finished her ward, was looking at his own hand as if it had committed treason.

"What—" he started, and stopped, and started again. "What was that? What did you just do?"

"I think," said Paloma, whose voice was steadier than she had any right to expect, "that's my question."

He was younger than she'd first taken him for, mid-thirties perhaps, dark-haired, with the kind of face that had been handsome once and had since decided other qualities were more practical. There was a scar through one eyebrow and a weariness in his eyes that she recognized intimately, because she'd been wearing the same one for three days. He looked at her the way a starving man looks at bread he suspects is poisoned.

"You're from outside," he said. Not a question. His grip on her arm hadn't loosened, but it had changed quality—less arrest, more anchor, as if he were afraid she might blow away. "You crossed the line."

"The line's down." She jerked her chin toward the shattered gate-stone. "Or hadn't you noticed."

"We noticed." Bitterness, deep and old. One of the other soldiers muttered something that might have been a prayer. "The elders say it's temporary. The high priest says the stars will return when we've atoned. Nobody says anything useful." His eyes narrowed. "But you knew it was down before you crossed. You came *toward* it. Nobody comes toward it."

"Nobody sane," agreed the young one with the matches.

"Felix," the older soldier said, without turning his head, and the young one shut up.

Felix. Paloma filed the name away with the automatic thoroughness of a woman who had grown up memorizing things for people who might kill her later. Felix Vasquez, then, if the patrol rolls were anything like guild records—border-born, sworn young, trained in ward-grammar he had no business knowing. Which meant the valley hadn't abandoned the covenant after all. It had just buried it so deep its own soldiers were doing sigilcraft in their sleep without knowing what to call it.

That was interesting. That was possibly the most interesting thing she'd learned in a year, and she was too tired to appreciate it properly.

"I'm looking for the sanctuary," she said. "The old one. At the heart of the valley."

Something flickered across Felix Vasquez's face—too fast to name, gone before it landed. "Why?"

"Because I'm a sigilwright, and your wards are broken, and unless someone who knows what they're doing gets to the root of it, everything outside those stones is going to come through here and eat your valley down to the bedrock." She pulled her arm free of his grip—he let her, which told her something—and pushed back her hood so the lantern light caught her face. "My name is Paloma Ibarra. Ring any bells?"

It rang bells. She watched them ring. She watched recognition move through the patrol like a current through water—the older soldiers stiffening, the young one with the matches actually stepping backward, one of them making a small sign against evil that her grandmother would have found hilarious or blasphemous, depending on the decade.

*Ibarra.* The name her family had paid for in blood for nine generations, and here it was, working like a curse word among the very people her family had bled to protect.

"They said the Ibarra line ended," Felix said slowly. "Thirty years ago. The priest said—"

"The priest," Paloma said, "burned a great many things thirty years ago. I'd be careful about trusting his inventory." She was swaying on her feet now, and she knew it, and she hated it. Three days. It came down to three days of no sleep and bad water, and her body was calling in its debts at the worst possible moment. She locked her knees and lifted her chin and put on the voice her guildmasters had taught her, the one that made apprentices stand straighter. "So. Are you going to take me to your elders, or are you going to leave me standing in a field while the things that followed me finish crossing that line?"

Felix looked at her for a long moment. Behind him, the broken gate-stone sat in its scar of dead earth, and beyond it, out in the starless country she'd come from, something at the edge of the lantern's reach shifted its weight and went still again.

"We're taking you in," Felix said at last. "Both of those options, actually. In that order."

"Fine," said Paloma.

"And if you try any of that—" he flexed the hand that had finished her ward, still staring at it like a stranger's— "*writing*, on me or mine, I'll break your fingers myself."

"Understood," said Paloma, who had absolutely already noted the exact angle of his wrist and the length of his reach, purely as a matter of professional habit.

They bound her hands anyway—rope, coarse and competent, tied by the young one while Felix watched—and set off along the dead seam toward the valley's heart. Paloma walked in the middle of them, exhausted past fear, and let herself catalog what she saw: soldiers moving in good order despite obvious terror; fields harvested early and badly; distant farmhouses with every lamp burning, hoarding light against the failing sky. A valley that had been told nothing and suspected everything.

And Felix Vasquez kept glancing at her. Not at her face—at her hands. At the rope. As if he were trying to work out whether he'd just captured the answer to a question he'd been asking his whole life without knowing it.

Paloma knew that look. She'd worn it in the guild tower for twenty years.

*Hold on,* she told herself, putting one foot in front of the other, following the dead seam east into the heart of a valley that had been built on her family's bones. *Sleep first. Then find the oldest stone. Then find out what really happened thirty years ago.*

Behind them, far back along the ash road, at the broken gate-stone, the dark crossed the line.

No one looked back to see it.
