## 4. Terms of Parley

The holding cell beneath the wardens' hall had been a root cellar once, back when the valley was small enough that its fears fit in the ground. Now it held one woman, one table, one chair, and Felix Vasquez, who had been sitting in it for the better part of an hour trying to decide which of them was the prisoner.

Paloma Ibarra sat with her wrists bound in front of her—not with rope but with warden's cord, gray-woven and threaded through with cold iron beads that the elders swore could hold anything that moved by craft rather than muscle. She had not struggled against them. She had not spoken since they'd brought her down from the ash road. She sat the way soldiers sat when they'd learned that stillness was a weapon: spine straight, hands loose in her lap, eyes tracking every sound past the door.

Felix set his cup of water on the table between them. She didn't look at it.

"You haven't drunk anything," he said. "In six hours."

"I'm not thirsty."

"You're dehydrated from the road. Your lips are cracked. You crossed forty miles of open country without starlight to guide you, which means you weren't drinking because you couldn't stop moving." He turned the cup a quarter-turn on the wood, a small pointless gesture, and hated himself for it. "I've done that march. I know what it costs."

Something flickered behind her eyes—recognition, maybe, or the exhaustion she'd been holding off her face like weather. "Then you know I'll drink when there's a reason to trust what's in the cup."

"It's water. I drew it myself."

"From your own well?"

"From the hall's cistern."

"And who salts the cistern?" She said it mildly, almost conversationally, as if asking after his mother's health. "Warden's halls keep truth-salts for interrogations. Everyone knows that. It doesn't kill you. It just makes you honest, and honesty is worse."

Felix looked at the cup. Then at her. Then at the cup again, because looking at her had started to feel like standing too close to the edge of the collapsed ward-line—there was something on the other side of her attention that pulled.

"I didn't salt it," he said.

"No," she agreed. "You didn't. But you thought about whether to tell me that, and the thinking took too long, so now I wait for the second cup." She leaned back against the stone wall, chains whispering. "You're new at this part. The talking part. You're good at the walking-around-a-man part—I saw how you took me down on the road, clean, no wasted motion—but this is a different trade."

"I've questioned prisoners before."

"Soldiers. Men who break fast and cry faster." Her gaze settled on him fully for the first time, and Felix felt it like a hand laid flat against his sternum. "I'm not a soldier, Warden Vasquez. I'm the thing your elders have been burning paper about for three days. So let's not pretend this is a parley between equals. Let's pretend instead that it's a parley between two people who both want something the other one has, and see how long we can be civil."

He should have gone for the door. He knew that even as he stayed seated. Every rule of his post, every oath stitched into him at fourteen years old beside a god who never answered, said that a prisoner who talked like this was a prisoner working an angle, and the correct response was distance, procedure, the flat voice and the ledger of questions.

Instead he pushed the cup aside and folded his hands on the table.

"What do you want from me?"

* * *

She studied him for a long moment, and Felix understood that whatever answer came next had been chosen—that everything she said would be chosen, weighed, priced—and that he was going to listen anyway.

"That's better," she said. "A direct question. Do you know how rare those are? In my guild, we had a saying: *a sigil is a question asked so precisely that reality has no choice but to answer.* Your elders ask nothing precisely. They ask vaguely, in the negative—*what are you, who sent you, what do you know*—and reality answers them the way reality answers vague questions. With fog."

"The elders aren't here."

"No. They're upstairs deciding whether to burn me or hand me to the priest, and the priest is deciding whether to burn me himself or send me somewhere worse, and none of them have asked me a single question worth answering." She tilted her head. "But you—you stood over me on the ash road with your blade drawn and you didn't strike. The others wanted to. I heard them. *Kill her before the dark-things come.* And you said no. Why?"

"Because dead women don't answer questions."

"Liar." She smiled without warmth. "Dead women answer questions all the time. Ask any necromancer. You spared me because when you put your hand on my shoulder to turn me around, you felt it. Didn't you."

The cellar went very quiet. Somewhere above them, boots crossed the floor of the wardens' hall; the iron-bead lamp in its niche guttered and steadied.

Felix said nothing.

"You felt it," Paloma repeated, softer now, and there was something almost gentle in it, which was somehow worse than the mockery. "The pull. Like a hook behind your breastbone. Twenty years of silence and then the stars go out and suddenly—" she made a small motion with her bound hands, a fisherman's gesture, hauling on an invisible line, "—something reeling. You didn't capture me, Warden. You were drawn to me. The question is whether you're man enough to admit you don't know why."

His heart was doing something he refused to name. He kept his face the way he'd been trained to keep it, the mercenary's mask, the warden's blankness, and he said, "Tell me what you know about the wards."

"Oh, excellent. Deflect by demanding. Very elder-like. They're teaching you well before they feed you to the same fire." But she shifted forward, and Felix saw the cost of the performance hit her all at once—the tremor in her fingers, the gray under her skin, the way her breath caught like a woman surfacing. Whatever she'd crossed to get here had taken more than miles. "Fine. The wards. You want to know what the wards were for. Here is what I know, and I'll give it to you free, because the first coin in any bargain should be real.

"Your valley sits inside a seal. Not a wall—a seal, cut in blood and ink and oath, four generations ago, by a covenant of seven families across two realms. The seal does two things. It keeps something *in*, and it keeps something *out*. Your elders will have told you it keeps out raiders and wild magic and the general wickedness of the world. That's the nursery version. The true version is that it keeps out the thing your god went silent to hide from."

Felix's mouth had gone dry. "My god."

"Your silent god. The one the whole valley prays to and gets nothing back. The one whose priests stopped hearing voices before your father was born, and whose absence everyone agreed, by slow conspiracy, to call *mystery* instead of *wound*." She watched him with terrible attentiveness. "I know why it's silent, Warden. I know what bought the silence. I know the price that was paid, and I know the price that wasn't, and I know that the second one is why your stars are dark right now."

"Say it plainly."

"No." She shook her head, and for the first time she looked tired in a way that had nothing to do with the road. "Not because I'm cruel. Because if I say it in this room, with these walls, under that lamp, it becomes their property. The elders will take it and twist it and burn more paper, and in a year it'll be a doctrine, and doctrines are how people like me end up in cellars like this one. I'll say it to someone who asks the right question. Precisely. Like a sigil."

"And what's the right question?"

"You already know it. You've known it since the night the stars went out and you woke up with your god's hand around your heart for the first time in twenty years." Her voice dropped. "Ask me what it wants."

* * *

He left the cell then. He told himself it was procedure—he needed to report, to confer, to eat, to do any of the hundred things that were not sitting alone in the dark with a chained woman who spoke about his god the way a daughter speaks about a father.

He climbed the stairs into the grey afternoon light of the wardens' hall and found it in chaos. Runners in and out. The map table buried under fresh reports from the border stations: ward-stones cracked at Harrow Ford, the eastern palisade glyphs gone black and inert, three shepherds missing from the high pasture and one found alive but mute, staring east. Sergeant Bray caught his sleeve near the hearth.

"Vasquez. The priest wants the prisoner moved tonight. Up to the temple annex. There's talk of—" Bray glanced around, lowered his voice, "—of a rite. A questioning rite. Old ones. The kind that leave marks."

"On whose authority?"

"High Priest Corvane's own seal. And before you make that face—the elders signed it. All but Elder Maren, and Maren's been shut out of the council chamber, which tells you where the wind's blowing." Bray was older than Felix, heavy through the shoulders, a man who had walked the border for thirty years and believed in it the way farmers believe in rain. "Look. I don't love it either. But she's a foreign sigilwright caught inside a fallen ward-line the same night the stars died. If half of what the priest says is true—"

"Is it? True?"

Bray's jaw worked. "I don't know what's true anymore, Felix. That's the trouble. Three days ago I knew exactly what was true. Now there's a hole in the sky and a woman in our cellar and the old men are burning books." He squeezed Felix's arm and let go. "Just—when they move her, don't be the one holding the lantern. Trust me. You don't want to see what the questioning rites look like up close. Some of us remember the last time the temple got hungry."

He walked away, and Felix stood by the hearth with the heat pressing against one side of his face and the cold of the stairwell still clinging to the other, and understood that he had perhaps an hour, maybe two, before the decision stopped being his.

* * *

He went back down. He told himself it was to warn her—to give her the choice to talk before the temple made talking unnecessary. That was even mostly true. But when the door closed behind him and the lamplight found her face again, turned toward him already as if she'd heard his boots on the stairs and counted them, Felix knew that the truest thing in him had nothing to do with warning anyone.

"They're moving you tonight," he said. "To the temple. There's a rite."

Paloma absorbed this without visible fear, which frightened him more than screaming would have. "The questioning rite. Iron and ash and the litany of unmaking. Yes. My guild had records of it—we kept records of everything your valley did, all the things you forgot on purpose." She flexed her bound hands. "It won't kill me. It'll do something worse. It'll open me, and then everything I know will belong to the priest, and the priest serves the covenant's corpse, not its purpose. After that, no one reforges anything. The dark stays. The Hollow spreads. Your god stays silent until it forgets there was ever a voice."

"Then give me something," Felix said, and heard his own voice crack on it, rougher than he intended. "Give me a reason to stand between you and that rite. Because right now I have a chain of command, an oath, thirty brothers who'll follow orders, and a prisoner who talks in riddles about things I've prayed to my whole life and gotten nothing back. Give me one solid thing, Paloma Ibarra, or step aside and let the world do what the world does."

She was quiet for a moment. When she answered, all the performance had drained out of her voice, and what remained was low and level and absolutely certain.

"Your god's name," she said, "is Vael. The Quiet One. And it isn't silent because it's dead, or sleeping, or deaf, or because your ancestors sinned. It is silent because it *chose* silence—chose it the way a soldier throws himself on a breach—because the thing it holds beneath your valley must not hear a single word spoken aloud, ever, or it wakes. Every prayer your people ever whispered was a knife at its throat. It endured twenty years of your worship in silence because telling you to stop would have meant speaking." Her eyes were wet, and furious, and fixed on him. "That is what I know, Felix Vasquez. Do you understand now why I crossed the ash road? Do you understand why I came looking—not for your elders, not for your priest, but for *you*?"

The lamplight guttered. In the walls overhead, faint as a heartbeat, the broken ward-stones hummed their dying note.

"How," Felix said, and his voice came out barely above a whisper, "do you know my name? I never gave it. The sergeant wrote the report. No one—"

"Because your name is written in the inkwell," Paloma said. "Alongside mine. Alongside every soul ever bound by the covenant. You were sworn to Vael as a child, Warden. Sworn in blood, sworn by rite, sworn so deep that when the binding cracked three nights ago, the covenant reached for its own—and you felt it. You've felt it every hour since. Don't tell me you haven't."

He hadn't told anyone. He hadn't told Bray, hadn't told the elders, hadn't told the priest who had laid a thumb on his forehead at his swearing and pronounced him *sealed and serviceable*. The pull behind his breastbone, the hook, the line going taut every time he came within ten feet of this woman—he had filed it under duty, under instinct, under anything but the truth.

"You came looking for me," he said slowly. "Specifically. Me."

"I came looking for the covenant's last living anchor outside my own bloodline. The inkwell says there's exactly one." She held his gaze. "The rite that broke was supposed to be renewed by two hands, Warden. An Ibarra and a Vasquez. That was always the design. My family paid the blood; yours paid the silence. Four generations, and then my guild tried to do it alone, and it failed, and the failure is falling on your stars right now." She exhaled. "So yes. I came for you. And now your priest is coming for me, and in about an hour he'll come for you too, because once he reads the old registers he'll know what you are. So the only question left—the precise question, the sigil-question—is: which side of this are you standing on when the door opens?"

Felix looked at her for a long time. Behind his ribs, the hook pulled, patient as tide.

Above them, boots on the stairs—two sets, heavy, unhurried, the tread of men carrying a sealed order.

"I was never here," Felix said, and blew out the lamp.

* * *

Darkness took the cellar whole. He heard Paloma shift, heard the soft scrape of the warden's cord against stone—heard, beneath it, a whisper of movement that was not her, a dry sliding at the seam of the door, like something testing the gap. His hand found his knife. The sliding stopped.

In the black, her voice came close, barely air: "There's a grate behind the third wine rack. It opens onto the drainage culvert, and the culvert runs under the old wall to the river. Your elders sealed it eighty years ago and forgot to tell anyone. I read the building stones on my way in." A pause. "Well, Warden. You wanted a reason to stand between me and the rite. This is it. Choose fast."

The boots reached the landing. A key ground in the lock.

Felix put his shoulder to the wine rack.
