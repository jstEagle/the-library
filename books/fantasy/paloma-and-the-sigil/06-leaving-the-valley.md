## 6. Leaving the Valley

They came for her at the hour when the valley's lamps were lit, and Felix was the one who opened the door.

He had told himself it would be a simple thing. Keep her in the cellar of the waystation, keep his mouth shut at muster, keep his eyes down when High Priest Corvane's men—no, not Corvane, that was the city; the priest here was Father Aldous, a soft-bellied man with ink-stained fingers and a voice like warm oil—keep his eyes down when Aldous's men came asking questions. Three keeps. He had kept harder vows with less effort.

But the men who came up the ash road that evening were not asking questions.

There were six of them, and they moved wrong for priests' retainers. They moved like butchers entering a pen they already owned. Their leader was a lean man with a birthmark like a spilled candle down one cheek, and he carried not a censer or a scroll case but a length of iron rod wrapped in leather at the grip—the kind of tool used to break wards, or wrists, whichever came first.

Felix stood in the doorway of the waystation with the last light of the lamp behind him and did the arithmetic he had been avoiding for two days.

"You're Vasquez," the marked man said. "The patrol captain who caught the witch on the road."

"She's not a witch."

"Father Aldous says she is." The man smiled without warmth. "Father Aldous says a great many things since the stars went out. People believe him now. Fear makes them generous with their believing." He tilted his head toward the door. "Step aside, soldier. This is covenant work."

*Covenant work.* The words landed in Felix's chest like a stone dropped down a well. He had served the silent god for twenty years. He had knelt in the shrine-house every seventh day and felt nothing come back, and had called that nothing *faith*. And these men—these men with their iron rods and their butcher's walk—wore the same sunburst sigil he wore stitched into his collar, and used it as a license.

"The prisoner is under patrol authority," Felix said. "Interrogation orders came from Captain Reyes. If Father Aldous wants her transferred, he can bring papers."

"Papers." The man laughed, and his five companions laughed with him, the way men laugh when the joke is how long they intend to let you pretend. "The stars are dead, Vasquez. The wards are broken glass. There are no papers anymore. There's just what strong hands do before winter." He lifted the iron rod. "Last time I ask nice."

Felix had spent twenty years learning exactly one discipline: how to stand still while something inside him screamed. He had stood still through the silence of his god. Through the burning of the records. Through the elders' faces going smooth as river stones whenever anyone asked what the wards had actually held back. Standing still was the whole of his craft, honed finer than any blade-work.

He stepped aside.

For half a heartbeat the marked man thought he had won, and the triumph loosened him—and in that half-heartbeat Felix hit him in the throat with the edge of his hand, took the iron rod out of the air as the man folded, and put its owner between himself and the second man's drawn knife.

What followed was short, ugly, and entirely familiar. Felix had fought in the border skirmishes before the wards went quiet, and then in the tavern brawls after, and his body knew this work the way a plow-horse knows furrows. Two of them he dropped hard enough to stay dropped. One got the rod across the forearm and lost his taste for the evening. The marked man, wheezing on the floorboards, fumbled something from his coat—a whistle, bone-white, carved with a spiral—and Felix stamped it into splinters before it touched the man's lips.

Then it was over, and six men of the priesthood lay groaning in the lamplight, and Felix Vasquez stood among them holding a ward-breaker's rod, and understood with terrible clarity that there was no longer any version of the valley that contained him.

"They'll hang you," said a voice from the stairwell.

Paloma Ibarra stood on the third step with her hands bound in front of her—Felix had tied them himself, gently, two days ago, another small treachery against his own conscience—and she looked at the wreckage of the priest's men with an expression that was not gratitude. It was closer to grief. As if she had seen this exact scene before, in some other valley, with some other fool soldier, and knew how it ended.

"Yes," Felix said. "Probably."

"You could give me to them. Say I overpowered you. Say you fought bravely." Her voice was flat, exhausted, the voice of someone reciting a eulogy she'd attended too many times. "It might even be true by morning. I'm tired, mercenary. I've been running since the stars died. Maybe hanging is just a shorter road than the one I'm on."

Felix looked at her—this woman who had crossed a dying country alone, who spoke about his god like a scholar speaks of a wound, who had looked at him in the interrogation room with those dark, level eyes and said, *You came looking for me, didn't you? No. I came looking for you*—and he found that the arithmetic had changed somewhere without his permission.

"No," he said, and cut her bonds.

* * *

They left within the hour, because staying past the hour was death and staying past dawn was worse. Felix took what a deserter takes: his sword, his cloak, three days of bread, the waterskin, and a coil of good rope. He stood a moment in the armory with his hand on the shrine-box where his oath-medallion lived, the little tin sunburst he'd sworn on at nine years old with blood from a cut thumb, and in the end he took that too—not to wear, but because leaving it felt like leaving a piece of his own skeleton in a drawer.

Paloma watched him do it and said nothing, which was its own kind of mercy.

The valley at night, without stars, was a country of the drowned. Every path Felix had walked since boyhood had become unfamiliar, lit only by the sickly orange of the few lamps whose oil hadn't run out, each circle of light an island, and between the islands a darkness so complete it seemed to press against the skin. They heard dogs barking in the farmsteads. They heard, once, distantly, a bell ringing the alarm at the shrine-house—Aldous's men found, the hunt declared—and Paloma flinched at the sound as if the bell had struck her physically.

"They'll send riders to the ward line," Felix said. "All the crossings. But—" He stopped, because the next part of the sentence was treason, and saying it aloud made it real.

"But the wards are already broken," Paloma finished for him. "So there's no line to guard. Only a memory of one." She walked a few paces in silence. "Your elders will post guards at the memory anyway. Men die readily for memories. It's cheaper than thinking."

"You talk about them like you hate them."

"I don't hate anyone enough for that." She pulled her patched cloak tighter. "Hate requires attention. I simply know what they did. My family kept the other side of your valley's bargain for eleven generations, Vasquez. I know what your elders bought, and what my family paid, and neither ledger balances."

Felix wanted to ask. The questions had been building in him for days like water behind a dam—*what covenant, what payment, why does my god sleep, why did the fragment in the fire name the Ibarras*—but the night was for walking, and the darkness had teeth, and so he asked nothing and saved it all up, the way he'd saved up everything else for twenty years.

They reached the ward line an hour before dawn.

* * *

Felix had grown up inside the wards the way a fish grows up inside water: totally, invisibly, without once knowing what the water was. The border was a ridge of grey granite crowned with leaning standing-stones, and every child in the valley was taught that the stones were old, and holy, and not to be touched. Beyond the ridge the land fell away into the lowlands—other people's country, foreign and vaguely shameful, the place you did not go.

Now he stood on the ridge and saw what the wards had actually been, because he saw what their absence was.

The darkness beyond the stones was different. Not deeper—*thinner*. Inside the valley, the blackness after the stars died had a texture to it, a woven quality, like heavy cloth. Here the cloth had been cut away. The dark beyond the ridge moved. It drifted in slow currents like smoke underwater, and where it drifted, the world behind it seemed to... hesitate. A stunted pine on the far slope appeared and unappeared, like a thing being argued with.

"That's what's left of the working," Paloma said quietly. She had gone very still, and her voice had changed—become the voice she'd used in the interrogation room, the scholar's voice, precise and afraid. "Eleven generations of maintenance, unraveling. What you're seeing is the edges fraying. Give it a season and the whole weave goes, and then the dark won't drift. It will pour."

"What's in it?"

"I don't know. That's the truth, whatever else I've hidden from you. My family guarded the *sigil*, not the thing beneath it. We were lockmakers, Vasquez. Nobody asks the lockmaker what the thief looks like." She turned to him. In the faint grey pre-light her face was hollowed and grim. "Once we cross, magic will touch you. Real magic, unfiltered, no ward between it and your skin. Valley-born are... sheltered. Like a man who's never been in weather stepping into a storm. Some take it fine. Some don't."

"And if I don't?"

"Then I'll drag your body to the first village and tell them I found a handsome idiot frozen on the road." But her hand found his sleeve, briefly, and gripped it. "Stay behind me until it passes. Whatever happens—whatever you see, or hear, or feel—do not answer it. Nothing out here should get your name."

Felix looked back, once, at the valley.

The lamps of the waystations strung along the road like a rosary. The shrine-house bell tower, a black finger against the lesser blackness. The fields where he'd worked as a boy, the barracks where he'd slept for twenty years, the whole enclosed, lamplit, starless world that had been the entire sky of his life. Somewhere in it, Father Aldous was writing his name on a list of traitors. Somewhere in it, Captain Reyes was deciding whether to grieve him or forget him. Somewhere in it, the scorched fragment of record lay in the ashes of the burned archive, naming a sigil and a family, and the family was walking beside him into exile.

His god was in there too. Silent. Sleeping. Twenty years of silence, and Felix had never once thought to wonder whether the silence was peace or a gag.

"Goodbye," he said—to the valley, to the life, to the boy with the cut thumb—and stepped over the fallen line.

* * *

The world broke him open like an egg.

Later, he would have no clean memory of it—only fragments, the way a man remembers a shipwreck. He remembered that the first sensation was sound: a chord, vast and discordant, everywhere at once, as if every stone and stream and root of the lowland country were humming different notes of a song that had never been meant for unaugmented ears. He remembered falling to his knees on the near slope, the rope and bread spilling from his pack, his hands digging into soil that hummed against his palms like a struck bell.

He remembered seeing color that wasn't there. Threads of it, rising off the land like heat-shimmer—green-gold off the grass, deep blue out of the earth itself, and threading through all of it, thin as spider silk, lines of silver that ran in patterns too regular to be natural. Sigils, he realized dimly. Old ones. The whole lowland country was written on, under everything, the way a page is written on under the ink.

And underneath the song, underneath the colors, something noticed him.

It was not the god. He knew his god's weight the way he knew his own heartbeat, even silent—even *especially* silent; you learn the shape of a missing tooth with your tongue. This was other. Vaster and colder and more curious, a pressure turning toward him through the dark like the eye of something at the bottom of a well, and it reached for him—

—and a hand seized his collar, wrenched his head around, and Paloma's voice cracked against his ear like a whip:

"*Don't answer it!*"

She had chalk in her hand—he never learned where she kept it—and she drew on the ground beside him faster than he could follow, four strokes and a closing curve, and pressed his bleeding palm (when had he started bleeding?) into the center of it. The mark flared white. The pressure recoiled, hissing, offended, and withdrew into the general roar of the world like a wave retreating down a beach.

Then the storm passed through him and out, and Felix Vasquez lay face-down on foreign soil, shaking, sobbing without quite knowing it, alive.

* * *

When he could speak, which took a while, he said: "That was—"

"Don't." Paloma sat beside him, chalk dust to her elbows, watching the treeline with the wariness of a woman who has done this vigil before. "Whatever word you reach for first—terrible, wonderful—it'll be wrong, and you'll build years of mistakes on top of it. Just breathe. Then drink. Then walk."

He breathed. He drank. His hands would not stop trembling, and he stopped trying to make them.

Because the truth was that both words had been right. It had been terrible, the pressure of that noticing thing, the sense of standing naked in a room full of eyes. And it had been—there was no other honest word for it—*wonderful*. Twenty years inside the wards, twenty years of a god who gave nothing back, twenty years of a world with the volume turned to zero, and now the world roared. Even flattened on his belly in the dirt, Felix could feel it: the grass singing its green-gold note, the buried silver script pulsing slow as a sleeper's heart, the enormous unfinished symphony of a country that was *alive* all the way down. Terror and exhilaration, braided so tightly he couldn't have cut them apart with a sword.

"So this is what it's like," he managed. "Outside. All the time."

"This?" Paloma almost smiled, and it transformed her face, made her look young and unbearably tired at once. "This is a whisper. This is the world with the lights mostly out, holding its breath. You should have heard it before the stars died." Something moved through her expression then, quick and painful, and was gone. "Everyone should have heard it before."

Felix sat up. The eastern horizon had gone the grey of old ash—dawn coming, though dawn without a sun worth the name, only a bruised brightening. Below them the lowlands rolled away farther than anything in the valley ever had: woods, rivers, the smoke of distant villages, and beyond it all, hazy on the edge of sight, a smudge of mountains that might have been the forge-peak Paloma refused yet to speak of.

Somewhere out there were answers. Somewhere out there were the things that walked without light, and the refugees, and the ruins of a covenant written in his god's silence and her family's blood. Somewhere out there, very possibly, were riders from the valley who wanted his head for what he'd done to six of the priesthood.

He found that none of it frightened him as much as the thought of going back did. That was the discovery that undid him, lying in the wet grass of a foreign country with his hands still shaking: the valley had become, in a single night, the smaller of the two worlds.

"We should move," Paloma said, rising. "Daylight helps, but it doesn't protect. And there's a caravan road maybe ten miles south—refugees, if the rumors hold. We need food, news, and people around us who aren't hunting us." She paused, looking down at him, and for a moment the defiance dropped and he saw the exhaustion underneath, plain as a wound. "You can still turn around, Vasquez. The ridge is right there. Go home, throw yourself on Aldous's mercy, take the lash and the penance and keep your life. I'd think no less of—"

"I deserted," Felix said, getting to his feet. His legs held. His voice held. "I struck priests of my own order and I deserted, and the medallion's in my pocket, and I'm not giving it back to them. I'm giving it—" He stopped, surprised by his own honesty. "I don't know who I'm giving it to. But it isn't them."

Paloma studied him for a long moment. Then she nodded, once, the way soldiers nod, and turned south, and they walked down out of the hills together into the wide, dim, singing world.

Behind them, on the ridge, the fraying edge of eleven generations of work shivered in the windless dark.

And ahead of them, far to the south along the caravan road, something answered the morning with a sound that was almost—but not quite—a bell.
