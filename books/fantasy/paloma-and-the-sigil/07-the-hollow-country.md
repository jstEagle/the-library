## 7. The Hollow Country

The world outside the valley was dying by inches, and it did so politely, the way a hearth fire dies when no one remembers to feed it.

Paloma had known what starlessness would mean in the abstract—she had written treatises on it, or rather her mother had written them and Paloma had copied them out as a girl, ink-stained and bored, memorizing principles she never expected to see enacted. *Light is not merely light,* the old sigilwrights had taught. *It is permission.* The stars were the covenant's outward face, the visible half of an agreement written in blood and geometry, and every living thing below them had arranged its life around that permission without ever knowing it existed.

She had understood all of this the way one understands arithmetic.

Walking through it was another matter entirely.

Three days past the fallen ward line, the dimming had become a presence. It was not dark—not yet. The sun still rose, though it rose the color of pewter and gave off warmth the way a stone gives off warmth at evening: residual, apologetic, already leaving. The sky above them was a ceiling of unbroken grey, and beneath it the land had begun to forget itself.

"Look at the wheat," Felix said. He said little on the road—he was a man who rationed words like a besieged garrison rations grain—but he stopped now, crouched at the edge of a field that should have been heavy with late-summer gold.

Paloma looked. The stalks stood upright, green at the root, but the heads were empty. Not blighted, not withered—*empty*, as if the plants had grown to the exact shape of harvest and then simply declined to fill it. She pulled one and split the husk with her thumbnail. Inside there was nothing but a pale hollow, smooth as a seed of chalk.

"No blight," she murmured. "No rot. It just—" She turned the husk over. "It didn't finish."

Felix rose, brushing dirt from his knee. "Fields don't get tired."

"Everything gets tired." She let the husk fall. "You've just never seen anything tired enough to stop mid-sentence."

They walked on. Behind them, the valley's broken border lay like a scar across the hills—the shattered ward-line still faintly visible to her trained eye, a seam of dead geometry glowing dull copper whenever the grey light caught it right. Ahead, the country opened into lowlands she had crossed once before, years ago, fleeing in the other direction with nothing but her mother's grimoire sewn into the lining of her coat. She remembered the road then: loud with cart traffic, lined with shrines, every milestone carved with the small protective sigils that free sigilwrights sold for a copper and a blessing.

Now the milestones stood bare. Someone had chiseled the sigils off. All of them, all along the road, hacked away with such violence that two of the stones had been toppled entirely.

"They're afraid of it," Felix said, following her gaze.

"People are always afraid of the wrong things first."

"You think they're wrong?"

Paloma considered the gouged stone, the pale scars where careful lines had been. "I think," she said slowly, "that whoever did this believes the sigils *caused* the darkness. And I think that belief will keep them alive about a week longer than the truth will."

He looked at her then—a long look, the kind he rarely gave, his dark eyes narrowing slightly under the brim of his travel hood. He had been watching her more often since the ward line, since the moment the outside world's magic had poured into him like water into a cracked vessel and nearly stopped his heart. He hadn't spoken of it. Neither had she. But she had felt him flinch from every hedgerow since, the way a man who has been struck by lightning flinches from weather.

"The truth being?" he asked.

"That the stars went out because something very old stopped holding up its end of a bargain. And that no amount of chiseling stones will renegotiate it."

*And that my family's name is on the other side of that bargain,* she did not say. The words sat behind her teeth like a stone she had learned to carry. Four days she had known this mercenary. Four days since his patrol had found her on the ash road, since the interrogation that had somehow become a parley, since she had told him—because she'd had no choice, because the wards were falling and the god beneath the valley was stirring and someone in that valley needed to understand what was coming—that the covenant binding his silent deity had been cut by Ibarra hands, generation after generation, each one paying in blood.

She had not told him everything. She had told him the shape of the thing without its weight. That was a sigilwright's habit, she supposed: you showed people the diagram, never the knife.

A crow followed them for most of the morning. That was wrong too—crows should have been everywhere in harvested country, fat and insolent—but this one flew alone, and it did not caw. It circled twice overhead and then landed on a fencepost ahead of them and simply watched them pass, head tilted, one eye catching the pewter light like a drop of tar.

"Beasts are going strange," Paloma said quietly, when the bird finally lifted away. "I've been counting. Fewer birds each day. The ones left don't sing."

"What do they do?"

"Watch."

Felix's hand drifted toward the hatchet at his belt—an old habit, she was learning—and then fell away again. "Wolves?"

"Worse, probably. Wolves at least stay wolves." She thought of the report she'd heard in a border town two nights past, bought with the last of her coin: livestock found unharmed but standing in their pens in perfect circles, facing inward, silent, until they starved. A shepherd who had walked into the hills after his missing flock and come back three days later unable to say his own name, though he could still count, endlessly, patiently, to some number only he knew. "Things without starlight lose the thread of what they are. Animals feel it first because they never had words to hold on to."

"And us?"

She glanced at him. His face, in the flat light, might have been carved from the same grey as the sky.

"We have words," she said. "We'll last longer. Whether that's mercy or cruelty, I haven't decided."

* * *

They came upon the caravan at dusk on the fourth day, and Paloma smelled it before she saw it: woodsmoke, wet wool, dung, and underneath it all the particular sourness of unwashed fear, which has a smell the way grief has a weight.

The road bent around a low hill, and there in the fold of the land sprawled what had been, perhaps a week ago, a market train—merchants and pilgrims and farm families moving between towns—and had become a refugee column. Dozens of carts, some drawn by oxen, some by hand. Cook-fires in a ragged line. Tents of sailcloth and blanket. And people, hundreds of people, huddled in the grey dusk like moths that had forgotten the candle was gone.

"We could go around," Felix said.

"We need food. You need—" she almost said *medicine*, and stopped herself. What he needed had no name yet. "We need information. Where the roads stand, where the cults are gathering, whether Corvane's gates are open. Caravans know everything."

"Caravans also remember strangers."

"Then we'll be memorable in a useful way." She adjusted the collar of her coat, hiding the faded guild-marks stitched at the cuff—her one vanity, once; now a death sentence if the wrong eyes saw it. "Stay close. Let me talk."

The caravan's outer ring was picketed with carts lashed wheel to wheel, and the watch they kept was less a watch than a collective flinch—men and women with billhooks and kitchen knives staring out into the dimming fields as if the darkness itself might grow legs. One of them, a broad woman with a butcher's forearms and a voice like gravel in a sack, stepped into their path before they were within thirty paces.

"State your business and your town."

"Travelers," Paloma said. "From the east. We've food-craft and blade-craft to trade for a place at your fires tonight."

The woman's eyes went over them both—Paloma's worn coat, Felix's soldier's bearing, the weapons he made no effort to hide. "Blade-craft we have. Food-craft we'd take. But nobody joins the column without answering for themselves. Where's home?"

"Away," Paloma said. It was true enough. Home was a word that had been taken from her along with everything else, five years ago, in a single night of fire and guild politics.

The woman spat. "Everyone's from away now. Fine. But you walk the middle rows, not the edges, and if either of you goes near the wagons after full dark, the column judges you. We don't wait for magistrates anymore. Magistrates are a day's ride behind us and getting further."

"Judged how?" Felix asked. His voice was even, but Paloma heard the tension coiled under it, the mercenary reading the camp the way he read all terrain—for exits, for threats, for the exact point where order would fail.

The woman looked at him with something like pity. "However we have to."

Inside the ring, the caravan was a study in organized desperation. Fires burned in shallow pits because smoke was cheaper than lantern oil and nobody trusted flame that cast shadows upward anymore—there had been stories, Paloma gathered, listening to the camp's murmur, of lights behaving badly since the stars went out. Children were kept in the center of the camp, in a corral of overturned carts, and the sight of them—pale, quiet, playing a game that involved no running and almost no sound—tightened something in her chest that she had thought long since cauterized.

An old man by the second fire was telling a story, and a crowd had gathered because a story was the cheapest warmth available. Paloma slowed to listen, and Felix, obediently or willingly, slowed with her.

"—and my grandfather saw it, mind, with his own eyes, the night the comet passed. Said the sky opened like a book and something looked out. That's why the old families paid the tithe, you understand. Paid it regular, generation on generation, to the warden families. Blood-tithe, some called it." He lowered his voice, and the crowd leaned in. "My grandmother served in a great house down south. House Ibarra. She said the lady of the house would go up to the tower room certain nights of the year, and the whole household would hear singing that wasn't singing, and in the morning the lady would be white as flour and there'd be fresh-cut sigils on the tower floor, and the crops came in fine, and everyone agreed not to ask."

The crowd murmured. Someone laughed nervously. An older woman near the front crossed herself with a gesture Paloma didn't recognize—three fingers, then a fist against the sternum, an old style, pre-guild.

"And now?" the nervous laugher asked. "Now the stars are out. So the tithing failed? The Ibarra line broke it?"

The storyteller spread his hands. "Or the Ibarra line *ended*. Either way—" and here his voice dropped into the register of men who enjoy delivering doom, "—either way, whatever was being fed all those centuries isn't being fed now. And things that get hungry after a long fast don't stay polite."

Paloma stood very still. Felix, beside her, had gone motionless in a different way—the stillness of a man listening with his whole body. She felt his attention shift to her, deliberate, unhurried, and return to the fire. He said nothing. He had said nothing since the ash road, not about the name, not about what it meant that the woman he'd captured carried it. But he had started doing this—this quiet turning toward her whenever her family surfaced in the world's conversation, like a compass needle finding north.

*Ibarra,* the crowd whispered, tasting the word. Some of them spat after it. One young man, hollow-cheeked with hunger, said loudly, "My cousin swears the wardens ate the tithe-blood themselves. Kept themselves alive off it while the rest of us farmed. That's why the magic died—greed at the top, same as always."

"That's not—" Paloma began, and stopped, because what could she possibly say? *That's not how it worked* was true and useless. *That's exactly how it must have looked* was truer and worse.

Felix's shoulder pressed briefly against hers. It might have been accidental. In four days she had learned that almost nothing he did was accidental.

They moved on, and bartered for stew—barley and a stringy fowl, traded against Felix's whetstone skills and Paloma's honest claim to herb-craft, which got her put to work almost immediately in the caravan's sick-wagon, and which she regretted within the hour.

* * *

The sick-wagon was a covered cart that smelled of vinegar and despair, and it held eleven patients, and Paloma could help perhaps three of them.

Not because their ailments were beyond her. Because their ailments were not ailments.

A farmer's wife lay in the corner, sleeping twenty hours a day, waking only to murmur apologies—to whom, no one knew. A boy of maybe nine had stopped speaking entirely but had begun arranging pebbles into patterns Paloma recognized with a cold shock: simplified ward-geometry, the defensive circles from the first-year primers, laid out instinctively by a child who had never seen a primer in his life. When she knelt beside him, he looked up with eyes too old and pushed the pebbles toward her, as if asking her to check his work.

"You're feeling it too," she whispered to him. "The absence. Like a tooth gone from the jaw."

His expression didn't change, but his hand crept out and gripped her sleeve, and held on.

The third case frightened her most. A drover, arms bound to the wagon frame by his own brothers, eyes open and fixed on the canvas roof, counting. She heard it as she leaned close: a dry whisper, patient as erosion. Numbers climbing without hurry toward some total only he knew. His lips were cracked, his voice nearly gone, and still he counted.

"He started two days ago," one of the brothers said. He was a big man gone soft-eyed with helplessness. "After we passed the standing stones. The ones with the carvings knocked off. He got down off the cart and touched one of them, just touched it, and came back wrong."

Paloma examined the drover's eyes. The pupils responded to light, but slowly, reluctantly, like a tenant answering a knock at a door he no longer wished to answer. She had seen this before—in miniature, in the border towns, in animals. The world's creatures had lived their whole lives inside the covenant's shelter without knowing it, the way fish live in water, and now the water was draining, and everything that lived in it was gasping in ways it couldn't name.

"It's not sickness," she told the brother, because he deserved truth even if it was cruel. "It's the dark. The real dark, underneath the sky-dark. Your brother brushed against something that lives in it, or something in it noticed him. Keep him warm. Talk to him. Say his name often. Names are—" *anchors,* she almost said, *names are the oldest sigil there is,* but the guild had forbidden that teaching to outsiders generations ago, and old habits die hard even in exiles. "Names help," she finished.

The brother nodded and turned to his ruined sibling and began, in a rough, tender voice, to say his name over and over, like a prayer, like a rope thrown into deep water.

Paloma climbed down from the wagon with her hands shaking slightly, and found Felix waiting, and was grateful—furiously, unexpectedly grateful—for the solid fact of him.

"Well?" he asked.

"They're all going to be like this eventually. Everyone. Maybe slower, maybe faster. The covenant wasn't just walls and wards, Felix. It was a hum under the world, and everything alive tuned itself to it without knowing. Now the hum's stopped, and the silence underneath is—" She searched for the word and hated the one she found. "*Attentive.*"

"Attentive," he repeated flatly.

"Like something listening through the floor."

He absorbed that the way he absorbed everything, silently, storing it somewhere behind those dark eyes. Then he tilted his head a fraction, toward the center of the camp. "There's a meeting," he said. "Council of the cart-masters. They're deciding something. The butcher-woman tried to keep voices down, but walls of canvas are thin."

Paloma followed his glance. Around the largest fire, a knot of figures stood close together—the caravan's de facto leadership, heads bent. And apart from them, at the edge of the firelight, stood others. Watching. Men with the particular hardness of those who have decided that fear is a resource to be spent rather than suffered.

"I don't like the shape of that," Felix said.

"No," Paloma agreed. "Neither do I."

* * *

The trouble began an hour past full dark, and it began, as such things do, with a scapegoat.

They found her in the third row of carts: a traveling pot-mender, a widow named Sera Voss, whom Paloma had spoken with briefly at supper—a tired, funny woman who had shown Paloma a locket with her daughter's portrait and joked that at least the dark hid the grey in her hair. Now Sera Voss stood tied to a cart-wheel while a ring of torches—real torches, the first open flames Paloma had seen the caravan permit—burned around her, and a man Paloma recognized as one of the watchers from the council-fire stood before the assembled camp shouting.

"She had it hidden in her mending-basket!" the man cried. "Under the false bottom! Sigil-work! Charms! Marked stones! While our children go hungry and our beasts go mad, this woman has been carrying the very craft that killed the sky!"

"That's nonsense," Paloma breathed.

"It's *strategy*," Felix said grimly. "Look. They're not looking at her. They're looking at each other, checking who agrees. This isn't justice. It's a vote."

Sera Voss was talking—pleading, her voice carrying thin and cracked across the firelit space. "Mending-charms! Every pot-mender carries them, they keep solder true, they're nothing, they're *kitchen-work*—"

"Kitchen-work," the accuser spat. "Sigil-work. And where did the sigil-work get us? My father paid the tithe his whole life, and where are the stars? The warden families bled us dry for centuries with their marks and their mysteries, and the moment we needed them, what did they give us?" He flung his arm wide at the starless sky, and the crowd roared—not agreement exactly, but hunger, the terrible appetite of frightened people for something to punish.

Paloma's feet were moving before she decided to move them. Felix's hand closed on her arm—quick, strong, precise.

"Don't," he said.

"They're going to kill her for a soldering charm."

"Yes."

"Felix—"

"If you step into that firelight and defend sigil-craft, you will be standing next to her in ten minutes. And I will have to choose between pulling you out and revealing what I can do, and neither choice keeps us walking tomorrow." His grip didn't tighten; it didn't need to. His voice dropped low, urgent, stripped of everything but the arithmetic. "Paloma. Look at me. There are four hundred of them and two of us, and the thing they're afraid of is wearing your family's name."

She looked at him. His face was close in the darkness, and for once there was nothing guarded in it—only the cold, clear, terrible honesty of a man who had done this calculus before, in worse places, and survived it.

"You could live with that?" she whispered. "Standing here?"

Something moved behind his eyes—something that had nothing to do with tactics. "No," he said. "But I could live with losing you, which I couldn't do the other way. So do the arithmetic with me and hate it, and we'll find another way."

For a heartbeat she stood in the pull of two currents: the old Paloma, the Ibarra heir, who had been raised on the principle that the covenant's whole meaning was *protection of the unprotected*, who had lost everything for refusing to let the guild turn protection into slaughter; and the new Paloma, the exile, who had learned that martyrs solve nothing and dead sigilwrights recut no covenants.

Then the crowd surged, and the choice was taken out of both their hands.

Because Sera Voss, tied to the wheel, stopped pleading—and laughed.

It was not a mad laugh, or not only. It had a wire of clarity in it. "You fools," the pot-mender said, and her voice rang out over the mob with sudden, astonishing force. "You poor, blind fools. You think the marks killed the sky? The marks were the *bandage*. My grandmother mended pots in a warden house—Ibarra house, yes, go on, spit—and I'll tell you what she saw, since none of you will listen to anything else tonight. She saw the ladies of that house go up to the tower and come down gray-faced and bleeding, year after year, generation after generation, paying *their own blood* to keep the dark off fields like yours. And when the payments stopped—" Sera Voss's eyes swept the crowd, blazing, "—it wasn't the wardens who stopped them. Ask yourselves who profited. Ask who wanted the old bargain broken. Ask why the ones who ended it aren't here to be blamed!"

A ripple went through the crowd. Doubt, Paloma thought. Actual doubt. The mob's momentum stuttered—

And then the torch nearest the cart-wheel guttered, all at once, as if a hand had passed through it, and every shadow in the camp leaned the same direction, and the counting-drover in the sick-wagon, half a camp away, was heard by eleven witnesses to suddenly stop counting and begin, in a voice like a landslide, to scream.

The panic that followed was not a riot. Riots have targets. This was a stampede—people running in every direction, carts overturning, children shrieking in the corral, the butcher-woman's voice booming orders that dissolved into the noise. In the chaos, someone cut Sera Voss loose—or she cut herself loose; Paloma never learned which—and the pot-mender vanished into the dark between the wagons, and the mob, deprived of its sacrifice, turned on itself in smaller cruelties: accusations, scuffles, a cart set alight that three men fought over whether to save or strip.

Felix moved through it like a current through water, and Paloma stayed glued to his back, and together they reached the corral where the children huddled, and Felix—wordless, efficient—began righting the overturned cart that formed its wall while Paloma gathered the nearest children behind her and did the only magic she had left that cost nothing: she spoke to them, steadily, saying their names as their mothers gasped them out to her, name after name, an anchor-chain of names, until the small pale faces stopped staring at the shadows and started staring at her instead.

Later—much later, when the fires had been rebuilt and the camp had exhausted itself into a bruised, ashamed quiet—she and Felix sat at the edge of the firelight, sharing the last of the barley bread neither of them tasted.

"They'll do it again," Felix said. "Tomorrow or the day after. Next time they'll find someone whose basket holds something worse than soldering charms." He turned the bread over in his fingers. "Fear doesn't burn out. It just changes fuel."

"I know." Paloma stared into the flames. Somewhere behind them, the sick-wagon was quiet again; the drover had stopped screaming as abruptly as he'd started, and no one wanted to look too closely at what that meant. "That's the cost, Felix. This is what it looks like. Not monsters from the sky—monsters are almost a relief. This. Ordinary people eating each other because the old bargain held the floor up and nobody knew the floor was a bargain at all." Her voice came out lower than she intended. "Four hundred years my family paid so that no one would ever have to know. And the payment was hideous, and the secrecy was worse, and I have hated both my whole life. But I never doubted the *why* of it. Until tonight I don't think I understood it."

Felix was quiet a long moment. When he spoke, his voice had an odd quality—careful, almost gentle, which she had never heard from him. "Your family paid," he said. "Who collected?"

The question hung between them like a spark.

Before she could decide whether it was accusation or something else, a commotion rose at the camp's eastern edge—one of the night-watch stumbling in from the darkness, waving his arms, shouting words that emptied the fires of listeners in seconds:

"Lights! Lights on the road—east road—a whole line of them, coming slow—and they're singing, gods help us, they're *singing*—"

Paloma was on her feet. Beyond the picket line, far out on the black plain, a procession of small golden flames was winding toward the caravan through the starless dark, dozens of them, moving in perfect, unhurried order. And drifting ahead of the flames on the windless air came the sound the watchman had named: voices, many voices, singing in unison a melody she had not heard since childhood—since the tower room, since her mother's arms, since the last night of her old life.

She knew that song. Every Ibarra child knew that song.

It was the covenant's lullaby. And it had not been sung aloud in five years.

Beside her, Felix had gone rigid, his eyes fixed on the approaching lights, and when he spoke his voice was barely a breath.

"Paloma. Who walks with the old songs?"

She didn't answer. She was already counting the lights, and her mouth had gone dry, because there were seven of them, and seven was the number of the guild's ruling circle, and the guild had sworn her death.

"They're not here for the refugees," she said.
