## 8. First Fragment: The Oathstone

The drowned chapel announced itself by smell before sight—old stone and river silt, the mineral breath of a place that had been holding its breath underwater for a hundred years. Paloma smelled it through the gray morning fog as they came down off the ridge, and her stomach tightened with a recognition she had not felt since she was nine years old, sitting cross-legged on her mother's workbench stool while her mother traced diagrams in spilled salt.

*The chapel at Veymar's Ford,* her mother had said. *If you ever need the first stone, go where the water keeps it. Water forgets nothing, mija. It only waits.*

"What is it?" Felix asked. He had learned to read her silences in the two weeks since they'd left the valley—or perhaps he had simply learned that her silences were louder than most people's speech.

"A chapel." She pointed through the fog to where the river bent wide and slow around a shoulder of drowned land. Half a bell tower rose from the water like a broken tooth, its upper arches crusted with pale moss, its lower half swallowed whole. "Or what's left of one. The ford flooded after the binding. My mother said—" She stopped herself, the way she always stopped herself when a sentence began with *my mother said*, as if the words cost something she could no longer afford.

Felix waited. He was good at waiting. It was, she suspected, the mercenary in him—the patience of men who stand watch through dead hours—but there was something else in it too, something gentler that he probably didn't know was there.

"Say it," he said finally. "Even if it hurts."

She almost laughed. *Even if it hurts.* As if he understood that everything out of her mouth about her family came out bleeding. "My mother said the covenant's first fragment was kept here. The oathstone. Before the guild moved it, before they built vaults for it, this is where the first Ibarra cut the first line of the first sigil. They sank the chapel on purpose. Drowned it so no one could walk in and take it."

"And we're going to walk in and take it."

"We're going to swim in and take it," she corrected, and had the small satisfaction of seeing him grimace.

* * *

The water was colder than the season justified. Felix went in first because he insisted on going in first at everything, which Paloma had decided was either chivalry or a deep-seated inability to let anyone else take a risk on his behalf, and which she suspected was both. He waded until the bottom dropped away, then swam with long, economical strokes toward the tower, his pack held high over his head in one hand like it weighed nothing.

Paloma followed, and the cold closed over her like a fist. Beneath the surface the world turned green-black and silent. She could see the chapel's roof below her, slates furred with weed, and windows like dark eyes, and for one vertiginous moment she felt as though she were swimming above a sky rather than below one—as though the drowned building were a second world inverted, and something down there in the dark of the nave might look up at her the way people look up at stars.

*Stars.* There were none now. That was why they were here. That was why any of this was happening—the wards fallen, the valley unsealed, the god her family had bound gone silent behind a broken seal, and the world outside dimming week by week like a lamp running out of oil. Three fragments remained scattered from the old covenant. Her mother's notes named them all. The oathstone was the first, the keystone, the piece without which the others were only relics.

Her foot struck something solid and she surfaced, gasping, inside the tower. Felix had already hauled himself onto a ledge of collapsed masonry just above the waterline, and he reached down and pulled her up beside him with an arm like an iron bar.

They stood dripping in the bell chamber, and below them, visible through the rotted planking of the floor, lay the nave—a vast flooded hall where the light fell in green shafts through the broken roof and lit nothing but water and shadow.

"It's down there," Paloma said quietly.

"You're certain?"

"My mother drew me a picture of it when I was six years old. I thought it was a story." She looked at him. "Most of my childhood turns out to have been instructions."

Something moved in his face—not doubt exactly, but the wariness of a man who had spent twenty years being lied to by institutions and had developed a taste for the flavor of lies. "And the thing guarding it? Your mother's notes mention a guardian?"

"They do." She hesitated. "A wight. A keeper. Bound to test anyone who comes for the stone."

"Test them how?"

"Honestly, I don't know. The notes say only—" she recited from memory, because the memory was burned into her the way all her mother's lessons were burned into her, "*'It will ask you who you are. Answer truly or not at all.'*"

Felix looked down through the broken floor at the green dark. "I've fought bandits, border raiders, a bear once, and a man in Corvane who cheated at dice with loaded knuckles. None of them frightened me the way a question does."

"That," said Paloma, "is because you've never had to answer honestly before."

He gave her a look that was hard to read in the dimness—wounded, maybe, or amused, or both—and started down through the gap in the floor.

* * *

The nave was waist-deep in places, chest-deep in others, and the water was so still it seemed less like water than like glass they were wading through. Their footsteps made no sound. Their breathing sounded enormous. Along the walls, faded frescoes showed figures Paloma recognized from her mother's diagrams: the first covenant-makers, standing in a circle, hands joined, and above them a great star rendered in gold leaf that had not yet tarnished to black.

Felix stopped beneath it. Paloma watched him stare up at the golden star, and saw something move across his face that she had never seen there before—something raw and young, like a man looking at a portrait of his own face from childhood.

"That's it," he said. "That's what I prayed to. All those years in the valley, all those silent prayers into silence." His voice was strange. "I never knew what it looked like. I dreamed it sometimes. It looked like this."

"Felix—"

"The altar," he said, in a voice that had shut like a door. "Where's the altar?"

At the far end of the nave, where the apse curved, the water shallowed onto a broad stone platform, and on the platform stood the stone.

It was smaller than Paloma had expected—no taller than a man's knee, roughly ovoid, its surface covered in script so dense and fine it looked like a swarm of black bees frozen mid-flight. Even drowned, even dimmed, it seemed to drink the light around it. When Paloma's eyes slid across it, they wanted to slide away again, the way eyes slide off a bright thing.

And standing before it, motionless in the knee-deep water, was the guardian.

It had been a person once. That was the first thing Paloma understood, and the understanding arrived like a blow. It wore the rotted remnant of robes that might once have been white; its skin was the color of wet slate; its hair hung in black strands that moved gently in a current Paloma could not feel. Its face was human, or had been—hollow-cheeked, patient, terribly calm. It did not breathe. The water did not ripple where it stood.

"Oh," Felix breathed. "Oh, that's—"

"Do not draw your blade," Paloma said quickly, grabbing his wrist. "Whatever happens, whatever it does—do not draw your blade. It isn't a fight. It never was a fight. It's a door."

The wight raised its head.

Its eyes were the worst part. They were not empty, as Paloma had braced herself for. They were full—full of a patience so vast it made the drowned century around them feel brief, full of a weariness that went down further than the chapel foundations, down past the mountain roots, down to some bedrock of sorrow that had been here before the chapel, before the valley, before the names Ibarra and Vasquez meant anything at all.

When it spoke, its voice was dry as pages turning.

"*Two come to the stone. Two must be asked. The stone does not open for one alone, and it does not open for liars.*"

Paloma stepped forward, water dragging at her legs. "We come for the oathstone. To reforge what was broken."

"*All who come say that,*" the wight said. "*The last who came said it too, forty years past, and the stone refused them, and they tried to take it anyway, and the water keeps them.*" It gestured, very slightly, with one slate-gray hand, and somewhere in the green dark behind them something shifted—something soft and swollen and human-shaped, turning slowly in the current like a leaf.

Paloma's mouth went dry. Beside her, Felix had gone very still.

"*Who are you?*" the wight asked. "*Answer truly, or not at all.*"

Paloma closed her eyes. Every instinct she had honed in fourteen years of exile told her to shape the answer, to give the version of herself that would pass—competent, penitent, worthy. And every lesson her mother had ever taught her said the same thing the wight said: *truly, or not at all.*

"I am Paloma Ibarra," she said. "Daughter of Marisol Ibarra, who died owing a debt she couldn't pay. Last heir of the covenant that bound your god—" she caught herself, corrected, "—bound *the* god. I didn't break the covenant. But my family built it, and my family failed it, and I ran from both truths for fourteen years. I came back because there's no one else left to come back. I want to fix what my blood broke. And I am afraid that wanting isn't the same as deserving."

The silence afterward was so complete she could hear the water settling against her ribs.

Then the wight inclined its head, slow as a tree bending in wind, and turned its hollow eyes on Felix.

"*You,*" it said. "*Who are you?*"

Felix was quiet for a long moment. Long enough that Paloma glanced at him and saw his jaw working, saw the struggle in him like a man wrestling something underwater.

"I'm Felix Vasquez," he said at last. "Border patrol, third rank, Valley of the Silent God. Twenty years I served a god that never answered me, and I loved it anyway, and I don't know if that's faith or foolishness. I deserted my post two weeks ago. I'd do it again. I don't fully trust her—" a flick of his hand toward Paloma, and she flinched, "—and I don't fully trust myself around her, because she knows more about my god than I do and part of me hates her for it. I came for the stone because if the world goes dark I lose the only thing I ever believed in, and I would rather die than lose it ignorant."

The wight regarded him for a long, long moment.

Then it smiled. It was the most terrible thing Paloma had ever seen—not cruel, but sad beyond bearing, the smile of someone who has heard ten thousand confessions and forgiven none of them lightly.

"*True,*" it said. "*Both true. The stone accepts you.*"

It stepped aside.

And the oathstone began to rise.

Water sheeted off it as it lifted, streaming, the black script on its surface kindling with a faint silver light, and it drifted through the air between them like a sleeping bird, close enough to touch. Paloma reached out with both hands, trembling—

—and the wight spoke again.

"*One gift the stone gives freely to those it accepts,*" it said. "*Truth. The stone remembers what was sworn upon it, and shows what was sworn. Look, and be looked at.*"

The silver light flared.

* * *

Felix did not choose to look. The looking chose him.

The nave vanished. He stood in open air under a sky crowded with stars—real stars, thousands of them, a sky like the one he had grown up beneath before it went dark—and he knew, with the bone-deep certainty of dreams, that this was long ago. This was the night of the binding.

Below him spread a hillside sanctuary: white walls, cedar roofs, a temple complex he had never seen and knew instantly, the way you know things in visions. His god's sanctuary. Priests moved among the buildings in robes of undyed wool, and their voices rose in evening song, and the song was *warm*, it poured over him like sunlight, and Felix realized with a lurch that he was hearing his god's voice—not the silence, not the twenty years of nothing, but the voice itself, carried in a hundred human throats.

Then the horsemen came.

They came down out of the dark in a crescent, torches low, blades bare, and Felix tried to scream a warning and had no mouth to scream with. He was only a point of view, a witness nailed into the sky. The priests had time to turn, time to see, time to begin—of all things—to *sing louder*, as if the song itself were a wall, as if the last act of their faith was to raise their voices against the dark.

The first priest died singing. So did the second. The horses rode through the song and the torchlight found the white robes and made them red, and Felix hung in the starry air and watched his god's sanctuary burn, watched men and women cut down in the courtyards, watched a boy no older than he had been at his own swearing-in run with a censer still swinging in his fist until a sword took him at the knees.

And the killers, when they dismounted, when they walked among the bodies with torches held high—he knew them.

Not their faces. Their *sigils*. Cut into leather bracers, branded into the pommels of their swords, inked on the backs of their hands in patterns he had spent two weeks watching Paloma trace absently in the dirt when she thought he wasn't looking. The interlocking lines of the Ibarra craft-mark. The covenant's own geometry, worn by the people who had made it.

Paloma's ancestors killed his god's priests. On the night of the binding, the price of the silence had been paid in the blood of everyone who could hear the god speak—and the ones who paid it were the same family whose daughter slept ten feet away from him every night, whose life he had saved at the ward-line, whose name he had begun, without noticing when, to say to himself like a prayer.

The vision released him.

* * *

He came back to himself kneeling in the floodwater of the nave, gasping, his hands shaking so badly he had to lock them together to keep them still. The oathstone hung in the air before him, silver-lit, patient. The wight watched him with its terrible calm eyes. And Paloma—

Paloma stood three feet away, the stone clasped against her chest, staring at him with a face drained of all color.

"You saw," she whispered. "What did you see?"

The lie assembled itself in his mouth with terrible ease. He was good at lies; he had been lying to superiors since the day he met her, hiding what she knew, hiding what she was, hiding the small treasonous architecture of his own choices. One more lie. One more, and she would never have to know he knew, and the road ahead would stay smooth, and the fragments would get gathered, and the stars would come back, and no one would ever have to stand in the wreckage of this particular truth.

"Nothing," Felix said. "The stone showed me the binding. The night it was made. Nothing more."

He watched her believe him. He watched the relief move through her like a tide, watched her shoulders come down from around her ears, watched her nod and turn to thank the wight in a voice that shook only a little. And underneath his own steady breathing, underneath the mercenary's flat calm he had worn for twenty years like armor, something in Felix Vasquez quietly tore along a seam that had been there longer than he knew.

The wight's eyes found his as Paloma turned away. Held them. It did not smile this time. It looked at him the way the stone had looked at him—with that vast, sad patience—and Felix understood that it knew. That it had shown him what it showed him deliberately. That truth, given freely to those it accepts, is also a burden, and burdens are tests too, and he had just failed the first one.

"*The stone goes with you,*" the wight said aloud, bowing its head. "*So does what you carry. Both weigh the same, in the end.*"

They swam out through the broken tower into the gray morning, the oathstone wrapped in oilcloth against Paloma's chest, the drowned chapel sinking back into its century of silence behind them. The fog had thickened on the river. Somewhere out in it, Paloma was talking—plans, routes, the next fragment's location, her voice bright with the first hope he'd heard in it since the ward-line—and Felix answered in the right places, and nodded, and walked.

That night, when she slept, he sat up by the dead fire and looked east, where the stars should have been, and found that he could no longer pray to his god without seeing the boy with the censer, falling.

He did not know, yet, that the vision had withheld as much as it showed—that the truth the wight kept was larger than the truth it gave. He only knew that the woman sleeping ten feet away wore the sigils of the men in his nightmare, and that tomorrow he would follow her again anyway, and that he no longer knew which of those facts was the betrayal.
