## 9. The Mercenary's Ledger

Felix had learned to count before he learned to pray.

That was the first thing the ledger taught him: everything was entered, everything was owed. His mother sold him to the temple guard in the spring of his ninth year for two sacks of barley flour and a forgiveness she never explained. He remembered standing in the courtyard of the Godshouse with a chalk mark on his collar—the mark of purchase—and an old quartermaster named Doss kneeling before him with a leather-bound book open across his knees.

"Name," Doss said.

"Felix."

"Felix what?"

"Just Felix." He hadn't known his father's name. He still didn't.

Doss wrote it down anyway, just *Felix*, and beneath it the terms: *sworn at nine years, term of twenty, paid in bread, salt, shelter, and instruction; debt collectible in service.* Then he turned the book around and made Felix press his thumb into an inkstone and then onto the page, and the ink sank into the whorls of his skin and stayed there for three days no matter how he scrubbed.

"That's you," Doss said, tapping the print. "In the god's book now. You'll want to remember that on cold nights."

Felix had asked the only question that mattered to a nine-year-old: "Does it hurt?"

Doss looked at him for a long time. Outside the courtyard wall, the mountains stood gray against a gray sky, and somewhere beyond them the border wards hummed their note that nobody heard anymore because they'd always heard it, the way you stop hearing your own heartbeat.

"No," Doss said finally. "The god doesn't hurt. That's not what silence is."

* * *

They swore him at midsummer, along with eleven other children from the year's intake, and the rite was nothing like he'd feared. No knives. No blood. They walked the children up the switchback path to the Listening Chapel—a small stone building wedged into the mountain's shoulder where the valley's oldest ward-stone sat embedded in the altar like a tooth in a jaw—and there the high priest of that era, a soft-spoken woman named Mother Ilex, had each child place both hands flat on the ward-stone and say the words after her:

*I am yours. My hands are yours. My years are yours. Speak through me or speak not at all; either way, I am listening.*

Felix felt—something. Not a voice. Not warmth. More like the moment when you step from bright sun into a deep room and your eyes haven't adjusted yet: a vast dark presence adjusting itself around him, patient, enormous, attentive. It noticed him. That was the whole of it. For nine years he had been a thing bought with barley flour, and in one instant on the cold stone he became a thing *noticed*, and the noticing went through him like a bell struck once, low, below hearing.

He wept. All the children did, though none of them could have said why, and Mother Ilex knelt among them with her gray robes pooled around her and said, "Yes. That's how it feels the first time. You're allowed to cry about being seen."

Afterward they were given salt bread and honey, and their names were entered in the great ledger in the Godshouse vault, and Felix went to sleep that night in the barracks he would occupy for the next eleven years feeling—for the first time in his remembered life—that someone knew exactly where he was.

He understood later, much later, standing over Paloma's sleeping form on the ash road with the vision of the oathstone still burning behind his eyes, that this was the cruelest thing the covenant ever did to him. Not the service. Not the silence. It gave a motherless child the sensation of being *held*, and then it stopped speaking, and left him holding nothing but the memory of weight.

* * *

The silence began the winter he turned fourteen.

He'd been five years sworn. He knew the drills, the patrol routes along the valley rim, the nineteen hand-signals of the ward-watch. He was beginning to feel the wards themselves—not hear them, exactly, but feel them the way a sailor feels weather in old scars—when the change came.

It wasn't dramatic. Nothing in the valley was ever dramatic; that was the whole point of the valley. But on a morning in late autumn, Felix stood his watch at the Rim Gate and realized the hum under the world had shifted key. And that night, in the chapel where the sworn children gathered for evening devotion, the presence he'd felt since he was nine—the great patient attention, the bell-note below hearing—was simply *not there.*

Not gone. He would spend twenty years learning the difference. Gone would have been clean. This was worse: the presence was still there, the way a man behind a locked door is still there. He could feel the shape of it against something, pressed close, utterly still. Holding its breath.

The adults pretended nothing had happened. That was the second lesson. The priests led the devotions in the same cadence, the elders met in the same hall, the ward-watch walked the same routes, and if any of them felt the difference—if any of them lay awake at night staring at the rafters the way Felix did, pressing his mind against that terrible held-breath stillness and getting nothing back—none of them said so.

He tried asking. He was fourteen and frightened and he chose Mother Ilex's successor, a narrow man named Father Callow, and caught him alone after rites and said, "The god's gone quiet."

Father Callow's face did something complicated and fast, like water closing over a dropped stone. "The god has never been loud," he said. "That is what makes it holy. Go to your barracks, Vasquez."

"But before—"

"*Go.*"

Felix went. And he learned the third lesson, which was that in the valley, the answer to every question about the god was a version of *go to your barracks*. By sixteen he'd stopped asking. By eighteen he'd stopped noticing that he'd stopped. The silence settled over him the way snow settles on a roof—gradually, completely, until the weight was just part of the architecture.

But here is what the silence cost, and he kept the account even when he refused to read it:

It cost him the consolations. Other soldiers in other lands—he learned this later, from mercenaries who worshipped loud gods of iron and storm—came back from killing and prayed and were answered, or at least believed they were answered, and that belief let them sleep. Felix came back from his first fight—skirmishers out of the pass, three dead, one of them by his hand—and knelt in the chapel until his knees bruised and felt nothing but that muffled, breath-held stillness, and rose knowing that whatever he'd done, he carried it entirely alone. There would be no absolution spoken into him. There would be no voice saying *it was necessary* or even *I saw.* Every act of his life was unwitnessed by the one witness he'd been promised.

It cost him trust. A man who cannot verify his god begins to wonder whether there was ever anything behind the door. He watched brothers of the watch lose that argument with themselves and drift—first to drink, then to bitterness, then over the ward-line into exile, one by one, a slow hemorrhage the elders recorded as desertion. Felix stayed. He told himself it was loyalty. Some nights, honest in the dark, he admitted it was simpler than that: he had nowhere else the bell had ever rung.

And it cost him—this was the entry he kept lowest in the ledger, the one written smallest—the ability to imagine being chosen for anything but use. His mother had sold him. The god had taken him. Both transactions had the same grammar: *you are worth this much, payable in service.* When Paloma Ibarra stood in the ash road with soot to her elbows and looked at him like he was a person rather than a post, he had not known what to do with his face.

* * *

The morning after the drowned chapel, Felix woke before dawn out of a habit older than memory and lay watching the gray light move across the ruined ceiling while the vision replayed itself against the inside of his skull.

The wight had shown them honesty, it said. It had shown each of them a truth and waited to see what they did with it. Paloma had wept over hers—some inheritance of guilt, some debt of blood he hadn't yet understood—and had turned to him afterward with red eyes and said, "Whatever it showed you—" and he had said, "Nothing I didn't know," and the wight had let them pass.

It hadn't shown him nothing. It had shown him the founding.

He saw it again now, in the pre-dawn gray, with the terrible clarity the dead keep in their gifts: the valley as it had been before the valley, a green country under a full sky of stars, and the god's people gathered on the mountain shoulder where the Listening Chapel would someday stand. And coming up the same switchbacks, another procession—Ibarra sigilwrights in robes stitched with luminous thread, their hands gloved in lead-white silk—and between the two processions, the god's priests walking unarmed, carrying nothing, singing.

He saw the sigilwrights cut the first line of the binding into the living rock. He saw the god's priests kneel and offer their throats—not in defiance, in *surrender*, that was the detail the vision would not release, they knelt and bowed their heads like men presenting their necks to a beloved executioner—and he saw the Ibarra matriarch raise the cutting-blade, and he saw the blade come down, and he saw the blood run into the channels of the new-cut sigil and the stars begin to go out overhead, one constellation at a time, snuffed like candles in a closing room.

And he saw—last, worst—that the god had been there the whole time. Watching. Its attention spread over the mountain like a hand over a candle flame, and it had not struck the blade down. It had *held still.* It had let its own priests die into the channels of its cage, because the alternative—

The alternative was the thing the vision showed only in silhouette. A shape at the edge of the sky, vast and wrong, turning toward the valley with something that was not hunger and was worse than hunger. The reason. The *why* that the kneeling priests must have known, that the Ibarra matriarch must have known, that had made throat-offering and star-extinction the merciful arithmetic.

Then the vision ended, and the wight had looked at Felix with its drowned eyes and waited, and Felix had lied to a creature that tested honesty, and it had let him pass anyway—which frightened him more than any punishment would have. As if the wight understood that some truths need time before they can be borne. As if it were keeping his account too.

* * *

Paloma was already awake when he came down to the road. She sat on a fallen lintel with the oathstone wrapped in oilcloth across her knees, turning it over and over without seeming to know she was doing it, and when she glanced up at him her face did the thing it did every morning: assembled itself, piece by piece, into composure.

"We make for the river crossing today," she said. "There's a town past it called Marrow's Ford, or there was. If the fragments are scattered the way my mother's notes say, the second one will be south of there. In cult territory, probably." She paused. "You slept badly."

"You were awake half the night yourself."

"I'm always awake half the night. That's different." She studied him with those quick gray eyes that missed less than he wanted them to. "The wight showed you something. Yesterday. Before it showed me mine."

His heart performed a single hard beat, like a fist on a door, and then went quiet. Twenty years of ward-watch discipline: face still, breathing level, hands easy. He'd stood that discipline through skirmishes and interrogations and one memorable night when Father Callow had questioned him for six hours about a missing record. It should have held now.

"The wight shows everyone something," he said. "That's its function. Test, vision, verdict."

"What did it show you?"

"Old history." He crouched by the dead fire and began raking the coals apart with a stick, giving his hands work. "Founding-era stuff. Priests and sigilwrights. Your family, actually."

Her stillness had a quality he was learning to read—the stillness of a woman deciding, very quickly, how much to say. "And?"

"And nothing. It's dust, Paloma. Everyone in it has been dead five hundred years." He looked up and gave her the truth-shaped lie he'd been polishing since yesterday: "It showed me your people killing mine. It showed me why I should hate you. It's wrong, or it's incomplete, or it's a test, and I've decided I don't care which. Is that what you needed to hear?"

Something moved behind her eyes—relief, and beneath the relief, a flicker of something else that might have been disappointment, or recognition, one liar acknowledging another's craft. "My people didn't kill anyone who didn't consent," she said quietly.

"I know." He hadn't known yesterday. He knew now, and the knowledge sat in his chest like a swallowed coal: *the god consented. The god chose this. Everything I mourned, it ordered.* He fed the lie a little more truth to keep it fat. "That was in the vision too. The kneeling. I just don't understand it yet."

"No," Paloma said softly. "Neither do I. Not all of it." And he saw her decide, in real time, not to push—not because she believed him, but because she was carrying secrets of her own and knew the weight of being asked. "Come on. River crossing by noon, if the ford hasn't drowned."

She hoisted the oathstone and started down the ash-gray road, and Felix followed, and the ledger in his head opened itself unbidden and made its entries: *One lie, told kindly. One truth, withheld. Balance outstanding.*

He had spent his whole life believing that loyalty meant service rendered and debts paid in full, every entry visible, every account square. Walking behind Paloma Ibarra through the dimming country, Felix understood that he was learning a different kind of loyalty—one with a column in the book that never got totaled, entries made in ink that didn't sink into the skin but into something deeper, and the first rule of that column was simple:

Some things you carry so the person beside you doesn't have to.

He just hoped, watching her shoulders set against the wind, that when her own hidden entries finally surfaced—and they would; he'd seen the shape of omissions often enough to know she kept a ledger too—they would find that the debts balanced. That two incomplete truths, laid together, made something whole.

Behind them, far to the north, the valley's broken ward-line gave off its faint dying hum, and Felix found himself thinking of the Listening Chapel, and the ward-stone, and a nine-year-old boy pressing his palms to the cold rock and feeling, for one shining moment, *noticed*.

Hold on, he thought at the silent god, in the wordless way he'd learned over twenty years. I'm coming. And this time I'm bringing questions.

Ahead, the river glimmered under a sky with no stars waiting in it, and on the far bank, faint on the wind, came the sound of many voices raised in a hymn he didn't know—yet somehow, horribly, recognized.
