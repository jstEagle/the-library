## 10. The Sigilwright's Daughter

The rain came at dusk and did not stop.

Paloma felt it before she heard it—a fine cold prickle along the scarred skin of her forearms, where the old guild-ink sat beneath her flesh like buried rivers. Sigilwrights were said to feel weather in their marks, and it was true, though not in the way the ballads claimed. It wasn't poetry. It was the ink remembering that it had once been alive, that it had been mixed with blood and starlight and the ash of burned oaths, and that everything alive wanted to move when the sky moved.

She crouched beneath a leaning slab of granite at the edge of what had been an orchard—she could still see the rows of dead trees stretching away into the grey, their branches black and bare as writing gone wrong—and watched Felix Vasquez build a fire with the methodical patience of a man who had done ten thousand small survivable things.

He was better company than she had expected. That was the trouble.

For six days they had walked out of the hollow country together, and in six days he had asked her perhaps forty questions, and she had answered thirty-nine of them truthfully. She was Ibarra. She was a sigilwright of the Ninth Circle, trained at Vessa-Sol until the guild cast her out—or rather, until she ran before they could finish casting her out; those were different things, and the difference mattered, though she had flattened it for him into a single convenient word: *exiled*. She knew the covenant. She knew why the wards had fallen. She knew his god's true name, or enough of it to make the air go strange when she whispered it over their shared fire on the third night, and she had watched him flinch as if she'd touched an open wound.

All of that was true. All of that was honest.

And all of it was arranged around a hole, the way a wall is arranged around a door.

The rite. The renewal. What the covenant demanded every generation, and what her family's blood had paid for two hundred years, and what had failed—the word everyone used, *failed*, as if the covenant were a lamp that had simply guttered—as if no one's hands had been on the wick.

She had told him the binding broke because the last payment was never made. That much was true too. She had simply never said *whose* payment, or *what kind* of payment, or what her mother had pressed into her palms on the last night and said, with the terrible calm of a woman who has already done her grieving: *Run, little bird. Run and don't look back, because if you look back I will ask you to stay, and if you stay you will die, and I have loved you too badly and too long to watch it.*

The fire caught. Felix fed it a curl of birchbark, then a few dry sticks from the bundle under his cloak, and the light climbed his face in increments—jaw first, then the crooked line of his nose, then eyes that were darker than they should have been for a man of the valley, almost black, almost like looking down a well. A well with something patient at the bottom.

"Rain's easing," he said.

"It isn't."

"No." He almost smiled. "But you looked like you needed to hear something kind."

Paloma turned away before her face could betray anything, which was foolish, because her face had been betraying her since she was six years old and her grandmother had caught her practicing forbidden glyphs on the inside of her own wrist. *You wear your heart where anyone can read it,* the old woman had said, gripping her chin. *A sigilwright who can be read is a sigilwright who can be used.* So Paloma had learned. She had learned so well that now, when she most needed to be legible—to this mercenary with his silent god and his soldier's silences—she found she could only produce the old smooth surfaces, the practiced truths, the wall around the door.

"I'm going to tell you something," she said.

Felix looked up. He didn't say *go on* or *finally*. He just waited, the way he did, with his hands loose around his knees and the fire painting one side of him gold. Waiting was a weapon he carried without seeming to know it. People confessed to men who waited. She had seen it happen in the guild's hearing chambers—hardened oath-breakers undone not by questioning but by the long patient silence of the Inkwright who simply *waited*.

So she made herself speak before the silence could do its work.

"You know my family kept the covenant. You know we paid for it, generation after generation. Blood-tithe, service, the cutting of the border sigils every seventh year." Her voice came out level. Good. "What I haven't told you is how it ended. Not the ward-failure—I've told you about that. I mean the end of *us*. Of me, in Vessa-Sol. Why I'm really exiled."

The rain ticked against the granite above them. Somewhere off in the dark, one of the lightless country's wrong birds made its wrong sound—a wet clicking, like a lock testing itself.

"The guild called a convocation the winter before last," Paloma said. "After the tithe failed. After—" She stopped. Started again, from a safer distance. "Every generation, the covenant must be renewed. That's the whole architecture of it. A covenant isn't a wall, Felix. Walls stand on their own. A covenant is more like a heartbeat. It has to be re-beat, again and again, or it stops. And the renewal requires an Ibarra. Always has. Our blood is the ink. Our names are written into the deep sigil at the forge-peak, and each generation one of us goes up the mountain and—"

"And pays," Felix said quietly.

"Pays." The word tasted like iron filings. "Yes. My aunt Sera went up when I was nine. My uncle Tomas before her. My mother was meant to go next. The convocation named her. And she—" Paloma's hands had closed into fists without her permission; she opened them deliberately, finger by finger, the way her mother had taught her to still a shaking hand before delicate work. "She refused. Or she couldn't. There are stories about what happens to an Ibarra who refuses, and none of them are good, and my mother chose the worst one anyway. She broke her own name out of the sigil. Unwrote herself. Do you understand what that means? She took a knife to the deepest working our family ever cut and she *erased her own name from it*, and the backlash—"

She had been eleven. She remembered the sky going white over Vessa-Sol, and every piece of glass in the guildhall singing one high note, and her mother standing in the courtyard with blood running freely from both ears and both eyes, smiling at her, saying nothing, because there was nothing left in her that could touch language anymore. They had kept her alive for four years after that. Four years of a woman who blinked at sunlight and sometimes, very rarely, turned her head toward the sound of Paloma's voice, as if the name itself still lived somewhere in the ruin of her.

"—the backlash unmade her," Paloma finished. "Mostly. The guild called it apostasy. Called it the reason the tithe chain broke, the reason the covenant weakened, the reason your stars went dark twenty years later. Maybe they're right. Maybe it's even true. But here's the part I left out, Felix, and I'm telling you now because you deserve it and because I'm tired of carrying it alone."

He hadn't moved. His stillness had changed quality, though—from the waiting-stillness of a listener to the coiled stillness of a man watching a fuse burn toward something.

"After my mother, the convocation didn't end the rite. They didn't decide the price was too cruel. They decided the price was *correct* and the supply was insufficient." Her mouth was dry. She made herself keep going. "They named me. Sixteen years old, ninth circle, best hand with a cutting stylus in three generations—they said it themselves, they said it like praise, like honor. *The daughter will renew the covenant. The daughter will pay what the mother refused.* And when I said no, they didn't argue. They began preparing me. Fasting rites. Ink-blessings. They started writing the renewal sigils onto my skin while I slept, so that by spring I would be—"

"*Ready*," Felix said. The word came out flat and hard.

"Ripe," Paloma corrected, and hated how steady her voice stayed. "Like fruit. Like a lamb. I ran the night before the final cutting. Took nothing but my stylus case and my mother's notes. The guild sent hunters—not to kill me. To *retrieve* me. You can't renew a covenant with a corpse, after all. You need a living Ibarra, willing or otherwise, and they had two years of preparation invested in making sure I'd survive the rite." She laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "So yes. Exiled. Hunted by my own guild for apostasy. Last heir of the covenant that sealed your god behind the sky. That's the whole of it. That's what I am."

The fire popped. Rain dripped from the granite edge, slow as a leaking clock.

Felix was quiet for a long time. When he finally spoke, his voice was careful in a way she had learned to distrust.

"They wrote the sigils on you while you slept."

"Yes."

"Show me."

Her whole body went still. "No."

"Paloma."

"They're *mine*," she said, sharper than she intended, and then, softer, because the sharpness had cost her something: "They're mine. Whatever the guild put on my skin, I've spent two years learning to own it instead of being owned by it. I'm not going to strip in the rain to show a mercenary where they cut me."

Something moved through his expression—hurt, maybe, quickly folded away. He nodded and looked back at the fire, and Paloma sat with the shape of what she'd just done, which was refuse honesty to a man she had just demanded honesty from, and understood with cold clarity that she was circling him the way she'd circled the guild examiners for years: giving truth in measured doses, always keeping one hand closed.

Because there was one thing more. One thing she had not said, sitting right at the center of the confession like a stone at the heart of a fruit.

The rite hadn't failed because her mother refused. Refusal caused delay, damage, weakening—but the covenant had held for twenty years after. It had failed—no. It had been *broken*, deliberately, by people who wanted it broken, and she knew because she had found her mother's notes, the real notes hidden beneath the false ones, and the real notes said: *They do not want renewal, little bird. Renewal keeps the balance. They want the gate. Ask yourself who profits when the god cannot answer prayers, and follow the money to the ninth circle.*

She had never followed it far enough. She had run instead. And now the wards were down, the stars were dead, and whatever her mother had feared was walking around loose in the world wearing the silence like a coat.

That was the omission. Not the rite. Not the sacrifice. The *direction* of the knife.

*Later*, she told herself, in the old familiar voice. *When we're past the borderlands. When he's bound to the road with me and can't turn back. When telling him costs less.*

It was a liar's arithmetic and she knew it.

* * *

Felix banked the fire and took first watch, because he always took first watch, and Paloma lay with her back to the warmth and did not sleep.

She listened to him breathe. She had learned his rhythms the way she learned any text—attentively, hungrily, reading for structure. The long even cadence of a man conserving himself. The small hitch when he dreamed, twice a night usually, always brief, always mastered within three breaths. Once, on the fourth night, she had heard him whisper something in the dark, one word, in the old valley tongue, and she had recognized it because it appeared in her mother's notes: *still*. A prayer-word. A leash-word. The word the valley's faithful used to hold their god quiet, the way you'd say *stay* to a dog.

He thought his god was sleeping. He thought the silence was peace.

She rolled onto her back and looked up through the gap in the granite at a sky with nothing in it. No stars. No moon worth the name—just a bruised grey disc, dim as a coin at the bottom of a well. The hollow country's darkness was total in a way darkness shouldn't be; it had texture, weight, intention. Things moved in it. On the second day they had passed a village where the people had nailed shutters over their windows and painted eyes on the boards, dozens of eyes, staring outward into the dark, because—as the last living villager had explained, in the flat voice of someone who has stopped expecting to be believed—the dark got shy if it thought it was being watched.

Somewhere out there, her guild was moving too. She could feel it in the ink. Every few days, a faint pull, northeast, toward Corvane, like a hook set gently in her flesh. The Ninth Circle had gone to the city. Which meant the Ninth Circle knew something. Which meant her mother's warning was already in motion, had been in motion for twenty years, and Paloma had spent those twenty years running in exactly the wrong direction.

*Run, little bird.*

*I ran, Mama. I ran so far I ran into the middle of it.*

"Felix," she said, before she could stop herself.

A pause. Then: "I'm here."

"If I tell you something true tomorrow—something worse than tonight—will you still walk with me?"

The silence stretched. Rain again, thinner now, almost done. When he answered, his voice had a strange doubled quality, as if he were speaking from slightly further away than his body stood.

"I'll walk with you," he said. "Whatever it is."

"That's not an answer. That's a promise, and promises are cheaper."

"No," he said. "It's an answer. I just don't know yet if it's the one you want."

She turned that over in the dark, examining it for traps the way she examined any unfamiliar glyph, and found—nothing. No trap. Just a man answering honestly, which was somehow more unsettling than a lie.

Sleep took her eventually, shallow and salted with dreams. In the dreams her mother stood at the foot of the forge-peak with her hands open, and the mountain's shadow fell across them both, and the shadow had a shape she almost recognized—a star-shape, a hunger-shape—and her mother's mouth was moving, saying words that arrived a half-second after her lips stopped, like thunder after lightning, and Paloma woke twice with the taste of ash in her mouth and the certain knowledge that she had forgotten something vital, something written, something her mother had bled to tell her.

* * *

Morning came grey and windless. They walked. The dead orchard gave way to a ridge road, and the ridge road to a river valley where the water ran strangely slow, as if even the current had grown uncertain without stars to steer by.

It was midday, crossing a ford, boots in the freezing shallows, when Felix spoke.

"My mother sold me to the temple when I was seven."

Paloma stopped in the water. He kept walking, three steps, four, then stopped too, facing away from her, watching the slow river slide past his knees.

"You've told me about your family. Your blood, your guild, your mountain." His voice was even, but she could hear the effort holding it even, the way she could hear the effort in a badly balanced sigil. "Two hundred years of Ibarras paying for the covenant. I've heard it all. And I've told you about the valley, the patrols, the elders, the priest burning records. But I've never told you what I *am* to the god. What I was made."

"Felix—"

"A child-soldier," he said. "Sworn at seven. Marked at nine. They cut the oath into me the same way your guild cut the renewal sigils into you—while I couldn't say no. And for twenty years I prayed to a god that never answered, and I told myself the silence was holiness, and I built my whole life on top of that story the way you build a house on a foundation you're afraid to look under." He turned. Water streamed off his boots. His face was pale and absolutely calm, the calm of a man standing on a ledge. "Last night you told me the truth you could afford. I'm telling you the truth I can afford. And we're both going to keep walking, and neither of us is going to ask the other the question we're actually thinking."

Paloma's throat was tight. "Which question is that?"

He smiled, and it was the saddest thing she'd seen since her mother's ruined face.

"The one about what we're still hiding," he said, and stepped up the far bank, and held out his hand to help her up, and she took it, and neither of them said another word about it.

But that night, making camp in the lee of a fallen waystone whose carved covenant-marks had been chiseled away by deliberate hands—recently, she noted; the stone dust was still fresh in the grooves—Paloma opened her mother's packet of notes by firelight and found, tucked between pages she had read a hundred times, a scrap she had never seen before. New. Slipped in since the last time she looked. Her own handwriting, copied in her sleep-walking hours, in the ink-blessed script the guild used for binding-work:

*The ninth circle knows the gate. The ninth circle holds the key. Follow the ink to Corvane.*

She looked up across the fire at Felix, who was watching her with those well-dark eyes, and who had clearly seen her face change, and who said nothing at all.

Neither of them slept well.
