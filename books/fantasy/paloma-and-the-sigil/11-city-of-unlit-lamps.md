## 11. City of Unlit Lamps

*Felix*

Corvane announced itself by smell before it did by sight: woodsmoke and tallow and the sour tang of too many bodies huddled against a cold that no longer loosened, even at midday. Then the road crested a low ridge of slag and old cinder, and there it was—the greatest city in the world, spread across three hills like a spilled inkwell, its ten thousand lamps unlit.

Felix had seen Corvane once before, as a boy of fourteen, marching behind his father's company. He remembered it blazing. Lanterns strung bridge to bridge like caught stars, windows gold-leafed with candlelight, the river itself carrying reflections so dense it looked like a second city drowned beneath the first. His father had bought him honeyed chestnuts from a brazier on the Sable Bridge, and Felix had thought: *this is what the world is. This is what we guard the valley against knowing.*

Now he stood on the same road, twenty years older and half a lifetime more foolish, and watched the dark city breathe smoke.

"Ten thousand lamps," Paloma said beside him. Her voice was flat, but her hands—wrapped in fingerless gloves against the cold—had gone very still. "They used to relight them every dusk. There was an office for it. The Lamplighters' Guild. My mother said their oath was the only one in the city written on paper instead of skin, because they were never meant to be anything but ordinary."

"And now?"

"Now they're probably the busiest guild in Corvane." She shouldered her pack. "Trying to light lamps that won't take flame."

She was right about that. As they came down through the outer wards—open gates, here; Corvane had never walled itself in, wore its size like armor—they passed lamplighters working the low streets with long poles and desperate industry. They'd oil a wick, strike a spark, cup the flame tenderly—and watch it gutter blue, then brown, then die, as though the fire itself had lost faith. The lamplighters' faces had the particular blankness of people performing a ritual whose meaning had drained out the bottom.

"They'll blame someone," Felix said quietly.

"Of course."

"When the valley's crops failed, when the wells went bitter, my elders always blamed someone. A foreigner. A witch. A family with the wrong name." He glanced sideways at her. "You're carrying a satchel full of sigil-craft and you have your grandmother's name stitched inside your collar. You might as well be wearing a sign."

"I know what I'm wearing." She pulled her hood lower. "I've been known before, Felix. It's not the worst thing that can happen to a person."

"No," he said. "It's just usually the thing that happens right before the worst thing."

* * *

*Paloma*

The city took them in because cities always do—it was too large and too frightened to examine everyone—but it pressed close while doing it. Paloma felt Corvane's fear the way she'd learned to feel a failing ward: a pressure behind the eyes, a wrongness in the air's grain. The streets were crowded past sense. Refugees from the dimming countryside camped in the squares under awnings of patched canvas, and the markets sold tallow candles at prices that would have been obscene even when candles worked.

And everywhere, everywhere, the yellow scarves.

She saw the first one within an hour of passing the gate—a woman selling roasted onions, a strip of saffron-yellow cloth knotted at her throat. Then another. Then a dozen. By the time they reached the Sable Bridge, Paloma understood she wasn't looking at a fashion. It was a uniform without an army. Or rather—an army without uniforms yet.

"The Second Dawn," she murmured.

"You know them?"

"I know *of* them. Every sigilwright knows of them." She kept her voice beneath the market noise. "They started as street preachers, years ago, back when the stars were only flickering. Saying the darkness was a cleansing. That the true dawn would come not from above—" she tilted her chin at the starless sky, visible even now, a pale bruised gray like the underside of a closed eyelid— "but from below. From man. That if humanity could forge its own light, it would never again depend on heaven's whims."

"And do they say how this forging is done?"

"They used to say prayer and purity." She watched a yellow-scarved man hand out bread to refugee children, his smile wide and warm and practiced. "Lately I hear other words. Words like *anchor*. Words like *vessel*." She didn't look at Felix when she said it. "Words that mean a person."

The bread-giver's eyes swept the crowd as he worked, and Paloma saw what the warmth was for: the eyes were counting. Cataloguing. Looking for faces that flinched at certain words.

She took Felix's sleeve and drew him toward the bridge's under-stairs, into the smell of river rot and old rope.

"There's a place we need to go," she said. "Before anything else."

"Not the archive? You said the inkwell—"

"The inkwell is in the cult's vaults now. That's the whole point. But vaults have histories, and histories have doors, and I knew Corvane before I knew exile." She met his eyes. In the shadow under the stairs her face was all planes and exhaustion, and still, stubbornly, beautiful to him in a way he hadn't yet found language for. "There was a woman named Sereth Vail who kept a bindery off Cooper's Row. She bound covenant-work for my mother. If anyone remembers which walls in this city are hollow, it's her."

"If," Felix said.

"If," Paloma agreed. "It's been six years."

* * *

*Felix*

Sereth Vail's bindery was a burned shell and Cooper's Row was a soup line, but the woman herself had moved two streets over into a cellar behind a chandler's shop, and she remembered Paloma with a cry that was half joy and half grief, and hugged her hard enough to crack ribs.

Then she held Paloma at arm's length, looked at her properly, and her face changed.

"Oh, child," she said. "Oh, you shouldn't have come."

"So everyone keeps telling me."

"They're not telling you to warn you. They're telling you because they want it noted later that they told you." Sereth was sixty if she was a day, small and quick as a wren, with ink stained so deep into her fingers they looked dipped to the knuckle. She shut the cellar door, threw a bolt, and lit—of all things—a candle, which guttered but held, down here where the air was thick and still. "The Second Dawn has lists. Real ones, written ones. Sigilwrights, covenant-heirs, anyone trained in the old marks. Three taken this month from the Tanners' Quarter alone. Taken *gently*, mind—that's the horror of it. No blood, no broken doors. They knock, they smile, they offer protection and purpose, and the families hand their own over smiling back. And those who refuse—" She made a gesture, vague and terrible. "Disappear politely."

"What do they want with sigilwrights?" Felix asked.

Sereth looked at him for the first time, taking his measure with the frankness of the old. "Who's this?"

"A friend."

"A friend who asks questions like a soldier."

"A friend who asks questions like a soldier," Paloma confirmed.

Sereth snorted, but some of the tension went out of her shoulders. "What do they want? What any frightened people want. Someone to blame and something to worship, and ideally the same thing wearing both faces." She lowered herself onto a stool amid bolts of waxed linen. "Their magistrate—Oren Hale, remember the name, everyone will soon enough—has been preaching that the old covenant was a cage built around mankind's birthright. That the sigilwrights *knew* how to make light and hoarded it. And lately—" her voice dropped— "lately he's stopped preaching and started building. They've bought the old Customhouse on the Vair. Carts go in at night. Nothing comes out."

Paloma had gone very still. "Carts carrying what?"

"Stone. Salt. Iron." Sereth's eyes flicked to the satchel at Paloma's hip. "And books, girl. Whole cartloads of books. Every text on the covenant craft that's changed hands in this city in five years, and some, I'd wager, that never changed hands honestly."

*The inkwell,* Felix thought. *It's already in the vaults, or nearly.* But he said nothing. Some knowledge was a lamp; some was a fuse, and you didn't carry fuses into crowded rooms.

"We need to reach the Customhouse undercity," Paloma said. "The old service tunnels. They run beneath—"

"Child." Sereth reached out and gripped her wrist. "Listen to me. You cannot walk Cooper's Row by day anymore. You cannot walk *anywhere* by day. Because—" and here the old woman's face did something complicated, sorrow and apology braided together— "because there are sketches going around. Handbills. A likeness of a woman wanted for questioning in the matter of the failing lights. And the likeness is six years old, but it's *good*, girl. Your mother commissioned it herself, gods rest her, so the guild could find you when you ran. And the Second Dawn has been nailing it to every post from here to the river."

The cellar went silent except for the candle's small struggle.

"That's why they hug you at the door," Felix said quietly. "That's why she checked your face."

"It's why I bolted the door after you," Sereth said, "and why I'll unbolt it for no one else tonight." She squeezed Paloma's wrist once, hard, and let go. "Six years ago I'd have hidden you forever. Tonight I can give you until dawn and a map of the tunnels, and my love besides, and you'll have to make all three enough."

* * *

*Paloma*

They left before the fourth bell, down through Sereth's coal cellar into the service tunnels that laced beneath Old Corvane like veins in a leaf—tunnels built centuries ago to move goods and ash and, in less polite eras, things better unmoved by daylight. Paloma walked first with the candle shielded in a shutter-lantern, reading the brickwork the way her mother had taught her to read everything: for the marks underneath.

The tunnel walls were covered in them. Not covenant-work—older, cruder, folk-magic scratched by generations of hands that had needed to believe a chalk spiral could keep the dark polite. Warding scribbles. Little suns. Names of children, beseeching protection. And newer than all of these, fresh as this week: the Second Dawn's mark, a rising half-circle over a horizontal line, painted in something that shone faintly even without light.

Painted *over* the little chalk suns. Erasing them.

"They're marking territory," Felix said behind her. His voice in the tunnels was different—lower, flatter, the mercenary surfacing through the man. "Like a company staking a route."

"Worse. Look at the paint." She touched the wall without quite touching it. "There's a suspension medium in this. Bone-ash and quicksilver, maybe. It holds a charge. These aren't graffiti—they're a lattice. Someone taught the cult enough craft to build a sensing-web under the whole district." Her stomach tightened. "Which means someone *in* the cult knows real sigilcraft. Not hedge-work. Real."

"How much does that change our evening?"

"It changes whether we're hunting a vault or walking into a mouth."

They went on anyway, because the alternative was staying in a cellar until Sereth's charity curdled into risk, and because the inkwell was down here somewhere beneath them, behind the Customhouse walls, among the stolen books—and an inkwell holding the name of every soul ever bound by the covenant was not a thing to leave in the hands of men who painted bone-ash in the dark.

Paloma was thinking about names—about the specific weight of her own on a handbill, six years stale and nailed to a thousand posts—when the lattice found them.

She felt it as heat first, along the wall to her left, the painted half-circle warming like a stove-top. Then the next mark ahead, glowing up through its paint. Then, behind them, a third, and a sound that turned her blood to well-water: a bell. Not loud. Small, silvery, almost gentle. The kind of bell a shopkeeper rings.

Somewhere in the dark, many feet began to move.

"Tunnel fork ahead," Felix breathed. "Left goes down—I can feel the air pull. Right runs level toward the river."

"Right. Down means their cellars, and cellars mean corners."

They ran. The candle in its shutter threw their shadows huge and staggering against the brick; behind them the silver bell rang again, closer, and joined by a second, and a third, a little carillon of pursuit, and Paloma understood with sick clarity that the bells weren't for the hunters. They were for *her*. Announcing her. Turning every faithful ear in the district toward the sound of a woman running.

A grate ahead—Felix hit it with his shoulder once, twice, and iron gave with a scream of rust, and they were through into a wider gallery, an old cistern by the echo, and then torchlight bloomed behind them, dozens of torches, and a voice rolled down the tunnel, warm as honey poured over a blade:

"Sister! Sister Ibarra!"

Her name. Her whole name, spoken with love, into the dark.

"You don't need to hide from us! We know why you came home! You came to finish what your family started—and we *forgive* you—we only want to *help* you—"

"Keep moving," Felix said, low and hard, his hand steady on her back between her shoulder blades.

They ran through the dark while the city above them, the great blind city of ten thousand dead lamps, rang its little silver bells, and Paloma Ibarra fled through the veins of Corvane carrying her grandmother's name in her collar and her mother's craft in her bones, known—known, hunted, *cherished*—by ten thousand strangers who loved her exactly as much as they feared her.

The tunnel ahead split again, and this time neither path was dark: both glowed ahead with the soft bone-light of fresh-painted marks, converging, closing, a mouth being drawn shut around them.

And standing in the junction, unhurried, lantern raised, waiting as if he'd been waiting all night, was a man in a magistrate's gray coat with a scholar's kind eyes and a yellow scarf folded with surgical neatness at his throat.

"Paloma Ibarra," Oren Hale said, and his smile was the warmest thing in the city of unlit lamps. "We've read so much about you."
