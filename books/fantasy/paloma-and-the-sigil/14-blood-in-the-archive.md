## 14. Blood in the Archive

*Felix*

The archive beneath the Temple of the Second Dawn smelled of lamp oil and old vellum and, underneath both, something sweeter that Felix did not want to name. Incense, he told himself. Just incense.

Paloma moved ahead of him through the stacks with a shuttered lantern held low, its light a thin gold seam across the floor. Three nights they had spent learning the rhythm of the vault — the changing of the cult's grey-robed wardens, the hour when the false dawn bells rang and every ear in Corvane turned upward toward a sky that gave them nothing back. The inkwell sat in the inner reliquary behind a door of black iron, and Paloma had picked its lock twice in rehearsal without breathing hard.

She was good at this. That surprised him still, though it shouldn't have. He had spent his life around soldiers who treated stealth as a lesser discipline, something for assassins and cowards, but watching Paloma work he understood that what she did was its own kind of war — patient, precise, fought against locks and wards instead of men.

"Here." Her whisper barely disturbed the dust. She knelt before the iron door and drew three sigils in the air with a stylus of pale bone, and the lock sighed open like an old man settling into sleep.

The inkwell was small. That was the first thing Felix noticed, and the thing that made his skin crawl afterward — that so much weight could live in something the size of a fist. It was cut from a single piece of dark glass, banded in silver gone green with age, and when Paloma lifted it from its cradle the lantern flame bent toward it, hungry.

"It's warm," she murmured.

"Is it supposed to be?"

"Nothing about this is supposed to be anything anymore." She wrapped it in wool and stowed it against her ribs, and her hand stayed there a moment, pressed flat over it, as if she could feel a heartbeat through the layers. "Every name ever bound," she said softly. "My mother told me about it when I was small enough to believe she was telling me stories. I thought I'd never see it."

Felix said nothing. He had been saying nothing for eleven days now, since the oathstone in the drowned chapel had shown him its vision — Ibarra hands, Ibarra knives, priests of his god going down screaming into dark water — and he had decided, with a deliberateness that felt like rot, to carry that knowledge alone. Every silence made the next one easier. That was the arithmetic of concealment, and he hated that he knew it by heart.

"Move," he said instead. "Bells in a quarter hour."

They went out through the archive's eastern gallery, past reliquaries of things the cult had confiscated — sigil-stones cracked deliberately down their centers, a child's practice-slate covered in careful runes, a banner stitched with a constellation Felix almost recognized. The cult did not destroy these things. They catalogued them. That was somehow worse.

They were twenty paces from the drainage grate that would take them under the processional road when the lamps came up.

Not all at once. One, then another, then a slow bloom of light along the gallery's length, and standing in it, holding the first lamp himself with an apologetic tilt of his head, was Doss Harrow.

* * *

*Paloma*

She had liked Doss. That was the obscenity of it, the part she knew she would be turning over for years. In the refugee caravan outside the Hollow Country, when the world was dimming and people were beginning to look at each other like meat, Doss had shared his bread with children not his own and stood watch shifts he wasn't assigned and laughed — actually laughed — at Paloma's terrible impression of a guild examiner. A carter, broad as a barn door, with a gap in his smile and frostbite scars on two fingers. He had walked with them as far as Corvane because his sister lived there, and when they'd parted ways at the Lamb's Gate he had gripped Felix's forearm and said, *keep your head down, the city eats strangers.*

Now he stood between them and the drainage grate, and his sister's name was nowhere in his face.

"Doss," Paloma said carefully.

"Don't." His voice cracked on it. "Don't say my name like we're friends. It makes it harder."

"They have your sister." It wasn't a question. Magistrate Hale's method was legible to anyone who'd read the cult's handbills: salvation offered, salvation withheld. "Where are they keeping her?"

"The Lantern House." He swallowed. "They showed me her through a window. Fed her while I watched. They said — they said all I had to do was be somewhere at a certain time and make noise." His eyes went to the iron bell-pull mounted beside the gallery arch, and Paloma understood the whole architecture of it at once: not an ambush sprung by soldiers but a trap sprung by a bell, one pull and the wardens would come running, and Doss would get his sister back, and the cult would get a sigilwright and the man who'd refused Magistrate Hale to his face.

"They told me you were thieves," Doss said. "That you stole holy things."

"We did steal a holy thing," Paloma said. "It belongs to neither of us. It belongs to everyone whose name is written inside it, which means it belongs to you too, Doss, whether you know it or not—"

"Please don't." His voice rose. "Please. I've got nothing else. You understand that, don't you? You've got each other and a plan and a god who — whoever. I've got a sister in a room with a lamp and a bowl of food and a man outside her door, and if I don't ring that bell—"

He reached for it.

Later, Paloma would try to reconstruct those three seconds and fail. She remembered the bell-pull's brass catching the lamplight. She remembered Doss's frostbitten fingers closing on the cord. And she remembered Felix moving — not lunging, nothing so dramatic, just *arriving*, the way weather arrives, sudden and total — and the sound his elbow made against the side of Doss's throat, and Doss going down against the reliquary shelf, and the crack of old wood, and the strange ringing quiet after.

Doss didn't get up.

"Felix." Her own voice sounded far away. "Felix, is he—"

"He's breathing." Felix was already kneeling, two fingers at the carter's throat, his face lit from below by the fallen lamp and absolutely empty, which was how she knew how bad it was. Empty faces meant full rooms, locked ones. "Crushed windpipe, maybe. Cracked skull on the shelf edge. Breathing."

"You didn't have to hit him that hard."

"No." Felix stood. He looked at his own right arm as if it belonged to someone he was renting space to. "I didn't."

* * *

*Felix*

The worst part — the part he kept returning to, worrying it like a sore tooth — was that his body had done it well.

Twenty years of soldiering does not wash out because you spend eleven days walking roads with a woman who draws sigils and sleeps sitting up and says *please* to locks. Somewhere under the man he'd been trying to become, the old machinery was intact, oiled, waiting. Threat assessment: bell within reach, alarm imminent, subject larger than you and desperate enough to die pulling that cord. Solution: strike the airway, drop him before sound, control the fall. He hadn't chosen any of it. He'd simply been chosen, the way a hammer is chosen by the nail.

And some cold clerk in the back of his skull had already begun writing the report. *One hostile neutralized. Minimal noise. Extraction achievable.* The clerk had no opinion about Doss Harrow's sister in the Lantern House. The clerk had no opinion about anything.

"Felix." Paloma again. Sharper. "We have maybe ninety seconds before someone notices the lamps are lit. We need to go."

"He'll choke if we leave him flat." Even now, the clerk filing notes. Felix rolled Doss onto his side, into the recovery position drilled into every child soldier of the valley's levies, and saw the gap-toothed smile slack and unconscious and frost-bitten fingers curled loose, and felt something in his chest tear along a seam he'd thought had scarred over years ago.

In the valley, killing had been simple. That was the covenant's mercy, or the valley's lie — the god silent, the borders warded, violence given shape and rules and a border to stay inside. He had killed raiders and called it duty and slept fine. Out here the ledger had no margins. He had killed a friend's trust with his elbow in a lamp-lit archive, and the man might die of it, and his sister would never know why the bell never rang.

"His sister," Felix heard himself say. "When this is done. When we're done. We find out where they took her."

Paloma stared at him. "You want to rescue the sister of the man who sold us?"

"I want the ledger to balance." He heard how it sounded and didn't care. "I'm owed a debt I can't pay him. So I pay it forward to the only person left who can collect."

For a moment she looked like she was going to argue. Then something in her face shifted — recognition, maybe, or grief recognizing grief — and she nodded once, sharp, and turned for the grate.

They went down into the dark under Corvane, and above them, faint through stone, the false dawn bells began to ring.

* * *

*Paloma*

The undercity received them the way water receives a dropped stone: briefly troubled, then closed over. Paloma followed Felix through channels of runoff and older, stranger water, past doors bricked shut generations ago, under bridges built by masons whose names the inkwell probably held. She kept one hand on the wrapped inkwell at her ribs. It was warmer than it had been in the vault. She chose not to think about why.

They emerged near the tanneries, where the smell of the river covered the smell of them, and climbed a rope Felix had rigged two days earlier to a loft above a chandler's storeroom. Only when the hatch was shut and barred did either of them speak.

"You killed him." She said it flatly, to strip it of accusation. Accusation would give him somewhere to hide.

"He's alive."

"You know what I mean."

Felix sat down against the wall with his forearms on his knees. In the grey light through the slatted window he looked ten years older than he had in the archive, and younger too, somehow — stripped back to something raw. "In the valley," he said, "the first time I killed a man, I was fourteen. Border skirmish. He came over the wall and I put a spear in him and the elders told me the god saw, and that the god's seeing made it clean. Not righteous. Clean. Like washing." He turned his hands over, studied the palms. "Tonight there was no god seeing. There was just me and a man who wanted to save his sister, and I put him down in under a second because that's what I'm *for*, Paloma. That's the thing I am, under everything. I keep telling myself I left it in the valley, and it turns out I packed it."

"You saved our lives."

"I *managed* a threat." The word came out of him with self-disgust so pure it was almost impressive. "That's the phrase. I can hear the sergeant saying it. Managed a threat."

Paloma crouched in front of him. Up close she could see the tremor in his hands, fine as a plucked string, and understood that it wasn't fear. It was the shaking of a structure that had taken a load it was built for and discovered it no longer wanted the building.

"Listen to me," she said. "I grew up in a house where my family's whole trade was deciding what lives and dies for the sake of a seal. My grandmother signed names into that inkwell knowing half of them would bleed for it. If anyone here has earned the right to tell you what you are, it's me — and I'm telling you: a weapon is what you use. A butcher is what you enjoy. Tonight you used it, and you hate it, and the hating is the whole difference. Don't you dare hand the hating over to me or to your god or to anyone. Carry it. It's yours, and it's proof."

He looked at her for a long moment. "When did you get wise?"

"I've always been wise. You were busy being tall and tragic." But her voice caught on the last word, because the inkwell had shifted against her ribs — warmed another degree, unmistakably now, like a hand closing gently around hers from the inside.

Felix saw her face change. "What is it?"

"It knows we have it." She unwound the wool. In the chandlery's grey light the dark glass seemed to hold a depth it hadn't held in the vault, and across its surface, rising like breath on a cold window, letters were surfacing — silver, spidery, arranging themselves into lines. Names. Dozens of them. Hundreds. And among them, glowing brighter than the rest, fresher, as if newly inked:

**VASQUEZ, FELIX OF THE VALLEY — SWORN AT AGE SEVEN. OATH UNDISCHARGED.**

Paloma read it. Read it twice. Felt the secret she'd been carrying since the chapel — *his name was in here all along, he was bound the same way my family bound everyone* — collide with a new and much worse understanding.

"Felix," she said quietly. "This entry isn't old. Look at the ink. It's fresh."

"What does that mean?"

"It means the covenant isn't finished with you." She looked up from the glass and found him looking at the name, and the expression on his face was the one she imagined wore her own features more often than she liked: the look of a person reading their sentence in a language they only half spoke. "And I think," she said slowly, "it means something out there just remembered exactly where we are."

Below them, far off toward the Temple district, the false dawn bells began to ring a second time.

They had rung once already tonight. Everyone in Corvane knew they only rang once.

* * *

*Felix*

He was on his feet before the thought finished forming. "Wardens don't ring it twice. It's ceremony. Ceremony means—" 

"Hale," Paloma breathed. "Hale is doing something public. At night. Without a scheduled dawn."

Through the slats, the sky over the Temple district was beginning to change. Not dawn — Felix knew dawn, knew its colors the way he knew the grain of his own spear-shaft — this was light without a source, a bruised gold spreading from the temple spire like ink dropped in water, climbing, brightening, and beneath it the city's voice rose: thousands of people pouring into streets, shouting, weeping, singing the cult's hymn.

*The second dawn comes. The second dawn comes for the faithful.*

"They're announcing us," Felix said. "Stolen relics, enemies of salvation — he doesn't need to catch us quietly anymore. He's turning the whole city into his net." He grabbed the rope coil, threw the hatch, and the singing came up to meet them, sweet and terrible, ten thousand throats believing with everything they had in a sun that did not exist.

Paloma clutched the inkwell to her chest. Against the rising light her face was pale and set, and Felix saw that she had already stopped looking at Corvane and started looking east, toward the dark line of mountains on the horizon, toward the forge-peak her mother's notes had named.

"The archive bought us hours, not days," she said. "We run tonight. North road, then the pass."

"And the fragments?"

"Two in hand." Her jaw tightened. "One to find."

Felix took one last look back at the burning-gold sky, at the city singing itself blind, at the Lantern House somewhere in its maze where a sister waited behind a door for a bell that would never ring.

*Hold on,* he thought, with no idea who he was promising, or how a mercenary with blood on his knuckles and his name freshly inked in a dead god's ledger intended to pay anyone back for anything. *I keep accounts now. All of them.*

Then he went over the roof's edge, into the dark, and ran.
