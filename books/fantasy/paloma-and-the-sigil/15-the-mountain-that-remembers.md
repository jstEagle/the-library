## 15. The Mountain That Remembers

The mountain had been waiting for them longer than either of them had been alive.

Paloma felt it before she saw it—a pressure in her chest like a hand pressed flat against the breastbone, gentle and insistent, the way her mother used to press two fingers to Paloma's sternum when she wanted her to pay attention. *Feel that?* her mother would say, kneeling beside the small sigil-circle chalked on the kitchen floor, flour still dusting her knuckles. *That's the world noticing you. Don't ever flinch from it.*

She flinched now. She couldn't help it.

They had been climbing for three days through country that grew stranger with every mile—forest giving way to pine, pine to bare grey scree where nothing moved except wind and the occasional wrongness of shadows that fell in directions the sun didn't justify. The stars were gone. They had been gone for weeks, and the sky above the high passes was a smooth, featureless dark, like a lid set over the world. Without starlight to anchor it, the night seemed less like an absence and more like a presence, something leaning down close enough to breathe against their necks.

Felix walked ahead, as he always did, his stride unhurried but constant, a mercenary's economy of motion that wasted nothing, not even grief. He carried both packs when she wasn't looking. She pretended not to notice. It was one of the small dishonesties they'd agreed upon without ever speaking of it—he carried more than his share, and she let him believe she didn't see.

"The trail splits ahead," he called back, voice low. He didn't need to whisper; there was no one left out here to hear them. But weeks of being hunted taught habits that outlasted the hunters. "Left goes up along the ridge. Right goes into a cut in the rock."

"Right," Paloma said.

He glanced at her. "You're sure?"

"I'm sure." She came up beside him and looked at the fork: two paths, one sensible and one that looked like a throat swallowed by stone. "My mother drew this mountain for me when I was seven. Ink on the back of a ledger page. She said—" Paloma stopped. The memory arrived whole, unbidden, the way memories of the dead always did, complete and merciless.

Felix waited. He was good at waiting. It was, she suspected, what twenty years of praying to a silent god taught a person—the discipline of standing in front of something that would not answer and refusing to walk away.

"She said the easy way is how the guild finds you," Paloma finished. "The hard way is how the mountain decides if it wants you."

"And did it want you?"

"I never got far enough to find out." She started toward the cut. "I was seven. My mother took me as far as the tree line, pointed at the peak, and told me that one day I would climb it alone, and that I would hate her for making me learn everything I needed to learn first."

"Did you?"

"Hate her?" Paloma laughed, and it came out cracked. "Ask me again when we're at the top."

* * *

The cut in the rock was older than roads.

Paloma knew it the way she knew letters—by shape, by lineage. The walls rose sheer on either side, and carved into them, worn nearly smooth by centuries of weather, were sigils. Not the decorative kind, not the merchant-grade wards the guild sold to caravans and nervous landowners. These were structural. Foundational. Each one the size of a door, cut deep, filled once with silver that had long since been pried out or crumbled away, so that now they stood as hollow outlines—a script written in absence.

She stopped at the first one and put her hand against it.

Nothing. Of course nothing. The covenant was broken; the network of power that had fed these marks had died with it. But her fingers remembered what they were supposed to feel—the hum of a living line, the sense of touching a vein. Her hand rested on cold granite and the silence of it went through her like a needle.

"This is the Processional," she said. Her voice sounded strange in the narrow space, flattened by stone. "Every sigilwright of my line made this climb. You don't get your mark until you've walked it end to end and can name every glyph in order."

"How many are there?"

"Ninety-one." She began walking again, trailing her fingertips along the wall without quite touching, the way you might pass your hand over the face of someone sleeping. "My mother could name all ninety-one in under four minutes. There's a story about her doing it blindfolded, drunk, during an argument with the Guildmaster, just to prove a point."

"What was the point?"

"That she was better than him." Paloma smiled despite herself. "She was right, too. That was the problem. Ibarra women were always right, and the guild hated us for it, and kept us anyway, because we were also the only ones who could do the work."

Felix walked half a step behind her, which she understood was how he guarded someone—not beside, where he couldn't watch both them and the path. "You miss her," he said. Not a question.

"I miss who I was when she was alive." The words came out before she could weigh them, and once out, she refused to take them back. "When she was alive, I was a daughter learning a craft. When she died, I became the last of something. Those aren't the same thing. One of them is a person. The other is a responsibility wearing a dead woman's clothes."

Ahead, the pass narrowed further, and the light—what little the overcast day allowed—dimmed to a grey murmur. Somewhere above them, wind moaned across the peak like a voice trying to remember a song.

* * *

They camped that night in a hollow beneath the forty-third glyph, because Paloma's mother had drawn the campsite too—*here, where the stone curves like a cupped hand, because the wind cannot find you*—and her mother had been right, as always. The hollow held them like something protective. Or something possessive. Paloma lay on her bedroll staring at the starless black above and could not decide which.

Felix sat propped against the stone, cleaning his blade though it didn't need it. He did that when his thoughts were loud.

"My mother's last lesson," Paloma said into the darkness, "was about this mountain. I was fourteen. She was dying—you know that part, I think, the fever year. She couldn't get out of bed, so she made me sit on the floor next to her and she talked for six hours while I memorized. Every glyph. Every turn of the path. Where the water runs, where the old forge-fires burned hot enough to vitrify the rock, which ledges lie." She turned her head to look at him. "I thought she was teaching me to be a sigilwright. She wasn't. She was teaching me how to bury her properly."

Felix's knife stopped moving.

"Because the forge-peak is where the Ibarra dead go. Not to the ground—to the fire. The original cutting-blade was forged up there, and the covenant says—" Paloma's throat closed. She worked past it. "The covenant says the hands that bind must one day return to the flame. So she gave me the whole mountain, top to bottom, inside my skull, because she knew I'd be carrying her up it someday. And then she died before she could teach me anything else. Anything that wasn't duty."

The silence stretched. Below them, somewhere in the dark valley, a wolf sang—one note, wavering, uncertain. Even the wolves had grown strange since the stars went out.

"When I was sworn," Felix said finally, quietly, "they gave me wine and a brand and a name for my god that no one else was allowed to say. I was nine years old." He turned the knife over in his hands. "For twenty years I thought that was the worst thing that had ever been done to me. Being given a god before I was old enough to choose one." He looked at her. "You're the only person I've ever met who understands what that is. Being handed a life you didn't pick, and loving it anyway, and hating it, both at once, forever."

"Yes," Paloma said. "Exactly that."

She wanted to say more. She wanted to tell him about the rite—the real rite, the one her guild had demanded of her, blood and death dressed up as renewal—and the words climbed her throat like climbers up a cliff face and then, at the last hold, let go. Not yet. He had secrets; she had seen that much in him, the careful pockets behind his eyes. She could keep hers one night longer.

But lying awake after he slept, listening to his breathing slow into trust, she found herself tracing the forty-third glyph in the dark with one finger, mouthing its name as her mother had taught her, and understanding—for the first time, truly—that inheritance was not a gift. It was a wound that opened exactly the size of whatever was handed through it. Her mother had poured a whole mountain through a fourteen-year-old girl, and the girl had scarred around the shape of it, and now every step upward pressed on the scar, and every press said the same thing:

*This was yours before you were born. This will be yours after you die. You were never asked.*

* * *

On the fifth day, they reached the snow line, and the Processional ended.

The ninety-first glyph stood alone on a shelf of black stone, larger than the others, its silver channels empty, its lines sharp-edged even after generations because no rain reached this height—only wind, and wind does not wear away purpose. Paloma stood before it with her breath smoking in the freezing air and named it aloud, as the rite required, though there was no examiner here but the mountain itself.

"*Veth-sura-kel,*" she said. "The Gate of Return. Ninth house. Cut by Sera Ibarra, first of the line, in the Year of Ash." Her voice echoed off the shelf and came back thinner. "Its charge is memory. Its price is forgetting."

Beside her, Felix had gone very still.

"You know it," she said.

"I know the sound of it." His face had changed—something behind his eyes shifting, like ice shifting on a river. "The priests in the valley had a chant. Different words, same cadence. We sang it every solstice, facing east." He frowned, troubled. "Facing *this*, I suppose. Toward the mountain. I never asked why. You never ask why, when you're sworn. Asking why is how you stop being useful."

"The valley was founded by covenant-keepers, Felix. Your priests' chants and my family's glyphs came from the same source. Two halves of one sentence." She touched the glyph's lowest channel, the place where the silver should pool. "We were never separate traditions. We were one rite, split apart so neither half could remember the whole."

"Who split it?"

"I don't know. My mother didn't know—or she wouldn't say." Paloma withdrew her hand. Above them, past the shelf, the true summit rose into cloud: a black spire veined with ancient slag, the forge itself, hidden now but burning once in her memory like a coal under ash. "That's the thing about my family. Every generation knew slightly less than the one before, and called it prudence. We inherited a locked house and were praised for keeping the doors shut."

She began the final climb, and Felix followed, and the wind rose around them with a sound almost like speech—almost like a great many voices, murmuring, expectant.

* * *

The summit broke open before them at dusk.

It was not what she had imagined in twenty years of imagining. She had pictured a crater, a ruined altar, some grand desolation worthy of the covenant's fall. Instead there was a plateau of fused glass-black rock, smooth as a frozen lake, and at its center a structure: low walls, a collapsed roof-beam, a hearth-mouth gaping dark. A house. A small, ordinary house, built at the roof of the world, its stones fitted with a precision no mason working today could match.

And above it, where the chimney would be—

Paloma's legs nearly failed her.

A sigil hung in the air. Not carved. Not drawn. *Suspended*—a wheel of faint ember-light turning slowly above the ruin, dim as a dying coal, holding on. The original mark. The master-pattern from which every ward, every oath, every glyph in the Processional descended. Broken, yes—fractured through its heart, one quadrant dark—but still turning, still *insisting*, after all these years, the way a heart keeps beating a little while after the body has decided otherwise.

"It's still lit," she whispered.

"Is that good?"

"I don't know." Her eyes stung with cold and something else. "It means someone kept paying. After my mother. After the rite failed. Someone came up here and fed it, alone, generation after generation, knowing it was broken, hoping—" Her voice broke clean through. She pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes. "Gods. All those years I spent hating my family for what they demanded of me, and someone was *still climbing this mountain*."

Felix's hand settled on her shoulder. Heavy, warm, wordless. He had learned, in these weeks together, when words were worthless.

Paloma lowered her hand and breathed until she could speak. Then she looked at the dark doorway of the little house—at the hearth within, banked with ash that did not scatter, preserved by the same stubborn magic that kept the sigil turning—and felt the mountain notice her fully for the first time, the way her mother's fingers had once noticed her, pressing gently against her chest.

*Pay attention. Don't flinch.*

Inside the hearth, in the ash, something glinted.

And as she took the first step toward it, the ember-wheel above the ruin shuddered, brightened—and spoke, in a voice like her mother's voice, worn thin by decades of solitude:

"*Who carries the Ibarra name up the Processional?*"
