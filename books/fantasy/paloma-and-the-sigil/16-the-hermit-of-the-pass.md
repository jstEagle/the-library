## 16. The Hermit of the Pass

The mountain did not want them on it.

Felix had climbed in bad country before—scree slopes in the Ashen Reaches, the black ice of the Kettering shelf—but this was different. The mountain *objected*. The wind came at them sideways and then stopped entirely, so that the silence pressed on his ears like water. The rock under his boots was warm. Not sun-warm; there had been no sun worth mentioning for weeks, only the bruised grey smear that passed for daylight now. This warmth came from below, steady as a sleeping animal's flank, and it made the hair on his arms stand up.

Paloma climbed ahead of him, small and stubborn against the grey, her breath tearing out in white rags. She hadn't spoken in two hours. Since the frozen battlefield, since the knight with the rusted heart who had thanked them with his last breath, she had gone quiet in a way Felix recognized because he'd worn it himself after his first killing: the silence of someone carrying something they haven't decided how to set down.

He let her carry it. He had his own load.

*They killed your god's priests,* the oathstone had shown him, weeks ago now, though some nights it felt like yesterday and other nights like another life. *Your Paloma's ancestors, in their white robes, cutting down the faithful in the grove while the sky went dark.* He had carried that vision through drowned chapels and burning archives and a city full of people who wanted her dead, and he had said nothing, and every day the silence between them grew a little heavier, a little more like the god's own silence, and he wondered sometimes if concealment was simply what faith turned into when it got old enough.

"Here," Paloma said suddenly, stopping so fast he nearly walked into her.

She was staring at a cleft in the mountainside—a crack no wider than a doorway, half-hidden behind a curtain of frost-stiffened juniper. Warm air breathed out of it, smelling of woodsmoke and something older underneath, something mineral and deep, like the inside of the world.

"How do you know?" Felix asked.

"I don't." Her hands were already moving, tracing a sigil in the frost on the stone—quick, practiced, the way other people crossed themselves. "My mother told me once, when I was small. *If you ever have to climb the remembering mountain, look for the door that breathes.* I thought it was a story to make me sleep." Her fingers paused. "She never told me stories to make me sleep. Every one of them was a map."

Felix looked at the cleft. His god—the silent one, the one whose presence had been a weight at the base of his skull for twenty years, familiar as his own heartbeat—was doing something he had never felt it do before. It was *leaning*. Like a man at a window. Like a prisoner hearing footsteps on the stair.

"Whatever's in there," he said, "it knows we're coming."

"Good." Paloma ducked into the crack without waiting for him, which was very like her, and Felix followed her into the dark with the particular resignation of a man who has long ago stopped pretending he had any choice about any of this.

* * *

The passage opened almost at once into a hollow in the mountain's heart, and Felix stopped dead on the threshold because the room was impossible.

It was round, and high, and lit—not by fire, though a fire burned in a low ring of stones at its center, but by the walls themselves. Thousands of marks covered the stone, floor to ceiling, spiraling inward: sigils, but not like Paloma's. Hers were precise, architectural, drawn with a ruler's discipline. These were cut deep and rough, gouged into the rock by hand over what must have been lifetimes, and they glowed with a faint amber light that pulsed, very slowly, like breathing. Like a heartbeat measured in years.

And among the sigils, everywhere, were stars. Carved stars, painted stars, stars scratched over and recut when they'd faded. Hundreds of them. A whole ceiling of remembered sky in a world that had lost its own.

"You're late."

The voice came from the far side of the fire, and Felix's hand went to his knife before his mind caught up. An old man sat cross-legged on a pallet of woven juniper, so still that Felix genuinely had not seen him until he spoke. He was ancient—not old, *ancient*—with skin like tanned leather and eyes the pale grey of river ice. His hair was white and long and unbound. He wore robes that might once have been fine, dyed a deep ceremonial blue, now patched with fur and sacking, and around his neck, on a cord of braided hair, hung a badge of office Felix knew from the oldest carvings in the valley temple: a circle bisected by a single vertical line.

The mark of the covenant-keepers. The order the valley claimed had never existed.

"You know us," Paloma said. Her voice was careful. Felix could hear the effort in it, the sigilwright holding herself level the way she held a stylus.

"I know what you are." The old man did not rise. "A daughter of Ibarra, come home to the mountain. And—" his ice-grey eyes moved to Felix, and something in them sharpened, "—ah. There you are. I wondered which of the sworn would come. It's been a long time since one of you walked this far."

"What do you mean, *which of the sworn*?" Felix asked.

The old man smiled. It was not a comforting smile. It was the smile of someone who has waited a very long time to say something and intends to enjoy saying it properly.

"Sit down, soldier. Both of you. Eat first. What I have to tell you goes down easier with bread." He nodded at a flat loaf warming on a stone near the fire, and Felix realized with a lurch that the man had baked for three. Had known, somehow, that three mouths were coming up his mountain. "My name was Aldous Vane. Before that it was nothing, the way all our names are nothing. I kept the covenant for sixty-one years. I am the last keeper who remembers it whole. And you—" the pale eyes settled on Felix again, patient as stone, "—you have been lied to since the day they put the oath on you. So eat. And then I will show you what actually happened in the grove."

Felix's stomach dropped through the warm floor of the mountain.

*The grove.*

He hadn't told anyone about the grove.

* * *

He ate because his body insisted, though he tasted nothing. Paloma ate too, sitting close to him, closer than she needed to, and he understood it was not affection—it was positioning. She was watching the old man the way she watched unstable sigils. Waiting for the flaw.

The hermit—Vane—let them finish. Then he rose, joints cracking like green wood, and shuffled to the wall of carved sigils, and pressed his palm flat against a spiral near the floor.

"Sixty-one years I kept watch here," he said. "Not praying. Watching. That was the whole of the covenant-keeper's work, girl, in case your mother never got that far: we didn't serve the god. We served the *silence*. Every hour of every day, one of us sat somewhere in this world and listened to the quiet, and made sure it stayed quiet, and grieved, because we knew what the quiet cost Him." He looked back over his shoulder. "You've felt it, haven't you, soldier. What the silence is like from the outside. Twenty years of it. Now imagine being the one who chose it."

"Chose it," Felix repeated. The words came out flat.

"The vision showed you the grove." Vane turned fully now, and the amber light of the walls moved across his ruined face. "White robes. Blood on the grass. Your god's priests dying. Am I wrong?"

"No," Felix said. His mouth was dry. Beside him, Paloma had gone very still.

"And you've spent every mile since deciding what that meant. That her family murdered their way to power. That the binding was a cage built on corpses. That your god was taken, screaming, and shut away." Vane shook his head slowly. "That is exactly what the valley elders wanted you to believe, when they burned the records. It's even true, in the way a knife is true. Now look at the rest of the blade."

He drew his hand down the spiral, and the sigils flared.

The chamber filled with light, and the light filled with images—not the hard-edged cruelty of the oathstone's vision but something softer, older, seen as if through water or tears. Felix saw the grove again, the same grove, the same white-robed figures—and saw what the oathstone had cropped away.

The priests were not fleeing. They were kneeling.

They knelt in rows in the trampled grass, dozens of them, heads bowed, and the men and women in white moved among them not with swords but with knives—small ones, ritual ones—and each priest, before the blade touched him, lifted his own head and bared his own throat. Some were weeping. One was smiling. At the center of the grove stood a figure of light that Felix's body recognized before his mind did, a presence vast and wounded and *awake*, and it was not struggling. Its radiance bent over the kneeling priests like a father bending over children, and where it touched them, they did not scream.

"They gave themselves," Paloma whispered. Her hand had found Felix's sleeve without her seeming to notice. "The payment wasn't taken. It was—"

"Volunteered," Vane said. "Every generation. The covenant demanded blood—Ibarran blood, keeper's blood, the blood of those who cut the seal—and the god refused, at first, to accept it from anyone who hadn't chosen it freely. Do you understand what I'm telling you? The binding was not a prison. It was a *surgery*. And the god held the knife Himself, and guided it, and wept while He did it, because the thing it cut out of the world was worse than anything a cage could hold."

The image shifted. The grove dissolved, and something else bloomed in the amber light, and Felix's skin went cold all over.

A star. But wrong—a star the way a wound is wrong, a star with hunger where its heart should be, hanging in a darkness deeper than night, and around it the world dimmed in widening rings, crops browning, rivers slowing, beasts turning their eyes toward it and forgetting everything else. He had seen its like before. In the ravine, dying, he had felt it sharing the silence with his god—ancient, patient, *waiting*.

"The Hollow Star," he said.

"The Hollow Star," Vane agreed grimly. "Older than the covenant. Older than the valley. It eats light, and it eats meaning, and worst of all it eats *names*—it unmakes things by making them forget what they are. Your god fought it alone for an age, soldier, and He was losing. Not because He was weak. Because you cannot kill a hunger. You can only starve it, wall it, hide it. And to hide a thing that hears everything, sees everything, tastes every prayer in the world like a shark tastes blood—" Vane tapped his own chest, "—you must go silent yourself. Utterly. Forever. Because every word your god spoke, every blessing, every answered prayer, was a thread the Star could follow back to the prison door."

The images wheeled. Felix saw the forging—he saw it whole now, the four fragments gathered, the Ibarras standing in a ring with their sleeves rolled back, and above them the great figure of light pressing its own hands together in a gesture Felix had never been taught but understood instantly: *seal me in with it. Seal me in, and stop your ears, and do not listen for Me, however long it takes.*

"He consented," Felix said. His voice sounded strange to him, distant, underwater. "He asked for it."

"He *begged* for it," Vane said. "The last thing He ever said aloud, to the first Ibarra, was this: *Tell my faithful I am not gone. Tell them the silence is My voice now. And tell them never, ever to try to wake Me until the Star is certain to stay buried—because if they call to Me too loudly, they will lead It straight to the door.*" The old man's ice-grey eyes fixed on Felix. "And then the generations passed, and the telling became ritual, and the ritual became law, and the law became a lie, because the elders of your valley decided it was safer for the faithful to believe their god was *taken* than to believe He had *chosen*—because a stolen god demands rescue, and a god who chose silence demands something much harder."

Felix's knees found the floor. He didn't remember deciding to sit.

Twenty years. Twenty years of prayers poured into a silence he had believed was absence, was abandonment, was the long cold proof that heaven did not answer soldiers. Twenty years of serving a god he'd privately mourned like a dead father—and the whole time—

"He heard me," Felix said. "All of it. Every patrol prayer. Every night in the barracks. He heard everything and couldn't say a word, because answering me would have—"

"—killed the world," Vane finished gently. "Yes. That was the covenant. That was always the covenant. Not a cage. A *vow*. Taken by a god, kept by a family, guarded by keepers, and paid for—" his gaze slid to Paloma, and his voice softened into something terrible, "—by everyone who loved Him enough to let Him go."

Paloma was crying. She didn't seem to know it; her face was composed, sigilwright-steady, but the tears ran freely down and dripped off her jaw onto the warm stone. "My mother," she said. "The payments. The rites. We thought—we were taught it was duty, it was price, it was—" She stopped. Started again. "She used to sit awake at night, listening to nothing. I thought she was grieving the family we'd lost. She was keeping Him company. Wasn't she. All those years. She was just *sitting with Him* in the dark so He wouldn't be alone in it."

"She was," Vane said. "As I did. As hundreds did, across the centuries, in caves and attics and forgotten chapels, one listener at a time." He looked at the walls of carved stars. "We were never a powerful order, girl. We were a *faithful* one. There's a difference, and the world is about to relearn it."

* * *

Later—how much later Felix couldn't say; time moved strangely in the breathing room—Paloma slept by the fire, finally, deeply, her exhaustion winning at last over vigilance. Vane sat across from Felix with a clay cup of something bitter and hot, and for a long while neither of them spoke.

Then Felix said, "The vision. The oathstone showed me the lie. Why? If the fragments are pieces of the covenant—if they're His—why show me the thing that would turn me against her?"

Vane considered him over the rim of his cup.

"Because the oathstone doesn't lie," he said at last. "It shows truth, and lets the viewer do the lying. You saw priests die in a grove, and you concluded murder, because murder was the story your valley raised you on. The stone showed you a wound and waited to see whether you'd flinch from it or look closer." He set the cup down. "You didn't look closer, soldier. You carried the lie for weeks, right next to the woman whose family died of it. That's not condemnation. That's just what oaths do—they make liars of us, because an oath is a story we agree to keep telling." His pale eyes glinted. "The question is what you'll do with the truth now that it's sitting in your lap heavier than the lie ever was."

Felix looked at the fire. At Paloma, asleep, her face young and unguarded for once, her hands curled loosely in her lap—the hands that would cut the new covenant, if there was going to be one. Hands descended from people who had volunteered their throats, generation after generation, to a god they loved enough to bury alive.

"There's something else," he said quietly. "In the ravine. When I was dying. Something shared the silence with Him. Something that talked."

The warmth went out of the room. Or perhaps it only seemed to; perhaps it was only Vane going still, utterly still, the stillness of a man who has waited sixty-one years for a question and dreaded it all the same.

"Tell me exactly what it said," the last keeper of the covenant said. "Word for word. Because if the Hollow Star has learned to speak in His silence, then everything we believed about how long we have—" he rose, stiff and swift, and pulled a dust-sheet off something in the corner that Felix had taken for a bundle of kindling, "—is wrong. And you both need to see what I've been keeping under this cloth since before either of you was born."

Beneath the sheet, catching the firelight, lay a map. Cut into a slab of the same warm stone as the walls, dense with sigils Felix had never seen—and at its center, freshly scored, as if within the last few days, a single line was burning itself slowly southward.

Toward Corvane.

Toward Hale.

Toward the false dawn.
