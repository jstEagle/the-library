## 21. Paloma in Chains

They took her boots first.

Paloma never learned the guard's name—the one who crouched at her cell door on the second night and unlaced them with careful, almost apologetic fingers—but she understood the logic of it immediately. Sigilwrights worked in ink and blood and intention, but they also worked in stance. Her mother had spent hours correcting the way she planted her feet for a long cutting: heels grounded, weight sunk, the whole body a channel for the line being drawn. Barefoot on cold stone, a sigilwright was half herself. It was a small cruelty, and it was precise, and precision like that told her more about her captors than any interrogation could have.

The cell was not a dungeon. That was the first thing that unsettled her. It was a room—high-ceilinged, dry, with a narrow window set too high to see out of but wide enough to admit a gray, starless light. There was a cot with a wool blanket. A basin. A chair. Even a shelf, empty, waiting, as if someone had considered what a prisoner might want to put upon it. The walls were whitewashed stone, and every inch of them had been painted over with a fine, repeating script so small she had to press her nose to the surface to read it.

Wards. Of course. But not crude ones. She traced the looping characters with one fingertip, feeling the faint resistance of fresh pigment, and felt something twist in her chest that was not quite fear and not quite admiration. The hand was good. Better than good. The lines were confident without being arrogant, each terminal flourish closed exactly where it should close, no wasted stroke anywhere. Whoever had written these walls had been trained in the old Ibarra school—or in a school descended from it—and had been trained *well*.

*You are admiring your cage,* she told herself. *That is how they win.*

But it was true, and she let it be true, because lying to herself about craft was the one lie she refused to tell. The wards were beautiful. And they were hers, in a way—her tradition, her inheritance, turned inside out and used to hold her. Somewhere in Corvane there was a hand that wrote like her mother's students had written, and Paloma lay awake on the cot under the blanket they'd given her, staring at the ceiling, trying to imagine whose hand it was.

She did not sleep well. In the valley, under Felix's grudging watch, she had slept better than she had in years—there was something about being guarded by a man who took guarding seriously that let a body finally unclench. Here there was no Felix. Here there was only the small repeating script on the walls, whispering its closed loops, and the memory of the moment in the undercity when the lamplighters' net had come down over her and a voice had said, almost gently:

*Careful with her hands. She's an Ibarra.*

They knew. They had known before she ever reached Corvane. That thought circled her like the ward-script circled the room, closing where it began, beginning where it closed.

* * *

On the fourth day—they brought food twice a day, so she counted in meals—the door opened without a key turning, which told her the lock answered to will rather than iron, and Magistrate Oren Hale came in carrying two cups of tea.

She had seen him once before, across the vaulted dark of the cult's sanctuary, when the chase through the undercity ended and the nets closed. He had been robed then, distant, a figure at an altar. Now he wore plain civic black, magistrate's chain, no ornament except a single ring of dull silver on his right index finger. He was younger than his reputation suggested—forty perhaps, with a scholar's stoop already forming in his shoulders and eyes the color of weak tea, ordinary and patient and utterly without warmth. He set both cups on the shelf. One he pushed toward her.

"Willow bark," he said. "And something for the headache. You've been frowning at my walls for four days."

"Your walls?" Paloma did not touch the cup. "I assumed the city's walls belonged to the city."

"The city belongs to whoever keeps it lit." He sat down in the chair—not behind her, not looming, but beside, angled, the posture of a colleague rather than a jailer. "You've been reading the script. Don't bother denying it; you've worn a shine onto the paint by the window. What do you think of it?"

"It's competent," she said.

Something flickered across his face—hurt, quickly mastered, and the quickness of the mastering told her the hurt was real. "Competent," he repeated.

"The terminals are closed properly. The spacing is disciplined. But you've anchored the whole array to a single node—" she nodded at the corner above the door, "—which means anyone who understands the school can unravel it from that point. My grandmother would have failed you for the redundancy. My mother would have failed you for the pride. A wall that wants to be admired is a wall that wants to be read."

Silence. Hale looked at the corner she'd indicated, and his jaw tightened, and Paloma felt the strange vertigo of recognizing, in an enemy's face, the exact expression she wore when her own work was criticized. She had worn it in her guild master's study at nineteen. She had worn it last month, on the ash road, when Felix had said her ward-drawing wasted motion.

He recovered. He even smiled, thin and genuine in its thinness. "Who taught you?"

"My mother. Who taught you?"

"Ah." He rose, walked to the window, stood with his back to her. "There it is. Straight to it. No tea, no courtesy. Very well—I'll answer, since you've earned it with that crack about the anchor node, which is correct, and which cost me three years to learn was correct." He turned. "My mother's name was Sera Hale. Before that, before she married, her name was Sera Voss. Does the name mean anything to you?"

Paloma kept her face still. It cost her. "Voss," she said carefully. "There were Vosses in the covenant rolls. Secondary line. Ink-keepers."

"Ink-keepers." He laughed, short and bitter. "Yes. That's what the Ibarras called us. The secondary line. The ones who ground the pigment and memorized the names and never, ever touched the blade. Four hundred years of service, and in all your family's histories we are a footnote—a footnote *you* didn't even remember until I said the name." He came back to the chair but did not sit. He stood over it, hands resting on its back, and Paloma saw now that his knuckles were scarred in the particular way of hands that had been burned learning fire-craft too young. "I was trained, Paloma Ibarra. Trained from the age of six, same as you. I can cut a binding-line in my sleep. I can write the covenant script blindfolded in my own blood. Do you know why you've never heard of me? Why there is no sigilwright named Oren Hale in any guild registry in this country?"

"Because the guilds don't register heretics."

"Because I was *tested*," he said, "and found wanting. When I was seventeen. The rite of measure—you know it, don't you, they still do it in exile, I'd wager they did it to you. They open the channels and see how much light the vessel holds. Mine held almost nothing. A trickle. Enough to light a lamp, not enough to cut a covenant. The examiner wrote in his ledger—" Hale's voice went flat and careful, the voice of a man reciting something he had recited ten thousand times to the mirror, "—'The vessel fails. Recommend the line be retired from craft-work.' Retired. At seventeen. Like a horse with a bad leg." He sat down at last, slowly, and picked up his own untouched tea, and held it without drinking. "So I became a magistrate instead. And then the stars went out. And then, wouldn't you know it, the world discovered that the little trickle in my veins was more than most people had left."

Paloma's hands were cold. She folded them in her lap so he wouldn't see.

Because here was the thing—the thing she had been refusing to look at since the nets came down, since the voice said *careful with her hands*: she recognized him. Not his politics, not his cruelty, not the cult with its false dawn and its burning of sigilwrights. *Him.* The shape of him. A child raised inside a great inheritance, measured against it, found insufficient, and hollowed out by the finding. She knew that hollowing from the inside. She had fled her own guild because they wanted to force the rite through her, yes—but underneath the flight, underneath the guilt she carried for her ancestors' bargain, there had always been a smaller, meaner fear she had never said aloud to anyone, not even to Felix at the fireline, not even to herself past midnight:

*What if the vessel fails?*

Her magic was strong—strong enough that the guild had marked her for the anchor's role, strong enough that her mother's notes read like instructions addressed to her specifically. But strength was not the same as sufficiency. Every Ibarra for twenty generations had been measured and found sufficient, and every one of them had paid for it in blood and years, and Paloma had lain awake more nights than she would admit wondering whether she was the generation the line broke on. Whether the examiner would write *the vessel fails* in his neat ledger hand, and whether she would spend the rest of her life as Oren Hale had spent his: competent, trained, and barred from the only thing that gave her name meaning.

She looked at him—at the scarred knuckles, the tea going cold in his hands, the hunger he thought he had hidden behind a magistrate's patience—and she thought: *there but for the examiner's ledger sits I.*

It was the most frightening thing she had felt since the stars went out. More frightening than the wight in the drowned chapel, more frightening than the Hollow Star's whisper through the mouths of Corvane's citizens. Because you could refuse a monster. It was very hard to refuse a mirror.

"You're looking at me strangely," Hale observed.

"I'm deciding whether to pity you or spit at you."

"Both, probably. People usually manage both." He drank the cold tea anyway, grimacing. "Let me tell you what I want, since we're being honest. I want the covenant reforged. Not restored—reforged. Your family's version was a leash and a bleeding post: one bloodline chained to the work forever, everyone else free to forget. I intend a better one. A stronger anchor. An anchor chosen for capacity rather than accident of birth."

"And that anchor is you." She made her voice bored. "A vessel that holds a trickle."

"A vessel that holds a trickle," Hale agreed pleasantly, "until the forging fills it. That's the secret your family kept, isn't it? The anchor doesn't need capacity beforehand. The rite *makes* capacity—it pours the god's overflow into the anchor and the anchor becomes the seal. Your ancestors weren't chosen because they were strong. They were strong because they were chosen. Any vessel will do, if the pouring is done correctly." He leaned forward. "Any vessel. Do you understand what I'm telling you? The examiner's ledger was wrong. The measure was wrong. There has never been anything wrong with me except that I was born into the wrong branch of the wrong family, in an age when the covenant was already sealed and there was nothing left to pour."

*Except,* Paloma thought, *the covenant was sealed precisely so there would be nothing left to pour. Except the silence was the mercy. Except everything your family served, you want to unmake so that you can be the one it serves through.*

But she said none of it. She was learning the shape of this conversation, and the shape was: he wanted her to argue. He wanted her to defend the old covenant, the old order, the Ibarra monopoly—because then he could cast himself as the reformer, the injured party, the man with the better plan. Every objection she raised would feed him.

So instead she asked, "Why am I alive?"

Hale blinked. "Because—"

"You have the fragments' locations. You have scholars, soldiers, a city. You caught me in your net within a day of entering Corvane. If any vessel will do, you need neither my blood nor my knowledge. So why am I alive, Magistrate? Why the boots off, the tea, the courtesy? Why haven't you simply begun?"

For the first time, he looked away from her. He looked at his own scarred hands, and when he spoke again his voice had lost its polish entirely, and what was underneath was rawer and far more dangerous.

"Because the pouring requires a living Ibarra to open the channel," he said. "The old texts are clear. The anchor receives, but the door must be opened by the line that made it. Blood calls to blood. I can forge the covenant around myself, but I cannot start it. I need an Ibarra to turn the key." His eyes came back up, and they were not cruel, which was somehow worse. "And you are the last."

The word hung in the whitewashed air. *The last.* Her family's graves behind her, her guild's knives ahead of her, her mother's notes burned or scattered or locked in this man's vaults—and here was the arithmetic of her life reduced to a single function: key-turner.

"I'll refuse," she said.

"I know."

"I'll refuse until you run out of patience, and then you'll do whatever people like you do to the refusing."

"Yes," Hale said simply. "Probably. I'd like to avoid it. Not for your sake particularly—for mine. I've read the accounts of coerced forgings. The channel opens crooked when the opener is unwilling. Half the disasters in your family's history came from Ibarras dragged to the blade in chains." He stood, smoothed his coat, and paused at the door. "So I won't drag you. I'll wait. I'll show you things. I'll let you read the texts yourself—all of them, including the parts your family suppressed, the parts about what the god really is and what it costs to hold it. I have twenty years of grievance and a city full of scribes, Paloma. I can be very persuasive given time."

"And if time runs out? The Hollow Star is loose. Felix is—" She stopped. She had not meant to say Felix's name, and Hale's head tilted slightly, filing it away, and she cursed herself with a fluency that would have impressed her grandmother.

"Felix Vasquez," Hale said. "The deserter. Yes. Left for dead in a ravine east of the city, by our reckoning." Something moved behind the weak-tea eyes—not triumph, exactly. Curiosity. "You care whether he lives."

"He's my sworn shield."

"He's a mercenary who abandoned his post for you, and you care whether he lives." Hale opened the door. Cold corridor air moved through the room, smelling of ink and lamp oil. "I understand caring whether someone lives, Paloma. I have built everything I am on it. Which is why I'm going to give you a piece of information free of charge, no conditions, because I want you to trust that I deal fairly." He stopped in the doorway, silhouetted against the corridor's dim lamps. "Your friend Felix is not dead. Our scouts lost him in the ravine, but the ravine's lower reaches connect to the deep ways under the mountain, and things move in the deep ways that leave tracks. Something came up out of those ways three nights ago and killed a patrol on the north ridge—killed them without touching them, left them kneeling in a perfect circle, faces turned toward the peak. Whatever is walking down there, it is walking toward the forge. And it is not one of ours."

The door began to close.

"If it reaches the fragments before we do," Hale said through the narrowing gap, "everything I've promised you becomes irrelevant. Think about that. Sleep on it. I'll bring the texts tomorrow."

The door shut. The lock spoke—one soft click of will answering will—and Paloma sat alone in the beautiful, competent cage with the cold tea going untouched beside her and her heart hammering a name she hadn't meant to say aloud.

*Felix.*

She pressed her palms against her eyes. This was what he did, this was what Hale did, this was the oldest trick in the interrogator's book—give the prisoner hope, give the prisoner fear, make the prisoner feel the world outside the cell depends on her cooperation. She knew the trick. She had been taught the trick, in the guild, in the chapter on defensive sigils applied to words.

And still. Still. Kneeling in a circle, faces turned toward the peak. Something walking in the deep ways toward the forge, and not one of theirs.

She thought of the Hollow Star's voice wearing a thousand mouths in the streets of Corvane. She thought of her mother's final note, the one line she had never shown Felix: *the rite requires an Ibarra, but the rite is not the only door.* She thought of Hale's scarred hands and his examiner's ledger and the terrible kinship she could not cut out of her chest no matter how she tried.

Then she got up from the cot, barefoot on the cold stone, and went back to the wall by the window, and put her nose against the paint, and began—to pass the time, she told herself, merely to pass the time—reading the anchor node.

If they wanted her idle, they should never have given her walls worth reading.

By the time the next meal came, she had found the flaw.
