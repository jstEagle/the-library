## 23. The Forge-Peak Opens

The pull did not let him sleep.

Felix had walked out of the ravine three days ago with cracked ribs and a coat stiff with his own dried blood, and every step since had been less a choice than a surrender to the thing in his chest—that patient, wordless current that had finally broken its twenty-year silence not with a voice but with a *leaning*, like a hand on the small of his back, steering him north and up.

He had tried to pray at night, the old way, kneeling with his forehead to the frozen ground the way he had as a boy in the valley barracks. Nothing came. But when he stopped praying and simply lay still, listening, he could feel it again: the god was not speaking. The god was *pointing*. And beneath the pointing, faint as a bruise under skin, something else pressed back—a second pressure, cold and curious, that made the hair on his arms rise even through three layers of wool.

He climbed.

The forge-peak had no name on any map Paloma had shown him, only a smudge of ink where her mother's notes said *here* and nothing else, as if even writing it plainly had felt dangerous. From the foothills it looked like any other mountain in the range—grey shoulders, a snowfield collar, wind-scoured stone. But as Felix gained altitude, following goat tracks and then no track at all, he began to notice what the mountain remembered. Old cairns, their stones fused together as if by heat no fire could make. A dead pine, blackened from the inside out, still standing after centuries because its wood had been changed into something that would not rot. And everywhere, threaded through the rock like veins through marble, lines of faint silver light—the same script he had seen on the oathstone, on the inkwell's casing, on the severed hand's gauntlet. The covenant's handwriting, written so large that the whole mountain was its page.

He was walking up the inside of a sigil. He understood that somewhere around the third hour, when the silver lines began to converge ahead of him like streams running downhill toward a single sea, and the pressure against his back grew so insistent that he stopped resisting it entirely and simply let it carry his feet.

The air changed first. It went warm—not spring-warm but forge-warm, dry and metallic, tasting of hot iron and struck flint. Then the sound: a low, rhythmic breathing from above, too slow for lungs, too regular for wind. In and out. In and out. The mountain itself seemed to breathe.

Felix crested the last ridge at dusk, and stopped.

The crater at the peak's crown was perhaps two hundred paces across, ringed by walls of black glass where the rock had melted and refrozen in waves, like a sea caught mid-motion. At its center, sunk into the floor as if the mountain had grown around it, stood the forge.

It was not a building. It was an anvil the size of a chapel, hewn from a single mass of meteoric iron gone blue-black with age, and above it hung a shaft of open sky where the clouds refused to cross—a column of clean, starless dark, perfectly circular, perfectly still. Around the anvil's base ran the covenant-script in bands wide enough to drive a cart through, and every line of it was lit, glowing with a steady amber light that pulsed in time with that slow breathing. The whole crater smelled of ozone and old blood.

And on the anvil lay a sword.

Felix knew it before he touched it, the way you know your own name. The cutting-blade. The original instrument, the fourth fragment, the thing every other fragment had been made to serve. It was longer than any blade he'd carried—nearly five feet of grey metal with no ornament at all, no maker's mark, no edge decoration, just a plain crossguard wrapped in leather so ancient it had gone hard as horn. It did not look forged. It looked *cut*, as if someone had sliced it whole out of the world and left the rest behind.

His hands were shaking when he reached for it. He noticed, distantly, that they were shaking the way they'd shaken at nine years old, the day the priests had cut his palm and pressed it to the oathstone and told him he belonged to the silent god now, forever, and that this was an honor. He had believed them. He had believed them for twenty years.

The grip fit his hand exactly.

Of course it does, he thought. It was always going to.

The moment his skin met the leather, the breathing stopped.

Not faded. Not slowed. *Stopped*—the way a man stops breathing when he hears a floorboard creak downstairs. The amber light in the script-bands guttered, steadied, and then began to race, flowing along the channels in the rock faster and faster until the entire crater floor was a wheel of burning script spinning around him, and Felix stood at its center with a dead god's sword in his fist and nowhere at all to run.

Then the sky-column above the anvil moved.

It was not clouds crossing it. It was something *in* it—something turning over slowly in that circle of clean dark, vast beyond sense, and for one vertiginous moment Felix saw it clearly: a star. Or the shape of a star, the idea of one, hanging in the wrong place and burning the wrong color—a white so deep it circled back around to black, a light that drank light. It had no face. It did not need one. He felt it notice him the way a drowning man feels the water close over his head.

*There you are,* said something that was not a voice, arriving not through his ears but directly behind his eyes, in the space where his own thoughts lived. *The little borrowed one. The oath-sworn. Come all this way.*

Felix's body tried to bolt. His legs simply declined the order. He stood rooted, sword in hand, sweat freezing on his spine, and made himself look at it—made himself hold the gaze of the thing every story in the valley had been built to keep him from ever hearing about.

"You're the Hollow Star," he said. His voice came out flat and small in the enormous quiet. "The thing they actually bound."

*And you are the question they never answered.* The presence turned its attention over him like a merchant examining coin, weighing, testing, finding the flaws. *Twenty years of silence, little sworn one. Did you think the silence was for you? That your god ignored your prayers out of indifference? No. Every word it spoke, I would have heard. Every prayer answered, I would have followed the answer home. So it swallowed its voice and sat in the dark with me, year upon year upon year, and let you all believe yourselves abandoned rather than risk my attention drifting toward the valley.* A pause, and something almost like amusement moved through the cold. *It loved you, in its fashion. Love is mostly silence, in my experience.*

"Stop talking." The words tore out of him before he chose them. "Stop—don't use its voice."

*I am not using its voice. I am using yours.* And now he heard it, the horrible truth underneath: the words were coming up out of his own chest, shaped by his own tongue's memory, wearing the cadence of every prayer he'd ever whispered into his cupped hands as a child. *That is what I do. That is what I am. I am the want underneath the want. Your god binds. I offer. Do you know how short a distance lies between those two things? Ask your Paloma. Ask her guild. Ask the mothers who fed the covenant their daughters' blood and called it devotion.*

"She never—" Felix's jaw clenched. "Her family paid. They didn't murder anyone."

*Didn't they?* The star's attention slid off him, momentarily, toward the south and west—toward Corvane, toward the chains Paloma was in right now if she still lived, and the thought hit him like a spear between the ribs. *Go and ask her. If she lives. Men break so quickly, little sworn one. Especially men carrying swords they don't understand.*

The pressure came then—not physical, not quite, but a leaning-in, a gravity with intent, pressing against the inside of his skull the way deep water presses against a diver's ears. *Put the blade down. Set it on the stone. One small act. You have spent your whole life obeying voices; here is one more, and this one offers instead of commands. I can give you back the sound of your god. I can open the silence like a door. Twenty years of prayers, answered, all of them, every whispered plea in every barracks and sickroom—wouldn't you like to hear it say your name?*

His arm trembled. The sword-tip drifted downward, an inch, two inches, toward the anvil's surface.

And underneath the star's cold honey-voice, deeper than it, older than it, Felix felt the other presence stir—the one that had pulled him up this mountain. It did not speak. It had spent twenty years not speaking, and it did not break that discipline now, even here, even with everything at stake. Instead it did the only thing it had ever done for him.

It leaned.

Not toward the anvil. Toward *him*—a pressure like a hand closing over his sword-hand, like a father's grip on a child's wrist an instant before he touches the stove. And through the contact, without one single word, poured something Felix had no training to receive and no defenses against: an image.

A memory. Not his.

A mountaintop under an older sky, stars thick as spilled salt. A circle of robed figures—Ibarras, generations of them, faces he half-recognized from Paloma's descriptions, her mother among them younger and fierce-eyed. And bound in the center, wrapped in chains of script, a shape of light that did not struggle, that held itself utterly still while the cutting-blade descended—and Felix understood, with a lurch that had nothing to do with his cracked ribs, that the binding was being *renewed*, that this ceremony had happened dozens of times across the centuries, and that each time, the god lay down under the knife willingly, silently, because the alternative was letting the Hollow Star hear so much as a whisper of where it slept.

The vision broke. The star's voice hissed through his teeth like steam through a valve.

*Clever prisoner,* it said, and for the first time there was something other than amusement in the cold—something thin and furious. *Still keeping your secrets. Still swallowing your voice. Even now. Even here.*

Felix lifted the sword off the anvil.

It took everything he had. His muscles had gone to wax, his knees wanted the ground, and the star's gravity pulled at the blade like a river pulling at a wading man's ankles—but the god's grip stayed closed over his hand, steady as bedrock, and inch by inch by inch he raised the cutting-blade until it pointed at the sky-column, at the turning dark, at the thing that had eaten kingdoms and worn their peoples' own voices to do it.

"No," Felix said. And then, because he was a mercenary and a liar and a man who had spent twenty years talking to a god that couldn't answer, and because some stubborn soldier's part of him had decided, standing in this burning crater, that the silence ended today: "You don't get my name. You don't get my want. My god kept quiet for twenty years to keep you deaf, and I'll be damned before I'm the one who speaks for it."

The Hollow Star looked at him for a long moment.

Then it laughed, and the laugh was the worst thing yet, because it used his laugh, his own rough bark of a laugh, bouncing off the glass walls of the crater.

*Then run, little sworn one,* it said, receding, folding itself back down into the clean dark like a serpent coiling into its den. *Run south. Your sigilwright is in chains, and the magistrate is already climbing toward me with stolen blood in his veins and my name sweet on his tongue. You have perhaps four days. Perhaps fewer. And when he opens the channel I will pour through it into a world with no covenant, no wards, and no silence left to hide behind—* the presence flickered, almost giddy, almost hungry enough to be pitiable— *and the first prayer anyone prays will be prayed to me.*

The sky-column sealed. The breathing resumed—slow, deliberate, feigned, a sleeper pretending to sleep. The amber script dimmed to embers.

Felix stood alone in the crater with a god's sword in his hand and his heart slamming against his cracked ribs, and he did the arithmetic he had been avoiding since the ravine: four days. Corvane was six days' hard march. Three, if he killed the horse he'd have to steal. Two and a half if the weather broke against him.

Paloma had maybe four days, and he had a mountain at his back, a blade older than nations in his fist, and no army, no plan, and no idea how to use either.

But for the first time since the stars went out, Felix Vasquez knew exactly what he was walking toward—and exactly what was waiting if he failed.

He slung the cutting-blade across his back, felt it settle there as if it had always hung from that shoulder, and started down the mountain at a dead run.

Behind him, deep in the sealed dark, something that wore his voice laughed softly, and waited.
