## 25. Storming Corvane

Felix had spent twenty years learning how to take orders and one winter learning how to give them, and he still was not sure which frightened him more.

He stood on a ridge of grey slate two miles east of Corvane, watching the city breathe its false light into the dark. The false dawn was three nights away—the cult's great ceremony, when Magistrate Oren Hale would raise his counterfeit sun over the walls and call it salvation. Already the light leaked up from the amphitheater district like a wound that would not close, a sick gold glow staining the underside of the clouds where no stars remained to answer it.

Behind him, camped along the ridge in the ruins of an old shepherd's village, were four hundred people who should not have followed him.

He counted them again, though counting changed nothing. It was a habit from his mercenary years—count your blades, count your arrows, count the hours until relief or ruin. But there was no relief coming for this. There was no pay. There was only Paloma, somewhere inside those walls with iron on her wrists and Hale's voice in her ears, and the three fragments of the covenant wrapped in oilcloth against Felix's chest like a second heartbeat.

"You're doing the thing again," said Sera Voss behind him. "The counting."

Sera had been a grain merchant before the dark came, then a refugee, then—after the night she'd driven off the hollow-eyed things that walked without light using nothing but a burning cart and a voice like a landslide—something adjacent to a soldier. She'd appointed herself his second within a day of meeting him, and Felix had learned long ago that some battles you simply did not fight.

"I'm assessing," he said.

"You're counting." She came to stand beside him at the ridge's edge, arms crossed against the cold. "Four hundred farmers, weavers, drovers, and one knight who has been dead longer than my grandmother. Against a city of thirty thousand and a cult that owns every gate." She snorted. "Assess away."

Felix didn't answer, because there was nothing honest to say. The arithmetic was obscene. He had run this math a hundred times since the ravine, since he'd crawled out of that black crack in the world with broken ribs and a god's silent pull dragging him toward a mountain, and every time the numbers came out the same: impossible, unless.

Unless the people themselves were the weapon. Unless what Hale feared most wasn't steel but refusal—a city full of souls who looked at the false dawn and chose, loudly, publicly, irreversibly, not to kneel.

* * *

The frozen knight had been the first miracle.

Felix had found Sir Aldric Marrow still standing in the battlefield below the mountain, still fighting a war that ended generations ago, still wearing the rust-red livery of a regiment called the Ninth Lances. They had given him mercy in the end—Paloma's hands and Felix's blade together—and Felix had expected that to be the last of the Ninth. Dead men did not muster.

But when word spread through the refugee camps that the knight who guarded the mountain road had finally fallen, soldiers came walking out of the dim country like ghosts answering a bell. Old men first, bent and white-haired, wearing scraps of the rust-red under patched cloaks. Then their sons. Then grandsons who had never worn the color but had grown up on stories of it. The Ninth Lances had never disbanded; they had simply been waiting, generation after generation, for a war worth the name.

Their captain was a woman named Odile Marrow, great-great-granddaughter of a man whose brother had stood beside the frozen knight, and she had looked Felix up and down on the day she arrived with sixty lances and said, "You're the one who gave him rest."

"We did."

"Then you can give us something to do." Her eyes had gone past him, to Corvane's false glow on the horizon. "My family kept his watch for six generations because someone had to. I'm tired of guarding a corpse. Give me a living enemy."

Felix had given her the plan's spine and hated himself a little for it. He knew what he was asking. He had been a mercenary long enough to know the difference between a battle and a slaughter dressed as one, and everything about Corvane screamed the latter—unless the gates opened from the inside, unless the city's fear could be turned into fracture, unless four hundred nobodies became a story too loud to ignore.

* * *

* * *

The hermit arrived on the second evening, walking down from the pass with a staff of black oak and the unhurried gait of a man who had made peace with dying slowly and saw no reason to rush it now.

"Kestrel-of-the-Snows," Felix greeted him. The old covenant-keeper had told them the truth in the pass—the truth about the god's chosen silence, the truth that had unmade and remade Felix's faith in a single firelit hour—and Felix still did not know what honorific to give him beyond the name he'd offered.

"You look terrible," Kestrel observed. "Ribs?"

"Healing."

"Liar. Sit down before they knit wrong." The hermit lowered himself onto a fallen lintel with a grunt and studied the camp with eyes that had watched centuries go by. "I heard you've been recruiting."

"The refugees came on their own. The Ninth came for their own reasons."

"And yet here you are, drawing lines in the dirt and assigning gates." Kestrel smiled without much humor. "In my day we called that command, and it was considered a burden rather than an ambition. How does it sit on you?"

Felix thought about lying, decided the old man would taste it, and answered honestly. "Like armor that doesn't fit. Every time I send someone to a post, I hear myself giving orders the way my captains gave them—like lives were ledger entries. Debits against the objective."

"And?"

"And I keep doing it anyway. Because if I don't, nobody does, and Paloma—" His throat closed around the name. He tried again. "She's in there because I was slow. Because I let us get separated on the retreat. So yes, I'll wear armor that doesn't fit. I'll wear it until she's out."

Kestrel was quiet a moment. Below them, the campfires of the refugees dotted the ridge like earthbound stars, small and stubborn, the only stars left in the world that anyone had lit by hand.

"When I was a young keeper," the hermit said at last, "my teacher told me that the covenant was forged twice. Once in blood and ink, on the mountain—that was the sigil, the great work, the thing songs are sung about. And once in something humbler. Bread shared across the ward line. A stranger's child nursed through fever. Small covenants, renewed daily, by people who owed each other nothing." He tapped his staff against the stone. "Hale thinks power is the mountain. He is wrong. Power is what keeps a covenant alive between the forgings. You have four hundred people down there who have shared bread in the dark for a season. That is not nothing, mercenary. That may be everything."

Felix looked down at the fires again—at Sera teaching farm boys to hold a shield wall, at Odile's lances running drill by torchlight, at children asleep in wagons because their parents refused to leave them behind. A covenant of small things, mustered on a ridge.

"All right," he said. "Tell me what a keeper sees that I don't."

* * *

They built the plan the way Paloma would have cut a sigil—in layers, each one hiding the next.

The outermost layer was noise. On the night of the false dawn, while Hale's thousands packed the amphitheater to worship his counterfeit sun, the Ninth Lances would come down on the eastern aqueduct with fire and hammers—not to breach it, but to make Corvane believe they meant to. Every soldier Hale had would want to be where the fighting sounded loudest, and Odile was very good at making fighting sound loud. Her grandfather had taught her war-drums built from wagon frames and ox hide, instruments that could convince a listener an army was ten times its size. Sixty lances, forty drums, and the acoustics of a stone gorge: a legion, as far as any terrified gate-captain could tell.

The middle layer was the river. Sera's refugees—drovers, millers, fishermen, people who had spent their whole lives reading water—would take the culverts beneath the western wall where the Greywash ran into the city. The cult had sealed the gates, but no magistrate seals a sewer he drinks downstream of, and the culvert grates had been mapped by a tanner's daughter named Bree who had grown up smuggling eels through them. Two hundred civilians, armed with knives and fury and lamp-oil, coming up out of the ground in the temple district while the faithful sang.

And the innermost layer—the layer hidden inside the layer hidden inside the layer—was Felix and eleven of the Ninth's best, going in the way everyone had forgotten to guard against: openly, in plain sight, wearing the stolen vestments of the cult's processional order, marching through the front gate among hundreds of genuine pilgrims pouring in for the ceremony.

"A knife doesn't sneak," Odile had said when he proposed it. "A knife walks in the open and is only a knife at the last inch."

"It's insane," Sera had said.

"Yes," Felix had agreed. "It's also the only version where we reach the amphitheater floor before Hale finishes whatever he's doing to her."

That had been three days ago. Now the false dawn was tomorrow, and the ridge camp had gone quiet in the particular way camps go quiet before a battle—not peaceful, but held, like a breath.

Felix walked the lines one last time. He stopped at the fires and let people look at him, because he had learned that much at least: they didn't need speeches, they needed to see that the man sending them hadn't hidden himself somewhere safe. He stopped where Odile's drummers oiled their hides in silence. He stopped where Bree the tanner's daughter was sharpening a filleting knife and asked her, quietly, whether she wanted to stay with the reserve, and she looked at him with such contempt that he never raised the question with anyone else again.

At the last fire, Kestrel sat alone, mending the strap of a satchel that Felix suspected held things no one else should carry.

"You should sleep," the hermit said, without looking up.

"So should you."

"Oh, I intend to sleep eventually. Permanently, in fact—it's on my schedule." He tied off the strap and finally met Felix's eyes. In the firelight the old face looked carved rather than aged, all planes and patience. "But not before I tell you the thing I climbed down this mountain to tell you, so sit."

Felix sat.

"Tomorrow, when you see her, she may not be entirely herself. Hale has had weeks. He is a failed heir, but failure leaves marks—it teaches a man where all the tender places are, because he has pressed on every one of his own." Kestrel's voice dropped low. "If the forging has begun, even badly, even falsely, she will be half in the rite and half out of it, and the part of her that knows your name may be buried deep. Do not mistake that for betrayal. And do not—this is the important part, boy—do not put your sword between yourself and her to solve it. Cut the circle, break the anchors, spill the ink. The person comes back when the working dies. Trust the person under the spell more than the spell."

"And if I can't tell which is which?"

"Then you remember what you felt in the ravine." Kestrel's eyes were suddenly very old and very kind. "You told me once that dying at the bottom of that crack, you stopped being able to imagine a world without her in it. That wasn't the god talking, and it wasn't the Hollow Star tempting you. That was just a man, knowing a true thing. Hold onto the true thing. Everything else in that amphitheater tomorrow will be a lie wearing light."

Felix's chest ached in a way that had nothing to do with his ribs. "When did I tell you that?"

"The night you staggered into my hermitage bleeding and half-mad, talking to a god that wouldn't answer." The hermit smiled faintly. "You talk a great deal when you think no one is listening. It's your best quality."

* * *

Dawn of the false dawn came grey and windless, and Corvane opened its gates to the faithful.

Felix walked among them in a processional robe of white and gold, hood up, face shadowed, the three covenant fragments sewn flat against his ribs beneath the cloth. Around him flowed a river of pilgrims—hollow-cheeked, hopeful, desperate people who had traded their hunger for a promise, carrying candles that would not be lit until the counterfeit sun rose. Eleven of the Ninth walked in twos and threes scattered through the crowd, rust-red hidden under white, moving with the loose-hipped ease of soldiers pretending to be devotees. Odile had picked them herself. None of them had families. All of them had sworn.

The gate guards barely looked. Why would they? The city wanted to be full today. Fullness was the point—thirty thousand bodies crammed into the amphitheater and the avenues around it, a congregation the size of a nation, all of them singing the hymn of the Second Dawn in voices cracked with starvation and hope. The sound rolled over the walls and out across the dim country like a tide. Felix had heard armies sing before marching. This was worse. Armies sang to steady themselves. These people sang because they had nothing else left to hold.

*Let them be lied to gently,* he caught himself thinking, and hated the thought, and kept walking.

The amphitheater had been Corvane's pride in the old days—open to the sky, ringed in white stone, built for festivals and trials. Now it wore the cult like a skin. Banners of gold-on-white hung from every tier. A scaffold of black iron rose at the center of the sand, and around it the faithful were arranging themselves in concentric rings, thousands upon thousands, a human sigil with a scaffold for its heart.

And on the scaffold—

Felix's breath went out of him in a slow, controlled burn.

Paloma stood at the center, bound upright to the iron frame with chains at wrist and throat, and she was *glowing*. Not the clean silver of her sigilcraft—he knew that light, had slept beside it, had trusted it—but a sickly amber, pulsing in time with a chant rising from a hundred robed figures at the scaffold's base. Her head hung forward. Her hair had gone lank. Even at this distance Felix could see the tremor in her shoulders, the way her weight hung entirely from her wrists, and the amber light crawling under her skin like something swimming in her blood.

*Half in the rite and half out of it.* Kestrel's voice in his memory. *Trust the person under the spell.*

Below the tiers, at the foot of the scaffold, a figure in robes of deeper gold than any other turned to survey his congregation, arms spread wide, drinking in the hymn. Oren Hale. Even from eighty yards Felix could feel the man's satisfaction radiating off him like heat off a forge-stone. At his side, acolytes carried a basin of ink so dark it seemed to drink the daylight, and a blade that Felix recognized with a jolt that nearly broke his cover—the cutting-blade's twin, or its stolen echo, whatever Hale had managed to forge in his vaults.

The sun-hymn swelled toward its crescendo. Above the amphitheater, the clouds began, impossibly, to brighten.

Felix touched two fingers to his chest, over the fragments, over his heart, and felt the eleventh man of the Ninth shift subtly closer at his shoulder. Across the arena, at the mouth of the tunnel nearest the scaffold, a torch flared and died—twice. Bree's signal. The culvert teams were under the wall. Somewhere to the east, faint as a headache, the first war-drum of Odile's phantom legion began to speak.

The false sun broke through the clouds, and thirty thousand people fell to their knees.

Felix stayed standing—the only upright figure in a kneeling world, a white-robed knife at the last inch—and drew his blade beneath his cloak.

"Now," he breathed.
