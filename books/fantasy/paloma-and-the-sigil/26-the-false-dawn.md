## 26. The False Dawn

The bells of Corvane began to ring before the sky had finished pretending.

Felix crouched on the roof of the tannery row with the hermit's old spyglass pressed to his eye, watching the great plaza below fill with white-robed bodies. Ten thousand of them, maybe more, packed shoulder to shoulder around the base of the Unlit Spire where Magistrate Oren Hale stood on a scaffold of pale timber, arms raised toward a horizon that refused to brighten. The false dawn. They had built it out of lanterns—thousands of them, hung on wires strung from every tower, every balcony, every eave in the city—and when Hale gave the signal they would all be lit at once, and the people of Corvane would weep and call it morning.

"They're going to burn the city down doing it," muttered Brannoc beside him. The frozen knight's old regiment had come to him in ones and twos over the past three days—forty-one men and women in mismatched armor, gray-haired, scarred, carrying weapons their grandchildren would have called antiques. "Lantern oil on that scale. One spark goes wrong—"

"It won't go wrong," Felix said. "That's the problem."

He lowered the spyglass. At the center of the plaza, on a raised dais beneath the spire, he could see the circle: nine pillars of white stone arranged in a ring, each carved with sigils that hurt to look at even from this distance. And chained between two of the pillars, kneeling, her wrists bound in iron bands covered in fresh-cut script—

Paloma.

She was alive. He made himself look again, made himself count her breaths, because the part of him that had been screaming since the ravine wanted very much to believe she wasn't. Alive. Kneeling. Her hair loose and filthy, her head bowed, and around her the nine pillars already glowing faintly, like coals deciding whether to catch.

"She's the anchor," said Sefa, the regiment's sergeant, a woman who had been fighting wars since before Felix was born. She said it without pity, which he was grateful for. Pity would have broken something in him right then. "Hale doesn't need her willing. The rite just needs her blood in the stone. We pull her out before first light, we pull the teeth out of the whole ceremony."

"And if we don't," Felix said quietly, "he reforges the covenant with himself holding it. And everything my god went silent to protect—" He stopped. Swallowed. Twenty years of silence sat in his chest like a swallowed stone, and beneath it, faint as a pulse under snow, the pull. *North. Up. The peak.* His god had been dragging him toward this city for six days without saying a single word, and Felix had finally understood what the silence meant: not absence. *Discretion.* A god keeping its voice down so the thing in the dark wouldn't hear where it slept.

"Positions," he said. "We move when the lanterns light. Every eye in this city will be on the sky."

* * *

Paloma had counted the stones.

That was the thing about terror that no one warned you: it sharpened. Her hands were chained above her head, her knees had gone numb against the dais an hour ago, and through all of it her mind kept working the way her mother had trained it to work, patient and cold, cataloguing. Nine pillars. Nine anchor-points of the original covenant ring, salvaged from the ruins of her family's forge-hold and reassembled here in the heart of Corvane like bones stolen from a grave. The script cut into her irons was Hale's own hand—she recognized the cramped, ambitious lettering, the strokes of a man who had learned sigilcraft secondhand and never forgiven the world for it. Sloppy work. Overworked lines. A boy trying to impress a teacher who was dead.

*You can use that,* her mother's voice said in her memory. *Sloppy ink is still ink.*

"Sigilwright Ibarra." Hale's voice came from behind her, warm as a hearth, and she hated how much it sounded like her own desperation given a magistrate's robes. He came around into her view, and up close his face was terrible—not cruel, never cruel, but *hungry*, lit from within by the same fever she had seen in her guild elders' eyes the night they told her what the renewal rite required. "I want you to know I take no pleasure in the discomfort. You especially should understand that what happens tonight is a mercy. The world is going dark, Paloma. Crops failing. Children born without shadows. Someone must hold the covenant, and your family is finished. Your line spent its last coin a generation ago." He smiled gently. "I'm simply picking up the ledger."

"You don't know what you're holding," she said. Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "The covenant isn't a tool, Hale. It's a *seal*. You don't pick up a seal. You hold the door shut and pray."

"I know exactly what I'm holding." He leaned close, and for one unguarded moment she saw beneath the warmth—the flayed, furious ambition of a man who had once cut a sigil wrong and been cast out for it, who had spent thirty years building a cult so that no one would ever tell him *no* again. "I failed once. Do you know what that does to a person? To reach for the craft and have it reject you? Your blood gets to do this by birthright. Mine has to earn it." He straightened. "Tonight, it earns."

Above the plaza, the first lantern caught.

Then the next, and the next, a fire spreading along the wires in both directions at once, and ten thousand people gasped as one, and someone began to sing—a low, wavering hymn about the second dawn—and Paloma watched the false light climb the towers of Corvane and thought, with a clarity that felt like falling: *they believe it. All of them. They'd rather have a lie that glows than a truth that's dark.*

Hale raised his hand. The singing stopped.

"The forging begins," he announced, to the city, to the dark, to whatever listened beyond the stars. "Let there be light."

He drew the knife.

* * *

Felix moved on the light.

It was, he had to admit, beautiful—the whole city blooming gold at once, the crowd's faces turned upward, every eye lifted away from the ground. Which meant that forty-one gray veterans of a war nobody remembered could cross the plaza's edge in the lantern-glow's blind spot, hugging the shadowed side of the granary wall, moving the way soldiers move when they've done it too long to think about it. Felix went first. He always went first now; he had stopped examining why.

The dais guards were cultists, not soldiers—devout, undisciplined, armed with ceremonial bills they clearly didn't know how to use. Sefa's people took the eastern pair without a sound. Brannoc put one large hand over a western guard's mouth and lowered him to the stones like a man setting down a sleeping child. In eleven seconds the dais was theirs, and Felix was running up its steps with his heart slamming against his ribs, and Paloma looked up at him with eyes that were half relief and half absolute fury.

"You're *late*," she rasped.

"We discussed the plan for two days," Felix said, cutting the chain above her wrists. "There was no version of it where I was early."

"There was a version where you were *sensible*—" She swayed; he caught her arm. Her irons hit the stone, and the script on them flickered and died, and across the plaza a sound went up from ten thousand throats—not alarm yet, but confusion, the hymn faltering mid-verse.

Because the pillars had stopped glowing.

"No," Hale said. It was almost a whisper. Felix turned and found the magistrate standing at the circle's center, knife still in hand, staring at the dead stone with an expression Felix had seen only once before—on a man watching his house burn. Then the expression closed, and became something far worse: calm. "No matter. The anchor's marked. The blood's drawn. The circle knows its shape." He looked directly at Felix, and smiled. "Do you know what the worst part of being rejected by the craft is, soldier? You learn to improvise."

He drove the knife into his own palm and slapped the bleeding hand against the central pillar.

* * *

Paloma felt it before anyone screamed.

The sigils flared—not Hale's sloppy script now but something older underneath, something the magistrate's blood had woken without understanding, and the nine pillars blazed white, and the air above the circle *tore*. Not like cloth tearing. Like skin. Like the sky over Corvane developing a wound, a long vertical seam of absolute darkness that drank the lantern-light as fast as ten thousand flames could make it, and through the seam came a pressure, a presence, a *attention*—vast and patient and starving, turning its regard upon the city the way a man turns his face toward a candle flame in a cold room.

And every person in the plaza heard, inside their own skull, a voice like honey poured over broken glass:

*I see you.*

The crowd did not panic. That was the horror of it. The crowd went *quiet*—ten thousand people standing in perfect stillness, faces tilted up toward the tear, expressions softening into wonder, into longing, into love. Paloma had read about this. Her mother's notes described it in a hand that shook: *the Hollow Star does not command. It offers. That is why it cannot be fought with walls or wards. You cannot garrison a man against his own desire.*

"Felix." Her voice cracked. "Felix, we have to get them *away*—"

But Felix wasn't listening. Felix had gone rigid beside her, and his face—she had learned his face these past weeks, its weather, its silences—was showing her something she had never seen on it. Not fear. *Recognition.*

"It's talking to me," he said. Very quietly. "It knows my name."

* * *

What the Hollow Star offered Felix Vasquez, in the space between one heartbeat and the next, was a voice.

Not words. Not a vision. Simply, suddenly, unbearably: his god's voice, alive, present, *speaking*—the voice he had prayed into for twenty years and received nothing back, warm and vast and full of a love so old it predated language, saying his name the way a father says it, saying *Felix, well done, faithful child, you found me, you kept faith through the silence, come home*—and it was perfect, it was everything, it was the exact shape of the hole he had carried since he was nine years old and sworn with blood he hadn't understood—

—and it was a lie, and some small surviving mercenary-corner of his mind knew it was a lie, because his real god was silent, had chosen silence, had *paid* for silence, and the thing offering him this counterfeit warmth was the reason why.

"No," Felix said aloud, to the sky, to the tear, to the whole listening city. "That's not him. He doesn't speak. He *keeps quiet*—it's the bravest thing anyone's ever done, and you—" his voice broke, and he hated it, and kept going anyway, "—you don't get to wear his voice."

The tear shuddered. Somewhere in its depths, something that had expected easy prey felt, for the first time, resistance.

Around the plaza, though, the crowd was not Felix Vasquez. The crowd had received its own offers—ten thousand private heavens, whispered simultaneously—and Paloma watched a woman near the western barricade begin to laugh and weep at once, reaching toward the dark, and then another, and another, and the hymn started up again but changed, the words warping mid-verse into something older, something that made the hermit's dying warning echo in Paloma's ears: *when the Star speaks through many mouths, run.*

"They're converting," she breathed. "The whole plaza—they're—" She grabbed Felix's arm. "The fragments. Do you have them?"

He patted his chest, where the oathstone and the inkwell rode in a sling against his skin. "Both."

"Then we haven't lost. We've *started* something we can't finish, and we need to be somewhere defensible when it finishes us." She was already moving, pulling him off the dais, toward the granary wall where Sefa's veterans held a gap in the chaos. Behind them Hale stood motionless at the circle's center, arms spread, face transfigured, drinking in the attention of the thing he had summoned—until the tear's light, if light it could be called, bent downward and touched him, and his transfiguration curdled into something else entirely.

He screamed. Briefly. The sound cut off in a way that suggested the scream had been *collected*.

"Oh," Paloma said softly, watching the magistrate's body straighten, settle, go calm. Watching him turn his head, slowly, with new ownership, to track them across the plaza. "Oh, no. It took him. It's *wearing* him."

The thing that had been Oren Hale smiled with his mouth, and spoke with his voice, and the voice reached them across the whole roaring plaza as if he stood at their shoulders:

*"Two hands. How clever. How very clever, little heirs. Come then. Bring me your pieces. I have waited so long inside the dark—and you are going to open the door the rest of the way."*

Felix pulled Paloma through the gap in the wall. Behind them, the false dawn burned on over Corvane, and the tear in the sky widened by a finger's width, and the converted city began, in eerie unison, to walk.
