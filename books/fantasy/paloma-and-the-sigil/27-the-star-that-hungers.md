## 27. The Star That Hungers

The silence after Felix broke the circle lasted three heartbeats.

Then the Hollow Star poured through the torn channel like water finding a crack in a dam, and Corvane began to speak.

Not all at once. That was the horror of it—the slowness. A woman selling chestnuts near the plaza stopped mid-cry and turned her face up to a sky that held no dawn, only the bruise-colored afterglow of the false ceremony, and said, in a voice that was hers and not hers: *"I see you, Marta. I know what you buried under the kitchen floor."* Then she smiled and went back to her brazier as though nothing had happened.

Felix stood in the wreckage of the ritual circle with Paloma's wrist gripped in his hand and watched a city turn its face toward something none of them could see. Tens of thousands of people, all at once or nearly, tilting their chins like sunflowers toward a light that wasn't there.

"It's in them," Paloma whispered. Her fingers were still bleeding where Hale's chains had torn the skin. "It's not possessing them—it doesn't need to. It's just... talking. Through whatever's already in them."

"The want," Felix said.

"The want," she agreed grimly. "Every prayer is a door. It told us that much was true."

Around them, the plaza was filling with people who should have been fleeing. They came out of tenements and wine-shops and the gutted remains of the cult's temple, walking unhurried, their eyes reflecting a light that existed somewhere behind their faces. An old man knelt in the gutter and wept with joy. A child of six or seven stood perfectly still among the running legs and said, clearly, pleasantly: *"You can have her back, you know. Mothers are such simple things to mend."*

Felix pulled Paloma toward the colonnade at the plaza's edge. "We need to get out of—"

*"Felix Vasquez."*

His name came from everywhere. From the mouth of a baker's boy hauling bread. From three women leaning from a balcony. From a dying man propped against a fountain, who lifted his head as though called by a beloved friend.

He stopped walking. He hated himself for stopping.

"Don't listen," Paloma said sharply. "Felix. It has nothing but lies and things you already want—it doesn't *make* anything, it only *offers*. Don't listen."

But the voice was already speaking, gentle as his god had never been, warm as the valley hearths of his childhood, and it knew things about him that no one alive could know.

***

*You have been so patient,* it said, in the voice of a hundred Corvanians and none of them. *Twenty years of silence. Twenty years of kneeling to a god who would not so much as breathe on you. Do you know why?*

Felix kept moving. One foot, then the other. Paloma's hand in his, slick with blood. The colonnade ahead, shadowed, empty—too empty.

*Because it cannot speak. Because it gave its voice away, or had it taken—I know which, Felix. I was there when it happened. I remember your god before it was mute, before the covenant stitched its mouth shut. It sang, once. And the singing was beautiful, and the beauty was a lie, and they cut the song out of it to keep me sleeping.*

"That's not—" he started, and caught himself. Arguing was listening. Arguing was a door.

*I could give it back,* the star said, and now it used one voice only: an old soldier named Bren, whom Felix had drunk with in the ravine country two winters ago, dead now or worse than dead. *One word from you, and the silence ends. Your god speaks again. Says your name. Tells you whether all those years of faith were worth anything—or whether you knelt to a stone for two decades while it forgot you entirely.*

*Don't you want to know?*

And that was the knife of it, turned precisely. Not the promise of the voice itself—he could have refused that easily, he thought, maybe—but the question underneath. The doubt it named out loud. All those years in the valley, praying into silence, telling himself the silence was holy. Had it been holiness? Or had it been abandonment, dressed up by priests who needed it to be?

A woman stepped out of a doorway directly into their path. Middle-aged, flour on her apron, tears streaming down a serene face. She did not look at Felix. She looked *through* him, and spoke with the star's warmth:

*Say yes, and you will hear it say: "Well done, my son." Isn't that what you've wanted since you were eight years old and they put the brand on your arm?*

Felix's branded arm burned. Actually burned, heat crawling along the old scar, and he couldn't tell if it was memory or magic.

"No," he said aloud, and his voice cracked in the middle of it, which was the most honest sound he'd made in twenty years.

The woman tilted her head. The warmth went out of her expression like a lamp snuffed, and she simply stepped aside and let them pass, and that frightened him more than any threat would have.

*Refusal noted,* the star said, in a thousand voices now, receding, amused. *We have time, Felix Vasquez. I am very old, and very patient, and I keep every ledger. Yours is long.*

***

They ran.

Through the colonnade, down the fish-market stairs, along the canal road where the water lay black and unmoving beneath a sky the color of old ash. Behind them the plaza filled—not with soldiers now, but with neighbors. The cult's hardmen had been broken at the ceremony; Hale's guard scattered or dead. It didn't matter. The city itself had become the enemy's body, and every citizen in it a mouth.

Paloma pulled him into the shadow beneath an aqueduct arch and pressed her back against cold stone, breathing hard, listening.

"Listen for what?" Felix breathed.

"For it to stop offering." Her eyes were closed. "When it stops talking, it's found someone worth talking to longer. When it goes quiet around us specifically—" her jaw tightened "—it means it's saving something."

The city murmured around them like a hive. From a window above: *"Your husband didn't leave you. He's in the cellar of the Kestrel House. I can show you. He's been waiting."* From a beggar in the reeds: *"Sixty crowns under the third flagstone of the mint steps. No one will ever count them missing."* Small wants, mostly. Bread-level wants. The star seeding itself through ten thousand small mercies, buying the city a coin at a time.

Then the murmuring near them thinned. Stopped.

Paloma opened her eyes. "There it is."

The quiet pressed in like deep water. In the arch's shadow, with the whole city chattering around them, a bubble of perfect attention settled over the space where they crouched—and then the voice came, and it was not warm at all anymore. It was careful. Almost tender. The way you speak to something you intend to break slowly.

*Paloma Ibarra.*

Her breath went ragged. Felix reached for her hand and found her fingers ice-cold.

*Your mother died badly, little sigilwright. Alone, in a room that smelled of ink and burning, cutting a covenant she already knew would fail because the guild had eaten half its strength feeding me its lies. She called your name at the end. You were four hundred miles away, learning to hate your own hands.*

"Stop it," Paloma whispered.

*She is not gone. Nothing is ever gone, here where I live. The dead are threads; I hold the loom. I can weave her back—her voice, her laugh, the weight of her hand on your head when you were small and afraid of thunder. Not a ghost. Not a shade. Her. Every year the guild took from your family, returned. Your father. Your grandmother. The whole murdered line of the Ibarras, standing in your mother's kitchen, asking where you've been.*

Paloma made a sound Felix had never heard from her—a small, cracked thing, like ice giving way—and he understood with sick clarity that this was the offer she could not argue with, because it wasn't greed being weaponized. It was grief.

"They wouldn't be real," she said, loud enough for the star to hear, her voice shaking. "You don't make things. You only *copy* them. You're hunger wearing a shape. My mother taught me the difference between making and mimicking before I could write my own name."

*A copy loved is indistinguishable from the original,* the star said reasonably. *Ask any mourner.*

"And that's the trap, isn't it." Paloma laughed—wet, furious, terrified. "That's always the trap. You don't want our yes. Our yes is nothing. What you want is for us to *want* to say yes—to carry the wanting for the rest of our lives like a splinter. You're not buying our souls tonight. You're installing the debt."

Silence. Long, considering, hungry.

*You sound like your mother,* it said finally, and there was something in it that might have been admiration or appetite. *She said almost exactly that, at the end. Right before she cut the seal that failed.*

Then the attention withdrew, snapped off like a hand closing, and the ordinary murmur of the corrupted city rushed back in around them, and Paloma folded forward until her forehead nearly touched her knees, shaking.

Felix held her shoulders and said nothing, because there was nothing true enough to say.

***

They moved again at what passed for dusk—an hour when the sky darkened from grey to darker grey, the stars still absent, the moon a rumor. The refugee quarter near the south wall had partially emptied during the ceremony; those who remained had barred their doors against their own neighbors. Felix got them over a tanner's fence and into a drying shed that stank of lime and old hides, and there, finally, they allowed themselves to stop.

Paloma sat against a stack of stretchers and worked silently at rewrapping her wrists. Felix built no fire. Fire drew eyes, and every eye in Corvane was a window now.

"It showed you something," she said at last. Not looking up. "In the plaza. I saw your face."

"It offered me my god's voice." He said it flatly, because hiding it had become impossible somewhere between the aqueduct and here, and because chapter seventeen of their lives—the confessions at the fireline—had taught him what concealment cost. "Told me it could end the silence. Let me hear it say—" His jaw worked. "Let me finally know if any of it meant anything. Twenty years of prayer into a well. It asked if I wanted to know whether I'd been kneeling to something that forgot I existed."

Paloma looked up. "What did you actually feel?"

He almost deflected. Old habit. Instead he made himself sit with the question the way he'd learned to sit with her questions—honestly, even when honesty scraped.

"Like someone read my diary aloud in a market square," he said. "It didn't offer me a gift, Paloma. It offered me my worst suspicion, gift-wrapped. And for about two heartbeats I wanted it more than I've wanted anything in my life." He rubbed the branded scar on his forearm. "That's what it does. It doesn't lie, exactly. It just holds up the ugliest true thing in you and asks if you'd like company."

"It knew my mother's last words." Paloma's voice was very quiet. "Or it claimed to. And the awful part—the part I'll be chewing for years—is that I believe it *could*. I believe it really does hold the dead the way it says. Which means every person in this city who says yes tonight gets something real. Something that loves them, maybe, in its hollow way. And they'll defend it. They'll fight for it. We haven't fought an army yet, Felix—we've fought believers, and believers can be reasoned with eventually. This is different."

"What do we do?"

She was silent a long moment, flexing her bandaged hands, and when she answered, her voice had the flat steadiness he'd learned to trust more than confidence.

"We run for the peak. Tonight, while the city's still drunk on its own offers. Because what it did today wasn't the attack—it was the recruitment. It poured itself through a crack Hale tore open, and it's loose in forty thousand people now, and it will grow, and every day we wait it gets harder to walk out those gates." She stood, wincing. "Three fragments, one blade, one mountain. Everything else is weather."

Felix rose beside her. At the shed's warped door, he paused, looking out across the rooftops of Corvane—ten thousand chimneys, ten thousand homes, and behind how many windows a family sitting in candleless dark, listening to a voice that promised them everything they'd lost?

"Paloma," he said. "The thing it offered me. The voice." He hesitated. "If it had come to me alone—if you weren't standing there—if it had been a year ago, in the valley, before any of this—"

"I know," she said simply. "Me too."

That was the whole confession, and both of them understood it, and neither needed to dress it up.

They slipped out into the ash-grey streets, heading south, toward the wall, toward the mountain that remembered. Behind them, Corvane glowed faintly—not with firelight, not with lamplight, but with something that had no business glowing at all: a soft, sourceless radiance rising off the city like heat shimmer, like breath in winter, like a star lying on its back at the bottom of the world, digesting.

And above it all, high in the lightless sky where the stars should have been, one new point of light flickered into being—red, wrong, patient—and began, very slowly, to move.

Toward the forge-peak.

Paloma saw it first. She stopped dead in the middle of the street, and Felix watched her face go white as old bone.

"It knows where we're going," she said. "It's known all along. It's not chasing us, Felix."

"Then what—"

"It's *racing* us home."
