## 28. Retreat to the Peak

*Felix*

The wounded changed everything.

Felix had run retreats before—quick, brutal affairs where you left the slow behind and prayed the dead didn't remember your face. But the refugees from Corvane would not be left. They had followed him out of a city that had stopped being a city, had watched their neighbors' eyes fill with starlight that wasn't starlight, and they clung to him now the way drowning men cling to driftwood: gratefully, suffocatingly, and with no intention whatsoever of letting go.

"Vasquez." Sera limped up beside him, one arm bound to her chest, her face gray under the soot of the burning granaries. She had been a caravan guard before Corvane; she was the closest thing his ragged column had to a second in command. "The rear guard says they've reached the river district. The converted don't sleep. They don't eat. They just *walk*, and they sing while they walk."

"What do they sing?"

Sera's jaw worked. "Their own names. Over and over. Like they're afraid of forgetting them."

Felix looked back down the slope of the Ash Road, toward the smudge of orange light on the horizon where Corvane burned its own lamps for the first time in a generation—not to see by, but to be seen by the thing that had poured through the torn circle. Behind them came perhaps four hundred souls: refugees, deserters from Hale's host, a handful of the frozen knight's old regiment who had marched out of their long vigil still wearing rusted mail and calling Felix by names no living man should have known.

And three fragments of the covenant, wrapped in oiled cloth against his back like a burden of guilt.

"The pass," he said. "How far?"

"Two days if we were healthy. Four as we are."

"We don't have four."

"No," Sera agreed quietly. "We don't."

Above them, at the roof of the world, the forge-peak waited—a black fang against a sky that still refused to give them stars. The Hollow Star's corruption spread outward from Corvane like ink through water; each night the darkness came a little closer, and each night fewer of the real constellations survived it. Felix had grown up beneath an empty sky and had thought he knew what a starless night felt like.

He hadn't known anything. This new dark *leaned*. It pressed against the fires they dared to build and drank the warmth from them. It whispered in voices pitched just below hearing, and every hour another refugee would stop mid-stride, tilt their head as though listening to a beloved friend, and have to be shaken bodily until the light went out of their eyes again.

They had lost eleven that way since leaving the walls.

"Break camp," Felix said. "We move now, tonight. If the converted reach us on open ground we're finished."

"And if we reach the pass too late?"

Felix touched the wrapped weight of the oathstone through the cloth. "Then the hermit holds it alone, and we all learn what his prayers are worth."

But even as he said it, he knew. He had known since the old man had pressed his cracked staff into the mud at the tree line and said, in that voice like dry leaves: *I will hold the pass. It is why I stayed alive this long.* Some men were born to die holding doors. The hermit had simply waited two centuries for the right door.

The column moved. Behind them, faint and terrible, the singing grew louder.

* * *

*Paloma*

Paloma walked at the center of the column because the wounded needed her there, and because walking kept her from thinking about what waited at the end of the road.

She carried the inkwell against her heart, wrapped in three layers of wool, and she spoke to it sometimes when the whispers from the dark grew too loud. Inside it were every name ever bound by the covenant, written in her ancestors' hands across generations—her grandmother's careful script, her mother's fierce slanting letters, and somewhere near the end, in a soldier's blunt strokes, *Felix Vasquez*. Two thousand years of blood debt in a vessel smaller than a fist.

Two thousand years, and her family had never once written down how to survive the ending.

"You're limping," Felix said, materializing at her elbow the way he did, silent as a man raised by something that didn't breathe.

"I'm conserving energy efficiently."

"That's limping."

"It's *strategic* limping." She glanced sideways at him. In the firelight of the torches they still permitted themselves, his face looked carved from the same stone as the mountain ahead—all hard planes and shadow, except around the eyes, where something stubbornly human had survived everything Corvane had done to them. "How many?"

"How many what?"

"Don't play at being stupid, it doesn't suit you. How many have we lost to the whispering?"

"Fourteen." He didn't look at her. "One of the knight's regiment. A child's mother. The others I barely knew their names."

"The inkwell knows their names." Her voice came out strange. "That's the horror of it, Felix. Everyone we lose is already in here. Written down. Bound. Their names are *in my hands* and I can't do anything but carry them up a mountain."

Felix stopped walking. For a moment Paloma thought he was going to say something gentle, which would have been unbearable. Instead he took her hand—the ink-stained one, the sigilwright's hand, scarred from the false forging—and pressed it flat against the wrapped bundle on his back. Through the oilcloth she felt it: the oathstone, warm as a sleeping animal.

"They're not just written down," he said. "They're *held*. There's a difference. My god held me my whole life without ever saying a word, and I used to think that was abandonment." His thumb brushed her knuckles, once, and then his hand was gone and he was walking again, and the column closed around them as though nothing had passed between them at all. "I know better now. Hold on to that difference, Paloma. We'll need it at the forge."

She stood a moment longer, watching his back disappear into the marching dark, and then she followed.

Behind them, at the edge of the torchlight, the singing had stopped.

That was worse. That was infinitely worse. Things that hunted you sang because they wanted you to hear them coming. Things that went silent wanted you to turn around.

"MOVE!" Felix's voice cracked across the column like a whip. "Double pace! NOW!"

The mountain loomed above them, patient and black, and they ran toward it like children running toward a lit window.

* * *

*Both*

The pass was exactly as narrow and cruel as the hermit had promised: a throat of stone where the mountain's shoulders pinched together, walled on one side by a cliff face streaked with old snow and on the other by a drop into cloud. The wind screamed through it with a sound almost like voices, and Paloma had to wonder, with a fatigue-addled sort of detachment, whether the mountain itself had been whispering to travelers for millennia and everyone had simply agreed to call it weather.

The hermit stood at the mouth of it, leaning on his staff, waiting for them as though he had been waiting for two hundred years.

Which, Paloma reflected, he had.

"You brought more than I expected," the old man said, surveying the ragged column with milky eyes that somehow saw everything. "And less than I hoped. The fragments—are they here?"

Felix unshouldered the bundle. The oathstone caught the torchlight and threw it back wrong, deep and gold, the way honey throws back candleflame. Beside it the inkwell sat in its wool wrappings, and beside that, grim and heavy, the severed hand of the knight who had fought a war that ended before either of them was born. Three pieces of a covenant that had once spanned the world, small enough to hold in two arms.

"Three," Felix said. "The fourth waits at the peak. The blade."

"And the Hollow Star follows you like a hound follows blood." The hermit nodded, unsurprised. "It has always known where the peak is. What it has never known is the *way*—" he tapped the stones of the pass with his staff, and somewhere deep in the rock something answered, a low harmonic hum that Paloma felt in her teeth— "and I am the last keeper of the way. While I stand here, the pass stays shut to it."

"While you stand here," Paloma repeated. The words tasted like copper. She looked at the old man—at the great age folded into his small frame, at the covenant-keeper's mark half-faded on the back of his hand, twin to the ones on hers—and understanding arrived with the gentleness of a landslide. "No. No, there has to be another way to seal it. There's always another way. We found one for the rite itself, we—"

"Child." He smiled, and it transformed his ancient face into something young. "I have been dying since before your grandmother's grandmother drew breath. The binding kept me alive past all sense or mercy, and when your Felix cut the channel at Corvane—" he inclined his head toward Felix, who had gone very still— "the binding began to fail. I am not sacrificing myself. I am simply choosing the manner of something that was already happening. There is a difference. Your Felix taught me that phrase. I find I like it."

"The converted will reach the pass within a day," Sera said quietly. She alone seemed to understand what was being decided, and her voice was steady in the way of soldiers delivering eulogies. "Maybe less."

"Then maybe less is what I need." The hermit turned and pressed his staff into Felix's hands. Up close, Paloma could see the wood was older than any tree—older, perhaps, than trees, grained with patterns like frozen starlight. "For the forge. It was cut from the first ward-tree, back when the valley was young and the god still spoke. It belongs at the cutting. And you—" he gripped Felix's forearm with surprising strength— "you asked me once, on the mountain path, why your god chose silence. I told you it was to hide from the Hollow Star. That was true. But it was not the whole truth."

"Tell me," Felix said hoarsely.

"When you stand at the blade, you will understand without words. He was always better at showing than telling." The hermit released him and stepped back, out of the torchlight, into the shadow of the pass. "Go. Climb. Do not look back—I mean that literally, children, looking back is a door and I cannot bar all the doors. And Paloma Ibarra—"

"Yes?"

"Your mother's notes. The ones you have not yet been able to make yourself read." His smile turned knowing, and unbearably kind. "Read them tonight. At the fireline, before the final climb. She wrote them for exactly the moment you are standing in, and you will want her voice tomorrow."

Paloma's throat closed. She managed a nod.

The column filed past the old man into the throat of the pass—four hundred refugees, the wounded carried on litters, the knight's ghost-regiment saluting him with rusted gauntlets raised. Felix was the last but one. He stopped before the hermit, opened his mouth, and Paloma watched two men of few words discover together that there were no words sufficient.

What Felix did instead was simple. He knelt, briefly, the way he must have knelt as a child soldier before his silent god, and pressed his forehead to the old man's hand. Then he rose and walked past, and did not look back, and his shoulders shook only once.

Paloma paused at the hermit's side. "Keeper," she said, using the old title, the one her mother had taught her. "When you see them—my mothers, my fathers, all the Ibarras in whatever comes after—tell them the daughter made it to the forge."

"Daughter," the hermit said gently, "when you see them yourself, you can tell them personally. Now go. I have a door to hold, and you have a mountain to answer."

She went. She did not look back either, though every step screamed at her to.

Behind them, the humming of the pass deepened into a chord like a cathedral organ, and the mountain sealed itself behind them with a sound like a held breath finally released.

* * *

*Paloma*

They made the fireline as true night fell—the last shelf of flat ground below the final ascent, where the wind dropped just enough to let a hundred small fires burn without scattering. The wounded were laid close to the flames. The fragments were set in the center, in a ring of salt Paloma traced with hands that only trembled a little.

She sat apart from the fires, on a stone still warm from some memory of the mountain's deep heat, and unwrapped her mother's notes.

She had carried them since the drowned chapel. Through Corvane, through the archive, through the ravine country and the frozen battlefield. She had read the first page forty times—the opening invocation, formal and safe, the kind of thing any guildmaster might write—and had never turned past it, because turning the page meant admitting her mother had known how this ended.

Tonight, because a two-hundred-year-old man was sealing himself into a mountain pass to buy her time, she turned the page.

*If you are reading this,* her mother had written, in the fierce slanting script the inkwell knew so well, *then the covenant has broken, the stars are dark, and you have done the impossible thing and gathered the fragments. I am proud of you. I am sorry. Both of those are true forever.*

*You will have learned by now that the old rite demands an Ibarra surrender everything. You will also have learned—or will soon—that the guild perverted this into slaughter, and that you fled them for it, and that you have spent every mile since wondering if you are running from your inheritance or toward it. Stop wondering. Here is what the guild never understood, because understanding it would have cost them their power:*

*The sacrifice is not death. It was never death. Death is easy; the mountain takes death gladly and gives back nothing. The sacrifice is *surrender*—the giving-up of magic entire, willingly, into the new covenant, so that the binding is fed not by a victim but by a gift. An Ibarra must choose powerlessness. That is the price our family was always meant to pay, and the reason we were given so much power to begin with: someone must be able to afford to lose it.*

*There is a third path, little star. There was always a third path. The covenant of shared burden—two hands, one sigil, neither dominant. My own mother dreamed of it and died before she could cut it. Perhaps you will finish her work. Perhaps you will find something even she could not imagine. Either way—*

Here the handwriting changed. Shakier. Slower.

*Either way, whoever stands with you at that blade—if they are worthy of you, and I suspect anyone who survived this far must be—hold their hand when you cut. Not for the magic. For the courage. Magic I can teach. Courage comes only from company.*

*All my love, longer than the stars,
—Mama*

Paloma read it twice. Then a third time, tracing the final shaky line with her fingertip, and she did not cry, quite, but the wind off the peak sounded for a while very much like a woman laughing.

When she looked up, Felix was sitting beside her. She hadn't heard him come. She wondered if she ever would.

"She knew," Paloma said. "About the third path. About the shared burden. She knew, and she died anyway, and the guild buried her knowledge with her."

"Not buried." Felix nodded at the pages in her hands. "Written down. Hidden where only someone desperate enough and stubborn enough would keep reading." Something moved at the corner of his mouth—not quite a smile, but the place where smiles lived. "Sounds like she knew you pretty well."

"Felix." Paloma closed her mother's notes and held them against the inkwell, against her heartbeat. "Tomorrow, at the blade. Whatever happens. Will you—"

"Already planned on it." He looked out at the dark, at the sealed pass below, at the distant smear of corrupted night crawling up the world's skirts. His jaw tightened. "Paloma. Listen."

She listened. Beneath the wind, beneath the murmur of four hundred frightened people, she heard it: a low, rhythmic thrumming, rising through the mountain's bones like a pulse. Like footsteps. Like something vast beginning to test the door a dying man was holding shut, and finding it—for now, only for now—closed.

"How long does he have?" she whispered.

Felix's hand found hers in the dark, and held it, the way her mother had asked.

"Not long enough," he said. "Rest. Tomorrow we climb."

Above them, through the torn and darkened sky, one single star—small, defiant, impossibly distant—flickered back to life over the summit of the forge-peak, as if the mountain itself were showing them exactly where to bring the dawn.
