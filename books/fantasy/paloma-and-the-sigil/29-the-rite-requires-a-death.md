## 29. The Rite Requires a Death

The forge-peak's shelter was a half-collapsed waystation built into the mountain's shoulder, its walls of fitted black stone still warm from some heat that had nothing to do with fire. Paloma felt it through her boots as she knelt among the wreckage of their retreat—scattered supplies, a cracked water skin, the hermit's staff laid reverently against the wall where Felix had placed it after they carried him down from the pass.

She had not slept. None of them had, not truly. Wounded men dozed in fevered snatches by the entrance, and beyond them the sky stayed black—not the honest black of night, but the starless void that had followed them since Corvane, since the false dawn tore open and something on the other side began to lean against the world like a drunkard leaning on a door.

Three fragments lay wrapped in oilcloth at the center of the room: the oathstone, dull as river gravel; the inkwell, cold enough to frost the cloth around it; the severed hand, which no one liked to look at and everyone checked on anyway, as if it might crawl off in the dark. Three pieces of a covenant older than any nation she could name. And somewhere above them, past the switchback trails and the shattered gate, the fourth fragment waited in the forge itself—the cutting-blade, and the thing it had been made to hold shut.

Her mother's satchel sat in her lap.

Paloma had carried it for eleven years without opening the inner pocket. She had told herself this was grief, and it was, but it was also fear, and she knew the difference now in a way she hadn't at nineteen. Grief keeps you close to what you lost. Fear makes you carry it without looking down.

The inner pocket opened with a drawstring gone soft with age. Inside: a bundle of letters bound with waxed thread, a small tin of needles, and a notebook bound in grey leather, its cover stamped with the Ibarra sigil—a circle broken by a single deliberate cut, then healed over with a second line drawn beside the wound rather than across it. *Mending, not erasing,* her mother used to say, tracing it with one finger. *That is the whole of our craft, little dove. Everything else is vanity.*

Paloma untied the thread.

* * *

The early pages were ordinary. Supply lists. Notes on pigment stability. A recipe for ink that resisted humidity, annotated in her mother's cramped leftward-slanting hand with complaints about the guild's insistence on inferior oak gall. Paloma read these first entries slowly, letting the voice rebuild itself in her memory—the dry humor, the impatience dressed up as precision. Her mother had been dead eleven years and still managed to sound annoyed at her.

Then, two-thirds through, the handwriting changed.

It didn't deteriorate so much as compress, the letters growing smaller and tighter, as though the writer were trying to fit more truth onto each page than paper could reasonably hold. The dates stopped being dates and started being weather. *Rain again. The wards hum at night. Marisol heard it too.* Names appeared that Paloma didn't know, then names she did—guild elders whose portraits hung in the Corvane hall, men and women she had been taught to revere.

*They have changed the rite,* her mother wrote, in an entry dated the year before the binding failed. *I keep saying "changed" as if it were recent. It was not recent. I have been reading the deep archive, the ledgers no one opens because no one wants to know what we actually did. The rite has been wrong for four generations. Perhaps longer.*

Paloma's hands had gone cold. She read on by the light of a single tallow stub, hunched over the book like a thief.

*The covenant requires a death. That much the guild teaches truly. But we have let ourselves believe the death is a body. A life poured out on the stone, blood and breath, finished. It is easier to teach that. Easier to demand. A death you can take from someone else.*

*It is a lie. The original rite—the one cut into the foundation stones of the forge itself, the one my grandmother's grandmother would have known—is not a killing. It is a surrender. The recutting demands that an Ibarra give up their magic entirely. Not spend it. Not pour it out in a moment of glorious sacrifice. Give it up. All of it. Permanently. The power that lets us write the world must be fed into the new sigil whole, and the hand that feeds it comes away empty forever. That is the death the covenant asks for. The death of the self we were born as.*

*No wonder they perverted it. Who would choose that?*

Paloma set the book down. Her fingers were shaking.

Outside, someone coughed—the wet, rattling cough of a man with a broken rib he couldn't afford to favor. One of the frozen knight's old regiment, who had come with them from the pass and were now the last soldiers she had. Through the doorway she could see the black sky, absolute, and beneath it the distant glow of Corvane's false dawn burning on the horizon like a wound that wouldn't clot.

She picked the book back up.

*I confronted Elder Serrano with this,* the next entry read. *I brought him the foundation rubbings. He looked at them for a long time and then he said—and I will write his exact words because I want to remember them when my courage fails me—he said, "Yes, Aldara. We know. We have always known. But a guild cannot run on volunteers."*

*A guild cannot run on volunteers. As if the covenant were a harvest and the magic-takers merely laborers who needed managing. As if the choice itself were an inefficiency to be engineered around.*

*So they found a substitute. If the rite needs an Ibarra emptied of power, why not empty the Ibarra first—by taking everything, including the life? Dead is certainly empty. The sigil accepts it. It has accepted it for generations. But it accepts it the way a starving man accepts spoiled bread: it keeps him alive and poisons him slowly. Every renewal since the perversion has been weaker than the last. Every binding thinner. The god has been held in a cage patched with corpses, and we called it devotion.*

*And the last payment—the one that should have come twenty years ago—was never made at all. Because the heir they chose refused. Because she ran.*

Paloma closed her eyes.

She knew, before she turned the page, whose name she would find there. She had known it in her bones since the night the stars went out, since she crossed the ash road and felt the collapsed wards like an open wound in the world. Her mother had written about her obliquely before—*the girl is stubborn, she gets it from me, may the circle forgive us both*—but this entry named her plainly, in the compressed desperate hand of a woman writing a letter she never intended to send.

*Paloma. If you are reading this, then either I am dead or you are very good at picking locks, and knowing you, both. Listen to me. Whatever they tell you, whatever they show you in the guild hall with all its candles and its solemn faces—there is another way. There was always another way. The rite does not require your death. It requires your surrender. They will tell you those are the same thing. They are not. Surrender is harder. Surrender means living afterward, in a world you can no longer touch with power, being only what your hands and heart can make of themselves. Killing you is easy for them. Letting you live diminished is unbearable. That is why they changed it. Not for the covenant. For their comfort.*

*If you run, I understand. I ran too, once, in every way but the body. But if you ever stand at the forge, little dove, know that the trap they built was never the rite. The trap is the belief that you owe them your death. You don't. You owe the world your honesty, and the god your mending, and yourself your life—your actual life, the one that continues on the other side of the giving.*

*The circle breaks. The circle heals. Beside the wound, never across it.*

*—Your mother, who loved you badly and tried anyway.*

* * *

Paloma did not know how long she sat there. The candle burned down to a puddle; someone relit it without asking, and she registered the kindness distantly, the way you register rain on a roof.

The trap of inheritance. Her mother had named it, and the naming changed nothing and everything. Because here was the shape of it, laid bare at last: her whole life, her exile, the guild's hunters, Hale's obsession, even the horror of the sacrificial rite she'd fled—all of it had been built on a lie of translation. *A death is required*, the guild said, and meant a corpse. Four generations of Ibarras had gone to the stone believing they were fulfilling the covenant, when they were being harvested by men who found voluntary diminishment intolerable. Her ancestors' blood hadn't sealed anything. It had thinned the binding year by year, corpse-patch over corpse-patch, until the wards failed and the stars went dark and a mercenary named Felix Vasquez stood on a valley wall watching his whole cosmology shatter overhead.

And the true rite—she turned the thought over, testing its edges—was worse. Not physically. Physically it asked nothing but presence and consent. But it asked for the one thing the guild had spent centuries ensuring no Ibarra would ever willingly give: the end of herself as she was made.

Paloma looked at her hands. Sigilwright's hands, ink-stained at the knuckles, scarred from the drowned chapel and the frozen battlefield and the vaults beneath Corvane. Hands that could write a ward strong enough to turn a wight, that had held a collapsing channel open while Felix dragged wounded men clear. If she gave up her magic, those hands would be just hands. She would feel the craft dying in her the way an amputee feels the ghost limb—every day, every hour, reaching for something that is no longer there.

And she would live.

That was the terrible arithmetic her mother had tried to give her, wrapped in love and hidden in a locked pocket for eleven years. The guild's version offered a clean ending: pain, blood, done. The true version offered no ending at all. It offered decades of mornings waking up ordinary. Of watching students learn what she could no longer perform. Of standing in a world full of starlight she had relit and being unable to light so much as a candle-wisp with a word.

*Being only what your hands and heart can make of themselves.*

She thought of Felix then—not dramatically, not as a figure in a story, but specifically. Felix asleep against the far wall, one hand curled near his knife even in exhaustion, his face in sleep younger and more frightened than he would ever permit it to be awake. Felix, who had been sworn to a silent god at seven years old and had built his entire self around that debt. Felix, whom the god would one day offer a bargain of its own—she was sure of it, sure the way she was sure of ink and stone. A silent god, imprisoned for generations, needing a warden. Needing someone willing to sit in the dark in its place.

Two traps, then. Hers: the lie that inheritance demanded her death. His: the lie that loyalty demanded his imprisonment. Both forged by institutions that preferred sacrifices to surrenders, endings to continuances, because an ending could be administered by someone else and a surrender had to be chosen.

Her mother's voice, dry and impatient even from the grave: *Stop philosophizing, little dove. Read the rest.*

There was more. Of course there was more. The final pages of the notebook contained diagrams—foundation rubbings of the forge's inner chamber, the original rite rendered in her mother's precise notation. And at the bottom of the last page, in handwriting that had begun to fail, a single unfinished line:

*There may be a third path. Two hands, one sigil—if the burden could be shared, if the covenant could be cut between two willing—*

The sentence stopped. The next page was blank. Whatever her mother had glimpsed, she had died before she could prove it, or dare it, or perhaps even name it fully to herself.

But she had named it enough.

Paloma sat back. Around her, the waystation breathed with the sounds of the wounded and the watchful, and above it all the Hollow Star pressed against the seam of the world, patient as rot. Somewhere on the trails below, Hale's converted host was climbing. They had a day, perhaps two. Maybe less.

She rose, knees stiff, and crossed the room to where Felix slept. She stood over him for a long moment, the notebook held against her chest like a shield, and understood that what she had to say next would require more courage than the forge ever would.

"Felix," she said quietly.

His eyes opened instantly—soldier's reflexes, no transition, no confusion. He looked at her, at the book, at her face, and whatever he saw there made him go very still.

"We need to talk," Paloma said. "About what the rite actually costs. And about what your god is going to ask you."

Because she had read the old ledgers too, in the years of her exile, and she knew what a silent god wanted most in all the world.

It wanted someone to take its place.
