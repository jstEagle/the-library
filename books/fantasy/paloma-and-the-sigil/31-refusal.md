## 31. Refusal

The fire had burned down to a red heart by the time Felix found the words.

They were in the high hollow below the pass, the one the hermit had called the Kettle—a bowl of granite where wind came to die and the stars, those few that had returned since the false dawn cracked the sky open, seemed close enough to touch. Paloma sat across from him with her mother's notes spread over her knees, pages soft as cloth from handling, her lips moving faintly as she read. The light of the fire moved on her face and made her look, for a moment, like someone who had never carried anything heavier than a satchel of ink.

Felix had been carrying his secret for two days now. It sat in his chest like a swallowed stone, and every hour it grew heavier, and every hour he told himself he would speak of it *tomorrow*, and every tomorrow arrived and found him silent still. He knew this silence. He had lived inside it for twenty years. He knew how it started small and reasonable—*there is no need to frighten her, not yet, not while she sleeps*—and how it ended with a man alone in a valley, praying into a void that never answered, mistaking his own cowardice for devotion.

He would not end that way again.

"Paloma."

She looked up. In the firelight her eyes were the color of wet slate, and there were shadows under them deep enough to hold water. She had not slept properly since the hermit died holding the pass; none of them had. But she smiled at him, tired and crooked, the smile that had become, without either of them naming it, the thing he checked for first each morning.

"You've got your confession face," she said.

"My what?"

"The face you make when you're about to tell me something you'd rather eat than say." She closed the notes on her finger to keep her place. "You wore it before the oathstone. You wore it at the fireline. I've started keeping score."

He almost laughed. That was the cruelty of her—that she could make him want to laugh at the exact moment he most needed to be serious. He fed a branch to the fire and watched it catch, and when he spoke, his voice came out lower than he intended.

"At the ravine," he said. "When I was dying. My god spoke to me."

Her finger slipped from the page. "You said it pulled you. You said—"

"I said it pulled me toward the peak. That was true. But it wasn't all of it." He turned his hands over, palms up, the way he had knelt as a child in the temple of the silent god, waiting for a voice that never came. "It offered me a bargain. A way to seal the channel the false dawn tore open. Alone. Without you. Without anyone."

Paloma went very still. He had learned to read her stillnesses in the months they had traveled together—the stillness of calculation, the stillness of fear, the stillness of a woman deciding exactly how much truth she could afford to spend. This one was none of those. This one was the stillness of someone bracing.

"It offered to make me its warden," Felix said. "Its new silence. The way it has been silent for twenty years—no. Longer. Since the binding." His throat worked. "I would take its place, Paloma. Body and soul. I would sit inside the dark with the Hollow Star pressing against the seal, forever, listening to it hunger, and no one would ever know my name again. Not even me, eventually. The god was very clear about that part. It said the forgetting was a mercy."

The fire popped. Somewhere above them, wind worried at the lip of the pass, and beneath the wind—if you listened the way Felix had learned to listen—he could feel the Hollow Star's attention, vast and patient, turning over the world like a tongue searching for a sore tooth.

"And you've known this," Paloma said quietly, "for two days."

"Yes."

"Why?" The word cracked in the middle. "Why would you sit on that? We could have—we could have been looking for another way since the ravine. Every hour, Felix. Every hour we've spent walking, I could have been reading, thinking—"

"Because I was going to say yes."

The words fell into the fire and burned there. Paloma stared at him.

"That's why I didn't tell you," he went on, and now that the dam had broken he could not stop, the confession pouring out of him the way blood once had, hot and unstoppable. "Not because I was ashamed. Because if I told you, you'd find a reason to refuse it, and I wanted—I *wanted*—" He pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes. "I have been sworn to that god since I was nine years old. It gave me purpose when I was nothing, a spear-boy sleeping in temple straw. Its silence was the shape of my whole life. And when it finally spoke, do you know what it asked? Not my service. Not my sword. It asked if I would *take its place*. It has been looking for someone willing, Paloma. All these years. It's tired. Gods can be tired, no one tells you that. And some rotten, faithful part of me heard that weariness and thought: *at last. At last something I can give it.*"

"You would have left me," Paloma said. Not angry. Worse than angry—wondering, the way one wonders at a wound found on one's own body. "You would have gone into the dark and let me think you dead in a ravine, and I'd have climbed this mountain believing I'd failed you, and you'd have let me believe it because it was *easier*—"

"Yes." He did not soften it. She deserved the ugly shape of it, unsoftened. "Yes. That is exactly what I would have done. That is what twenty years of silence taught me to do—to call sacrifice holy so I never have to call it what it is. Running away with better lighting."

Paloma was quiet for a long moment. When she spoke again, her voice had changed—not gentler, but steadier, the voice she used when cutting sigils, when a single wrong stroke meant blood.

"Then here is mine," she said. "Since we're spending truths tonight."

She reached into the inner pocket of her coat, where she kept the things she could not bear to lose, and drew out a folded square of vellum gone brown at the creases. She did not unfold it. She held it in both hands, the way one holds a bird with a broken wing.

"When my guild tried to force the renewal," she said, "the rite they used—the rite that 'failed' a generation ago—it required an Ibarra's death. I told you that. What I didn't tell you is that they didn't intend it to be my mother. They intended it to be *me*. I was nineteen. They had already cut the receiving-sigil into the floor of the sanctum. My own name was in the center of it, in my mother's hand." Her thumb traced the folded edge of the vellum, back and forth, back and forth. "My mother found out. She took my place. That's the rite that failed—or didn't fail, depending on how you read it. She died, and the covenant held for another few years, and everyone called it a tragedy instead of a murder, and I ran, and I have been running for eleven years telling myself I escaped."

She looked up at him across the fire.

"So when you say you were going to walk into the dark and leave me grieving—understand that grief is a country I already live in, Felix. You wouldn't have been sparing me anything. You'd just have been moving me to a worse neighborhood in it."

The wind came down through the pass then, carrying the smell of snow and cold iron, and neither of them spoke until it passed. Felix found that his eyes were burning. He did not wipe them. Some things deserved to be seen.

"So," he said at last. "We're both liars. We've established that. Thoroughly."

"We're both *faithful*," Paloma corrected. "That's the disease. Lying is just the symptom." She unfolded the vellum at last, and he saw it was a letter, old, the ink faded to the color of dried violets. "This is my mother's hand. The last thing she wrote me. I've read it a thousand times and I always stop at the same line, because the line after it frightened me too much to finish."

"What does it say?"

Paloma read aloud: "*The covenant was never meant to be carried alone. Remember, little star: a sigil cut by one hand is a chain. A sigil cut by two—*" She stopped.

"*Is what?*" Felix leaned forward. "Paloma. Is what?"

"I don't know. I never read the rest. Until tonight, I think I was afraid of what it would ask of me." She laughed, short and unsteady. "And now I've heard what your god asks of you, and suddenly whatever's on this page seems very tame."

She smoothed the vellum flat against her knee, tilting it toward the firelight, and read:

"*A sigil cut by two hands is a bridge.*"

Silence, except the fire.

"There's more," she whispered. Her eyes moved down the page, faster now, hungry. "Felix—there's *so much more*. She wrote it all out. Why did I never—why did I fold this up and hide it like a—" She shook her head. "Listen. Listen to this. '*The first covenant was cut by one because the Ibarra of that age feared to share the burden. Pride, dressed as duty. She bound the god alone and paid alone, and every generation after paid alone, and the payments grew heavier because a burden carried by one grows heavier, not lighter. But the original craft—the craft from before the pride—allowed for a shared cutting. Two anchors. Two hands. The burden halved and doubled at once: halved upon each, doubled in strength. No single failure can tear it. No single death can break it. But it requires—*'"

Paloma's voice caught.

"It requires what?" Felix asked.

She looked at him across the fire, and he watched her arrive at the sentence before she read it, watched her understand it a full breath before her lips could form the words.

"*It requires trust,*" she read. "*Not faith. Faith is belief without evidence, and the old covenant ran on faith, which is why it ran on blood—blood was the collateral, the surety, the proof of commitment when trust was absent. A shared cutting cannot run on blood. There is no collateral between two people who trust each other. There is only the giving itself, unrecoverable, unconditional. Each anchor must place their whole self in the other's keeping and cut blind, trusting the second hand to meet theirs. If either hesitates, the sigil fails. If either lies—even to themselves—the sigil fails. There is no rite to force it. There is no sacrifice to substitute for it. It cannot be demanded, inherited, or stolen. It can only be given.*"

The fire settled. Above the pass, through the notch in the peaks, the wounded sky pulsed its slow bruised violet where the Hollow Star pressed against the thinning seal.

Felix became aware that his heart was going very hard and very fast, like a fist knocking on a door.

"A bridge," he said. "Not a chain."

"Not a chain." Paloma's hands were trembling. She pressed them flat against her knees. "Two anchors. Neither dominant. Neither sacrificed. The god keeps its freedom and its watch; the world keeps its stars; and we—" She stopped, and started again. "We keep each other. That's the price. That's the whole terrible price. Not blood. Not magic. Not silence. Just—" She gestured helplessly at the space between them, the fire, the night, everything. "*This.* Given completely. With no taking it back."

"It'll fail," Felix said. "You know it will fail. Look at us. I hid a god's bargain from you for two days. You hid your mother's death from me for eleven years. We are possibly the two least qualified people in the history of the world to attempt a sigil that runs on trust."

"Oh, absolutely," Paloma said. "We're catastrophic candidates. We should be disqualified on sight." And then she laughed—really laughed, ragged and disbelieving, tears standing bright along her lashes—and Felix felt something in his chest come loose, some plate of armor he had worn so long he had forgotten it was armor. "Which means, mercenary, that we have exactly one job between now and the forge-peak."

"Sharpen the blade?"

"Tell each other the truth." She held his gaze across the fire, and her slate eyes were fierce and terrified and certain, all at once. "All of it. Everything we've hoarded. Before we climb one step higher. Because if we walk into that forge with secrets still in our pockets, the sigil will taste them, and it will fail, and everything—your god, my family, the stars, the world—fails with us. So." She squared her shoulders like a woman facing a firing line. "Ask me anything. Anything at all. I will answer it true. And then you'll owe me the same."

Felix looked at her for a long time.

He thought about the boy in the temple straw, praying into silence. He thought about the vision in the drowned chapel, and the frozen knight still fighting his ended war, and the hermit dying on the pass with the enemy's name in his mouth. He thought about every wall he had ever stood behind—valley walls, ward walls, the walls of his own sworn silence—and how every one of them had been built, in the end, out of the same material: the fear that if the world saw him plainly, it would find nothing worth keeping.

And here was this woman, this exiled, exhausted, magnificent liar, offering to stand in the open with him. No wards. No walls. No blood-price to hide behind.

"Tomorrow," he said. "At first light. We start with the vision the oathstone showed me—the one I never told you. The one about your ancestors and mine." He saw her flinch and did not look away. "All of it, Paloma. Even the parts that will make you hate me for knowing. Especially those."

"I could never—"

"You might," he said gently. "And I'd rather survive your hatred than your pity. First light. Then the notes—all of them, every page you've skipped because it frightened you. We read them together. And then we climb."

Paloma nodded slowly. Then she rose, crossed the space between them, and sat down beside him on the cold stone, shoulder to shoulder, close enough that he could feel the warmth of her through two layers of travel-worn cloth. She did not take his hand. She did not need to. Something older than touch passed between them there in the Kettle, under the wounded sky—something that felt, for the first time in either of their lives, like the beginning rather than the payment.

"One more thing," she murmured, drowsy, her head tipping toward his shoulder. "When we cut it—two hands, one sigil—which hand do you suppose goes on top?"

Felix smiled at the fire.

"Neither," he said. "That's the whole point."

Above the pass, far to the east, past the bruise in the sky, something flickered—a point of light where no light should be, faint and uncertain, like a candle carried a long way through the dark by someone who refused to set it down. Neither of them saw it. But the god felt it. And in the silence where a god's heart should be, something that had not stirred in twenty years stirred again, and turned, with the last of its fading strength, toward the mountain—

—because it was not the only one climbing.

* * *

Dawn came gray and clean to the Kettle, and found them already awake, the notes spread between them, her mother's voice rising off the pages like heat off stone. They read until the light strengthened. And it was Paloma, three pages deep into a section headed *On the Cutting of Shared Seals*, who suddenly went rigid, her finger stopping on a passage as though the ink had bitten her.

"Felix," she said. "There's a condition. One we haven't—"

She turned the page.

The page after it was blank.

No—not blank. She tilted it toward the light, and both of them saw it at once: a watermark, pressed deep into the paper, visible only when the sun struck it at an angle. A sigil. One neither of them had ever seen—and yet Felix knew it instantly, the way one knows a face from a dream, because he had seen its echo burned into the scorched fragment in the high priest's fire, a lifetime ago.

Beneath it, in his mother's smallest, most careful hand, Paloma's mother had written a single line:

"*The third path exists. But it must be cut at the place where the first covenant bled—and the blade remembers who it has killed.*"

Paloma looked up at Felix, and the hope on her face had sharpened into something that was almost fear.

"The forge-peak isn't just where the sigil was made," she breathed. "It's where someone *died* making it. And the blade—the original blade, the fourth fragment—"

"—remembers," Felix finished quietly.

Far below them, at the tree line, a horn sounded. Once. Twice. A third time, longer, and answered by many.

Hale's army had come.
