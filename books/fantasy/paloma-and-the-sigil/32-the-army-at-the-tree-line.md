## 32. The Army at the Tree Line

Felix had stood watch on a hundred cold dawns in the valley, and he knew every grammar of fear the body could write. He knew the fear before first contact, the dry-mouth stillness of it; he knew the fear in the middle of it, when thought burned away and only training remained; and he knew the fear after, which was the worst one, because that was the fear that stayed.

This morning's fear was none of those. This morning, watching the tree line below the forge-peak turn black with men, Felix felt something he had no name for. Not dread exactly. More like the moment in a dream when you realize you have to speak in front of everyone and you open your mouth and find you actually have something to say.

"They're early," said Sergeant Bray, beside him. She was refugee-born, a dyer's daughter from the lowlands who had taken up a spear three weeks ago because the alternative was weeping. Her knuckles were white around the shaft. "I told you they'd come at dawn. Nobody ever comes at dawn."

"Everybody comes at dawn," Felix said. "Dawn is when cowards feel brave and brave men feel tired."

"And which are we?"

"Both. That's why we're holding this line."

Below them, out of the pines, the cult's army came like spilled ink. Felix counted by habit—the old mercenary's arithmetic, ten files deep, then twenty—and stopped counting when the numbers stopped meaning anything. Three thousand, perhaps more. They marched without drums, which was worse than any drumming, because drums were honest. The silence of their advance said everything about what Magistrate Oren Hale believed: that resistance was a formality, a clerical error soon corrected. They carried banners of unbleached white, each stitched with the false dawn—a sun half-risen, its rays ending in hooks. In the grey light they did not glow. They simply refused to be dark.

And above them all, where the sky should have held stars, there was nothing. It had been nothing for so long now that Felix sometimes forgot what he was missing until he looked up and felt the absence like a pulled tooth.

"You've done this before," Bray said quietly. "Held a line against bad odds."

"Once or twice."

"How many times did you hold?"

He thought about it honestly, because she deserved honesty and because lying to your own people before a battle is a debt you pay for afterward. "Twice out of five," he said. "The other three, we ran, and running saved us. Today we can't run. So I'd put our odds better than my average."

She laughed—a short, startled bark, quickly strangled. Around them along the ridge, other soldiers heard it and turned, and some of them smiled, and Felix understood suddenly that this was his job now. Not the spear-work. Not the killing. The joke. The calm voice. The arithmetic spoken aloud so that three hundred frightened people could borrow his steadiness the way you borrow fire from a neighbor's hearth.

*You learned to lead for pay,* he thought. *Now learn to do it for free.*

* * *

They had made their preparations in the nine days since the retreat from Corvane, and the mountain had helped as much as it could—which was not much, but was not nothing.

The forge-peak rose from a shoulder of broken country: scree fields below, then a shelf of hard ground where the old covenant-keepers had built their approach road, switchbacking up through three natural terraces toward the forge itself, hidden now behind its veil of heat-shimmer and stone. The road was the only way up for an army. Everything else was cliff and knife-rock. Which meant the battle would be fought on the terraces, terrace by terrace, and Felix intended to make Hale buy each one at retail.

On the lowest terrace they had cut the road in two places and stacked the rubble into breastworks chest-high. Behind the breastworks: sixty of the knight's old regiment, men and women who had followed Captain Aldric Vane down out of the frozen battlefield where their commander had kept faith for two hundred years. They drilled in silence, moved in silence, and fought—Felix had seen it at Corvane—in a discipline so old it had gone past doctrine into something like religion. Vane himself stood at their center, gaunt and grey, the mercy-killing still living behind his eyes, wearing armor patched with steel from four centuries.

Behind them, on the second terrace, the refugees. Two hundred and change, armed with spears ground from fence posts, with hatchets, with the short curved knives of sheepherders. Untrained. Terrified. And staying anyway. Felix had expected maybe fifty to stay when he'd asked for volunteers. He had gotten two hundred, and he still did not fully understand why, except that people who had watched their crops die and their children go strange-eyed with hunger had decided somewhere in the last season that they were finished being run over. He had drilled them as best he could—not to fight well, but to not break. Hold the wall. Thrust and step back. Do not chase. The rest was luck and the mountain.

And on the third terrace, closest to the forge: Paloma's sigils.

Felix climbed to check them at mid-morning, picking his way up through the shimmer. She was kneeling at the wardline with her sleeves rolled back, ink to her elbows, drawing the last anchor-stone's pattern with a stylus of bone. Even stripped of most of her power—even now, with whatever she'd already fed into the fragments—her work had a rightness to it that Felix felt in his teeth, the way you felt a true note in a song. Lines that looked simple until you realized no simpler line could do the job.

"Tell me again what these do," he said, crouching beside her.

"They slow them down." She didn't look up. "A sigil isn't a wall, Felix. It's a question asked very forcefully. 'Why are you here? What do you want? Are you sure?' Most men, most of the time, aren't sure enough. They'll hesitate at the wardline without knowing why, and hesitation is worth thirty men."

"And if they are sure?"

"Then the sigil spends itself asking, and breaks, and I'll feel it go." She sat back on her heels and wiped her hands on her thighs, leaving grey streaks. There was ink in the creases of her knuckles and a smear across her jaw where she'd pushed her hair back, and Felix caught himself looking too long and made himself stop. There would be time for looking later, if there was a later. He had started allowing himself that thought—*if there is a later*—and hated how much comfort it gave him. Before her, a later had been an abstraction. Now it was a thing he wanted, specific and warm, and wanting it made him easier to kill.

"The Hollow Star will help them," she went on. "It pours courage into the empty. Men with nothing inside them take its offer gladly. So expect the front ranks to be fearless. Fearless isn't skilled, though. Remember that. A fearless man still bleeds."

"Aye." He hesitated. "Paloma. If it goes wrong down there—"

"It won't go wrong."

"It might."

"Then don't say goodbye to me now." She finally looked up at him, and her eyes were steady and furious and alive. "Say it never. Say it after. I've spent my whole life being the last of something, Felix Vasquez. I refuse to practice being the last of us."

He nodded, and found he had no voice, and climbed back down to the line.

* * *

The cult reached the foot of the scree at noon, and for an hour, nothing happened.

That was Hale's doing, Felix knew. The Magistrate was no fool; he had bought his victories cheaply all his life, with money and promises and other people's desperation, and he would not spend lives on a hillside when patience might be free. So the white banners spread out below the tree line in perfect order, and horns sounded once—long, flat notes, like a held breath—and then the whole army simply waited, letting the defenders look at them, letting the numbers do their work. Letting three hundred people count three thousand and do the arithmetic themselves.

Felix walked the first terrace wall, unhurried, visible, and let them count him instead.

At the western end, Vane's regiment stood in ranks so straight they might have been drawn with a cord. The old knight caught Felix's eye and touched two fingers to his breastplate—an antique salute from an army dead two hundred years—and Felix returned it awkwardly, feeling like a boy saluted by a monument. Last night, by the fires, Vane had told him quietly: "We held a pass once, in the war that ended without our knowing. Two hundred years, we held it. I know how this day goes, Captain. Either the line holds and we are relieved by history, or it breaks and we are relieved by death. Both are honorable. Choose neither in advance."

At the eastern end, Bray's refugees clustered behind their wall of road-rubble, and Felix stopped among them and shared out the last of the dried plums from his pack, walking down the rank, handing them out one by one, saying names. Marta. Osric. Little Tam, who was fifteen and lied about it. He had learned all their names in nine days and it had cost him sleep, and now he spent the sleep like coin, because a man whose name you know will hold a wall longer than a stranger.

"You remember the drill," he said, loud enough for the whole rank. "Thrust and step. Nobody chases. When the horn sounds twice, fall back to the second terrace in order, and nobody runs unless I run, and I promise you I'm too mean to run." Laughter, thin but real. "They outnumber us. Fine. Numbers climb hills badly. We hold the high ground and make them come up single file to die politely. And when the sun sets tonight—" he pointed up, at the blank sky, at the absence where the stars should be— "we'll be one night closer to getting those back. Every one of you has someone who wants to see them again. So does the woman up the hill. So do I. That's what we're fighting for. Not the peak. The sky."

No cheering—they weren't an army that cheered yet—but a sound ran along the wall anyway, a settling, like timber taking weight.

Then, below, the horns sounded twice, and the white banners began to move.

* * *

They came up the scree without order and without fear, exactly as Paloma had promised, and the first hour was everything Felix had feared and less than he had hoped.

Fearless men are terrible climbers. The scree field broke their rush apart before the fight even began, sending slides of stone down the slope, and by the time the vanguard reached the cut road they came in ragged, in twos and threes, into the mouths of the gaps. At the western gap, Vane's regiment received them like a door closing—shield-wall locked, spears moving in the short economical rhythm of men who had done this before the valley was walled. At the eastern gap, Bray's people held worse and bled more, but they held, thrust and step, thrust and step, and Felix moved along the line doing the work he knew: filling gaps, dragging wounded back, shouting the small corrections that keep a wall a wall instead of a crowd.

The sigils sang.

He felt them before he saw their effect—a pressure in the ears, a taste like copper rain—and then the cult's second wave hit the wardline below the wall and simply... faltered. Hundreds of men stopped at once, staring at their own feet, at the faint silver traceries glowing up through the dirt of the road. Felix watched a big man in white stand there while a spear took him, still frowning downward, puzzled, as though the ground had asked him a question he couldn't answer. Hesitation, worth thirty men. Paloma had undersold herself.

For two hours it worked. For two hours the terraces held, and the slopes below grew littered with white, and Felix allowed himself, in the small cold room at the back of his mind where mercenaries keep their ledgers, to mark the column: *holding.*

Then the Hollow Star noticed them.

It came without shape at first—a wrongness in the light, the shadows under the breastworks leaning the wrong way—and then it came as a sound, felt rather than heard, a vast patient attention turning toward the mountain like an eye. The faltering in the cult's ranks stopped. The fearless became something worse: coordinated. Waves began arriving in proper order, shields up, climbing over their own dead with the calm of ants. And along the eastern wall, Felix saw the refugees begin to hear it—that whisper he had heard in the ravine, the one that offered each man his deepest want. He saw Tam, the fifteen-year-old liar, go still at his post, eyes going soft and distant, spear drooling toward the dirt.

Felix crossed the wall and slapped him, once, hard.

"Stay here," he said. "Whatever it's showing you—it's a lie with good lighting. Look at me. Look at *me.*"

Tam blinked, wept once, and set his feet again. All along the wall, others were harder to save. Three men dropped their spears and walked down the slope toward the enemy with their arms open, smiling, and the white ranks parted to receive them, and Felix did not see them again. He marked it in the ledger, in the column where he kept the costs he couldn't repay, and he kept walking the line.

By late afternoon they had given the first terrace. Twice, in order, at the double-horn, exactly as drilled; twice, Vane's rearguard covering the pull-back with a precision that made Felix's professional heart ache. The second terrace was narrower, steeper, better walled, and there they settled in as the light went long and red, and the dead lay thick on the slope below, and both sides breathed.

Bray found him at the western anchor, binding a gash on his forearm. She was spattered to the shoulders and one eye was swelling shut, and she was grinning like a wolf.

"We held," she said.

"We held."

"First time I've held anything in my life except a dye-vat." She sat down heavily against the stones. Below them the cult's horns sounded retreat-not-defeat, the flat notes pulling the survivors back into the tree line's shadow, and for a little while the mountain was quiet except for the crying of the wounded and the wind off the ice-fields above.

Vane came along the wall, counting his dead with his lips moving. When he reached Felix, he said only: "Two hundred years I held a pass for a war that was over. Today I held a wall for a war that matters. I find I prefer it." Then he moved on, because old soldiers waste nothing, not even compliments.

Felix looked up the slope, toward the third terrace, toward the shimmer of the forge. Paloma stood at the wardline, small against the sky, one hand pressed flat against the largest of her anchor-stones. Feeling for cracks. As he watched, her head came up sharply, and she looked not down the slope at the retreating enemy but east, past them, toward the far passes—and her hand came away from the stone as if it had bitten her.

Then Felix felt it too, rising through the soles of his boots: a tremor in the mountain, rhythmic, patient, deliberate. Not the rumble of rockfall. A knocking. Something below the forge-peak's roots, in the dark where the Hollow Star had been kept for generations, pressing its palm against the inside of the world.

And down in the valley of the tree line, the white banners began to move again—all of them at once, no horns, no order—and above the army, impossibly, a light gathered. Not the sun. A false dawn, hooked rays and all, burning black-edged in the empty sky, and under its glare Felix heard Hale's voice arrive everywhere at once, carried on the Star's borrowed air, gentle as a father waking a child:

*"Enough. Give me the sigilwright, and the rest of you may go home."*

Beside Felix, Bray spat and picked up her spear. On the wall, two hundred exhausted people did the same. And up the slope, Paloma turned to face the false light with her ink-stained hands open at her sides, and shouted down to him, clear across the dying day:

"Felix! It's not coming up the road anymore—Hale's opened the channel! Whatever's under this mountain, it's coming up to meet him!"
