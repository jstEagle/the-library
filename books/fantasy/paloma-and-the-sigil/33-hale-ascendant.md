## 33. Hale Ascendant

The mountain screamed before the gates did.

Paloma felt it first as a wrongness in her teeth—a high, grinding note beneath the stone, like a bow drawn across a cracked violin string. She was kneeling in the forge's antechamber with her mother's notes spread across her knees, ink-stained fingers tracing a diagram of the old covenant's anchor-points, when the whole peak shuddered and a fine rain of mineral dust sifted down from the vaulted ceiling.

Then came the sound from below: iron on iron, shouts in the dark, and beneath it all a low chorus of voices that did not sound entirely human anymore.

"They're through," Felix said. He was already moving, sword half-drawn, standing in the doorway where the light of the forge-fire behind her met the darkness of the stair. His face was gray with ash and exhaustion. Three days they had held the approach—three days of barricades, of the frozen knight's regiment singing their old war-songs to keep morale alive, of refugees passing water and arrows hand to hand along the switchbacks—and three days had bought them exactly this: an hour's warning.

"How many?" Paloma asked, though she already knew it didn't matter.

"More than we have arrows for." He hesitated in the doorway, and she saw him weighing something—the old mercenary calculus, lives against objectives, retreat against stand. "The pass held longer than I thought it would. The hermit bought us—"

"I know what he bought us." Her voice came out harder than she intended. The hermit's body was still lying where they'd been able to retrieve it, wrapped in his own threadbare cloak, waiting for a burial the mountain might not grant them time to perform. She folded her mother's notes closed with hands that would not quite stop trembling. "Felix. If they reach the forge—"

"They won't."

"They *will*. We both know they will. The question is only what happens after." She stood, pressing the notes into the satchel at her hip, and looked past him down the long throat of stairs that wound away toward the lower galleries. Somewhere down there, torchlight was beginning to flicker against the walls like firelight seen through a keyhole. "Get everyone you can into the deep vaults. The ones behind the oathstone chamber. If the mountain cracks—"

"If the mountain cracks, nowhere is safe."

"*If* it cracks, I'd rather be under a hundred feet of stone than ten." She caught his arm. His pulse beat fast beneath her fingers, or perhaps that was her own heartbeat echoing through her grip. "Please. You hold people together better than walls do. Go be a wall."

Something moved across his face—not quite a smile, but its ghost. "And what will you be?"

"The thing they're climbing toward." She released him. "Go."

He went. She watched him take the stairs two at a time, shouting orders in that parade-ground voice that could cut through panic like a blade through wet canvas, and then she turned back to the forge.

* * *

The great forge-hall of the covenant had been built to intimidate, and even hollowed and cold it succeeded. It was a cavern the size of a cathedral nave, ribbed with pillars of natural basalt, its ceiling lost in darkness above. At its center stood the anvil—anvil was too small a word; it was an altar of black iron the size of a wagon bed, pitted with centuries of sigilwork cut so deep into its surface that the cuts had their own weather, their own shadows. Around it, arranged in the pattern her mother's notes called the *Crown of Hands*, were the fragments: the oathstone, dull and patient as a sleeping animal; the inkwell of names, its stopper sealed with wax and warning; the severed hand, still curled around nothing, still faintly warm.

And on a rack of blackened silver above them all, the cutting blade.

Her mother had described it once, in one of the few lessons given without anger: *It does not cut metal, Paloma. It cuts agreement. Everything else is theater.*

Paloma walked the Crown of Hands slowly, checking each fragment against the diagrams, feeling the low hum of them—three-quarters of a covenant, a chord missing its resolving note. Beneath the hum, far deeper, she could feel the other thing: the channel Hale's incomplete rite had torn open in Corvane, still unhealed despite everything, a thin cold draft blowing through the world's skin. Through it, faint as starlight through fog, something listened.

She had spent the last three days trying not to think about what it heard.

The mountain screamed again. Dust fell. From the direction of the lower stairs came a sound that made her stomach clench: not battle-cries now but chanting, hundreds of voices raised in the cadence of the Second Dawn liturgy, and threaded through the chant, growing louder, a grinding rhythm of pick and pry-bar against ancient door-seals.

They weren't trying to fight their way up. They were trying to *unseal* the mountain—Hale knew enough sigilcraft, stolen or inherited, to know that the forge's defenses were keyed to consent, and that no garrison, however brave, could hold a door whose locks were being politely asked to open.

"No," Paloma whispered. "No, no—" She snatched up the satchel and began pulling out chalk, ink, the bundle of prepared sigil-papers she and Felix had cut by lamplight over the last week. Her mother's notes said there was a warding configuration for the forge itself, a final seal, but it required—

It required an Ibarra's blood freely offered, written in her own hand, at every anchor-point of the Crown.

Of course it did. Everything in this cursed inheritance required her blood. Her family had been paying installments on this debt for nine generations, and the interest had never once been forgiven.

She knelt at the first anchor-point and drew the knife across her left palm.

* * *

The pain was familiar now, almost companionable. That frightened her more than the chanting did—how easily the body learned to treat its own ruin as routine. She worked quickly, blood-sigils blooming dark red against pale stone, speaking the binding syllables her mother had drilled into her before she'd been old enough to understand what they bound. Magic answered her the way it always did now: eagerly, hungrily, pouring up through her arm like water finding a breach, and she let it, because there was no strength left in her for caution. Her magic had been leaving her in increments since Corvane—she could feel it, a receding tide, the covenant's coming surrender already announced in her marrow—and whatever remained, she meant to spend it here if she must.

Second anchor. Third. The chanting grew louder. Between verses she began to hear individual voices, and that was worse: she recognized some of them. The caravan woman who had mended Felix's cloak outside Hollowmere. A boy who'd sold them honey-bread in the refugee camps, who had laughed at Felix's terrible jokes. The converted city had come up the mountain wearing the faces of everyone they'd failed to save, and it was singing.

Fourth anchor. Fifth. Her vision swam. She pressed her bleeding palm flat against the stone and pushed magic through until her teeth ached and her ears rang, and the seal took—she felt it lock into place, five points of herself hammered into the mountain's crown—

And then the doors at the bottom of the great stair opened all at once, with a sound like a gasp.

Light poured in. Not torchlight—*dawn-light*, the false kind, the manufactured radiance the cult had perfected in Corvane, gold without warmth, day without sun. It flooded up the stairwell and across the forge floor, and in its glare came Oren Hale.

He looked worse than he had in Corvane, and better. Worse because his left arm ended at the elbow now, bound in white linen gone brown at the edges; better because whatever had broken inside him when the incomplete rite tore open the channel seemed to have reassembled itself into something serene. He climbed the last steps unhurried, flanked by a dozen of the converted, their faces lit with that terrible contentment she remembered from the city—the look of people whose wanting had finally been answered by something.

"Sigilwright," he said. His voice carried in the vast hall without effort. "You've been busy."

"You're supposed to be dead," Paloma said. She got to her feet, putting herself between Hale and the Crown of Hands, and was distantly surprised to find her legs held. "I saw the Star take the forging circle. I saw what it does to—"

"To failures?" He smiled. Up close, she could see that his remaining hand was stained black to the wrist, ink or something older than ink sunk permanently into the skin. "Yes. I saw it too. Do you know what I learned, watching Aldous Vane scream himself into smoke? That the Hollow Star doesn't punish ambition. It punishes *insufficiency*. It consumed him because he tried to hold a covenant alone, with stolen blood, with a borrowed anchor." He walked forward, and the converted parted around him like water. "I've had weeks to correct my insufficiencies. And you—" he nodded at her bleeding hand, at the five sigils glowing faintly along the Crown, "—have been very helpful. An Ibarra's own seal, freely offered, on every anchor-point. Do you know what that means, Paloma?"

She knew. Cold slid down her spine.

"It means the hard part is done," Hale said softly. "You've anchored the covenant for me. All I need now are the fragments, and blood." He lifted his ruined arm slightly. "Mine has been flowing for days. Freely. Continuously. The Star has grown *very* fond of me."

"You'll be consumed like Vane."

"Perhaps." He said it lightly, terribly, the way a man agrees that he may catch a chill. "But I'll be consumed holding the covenant, which is more than any Ibarra can promise. Your family wrote itself into this prison for nine generations and never once asked what it would feel like to *hold* it. I intend to know. Even if knowing lasts only a moment, I will have held the dawn in my hand, and your ancestors' careful little cage will be open, and every starving soul in this dimming world will see what a real sky looks like."

"That's not what the covenant is *for*—"

"Nothing is *for* anything!" For the first time his serenity cracked, and underneath it she saw the failed apprentice, the heir cast out, the man who had bled on guild floors begging to be taught what his blood was worth. "That's the lie your whole family told the world. Nothing is for anything except what we make it for. The god didn't bind the Star for justice—it did it out of fear. Your ancestors didn't seal it for mercy—they did it out of guilt. Well. Guilt made them wardens. Ambition will make me a king." He gestured, and the converted moved.

What followed was not a battle so much as a tide. Paloma threw everything she had left—the sigil-papers, the prepared wards, raw force torn straight out of her diminishing reserves—and it slowed them the way a stick slows a river. She fought toward the anvil, toward the blade, and got a hand on the blackened silver hilt just as two of the converted seized her arms, and then Hale was there, his single hand closing over hers on the grip, and his touch was *cold*, cold as the channel, cold as the space between dead stars.

"Thank you," he said, "for gathering these. I'd have wasted months."

He pulled the cutting blade free.

* * *

Everything after that happened quickly, and Paloma's memory of it would remain forever fractured, like glass dropped on stone.

She remembered the blade rising. She remembered screaming words—she couldn't recall later whether they were curses or bindings or simply her mother's name. She remembered Felix's voice somewhere above, roaring, closer than it should have been, and the crash of someone falling down the long stair.

Most of all she remembered the moment the blade touched the anvil and Hale spoke the opening syllables of a forging.

Not her family's forging. Not the careful, grieving rite her mother's notes described, the covenant of shared burden cut by two hands. This was the cult's liturgy dressed in stolen craft, a forging with one hand and one will and an ocean of borrowed blood behind it, and the mountain *knew* the difference. The mountain had been built to receive covenants, and it received this one the way a healthy body receives poison.

The crack ran through the forge-hall from crown to root—a soundless flash first, every sigil on every pillar blazing white, and then the sound arrived, a groan so vast it was less heard than suffered. Basalt split. The altar of black iron buckled at its center like a ship's hull. And between the fragments of the Crown of Hands, where the air had always been merely air, something opened.

Paloma had seen the channel in Corvane. She had thought she understood it: a wound, a thinness, a draft of elsewhere leaking through.

This was not a wound. This was a *door*.

Through it she saw—or failed to see, for seeing broke against it—the Hollow Star entire. Not the hungry whispering presence that had spoken through the city's people. That had been a finger through a keyhole. This was the hand, and the arm, and the vast blind body behind it, a corruption the size of a fallen heaven, coiled in the dark between stars where the covenant had kept it for nine generations, and it was looking at her. It had always been looking at her. Every prayer her family had ever swallowed in silence had been a coin dropped down a well, and now the well was empty and the thing at the bottom was climbing.

It poured through.

Not light—unlight, a flood of perfect absence, spreading across the forge floor like spilled ink running uphill. Where it passed, the false dawn of the cult's lamps guttered and died. One of the converted turned toward it with arms raised in welcome, mouth open in ecstasy, and the unlight took his welcome and emptied him, and he folded like a coat slipping off a hook, and he did not get up, and the other converted began, at last, to scream.

Even Hale screamed. But he did not stop.

Paloma watched him plant the cutting blade upright in the cracking anvil, watched him press his bleeding stump against the black iron, watched him pour himself into the rite with the total abandon of a man leaping into a fire because the alternative was living. The forging syllables tore out of him in a language that hurt to hear. The fragments rose—the oathstone lifting from its socket, the inkwell spinning, the severed hand uncurling at last, fingers spreading as if reaching for something—and around them the Hollow Star's unlight gathered like a storm learning to pray.

And through it all, impossibly, Hale was laughing.

"Do you see?" he shouted, over the groaning of the mountain, over the screams, over the vast slow attention of the thing beyond the door. "*Do you see what I'm making?*" Blood ran freely from his ruined arm, and where it struck the iron it burned away into sparks of that same false gold, and the forging drank it, and the mountain shuddered, and high above them the peak itself began, visibly, to come apart—cracks racing up the cavern walls, whole slabs of ancient stone shifting, dust falling like gray snow.

Paloma crawled. She didn't decide to crawl; her body decided, dragging her through the chaos toward the one thing that mattered, the one thing her mother's notes had promised and her own failing magic still might reach. Behind her the world was ending in miniature, Hale ascending on a pillar of stolen dawn and hungry dark, the covenant's fragments orbiting him like moons around a dying sun.

Her fingers found the edge of the anvil. She pulled herself up against it, cheek pressed to iron still warm from her blood, and looked at the cutting blade, planted and burning, a hand's breadth from her eyes.

One hand's breadth. One blade. Two hands, her mother had written, in ink now soaked with her own blood in Paloma's satchel. *A covenant of shared burden, cut by two hands.*

"FELIX!" she screamed, into the ruin of the world. "FELIX, THE BLADE!"

The mountain cracked again—and this time the crack did not stop.
