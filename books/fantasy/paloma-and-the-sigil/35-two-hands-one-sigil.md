## 35. Two Hands, One Sigil

The mountain was still screaming.

Felix felt it through the soles of his boots—a deep, grinding groan that came up out of the rock like the voice of something being flayed alive. The forge-peak's great chamber had been a cathedral once, or close enough: vaulted ceilings of black stone veined with silver, the old covenant-sigil carved in a ring across the floor wide enough to hold a hundred worshippers. Now half that ring was shattered where Hale's stolen forging had torn through it, and through the wound poured a light that was not light—the Hollow Star's hunger, cold and colorless, spilling across the stones like water finding every crack.

Hale was gone. What remained of him stood at the center of the broken ring, a tall shape of shadow and starlight wearing the memory of a magistrate's coat, and it was laughing—or the Hollow Star was laughing through what had been Hale's mouth, and Felix could no longer tell the difference and did not want to try.

*Hold the line,* he told himself. *That is all you know how to do.*

Paloma was at the far side of the chamber, at the anvil-forge where the cutting-blade lay, and between them stretched forty feet of ruined floor and everything the Hollow Star could throw into it.

"Felix." Her voice reached him thin and clear over the mountain's groaning. She had the blade in both hands now—*Vesper*, her mother would have called it, though Paloma only ever said *the knife*, as if naming it too grandly might wake something. "The rite. It has to be now."

"I know."

"Both hands, Felix. Both of us. If you hesitate—"

"I won't hesitate."

She almost smiled. In the dead light of the Hollow Star's spill her face looked carved from ash, but her eyes were steady, and they were on him, and that steadiness was the only thing in the world he trusted completely. "You always hesitate," she said. "You hesitated for three days before you asked me to share your fire. You hesitated outside Corvane. You'll hesitate now, and I will love you for it, because hesitation means you understand what we're about to lose."

He wanted to answer her. There was no time. The shadow-that-had-been-Hale turned its head toward them both, and when it spoke, its voice came from everywhere—from the walls, from the floor, from inside Felix's own skull, wearing the accent of his childhood valley like a stolen coat.

*TWO HANDS.* The laughter again, vast and amused. *THE OLD RITE NEEDED ONE ANCHOR AND A THOUSAND SERVANTS. THE NEW ONE NEEDS ONLY TWO. HOW CONVENIENT. HOW* ***EDIBLE.***

"It lies," Felix said aloud, to Paloma, to himself. "It offered me my god's voice. It offered you your family. It doesn't know how to say anything true."

"No," Paloma said. "But it knows how to say things *almost* true. That's worse." She set the blade down on the anvil and began, with hands that did not shake, to unbind her sleeves. Beneath them her forearms were already inked to the elbow—the false forging she'd begun in Corvane, the unbinding hidden inside a binding, lines of sigilwork crawling up her skin like black ivy. "Felix. Come here. We do this together or not at all."

He crossed the floor. Every step, the cold light licked at him, whispering—he heard his god's name in it, heard the silence of twenty years offering itself back to him filled with sound—and he kept walking, because Paloma was waiting, and because she had taught him, without ever meaning to, that some silences are chosen and some are prisons and the difference is whether someone cut the bars around you or you cut them yourself.

At the anvil, she took his hands.

Her fingers were ink-stained and cold and trembling after all—not from fear, he realized, but from effort, from holding back the tide of her own inherited craft long enough to let him arrive. "Your blood first," she said. "Oath-sworn. That's what my mother wrote. Not a sacrifice. An *oath*. Blood given, not taken."

"And yours?"

"Ink. Craft. The covenant has always been paid in blood by people who didn't choose it." Her jaw tightened. "This one gets paid in choice. Both halves. Neither dominant. That's the third path, Felix. That's what she found and they killed her for."

Below them, through the mountain's bones, the Hollow Star surged against the failing channel, and the whole chamber lurched like a ship in a storm. Somewhere behind them, at the pass, the hermit's grave was still warm. Somewhere below, the allies who stayed were dying to buy them these minutes. Felix thought of the frozen knight on the battlefield that never ended, of the wight in the drowned chapel asking only for honesty, of every soul in this unraveling story who had outlived their purpose and kept standing anyway.

"Tell me what to do," he said.

Paloma looked at him for a long moment. Then she drew the blade from the anvil and pressed it into his right hand, closing his fingers around the grip with her own.

"We do it together," she said. "One cut. Your hand holds the steel. My hand guides it. And we speak the covenant as one voice, and neither of us lets go."

* * *

Paloma had imagined this moment a hundred times since leaving Corvane, and in none of those imaginings had it been quiet.

But it was quiet now. The Hollow Star's roaring fell away as if a door had closed; the mountain's groaning dimmed to a heartbeat underfoot; even her own terror receded, not vanishing but stepping back, making room. There was only the anvil beneath the blade, and Felix's hand warm around hers over the hilt, and the sigils on her arms beginning to glow—a soft gold, nothing like the cold light behind them, the color of lamplight in a window seen from a long way off in the dark.

*This is what they took from us,* she thought. *Not the magic. The sharing of it.*

Her mother's notes had been explicit, and merciless in their simplicity: *A covenant cut by one hand belongs to one hand. A covenant cut by two belongs to neither, and therefore to everyone. This is why they feared it. This is why they perverted it into sacrifice. One death is easier to govern than two equal lives.*

She had read those lines four times before she understood that her guild had not failed a generation ago. They had succeeded—at exactly what they intended. A binding that required blood, generation after generation, was not protection. It was a leash. And her family had held the other end until the leash strangled them too.

No more.

"Felix," she said, and her voice came out steady, which surprised her. "Look at me."

He did. His eyes were wet—he would deny it later, she knew, would claim it was forge-smoke—and there was no hesitation in them at all now, only a kind of terrible readiness, the readiness of a man who had spent his whole life following orders and had finally, finally chosen one of his own.

"When we cut," she said, "the covenant will take what it takes. From me, my craft. All of it. I'll be—" She stopped. Swallowed. "I'll be ordinary, Felix. For the rest of my life. No sigils. No sight. Just hands and ink and knowing."

"And from you?" she asked him, though she already knew; the inkwell had shown her his name, and the oathstone had shown her his history, and between them she had assembled the shape of what twenty years of silence had cost him. "What does it take from you?"

"The debt," he said simply. "All of it. Every prayer I owed and never got to say. Every year of service sworn before I was old enough to know what swearing meant." He exhaled slowly. "I'll be free, Paloma. That's the price. Freedom. And I've been so afraid of it for so long that I can't even tell if I want it."

"You do," she said. "I've watched you want it since the ash road. You just didn't have anywhere to put it yet." She turned her hand under his, lacing their fingers through the blade's grip, steel between palm and palm. "Put it here."

Above the anvil, the old sigil-ring flared—every surviving line of it, silver veins lighting across the black stone, and beyond the chamber, beyond the mountain, she felt the valley's fallen wards stir like a sleeper turning over. The god was near. She could feel it the way you feel weather coming: a pressure, a presence, something vast holding very still so as not to crush what it loved.

"Now," she whispered. "Together. On three. One—"

The Hollow Star screamed.

The door she'd felt closed burst open, and the cold flooded in—not around them but *through* them, through the channel Hale's failure had torn, offering, offering, offering. Felix saw his god alive and speaking. Paloma saw her mother's kitchen, her father's laugh, the whole murdered tree of Ibarra restored and warm. It poured everything it had into them in one last flood, every deepest want shaped perfectly to the soul that held it—

And they cut.

One motion. Two hands. The blade went through the air above the anvil in a single arc, and where it passed, Paloma spoke the words her mother had died protecting and Felix spoke the oath he had carried since he was nine years old, and the two voices—one inherited, one sworn—met in the middle the way their hands met around the steel, neither leading, neither following, *equal*.

The new sigil burned itself into the world.

* * *

There was no sound. That was the strangest part. Felix had expected thunder, expected the mountain to split, expected the Hollow Star's rage to shake the peak apart. Instead there was silence—but a different silence entirely, a living one, the silence of a held breath releasing.

Light rose out of the floor in a slow golden wave, following the path of the blade, writing the covenant into stone and air and flesh. Where it touched the cold spill of the Hollow Star, the darkness did not flee—it *closed*, like a wound knitting, like a mouth being gently shut. The channel narrowed. The shadow-that-had-been-Hale shrank with it, folding inward, its stolen laughter thinning to a whine, to a whisper, to nothing, sealed behind a door forged of two hands' consent.

And then—through the closing dark, through the last sliver of the channel, as if leaning close to speak through a keyhole before the lock turned forever—his god said his name.

*"Felix."*

Twenty years of silence ended in a single word, and the word was gentle.

It was not the voice he had imagined all his life—not thunder, not command, none of the terrible music the priests had promised. It was tired. It was warm. It sounded, absurdly, like his grandmother reading aloud on a winter night, like every kind voice he had ever half-remembered and never been able to place. It said his name the way you say the name of someone you have been waiting a very long time to see come home.

*"Thank you. Both of you. It heard me praying all these years—that was the danger. Every word I might have spoken, it strained to hear. So I gave you silence instead, and asked you to keep faith with a god who seemed to have abandoned you, and you did, child. You did. I am sorry. I will spend what remains of eternity being sorry, gladly."*

Felix found he was crying without any decision to do so. Beside him, Paloma's hand was still locked in his over the flat of the blade, and her tears fell on the steel and steamed away in the sigil-light.

*"The binding is cut anew,"* the god said. *"Two anchors. Shared burden. It will hold as long as you both hold it—and it asks nothing more than that you hold it together. No blood. No silence. Only this: do not let go of each other. That is the whole of the law now. I made it small on purpose. Great laws break. Small ones, kept daily, endure."*

The voice was fading now, not withdrawing but settling, sinking into the world the way warmth sinks into a house. *"I must go and mend what is mended, and grieve what is grieved. But hear me, Felix Vasquez, last sworn of my servants: your oath is paid. Paid in full, and overpaid, and I release you—not because you bought freedom, but because freedom was always yours, and I was too bound myself to say so. Go and live. That is the finest prayer you could offer me."*

And then, softer, so soft Felix felt it more than heard it, meant perhaps for both of them:

*"The stars remember how to burn. Watch the sky tonight."*

The light closed over the last of the channel. The Hollow Star's whisper cut off mid-word, mid-*offer*, sealed away behind a covenant written in shared consent, and the great chamber of the forge-peak went still for the first time in a generation.

Felix let go of the blade.

His legs went out from under him. He sat down hard on the scorched stone, and Paloma came down beside him, and for a while neither of them said anything at all. The sigil-ring glowed softly around them, gold and patient, a lamp left burning in a window. Through the shattered crown of the chamber he could see sky—actual sky, scoured clean of cloud by whatever force had swept the peak—and the first stars were coming out.

One. Then another. Then a scatter of them, hesitant, like people lighting lamps after a long illness, unsure whether the dark was truly over.

"They're back," Paloma whispered. Her voice cracked in the middle of it. "Felix. They're *back*."

He looked at his hands. They were just hands—no oath humming in them, no silent leash, no debt. Empty. Free. Terrifyingly, wonderfully empty, the way a room is empty after the furniture of a lifetime is carried out, ready for anything.

He reached over and took Paloma's hand. Ink-stained, magic-less, ordinary now, and worth more to him than any god's favor ever had been.

"Not alone, though," he said. "You heard him. Small law. Kept daily."

Paloma laughed—a wet, broken, real laugh—and leaned her head against his shoulder, and above them the stars came out one by one over the valley and the world, and far to the east, past the mountains, past the borders of every map either of them had ever known, a faint second star flickered uncertainly into being, as if something else, somewhere, had also just remembered how to burn.

Neither of them saw it yet.

They would.
