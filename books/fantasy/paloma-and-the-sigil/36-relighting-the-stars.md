## 36. Relighting the Stars

The forge-peak's crater still smoked when Paloma knelt at the center of the new sigil.

It was not a grand thing to look at—she had expected, all her life, that the covenant would be cut in fire and thunder, that the rite would demand an altar of black iron and a chorus of chanting heirs. Instead it was two circles overlapping, drawn in ash and her own blood and Felix's, their lines imperfect where his hand had shaken and hers had bled too fast. The old covenant had been a wall. This one was a bridge. Her mother would have wept at its simplicity. Her mother had died trying to build something far more complicated, and Paloma thought, with a grief that no longer cut but only ached, that simplicity had been the one lesson the Ibarra line had never learned until it was nearly too late.

Felix stood at the edge of the circle, not in it. That was the design—the first honest design in four generations. The old rites had put everything on one pair of shoulders: one anchor, one sacrifice, one bloodline paying for the silence of a god while everyone else slept soundly beneath warded stars. Her grandmother had paid. Her mother had fled rather than pay. And Paloma had spent her whole exile believing she would die on some cold altar to balance an equation she'd never been asked to solve.

But this was not that equation. Felix's oath-blood anchored one arc; her inherited craft anchored the other; and between them, the god itself held the keystone—not as prisoner this time, but as pillar. *Two hands, one sigil.* The hermit had said it before he died holding the pass, and she had carved it into the mountain's memory with the cutting blade still warm from the forging.

"You don't have to do this part," Felix said quietly. He always said it quietly, as if loudness might make the choice heavier than it already was. "The god told me the channel is closed. The Hollow Star is bound. You could keep your magic."

"And let it sit in me like a splinter?" She smiled, though her mouth felt strange doing it. "Every scrap of Ibarra power I carry is thread from the old covenant, Felix. If I keep it, the new one has a hole in it shaped exactly like me."

He didn't argue. He had learned, over these terrible months, that arguing with a sigilwright about the mathematics of sacrifice was like arguing with a river about which way was downhill. But he came closer anyway, boots crunching on glassed ash, and crouched so they were eye to eye.

"Then I'll be right here," he said. "Whatever it feels like. However long it takes. I'll be right here."

"That's the whole point of you," she said. "Being right here."

She turned to the work before her hands could fail her.

* * *

Surrendering magic, it turned out, was nothing like the guild had described. The elders of her order had spoken of it as drowning—a black tide, a severing, a death of the self. They had used those words, she understood now, deliberately, to make the alternative seem gentle. To make a knife seem like mercy.

What it actually felt like was this:

She opened her hands over the overlapping circles and spoke the unbinding word her mother had whispered to her as a bedtime story disguised as a lullaby—*the last gift goes home*—and the magic rose out of her the way heat rises off stone at dusk. Not torn. Not ripped. *Released.* It poured up through her arms in slow ribbons of pale light, and each ribbon carried something with it.

The first ribbon took her mother's voice. Suddenly she could not remember the exact timbre of it, the way it frayed on certain words, and she gasped because she hadn't known the memory lived in her magic until it was leaving. The second took the weight of every sigil she had ever cut—her fingers forgot the pressure of the stylus, the precise angle of a closing stroke, and twenty years of craft drained out of her hands like water through open fingers. The third took the sight: the deep-seeing that had let her read the world's hidden script since childhood, and the world went flat and ordinary and mute around her, a book with all its letters fallen off the page.

She cried out then. She couldn't help it. It was amputation—it was exactly amputation, a limb leaving the body, and the phantom ache bloomed instantly where the power had been, itching in fingers that could no longer hold a sigil, burning in eyes that could no longer see what was true.

"I'm here." Felix's hand closed around hers, callused and warm and utterly without magic, utterly human. "I'm here. Keep going."

"The stars," she managed. "Watch the stars. Tell me—"

And he looked up, because he was Felix Vasquez, who had kept watch for twenty years under a god's silence, and watching was the holiest thing he knew.

* * *

The first star returned over the western ridge.

Paloma saw nothing—but she heard everything. The new covenant drank her surrendered magic the way dry earth drinks rain, and she felt it move, felt herself become current instead of vessel, pouring down through the mountain's roots, out along the ley-lines the old covenant had poisoned with secrecy, out into a world that had gone dark and strange and starving. She felt the moment her power touched the valley's broken wards and did not rebuild them as walls but wove them as lace—openwork, permeable, a border that remembered both sides.

She felt the moment it reached the sky.

"Paloma." Felix's voice cracked on her name. "Paloma, look—no. You can't see. So listen. Listen to them. There's one—there's one, right over the ridge, it's small and it's blue-white and it's *burning*, it's actually burning—"

She laughed, or sobbed, or both. "One?"

"There's a second." His grip tightened. "East now. Over the valley. Two. Three—Paloma, three more, they're coming in a line, like someone walking through a dark house lighting lamps—"

*That's exactly what it is,* she thought, from somewhere vast and diffuse and no longer entirely a body. *Someone walking through a dark house, lighting lamps.* Except the someone was her. All the hoarded power of four generations of Ibarras, the inheritance that had been a chain, spent at last as it should have been spent—not hoarded, not defended, not traded for silence. Given away. Scattered across the sky like seed.

The fourth star. The ninth. Somewhere below, in the refugee camps at the mountain's foot, people were shouting. She could feel them faintly now, the way she had once felt ley-lines—thousands of small hearts stumbling out of tents and wagons, faces tipped upward, and the collective sound of a world remembering hope was a physical thing, a pressure, a warmth against the inside of her skin.

A twentieth star. A fiftieth. The Hollow Country's dead fields caught the returning light and something in the soil sighed.

"It doesn't hurt," she heard herself say, wonderingly. "Felix, it doesn't hurt anymore. It feels like—" She searched for the word and found it waiting, obvious, inevitable. "Forgiveness. It feels like being forgiven."

"For what?" he asked softly. "You didn't do anything wrong."

"No," she agreed. "But my name did. For four hundred years my name did." The last ribbons of magic left her—her guild-training, her exile-hardened cunning, the raw talent that had made the elders call her the finest sigilwright born in a century—and went up, and up, and became the hundredth star, the two-hundredth, the slow tide of light rolling east toward Corvane where a city full of coerced believers was looking up at a sky that no longer needed their false dawn. "And now it doesn't have to carry it alone anymore. Now it's everyone's."

The last of it went out of her like a breath released after a very long dive.

* * *

She came back to herself lying in the ash with Felix's coat draped over her and the night sky above her so thick with stars that it looked like the stories children were told about the time before.

Her body felt hollowed and light, like a house after the furniture is sold—every room echoing, every floorboard familiar and bare. When she lifted her hand, no light answered it. When she looked at the overlapping circles of the new covenant, she saw only scratched lines in stone, no deeper script, no hidden grammar. The world was just the world now. Flat, ordinary, mute.

And it was enough. That was the miracle she hadn't predicted—that it was *enough*. She had built her entire identity on the power, had fled her guild rather than surrender it to their rite, had crossed a dying country to save it, and now that it was gone she discovered the self underneath it had been there all along, patient as a foundation. Paloma remained. Not the sigilwright. Not the last heir. Just Paloma, who was stubborn and tired and loved a mercenary with a silent god, and who knew more about the architecture of covenants than anyone alive, even if she could never again cut one.

Felix was sitting beside her, watching her face with an expression she'd learned to read somewhere on the ash road: fear and tenderness fighting for the same ground.

"Well?" he asked.

She considered the question honestly. The phantom limbs ached. Her mother's voice was gone from her memory, though the words her mother had said remained, and perhaps that was the fairer bargain—to keep the meaning and lose the music. Her hands would never hold a stylus with authority again.

"I feel like an amputee," she said at last, "who's just been told she never needed that arm to embrace anyone."

Felix exhaled a laugh that was half relief, half grief on her behalf. "That's the strangest sentence I've ever heard."

"I'm a sigilwright. We specialize." She sat up slowly, and the stars wheeled overhead, hundreds of them, thousands, the whole ancient ceiling of the world restored. Far to the south, over the valley, she could pick out the constellations the elders had named—the Watcher, the Scales, the Shepherd—and thought of the high priest burning records in the dark, and felt, unexpectedly, no hatred. Only the tired certainty that reckoning was coming for him too, and that it would be fair, because fairness was the whole point of everything they had built here tonight.

Below them, the campfires of the refugees had doubled, tripled—people refusing to sleep, people singing badly, people pointing up and naming stars their grandparents had named. Somewhere among those fires was the frozen knight's regiment, keeping a vigil for a commander finally at rest. Somewhere was the caravan friend who had betrayed them and then, at the end, come back, carrying water to the wounded. Somewhere was a world that would wake tomorrow needing to learn how to live under open borders and lit skies, and would get it wrong, and try again.

"Felix," she said. "What happens to your god now? Truly. No silence, no debt. What does a god *do*?"

He was quiet for a long moment. Above them a star flickered—just once, just slightly, in a way that stars did not flicker—and Felix went very still, the way he always went still when the divine brushed past him.

"I think," he said slowly, "it's learning how to answer."

The star steadied. But far to the east, low on the horizon where the sky met the ruined lands beyond the Hollow Country, another light had appeared—one that did not belong to any constellation she knew, faint and uncertain, almost shy, as if something were testing whether it was allowed to shine.

Paloma squinted at it, hollow-handed and mortal and strangely unafraid.

"Felix," she said. "What is that?"
