## 37. The God Speaks

The mountain was still breathing.

Felix felt it under his boots as he descended the forge-peak's eastern stair—a slow, tectonic exhalation, the stone itself easing after days of strain. Cracks webbed the switchbacks where Hale's forging had nearly split the world open, and in places whole steps had slumped into rubble, but the stair held. The mountain held. Everything held, now that the sigil was cut and the channel closed and the Hollow Star sealed behind a covenant written by two hands instead of one.

He stopped on a landing halfway down, where the rock jutted out over the cloud sea, and he did what he had done every morning since the cutting: he listened.

For twenty years, listening had been an act of faith without reward. He had knelt on cold flagstones in the valley's shrine-house and pressed his forehead to stone worn smooth by generations of soldiers' foreheads, and he had heard nothing. Not silence exactly—silence implies absence—but a held breath. A door shut from the other side. He had built his whole life inside that silence the way a man builds a house against a cliff, trusting the cliff to stand because it had always stood.

Now the door was open, and the voice that came through it was not what he expected.

It was not thunder. It was not the choir of a hundred burning stars, though Felix had imagined all of that during the long years of his boyhood prayers. It was closer to the sound of a fire settling in a hearth at night, or rain finding a roof it knew. It did not speak in words so much as in *arrivals*—a warmth arriving behind his sternum, a certainty arriving in his hands, a sense of being *known* arriving so completely that it bordered on terror and then, gently, passed beyond it into something else entirely.

*I am here,* the presence said, which was not language but which Felix understood as clearly as his own heartbeat. *I have always been here. You were never praying into nothing.*

"I know," Felix said aloud. His voice came out rough. The wind off the cloud sea took it and scattered it. "I know that now."

Below him, the camp they had made in the mountain's shadow was waking. Cookfires smoked. The refugees from the Hollow Country moved among the wounded with the careful, exhausted tenderness of people who had survived something they could not yet name. Marta—the caravan woman who had once sold them to the cult and then, in Corvane's burning streets, sold herself back to them with a knife in her teeth—was arguing cheerfully with one of the frozen knight's old regiment about the proper way to splint a broken arm. Somewhere a child was laughing, actually laughing, three days after the sky had almost ended.

And above it all, faint but unmistakable, the stars were still there. Pale in the daylight, invisible to anyone who didn't know to look, but there. Felix could feel them the way he could feel the sigil scar on his palm—two thin lines crossing, cut by his own blood and Paloma's, healed now into something like a brand and something like a gift.

The presence stirred again, and this time it came with weight, with *attention*, the way a large animal turns its head.

*You have questions.*

Felix laughed—a short, disbelieving sound. "I've had questions for twenty years. I had questions when the elders burned the records. I had questions when you went quiet and every priest in the valley told me silence was devotion." He looked down at his hands, at the sigil scar. "I had questions when I learned my god consented to its own imprisonment. When I learned the silence wasn't punishment. It was *protection*. You kept your mouth shut for two centuries so a starving thing couldn't hear where you were hiding."

*Yes.*

"You let us think you'd abandoned us."

*Yes.* The arrival of that word carried sorrow in it, old and worn smooth, a grief the god had carried alone through every generation of Felix's line of soldiers. *Silence was the only honesty left to me. Anything I said, it would have heard. Every prayer answered would have been a lantern lit in a dark room where something hunted by light. So I let you believe the worst rather than tell you anything at all.* A pause, vast and patient. *I am sorry. I know what my silence cost you.*

Felix thought of the boy he had been. Nine years old, orphaned in the border skirmishes, sworn into the god's service with ash on his forehead and a spear too big for his hands. He had prayed every night for a voice, a sign, a single word—and gotten nothing, and been told that nothing was holy, and believed it, because the alternative was unbearable. He had grown into a man who mistook endurance for faith and numbness for peace. He had killed easily because the god's silence had taught him that the world ran on duty alone, unwatched, unrewarded, and a man who believes no one is watching learns very quickly what he is capable of.

"It cost me everything," Felix said quietly. "And then it gave me everything back. I don't—" He stopped. Started again. "I don't know how to hold both of those at once."

*You do,* said the god. *You've been holding both since the ravine.*

* * *

They came down from the peak the next morning, and the god came with them.

Not visibly—though Felix suspected that if he asked, it might show itself, and he found he didn't want to ask, not yet. Some part of him understood that seeing his god would make it smaller, the way naming a fear sometimes shrinks it past recognition. For now the presence rode along at the edge of his awareness like a second pulse, and everywhere it passed, small things mended. The frost-blighted crops along the mountain road straightened overnight. A feverish child slept soundly and woke clear-eyed. In the ruined chapel where they sheltered the third night, the shattered altar-stone knit itself back together while the refugees slept, and in the morning no one could agree on whether it had ever been broken.

Paloma noticed. Of course she noticed. She had given up her magic to relight the stars—poured every drop of her inherited power into the new covenant until the well of her was empty—and now she walked through a world quietly healing around her, watching miracles she could no longer touch, and she bore it the way she bore everything: spine straight, chin level, eyes that gave away nothing except to someone who had learned to read them.

Felix had learned to read them.

"Your god is showing off," she said that night, sitting beside him at the fire with her hands wrapped around a tin cup of tea she couldn't taste anymore. The loss of her magic had taken more than power; it had dulled her senses to the unseen, and she mourned that quieter loss the way an amputee mourns an itch in a missing limb.

"He's making up for lost time," Felix said. "Two hundred years of things he wanted to fix and couldn't risk touching."

"Mm." She blew on her tea. "Does it bother you? Being... attended to?"

Felix considered the question honestly, because she deserved honesty and because he was still learning how to give it. "It should. Twenty years ago I'd have crawled across coals for one hour of this. Now..." He turned his cup slowly. "Now it feels like being watched by someone who loves me, and I keep waiting for the bill."

"That's not how grace works," Paloma said.

"How would you know?"

"Because I spent my whole life believing love was a debt instrument." Her mouth curved, dry and sad. "Every gift in my family came with a ledger attached. Power passed down, blood owed forward. I reforged the covenant specifically so no one would ever have to pay again." She looked up at him, firelight catching the gray in her eyes. "So when I tell you your god isn't keeping accounts, Felix Vasquez, believe me. I know what accounting looks like. This isn't it."

He wanted to answer that. He opened his mouth to answer that—and the god arrived.

Not gradually. All at once, the way dawn arrives, the way a verdict arrives. The fire did not change; the night did not change; but the space between Felix and the flame filled with presence until it seemed the whole darkness had leaned in to listen. Paloma stiffened beside him. She couldn't feel it—not directly—but she could feel *him*, the way his breathing changed, the way his hand tightened on his cup.

*Felix Vasquez,* said the god, and his name in that voice was not a summons. It was a benediction. *Sworn at nine years. Faithful through silence. Faithful through worse than silence—you were faithful even when you believed I had failed you, and that faith shames me with its generosity.*

"Don't," Felix said hoarsely. "Don't do penance at me. You kept a starving god locked away from the world for two hundred years. You paid enough."

*I am not doing penance,* the presence said, and there was something in it that might have been amusement, ancient and gentle. *I am doing release. Hear me, soldier. Your oath ended at the forge-peak. It ended when the old covenant died and the new one was cut by hands that were never mine to command. Whatever bound you to me—your childhood vow, your blood, your twenty years of service in the dark—all of it burned with the old sigil. You are free. You have been free since the moment the stars came back, and I have waited these three days to say it plainly because I feared you would not believe me.*

The cup slipped in Felix's grip. He caught it, barely, tea slopping over his knuckles, and he barely felt that either.

"Free," he repeated. The word felt foreign in his mouth, a coin from a country he'd never visited.

*Free. No debt. No service. No title, no duty, no claim upon you whatsoever. I release you as I should have released you decades ago, and I ask nothing in return—because asking is what the old order did, and the old order is ash.* The presence softened, gathered close, warm as a hand on the shoulder. *But I may offer. Once. Freely, with no obligation, no fine print, no ledger. Ask anything of me, Felix Vasquez, son of the border wars, last faithful man of a faithless age, and if it lies within my power, it is yours.*

The fire crackled. Paloma's breath had gone very quiet beside him. Somewhere across the camp, the child laughed again in its sleep, or dreamed it did.

Felix sat with the offer the way a man sits with a key to a door he has stared at all his life.

His mind went first, treacherously, to the obvious things. His dead comrades, restored. His parents, whom he barely remembered, returned to him whole. Strength, wealth, the god's voice in his ear forever, counsel on demand, power such as Magistrate Hale had died grasping for. All of it lay within reach. All of it was *offered*, freely, and the most frightening thing—the thing that made his chest ache—was that he believed the offer. There was no trap in it. He had spent twenty years reading silences and thirty years reading men, and there was no trap here. Only patience, and sorrow, and something so old and so tired and so fond of one stubborn mortal soldier that it had crossed death and silence and the hunger of the Hollow Star just to set this one choice before him.

And that was how he knew what to ask. Because the god loved him, and everything the god had ever done wrong had come from loving guarded things too tightly.

"The wards," Felix said.

Beside him, Paloma went still.

"The valley's wards," he said again, louder, speaking to the fire, to the night, to the presence folded through all of it. "When we crossed the border going out, the magic nearly killed me. And I grew up inside those wards—I *was* the ward, patrolling them, bleeding on them—and I never once asked why they hurt so much to touch. Why they were built to keep people in as much as keep things out. Why the elders needed walls so thick they had to burn their own records to hide what the walls were for." His voice steadied. "You want to give me something? Rebuild them. Gentler. Make them a door instead of a wall. Let people walk out of the valley and see the stars you gave back, and let strangers walk in without their blood boiling. No more children raised thinking the world ends at the tree line. That's what I want. That's all I want."

The silence that followed was long—but it was a different silence than the old one. It was the silence of someone turning a request over with care, the way Paloma turned a page of her mother's notes.

*You could have asked for anything,* the god said at last, and the wonder in it was unmistakable. *You could have asked for me, forever. Instead you ask me to open a gate.*

"I spent my whole life on the wrong side of a wall I was guarding from the inside," Felix said. "I know what it does to people. Walls don't protect faith. They just hide it from the weather until it rots." He glanced sideways at Paloma, whose eyes were bright and wet and fixed on him with an expression he would remember for the rest of his life. "Everything wrong in that valley started because somebody decided safety meant secrecy. Break the secrecy. Keep the safety. If you can."

*I can,* the god said simply. *And I will. Not as payment—as promise. The valley will have wards woven light and porous as linen, strong against the hungry dark and blind to honest travelers. Any soul may leave. Any soul may enter. The border will be a threshold, not a cage.* A pause, and then, softer, with something that in any lesser voice would have been called tenderness: *You are the finest thing my silence ever made, Felix Vasquez. Do not tell the priests I said so. They will only argue about doctrine.*

Felix laughed—really laughed, helpless and cracked and young, the laugh of a nine-year-old boy who had finally, after twenty years, heard his god's voice and discovered it sounded like this. Like a hearth. Like rain on a known roof. Like forgiveness that had never once required asking.

Paloma reached over and took his hand, the one marked with the sigil scar, and held it, and neither of them said anything, because there was nothing that needed saying that the fire and the stars and the presence folding gently around them both hadn't already said.

*Rest now,* the god murmured, receding like a tide, leaving warmth behind it. *Tomorrow you walk home. Both of you. And home, I suspect, will have hard questions waiting—valleys always do, when their walls come down. But that is tomorrow's work. Tonight, soldier: rest. You have earned it a hundred times over, and I am, at last, in a position to say so.*

The presence withdrew. The fire burned. Above the black shoulder of the mountain, the stars wheeled on, bright and numerous and real, and Felix Vasquez—unsworn, unbound, unemployed for the first time since he was nine years old—sat under them holding a woman's hand and a cooling cup of tea, and felt lighter than he had ever felt in his life, and slightly terrified of it.

"Unemployed," Paloma said at last, her voice thick. "What on earth will you do with yourself?"

"No idea." He smiled at the fire. "First time in twenty years that's been allowed to be true."

She laughed, and leaned her head against his shoulder, and for a while they simply watched the flames.

Then, down in the dark below the mountain, along the road they would take home, a light appeared. Small, bobbing, distant—a lantern, or several, moving fast. And beneath the wind, faint but growing, came the sound of many voices raised in something that was not yet song and not yet shouting, traveling toward the forge-peak through the starlit night.

Paloma lifted her head. "Felix."

"I see it."

"They're coming *up* the mountain road," she said. "At midnight. Nobody climbs at midnight unless—"

Unless they were coming to find them. Unless word had already traveled ahead of them, down through the recovering valleys and the dimming plains, faster than any rider should have managed. Unless the world outside had heard that the stars were lit again, and that two people on a mountain had done it, and had begun, all at once and from every direction, to move.

Felix rose, drawing his coat tight, and watched the lanterns multiply along the road below like sparks catching in dry grass.

"Well," he said. "So much for resting."
