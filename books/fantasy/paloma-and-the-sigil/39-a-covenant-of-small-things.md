## 39. A Covenant of Small Things

The schoolhouse had been a granary once, and before that—according to the oldest woman in the village—a chapel to a god whose name no one could remember. Paloma liked that lineage. A building that had held grain and prayer could hold chalk dust and questions just as well.

She swept it every morning herself. There was a stubbornness in the act that she needed: the Ibarra heir, last of the covenant line, bent over a broom in a valley that had spent three generations pretending her family's name was a curse rather than a debt. The broom whispered over the floorboards where the ward-lines of the old sigilwork still showed faintly beneath the whitewash, like veins under skin. She had not covered them. Let the students see what craft looked like when it aged.

The morning light came through windows that Felix had re-glazed himself, badly, with a craftsman's confidence and none of a glazier's skill. Two panes were already clouded with condensation that would never fully clear. Paloma had stopped teasing him about it only because he'd started repairing things around her schoolhouse unasked—the door hinge, the stove flue, the wobbly third bench—and she suspected the teasing was the only payment he understood how to accept.

Outside, the valley was green in the way it had not been for a year. The stars had come back eight months ago, one by one, like lamps relit in a house everyone had assumed empty, and the land had remembered itself slowly after: first the grass, then the bees, then the strange pale moths that had vanished from the border stones and now returned to eat holes in her students' homework if they left it out overnight. The elders still met in their long hall and spoke carefully, as men do who have been caught lying by history itself. The high priest had resigned. The records he'd burned were gone forever, but Paloma was writing new ones—not replacements, but corrections—and the elders, to their credit or their cowardice, did not stop her.

Today was the day. The first day.

She had posted the notice three weeks earlier on the market board, in plain ink, no sigilwork at all:

*Sigilcraft taught openly. No bloodline required. No fee. Bring nothing but your hands and your reasons.*

Felix had read it over her shoulder and said, "You'll get farmers' sons who want to fix their fences and girls who want to blow something up."

"Both are legitimate uses of applied geometry," she'd said, and he'd laughed the way he laughed now—easily, without checking whether the world permitted it—and she had felt the old grief shift its weight inside her, the way a healed bone aches differently than a broken one.

Now she stood in the swept granary with forty minutes yet before anyone might come, and she was terrified.

Not of teaching. She had taught before, in exile, in the guild halls of Corvane before the guild turned on her, in whispers to apprentices who were never allowed to know why they were learning in code. Teaching was the easy part; teaching was ink and patience. What terrified her was the openness. Every secret she had ever kept had been load-bearing. Her family's name, the covenant's terms, the rite her mother died refusing to complete, the truth about the god's silence—all of it had been walls, and walls had been the whole architecture of her life. Now she was proposing to teach children the craft that had built those walls, in a room with uncovered windows, and trust them with it.

*Powerless but potent.* She flexed her right hand, the one that had cut the new covenant, and felt nothing where the magic used to pool. The loss had not dulled in eight months. It had simply changed shape—from an amputation into a phantom limb, from screaming into weather. Some mornings she reached for a working out of pure habit and found the emptiness there, clean and total, like a well that had been filled with stone.

And some mornings—more of them now—she found she didn't mind. Because the emptiness meant the stars were lit. Because the emptiness was hers, chosen, paid for with her own hand instead of her mother's blood. Grief and relief had married somewhere along the road from the forge-peak, and their child was this strange calm she carried now, this ability to stand in an empty room and feel afraid and glad in the same breath.

The door creaked.

Paloma turned, expecting Felix—he came early everywhere, a habit of twenty years of patrols—but it was a girl. Perhaps fourteen, thin as a fence rail, with flour on her sleeves and the particular rigid posture of someone who has argued with her parents all the way here and won.

"You're the Ibarra woman," the girl said. Not a question. An accusation wearing a question's clothes.

"I'm Paloma."

"My grandmother says your family bound the god that kept us safe." The girl's chin came up. "My grandfather says your family *saved* us, and my father says we don't talk about it at table. So which is it?"

Paloma set down the broom. This, then. This was the first lesson, arriving before any student she'd planned for, exactly the way real lessons always did.

"Sit down," she said, "and I'll tell you the true version, which is longer and worse and better than all three."

The girl sat. Her name, it emerged over the next hour, was Sefa, and she had walked four miles from the mill villages because she had seen the night the stars went dark and the night they came back, and she wanted—this was the sentence she finally produced, red-faced, staring at the floor—to *know things*. Not to be told things. To know them, the way you know fire is hot, in the hands.

By midmorning there were nine of them. A farmer's son who wanted to mend fences, as predicted, and who blushed when Paloma told him that ward-craft began exactly there, in the mathematics of a good fence. Two brothers from the border farms whose father had died holding the tree line against Hale's host, and who watched her with the flat attention of boys deciding whether the world deserved their trust again. A widow in her fifties who announced she was too old for nonsense and then took more notes than anyone. And near the back, having arrived late and saying nothing, a boy of perhaps sixteen with burn-scarred forearms and eyes that tracked every mark Paloma chalked on the slate with an intensity that made her think of another burned boy she had once known, in another life, in a guild that ate its children.

She taught them the first thing her mother had ever taught her. Not a sigil. Not a working. A way of looking.

"Everything that holds," she said, drawing a single line on the slate, "holds because something consents to be held. A knot. A wall. A promise. Craft is the art of asking properly. Your grandmother's generation thought power was the art of compelling. They were wrong, and the wrongness nearly ended the world, and I am the last person alive who can tell you so from experience."

Nine faces. Flour dust and grief and hunger. One of the border boys raised his hand—an actual raised hand, in a ruined granary, as though the forms of civilization were worth rebuilding—and asked, "Can you teach us to do what you did? On the mountain?"

"No," Paloma said, and let the silence sit until they understood it wasn't refusal but honesty. "What I did required thirty generations of inherited craft and a god's permission and a great deal of luck. What I can teach you is smaller. How to bind a door against wind. How to write a ward that keeps honest things honest. How to read the old sigils so that no one can ever again lie to you about what they mean." She chalked a second line, crossing the first. "A covenant of small things. That's what I'm offering. It turns out small things are the ones that hold."

* * *

Felix came at noon, as he always did, carrying bread and a report disguised as conversation.

"The mill villages sent three more," he said, setting the loaf on her desk—which was a plank across two barrels, because dignity was for people with furniture. "Word's spreading. Old Marta says by spring you'll need the tithing barn."

"The tithing barn leaks."

"So does everything in this valley. It's why the wards kept falling." He leaned against the doorframe, arms crossed, doing his patrol-stance, and she watched him do the other thing he always did at noon: look at her for three seconds too long, then away, then pretend he hadn't.

Eight months. Eight months since the forge-peak, since the cutting blade sang through both their hands at once, since the god's voice returned—gentle, brief, like a parent waking a child gently from a nightmare—and released Felix Vasquez from an oath he'd sworn at seven years old with blood he hadn't consented to give. Eight months of him rebuilding her schoolhouse pane by pane, hinge by hinge, walking the border by day, sweeping her granary floor when he thought she wouldn't notice, and neither of them saying the obvious thing.

It wasn't shyness, exactly. Or it was, but shyness built on stranger foundations. He had been a man owned by a silence for twenty years; she had been a woman owned by a name for thirty. They had both been freed within the same hour, on a mountaintop, holding the same blade, and freedom, it turned out, was a country neither of them had maps for. He knew how to be loyal. She knew how to be dutiful. Neither of them knew how to simply *choose*, in daylight, without a covenant forcing the choosing.

But today, watching him pretend to inspect the window glazing he himself had botched, Paloma decided she was done waiting for a rite. She had spent her whole life performing ceremonies correctly. She could damn well do without one.

"Felix."

"Mm." He examined a clouded pane with tremendous professional interest.

"What am I to you?"

The question landed the way her questions always landed on him—like a ward-line snapping shut. He went very still. Then, because he was Felix, he tried to answer it like a soldier reporting terrain: "You're—" He stopped. Started again. "You're the person I—" Stop.

Paloma waited. She had learned to wait from mountains.

"The ledger doesn't balance," he said finally, to the window. "That's the problem. Everything I know how to feel comes in the shape of debts. You saved my life on the ash road. I owe you. You pulled me out of the ravine. I owe you. You cut the covenant with me, which means half of whatever I am now is—" His jaw worked. "I keep trying to pay it back and you keep not letting me, and I don't know what's left when there's no debt. I've never been anything without one."

"There's a word for that," Paloma said. "It's not a mercenary word."

"Don't say it yet." He turned around. His face was doing something complicated—the patrol-face cracking along lines she'd mapped months ago. "Because if you say it while I'm still trying to figure out the arithmetic, I'll answer like a man settling accounts, and you deserve better than a settled account. You deserve—" He exhaled. "Gods. Twenty years of silence and now I can't find one sentence."

She crossed the room. Took the bread out of his hands, because he was strangling it, and set it on the desk.

"Then don't find the sentence," she said. "Find the true thing under it. You're better at true things than sentences. You proved that at the fireline, when you confessed the vision and hated every word of it and said them anyway."

"That was different. That was confession. Confession has rules."

"This has rules too. Rule one: no debts. Rule two: no oaths. Rule three—" she hesitated, and found her own voice going less steady than she'd planned, which was somehow a relief, "—rule three is that we say the frightening part first, because we learned on that mountain what happens when frightened people save the frightening part for later."

He looked at her. Really looked, the way he had looked at her across the drowned chapel when the oathstone showed him her ancestors killing his god's priests, and he had carried that wound alone for weeks rather than let it make him cruel. The bravest thing he'd ever done, she'd thought then, was not the fighting. It was the staying.

"The frightening part," Felix said slowly, "is that I don't want the valley anymore."

She blinked. Of all the confessions she'd braced for—

"When the god offered me anything. Anything at all. Wards rebuilt, voice restored, home restored—I asked for open borders, and I meant it, and I thought that was the whole of what I wanted. But it wasn't. It was—" His scarred hands opened, closed. "I stood at the border last week, on watch, and I realized I was facing outward and hoping. Hoping for what, I couldn't tell you. Something past the wards. And then I caught myself counting the hours until noon, until I could walk up here, and I understood that the valley stopped being home somewhere on the road to Corvane. Home is—" He gestured helplessly at the granary, the chalk dust, the plank-desk, her. "Home is a room where someone teaches children to ask properly."

Paloma discovered her eyes were burning. She was thirty-four years old and had buried a mother, a guild, a birthright, and a world, and this—this clumsy soldier failing to find a sentence in a leaking granary—was what undid her.

"The frightening part for me," she said, when she could, "is that I'm glad I lost it. All of it. The power, the name, the seat the elders offered me. I'm *glad*, and some mornings I hate myself for being glad, because my mother died rather than pass that power down and here I am relieved to be rid of it. But it's true. I am more myself with empty hands than I ever was full ones." She looked up at him. "Empty hands can hold things, though. That's the part nobody tells you. Full hands can't."

Felix was quiet for a long moment. Then he laughed—one short, disbelieving breath—and said, "We're terrible at this."

"We're the worst two people in the valley at this."

"We reforged a cosmic covenant and we can't—"

"—say four words—"

"*Five words,*" Felix said, "and I've been carrying them since the ravine, actually, since before the ravine, since the frozen battlefield probably, and I keep trying to file them under debt or duty or gratitude and they won't fit in any drawer I own." He stepped closer. Close enough that she could see the gray coming into his temples that hadn't been there a year ago, earned honestly. "So here they are, out of the drawer, no ledger, no oath, no covenant. Just a man asking. Stay. Be—whatever the word is. With me. Not because either of us owes it. Because we choose it. Daily. Like sweeping a floor."

Paloma smiled until her face hurt.

"The word," she said, "is love, you enormous idiot, and yes."

He kissed her the way he did everything—thoroughly, cautiously at first, then with the full commitment of a man who had once held a mountain pass alone against a cult's vanguard. Somewhere outside, one of her students whooped, and another shushed him, and neither of them cared.

When they broke apart, Felix rested his forehead against hers and said, "Rule four?"

"Rule four," Paloma agreed, "is that we tell each other the frightening parts *before* they become secrets. Starting now. So." She steadied herself. "The elders offered me the seat again yesterday. Hereditary seat, Ibarra restoration, full honors. And I said no again, but Felix—they're going to keep asking, and some of them aren't asking kindly. Old Vess thinks the valley needs an anchor. A *person* to hang the new order on. And I've seen what happens to people who get hung on things."

His arms tightened slightly. "They can ask all they want. You're busy."

"I'm serious. There's a faction—small, but Vess is patient. They think the covenant should have an Ibarra at its head even a powerless one. Symbolic authority. And symbols, Felix, are just sigils made of people. I've cut enough of them to know."

"Then we teach the students fast," Felix said, "and we make a valley full of people who know what symbols cost." He pulled back and looked at her with the expression she'd learned to trust more than any vow: the flat, level look of a man assessing ground. "Besides. You're not the only one getting offers. The border garrisons want me to train wardens. Proper ones. And yesterday a rider came from beyond the pass—east, past the hermit's country—asking for 'the man who walks between realms.' Said there are settlements out there that saw the stars come back and want to know what it means. Want to learn."

Paloma went still. "From the east."

"From the east." He nodded slowly. "Where the second star flickers. You've seen it."

Everyone had seen it, on clear nights—a faint uncertain point in the eastern sky, flickering like a candle in a draft, a star that hadn't been there before the relighting. The astronomers called it an anomaly. The priests called it a blessing. Paloma, who had cut the covenant herself, who knew every star that belonged to the sky she had bought back, called it something else.

She had never told anyone. Not even Felix.

Especially not Felix.

"It means," she said carefully, "that the sky is bigger than our covenant. We sealed the Hollow Star. We didn't seal the heavens. Things move out there. Things we never had names for, because our ancestors were busy with one war and forgot the sky has others."

Felix studied her face with a patrolman's patience, and she knew—he knew—that she was holding something back, and she knew that he knew, and she watched him decide, deliberately, visibly, not to press. *Rule four,* his expression said. *Working on it.*

"Well," he said instead, turning toward the door, toward the afternoon light and the sound of students returning up the lane with their slates and their grievances and their hunger to know things in their hands. "Then I suppose somebody ought to go east eventually and find out. But not today." He glanced back at her, and the whole cracked-open tenderness of his face returned like sun through bad glazing. "Today there's bread to eat and a floor to sweep and nine children who think crossing two lines makes a ward. Let the east wait. We're busy building the small things."

Paloma picked up the broom.

Outside, the first afternoon students were cresting the hill—the mill-village girl in front, running, flour still on her sleeves—and above the valley the stars' daytime absence was as trustworthy as stone, and far to the east, invisible behind the blue, something flickered.

She swept. It held.
