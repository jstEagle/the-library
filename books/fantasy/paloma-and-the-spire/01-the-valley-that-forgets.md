## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The morning Emrys turned nineteen, the first thing they heard was a goat complaining about the weather.

Not bleating—complaining. There was a difference, and the difference was the whole of Emrys's life. Old Bramble's voice came up through the floorboards of the loft, gravelly and aggrieved, in the way she spoke when her joints had stiffened overnight and the fog had crept into the pasture like something with intentions. *Cold again. Cold again. Two legs sleep warm while I stand in wet grass. If I had hands I would open every gate in this valley.*

Emrys lay still beneath the wool blanket and listened to the house wake around them. Their mother moving in the kitchen below, setting the kettle on the iron stove. The fire ticking as it caught. Outside, the rooster—whose name was officially Rooster but who privately called himself the Warden of All He Surveyed—announced the dawn to an audience of hens who could not have cared less.

*Another day,* said the hens, collectively, in the flat tone of creatures who had seen ten thousand dawns and found them adequate at best. *Grain. Grain would improve it.*

Emrys smiled at the underside of the roof beams and did not answer any of them.

That was the rule. The only rule that mattered, though their mother had never once spoken it aloud. You could listen—listening was safe, listening left no mark—but you must never, ever answer. Not in words, not in whistles, not in the small involuntary sounds of delight that wanted to escape whenever a fox told a joke or a horse described its dreams. Because Hollowmere was a valley that forgot, and forgetting required silence, and silence required everyone in it to be ordinary all the way down to the marrow.

Emrys Ashgrove was not ordinary. They had known it since before they could name it, back when they were small enough to believe that everyone heard the sparrows arguing about the quality of the eaves, or the old dog at the mill describing, in exhaustive detail, the exact smell of every person who passed his gate. It had taken years—and one terrible afternoon when they were seven, and had answered a dying wren in front of the tanner's son—to understand that what lived in their throat was not a gift here. It was a death sentence wearing a neighbor's face.

So they kept it. They folded it small and kept it behind their teeth like a stone, and the valley went on forgetting, and Emrys went on hearing everything.

*If I had hands,* Bramble said again, mournfully, from the pasture. *I would open every gate.*

"Don't tempt me," Emrys murmured into the pillow, so quietly that even the mice in the wall—mice who missed nothing—would strain to catch it. "Some of us have chores."

The mice found this very funny. Mice always did.

* * *

Hollowmere in late autumn was a bowl of gold and smoke. The valley sat cupped between two ridges of the Greyback hills, terraced with barley fields gone amber, orchards heavy with apples that would be pressed into cider by week's end, and a single road that wound down from the northern pass and then, as if thinking better of it, wound right back out again. The village proper clustered around a millpond where the water moved so slowly it seemed to be considering its options. There was a smithy, a chapel with a bell that rang slightly flat, a tavern called the Drowned Lantern whose sign had been repainted so many times the original name was lost under a century of varnish, and forty-odd houses with slate roofs and gardens dug deep and defended like fortresses against the deer.

Everyone knew everyone. Everyone's grandmother had known everyone else's grandmother. The valley's history was kept in three places: the chapel records, the elder's memory, and the stories old Tam Vetch told at the tavern after his third cider, which were roughly sixty percent true on a good night.

It was, by any honest measure, a good place. Emrys knew that. They loved it the way you love a hand that holds you gently and does not let go.

They spent the morning helping their mother bring in the last of the onions before the frost. Maren Ashgrove worked in silence mostly—not the strained silence of someone withholding, but the easy quiet of a woman who had made peace with the shape of her days. She was broad-shouldered and gray-streaked, with forearms that could wring a confession out of laundry, and she had a way of looking at Emrys sometimes—a long, level look, as if checking a lock—that Emrys pretended not to notice.

"You're miles away," she said, tossing an onion into the basket.

"I'm right here."

"Mm." She knelt in the dirt beside them, working the loose soil away from the bulbs. "Bramble's been loud this morning, has she?"

Emrys's hands stilled for half a heartbeat. Only half. "She's a goat, Mum. Goats are loud."

"Goats are," Maren agreed, and said nothing else, and the space where the nothing else lived was wide enough to drive a cart through.

Their mother had never asked directly. Not once in nineteen years. But there had been a moment—Emrys remembered it with terrible clarity—when they were nine, and a neighbor's cart horse had thrown a shoe on the mill road, and the animal had been screaming its pain and fear into the morning, and Emrys had walked straight up to it in front of six witnesses and put a hand on its neck and *spoken*, and the horse had gone calm as still water. Six witnesses. One of them had been Elder Sorrel.

That night their mother had sat on the edge of their bed for a long time without speaking. Then she'd said, "In this valley, we keep our voices small," and kissed their forehead, and that had been the whole of the conversation for a decade.

*Keep your voice small.* As if the problem were volume. As if Emrys could simply whisper their way past the thing inside them. But they understood what she meant, really meant: keep it hidden. Keep it buried. Bury it deeper than the well, deeper than the dead, and maybe the valley's forgetting would extend to you too, and no one from beyond the pass would ever come asking questions about the child who talked to animals.

Because that was the other thing about Hollowmere, the thing nobody said plainly but everybody knew the way you know weather coming: the wards. The valley was warded. Had been, supposedly, since before the chapel records began. What the wards kept out—or in, depending which story you believed—was a matter of tavern debate, but everyone agreed on one point: magic did not cross the border. Magic did not grow here, pool here, linger here. Whatever ran wild through the world beyond the Greybacks, Hollowmere stood in a pocket of quiet, and things like Emrys—beast-speakers, hedge-touched, whatever the old word was—could live out their lives here unmolested, because here, their gift was a seed in frozen ground. Dormant. Deniable.

Beyond the pass, the stories said, such people were found. And found people did not come home.

"So," Maren said, standing and brushing off her skirts, "the harvest feast is Saturday, and I need you to take a basket to Widow Hale, and stop letting Sable bully you at the window."

"He doesn't bully me. He negotiates."

"He extorts you. There's a difference, and the difference is that he wins."

Emrys grinned despite themselves. "He's got a hard life, Mum. Winter's coming."

"He's a crow. Crows thrive on hardship; it's the only thing they respect." She hefted the onion basket onto her hip. "Basket. Widow Hale. Before noon."

* * *

Sable was waiting on the fence post by the time Emrys reached the lane, feathers slicked black as spilled ink, head cocked at an angle of profound judgment.

*You're late,* the crow said. *I've been sitting here since the frost was still on the rail. My feet are cold. My dignity is compromised. Where is it?*

"Where's what?"

*Don't. Don't do the innocent face. You know exactly what. Bread. The good kind. The dark loaf with the seeds that your mother pretends is for the table but is obviously, structurally, intended for me.*

"It's for the table."

*Then why did she leave the end crusts in the cloth by the door this morning, wrapped separate, like a woman packing a parcel?* Sable hopped along the fence rail to keep pace as Emrys walked, wings half-spread for balance, tail flicking. *I have eyes, small one. Terrible eyesight compared to mine, but functional.*

"You watched my mother pack bread."

*I watch everything. It's my whole personality.* A pause, and then, in a different register—the sly one, the one that meant a bargain was incoming—*Crusts, and I'll tell you what the foxes were saying about the north ridge last night.*

Emrys stopped walking. The foxes ranged far, farther than anything else in the valley, up into the high passes where the border stones stood, and lately their reports had grown strange. "What were they saying?"

*Ah-ah.* Sable fluffed up, insufferable. *Bread first. Information is perishable; bread keeps. This is economics.*

"You are the worst creature in this valley, and I include the wasps."

*Flattery noted and accepted.* The crow dropped from the fence, landed on Emrys's shoulder with the familiar weight of cold talons through linen, and rode there toward Widow Hale's cottage like a small feathered tyrant reviewing troops.

This was how it had started, four years ago: a young crow with a broken wing in the woodpile, screaming curses at Emrys in the raw grammar of corvid grief, and Emrys—foolish, fourteen, alone in the trees where no one could see—had answered. *Hold still. I'm trying to help you.* And the crow had gone silent, head swiveling, and then said, in a voice like a knife being drawn slowly, *Well. Isn't that interesting.*

Her wing had healed crooked. She'd stayed anyway. She claimed it was because Emrys owed her a debt of bread that compounded daily, but some mornings, when Emrys woke early, Sable was already on the windowsill, simply watching the valley wake, keeping company. Neither of them ever mentioned those mornings.

At Widow Hale's cottage, Emrys delivered the basket and endured twenty minutes of commentary on their hair, their prospects, and the moral character of the Drowned Lantern's new brewer. Sable waited outside on the gutter, conducting a hostile negotiation with the widow's cat—an enormous orange tom named Captain, who communicated exclusively in threats and had once, according to Sable, "made an offer on my life so crude I refuse to repeat it."

Then they took the long way home, up the sheep track toward the orchard walls, where the view opened over the whole valley: the rooftops, the millpond holding a scrap of sky, the smoke rising straight in the windless cold. Emrys sat on the low stone wall with a heel of bread, broke off the first piece, and held it up.

Sable took it with surgical delicacy. *Payment received. Now. The foxes.*

"Go on."

*There's a vixen—Red, the one with the torn ear, you know her—she ran the ridge line two nights ago, all the way to the standing stones at the pass. And she says—* Sable paused, and Emrys had learned to read the crow's pauses the way sailors read clouds. This one was long. *She says the stones are singing.*

"Singing."

*Humming. Buzzing. Take your pick. A sound she can feel in her teeth. And the other foxes say the same. And the badger at the western boundary says the ground near the border stones has gone quiet—too quiet, no worms, no roots moving, like the soil itself is holding its breath.* Sable swallowed the bread and fixed Emrys with one round black eye. *And here is the part I don't like, small one, the part I've been chewing on for three nights: Red says the sound is getting weaker. Whatever the stones are doing, they're doing less of it. Every night, a little less.*

The wind moved through the bare orchard rows, and somewhere down-valley the flat chapel bell rang noon, and Emrys felt the words settle into them like cold water into boots.

"The wards," they said quietly. "You think the wards are failing."

*I think,* said Sable, *that I flew the length of this valley when my wing was new-mended, top to bottom, and I know its every stone and shadow, and I am telling you that the edges of it have started to smell wrong. Like a room where a fire has just gone out. Still warm. Not for long.*

Emrys looked out over Hollowmere—the amber fields, the smoke, the slow silver pond—and felt the strangest sensation, one they couldn't immediately name. It took a moment to recognize it, because they had spent a lifetime starving it whenever it stirred:

Hope.

If the wards failed—if the quiet that smothered their gift thinned or broke—then what? The world beyond the pass was dangerous, yes, deadly to people like them, the stories were unanimous on that. But it was also *large*. It had rivers that spoke and forests that remembered and, somewhere, perhaps, other people with stones behind their teeth, people who had learned what the gift was *for*. Here, Emrys would spend seventy years listening to goats complain about the weather and saying nothing, and be buried in the chapel yard as a quiet, odd, unremarkable child of Hollowmere, and the whole of what they were would go unspent, like coins hoarded until they corroded.

And the same instant the hope rose, shame rose with it, hot behind the ears. Because wanting the wards to fail meant wanting the valley naked. Their mother lived here. Everyone they loved lived here. The wards were the only wall between Hollowmere and whatever waited beyond the Greybacks, and Emrys was sitting on a wall in the autumn light, secretly rooting for the wall to crack.

*You've gone somewhere,* Sable observed. *Your face does a thing. Like a door opening onto a dark room.*

"I'm fine."

*Liar. You're a terrible liar, which is fortunate for me, since lying is one of my principal trades and competition would be unbearable.* The crow hopped closer along the wall, and her voice, when it came again, had lost its edge—gone low, almost gentle, in the way she only ever was when no third party could witness it. *Whatever you're hoping for, hope it carefully. Things that sing in the teeth are not automatically friendly things. I've seen the world past these hills, small one, more of it than you'd credit. It is not kind to unusual voices.*

"You've never told me what's out there."

*You've never asked twice. First time, you were fifteen and I was eating your supper, and you didn't seem to be in a listening mood.* Sable ruffled her feathers against the wind. *Ask me again sometime. When you mean it.*

Down in the village, Maren appeared in the garden doorway, small with distance, and shaded her eyes to look up the slope. Looking for them. She always looked, at noon, at dusk—had always looked, all these years, with that expression Emrys had finally, recently, learned to name. It wasn't suspicion. It was the look of a woman counting her blessings and finding the number both sufficient and temporary.

"Coming," Emrys called, though the distance ate the word.

They slid off the wall and started down the track, Sable riding their shoulder, and behind them the valley lay golden and silent in the cold light, beautiful as a held breath. Ahead, the northern pass stood open between its standing stones, and the wind came down from it carrying nothing Emrys could hear—

Yet.

But that night, long after the house had gone dark, Emrys lay awake and listened to the far edge of things: the foxes running the ridgeline, the badger turning in its sett, and beneath it all, faint as a pulse under snow, something at the border that hummed.

Quieter than last week.

Weaker than last month.

Like old bones, wearing thin.
