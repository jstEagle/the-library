## 2. Wards Like Old Bones

The elder's house smelled of rosemary and old paper, and Emrys had hated the smell of both ever since.

They sat on a stool too small for them now—had been sitting on it since they were six years old, when Elder Maren first called them in and said, *You will listen to me, child, because no one else can teach you what you are.* The stool had not grown. Emrys had. Their knees knocked against the underside of the table, and Maren's tea went cold between them as it always did, because Maren never drank while she talked and she never stopped talking until she was finished.

Today she had not touched the tea at all.

"Say it back to me," Maren said. Her hands were folded on the table, and Emrys noticed—for the first time in eleven years of these lessons—that they were trembling. Not the palsy of age. Something finer than that. A shiver that lived under the skin like water under ice.

"The wards are woven into the valley's bones," Emrys recited, because reciting was safer than asking why her hands shook. "Stone and root and running water. They turn the eye away from Hollowmere. Travelers forget the road in before they reach the ridge. Magic cannot cross the border, and magic made inside—" their throat tightened around the familiar shape of the words, "—magic born here dies quiet, or is buried with the one who made it."

"And who made the wards?"

"No one knows."

"No one knows," Maren repeated softly. "Eleven years I have taught you, and you have never once asked me the question behind that answer. Why do you suppose that is?"

Emrys looked at the cold tea. At the window, where the late-autumn light came through amber glass and laid a honey-colored stripe across the floorboards. Somewhere outside, Sable was screaming at a jay over possession of a crust, and Emrys could hear every word of it—the crow's voice arriving in their skull clear as a bell rung beside their ear—and they had to keep their face very still so that Maren would not see them listening.

"Because asking makes it real," Emrys said finally.

Maren smiled, and it was the saddest thing Emrys had seen all year.

* * *

The valley had woken that morning wearing fog like a shawl, and Emrys had walked up through it to the shepherd's track with a basket of mended harness straps for Tam Wicker, and everything had been ordinary. Ordinary the way Hollowmere was always ordinary: the smell of woodsmoke and wet wool, the lowing of cattle in the long meadow, old Hollis arguing with his plow horse about a stone in the furrow (*stubborn, stubborn, all of you two-legs stubborn, fine, FINE, I'll go around*)—and Emrys laughing alone in the fog where no one could see, because that was the arrangement. The gift worked best unwatched.

It had been Sable who broke the morning open.

The crow had come down out of the birches like a thrown knife, landed on the fencepost beside Emrys, and said—not screamed, which was worse—said in that dry, precise voice she saved for things that mattered:

*Something is wrong with the edge of the world.*

Emrys had stood very still among Tam Wicker's harness straps. "Wrong how?"

*I flew the ridge at dawn.* Sable ruffled her feathers, once, hard, the way she did when she was frightened and wanted it to look like irritation. *The fog doesn't stop at the ridge anymore. It goes past. It goes out. And there's a place—* She stopped. Crows did not stop mid-sentence. Crows talked the way rivers ran; stopping meant something had dammed them.

"A place what?"

*A place where I couldn't hear myself think.* Her black eye fixed on Emrys with terrible steadiness. *Not your kind of hearing. Mine. My own thoughts. Gone. Like flying into a wall of wool.*

Emrys had carried those words down the hill like a stone in the chest, straight to Maren's door, because there was nowhere else stones like that could be put down.

* * *

Now Maren rose, joints cracking, and went to the corner cabinet where she kept the things she never showed anyone: a lock of hair tied with thread gone gray, a river-smoothed pebble, a clay whistle shaped like nothing in particular. From the bottom shelf she took a bundle wrapped in oilcloth, and unwrapped it on the table between the cold tea and the trembling hands.

Inside lay a strip of bark. Just bark—or so it seemed—except that it was dense as iron and dark as a winter night, and carved into its inner face were lines that hurt to look at. Not ugly. Worse than ugly. *Meaningful*, in a way that squirmed away from the eye like a fish in shallow water. Emrys leaned close and felt their gift stir without permission, reaching toward the marks the way a hand reaches toward a flame.

"Don't," Maren said sharply. "Don't listen to it. Especially don't speak to it."

"It's not alive."

"No." Maren's voice dropped. "But it remembers being part of something that was."

She turned the bark over. On the inner face, along one edge, ran a line of the deep carving—and that line was broken. Split clean through, the way a bone splits, with a pale seam showing where the darkness of the bark had cracked to gray.

"This came off the western wardstone three days ago," Maren said. "I didn't put it there. No one put it there. The ward shed it, the way a snake sheds skin, or the way—" she paused, choosing, "—the way a body sheds what it can no longer hold."

Emrys stared at the fracture. "The wards are dying?"

"The wards are *old*." Maren lowered herself back onto her chair with the care of someone handling a full cup. "Older than this house. Older than the mill, older than the chapel, older than any name we use. When the first families came into this valley, the wards were already here, already humming, already turning travelers' eyes from the road. Our ancestors found a fortress and moved into it, and they told themselves stories about who built it, and the stories got smaller every generation until now we say 'no one knows' and think that's an answer instead of a wound."

"But they've held. All this time, they've held."

"They held because whoever made them built better than they needed to. Or because—" Maren's eyes flicked to Emrys, quick and unreadable, "—they were maintained. There are carvings in the deep caves, Emrys. Under the falls. Figures with animal shadows standing at the valley's edge, mouths open. The old ones drew beast-speakers *into* the wards. As if the wards were sung, once. As if singing kept them."

The room went very quiet. Outside, the wind worried the eaves. In the back of Emrys's mind, Sable had gone silent too—she was perched on the windowsill, and though crows could not hear Maren's words, she could read a room the way sailors read weather.

"If the singing kept them," Emrys said slowly, "and there's been no singing for—"

"For as long as anyone remembers. Yes." Maren folded her hands again, pressing down on them as if to still the tremor by force. "We have been living in a house whose fire went out before our great-great-grandmothers were born, warming ourselves at coals we mistook for a hearth. And now the coals are going gray."

"How long?"

Maren did not pretend not to understand. That was one mercy of the old woman: she never wasted pity on pretending.

"I don't know. A season. Three seasons. Less, if the winter is cruel. The fracture in the bark is spreading—I have a piece from the eastern stone too, and it's worse. Whatever the wards are made of, they're failing the way old bones fail. Quietly. Then all at once."

*All at once.* The phrase settled into Emrys's chest and lay there, heavy and cold, and beneath it—shameful, small, unmistakable—something else stirred. Something that was not dread at all.

Because if the wards failed—

If the wards failed, then the border would be a border no longer. The fog would roll off the ridge and keep rolling, and the road out would be a road, plain and open, that anyone might walk. And Emrys—who had spent twenty-two years learning to fold their voice small enough to fit inside Hollowmere's silence, who spoke to horses in the stable dark and grieved with the valley's dogs when their puppies were drowned and laughed with crows and once, terribly, at fourteen, answered a dying fox's question aloud in the middle of the market square and spent a month afterward being watched by neighbors who said nothing but watched—Emrys would be able to walk out of the cage without opening a single lock, because the cage itself would fall down.

They hated themselves for the flicker of hope even as they fed it.

* * *

"You're thinking about leaving." Maren said it without looking up, sorting her terrible little relics back into their cloth. "Don't bother denying it. You've been thinking about leaving since you were twelve. You have your mother's face when you think about it—the same look she had at your age, staring at the ridge like it owed her money."

"My mother never wanted to leave."

"No. But she wanted to want to. There's a difference, and it eats people." Maren tied the oilcloth with fingers that would not quite obey her. "Listen to me, child, because I am old and I am allowed to say true things. If the wards fail, this valley does not become free. It becomes *visible*. Everything the wards turned away—every hungry thing, every hunting thing, every clever thing that learned long ago what a gift like yours tastes like—all of it will find us the way water finds a crack. Your hope is understandable. It is also a lantern, and there are moths in the dark beyond that ridge with teeth."

"What kind of moths?" Emrys asked, and heard their own voice come out smaller than they intended.

"I don't know." Maren looked up, and her eyes were wet and furious and ancient. "That's what I'm telling you. *No one knows.* We have been safe so long we have forgotten what safety was protecting us from. The knowledge died with the ward-makers, and whatever killed the ward-makers' world—" she gestured at the wrapped bark, at the window, at the whole soft green valley lying under the thinning autumn light, "—it's still out there. Waiting isn't the same as gone."

Emrys thought of Sable on the fencepost. *A place where I couldn't hear myself think.*

Something was already out there. Something new, or something returned.

* * *

They walked home in the last of the light, and the valley was so beautiful it felt like an accusation.

The long meadow had gone gold and bronze, and the cattle moved through it slow as clouds. Woodsmoke rose straight up from a dozen chimneys into windless air. Someone was calling a child home to supper, and the child was refusing, and the negotiation had the worn, beloved rhythm of a song sung ten thousand times. Emrys walked through all of it carrying Maren's words like a bucket of water that sloshed and would not set down.

*All at once.*

At the gate, Sable dropped out of the dusk onto the post, head tilted.

*Well?*

"She says the wards are failing. Old bones. Nobody knows how they were made or how to mend them."

Sable was quiet for a moment. Crows were rarely quiet, and Emrys had learned to respect the silence the way sailors respected a drop in the wind.

*And what did you feel,* Sable said at last, *when she said it?*

"That's none of your business."

*Everything you feel is my business. I ride your shoulder into the future, whether you invite me or not.* She hopped closer along the post, feathers sleek black shot with oil-slick blue. *You felt glad. Don't lie to me; I can taste a lie the way you taste salt. You felt glad, and then you felt ashamed of feeling glad, and now you're doing both at once, which looks exhausting.*

Emrys leaned on the gate. Below, in the kitchen garden, their mother was pulling the last carrots, her back bent, her hands sure, her whole life arranged in neat rows the way the garden was—everything in its place, everything tended, everything *contained*. She had known what Emrys was since the fox in the market square. She had never once spoken of it. That silence had been her love language, and Emrys had grown up fluent in it, and some nights fluency felt indistinguishable from suffocation.

"If the wards go down," Emrys said quietly, "I could leave. Really leave. Not sneak out to the ridge and come back by dark. Leave."

*Yes.*

"People die out there. People like me especially, wherever 'out there' is. Maren said—"

*Maren is a woman who keeps a lock of hair in a box and calls it wisdom.* Sable's voice softened, which for Sable meant dropping perhaps a quarter of its edge. *Listen, chick. I have flown over mountains you've never seen and cities you couldn't spell. The world out there is vast and stupid and mostly indifferent to you, and yes, some of it would eat you. But some of it would eat me too, and I fly anyway, because the alternative is a life spent calculating the exact size of your cage and calling the calculation prudence.*

Emrys huffed something that was almost a laugh. "When did you get wise?"

*I've always been wise. You only started listening when you got scared.* She sidled along the post until her shoulder nearly brushed Emrys's hand—a liberty she permitted no other living creature. *The wards fall or they don't. Either way, the question is the same, and you'll have to answer it eventually: are you going to spend your whole life being a secret? Because secrets don't survive, little speaker. Secrets just wait to be found out.*

From the garden, Emrys's mother straightened, pressed a hand to her back, and looked over at the gate—at Emrys standing in the blue dusk talking to no one. Her expression did something complicated: fear and love and resignation braided tight, shown for half a second before she smoothed it into a smile and lifted the basket of carrots.

"Supper!" she called. "Come in before you catch your death."

Emrys pushed off the gate. Behind them, far off toward the western ridge, the fog was rolling in off the high country, soft and white and patient—and for just a moment, in the failing light, Emrys thought they saw it hesitate at the ridgeline. Pause. Press gently against the invisible boundary like a hand testing a door.

Then it poured over, unhindered, and began to fill the valley.

Sable went rigid on the post.

*Emrys,* she said, and her voice had lost every scrap of its dryness. *The fog is inside the ridge. The fog has never been inside the ridge.*

Down in the valley, the evening bells rang for supper, sweet and ordinary, and nobody but a girl and a crow noticed that the world's oldest wall had begun, quietly, to come down.
