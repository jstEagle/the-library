## 3. The Night Without Stars

Midwinter came to Hollowmere the way it always did: slowly, then all at once.

For weeks the valley had been tightening itself like a fist. Frost climbed the north faces of the cottages and stayed there past noon. The millpond wore its lid of grey glass. Smoke rose from every chimney in thin, obedient columns, as if even the fire had agreed to keep quiet. Emrys had grown up believing that winter in Hollowmere was not a season but a posture — the whole valley drawing inward, holding its breath, waiting for the light to come back.

They had spent the last days of the old year doing what they always did: chores that took longer than they needed to, because the animals made them long.

The goats were the worst gossips in the valley. Old Marigold, the white nanny with the torn ear, had opinions about everything from the quality of this year's hay to the moral character of Tam Broadfoot's dog, and she delivered them all in a braying drone that Emrys heard not as sound but as meaning, warm and indignant against the cold air. *The hay is dry on one side. The boy kicks at his dog when no one watches. I remember. Goats remember everything.*

"I know," Emrys murmured, pitching the last of the feed. "You've told me four times."

*And you have listened four times,* Marigold said, with the deep satisfaction of a creature who considered being heard a form of worship. *That is why you are my favorite of the tall ones.*

Emrys smiled despite themselves. That was the thing no one in Hollowmere could ever know — that the silence they kept was not an empty silence but a full one, crowded with voices, a whole second village living alongside the first and visible only to them. The hens arguing over roosting rights like merchants over market stalls. The ox complaining about the cold in a voice like a landslide given patience. The mice in the granary walls running their tiny, anxious ledgers of seed and danger. And above it all, when Sable deigned to visit, the crow's commentary — sharp, funny, needling, the closest thing Emrys had to a friend who knew them entire.

Their mother called from the doorway, breath steaming. "Emrys. Come inside. It's starting."

The midwinter vigil. Every family in the valley would shutter their windows and sit by candlelight through the longest night, as they had for as long as anyone remembered. No one said anymore what they were waiting out. The elder, Maren, said the vigil was older than the reason for it, like a scar left after the wound was forgotten. Emrys's mother lit the candles anyway, all seven, because her own mother had, and hers before that.

Inside, the house smelled of tallow and rosemary and the dried apples strung above the hearth. Emrys's mother, Ilse, moved through the small rooms touching things as she passed — the table, the doorframe, Emrys's shoulder — a habit of hers, as if confirming each object still belonged to the world.

"You're pale," she said.

"It's cold."

"You're always cold. You're never pale." She studied them with those grey eyes that missed so little it was sometimes frightening. "Sit. Eat something."

Emrys sat. They ate. Outside, the wind came down off the rim of the valley and combed through the bare orchard, and Emrys heard the trees talking to each other in their slow creaking way — not words exactly, more like old people holding hands in the dark, saying *still here, still here, still here.*

That was how the night began. Ordinary. Warm. A lie, though none of them knew it yet.

* * *

It was near midnight when the dogs started.

Not barking. Emrys had heard dogs bark their whole life — at foxes, at strangers, at thunder. This was different. This was a sound that came up out of the chest like water forced through a narrow place, high and continuous, and beneath it, in the language only Emrys could hear, a single word repeated over and over by every dog in the valley at once:

*DOWN. DOWN. DOWN. DOWN.*

Emrys was on their feet before they understood why, candle flame guttering with their movement. "Mother—"

Ilse looked up from her mending. "What is it?"

"The dogs—" Emrys pressed a hand flat against their sternum, where the voices landed. "Something's wrong. Something's coming down off the ridge, or—no." They stopped. Listened harder. The word wasn't *down*, they realized. It was older than that, rougher, the way the oldest animals spoke when they weren't speaking to humans at all. *Beneath. Beneath. Beneath.* "No. Something's wrong underneath us."

"Emrys." Their mother's voice went flat and careful, the voice she used for wounds and funerals. "What do you hear?"

And there it was — the question of Emrys's whole life, asked at last without fear in it, only urgency, and Emrys almost laughed at the timing. Twenty years of silence, and now there was no time to explain.

"The animals talk," they said. "They've always talked. To me. I can hear them. Mother, I'm sorry, I never—"

Ilse's face did something complicated — grief and recognition and a terrible tenderness all at once, as if some suspicion she had carried for years had finally been confirmed and she hated herself for having carried it. "I know," she said quietly. "I've known since you were six and told me the dead hen was sorry."

Emrys stared at her.

"We don't have time," Ilse said. "Tell me what you hear *now*."

But Emrys was already hearing it. The horses in the Broadfoots' field had begun to scream too, and under the screaming the land itself — the patient, deep voice of roots and stone and sleeping seed — had changed key. For twenty years it had murmured its one long note of contentment, the note of a warded place, safe as a hand around a candleflame. Now the note was fraying. Emrys could feel it fraying the way you feel a rope burning through your grip, strand by strand, somewhere far below the frost line, below the wells, below anything with a name.

"The wards," Emrys whispered. "Mother, the wards are breaking."

"That's not possible. The wards are—"

*I know what they are,* Emrys almost said. *Older than memory. No one alive knows how they were made. Maren said it herself, three weeks ago, standing at the border stones with her hands on the cold rock, looking afraid for the first time in anyone's lifetime.* But the sentence died, because at that moment every animal in Hollowmere stopped screaming at once.

The silence was worse.

Then the stars went out.

* * *

Emrys saw it through the window, and would see it again behind their eyes for the rest of their life: the sky over the valley, hard and clear and salted with midwinter stars — and then, between one heartbeat and the next, simply *gone*. Not clouded. Not veiled. Gone, as if a hand had closed over the whole bowl of heaven, as if the night had been a page and someone had turned it. The darkness that remained was total in a way darkness had never been before. It had weight. It leaned on the rooftops. The snowfields, which should have glowed faintly under starlight, went black, and the blackness ate the edges of things until the valley seemed to be floating free of the world.

Beside them, their mother made a small sound, and Emrys realized she was praying — actually praying, in the old cadence, the one used for deaths.

And in the new dark, the animals began to speak.

All of them. At once. Every creature in Hollowmere — dogs and cattle and crows and the deer driven down from the high forest, the owls, the foxes, the fish rising stunned beneath the ice of the millpond — every throat opened and poured out the same language, the deep language, the one beneath all their separate tongues, and it crashed into Emrys like a wave into a glass house.

*IT IS UNDONE. IT IS UNDONE. THE SHEPHERDS ARE GONE. THE LIGHTS ARE EATEN. IT IS UNDONE, IT IS UNDONE, WE ARE NAKED UNDER NOTHING—*

Emrys went to their knees. There was too much. There had always been too much, but the wards had been a dam their whole life, and now the dam was gone and the whole grieving voice of the living world was flooding through them, terror upon terror upon terror, a thousand hearts hammering the same wordless scream. They tasted blood. Their nose was bleeding; they could feel it on their lip, hot in the cold room.

Through it, distantly, their mother's arms were around them, her voice cutting through in fragments: "*—breathe, breathe, look at me, Emrys, look at me—*"

"They're so frightened," Emrys gasped. "Everything is so frightened. Mother, they can feel it — whatever took the stars, they can feel it *coming*—"

"Then we hide." Ilse's voice was iron wrapped in wool. She pulled back to look at them, and her face in the candlelight was the face of every generation of Hollowmere mothers compressed into one. "We bar the door. We put out the candles. We stay small and quiet until whoever did this passes over. That is what the vigil is *for*, Emrys. That is what it has always been for. We survive by being nothing. We have always survived by being nothing."

And Emrys, kneeling in their own blood on the floorboards, hearing the whole valley scream in a language no one else alive could understand, felt something crystallize in their chest — cold and bright and terribly clear, like the first drop of water refreezing at thaw's end.

Being nothing had kept them alive. Being nothing had also been killing them, slowly, politely, for twenty years. And tonight, being nothing would mean sitting in the dark while the world outside ended, able to hear every death and help none of them.

"No," Emrys said.

Their mother flinched as if struck.

"I said no." Emrys got to their feet. The voices were still pouring through them, but they found, underneath the flood, a strange steadiness — the steadiness of a decision already made before the mind caught up. "You hid me my whole life. I let you. I understood — I thought I understood. But Mother, I am the only person in this valley — maybe the only person for a hundred miles — who can hear what's happening out there. If I hide, and it goes wrong, we won't even know *which way it came from*."

"Emrys—"

"Maren said the wards were older than memory. She said no one alive knows how they were made." Emrys wiped their nose with the back of their hand. "Someone made them once. Someone who could speak to beasts, I'd bet my life on it — because the wards didn't just keep magic *out*, they kept the voices *quiet*, they've been damming me my whole life, which means they were built by someone like me. Which means someone like me built them. And if they can be broken—" their voice cracked, and they hated it, and pushed through anyway, "—then someone like me might be able to learn how they were *made*."

Outside, the dogs began to howl again, and this time the word beneath the howling had changed. Emrys stilled, listening, and their blood went colder than the midwinter night.

*RUN,* said every animal in Hollowmere. *RUN. RUN. RUN.*

Not away from something. Toward nothing. Just *run*, the oldest instruction there was, older than dens and burrows and nests, the word written into the marrow of every living thing since the first creature grew legs.

Ilse grabbed their wrist. "You are not leaving this house. Emrys, look at the sky. Look at it! Whatever has done this will eat a child who walks into the dark carrying the very gift it hunts—"

"It already knows where I am," Emrys said quietly. "It knows where all of us are. The wards were the only secret we ever had, and they're gone." They covered their mother's hand with their own. Her fingers were shaking. In twenty years, Emrys had never once felt their mother's hands shake. "I'm not leaving *you*. I'm leaving *before*. There's a difference, and it matters."

For a long moment neither of them moved. The candles burned. Somewhere beyond the walls, the whole animal world screamed its one word into the starless dark.

Then Ilse Ashgrove did the hardest thing Emrys would ever watch her do. She let go.

She turned, crossed the kitchen, and began pulling things from cupboards — not food first, but the heavy woollen socks, the oiled canvas cloak that had been Emrys's father's, the flint and steel, the little clay pot of rendered fat. She worked in silence, jaw tight, tears running freely down a face that refused to change expression, and Emrys understood that this was her way of screaming: to pack a bag for the child she could not keep, with hands that would not stop trembling.

They packed together. Bread and hard cheese. Dried apples. The skinning knife and the fishing line. A spare shirt. The small tin of willow-bark powder for pain. Ilse sewed a fold of silver coins into the cloak's hem without asking permission, and Emrys let her, because some arguments you lose on purpose.

At the door, she stopped them with one hand on their chest.

"Hollowmere," she said. "If it comes to choosing — come home. Promise me. Dead or alive, whole or broken — come home."

Emrys kissed her forehead, tasting salt. "I promise."

Which was, they suspected, the first lie they had ever told her. Or perhaps the truest thing they had ever said, depending on what *home* turned out to mean.

* * *

The valley at midnight was a held breath. Snow squeaked under Emrys's boots. Overhead, where the sky should have been, there was nothing — a blackness so complete it felt less like absence than attention, as if something vast had lowered its face close to the world and was watching.

The eastern road climbed toward the border stones, and Emrys walked it with the hood up and the bag straps biting their shoulders, and everywhere around them the night was loud — louder than any night had ever been. Foxes flowing downslope past the fields like spilled water, minds flickering *run run run*. A barn owl perched motionless on a fencepost, its thoughts a single bright thread of dread. Cattle pressing against the far side of barns, lowing, and under the lowing: *the lights are eaten, the shepherds are gone, we are naked under nothing.*

Halfway up the road, at the bend where the old rowan stood, a shape dropped out of the black onto the fence rail beside them, and Emrys's heart lurched and then broke open with relief.

*Sable.*

The crow's feathers were ruffled, her eyes catching what little light remained with an oily gleam. She looked, Emrys thought, like she had flown a very long way very fast, and had opinions about it.

*Well,* Sable said, in that dry, needling voice that Emrys had loved since childhood. *Took you long enough. I've been shouting your name across half the valley. You humans and your ears — decorative, the lot of them.*

"Sable—" Emrys's voice came out cracked. "The stars. Did you see—"

*I was in the capital when they went out.* The crow shifted on the rail, and beneath her usual sardonic edge Emrys felt something they had never felt from her before. Fear. Old fear, banked and bitter, like coals raked over and left smoldering. *Three days ago. Before your precious wards even cracked. And what I saw there, featherling — what I saw the king do with his own two hands and his smile full of too many teeth — you need to hear it. All of it. Because your valley isn't the beginning of anything. It's the last place the ending hasn't reached.*

Above them, in the sky where the stars had been, something vast and patient leaned closer.

Emrys gripped the strap of their bag, turned their back on the only home they had ever known, and said, "Tell me."
