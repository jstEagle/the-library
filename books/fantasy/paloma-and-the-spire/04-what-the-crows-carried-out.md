## 4. What the Crows Carried Out

The gift did not work the way Emrys had always assumed it worked.

They had thought of it as listening — a leaning-in, an ear pressed to the world's hidden door. And it was that, mostly. But on the third night after the stars went out, huddled in the root cellar beneath the collapsed byre with their mother's hand tight around their wrist and the valley screaming its wordless grief above them, Emrys learned that listening was only half of it. The other half was being listened *to*. The other half was invitation.

Sable came down through the broken vent like a scrap of torn night, feathers slicked with soot and something worse, and she landed on the upturned milk pail and looked at Emrys with one bright eye, and Emrys understood — not in words, not yet, but in the way one understands a held breath — that the crow was about to open her whole self, and that what poured out would not fit inside a conversation.

*Hold still,* she said, which was not a request. *And don't let go.*

Then the door opened all the way, and Emrys fell into a crow.

* * *

Falling, first. That is where memory begins when you have wings: not with sight but with the drop, the sweet sick lurch of air taking your weight. Sable remembered falling before she remembered anything else — falling out of the rookery behind the Royal Observatory on a morning in late autumn, when the capital still had a sky worth falling through.

She had been young then, by crow reckoning. Not fledgling-young, but young enough to think the city belonged to her because she could see all of it at once. And oh, the city from above — Aurelmar, the Gilded Seat, spread along the river Serle like a coin someone had dropped and decided not to pick up. White walls gone honey-colored in the sun. The Spire rising out of the palace district like a needle pulled through cloth, older than every map that tried to hold it, its summit lost in weather even on clear days. And above it all, the stars stitched into the dark like silver seed-pearls, each one named, each one charted, each one kept.

That was the thing outsiders never understood about Aurelmar, Sable remembered thinking, wheeling over the rooftops while the bells rang terce: the stars were not decoration. They were infrastructure. The Royal Cartographers' Hall maintained charts of the heavens the way harbormasters maintained charts of the coast — because the kingdom's oldest wards were anchored to them. Every ward-stone in every border valley, every quiet road, every blessed threshold drew its strength from patterns fixed overhead. The star-charts were not pictures. They were load-bearing walls.

Sable had known this the way crows know things: sideways, incidentally, from perching on windowsills where important men argued about important papers. She had been a palace bird for three generations of her line. Her grandmother had stolen crumbs from the old king's breakfast table. Her mother had once carried — though Sable had never quite believed this story — a rolled scrap of parchment from a cartographer's window to a spymaster's garden, and been paid in candied ginger for her trouble. Crows in Aurelmar were not vermin. They were a second postal service, a first-warning system, the eyes the city forgot it had.

So when the dreams started, the crows were among the first to notice.

They began that same autumn, spreading through the rookeries like a cold moving through a house. Birds woke screaming from dreams of a vast pale throat. Others simply stopped dreaming at all, sat motionless on their perches staring at nothing until they starved. The ravens in the Tower rookery turned on their keeper, who had fed them for thirty years, and drove him bleeding from the courtyard. And always, underneath, a sound none of them could name — a singing heard not with the ear but somewhere behind the skull, beautiful and patient, like water learning the shape of the stone it means to wear away.

*We thought it was sickness,* Sable remembered telling the others, perched on the Observatory's weathervane with the wind pulling at her feathers. *Sickness passes. We waited for it to pass.*

It did not pass. It climbed.

By midwinter the singing had a direction. It came from below — from under the city, under the river, under the roots of the mountains themselves — and it rose strongest through the oldest stones, and the oldest stones in all the kingdom stood in the palace district, and the oldest of those was the Spire. The birds began avoiding it. Whole flocks would detour rather than cross its shadow, and the people below noticed, and laughed, and said the crows had grown strange this winter, and went on about their days.

Sable had not laughed. Sable had flown to the Cartographers' Hall, because her mother's story about the parchment had left her with a habit: when the world goes wrong, find the people whose job is noticing.

* * *

The Hall smelled of ink and lamp oil and, that winter, of fear.

Sable knew the building's rhythms the way she knew the bell-tower's — which windows stuck, which sills caught the morning sun, which room belonged to which voice. She liked the map room best, all long tables and weighted corners, the great celestial charts hung from ceiling pulleys so they could be raised and lowered like sails. She had watched the cartographers work a hundred evenings: patient, precise people who spoke softly and measured everything twice, who treated the sky as a colleague rather than a mystery.

Wren Calloway had been the youngest of them, and the loudest, and the only one who talked to the crows.

Not the way Emrys talked — not truly, not in words. But she talked. She narrated her work in a dry, running commentary while she inked coastlines and corrected star positions, and she left bacon rinds on the south sill, and she had once spent an entire afternoon trying to teach Sable the word *declination*, and Sable had let her believe she'd succeeded because the look on her face was worth it. Wren Calloway noticed everything and believed almost nothing she was told, which in Sable's experience was the exact profile of a person worth following.

On the night the charts burned, Wren was the one who sounded the alarm.

Sable had been roosting in the Hall's rafters, half-asleep, when the shouting started — not alarm-shouting but argument-shouting, the sharp kind, and she'd dropped down to the gallery rail to watch. Below, in the map room, a cluster of royal guards stood among the tables, and at their center a man in palace livery unrolled a decree bearing the seal of the Crown, and the hall's master cartographer read it twice and went gray.

*By order of His Majesty,* the decree said, *all celestial charts are hereby recalled to the palace for revision.*

*Revision,* the master said. *Of the stars?*

*His Majesty's instructions are complete,* the man in livery said, and he smiled, and Sable — who had seen ten thousand human smiles from her various perches, who considered herself something of a connoisseur — felt every feather on her body lift away from her skin.

It was not that the smile was wrong, exactly. It had all the right parts. Lips, teeth, the little creases at the eyes. It was assembled correctly, the way a puppet is assembled correctly. But it moved a half-beat behind the man's voice, arriving late like thunder after lightning, and it held too long, and it showed too many teeth — not more than a mouth should hold, but each one given too much attention, each one presented, the way a merchant presents wares.

*The charts will be collected tonight,* the man said. *All of them. Including the working copies. Including the memory of them.* He paused, and the smile widened by a degree, a ratchet-click. *His Majesty is very concerned about accuracy.*

They burned them in the Hall's own courtyard, because there were so many.

Sable watched from the gutter above the archway while the cartographers carried out their life's work armload by armload — the great celestial charts with their silver-inked constellations, the coastal atlases annotated with ward-anchor points, the working copies, the drafts, the rubbings taken from standing stones, the field notebooks of dead surveyors. The guards piled them in the fountain basin, which had been drained for winter, and the man in palace livery struck the flame himself, and the fire went up green.

Green, Sable remembered thinking. Fire is orange. Fire has been orange since fire was invented. This fire burned green as foxfire, green as deep-sea rot, and it made no heat that she could feel from twenty feet above it, and the smoke did not rise so much as *pour*, sliding along the ground in slow rivers that the wind refused to touch.

The cartographers stood in a row and watched. Some wept. Most did not. The ones who did not frightened Sable more, because their faces had gone smooth and attentive, the faces of students waiting for a lesson, and when the man in livery turned to them and smiled his too-many-toothed smile, several of them smiled back. Perfectly. In time.

*Not everyone,* Sable remembered, and the remembering hurt, because the not-everyone was the whole tragedy folded small. *Some of them stayed wrong. Stayed themselves. The young one — the loud one, the one who fed us — she grabbed two charts off the pile before the guards reached the courtyard. Rolled them into a map tube and walked out the front door past four guards like she was delivering laundry. I saw her do it. I followed her because I thought, here is a person whose face still moves in the right order.*

Wren Calloway had gone to ground in a rented room above a chandler's shop in the Tanners' Quarter, and for six days she had done nothing but drink and try to redraw from memory the chart she'd saved, and fail, and drink again. Sable had perched on her windowsill and watched her do it, and had felt — crows feel such things, whatever humans believe — the specific misery of watching a clever creature break its own wings against a pane of glass.

On the seventh day, the king addressed the city.

* * *

He stood on the balcony above the Grand Processional, and the whole capital turned out, because a king's summons is not optional, and because the people wanted reassurance the way the drowning want air. Sable watched from the eave of the Guildhall, wedged between two frozen gargoyles, and the crowd below her was a sea of upturned faces, breath steaming in the cold, ten thousand people waiting to be told the strange winter meant nothing.

King Aldric had always been a good speaker. That was his reputation — a plain man, a steady man, a king who said what he meant. He stood at the balustrade in a fur-trimmed cloak, and he was not alone; behind him, half in shadow, stood a woman Sable had never seen before, tall and pale, wearing gray, her hands folded, her head inclined toward the king as if listening to something only she could provide.

*My people,* Aldric said, and his voice carried the way voices carry in dreams, reaching every ear at once without effort. *You have noticed the winter is long. You have noticed the nights are restless. Some of you have heard... singing.*

A ripple through the crowd. Beside Sable, hidden in the gargoyles' shadow, a pigeon trembled and made no sound at all, which was somehow worse than screaming.

*I tell you now: there is no cause for fear.* The king smiled, and from the Guildhall eave it was visible even at distance, wide and white and correct. *The stars you have lost are not lost. They are being gathered. Gathered and kept safe, as a shepherd gathers the flock before the storm. When the gathering is finished, the sky will be more beautiful than any of you remember. You will not need charts to find your way. You will need only to listen, and follow, and be glad.*

*Be glad,* the crowd repeated, some of them. Then more. The words moved through the mass of people like wind through wheat, heads bowing and lifting, and Sable watched a baker's wife say *be glad* with tears streaming down her frozen cheeks, saying it because the voice had asked, saying it the way a bell says a note when struck.

*And there are those among you,* the king continued, and here his voice changed, softened, became confiding, intimate, a secret shared with each listener individually, *who resist the gathering. Who cling to the old charts, the old names, the old ways of seeing. They mean no harm. They are simply afraid. Help them. Report them. The gifted, the listeners, the ones who speak with beasts — bring them to the light, so that they may be gathered too, and be glad.*

The crowd cheered. Ten thousand throats, cheering the hunting of their neighbors, cheering warmly, gratefully, and Sable pressed herself flat against the freezing lead of the eave and understood with total clarity that she was looking at the end of the world — not with fire, not with flood, but with a smile and an invitation, the world ending the way sleep ends waking: gently, and with consent, and forever.

She flew for Hollowmere that night.

* * *

The flight had taken nine days, and Sable remembered every one of them, because the land itself had changed beneath her wings.

The wards were failing everywhere — she could see it now, the way you can see a stain once someone points it out. The border valleys still glimmered faintly with their old protections, dull as banked coals, and the roads between them ran naked. Magic pooled in the low places like fog. In the hill country she had passed a village where every dog howled at dusk in perfect unison, and a village where every dog lay silent in the streets, dead or sleeping or gathered, and she had not stopped to learn which. She had crossed a forest where the trees grew in spirals, turning slowly toward a summit where something pale sang, and she had flown high, higher than sensible, until her lungs burned, because below the treeline the singing got inside you and started rearranging the furniture of your mind.

Twice she had seen the hounds.

The first time, at a distance: shapes moving through a harvested field at twilight, too large for wolves, moving wrong, joints bending in sequences joints should not know. The second time, closer — close enough to see that their coats were the color of drowned things, and that where their eyes should have been there was instead a soft pale glow, and that when they lifted their heads to the darkening sky, their mouths opened, and what came out was not a howl.

It was harmony. Four-part harmony, from throats built for killing, beautiful as vespers, and Sable had flown until her wings failed and then walked, and flown again, south and west, toward the one place in the world the singing had never managed to crack: a warded valley full of quiet people who had forgotten why they were quiet.

She had reached Hollowmere's rim on the night the stars went out.

She had felt the wards go — every bird within a hundred miles had felt it, a snap felt in the chest like a rope parting, like a bone breaking somewhere just outside the body. And she had seen the sky empty itself, star by star by star, not fading but *taken*, plucked from the dark with a precision that was somehow more obscene than any violence, and she had screamed with ten thousand other voices, feathered and furred and human, and then she had done the only thing left to do.

She had gone to find the child.

Because her grandmother had told stories, and her mother had confirmed them, and the crows of Hollowmere had spoken for years — warily, disbelievingly — of a human in the eastern farms who *answered*. Who spoke back. Who argued with magpies and traded insults with goats and once, memorably, had negotiated a ceasefire between the miller's cat and the churchyard sparrows. A beast-speaker, alive and growing, inside the last strong wards in the kingdom, at the exact moment the kingdom needed one.

*The old carvings say,* Sable remembered whispering to herself, wingbeats counting out the miles, *the old carvings say the beast-speakers bound the Spire once before. The old carvings say your kind were the wardens. The old carvings had better be right, little human, because they are the only plan anyone has left.*

* * *

The vision loosened.

Emrys surfaced from it the way a diver surfaces — too fast, gasping, the root cellar snapping back into focus around them: the smell of earth and spilled milk, their mother's grip bruising their wrist, the single candle guttering in the draft from the broken vent. Their whole body shook. Tears had frozen on their cheeks without their noticing.

Sable stood on the milk pail, feathers drooping, spent. Opening herself like that had cost her; Emrys could see it now, could feel the hollowed-out ache in the crow's small body as if it were their own.

*The charts,* Emrys whispered. Their voice came out ruined. *She saved two charts. The cartographer. Where — *

*Gone, probably. Or drunk. Or gathered.* Sable's voice was thin as wire. *But she was heading west, last I saw her. Toward Fenwick. Toward the border towns. People like her run to edges when the center rots.*

Above them, muffled by the earth, the valley's animals were still crying — the cattle lowing in broken phrases, the dogs keening, the wild things silent entirely, which was worst of all. And beneath it, faint but unmistakable, threading up through the soil itself, Emrys heard for the first time what Sable had flown nine days to warn them about: singing. Distant. Patient. Beautiful.

Coming from the north and east. From the capital. Getting louder.

*We leave tomorrow,* Emrys said, and did not recognize their own voice. *Before the singing gets here. There's a cartographer in Fenwick, you said — *

*I said she was heading toward Fenwick,* Sable corrected, tucking her head beneath one wing, already half-asleep with exhaustion. *I said nothing about her being useful. I said nothing about her being sober.*

But her eye, the one not hidden, stayed open a moment longer, fixed on Emrys with something that in a human face would have been called hope.

*Rest, little warden,* she murmured. *Tomorrow we find out if the old carvings were right.*
