## 5. The Cartographer's Debt

Fenwick smelled of wet wool and woodsmoke and fear.

Emrys had never seen so many people in one place. Hollowmere's market days gathered perhaps two hundred souls beneath the elms by the millpond, and even those crowds had always felt to Emrys like a held breath—everyone knowing everyone, everyone watching for the wrong kind of listening. Fenwick was a different animal entirely. It was a border town built where the King's Road spilled out of the eastern hills, all crooked lanes and leaning houses, and it was full to bursting with people who had come from the direction of the capital and were not going back.

They clogged the square outside the Wayfarer's Rest: families with everything they owned strapped to their backs, farmers whose carts had been abandoned three valleys away, a woman selling boiled sweets from a tray she guarded with her elbow like a shield. And everywhere, on every wall and doorpost and shutter, the same question written in charcoal and paint and scratched into wood with knives:

*Where did the stars go?*

Emrys pulled their hood lower and moved through the crowd like water through reeds. Three days out of Hollowmere, and they had learned the shape of the world's new grief. People did not scream about the dark sky. They went quiet about it, the way you go quiet about a death in the house. They lit lamps at noon. They looked up at the flat black ceiling of heaven and then quickly down again, as if the sky might notice them looking.

Sable rode the crook of Emrys's shoulder, feathers fluffed against the cold, and murmured commentary only Emrys could hear.

*Too many two-legs,* she said. *Smells like a rookery after a fox gets in. Keep walking. There's a stable behind that inn—I can smell hay and honest filth, which is more than I can say for these people.*

"I'm not going to steal a horse," Emrys muttered under their breath.

*Who said horse? I said mule. Mules are honest. Horses are liars with long faces.*

Emrys had learned, over three days of road, that Sable's advice came in two flavors: genuinely useful and elaborately self-interested, and that the trick was telling them apart before acting on it. The crow had appointed herself navigator, quartermaster, and conscience, in roughly that order of enthusiasm.

The trouble was money. Emrys had left Hollowmere with bread, cheese, a knife, their mother's grey shawl, and eleven copper bits found in the tin behind the flour jar, which their mother would have called stealing and which Emrys preferred to think of as an inheritance paid in advance. Eleven bits bought a bed in a hayloft one night and nothing the next. The hayloft option had expired last evening. This morning Emrys had woken stiff and cold in the lee of a shrine to some road-god whose face had been chiseled off long ago, and had come into Fenwick proper with a plan that consisted entirely of the words *figure something out* repeated until they took on the rhythm of footsteps.

*There,* Sable said suddenly, her talons tightening on Emrys's shoulder. *Stable. And something wrong in it.*

Emrys felt it a heartbeat later—not with the ears, but with whatever it was inside them that heard. A thread of animal terror, high and thin, pulling taut behind the Wayfarer's Rest. The town was full of human misery, but this was different. This was a mind in a small dark place, running in circles, screaming without sound.

Emrys was moving before deciding to move, slipping down the alley between the inn and the cooper's shop, following the thread. It led to a stable yard fenced with rickety palings, where a mule stood backed into the corner of its pen, ears flat, eyes rolling white, hooves striking sparks off frozen mud. A boy of maybe ten stood at the fence with a rope, calling to it in a voice cracking with frustration and something close to tears.

"Come on, Daisy, come *on*, we have to go, Papa says we have to go *today*—"

The mule screamed—a real scream, the sound of an animal past the end of its patience—and lunged along the fence, and the boy stumbled back and fell, and the rope went slack, and half the yard turned to look.

Emrys was over the fence before anyone could stop them, which was no one, because no one wanted to be near the mule.

Up close, the animal's terror hit like a wave breaking. It wasn't just fear of noise or crowd, though there was plenty of both. Underneath ran something older and stranger: the mule had been hearing things for three nights. Things in the dark where the stars used to be. A singing, far off, beautiful and wrong, and every other animal in the yard had heard it too and gone quiet in the way animals go quiet when they pretend to be stones—but Daisy was harnessed to a cart and could not pretend, and the song had been getting closer every night, and now here she stood in a pen too small, with ropes on her face and nowhere to run, and her mind was a room with the walls coming in.

*Oh,* said Sable softly, from the fence post. *Oh, poor thing.*

"It's all right," Emrys said aloud, and then, because the words meant nothing to a terrified mule, they reached out along the thread and let her hear what they actually meant.

It was hard to describe, even privately, what happened when Emrys stopped holding the gift back. It was like opening a window in a stuffy room after years of keeping it shut. The mule's panic poured in—vast, formless, a storm of smell and pressure and the memory of the singing—and Emrys let it pour through them and answered it, not with words exactly but with the shape of safety: stillness, dry straw, a hand that does not strike, the particular dignity of being spoken to honestly. *You are not crazy,* Emrys told her. *You heard it. We all heard it. But it is daylight now, and I am here, and no one is going to hurt you, and if you let the boy put the rope on gently, I will walk beside you the whole way out of this loud, terrible town.*

The mule's eye—the near one, huge and dark and ringed with white—fixed on Emrys. Her breathing slowed. Her ears came forward a degree, then another. Somewhere in the yard, the noise of the crowd seemed to recede, though Emrys knew it hadn't; it was Daisy who had stepped back from the edge of her own terror, using Emrys's calm as a handhold.

"There," Emrys said softly, aloud, for the benefit of the humans. "She's not vicious. She's frightened. Haven't you all been?"

The boy scrambled up, rope in hand, staring. "How'd you do that?" he whispered. "Papa's been trying since dawn. She bit him."

"She's been hearing something in the nights," Emrys said. "Something bad. She thinks it's coming for her." They kept their voice low and matter-of-fact, the voice of someone discussing weather. "Let her smell your hand first. Don't look her in the eye. Tell her where you're going—she likes knowing."

The boy, to his credit, did exactly as he was told, and in less time than it takes to tell it, Daisy stood quiet with the rope over her neck, trembling but tame, her warm flank pressed against Emrys's side like a wall against the wind.

That was when Emrys noticed the woman sitting on the mounting block at the yard's edge, and realized she had been there the whole time.

She was perhaps forty, dressed in a good traveling coat gone bad at the cuffs, the kind of coat that had once belonged to someone with means and now belonged to someone with neither means nor shame enough to throw it away. Her hair was dark shot with grey, chopped short in a way that suggested she had done it herself with a knife and no mirror. She sat with her elbows on her knees and a clay bottle loose in one hand, and she was looking at Emrys with an expression Emrys had never been on the receiving end of before and could not immediately name.

Not fear. Not disgust. Something hungrier than either.

"Well," the woman said. Her voice was roughened by drink but precise underneath, the voice of someone who had once chosen her words for a living. "Well, well, well."

"Is that your mule?" Emrys asked, edging slightly so that Daisy stood between them. Old habit. Put something large between yourself and attention.

"The mule belongs to the boy's father, who is presently drowning his sorrows inside and has been since the stars went out, which is fair enough." She raised the bottle, considered it, and set it down in the mud beside her boot with a deliberate click, like a card player folding a hand she intended to win some other way. "What I want to know is what you did. Just now. To that animal."

"Nothing. She was spooked. I calmed her."

"Mm." The woman rose. She swayed slightly, corrected, and crossed the yard with the careful precision of the very drunk, stopping an arm's length away. Up close her eyes were bloodshot and startlingly sharp, the eyes of a hawk kept in a dark room too long. "I watched you talk to her. I watched her listen. I've worked around livestock my whole life, on survey trips, mapping runs—you learn what calming looks like. That wasn't calming. That was *conversation*."

Emrys's heart began to slam. Every instinct honed across nineteen years in Hollowmere screamed at once: *deny, deflect, disappear.* "I don't know what you mean."

"No," the woman agreed. "You wouldn't. You're very good at not knowing what I mean. I can see the practice in it." She glanced around the yard—at the boy leading Daisy toward the inn steps, at the townsfolk already losing interest, at the flat black sky overhead—and lowered her voice to almost nothing. "Three nights ago the heavens went dark as a closed eye. Two days ago I watched a man in the market here stop mid-sentence, mid-*step*, and stand smiling at nothing until his wife shook him, and when he came back to himself he'd forgotten his daughter's name. And yesterday I saw a poster nailed to the tollhouse promising the stars will return if good citizens report 'the gifted' to the Crown's men." Her gaze came back to Emrys and stayed there, steady as a surveyor's line. "So you'll understand why I'm not inclined to be delicate. You're one of them. So am I, in my way. And I think we should talk somewhere with a roof and a lock."

Everything in Emrys said *run.* Run the way they'd run from Hollowmere, run from the screaming night, keep moving until the world ran out. But Emrys was tired—tired down to the marrow, tired in a way three days of flight had carved into bone—and the woman's eyes held no cruelty in them, only a desperate, furious kind of hope, and hope was the one thing Emrys had not been offered since the stars died.

Besides, Sable said, hopping down onto the mounting block, *she smells like maps and bad decisions. My favorite combination.*

"You're drunk," Emrys said.

"Gloriously," the woman agreed. "And I'll be sober by sundown, which is more than the world can promise. Wren Calloway." She stuck out a hand. It was ink-stained to the second knuckle, old stains, ground-in, the mark of a trade. "Formerly Royal Cartographer to the Court of King Aldric. Currently unemployed, unlicensed, and unwelcome in four provinces. And you are?"

Emrys hesitated. Giving a name felt like handing over a hostage. But the woman—Wren—had already given hers, and there was a debt-shaped thing hanging in the air between them, and Emrys had been raised better than their secrets allowed.

"Emrys. Ashgrove."

"Emrys Ashgrove." Wren tried the name like a key in a lock, and something in her face flickered—recognition, or the ghost of it. "Ashgrove. From the valley? Hollowmere?"

Emrys went very still. "You know it?"

"I mapped its borders once." Wren's mouth twisted. "Or rather, I was ordered to map around it. There's a blank space on the royal surveys where your valley should be, a blank space older than the surveys themselves, and when I asked why, my mentor told me some places are drawn as absences on purpose." She picked up her bottle, looked at it with loathing, and hurled it against the far fence, where it shattered. The boy with the mule jumped. Wren didn't seem to notice. "I have spent six years asking questions like that, Emrys Ashgrove, and it has cost me my position, my reputation, and very nearly my neck. And in all that time I have never once seen anything like what you did to that mule."

* * *

The room Wren rented was at the top of the Wayfarer's Rest, under the eaves, reachable by a staircase that complained the whole way up. It contained a bed, a chair, a table buried under paper, and a window that looked out over the crowded square and the dead sky beyond it. Wren lit two lamps against the premature dusk—everyone lit lamps early now—and dug through the papers until she surfaced with a dusty bottle of something amber and two cups of wildly unequal size.

"Water for me," Emrys said.

"More for me. Sensible." She poured generously, drank half of it at once, and then sat down heavily in the chair and looked, all at once, ten years older and infinitely tired. "Ask me why I'm drunk. Everyone wants to ask but nobody dares."

"Why are you drunk?"

"Because I drew the map that got us here." She said it flatly, without drama, which somehow made it worse. "Six years ago I completed the great survey of the northern reaches. Every river, every ridge, every road from the coast to the mountains. It was the work of my life. And there was a region on it—an area in the high country near the old Spire—where my instruments refused to agree with each other. Compasses spun. Distances wouldn't hold. I noted it as an anomaly and moved on, because I was young and ambitious and the king wanted his atlas." She stared into her cup. "Two years later the king started changing. Everyone saw it and no one said it—he grew brilliant, then strange, then wrong—and the men he gathered around him weren't the men who'd been there before. And one day Crown officers came to my office and confiscated every copy of the northern survey. Every sheet. My mentor, Master Ellery Vance, protested. He was the finest cartographer in the kingdom. He vanished that same week. No body, no trial, no record. Just an empty desk and a locked archive where my life's work used to be."

The lamp flames leaned in the draft from the window. Down in the square, someone was singing—a wavering, defiant human song, the kind people sing to prove to themselves they still have voices.

"They erased the anomaly," Wren said. "Whatever is in that high country, whatever the Spire sits on top of, the Crown has known something was wrong there for years, and they didn't fix it, they *mapped it out of existence.* And now the stars are gone and the king smiles with too many teeth and posts rewards for people like you." She looked up. "I have spent six years drinking myself stupid because I helped make a map of a lie. And then today I watch a stranger in a borrowed shawl have a full conversation with a mule in a stable yard, and I think: there. There is the person I have been waiting to fail into."

Emrys sat with that. Outside, the singer's voice cracked and gave up. Somewhere below, Daisy the mule stamped once, quietly, and settled.

"What do you want from me?" Emrys asked finally.

"Passage. Partnership. Pick your word." Wren leaned forward. "I need to get to the capital. Into the palace archive, if the gods are generous, or at minimum to people who remember what was confiscated and why. I can't do it alone—I'm a disgraced drunk with a forged travel pass and a talent for making powerful men angry. But a gifted beast-speaker who can calm a panicking mule in a yard full of strangers..." Her eyes glinted. "Do you know how many animals there are in the capital, Emrys Ashgrove? Horses, dogs, pigeons, rats in the walls of the palace itself. Animals see everything and report nothing—to anyone but you. You could be eyes where I can't go and ears where I can't listen. In exchange: I feed you, I shelter you, I get you through checkpoints with my pass and my nerve, and when this is done—if any of us lives to collect—maybe we find out together what your gift actually *is*, because I promise you it isn't a curse. Curses don't make mules feel safe."

Emrys's hands had gone cold in their lap. Everything in them that had survived Hollowmere recoiled from the shape of the offer—it was exposure, it was risk, it was walking deeper into the exact darkness they had fled. Their mother's voice lived in their head still: *stay small, stay quiet, stay.*

But stay where? The valley's wards were broken. The stars were eaten. The world had grown teeth whether Emrys hid from it or not, and here, at least, was a person who looked at the thing Emrys had spent a lifetime strangling into silence and called it a *talent.*

"I want to know what happened to the sky," Emrys said quietly. "I want to know what's singing in the dark. My whole life I've heard things no one else hears, and I thought I was alone, and then three nights ago every animal in my valley screamed at once, and I'm done pretending I don't hear it." They met Wren's bloodshot eyes. "If you take me toward the answers, I'll take you toward yours. But I'm not your tool, Wren Calloway. Partner or nothing."

For a moment Wren just looked at them, and then she laughed—a rusty, disused sound, like a gate opening after years shut. "Partner," she said, and stuck out her ink-stained hand again, and this time Emrys took it.

From the windowsill, where she had been preening with elaborate unconcern, Sable said, *Ask her about the Spire. Ask her how far.*

"How far to the capital?" Emrys asked.

Wren's smile faded into something graver. She rose, swept a drift of papers off the table, and spread out a cloth-backed map—hand-drawn, worn soft at the folds, clearly a treasured survivor of the confiscations. Her finger traced the road east, through hills marked in careful ink, up into the high country where the rivers began.

"Nine days by cart, if the roads hold," she said. "Less if we cut north through the hill passes." Her finger stopped at a point in the mountains, where the map showed a small, precise symbol Emrys had never seen before: a circle with lines radiating from it, like a stone dropped in still water.

"And what's that?" Emrys asked.

Wren was quiet a moment. When she answered, her voice had dropped, as though the walls of the rented room might be listening.

"That's the anomaly," she said. "The blank place. The Spire. And here's the thing I haven't told you yet, partner—" she tapped the symbol twice, "—every bird and beast within fifty miles of it has fled. My survey teams lost dogs there. Horses wouldn't camp. And yesterday afternoon, while you were calming that mule, I watched every crow in Fenwick take wing at once and fly *west*, away from the capital, like something behind them had shouted." She looked up, and her eyes went deliberately, meaningfully, to the windowsill.

To Sable.

Sable's feathers lay perfectly smooth. She did not move. But Emrys, who had spent a lifetime learning to hear what was underneath, heard the crow's heart hammering like a fist on a door.

"So," Wren said softly. "Before we shake on anything else—why don't you introduce me to your friend?"
