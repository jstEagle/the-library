## 6. Roads Where Magic Follows

The first thing Emrys learned about the world beyond Hollowmere was that it never stopped talking.

In the valley, the silence had been a kind of mercy—or a kind of prison, depending on the hour and Emrys's mood. The wards had muffled everything, pressing down on the land like snow on a roof, so that the voices of beasts came through thin and small, as if heard through a wall. Emrys had spent a lifetime leaning close to that wall, ear pressed to the stones of their own skull, straining for the mouse's gossip in the thatch or the old complaint of the miller's donkey. They had thought themselves gifted. They had thought the gift was *loud*.

Three days out from Fenwick, riding north in a cart pulled by a mule named Barley who would not stop apologizing for existing, Emrys understood how wrong they had been.

It began at dawn on the second day, when the road climbed out of the river lowlands onto a long bare shoulder of hill. The trees thinned. The sky opened—starless still, a bruised grey lid over the world—and Emrys felt something give way inside their chest, like a door swinging wide in a storm.

*Oh,* said the grass.

Not one voice. Not a hundred. Every blade, every root-thread, every worm turning in its dark corridor of soil, all of it speaking at once, and beneath it the deeper voices—the oaks along the hedgerow groaning their slow centuries-old grievances, the crows stitching commentary across the sky, the earth itself humming a note so low Emrys felt it in their back teeth, a note of mourning so vast and patient that it did not even know it was grieving.

Emrys slid off the cart before they knew they were moving. Their knees hit the frozen mud. Their hands went to their ears, which was useless, which had always been useless, because the voices did not come through the ears—they came up through the ground, through the bones, through the soft wet animal darkness of the body itself.

"Emrys." Wren's boots appeared in their blurring vision. "Hey. Look at me."

"I can hear—" Emrys's voice cracked. "Everything. It's all—it's *talking*, Wren, the whole—"

"The land. Yes." Wren crouched, and her face, when Emrys could focus on it, was grim with a recognition she clearly wished she didn't have. "I've seen this before. Once. A hedge-witch my father hired, years ago, when I was small. She stepped off the ferry at Cael Harbor and fell down in the street just like this. She told me—" Wren paused, and her jaw tightened around whatever memory she was chewing. "She told me the world was like deep water. That some people are born holding their breath, and some people are born with lungs full of it already."

"It won't stop." Emrys curled forward. Somewhere above them Sable landed on the cart's rail and croaked—not words, not yet, just a raw warning sound, a crow's version of shouting a name across a burning field. "Wren, it won't *stop*, they're all sad, everything is so sad, the hills have been crying since the stars went out and nobody—nobody is listening—"

Something touched Emrys's shoulder. Barley's muzzle, soft and enormous, breathing hay-scented breath against their hair. *There now,* the mule murmured, in the slow warm way of his kind. *There now, small friend. Breathe when I breathe.*

And Emrys did. In. Out. The mule's rhythm was plodding and stupid and utterly steady, and Emrys clung to it like a rope thrown into floodwater.

When they could see again—really see, the world no longer doubled by the shining overlay of voice upon voice—Wren was watching them with an expression Emrys couldn't read. Pity, maybe. Or the particular hunger of a mapmaker confronted with terrain no chart had ever recorded.

"You're going to tell me," Emrys rasped, "that it gets quieter."

"No," Wren said. "I'm going to teach you to stop listening. There's a difference."

* * *

They made camp that night in the lee of a ruined waystation, its roof fallen in, its walls furred with frost. Wren built a small fire and then, instead of food, produced from her pack a battered tin cup, a length of cord, and a strip of dried hide.

"Hold this," she said, handing Emrys the hide.

It was ordinary leather, cracked with age. But the moment it touched Emrys's fingers, it *spoke*—the ghost of the ox it had been, the tanner's yard, the salt and lime and fear, all of it rising up like steam.

Emrys dropped it. "Everything has a voice. Everything remembers."

"Yes," Wren said. She picked the hide up herself without flinching, and Emrys realized with a small cold shock that she felt nothing from it at all. To her it was only leather. "Now imagine what you're hearing isn't one voice. Imagine it's ten thousand, all talking over each other, forever, and you were raised in a room where there was only ever two or three. Your gift isn't broken, Emrys. Your *filters* are. Back home, the wards filtered for you. Out here, you're drinking from the ocean through a straw the size of a pin."

"So how do I make the straw bigger?"

"You don't." Wren tied the cord to the cup handle and swung it slowly, pendulum-steady, in front of Emrys's eyes. "You build a door. Watch the cup. Only the cup."

The cup swung. Left. Right. Left. Its dented surface caught the firelight in a wobbling line.

"What am I doing?" Emrys asked after a while.

"Listening to nothing. Keep going."

"But I can't listen to nothing. I can hear the worms under us arguing about the frost line. I can hear a fox two miles east thinking about a henhouse. I can hear the *road*, Wren, the road remembers every foot that's ever—"

"*Cup.*"

Emrys stared at the cup until their eyes watered. And slowly—grudgingly, the way a stubborn mule accepts a halter—the voices receded. Not gone. Never gone. But pushed back, dimmed, as if Emrys had drawn a curtain across a window that had always stood naked to the light. Behind the curtain the murmur continued, vast and sorrowful, but it became weather rather than drowning. Something happening outside them rather than inside.

"There," Wren said softly. "That's the door. Feel where it sits?"

Emrys reached inward with their attention and found it: a boundary, faint but real, drawn in a place that wasn't quite muscle and wasn't quite mind. "Behind my ribs. Like... a held breath."

"That's your shield. Everyone's sits somewhere different. Mine—" Wren stopped. Swung the cup again. "Mine doesn't matter. What matters is that you can find yours when you need it. Now here's the hard part. You have to hold it while doing everything else. Walking. Talking. Sleeping. Especially sleeping, because sleep is when the door falls open, and if the land starts pouring in while you're dreaming you'll wake up having forgotten which thoughts are yours."

"How do you know all this?" Emrys asked. "You're a cartographer."

For a moment the fire popped, and Sable's feathers rustled on her perch atop the broken wall, and Wren looked very tired and very far away.

"My mentor," she said finally, "was a woman named Odile Vasse. Before she was spymaster, she was a surveyor, and before that she was—other things. She taught me that a map is a lie we agree on. Lines on paper saying *here ends the known*. But the world doesn't end anywhere, Emrys. It just gets louder. People like you have always existed. The Crown used to train them. Used to call them 'listeners.'" Her mouth twisted. "Then the Spire changed hands, or changed something, and listeners started disappearing, and the training manuals burned, and everyone agreed very hard to forget. So no. I'm not a teacher. I'm a woman with half a memory and a debt, teaching you the pieces I overheard through a door I was never supposed to be behind."

Emrys turned the cup over in their hands. Through the shield, its voice was a whisper now, a dead ox dreaming of clover. "Why help me? You could take Barley and go to the capital alone."

"Because in Fenwick you put your hand on a mule that three men and a whip couldn't calm, and it calmed." Wren fed a stick to the fire. "Because the king is wearing a face that isn't his, and I watched it happen and did nothing, and because if there's even one person left in this world who can hear what's true underneath all the lying—" She broke off. Shrugged, too casually. "Because someone has to draw the map of what's actually there. Might as well be me. Sleep. I'll take first watch."

Emrys lay down on the bedroll with the shield held loose but present, a curtain drawn to a crack, and listened—as much as they allowed themselves to listen—to the land grieving under the starless sky.

* * *

By the fourth day, the shield had become muscle-memory, and Emrys hated how much better they were getting at not hearing.

That was the treachery of it, they realized. The valley had taught them that silence was safety. Now the whole wounded world offered them the same bargain: draw the curtain, keep the door shut, walk through the grief of hills and rivers and slaughtered woods without letting any of it past your ribs. It worked. It worked beautifully. They could ride all day through a countryside that whispered and wept on every side and arrive at evening nearly rested.

Nearly. Because the curtain was never perfect, and things leaked through.

On the fourth morning it was a stone wall, ancient and tumbled, that caught them—a farmstead abandoned so long the forest had begun to reclaim it, and the wall's voice was a child's counting rhyme worn smooth by generations of small hands running along its top. Emrys reeled in the saddle and gripped the cart's rail and breathed until the rhyme faded to a hum.

At midday it was a river ford, and the water's voice was rage—cold, bright, furious rage—at what had been done upstream. Emrys didn't ask what. Some doors were better kept shut.

And at dusk, on a long empty stretch of road between dead orchards, it was the birds.

They came down out of the grey sky in a ragged spiral—starlings, hundreds of them, wheeling and reforming, wheeling and reforming, and their collective voice poured through the crack in Emrys's shield like smoke through a keyhole. Not words. Shapes. An image pressed directly into the soft tissue of Emrys's mind:

*A column of people on this same road, days ago. Hundreds. And walking among them, at the rear, figures that moved wrong—too smooth, faces like masks left out in the rain—and behind them, hanging in the air like heat-shimmer, a sound that was not a sound. A song. And the people turning, one by one, toward the song, smiling, smiling, walking away into the fields and not coming back—*

The starlings scattered. Emrys came back to themselves with Wren's hand fisted in their collar, holding them upright, and Sable screaming on the cart rail, and Barley standing stock-still with his ears flat.

"What did you see?" Wren demanded.

"People." Emrys's teeth chattered. "Refugees. On this road. And something *singing* to them, and they followed it, they smiled and followed it—" They looked up at Wren, and the question came out of them before they could stop it, small and terrible: "Wren. Is the singing why there's no one on the roads anymore?"

Wren didn't answer right away. She looked down the empty road, north, toward the capital and the dark, and Emrys watched her do the same arithmetic they had just done. Four days of travel. Empty villages. Abandoned fires, cold meals, doors standing open. They had both been telling themselves it was fear of the vanished stars. Fear made people hide. Fear was survivable.

But fear didn't leave supper rotting on the table.

"We make for the next settlement," Wren said at last, her voice flat and hard, the voice she used for maps and distances and other true things. "There should be a market town called Thistlebrook, two days on. If there are refugees, they'll pool somewhere. Water, walls, numbers." She turned and fixed Emrys with a look. "And you're going to practice that shield until it's iron, because if what those birds showed you is real—if there's something out here that hunts through *sound*—"

"Then it might hear me too," Emrys finished quietly.

Sable dropped from the rail onto Emrys's shoulder. Her talons pricked through the coat, deliberate, grounding. And when she spoke, her voice was stripped of all its usual acid, down to something old and careful.

*Little speaker,* she said. *Whatever sang to those people—I have heard it before. Long ago. Far from here.* A pause, and Emrys felt the crow's heart hammering tiny and fast against their neck, a wild bird's heart keeping time with their own. *And it is not finished singing.*

Far to the north, past the black hills, past the dead horizon where the stars should have been, something answered her.

Faint. Beautiful. Patient as the grave.

Emrys's shield held—but barely—and through the crack, for one terrible clear moment, they heard the land itself fall silent, everywhere at once, listening.
