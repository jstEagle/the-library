## 7. The Choir on the Wind

The camp announced itself by smell before sight—woodsmoke and wet wool and something underneath both, a sourness like milk left too long in the sun. Wren called it the smell of too many people with nowhere to go. Emrys called it the smell of fear that had stopped bothering to hide.

They crested the last hill above the river crossing as the light was failing, and there it was: a sprawl of tents and lean-tos and overturned carts spilling down the bank toward a bridge that had clearly never been meant to hold this much grief. Hundreds of people. Maybe a thousand. Cookfires dotted the mud like fallen constellations, and above them hung a sky that was still, after six days, wrong. No stars. No moon. Just a smooth black ceiling pressing down on the world like a lid on a pot.

Emrys had not yet gotten used to it. They suspected they never would. In Hollowmere, the stars had been so constant they were almost furniture—something you noticed only when you bothered to look up, which was rarely, because looking up meant standing still, and standing still meant being seen. Now Emrys caught themselves searching the darkness every few minutes, the way a tongue returns to a missing tooth. The animals had gone strange without them. Even here, even through the roar of human misery, Emrys could hear it: the foxes at the treeline whining questions at an empty sky, the rooks muttering to one another that the lights had been put out, someone must have put the lights out, who would do such a thing.

"Stay close," Wren said, pulling her cartographer's coat tighter around herself. She'd scrubbed the worst of the road off her face at the last stream, but she still looked like what she was—a woman running toward a city that had taken everything from her once already. "Refugee camps have rules. Unwritten ones, but they'll break your nose over them all the same. Don't stare. Don't ask about the singing."

Emrys stopped walking. "The what?"

Wren's jaw tightened, as if she'd said more than she meant to. "You'll hear it soon enough. Come on."

* * *

The camp received them the way a body receives a splinter—with suspicion, then grudging tolerance, then nothing at all. A woman with a ledger chained to her wrist assigned them a strip of ground between a tanner's wagon and a family of dyers from somewhere east; the dye-stained hands of the children left blue prints on everything they touched, including, eventually, Sable's tail feathers, which the crow bore with the affronted dignity of a duchess who has been splashed by a carriage.

"You smell like valley," Sable muttered from Emrys's shoulder as they spread their bedroll. "Grass and hiding. Half these people can smell it too, they just don't know what they're smelling. Keep your ears shut tonight, little warden. Crow's advice is free and worth every coin."

But Emrys had never once managed to keep their ears shut, not in twenty years of trying, and the camp at nightfall was like plunging into a river after a lifetime of puddles.

Outside Hollowmere, the gift did not wait politely to be used. It pressed in. Every creature within half a mile spoke whether Emrys wished to hear or not—the horses complaining of sore hooves, the dogs cataloguing a thousand smells into anxious poetry, the rats in the granary wagons conducting their small gray politics. And threaded through all of it, the humans, whose voices Emrys should not have been able to hear at all and yet somehow did—not words, never words, but the shapes beneath them, the way grief bent a person's inner weather. It was exhausting. It was like standing in a rain where every drop was someone else's feeling.

And under everything, faint as a hairline crack in a bell, there was the thing Wren had refused to name.

A singing.

Not from any throat in the camp. From everywhere else. From the dark fields beyond the firelight, from the river, from the direction of the capital, from the starless sky itself. A melody with no beginning that Emrys could find, sweet and slow and patient, the way water is patient with stone.

It was beautiful. That was the horror of it. Emrys lay awake on their strip of mud between the tanner and the dyers, listening to the most beautiful sound they had ever heard, and understanding—for the first time, in their bones rather than their head—that beauty could be a mouth.

* * *

Morning brought porridge, gossip, and the woman with the ledger, whose name was Marta and who ran the camp's ration line with the iron efficiency of someone who had decided that if the world was ending, it would at least end in orderly queues.

It also brought the survivors.

They came in ones and twos at first, then in a slow trickle all day—people drifting toward the big fire near the bridge where an old soldier named Corvin held court, telling what he'd seen, because telling it seemed to be the only thing keeping him upright. Emrys sat at the edge of the circle with a bowl of barley they had no intention of eating, and listened.

"We were harvesting late, that's all," Corvin said. His voice was flat as a road. "Late harvest, early dark, no stars to work by anymore. My brother Tam was three rows over. Then the singing started." He paused, and everyone in the circle leaned in despite themselves, the way people lean toward a fire. "Came out of the west. Out of the air. Not loud—you had to stop breathing to hear it proper. And it was—" His hands made a shape in the air, cupped, like holding something precious. "Gods forgive me for saying it, but it was lovely. Like my mother's voice. Like every voice I've ever loved, folded into one."

"What happened?" a dyer's wife asked softly.

"Tam stood up straight, mid-row, sickle still in his hand. Turned his face west. Smiled." Corvin's cupped hands closed into fists. "I called his name. He didn't— it wasn't that he didn't hear me. It was that my voice had become very small to him. Like a bird chirping far away. He walked west. I followed him for two days. On the second day we came to others, dozens of them, all walking the same road, all smiling, and ahead of them—" He stopped. Drank. Started again in a whisper. "Ahead of them the land was wrong. Grass growing in patterns. Trees leaning together like people whispering. And the singing louder, and Tam turning to me saying *come along, brother, don't you want to know the words?*"

The circle was silent. Somewhere behind Emrys, a horse shifted its weight and murmured, low and private, to another horse: *the two-legs are telling the story again. The story about the song. The song that takes them.* Emrys's skin prickled. The animals had a name for it already. Names were how beasts survived things.

"And you ran," someone said.

"I ran." Corvin stared into the fire. "And the shame of it is, part of me wanted to stay. Part of me wanted the words."

* * *

Through the afternoon, the stories braided together, each one a variation on the same terrible theme.

A ferryman whose daughter had stepped off his own boat into the river, smiling, and swum downstream against the current, singing back to the singers. A nun from a cloister in the hill country who said the singing had come into the chapel during vespers and harmonized with the hymn until the sisters couldn't tell which voices were theirs—and by morning, nine of them were gone, walking, and the chapel bell rang by itself at hours when no hand touched it. A boy of perhaps twelve who hadn't lost anyone but had heard it anyway, from miles off, and had stuffed his ears with wax and wool and still dreamed the melody every night, waking with it on his lips.

"It knows your favorite songs," the boy said, with the terrible precision of children. "That's how it gets in. It sings you the song you love best, but a little bit different, a little bit better, until you want to go find where the real one lives."

Emrys sat very still.

Because Emrys knew that trick. Knew it intimately. Had known it since childhood, though they'd never had a word for it before this moment, sitting in the mud with cold barley going gluey in their lap.

Beast-speech worked the same way.

When Emrys spoke to an animal—truly spoke, not the mimicry of sounds but the deep speech, the one that lived below language—they did not force. They *offered*. They found the shape of what the creature loved and held it up: the memory of warm fur, the promise of safety, the image of a full belly and a dry den. Every beast-speaker learned early that you cannot command a living thing. You can only sing to its wanting. Mother had called it *the gentle way*, and taught that it was holy, that it was the old covenant between the speaking and the unspoken kinds.

And the Choir sang to human wanting. It found the beloved voice, the mother's lullaby, the hymn, the favorite song—and it offered them back, perfected, until walking toward it felt like coming home.

Same root. Same grammar. Same gentle, awful craft.

Emrys set down the bowl. Their hands were shaking.

* * *

Sable found them at dusk, down by the river where the noise of the camp thinned and the water talked to itself over the stones. The crow dropped onto a deadfall beside Emrys and said nothing for a long moment, which for Sable was an event on the order of an eclipse.

"You figured it out," Sable said at last. Not a question.

"The Choir speaks beast." Emrys's voice came out strange, distant. "Or beast-speech and the Choir both come from somewhere older. The same root tongue. When I talk to a fox, I offer it what it loves. When the Choir calls a farmer, it offers him what *he* loves. It's the same sentence, Sable. Same structure. Same—" They groped for the word. "*Grammar.*"

"Mm." Sable preened once, twice, a mechanical motion, feathers without attention. "And now you're wondering what that makes you."

"I'm wondering what that makes *us*." Emrys turned to look at her, this sharp-tongued scrap of black feathers who had appeared at the valley's edge the week the wards began to fail, who had guided them to Fenwick, who had never once explained why a crow cared about kings or stars. "If the Choir speaks the old tongue—if it *is* the old tongue, twisted—then the wards around Hollowmere, the ones built to keep magic out—"

"Were built to keep *it* out," Sable finished. "Yes. Your elders forgot why the walls were raised. Walls always outlive their reasons. But whatever your valley's founders were afraid of, they weren't afraid of hedge-witches and mule-charmers, little warden. They were afraid of a song. And they built the wards out of the only material that could stand against it."

Emrys waited. The river filled the silence.

"Voices," Sable said. "Beast-voices. Why do you think the wards needed a valley full of creatures to hold? Why do you think they failed the same night the stars went out? The Choir didn't break your walls, Emrys. It unplugged them. It's been eating the singers, one species at a time, working inward for longer than your valley has had a name. Hollowmere wasn't a refuge." Her head tilted, one bright eye catching the last grey light. "It was a garrison. And you were never the prisoner."

Emrys's breath left them slowly. All their life—the hiding, the hunched shoulders, the mother's whispered rule of *never let them hear you*—and underneath it all, the valley had been a fortress, and the beasts its stones, and Emrys a soldier who had spent twenty years apologizing for carrying a weapon.

"That's why it wants me," Emrys said. "Not just any gifted. A beast-speaker. Because I speak the Choir's own language."

"Because you speak the Choir's own language *better than it does*," Sable said. "It learned the root tongue the way a thief learns lockcraft. You were born holding the original key. There's a difference between picking a lock and being the locksmith's heir." She ruffled, resettled. "Which brings us to the unpleasant part."

"There's an unpleasant part?"

"There's always an unpleasant part. Listen." Sable hopped closer, and her voice dropped into the register she used only for true things. "The refugees say the singing is beautiful. They say it offers you what you love. Fine. True. But here's what the birds know, and the foxes, and every wild thing between here and the capital: the Choir doesn't sing *to* beasts. It sings *through* us. We're not its audience, little warden. We're its instrument. Every crow that carries a message, every hound that hunts for it, every borrowed throat—it composes with what it takes." Her eye fixed on Emrys, unblinking. "So ask yourself. If it learns that a beast-speaker exists—a speaker who can walk into its song and not drown—what do you suppose it will offer *you*?"

Emrys looked out at the darkening water, at the camp's thousand fires reflected and broken on the current, at the starless sky pressing down like a held breath.

It would offer home. It would offer a valley where they never had to hide again, where every creature would turn to listen when they spoke, where the silence they'd kept all their life would finally, finally be answered. It would sing in their mother's voice. In Sable's. In the warm anonymous chorus of every animal they had ever loved.

And some traitor chamber of Emrys's heart, the one that had been caged for twenty years, would want to follow the sound.

"I need to learn more," Emrys said. "From the survivors. The ones who heard it longest and lived. If I'm going to face that thing, I have to know its music before it knows mine."

"Then sleep fast," Sable said, "because word travels faster than crows, and yours is a name the wrong throats would pay dearly to carry." She lifted off the deadfall, beat twice, and vanished into the dark—but not before Emrys heard her mutter, almost too low to catch: "*And the wrong throats are closer than you think.*"

Out beyond the firelight, at the edge of the fields where the grass grew in patterns no wind had drawn, something patient began, very softly, to hum.
