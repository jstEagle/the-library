## 8. A King's Empty Eyes

The night the king changed, Wren Calloway was mapping the palace sewers, and she had never been happier.

It was not a glamorous assignment. The Master of Charts had assigned it to her as punishment—*discipline for ambition, Calloway; you drew the eastern marches without authorization again*—but Wren had spent three days underground with her lantern and her calipers and her ink-stained fingers, and she had discovered that beneath the capital there was an older city, a foundation-city of arches and cisterns built by hands no record survived. Every corridor she measured that did not appear in any royal archive felt like a small theft from oblivion itself. That was what cartography was, she had told her mentor Odile once, when she was young enough to say such things without irony: *a war against forgetting.*

She surfaced near midnight, smelling of silt and lamp oil, carrying forty pages of notes on tunnels that officially did not exist. And it was there, in the service court behind the kitchens, wringing river water from her bootlaces, that she saw King Aldric come down from the old Spire.

She would remember the moment afterward with the terrible clarity of a thing witnessed too early. He came alone. No guards. No torch-bearers. The Spire had been closed for two generations—sealed by royal decree, its doors barred, its windows shuttered against whatever the kings of old had kept up there—and yet here was Aldric walking out of its shadow at midnight, unhurried, his boots silent on the frost-slick stones.

He stopped when he saw her. Smiled.

"Cartographer," he said. "You've dirt on your face."

Wren dropped into a bow so fast she nearly overbalanced. "Your Majesty—I didn't expect—" She fumbled for words. Kings did not wander. Kings did not notice cartographers. "Forgive me, I was surveying the—"

"The undercity." His voice was warm. Amused. "Yes. I know. I have always known what moves beneath my city, Mistress Calloway. It is one of the privileges of the crown." He tilted his head, and the moonlight caught his face, and something about it snagged in her mind like a fishhook—the smile was correct, precisely correct, every tooth where a smile should be, and yet it sat on him like a mask worn slightly too wide. "Tell me," he said, "do you love your work?"

"I—yes, Majesty."

"Good. Hold on to that." He resumed walking, past her, toward the lit windows of the keep. Then he paused, without turning, and said: "The stars are very bright tonight, are they not?"

Wren looked up. They were. Hard, cold, countless—a sky like scattered salt on black velvet.

"They are, Majesty."

"Mm," said Aldric. "For now."

She thought about that for eleven years afterward, in cells and taverns and border towns where the ale tasted of regret. *For now.* She had stood three paces from the moment everything broke, and she had bowed, and gone to bed, and written in her journal only: *Saw the King by the kitchens. Seems well. Tired, perhaps.*

Seems well. She could have laughed herself sick at it, some nights. Other nights she could not laugh at all.

* * *

The changes came slowly, which was the worst part. Slowly enough that everyone could explain them away.

In the first weeks after that night, Aldric seemed better than well—he seemed *lit*. He rose before dawn and worked until long past dark. He read everything: petitions, ledgers, the dispatches from the northern garrisons that no king had troubled himself with in thirty years. He summoned scholars and questioned them with a ferocity that left old men stammering and young ones glowing. He commissioned new roads, new granaries, a survey of the whole kingdom so ambitious that the Master of Charts wept with joy and tripled Wren's staff.

"He has found his fire," people said. "Aldric has finally become a king."

Wren said it too. She wanted it to be true. She was twenty-four years old and drunk on the golden age unrolling before her, and when the first wrongness came she filed it away like a surveyor's error to be corrected later.

The wrongness was this: the king stopped sleeping. Not slept badly. Did not sleep. His chamberlain mentioned it once, laughing, at a feast—*His Majesty says dreams are a tax paid by lesser men*—and the court had laughed, because the chamberlain had meant it as a joke, or had believed he did. But Wren, who stayed up nights over her charts, knew what it was to watch the candles burn down in the palace, and she had begun to notice that the light in the royal apartments burned every night now. All night. From dusk to dawn, steady, unwavering.

And the king's eyes, when she delivered the first sheets of the Grand Survey, were the eyes of a man who had not blinked in a week.

"Beautiful," Aldric said, spreading her maps across the council table. His fingers traced the coastline of the Sea of Glass, and Wren watched his pupils move—not reading, she realized. Not reading at all. Following the lines the way a man follows the bars of music. "Do you know what I see when I look at these, Mistress Calloway?"

"Geography, Majesty?"

"I see a song." He smiled that wide, correct smile. "Everything is a song. The rivers know their parts. The mountains hold their notes. Even you—" his finger lifted from the map and pointed at her, gently, almost fondly, "—even you are a line of music, cartographer. You simply haven't learned to hear yourself yet."

The room laughed politely. Wren laughed too. Later, alone, she could not stop her hands shaking, and she could not have said why.

* * *

By spring, the king had begun to change the heavens.

It started with the astronomers. Aldric dissolved the Royal Observatory—quietly, administratively, folding it into a new office called the Bureau of Celestial Accounting—and its star-charts, the great vellum atlases compiled over nine hundred years, were carried down to the Bureau's cellars for "recataloguing." The court astronomer, old Master Ilvane, protested. He was retired within the month with a generous pension and a house far away, and no one ever spoke to him again.

Then the king began commissioning new charts—or rather, *un*-charts. Maps of the sky with the constellations erased. Wren herself was ordered to prepare them: blank heavens, featureless dark, with only the sun and moon remaining, rendered in gold leaf.

"It's for the new calendar," the Undermaster told her, not meeting her eyes. "The King wants a sky without the old superstitions. A clean sky."

"A sky isn't a superstition," Wren said. "Those charts are nine centuries of observation. My grandmother's grandmother helped draw the Hunter. Erasing them isn't cataloguing, it's—"

"Calloway." The Undermaster's voice dropped. He glanced at the door. "Draw the maps. Burn nothing, question nothing, and go home before dark. Please."

She drew them. That was the sentence she would carry longest, through all the years that followed: *she drew them.* Forty-one blank skies, each one a small murder of the real one, executed in her own careful hand, in her own beloved ink. She told herself it was obedience, that it meant nothing, that ink on vellum could not unmake actual stars.

But when the first true darkness came—when the stars over the capital guttered out like snuffed candles, constellation by constellation, over a single terrible autumn—Wren lay awake in her attic room and understood that somewhere in the machinery of the world, her forty-one blank skies had been a kind of permission.

* * *

Her mentor vanished in the last week of autumn.

Odile Voss had been First Cartographer of the Realm for nineteen years. She was a dry, sharp-edged woman who drank too much coffee and loved Wren the way a whetstone loves a blade—with friction, mostly, and absolute fidelity. It was Odile who had pulled Wren off the streets at fourteen, caught her sketching the harbor fortifications with a spyglass and a stolen notebook, and instead of reporting her to the Watch had said, *Well, you got the bastions wrong, girl. Come inside and I'll show you why.*

It was Odile who first noticed what the Grand Survey was becoming. The new roads the king commissioned did not lead anywhere. They radiated—from the capital outward, yes, but also converging again, in patterns Wren only saw when Odile pinned sixty of her own maps to the wall of the map-room and stepped back, arms crossed, face gray.

"Look," Odile said.

Wren looked. Roads, bridges, aqueducts, drainage channels, beacon-towers. Individually sensible. Together—

"Together they're a sigil," Odile said quietly. "A country-sized one. Centered on the Spire." She turned. "How long has the king been having you draw without telling you what you're drawing?"

"He tells us what everything's for—"

"He tells you a *story*. There's a difference." Odile took down the maps, one by one, rolling them with hands that were not quite steady. "I served his father. I served his grandfather. Kings are vain, stupid, cruel, and occasionally wise, but they are always *human*, Wren. Whatever is signing those decrees now, I want to know what it is before it finishes drawing whatever it's drawing. Copy the master plans. Quietly. Bring them to me."

So Wren copied them. For six weeks she stole the kingdom's secret out from under the kingdom, tracing the sigil-roads onto thin paper hidden inside her coat, and every night she brought another piece to Odile, and every night Odile grew grayer and quieter, consulting books Wren wasn't allowed to see, sending letters by means Wren didn't ask about.

On the last night, Odile met her in the map-room after dark and locked the door.

"You're going to leave the capital," she said. No preamble. Her voice was flat and final, a verdict being read.

"What? No. I'm not leaving, the survey—"

"Is finished. Its purpose is fulfilled. Whatever the sigil was for, it's nearly done, and when it's done, everyone who knows will be either useful or dangerous." Odile pressed a sealed packet into her hands: heavy paper, wax stamped with Odile's personal mark, the little compass-rose she'd worn on a chain for thirty years. "There's money in here, letters of introduction, and the name of a woman in Fenwick who owes me and hates me in equal measure. Go north. Disappear. Do not write to me. Do not come back. Do not—"

"Odile, you're scaring me."

"Good," her mentor said. "Fear keeps the blood moving." For a moment—just a moment—the flint cracked, and Wren saw underneath it something she had never seen in fifteen years: Odile Voss was terrified, and trying with everything she had to hand that terror to her student instead of keeping it. "Listen to me. The king asked for you today. By name. He asked the Undermaster where you slept, what you ate, whether you had family." Her grip tightened on Wren's wrist. "Kings do not ask after cartographers, Wren. Something in that palace has noticed you, and I do not intend to let it keep you. Now promise me you'll go."

"I'll go," Wren whispered. "Come with me."

Something moved through Odile's face then—regret, or grief, or a decision already made. "Someone has to stay and misfile things," she said. "Go. North. Now."

That was the last time Wren Calloway ever saw her mentor alive—or however one should put it, since no body was ever found. Two days later the map-room was sealed by royal decree. Three days after that, Odile's townhouse was emptied by clerks of the Crown, her books confiscated, her name struck from the rolls of the court. When Wren, disobeying everything, crept back to the capital's edge and made inquiries, she learned that the First Cartographer had resigned suddenly, for reasons unstated, and departed southward, alone, at night.

No coach had been hired. No horse purchased. No ship's passage recorded in any harbor book.

Southward was where the Spire stood.

Wren stood at the north road gate at dawn, her stolen copies against her ribs, and looked back at the city—the towers, the smoke, the pale shape of the Spire rising above it all like a needle through the heart of the world—and she made herself a promise with the specific, corrosive gravity of guilt: *I will find out what I helped draw. And I will unmake it, if it's the last line I ever set down.*

Then the wards of Hollowmere fell, the stars went out entirely, and the world gave her the chance to keep it.

* * *

Eleven years is a long time to drink in border towns.

By the time Emrys Ashgrove walked into the Ferryman's Rest at Fenwick, leading a mule that had screamed itself hoarse, Wren had stopped counting the years and started counting the reasons she hadn't yet drunk herself into the river. The promise was one of them. It sat in her chest like swallowed ballast—useless, permanent, hers. She had the stolen sigil-maps still, wrapped in oilcloth under a floorboard, taken out and stared at on the bad nights. She had Odile's compass-rose pendant, which she wore under her shirt and touched like other people touch saints' medals. What she did not have was any idea how a lone disgraced cartographer was supposed to walk into a palace hollowed out by something ancient and take it apart.

And then, in the reeking yard of a third-rate inn, she had watched a stranger put one hand on a panicking mule's sweat-dark neck and *speak* to it—and the mule had answered.

Not calmed down. Not responded to a soothing voice. *Answered.* Its ears had swiveled, its breathing changed, and it had made a low sound that was unmistakably, impossibly, a reply—and the stranger had nodded as though hearing words.

Wren had gone cold all over, colder than the Fenwick wind, and then hot, and then—God help her—*hopeful*, hope arriving like a flood through a broken door, because eleven years of staring at stolen maps had taught her exactly one thing about the sigil centered on the Spire: at its heart, in the oldest layer, in the carvings the king's clerks had ordered scraped and redrafted and scraped again, there were figures. Human figures. Standing among beasts, mouths open, and beasts standing around them with their heads bowed as if listening.

Every scholar who'd examined the drawings had assumed the humans were commanding the animals.

Wren had spent eleven drunken years suspecting they had it backwards.

She bought Emrys a drink she could barely afford and asked questions she pretended were casual, and when the young stranger's story spilled out—Hollowmere, the wards, the night without stars—Wren had felt the ballast in her chest shift for the first time in a decade, and she'd heard herself saying the words before she'd decided to say them:

"I can get you to the capital. I know the roads, I know the gates, I know people who owe me more than they fear the Crown. And you—" she'd jabbed a finger at Emrys, at the impossible gift sitting in them like a coal, "—you're the first thing in eleven years that makes the trip worth the risk. So. Partners?"

Emrys had looked at her with those wary valley eyes, weighing her the way one weighs a bridge before trusting it, and Wren had recognized the look, because she'd been weighing herself the same way for eleven years.

She just hoped—if there was any hope left to be had—that when the weight came down, she'd prove stronger than she feared.

Because she had drawn the blank skies. She had copied the sigil. And somewhere south, in the dark beneath the Spire or the dark inside the king, Odile Voss might still be paying for it—and Wren Calloway intended, this time, to be the one who held the pen.
