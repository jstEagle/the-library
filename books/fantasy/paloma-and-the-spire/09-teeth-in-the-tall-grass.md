## 9. Teeth in the Tall Grass

The road south of Fenwick ran through country Emrys had never seen and could not stop listening to.

They had been three days out from the border town, following Wren's battered maps through a landscape that grew greener and stranger with every mile—hedgerows gone feral, orchards swallowed by their own fruit, stone walls crumbling into lines of gray teeth half-buried in moss. The world beyond Hollowmere was louder than Emrys had ever imagined it could be, and though Wren had taught them to fold their gift down small, to wrap it in layers of imagined wool until the land's grief became a murmur instead of a roar, there were moments when the shielding slipped. When it did, Emrys heard everything: the slow complaint of roots splitting stone, the old sorrow of rivers remembering mountains they would never see again, the thin, continuous keening of birds who no longer trusted the sky.

Sable rode on Emrys's shoulder through most of it, a warm weight of feathers and commentary.

"You're doing the thing again," she muttered, beak close to Emrys's ear. "The thing where your face goes empty and your feet keep walking but you're not *here*. I've seen rabbits do it when the hawk comes. It doesn't end well for rabbits."

"I'm listening."

"You're always listening. That's the problem. Listen with one ear. Keep the other one on the road." She ruffled her feathers, and Emrys felt her shift her weight—a crow's way of settling in for a long grievance. "In the capital we had a saying about people who walked around with both ears open. We said they died surprised."

"You were a spy," Emrys said. "You'd say anything to sound wise."

"I was an *excellent* spy," Sable said, with dignity, "which is why I'm alive to say it."

Ahead, Wren Calloway reined her mule to a halt where the road bent around a hillside dense with tall grass—summer-grown, waist-high, gone gold at the tips, moving in long slow waves under a wind that smelled of rain that hadn't fallen yet. She stood in her stirrups and shaded her eyes, and Emrys watched the change come over her face: the habitual sardonic droop of her mouth tightening into something older and more careful. The cartographer reading terrain the way other people read scripture.

"What is it?" Emrys asked.

"Grass," Wren said. "Too much of it. Somebody should have cut this stretch weeks ago—it's beside a road, it borders pasture, farmers around here cut every blade they can reach before midsummer. And look." She pointed with her chin. "No birds."

Emrys listened. The hedgerow to the east chattered with sparrows; somewhere behind them a wood pigeon went on with its idiotic cooing. But over the grass itself—the wide, waving, innocent-looking sea of it—there was nothing. No larks. No ground-nesting wrens. Not even the insects had voice enough to fill it.

"The land's avoiding it," Emrys said quietly. They hadn't meant to speak aloud; the words came up through them like water finding a crack. "It's not empty. It's *avoided*."

Wren looked back at them, and for a moment Emrys saw the court cartographer she must once have been—the woman whose maps had charted things no map should hold—before the sardonic mask slid back into place. "Right," she said. "We go around. There'll be a ford downstream; adds half a day, but I'd rather lose the daylight than whatever's making the grass feel guilty."

"It doesn't feel guilty," Emrys said. "It feels *held*. Like something's lying in it so still that even the grass has learned not to move wrong."

Sable's talons tightened on Emrys's shoulder, just slightly. A small pressure. A crow's way of saying *I agree and I hate that I agree.*

They turned off the road onto a shepherd's track that wound down toward the river, and for perhaps twenty minutes Emrys let themselves believe the detour had worked. The grass fell behind. The river appeared below, brown and unhurried, willows trailing their fingers in it. Wren began, cautiously, to hum—an old marching tune, tuneless, the kind of sound a person makes to prove to themselves that the world is ordinary.

That was when the horses on the far bank stopped grazing.

Emrys noticed it before they understood it: three shaggy ponies lifting their heads all at once, ears pricked toward the willow stand upstream, standing in the absolute stillness of animals who have decided, unanimously and without discussion, that something is wrong. Then the ponies turned and trotted away, fast, without looking back.

"Sable," Emrys whispered.

"I know," Sable said. "I know. Get behind the mule."

"*Down*," Wren snapped, already sliding from the saddle, one hand fisted in the mule's halter, the other dragging at the long knife she wore beneath her coat. "Both of you, down, into the—"

The willows exploded.

Emrys had heard the Choir-hounds described twice: once by a refugee at the camp outside Marrow Ford, in a voice that kept stopping and starting like a cartwheel over ruts, and once by Sable, who had described them in the clipped, bloodless shorthand of a bird who has seen too much and intends to survive it. Neither description had prepared them. The thing that came out of the willow stand was dog-shaped the way a nightmare is door-shaped—it had the suggestion of a hound, the long low line of skull and spine, the four limbs built for covering ground—but its surface moved wrong, as if the skin were a loose sleeve over something else, something that flowed and rearranged itself beneath. It was pale. Not white, not gray: pale the way a starved person is pale, the color drained out and used for something else. Its eyes were the worst part. They were human-shaped, set deep in the long hound's skull, and they were open, and they were aware.

And it was screaming.

Not with its mouth. The mouth hung slack, dripping, doing nothing at all. The scream came from inside it, from everywhere in it, and it came in a language Emrys had known since before they could walk—the beast-speech, the old tongue, the language of teeth and fear and flight—but broken, warped, sung instead of spoken, stretched across a melody that did not belong to any living throat.

*Run run run run run run run—*

Emrys dropped to their knees in the mud of the riverbank, and the shield Wren had taught them tore open like wet paper.

The hound's voice poured in.

*Run run run why won't you run I am running I have been running for days there is no out there is no out the song is in my legs the song is in my TEETH run run run I don't want—I don't want—I don't remember what I—MARA MARA my name is MARA why can't anyone hear my name—*

"Emrys!" Wren's voice, very far away. "Emrys, get UP—"

The hound came down the bank in three bounds, and time went strange. Emrys saw it all with terrible clarity: the mule screaming and wrenching free of Wren's grip, the flash of Wren's knife, Sable rising off Emrys's shoulder in a black fury of wings, screaming curses in crow-language that Emrys's gift translated into a stream of obscenely creative threats involving the hound's ancestry and eventual destination. The hound struck Wren's raised arm and knocked her sprawling, the knife spinning away into the shallows, and then it stood over her, jaws opening, and it did not bite.

It hesitated.

Because Emrys was speaking.

Later, Emrys would not remember deciding to speak. They would remember only the words coming out of them, in the old tongue, raw and unshielded, the first time in their life they had ever used their voice at full volume in the open air:

*I hear you.*

The hound froze. Every wrong-flowing muscle in its body locked at once. Its head swung around, and those human eyes—those aware, drowning, desperate eyes—found Emrys kneeling in the mud, and the scream inside it changed key.

*You—you HEAR—no one hears no one has heard since the Spire since the singing since they took my name and gave me teeth—hear me hear me HEAR ME I don't want to be this I was a person I had a garden I had a sister her name was—her name was—*

Her name was gone. Emrys felt the absence like a socket with no tooth: a shape in the creature's memory where a name had been, scooped clean, filled with song. The Choir took names first. The refugee at Marrow Ford had said that too, weeping: *it sings you until you forget what you were called, and then you belong to it, because a person without a name can't refuse anything.*

*Mara,* Emrys said, using the name the creature still clutched like a coin in a fist. *Mara. Your name is Mara. Hold it. Hold it and listen to me—can you slow down? Can you make the legs stop?*

*I can't the song is IN the legs—*

*Then fight it. One breath. One breath where you're not running. Do it for the garden. Do it for the sister whose name they stole—she's still yours even if hers is gone. One breath, Mara.*

And the hound—one breath, two, three—went still.

It stood over Wren trembling like a plucked string, and in that stillness Emrys felt what was underneath, and wished with their whole body that they never had. Because underneath the hound-shape, underneath the Choir's song wrapped around it like wire around a wound, there was a person. Faint, flickering, but unmistakably a person: a warmth, a wit, a stubbornness, a woman who had grown things and loved things and laughed at things, compressed now into a cell no wider than a scream. The Choir had not killed her. That was the horror of it, the thing Emrys knew they would carry for the rest of their life: the Choir did not kill what it took. It *kept* it. It kept everything, awake, aware, wearing it like clothing, and the taken screamed inside their stolen bodies forever, and the Choir called that harmony.

*Please,* Mara said, from the bottom of her cell. In the beast-speech the word had no ambiguity; there was no polite *please*, no social *please*. This was the please of a drowning person grabbing at a hand. *Please. I can feel her in here with me, the thing that wears me. If I ever get loose—if there's ever a moment—I'll tear myself apart before I let it use my hands again. But I'm so tired. I've been running inside myself for eleven days. Please. Don't leave me like this.*

"Mara—" Emrys's voice cracked, in the human tongue now, tears cutting tracks through the mud on their face. "Oh, Mara, I don't know how—"

The stillness broke.

Emrys felt it happen—felt the Choir notice the stillness, notice the *listening*, and come down on its own hound like a fist. The song surged. Mara's flickering warmth was shoved down, drowned, buried under wave after wave of melody, and the hound's body shuddered and reared back, and the slack mouth finally opened, and this time the scream came out of it in the air, audible, awful, a sound with a woman's grief inside a beast's throat.

*RUN RUN RUN RUN—*

"No," Emrys heard themselves say. "No, no, *Mara*—"

The hound lunged—not at Emrys, at Wren, who had recovered her knife from the shallows and was scrambling up the bank—and Sable hit it in the eyes, all talons and fury, and Wren did the thing that Emrys would later understand had saved all their lives: she did not retreat. She stepped *inside* the lunge, under the arc of those terrible jaws, and drove her knife up behind the hound's skull with both hands and all her weight and a sound torn out of her like something ripping.

The hound collapsed.

It did not die cleanly. Nothing made by the Choir died cleanly. For a long moment it lay in the shallows, limbs pedaling, the flow beneath its skin slowing, stuttering, and Emrys knelt in the mud with their hands pressed over their ears and their gift wide open despite everything, because they would not—could not—look away from what was happening inside it. They heard the song unraveling. They heard Mara rise up through the wreckage of it, through the last fraying wires, and for one instant—one clear, ringing, unbearable instant—she was entirely herself, a woman named Mara with a garden and a sister, dying in a body that had never been hers, and her last word in the old tongue was not *run*.

It was *thank you*.

Then nothing. The pale shape in the water went still, and as Emrys watched, it came apart—not rotting, not bleeding, simply loosening, the way a knot loosens, unwinding into strands and the strands into a fine gray sediment that the river took gently downstream, as if it had carried such cargo before.

* * *

Emrys did not remember getting to the willows. They came back to themselves kneeling at the base of the largest one, arms wrapped around their own knees, having vomited until there was nothing left and then continuing to heave anyway, dry and wracking, while the river went on being a river and the sparrows in the hedge resumed their chatter as if the world had not just shown them what it was made of.

Sable landed in front of them. Close. Closer than she ever sat, near enough that Emrys could see the fine barbs of her feathers and the flat black shine of her eye.

"Well," she said quietly. No mockery in it. None at all. "Now you know."

"Now I know," Emrys whispered. Their voice sounded borrowed. "It was a *person*. Sable, it was a person the whole time, and I talked to her, and she thanked me, and Wren—Wren had to—" Their stomach clenched again and they pressed their forehead against their knees. "I told her to hold still. I told her to hold still and that's when they noticed, that's when they came down on her, if I'd just left her running maybe—maybe the song would have—"

"Maybe nothing," Sable said. "Listen to me, hatchling, because I'm going to say this once and I don't repeat myself. You gave her three breaths that belonged to her. Three breaths in eleven days. Out here, in this whole godforsaken loud world, you were the only voice that reached into that thing and found the woman inside it. Whatever the Choir does next, however many of those creatures we meet between here and the Spire—that woman died knowing someone had heard her name." She paused. Her head tilted, that quick mechanical crow tilt, but her eye stayed soft. "Most people never get that. Most people die unheard. She didn't."

Emrys wanted to say something. What came out instead was a question, small and dreadful, the question that had been forming in them since the moment they had felt Mara's warmth flickering inside the hound-shape:

"Sable. The people the Choir takes. Are they all still in there? All of them? All the hounds, all the—the vacant-eyed ones from the camps, everyone?"

Sable was silent for a long moment. A crow's silence, which is longer than a human one.

"Yes," she said finally. "As far as I know. As far as I *ever* knew." And then, quieter, in a voice Emrys had never heard her use: "Which is why I follow you. Which is why I've followed you since the valley. Because you're the only one who can hear them, Emrys. And someone has to."

Footsteps in the shallows. Wren came around the willow trunk, pale, her coat sleeve torn and dark with blood where the hound had struck her, her knife still in her hand. She looked at Emrys for a long moment—at the mud, the vomit, the wrecked face—and Emrys braced for pity or pragmatism, whichever flavor of kindness Wren dealt in.

What Wren said was: "It stopped when you spoke. Did you see? Mid-lunge, it *stopped*. It couldn't finish while you were talking to it." She crouched, wincing, and met Emrys's eyes. "Eleven days, you said. It's been eleven days since Marrow Ford. They're taking people as they go, leaving these things along the roads like—like sentries. Like snares." Her jaw tightened. "And they're between us and the capital, Emrys. However many there are, they're between us and the capital."

Emrys looked up at her, and past her, at the river carrying the last gray traces of Mara away toward the sea, and felt something settle in their chest—not peace, nothing like peace, but a hard cold clarity, the same feeling they'd had the night the stars went out, when terror finished becoming resolve.

"Then we'd better teach me to do it faster," they said. "Next time, I want to reach them before you have to use that knife."

Wren held their gaze a moment, then nodded slowly, and turned back up the bank to check the mule. Sable hopped onto Emrys's knee, and together they watched the water run.

Far downstream, where the willows thickened and the light failed, something pale lifted its head from the reeds—and began, softly, to sing.
