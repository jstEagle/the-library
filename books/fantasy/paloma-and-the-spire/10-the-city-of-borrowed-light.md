## 10. The City of Borrowed Light

The capital announced itself before they saw it — a stain of amber on the horizon where the night should have been black, a glow like a wound that refused to close. Emrys stood at the crest of the last hill with Wren's cart creaking behind them and felt the light reach out across the fields and touch their face, warm as a hand.

They had not seen so much light since the stars went out.

"Vaelhar," Wren said, and the name came out of her rough, like something she'd swallowed years ago and never fully got down. She pulled her hood lower over her cropped hair. "City of a hundred thousand souls, give or take whatever the Choir's eaten since midwinter. Rule one: we don't talk about why it glows."

"Why *does* it glow?"

"Lamplight. Oil, tallow, whale-fat, anything that burns." Wren flicked the reins and the mule — a placid gray mare named Bess who had taken an immediate liking to Emrys and kept turning her great square head around to breathe on them — plodded forward down the road. "The Crown ordered every lamp in the city lit at dusk and kept burning till dawn. Ten thousand flames, every night. They call it the Vigil of Return." Her mouth twisted. "As if enough fire could shame the sky back into working."

Emrys looked at the glow a long moment. It was beautiful, in the way a fever is beautiful. And beneath it, faint but unmistakable, they could already hear what the light was for: fear. A whole city humming with it, ten thousand lamps burning against a dark that had teeth now.

Bess rumbled low in her chest, a sound Emrys felt through the cart boards more than heard. *Bad place,* she said, in the slow, deep way horses spoke, all weight and little grammar. *Bad-smelling place. Many hands. Keep your head small.*

"I intend to," Emrys murmured, and then caught themself, eyes darting to Wren. But Wren was squinting at the road ahead, and if she'd heard, she gave no sign. That was the arrangement between them, unspoken and fragile as spun sugar: Wren knew what Emrys was, had known since Fenwick, and neither of them said it aloud within earshot of strangers. In Hollowmere, silence had been a wall Emrys lived behind. Out here it was becoming something else — a discipline, a craft, a thing done together.

The road thickened with traffic as they descended. Farmers' carts, refugees' bundles, merchants' wagons, all funneling toward the gates like water toward a drain. And everywhere, walking among them, animals.

That was the part Emrys had not prepared for. In the valley there had been cows and chickens, Sable, the occasional indignant fox. Here there were dogs on ropes and dogs off ropes, cats flowing along fence lines like spilled ink, pigeons in their thousands wheeling over the rooftops, horses, mules, goats, a bear in a traveling menagerie's cage who watched Emrys pass with small, intelligent, miserable eyes. Each one of them a voice. Each one of them talking.

*—smell of burning, always burning now—*

*—the tall one took the baker's boy, took him right off the street, singing, he was singing—*

*—don't look at the posters don't look at the posters don't—*

*—new person new person new smell new person—*

It came at Emrys like weather, like standing in a river with the current pulling at every seam of their clothing. They gripped the cart's rail and breathed through their nose, counting, the way Wren had taught them on the road. *Find one voice. Hold it. Let the rest be rain.* They found Bess's slow heartbeat of a mind and held onto it like a rail in floodwater.

"You've gone gray," Wren said quietly, without looking at them.

"It's loud here."

"The city or the—" She didn't finish. She never finished that sentence outdoors. "We're nearly at the gate. Face like bread. Boring, patient bread."

Emrys arranged their face into what they hoped was bread.

* * *

The gate of Vaelhar was a mouth, and it chewed slowly.

Two queues stretched away from the arch — one for carts, one for foot traffic — and between them stood soldiers in the king's blue and silver, checking papers, peering into wagon beds, holding lanterns high even though it was barely midafternoon. Above the arch itself, where once there must have been a carving or a coat of arms, hung a vast painted banner: a stylized crown above a row of flames, and beneath it, in letters tall enough to read from the far side of the square:

**THE STARS WILL RETURN WHEN THE UNWORTHY ARE REVEALED. REPORT THE GIFTED TO YOUR DISTRICT WARDEN. THE CROWN SEES ALL. LOYALTY IS LIGHT.**

Emrys read it twice and felt something cold walk down their spine on small feet.

*Loyalty is light.* Someone had written that. Someone had painted it, raised it, paid for it. Somewhere in that glowing city was a room where a person had sat down and decided that the way to bring back the stars was to hunt children who talked to crows.

Beside the main banner, smaller notices fluttered on the stone, woodcut images above blocks of text. Emrys made themselves look. One showed a figure with wide eyes and a too-long shadow, whispering to a dog; the caption read **THE GIFTED SPEAK WITH ABOMINATIONS. THEY CALLED DOWN THE DARK.** Another showed a family at a window, pointing outward, faces serene: **REPORTING IS AN ACT OF LOVE.**

"They're blaming us," Emrys said. The words came out flat with shock. "For the stars. They're telling everyone the gifted did it."

Wren's jaw worked. "The Choir needs the gifted dead. Not because we're dangerous to it — because we can *hear* it. So it wears the king, and the king makes it law, and the law does its killing politely, with paperwork." She handed their travel papers forward as the queue shuffled them up to a soldier. "Smile at nothing. Answer only what you're asked. You're my cousin's kid from the valley country, come to work in the chandlers' quarter. Your name is Merrin Ash. I've already forgotten your real name and so have you."

"Merrin Ash," Emrys repeated.

The soldier who reached their cart was young, with wind-chapped cheeks and the haunted look of someone who had been told the dark was an enemy and believed it completely. He checked Wren's papers — her face did something complicated when he read the name Calloway, and Emrys saw her knuckles whiten on the rail — then leaned over the cart bed, lifting the canvas over their supplies.

Under the canvas, curled in a basket they'd bought in Fenwick, Sable opened one eye.

Emrys's heart stopped. They had argued about this at the last inn, Wren insisting a crow was innocuous, common as dirt, and Emrys privately terrified that Sable would say something — Sable had opinions about everything and a vocabulary that would shock a dockworker — at precisely the wrong moment.

The soldier looked at Sable. Sable looked at the soldier.

Then she fluffed herself up, half-spread her wings, and croaked — a perfect, ordinary, mindless crow sound, *caw, caw* — and hopped twice along the basket rim as if performing crow-ness for a wage.

"Just a scavenger," the soldier muttered. "Follows carts." He waved them through. "Chandlers' quarter's east past the bell tower. Curfew at dusk. Lamps stay lit, doors stay shut, and if you hear singing in the night—" he paused, and his young face did something terrible and brief, some grief he hadn't been permitted to process, "—you bar the door and you do not open it. No matter whose voice it is."

He waved the next cart forward before Emrys could ask what he meant. But the beasts near the gate answered anyway, a dozen small voices overlapping:

*—they took the miller's daughter that way, the singing, she walked out the gate smiling—*

*—her mother opened the door. Her mother opened the door and followed—*

*—no one comes back from the singing—*

Emrys held Bess's slow mind and looked straight ahead and did not hear any of it, outwardly, at all.

* * *

Wren's old lodgings were in the mapmakers' quarter, which turned out to be a narrow lane of leaning houses behind the Guildhall, and which turned out further to be watched. Emrys understood this before Wren said a word, because the moment they turned into the lane, the sparrows under the eaves went quiet in a way sparrows shouldn't — not relaxed-quiet, but held-breath-quiet — and a cat on a windowsill stared at them with its whole body rigid, and then, deliberately, looked away.

*Men in the house with the green door,* the cat said, in the bored, precise tone cats used for things they considered beneath their dignity but not beneath their interest. *Three days now. They sleep in shifts. They went through her papers. They wait.*

Emrys touched Wren's elbow, and steered her, gently, past the green door without breaking stride, murmuring, "Company. Three of them. Waiting on you."

Wren didn't miss a step. Ten paces later she said, softly, "Odile, then. If my rooms are turned, Odile's the only one left who owes me enough to hate it."

"Who's Odile?"

"A friend." A pause. "A former friend. A woman I betrayed by being arrested, more or less. Cartographers who fall from grace tend to land on the people who helped them climb." She turned down a narrower street, then another, and Emrys lost any sense of direction almost immediately. Vaelhar did not seem built so much as accumulated, layer on layer, lanes folding into courts folding into alleys, and everywhere the lamps — hanging from brackets, burning in windows, clustered on poles at every corner, thousands upon thousands of little fires eating oil and money and nerve.

And everywhere, the posters.

They were pasted on walls, nailed to doors, layered three deep in places, older ones bleached and newer ones sharp over them. **LOYALTY IS LIGHT.** **THE GIFTED CALLED DOWN THE DARK.** **HAPPINESS IS OBEDIENCE.** One, fresher than the rest, bore a woodcut of a hooded figure with a bird on its shoulder, and Emrys stopped breathing for a moment until they read the caption: **TRAITOR CARTOGERAPHER WREN CALLOWAY — INFORMATION LEADING TO CAPTURE REWARDED WITH 200 CROWNS.**

"They spelled cartographer wrong," Wren said, staring at it. "I spent eleven years in the Royal Survey and they spelled my title wrong on my own wanted notice." But her voice shook slightly, and Emrys pretended not to notice, because that was a kindness Wren would accept and pity she would not.

*Careful,* said a dog, a brindled cur tied outside a butcher's shop, watching them with liquid worried eyes. *The paper-men come at dusk. They tear down yesterday's papers and put up today's papers and they look at faces. They are learning faces. Do not be a face they learn.*

Emrys pulled their hood lower and walked faster.

* * *

Odile's establishment, when they finally reached it, was a tea house called The Steady Compass, wedged between a coffin-maker and a shop that sold nothing but lamp oil in quantities ranging from modest to ruinous. Inside it was dim, crowded, and smelled of bergamot and secrets. Every table was full, every patron bent over cups, and the conversation hummed at exactly the volume of people saying nothing important very carefully.

Wren spoke to the woman behind the counter — a broad, gray-streaked, formidably calm woman with forearms like a dockworker and eyes that catalogued Emrys in a single sweep and filed them somewhere permanent. She said nothing. She jerked her chin toward a curtain beside the counter, and Wren went through it as if the gesture were a key.

The back room was small and lined with shelves of jars whose contents Emrys chose not to examine. Odile closed the curtain, poured three cups of tea from a pot that had clearly been waiting, and said, "Eleven months, Calloway. Eleven months I've watched your name go from the guild rolls to the gossip rolls to the gallows rolls, and now you walk in with a stranger in a hood and expect tea."

"I expect tea and a floor and your discretion," Wren said. "In roughly that order."

"My discretion is expensive these days. There are men in your old rooms who report to people who report to the Spire itself. Do you understand what's happened to the city? Half my network has gone quiet — gone *vacant*, some of them. Found wandering, smiling, useless. The other half won't meet my eyes." Odile set down the pot harder than necessary. "So no, I don't expect you'll get my discretion cheap."

"I'm not here for me." Wren glanced at Emrys — a glance weighted with everything they hadn't yet agreed to say aloud — and Emrys realized, with a lurch, that they were going to have to decide right now how much truth this stranger got. Wren had told them, two nights ago on the road, that reaching the capital was only half her purpose; that inside the palace archives, sealed under her mentor's confiscated seal, lay the original surveys of the Spire, the star-charts the king had burned copies of, the maps that might show a way in. To get them, she needed Odile. To trust Odile, she apparently needed to gamble with Emrys's life.

Emrys made the decision before the fear could vote. They pushed back their hood.

Odile went very still.

She looked at Emrys's face — ordinary face, valley-pale, tired — and then at the space just above their shoulder, where Sable had materialized on the shelf-edge with the silent suddenness crows possessed and ordinary birds did not. Sable regarded Odile with one glittering eye.

"Well," Odile said at last, very quietly. "The Crown is offering two hundred crowns for a disgraced mapmaker, and I have sitting in my tea house the one thing in this city worth considerably more than that, drinking my tea." She sat down slowly. "You'd better start at the beginning, Calloway. And you'd better tell me why a beast-speaker — the first I've seen alive in twenty years — is sitting in my back room instead of running for the border while the running's good."

"Because the stars went out," Emrys said. Their own voice surprised them — steady, though their hands weren't. "Because the king isn't the king anymore. Because I can hear him, underneath. Asking for help." They wrapped their hands around the hot cup to stop the trembling. "And because I am done being silent in rooms where people need to hear the truth."

Something moved behind Odile's careful face — not warmth, exactly, but the recognition of a fellow professional. "Twenty years ago," she said, "there was another one like you. The Court called her a witch and burned her in Cathedral Square, and the crowd sang hymns, and I carried the intelligence that put her there, and I have drunk to forget it every winter since." She lifted her cup. "So. Beginning, middle, end. Talk fast. The lamplighters change shift at dusk, and after dusk, this city belongs to things that sing."

Outside, through the shutter slats, the amber glow of ten thousand lamps was deepening as the true dark came on. And along the eaves of The Steady Compass, in the gathering cold, the sparrows had begun, very softly, to sing — not birdsong, Emrys realized, blood turning to ice water, not birdsong at all — a thin, sweet, harmonized thread of notes that slid under the door and coiled around the room like smoke.

Sable's feathers flattened. Odile's cup froze halfway to her lips.

"That's new," Odile whispered.

Emrys was already moving toward the curtain, listening with the whole of their cursed, precious gift — because beneath the sweetness, beneath the harmony, one voice was fighting the song, ragged and human and familiar, calling a name.

Calling *Merrin*. Calling *Ash*.

Calling, beneath both names, the one only two people alive still knew.
