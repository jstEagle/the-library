## 11. Old Friends, New Prices

The city of Vell had never been kind to Wren Calloway, but it had always been honest about it. It did not pretend, the way the capital pretended, that its streets were clean or its welcome warm. Vell was a smuggling town built into a river gorge, all crooked bridges and stairways carved by hands long dead, and it smelled perpetually of wet stone, tar, and money changing hands in places where no lantern burned.

She left Emrys and Sable at a chandler's loft above the tannery district — the smell would keep away anyone with delicate sensibilities, which in Wren's experience meant anyone who might ask questions. The boy had wanted to come. Of course he had. They always wanted to come, the ones who'd spent their whole lives hidden and then discovered the world could hear them back.

"You'll be two hours," Emrys said. Not a question. The mule, Biscuit, stood beside them with her ears swiveled toward Wren like two furry satellite dishes, and Wren had to remind herself, again, not to say anything aloud she didn't want overheard by every animal within half a mile.

"Two hours," Wren said. "If I'm not back by dark, take Biscuit and go north to—"

"I know where to go." Emrys's jaw set in that particular way, the way of someone who had spent nineteen years being told to stay quiet and stay small and had finally, violently, stopped. "You keep treating me like luggage."

"Luggage doesn't get people killed."

"No," Emrys said quietly. "Luggage gets abandoned."

Sable, perched on the windowsill with her feathers slicked black as spilled ink, made a sound that was unmistakably, obscenely, a laugh.

Wren didn't answer. She pulled her hood up and went down the stairs into the gorge-smell of Vell, and she hated how much the boy had learned already about the shape of her guilt.

* * *

Odile Marchetti kept her offices above a funeral parlor, because — as she'd once explained to Wren over cards, years ago, when Wren had still been allowed to hold royal commissions and Odile had still been merely clever rather than dangerous — grief was the only door through which anyone in power would walk willingly. People came to funerals. People talked at funerals. And nobody, ever, questioned a woman in mourning gray carrying flowers up a back staircase.

The parlor was called The Quiet Rest. Its sign had been freshly painted. Its proprietor, when Wren slipped him a coin, looked at her face for exactly one second too long before jerking his thumb toward the stairs.

"She said you'd come," he said. "Said you'd come thin, and guilty, and smelling of horse."

Wren climbed the stairs wondering how much of that description Odile had invented and how much she'd simply deduced from knowing Wren for eleven years.

The office at the top was smaller than memory insisted. Shelves crowded the walls, stuffed not with books but with boxes — hundreds of them, labeled in a cipher Wren had once cracked for practice and now couldn't recall. A single window overlooked the gorge, and the light coming through it was the color of dirty pewter. Behind a desk built for someone twice Odile's size sat a small woman with silver-shot hair cropped close to her skull and eyes the flat gray of a blade that has been sharpened so many times it is mostly absence.

"Wren Calloway," Odile said. "They told me you were dead."

"They told me *you* were retired."

"One of us was misinformed." She gestured at the chair across from her. It was positioned, Wren noted, directly beneath a ceiling beam with a convenient handhold, in front of a window with a convenient drop. Even her hospitality was an escape plan. "Sit. Tell me why a disgraced cartographer walks into my funeral parlor after three years, wearing a guard captain's cloak that isn't hers, with river mud on her boots and a look on her face like a woman carrying something explosive."

Wren sat. "Because she is."

"Mm." Odile folded her hands. Around her wrists, Wren saw, she wore plain wool wrappings — the kind soldiers used to keep their sleeves out of their work. Odile had never done manual work in her life. Something had changed since Wren last saw her. Something had made her practical. "I'll save us both the dance. I know what happened in the capital. I know the king burned the star-charts. I know the Observatory was emptied and its staff either fled or went — quiet. I know the Crown is paying good silver for reports of 'the gifted,' and I know that whatever sits on the throne now is not entirely Aldric Vane, because I have seen him eat, and Aldric used to talk with his hands, and this thing talks with its teeth." She paused. "So. You're going to tell me what you're carrying, and then I'm going to tell you what it will cost, and neither of us is going to enjoy it."

For a moment Wren almost laughed. Eleven years, and the woman hadn't changed a syllable.

Then she thought of Emrys upstairs in the chandler's loft, listening to mice gossip through the floorboards, and the laugh died where it stood.

"The charts weren't destroyed," Wren said. "Not all of them. I kept the originals. The master maps of the old wardens' network — the spire-lines, the anchor points, the routes beneath the capital. The Crown thinks they burned with everything else." She reached into her coat and laid the oilcloth packet on the desk between them, and watched Odile's hands — those still, precise hands — actually tremble before they stilled themselves.

"Where did you get these?"

"My mentor hid them in my quarters the night before she vanished. Wrapped in my own rejected drafts. Whoever tore apart her office never thought to search the apprentice's rubbish." Wren's voice came out flatter than she intended. "By morning she was gone, and I was promoted into her position, and I understood that the promotion was a leash. I drew what they told me to draw for eight months. Then I got drunk at the Autumn Ball and told a duke's son that the king had smiled at me with too many teeth, and here we are."

"Here we are," Odile agreed softly. She unwrapped one corner of the packet, just enough to see the vellum edge, and closed it again with something close to reverence. "You understand what this is worth. Not in silver. In blood. Half the old families in the capital would burn this to stay safe. The other half would kill to control it. And the thing on the throne—" her mouth thinned "—would use it to finish what it started."

"That's why I need you. Not your network. Your *access*. There are sealed archives under the Spire — pre-dynastic records, from before the Choir's history was rewritten. My maps show the doors. They don't show me how to get past the king's new honor guard without dying. You can."

"And if I do," Odile said, "what do you intend to find down there?"

"A way to unmake it. Or a way to kill it. Whichever exists." Wren leaned forward. "There's a beast-speaker involved, Odile. A real one. First in living memory. The old carvings say only beast-speakers could bind the Spire. Which means only a beast-speaker can unbind it. That boy upstairs might be the only creature on this continent who can end this."

Something moved behind Odile's gray eyes — quick, hungry, gone. Wren had seen that look before, across card tables, and it had never once meant anything good.

"A beast-speaker," Odile repeated. "In my city. During a purge."

"He stays hidden. He's careful. He—"

"He's nineteen and he's been hiding in a valley his entire life," Odile cut in, "and you've brought him to the one place in the world where his existence is worth a fortune and his discovery is worth a title. Yes. Very careful." She rose, went to the window, stood looking out at the gorge with her back straight and her wrapped wrists loose at her sides. "Do you know what they're paying, Wren? For a confirmed gifted? Do you know what they paid me last month?"

Ice slid down Wren's spine. "You reported someone."

"I declined to report someone," Odile said, still facing the glass. "A girl of eleven who spoke to rats. Her mother begged me — came to the Quiet Rest, bought a funeral for a father who isn't dead, just to get in the room. So I moved them. North, over the pass, into free territory. It cost me four contacts and a favor owed to a man who frightens me more than the Crown does." She turned. "So understand me clearly: I am not the villain in this story. But I am also not a saint, and saints die faster anyway. If I spend what it takes to open the Spire for you — my access, my people, my remaining favors — and it fails, the Crown will come asking why. And when the Crown comes asking, I will need to give them something, Wren. Someone."

"No."

"You haven't heard the terms."

"I don't need to hear them. I know what 'someone' means, and I'm telling you no."

Odile crossed back to the desk and sat, unhurried, folding herself behind it like a spider returning to the center of a web. "Then let's say them anyway, so we both know precisely what we're refusing." She laced her fingers. "If the mission fails — if you are taken, killed, or if the attempt on the Spire collapses and the Crown learns a beast-speaker was involved — you deliver the gifted boy to the palace guards. Alive. Unharmed. As a gift, freely given, by the spymaster Odile Marchetti, loyal servant of the Crown. His value buys my survival and my network's. Perhaps it even buys yours, if you play contrite enough."

"He'd be executed. Or worse — they'd *use* him. You said yourself the thing on the throne would want a beast-speaker. You'd be delivering him to the Choir."

"Yes," Odile said simply. "I would."

The word hung in the dusty air between them. Downstairs, faintly, a bell tolled for some stranger's funeral, and Wren thought absurdly of Emrys sitting in the loft telling stories to the mice, trusting her, following her south on nothing but a promise and a crow's bad advice.

"There has to be another price," Wren said.

"There isn't. Everything else I own is already spoken for." Odile's voice softened by perhaps a single degree, which from her was a flood of tenderness. "You think I don't know what I'm asking. You think I haven't priced a soul before and hated myself after. Wren — the alternative is that I refuse, you go to the Spire with your maps and your courage and your boy, and you die in the first courtyard, and the maps fall into the Crown's hands, and the Choir wins by Thursday. I am not offering you a good bargain. I am offering you the only bargain that leaves anyone alive afterward."

"And if it succeeds?" Wren asked. "If he does it — if the Choir is torn out of the king —"

"Then the debt burns. I'll feed the contract to the stove myself and pour you wine while it burns." Odile held up one finger. "*If.* Success forgives. Failure pays. That is the whole architecture of my profession, and I won't insult you by pretending otherwise."

Wren looked at the packet on the desk. At the window and its convenient drop. At the woman she had trusted once, in another life, with things smaller and easier than souls.

"If I sign this," she said slowly, "and it comes to it — if we fail and they come for him — I want it written that I deliver him. Not you. Me. You keep your hands clean and your network intact, and I walk him to the gates myself."

Odile raised an eyebrow. "Why? Sentiment?"

"Guilt." Wren bared her teeth in something that wasn't quite a smile. "And because if it ever comes to that moment, I want to be standing next to him. Whatever happens, I want him to hear it from me and not from a stranger with a writ. He's spent his whole life being handed over by silence, Odile. If it must happen, he'll at least get the dignity of hearing the truth out loud."

For a long moment the spymaster studied her, and something almost human flickered across the sharpened-blade face — pity, maybe, or its professional cousin.

"That's the stupidest clause I've ever included in a contract," Odile said, "and also the only reason I believe you'll honor it." She drew a sheet of paper from a drawer, dipped a pen, and wrote for a long time in her small, vicious handwriting. When she finished she turned the page around and tapped the final line. "Sign. Full name. The Crown respects paperwork; it's the one religion they have left."

Wren read it twice. Every line was exactly as Odile had spoken it, plus one sentence near the end she hadn't mentioned: *the undersigned attests the gifted individual acts of his own will and knowledge.* A fig leaf. A legal fiction so thin it was nearly transparent — Emrys knew nothing of this, would know nothing unless Wren told him — but Odile had included it, and Wren understood why. It gave Wren one thread of honesty to cling to. One technical truth she could offer him, someday, if her nerve failed.

It was not enough. It was not nearly enough. She signed anyway.

The pen scratched. Ink dried. Somewhere below, the funeral bell finished tolling, and the silence it left behind felt like a held breath.

"It's done," Odile said, sliding the contract into a box, closing the lid, turning a key. Already her manner had shifted — brisk, operational, the transaction sealed and shelved. "The archive door you want is the Ossuary Gate, east foundation, beneath the old chapel. The honor guard rotates at the third bell after midnight. I can put you inside with a forged work-crew writ, but only for one night, and only before the new moon — after that the Spire doubles its watch for the ritual. Whatever your beast-speaker intends to do down there, he does it in six days."

"Six days," Wren repeated.

"Five, now. It's past noon." Odile stood and offered her hand, and Wren took it, and the grip was brief and dry and cold as a coin. "One more thing. Bring him to me tomorrow. Both of you. Before I commit my people, I need to look at this beast-speaker myself and judge whether he's a weapon or a liability."

"He's neither. He's a person."

"Everything is a weapon or a liability, Wren. Persons especially." Odile opened the door and the pewter light of the stairwell fell across her face, carving it into planes. "Noon tomorrow. Come alone with him — leave the crow. Crows make my people nervous, and nervous people talk."

* * *

Wren climbed the chandler's stairs slowly, each step heavier than the last, rehearsing and discarding sentences. *I've made arrangements. There are conditions. If everything goes wrong—*

Every version sounded like a confession, because it was.

She found Emrys on the loft's narrow balcony, leaning against the rail, watching the gorge. Sable was gone — off somewhere in the rooftops, doing whatever crows did. The late light caught the boy's profile: the stubborn chin, the tired eyes, the ink-stain of exhaustion under them that hadn't faded since the night the stars went out. On the railing beside his hand, a pigeon sat uncharacteristically calm, head cocked, listening to whatever he was murmuring. When Wren stepped onto the balcony the pigeon startled and clattered away, and Emrys turned.

"You're early," he said. "That means it went well or terribly. With you it's hard to tell." His gaze narrowed, reading her face the way he apparently now read everything — the whole loud grieving world. "Wren. What did she ask for?"

And there it was. The moment. The thread of honesty Odile had so generously woven into the contract, fraying in Wren's hands.

She thought about lying. God, she thought about it — the ease of it, the mercy of it, *she drove a hard bargain but it's settled, we're in.*

Instead Wren gripped the railing until her knuckles went white and heard herself say, "We need to talk about what happens if we fail."

Emrys went very still. Beside him, on the roof ridge across the alley, a black shape dropped out of the sky and landed — Sable, feathers ruffled, head tilted, one bright eye fixed on Wren with an expression that looked unsettlingly like she had already heard everything and had opinions.

"Start talking," Emrys said quietly.

And Wren, who had signed her loyalty away an hour ago with betrayal folded neatly in reserve, began.
