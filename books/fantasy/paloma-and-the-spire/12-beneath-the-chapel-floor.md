## 12. Beneath the Chapel Floor

The chapel above was a lie told in gilt and candlelight.

Emrys had understood that within moments of stepping inside—understood it the way they understood weather before rain, a pressure behind the teeth. The royal chapel of Vael Astra gleamed with fresh gold leaf on every arch, its windows hung with new glass in colors no sky had ever worn, and at its heart stood a statue of King Aldric rendered three feet taller than any man could honestly be, his marble hands raised in blessing over a congregation that knelt with the rigid, careful stillness of people who had learned that worship was safer when performed correctly.

But beneath their feet, through a door behind the altar that Odile's key opened without ceremony, the air changed.

"Mind the stairs," Odile had said, handing Emrys a lantern that burned with a flame the color of old honey. "They're older than the palace. Older than the city, if the masons are to be believed. And mind something else besides." Her eyes had been flat and silver in the lamplight, the eyes of a woman who traded in truths the way other merchants traded in grain—one measure at a time, always keeping back the best of the harvest. "What's down there has been waiting a long time for someone who could read it. Don't let it read you first."

Now Emrys descended alone, because some doors only admitted one.

The stairs were cut from black stone that drank the lantern light rather than reflecting it, each step worn into a shallow bowl by feet long since turned to dust. Sable rode on Emrys's shoulder, uncharacteristically silent, her talons gripping tight enough to dimple the wool of their coat. Emrys had asked her once already if she wanted to wait above. She had answered by pressing her beak, very gently, against their ear—a gesture she reserved for things too large for words.

*Deeper,* she had said instead. *It remembers deeper.*

The gift had been growing louder all the way down. That was the thing about the capital that Emrys still hadn't learned to bear: there was nowhere quiet. In Hollowmere, the wards had pressed the world's voice down to a murmur, and Emrys had spent twenty years mistaking that silence for peace. Here, even under the weight of stone and the Choir's smothering presence, the land itself spoke—a low, continuous grief like water moving through the roots of a drowned forest. The city above sat on the bones of something much older, and the bones were not asleep.

At the bottom of the stairs, the passage opened into darkness that the lantern could only argue with, not defeat.

Emrys raised the light.

Pillars. Dozens of them, marching off into the black in rows too regular for nature and too ancient for the kingdom's memory, each one carved from the same light-swallowing stone as the stairs. The ceiling was lost somewhere overhead; the floor was smooth as frozen water. And on every pillar, covering every surface, ran carvings.

"Oh," Emrys said aloud, and their voice came out small and strange in the vast dark. "Oh, this is—"

*I know,* Sable said softly. *I know.*

The carvings showed people.

Not kings. Not saints in stiff robes with haloed heads. People—hundreds of them, thousands, rendered in a style so lifelike that Emrys's skin prickled with the certainty that the artists had worked from life. Farmers with calloused hands raised palm-outward toward the sky. Shepherds standing among flocks whose mouths were open mid-song. Children kneeling beside wolves, beside bears, beside great serpentine shapes that could only be river-drakes, their small hands resting on the animals' flanks in unmistakable intimacy. And every figure—every single one—had its mouth open, and from every mouth flowed lines: fine, deliberate channels cut into the stone, spiraling outward, joining, braiding together into rivers of speech that wound across the pillars and along the floor and up toward the vanished ceiling, until the whole crypt became a single vast diagram of voices converging.

Emrys walked between the pillars with the lantern held high, and the grief in the stones grew louder with every step, and they began, slowly, to understand what they were looking at.

Because they knew those mouth-shapes. They knew the set of the jaw, the angle of the throat. They had seen that exact posture in their own reflection a thousand times—in the stable mirror, in the black water of the mill pond—the unconscious configuration of a face speaking to something that was not human.

*These are speakers,* Emrys said, and the thought moved through them like cold water through cloth. *Sable. These are all—*

*Speakers,* Sable agreed. *Beast-speakers. Hundreds of years of them. Maybe thousands.* Her feathers were sleeked down tight against her body, which Emrys had learned meant fear worn as discipline. *I never saw this place. But I heard of it, once, in the capital, before—before everything. The scholars called it the Undercroft. They said it was sealed after the Spire was built. They said it was heresy.*

"Heresy," Emrys repeated, and laughed, and the laugh came out cracked. "It's a record. It's—" They stopped before a pillar wider than the others, its surface dense with figures arranged in rings, and the lantern light found something at its center that stopped their breath.

A tower.

Carved in meticulous detail, rising through ring after ring of figures: a spire, slender and terrible, its peak crowned with an open mouth of its own—not a human mouth but something else, something like a wound in the shape of a voice—and around its base, chained to it by those same channels of carved speech, stood a circle of figures with their arms linked. Holding it down. Holding it *shut*. The channels ran from their mouths into the tower's foundations like roots running into soil, and where the roots met stone, the sculptor had cut a pattern Emrys recognized with a jolt that went through them like a bell struck behind the ribs.

The pattern was the ward-script. The same interlocking marks that ringed Hollowmere. The same signs their grandmother had chalked on doorposts, that the elder traced in ash at the valley's border, that Emrys had grown up believing were as old and anonymous as the hills themselves.

Here, they had names. Here, they had faces.

"Sable," Emrys whispered. "These are the wardens. These are the people who made the wards."

*Read it,* Sable said. *All of it. Before we go up again, read all of it.*

* * *

They worked through the night.

Wren came down at some point—Emrys heard her boots on the stairs, heard her low whistle of astonishment echo off into the dark and get eaten—and then she was beside them, sketching by lantern light with the fierce, fast precision that came over her whenever maps were involved, copying the layout of the crypt onto paper, copying the ward-patterns, muttering to herself about sightlines and sightlines' absence and how in the name of every dead cartographer had a structure this size been built directly beneath a royal chapel without appearing on a single survey she'd ever touched.

"It did appear," Emrys said. "On the oldest ones. I'd bet anything. It just got... removed."

"Removed." Wren's jaw tightened. She didn't argue. She had spent two days now listening to Emrys describe what the beasts of the city whispered, and she had stopped being surprised by the shape of the Crown's lies. "My mentor had a fragment. An old survey, pre-Aldric. There was a notation he couldn't make sense of—*sub sacellum, vox*—beneath the chapel, the voice. He thought it was a transcription error." Her pencil paused. "He disappeared three weeks after he showed it to me."

Emrys looked at her. Wren kept her eyes on the page.

"I'm sorry," Emrys said.

"So am I." She resumed sketching. "Keep reading. If your gift can pull meaning out of these walls faster than I can copy them, then for once in my life I'm glad the world is loud."

And the world *was* loud. Louder here than anywhere Emrys had ever stood. The carvings were not merely pictures—Emrys understood that now, understood it the way they understood the mule in Fenwick, the hound in the tall grass, the grieving land itself. The sculptors had cut their work along the grain of something living. The stone remembered. When Emrys laid both palms against a pillar and let the gift open—not wide, never wide, just a crack, a keyhole's width—the pillar *spoke*.

Not in words. In impressions, in images pressed into their mind like flowers pressed into a book:

*A hill country green to the horizon. Speakers walking openly among beasts, and no one hiding, no one afraid. A tower raised by something that sang from below the world, singing fields barren and children hollow, until the speakers came—generations of them, whole villages giving their voices—to bind it. Not destroy it. Bind it. Because destruction was beyond them, and binding was not, and binding required singers, and singers required successors, and so the wards were woven into bloodlines and taught mother to daughter, father to son, valley to valley, a chain of hidden choirs holding down one buried song.*

*Then fire. Then edicts. Then the word "witch" spoken in a king's voice, and speakers dragged from their valleys, and the chain breaking link by link, valley by valley, until only the far places remembered—and then even the far places forgot, because forgetting was safer, because silence was survival, and a secret kept too well becomes indistinguishable from a lie.*

Emrys pulled their hands from the stone and sat down hard on the frozen-smooth floor and shook.

Wren crouched beside them. She didn't touch them—she had learned that—but she stayed, and after a while she said quietly: "You saw it. What it was for."

"They weren't witches." Emrys's voice came out scraped raw. "Wren, we weren't—we were never *cursed*. My whole life, my mother teaching me to swallow it, the elder saying the gift was a sickness the wards kept contained—it was a *duty*. We were the last links of a chain and nobody, nobody knew, we were guarding a prison and calling our own voices a disease—" Their breath hitched. "Hollowmere isn't a refuge. It's a garrison. It's the last post of a war everyone forgot we were fighting."

"And the wards failing," Wren said slowly, and Emrys watched the cartographer's mind move across the idea the way her pencil moved across paper, laying down contours, "wasn't decay. It was relief of duty. The last warden line thinning out. The prison door swinging on rusted hinges."

"The Choir didn't break in." Emrys stared up into the dark where the ceiling should have been. "It was already here. Under everything. It just finally had the strength to stand up."

Sable shifted on their shoulder. When she spoke, her voice was very quiet, stripped of all its usual blade-edge irony. *That is why it went for the stars first, little speaker. Not hunger. Well—not only hunger. It was tearing down the witnesses. The old bindings were written in starlight. Every star it swallowed took a word out of the lock.*

Emrys closed their eyes. Behind their lids, the carved circle of linked figures held the tower down, mouths open, voices flowing, and among them—now that they looked with the gift's inner sight rather than their eyes—they could see that the figures were varied. Old and young. Every manner of dress, every region's features. Speakers had come from everywhere, once. Had been *called* from everywhere. And at the head of the circle, larger than the rest, hands raised highest, was a figure whose face had been deliberately, carefully defaced—chiseled away sometime long after the carving was made, by someone who wanted that one person erased from the record.

Someone with a crown, perhaps. Or someone serving one.

"Wren," Emrys said. "Look at the leader of the binding circle."

Wren lifted her lantern. A long silence.

"They chiseled off the face," she said at last. "But not the crown-band. Look—the circlet's still there, cut into the hairline. Whoever led the first binding was royalty." Her pencil hovered. "Or became royalty after. Either way, someone in your history wore a crown, and someone in mine decided the kingdom shouldn't remember it."

"A king or queen who could speak to beasts," Emrys murmured, "bound the Spire with a choir of speakers, and founded the wards—and then the throne spent centuries hunting their descendants as witches." The full shape of it settled over them, heavy and cold and strangely steadying, the way deep water is steadying once you stop fighting it. "The Choir didn't corrupt Aldric out of nowhere. It's finishing an old war. It's erasing the last of the erasers."

*And it keeps the king alive inside itself,* Sable said, *because a king's voice is a master key to a kingdom's obedience. And maybe—* she hesitated, and Emrys felt the hesitation like a physical touch— *maybe because the blood still matters. Even corrupted. Even hollowed. The Choir wears the warden-king's body because the warden-king's body still fits the lock.*

Emrys went still.

The thought assembled itself in the dark of the crypt, piece by piece, the way Wren assembled coastlines from scattered soundings: the carvings, the defaced face, the crown-band, the Choir's strange patience, its possession of Aldric specifically rather than any easier vessel. The gift was a key—Odile's scholars had said so, half-scoffing. But keys turned locks. And locks had sides. And if the binding had been wrought by a speaker wearing a crown—

"Then it can be unwrought the same way," Emrys breathed. "By a speaker. By *this* speaker, maybe, if the blood runs true enough. Not killing him. Unbinding him. Freeing the king and re-binding the Choir in the same act, the way it was done the first time—"

"That's a very large conclusion to draw from a wall full of statues," Wren said. But her hand had gone still on the page, and her eyes, when they met Emrys's, held the particular bright terror of a woman watching hope arrive with its debts already itemized.

Before Emrys could answer, Sable's grip tightened on their shoulder—hard, sudden, painful—and her head snapped toward the far end of the crypt, toward the darkness beyond the last row of pillars, where the lantern light gave up entirely.

*Emrys.* Her voice had gone flat and fast, the voice she used for hawks and for worse things. *Something down here is awake. Something down here just started listening to us.*

Emrys reached out with the gift, careful, a crack, a keyhole's width—and felt it at once: a presence coiled in the deepest dark, vast and patient and *attentive*, and threaded through its attention, faint as a drowning man's hand breaking water, a human voice.

Faint. Familiar. Begging.

It was coming from below the crypt. From beneath even this.

And it was singing Emrys's name.
