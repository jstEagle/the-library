## 13. The Voice Inside the Crown

They dressed Emrys in borrowed clothes for the audience.

The garments came from Odile's stores—good wool, court-cut, a doublet the color of wet slate that made Emrys look like someone who belonged in a palace antechamber rather than a valley where the loudest sound on most days was the river arguing with its stones. A woman with quick fingers and no conversation buttoned them into it, tugging seams flat as though she were packing a parcel for delivery.

"You'll stand when they tell you to stand," Odile had said that morning, in her office above the map room, where the walls were papered in charts of a sky that no longer existed. "You'll kneel when they tell you to kneel. You will not speak unless spoken to, and if you are spoken to, you will say less than you think. The king's court is a machine for finding the odd tooth in an even smile. Do not be the odd tooth."

"And if he speaks to me?" Emrys had asked.

Odile's eyes, pale and unhurried, had rested on them a moment too long. "Then you'll have already failed at something. Pray it's only small."

Wren had said nothing through all of it. She stood by the window with her arms crossed, watching the lamplighters work their way up the Processional Way below, and Emrys had learned enough of her silences by now to know this one was fear wearing a practical coat. It was Wren who had gotten them the audience—not through Odile's channels but through her own, an old debt called in from a chamberlain whose career Wren had once saved with a corrected survey line—and it was Wren who would hang if it went wrong. Not the rope. Something quieter and worse: the confiscation of everything she had left, which was to say her name, her credentials, and the last shreds of her usefulness to anyone.

"Remember," Wren said now, straightening Emrys's collar with two brisk tugs that were almost gentle, "you're a hedge-witch's apprentice from the eastern marches, brought in because your mistress heard the Crown was seeking those with... sensitivities. You know nothing. You've seen nothing. Your whole life has been mud and mutton."

"That part's true, at least."

"Good. Truth is the best mortar for lies. Use plenty of it." Her hands paused at Emrys's throat. For a moment her fingers trembled there, just barely, like a compass needle near iron. "Emrys. If you feel it start—the hearing, the pull—you look at me. I'll be by the pillar on the left. You look at me, and you count the buttons on my coat, and you stay in the room. Whatever else is calling you, you stay in the room."

"I will."

Because that was the other thing, the thing Emrys hadn't told either of them fully: since Fenwick, since the roads where magic pressed down like deep water, the world had been getting louder. Not the beasts—they had always been there, a low chorus under everything, Sable's commentary a constant bright thread—but the land itself, the stones and roots and old dead things in the earth, all of it murmuring now that Emrys had stopped pretending not to listen. And beneath even that, fainter than breath on a winter window, something else had been threading through the capital's noise for three days. Something vast and patient and singing.

The Choir. Under the city like water under ice.

And today they were going to walk into the room where it kept its mouth.

* * *

The throne room of Vael Corath had been built to make human beings feel like weather—present, brief, and unimportant.

Emrys understood this within four steps of entering it. The ceiling rose so far overhead that the banners hanging from it seemed to float in cloud; the pillars marched away in ranks like a forest that had agreed to hold still; and at the far end, raised on seven white steps, sat the throne itself, carved from a single block of pale stone that Emrys's skin recognized before their mind did. Spire-stone. The same material as the tower that ate stars, hauled here and shaped into a seat, and every hair on their arms lifted away from it like iron filings from a lodestone turned wrong.

The court was assembled in glittering ranks on either side of the long carpet. Lords and ladies in winter silks, faces powdered to a uniform pallor, all of them watching the doors with the fixed brightness of people who had learned that attention was survival. Near the left pillar, exactly as promised, stood Wren in her cartographer's blues, hands folded, expression carved into professional blankness. She did not look at Emrys. That, too, was the plan.

A herald's voice rang out, absurdly bright against all that stone:

"The King of Vael Corath, Unconquered, Keeper of the Ninefold Crown, His Radiant Majesty Aldric the Third!"

Trumpets. The great doors at the far end swung wide.

And the king came in alone.

That was the first wrongness. Emrys had read what they could of court custom in Odile's books—a king walked with his shadow-guard, six at least, and a chamberlain, and a bearer of the sword of state. This king walked with no one. His footsteps on the marble made no echo, as though the room swallowed each one before it could bounce. He was tall, broad through the shoulder, a man perhaps fifty years old with grey coming into his dark beard, and he wore the crown the way other men wore hats, carelessly, as if it weighed nothing at all.

He smiled at his court, and the court smiled back, and Emrys felt the smile go through them like cold water finding a crack.

Too many teeth. Sable had described it that way, in her flat crow way, telling them about the night the star-charts burned in the palace yard while the king watched with too many teeth. Emrys had thought it a figure of speech. It was not. There was nothing anatomically wrong with the man's smile—it had exactly the right number of teeth, arranged exactly as teeth should be—and yet it was wrong the way a mask is wrong, the way a face painted on a shield is wrong. It reached all the way to his eyes without ever arriving anywhere behind them.

*There,* Sable had said, perched somewhere above the city on that terrible night. *That's what looked at me. Not him. Through him.*

Emrys knelt when the herald commanded it, along with forty other petitioners, and kept their eyes lowered, and listened.

The king took his throne. He received petitions. And he spoke.

His voice was beautiful. That was the second wrongness, and it was worse than the first. It filled the throne room without effort or amplification, rich and resonant, a voice like a cathedral organ played by a master, and every sentence it produced was perfect—measured, gracious, wise, each decree landing with the weight of inevitable law. He settled a boundary dispute between two river lords in nine sentences that left both men bowing, one relieved and one ruined, neither able to say why. He announced the spring levy with language so honeyed that half the court murmured approval aloud. He addressed a delegation of frightened merchants from the coast, whose towns had gone dark under the vanished stars, and told them that the heavens were merely resting, that the Crown held their sorrow close, that order was the mother of safety—and Emrys watched the merchants' shoulders come down, watched fear drain out of grown men's faces like dye from cloth in rain, and understood with a lurch why no one in this city had risen up.

It wasn't only the lamplight and the posters. It was the voice. The voice made obedience feel like being loved.

*Beautiful, isn't it,* whispered something at the edge of Emrys's hearing, soft as dust settling. *You could sound like that. Everyone sounding together. No more hiding in valleys. No more silence.*

Emrys bit the inside of their cheek until they tasted copper, and counted the buttons on Wren's coat. Fourteen. Fifteen. Sixteen.

Stay in the room.

* * *

The audience went on for the better part of two hours, and Emrys spent it doing the hardest listening of their life: listening to two things at once, the surface and the underneath.

The surface was easy to describe and hard to bear—the ringing decrees, the gracious laughter, the king rising twice to walk among his court, touching shoulders, blessing infants, his gloved hand resting briefly on the head of a child who would remember it forever as the best moment of her life. The court adored him. That was the horror of it; there was nothing to point at, no wound visible, no rot showing at the edges. If Emrys had crossed that carpet knowing nothing—if they were still the person who had crept out of Hollowmere with a packed bag and a head full of beast-speech—they would have seen a good king in a shining hall and gone home reassured.

But Emrys was listening underneath.

They had learned, on the road, how to open the gift by degrees, like cracking a door in a storm—Wren's shielding lessons, the breathing, the anchor-points, the discipline of choosing what to hear. Now they opened it a finger's width. Just enough.

Beneath the king's voice, beneath the organ-tone of it, there was a second sound.

At first Emrys took it for the building—the groan of old stone, the whisper of drafts through high windows. Then they found its rhythm, and their stomach dropped, because it was not the rhythm of wind or stone. It was the rhythm of speech. Words, worn nearly smooth, like a pebble tumbled in a river for years, but words.

*—cold, it's so cold, how long, how long has it been, please, someone, please—*

Emrys's breath stopped.

They kept their face still. They had practiced stillness their whole life; stillness was the first language Hollowmere had ever taught them, older than words. But inside, everything in them turned over like a boat in a squall, because they knew that voice. Not the man—they had never met the man—but the *kind* of voice. The texture of it. It was the voice of something drowning and refusing to stop calling, the voice of the hound in the tall grass that had screamed inside its own stolen body even as it tried to kill them, the voice of every taken thing the Choir had ever hollowed—

Except the hounds screamed and raged. This voice *begged.*

*Please. Please, I'm here, I'm still here, I can hear myself, I can hear what it says with my mouth, oh gods, oh gods, the little girl, did I hurt the little girl, I touched her head, I felt her hair under my hand and it wasn't me, it wasn't me—*

The king laughed at something a lord had said. The laugh rolled out across the throne room, warm as brandy, and beneath it the drowned voice made a sound that Emrys would carry to their grave: a thin, cracked sobbing, muffled, like crying through a wall, like crying through one's own closed throat.

*It isn't me. It isn't me. Someone hear me. Someone—*

Someone heard him.

Emrys knelt on cold marble in a room full of enemies and wept without moving a single muscle of their face, tears standing in their eyes, blurring the banners and the candlelight, and they did not dare blink them free.

He was alive. Aldric was alive. Not a remnant, not an echo, not some ghostly residue—the man himself, conscious, imprisoned in his own body, forced to watch through his own eyes while the thing wearing him unmade the sky and smiled at courts and blessed children with hands he could not stop. How long? Years. Years of this. Years of screaming into a throat that wouldn't obey, years of feeling his own mouth shape decrees he hated, years of the whole world believing him a monster—or worse, never believing anything at all, never suspecting, because the thing inside him was so very, very good at being a king.

Every plan Emrys had carried into this room died in that moment.

They had come, in the honest cellar of their heart, prepared to consider killing him. They had rehearsed it on the road north: if it comes to it, if there's no other way, a king is one life against a sky full of them. Wren had said it once, flatly, staring into a fire. Even Sable had cawed agreement in her grim way. One life. Arithmetic.

But arithmetic required the one life to be over. And the one life was not over. The one life was down there in the dark of his own skull, begging, hoping, *remembering the little girl's hair and grieving over it*—and what kind of rescue was a knife? What kind of warden ended a haunting by killing the haunted?

*—please,* said the voice under the voice, thinner now, fraying. *Please, whoever you are. I felt that. Just now. Something—someone—there's someone listening, isn't there? I haven't felt anyone listen in—*

He had felt it. The king's gaze moved along the kneeling petitioners, that beautiful blind smile fixed in place, but the eyes—underneath the wrongness of the eyes, deep down where the man still lived—those eyes swept the floor like a man searching a cell for a loose stone.

And then they passed over Emrys, and stopped.

Not the Choir's gaze. *His.* For half a heartbeat, the drowning man behind the king's face looked directly at the one creature in forty who could hear him, and Emrys saw—felt, the way one feels a hand grip theirs underwater—hope hit him like a blow. Hope and terror in equal measure, because hope was dangerous, hope was loud, hope might carry—

*Don't,* the voice breathed, ragged. *Don't let it see you see me. Please. It watches through me. When I hope, it looks for what I hope at. Go. Go, whatever you are, go—*

The king's head tilted, very slightly, like a bird considering.

The smile widened by one degree.

*Ah,* said the Choir, in the space behind Emrys's eyes, in a voice like a thousand singers drawing one delighted breath. *What a curious little instrument the court has admitted today. So sensitive. So* attentive.

Cold poured down Emrys's spine. They dropped their gaze to the marble, heart slamming, and gave the throne room their best impression of nothing at all: a tired apprentice from the eastern marches, mud and mutton, fourteen buttons, fifteen, sixteen, stay in the room, stay in the room—

The moment stretched. Somewhere a candle guttered. The Choir considered them the way a fisherman considers a line gone taut—without hurry, with interest, with all the time in a drowned world.

Then a chamberlain struck the floor with his staff, announcing the next petitioner, and the pressure withdrew like a tide going out, leaving Emrys shaking and salt-cheeked and alive.

* * *

Afterward, in the colonnade outside, with the grey afternoon light falling through the arches and the lamplighters already beginning their rounds below, Wren found Emrys standing very still beside a pillar, gripping the stone as though the palace might otherwise tip them into the sky.

"Well?" she asked, low. Her eyes flicked left and right—guards at the colonnade's ends, courtiers drifting past. "Did you hear anything?"

Emrys opened their mouth to give her the safe answer, the planned answer, *nothing, mistress, my head is empty as a drum.*

What came out instead, barely a breath: "He's alive, Wren. He's alive and he's begging."

Wren went white. Not gradually—all at once, like a snuffed lamp. Her hand shot out and gripped Emrys's sleeve, knuckles bloodless. "Say that again," she whispered, "and mean it, and be certain."

"He begged me to go before it noticed. He—" Emrys's voice cracked, and they mastered it. "He remembers the children he's blessed. He grieves over them. Wren, he's been awake in there the whole time. Every decree. Every smile. All of it, from inside."

For a long moment Wren didn't move. Emrys watched the idea land in her the way a stone lands in still water, ring after ring after ring—because Emrys knew what it meant to her. She had served that court. She had watched the king change and said nothing, filed her maps and kept her head, and all these months her guilt had fed on a simple, survivable story: the king was gone, the thing wore him, and whatever needed doing to end it was terrible but clean.

Clean was over.

"We have to tell Odile," Wren said finally, hoarse. "The rite, the scholars, all of it—the plan changes tonight. If he can be pulled free—"

Above them, from the colonnade roof, came a single harsh caw.

Sable dropped out of the grey air and landed on the balustrade an arm's length away, feathers roughed, eyes black and bright and—Emrys had known this bird for their whole life, had been scolded and mocked and guarded by her since childhood, and had never once seen her look afraid.

She looked afraid now.

"Not here," Sable said, in the speech only Emrys could hear, low and fast. "Not with the stone listening. The Spire's pets sit in these walls like fleas in a blanket." She hopped closer, and her voice dropped further, and there was something in it Emrys had never heard before—something that sounded, impossibly, like shame. "Little speaker. There's a thing you need to know before you go marching off to save him. About how the Choir found the valley's edge, all those years ago. About what I did."

Her claws clicked once on the stone.

"About what I *was.*"

Emrys stared at her, the king's drowning voice still echoing in their ears, and felt the ground shift under everything they had left.
