## 14. Sable's Bargain

The crow had counted the bells of Odile's safehouse the way she counted everything—by their weight.

Three bells since the scholars had gone to bed with their candle-ends and their fragments of rite, ink-stained and muttering like rooks settling into a hedge. Two bells since Wren had finally stopped pacing the map room below, her boots wearing a groove into floorboards older than any kingdom she'd ever charted. One bell since Emrys had climbed to the garret where the pigeons nested in the eaves and sat down on a crate beneath the one window that faced north, toward Hollowmere, and said nothing at all.

Sable knew that silence. She had spent six years learning its grammar.

She dropped from the rafters and landed on the crate beside them, close enough that her feathers brushed their sleeve. Emrys didn't flinch anymore. That was the trouble with being understood—you could not hide inside your own body the way other people hid inside theirs. Every tightening of a shoulder, every held breath, arrived in her mind as plainly as weather.

*You haven't slept,* she sent, in the way they spoke—not words exactly, but pressure and image, crow-thoughts pressed flat and passed between them like coins.

*Neither have you.*

*Crows don't need sleep. We need darkness and grudges.* She hopped once, resettled. *You have neither. Go down. Sleep.*

Emrys looked out the window. Below them the capital sprawled under its borrowed light, ten thousand lamps burning where ten thousand stars should have been, and somewhere past all of it the Spire stood black against a sky scraped clean. The city stank of tallow smoke and denial. Even here, three floors up behind shuttered glass, Sable could feel the Choir's hum in her hollow bones—a sound below sound, patient as rot.

*Emrys.* She said the name the way humans did, because some things deserved the weight of their true shape. *Tomorrow Odile's people finish the rite. The day after, we climb the Spire and I fly interference against things that used to be my colleagues. Tonight you should rest.*

*Tomorrow,* Emrys repeated. Their voice was thin. *Before tomorrow, there's something I've been waiting for you to tell me.*

The cold came up through Sable's feet first. Then her wings, folding tight against her back as though she could make herself small enough to vanish between the cracks of the world.

*I know,* she sent.

*Then say it.*

* * *

She had rehearsed this confession for years. In the field behind the Ashgrove cottage, watching Emrys learn to speak with the goats; on the long road with Wren's cart creaking toward Fenwick; in the refugee camp while the survivors wept over their hollow-eyed dead. A thousand versions, each one trimmed and polished until it might survive the telling.

Every version began the same place. So she began there.

*Sixty-one years ago,* she sent, *I was born in a rookery in the hills above the capital. My mother taught me three things: which refuse heaps turned fastest, which falcons were lazy, and never—never—to speak to anything that sang.*

*You mean the Choir.*

*I mean what became the Choir. It was smaller then. Hungrier in a different way. It didn't want kingdoms yet. It wanted listeners.*

Sable closed her eyes. Crow-speech was images, and so she gave Emrys the images whole, unsoftened, because they had earned that much and because mercy now would only make the wound later worse.

*A young king's court. Aldric's father's court—the old king, before him. There was a woman who kept the star-charts then, a warden-lineage, and she noticed that certain birds around the Spire had begun to sing songs no bird had written. Songs that felt like being warm. Like being known. Birds would land on the Spire's ledges to listen and forget how to leave. Forget their names. Forget their fledglings calling from the nests below.*

*And you listened anyway.*

*I went looking for it,* Sable said. *That's the part I need you to hold without softening. I wasn't tricked. I wasn't taken. I was curious, and curiosity is just hunger wearing a better coat. I flew to the Spire on my own wings and asked it what it wanted.*

The memory rose in her like water in a flooded cellar. The Spire's upper chamber, before the wards had been stripped from it—no. Not before. They had been *eaten*, gnawed through from the inside by something that had learned patience from watching the wardens die off one by one, childless, scattered, hunted by the very crown their ancestors had served. The last warden had been dead eleven years when Sable landed on that ledge. The door had been open the way a mouth is open.

*It showed me the sky,* she sent. *All of it. Every star as a lamp, every lamp as a throat. It showed me what it was making itself into—a voice so vast it would fill the space between everything, so nothing anywhere would ever again be separate or silent or alone. And do you know what I felt, Emrys?*

*Don't.*

*Relief.* Her claws dug into the crate's wood. *I was a crow. My whole life was cold, and garbage, and hawks, and fledglings that died of rain. It offered me a place in something endless. It offered me the end of being small. And I said yes.*

Emrys's breath stopped. In the dark of the garret their heartbeat sounded loud as a drum in a chapel.

*It made me a listener-speaker. A spy. There were others—jays, magpies, one old barn owl who'd forgotten more than the owl ever knew. We carried its song outward, seed by seed. To courts. To villages. Into the dreams of sleeping men who woke up gentle and wrong. And it told me about the valleys. The hidden places where beast-speakers still lived, the last of the warden blood, the ones whose voices could unmake everything it was building. It couldn't find them. Its song slid off wards like water off wax. But a bird could cross a border. A bird could watch. A bird could count hearth-smokes and remember faces.*

Sable opened her eyes. Emrys was looking at her now, and their face in the lamplight was very still, the way the valley went still before a rockslide.

*Hollowmere,* they sent. It was not a question.

*Nineteen years ago,* Sable said, *I flew to the edge of a valley I'd been circling for two seasons. There was a boy there—no. Not a boy. A child, four years old, sitting in the mud behind a goat shed, talking to a lame ewe. And the ewe was answering. I heard it across half a mile. I heard the shape of the child's gift, bright and enormous, and I thought: this is the one. This is the key it's been starving for. I flew back and I told it where to look.*

The silence that followed lasted long enough for the bell below to strike again. Four bells. Sable counted them because counting was the only thing keeping her from taking flight and simply never landing again.

*But the wards held,* Emrys sent at last, and their thought-voice had gone strange and flat, like ice over deep water.

*They held,* Sable agreed. *Because the Choir doesn't break wards—it waits for them to fail. It came to the border and it settled along it like frost on glass, and it waited, and it sang softly at the valley walls year after year after year, and every winter the wards grew thinner, and every winter I reported back: soon, soon, soon. Nineteen years I sat on your roof, Emrys. I watched you grow up. I ate from your hand. And every season I carried word of you south.*

*Why?* The question cracked open. Not *why did you betray me*—they had already absorbed that, she could feel it landing, stone sinking in black water—but the other question, the one underneath. *If you were its creature, why stay? Why warn me about hawks? Why teach me? Why—why sit with me every night for nineteen years?*

And here was the part she had never managed to polish. Here was the raw edge of it.

*Because of your mother,* she sent. *The winter I first marked you—I followed the smell of woodsmoke to the cottage, and through the window I saw a woman teaching a toddler to whistle, badly, both of you laughing. And something in me broke. Something the Choir had left intact precisely because it didn't think it mattered. It had hollowed out my obedience and my fear and my name, but it hadn't bothered with—* she searched for it, the crow-word, the pressure-shape— *with the small warm thing. Love, you'd call it. It didn't believe love was load-bearing.*

Her feathers shivered. *I flew back to the Spire's shadow and I lied. I told it the valley was empty. Old wards, no speakers, a dead line. It believed me because I had never lied to it before, and because it cannot imagine that a thing it has swallowed can still choose. And then I stayed. Nineteen years of penance, Emrys. Not loyalty—penance. I stayed to stand between you and the mistake I made. Every hawk I screamed away from you, every warning, every night I watched you sleep in the crook of that apple tree—that was me paying down a debt I will never finish paying, because the interest is that the wards are falling anyway. It found another way in. It always finds another way. But the first crack, the first hairline fracture in Hollowmere's safety—that came from my beak.*

* * *

Emrys stood up.

They moved away from the window, away from her, three steps into the dark of the garret, and Sabe watched their shoulders rise and fall with breaths that were too controlled, the breathing of someone holding a door shut against a flood.

*Say it,* Sable sent. *Whatever it is. Say it and let me carry it.*

*You lied to me.* Emrys's voice came out low and shaking. Out loud, in human speech, because some accusations needed the full weight of a throat. "Nineteen years. You sat on my windowsill. You were the first creature I ever spoke to—the first one that answered—and the whole time, the whole *time*, you were the reason the thing that killed the stars knew I existed."

"Yes," Sable said, aloud too, her voice a crow's rough rasp shaped around human words. "Yes."

"You told it about me." Emrys turned. Lamplight caught their face, and Sable saw that they were crying without seeming to know it, tears cutting clean lines down their cheeks. "You fed it my name. Before I could even talk, you—"

"I fed it a lie about you. Afterward. But yes—" her voice broke on the admission, a sound like a branch snapping— "before. Yes."

"The wards fell because of you." Emrys's hands curled at their sides. "My mother is in that valley right now with the sky going dark, because of you. The people at the camp—the ones who went vacant-eyed, singing—the road of bodies that leads back to the Spire starts at *your beak*—"

"It does." She spread her wings slightly, not in threat but in offering, baring herself the way a bird bares its throat. "Every mile of it. I won't take a feather of it back or explain a feather of it away."

"Then why—" Emrys's voice tore. "Why should I believe anything else you've said? The warnings? The hound—on the road, you circled high, you scouted, was that scouting or was that *reporting*? Is Odile's safehouse compromised right now? Am I—have I been walking toward the Spire inside a cage you built?"

"Ask the mule," Sable said. "Ask Wren's mule what I said to it outside Fenwick. Ask the gulls at the coast. Ask every animal we've met since we crossed the border whether I've sung a single note of its song. Emrys—*listen*. You of all people. Listen to me the way you listen to beasts, and tell me if there's anything of the Choir left in what I am."

Emrys went quiet. And they did listen—Sable felt it happen, felt the gift reach out and settle over her like a hand on a frightened bird's back, and she let it, she opened every door in herself, because there was no other proof she owned.

What Emrys found in her, she did not ask. She felt them find it, though: the cold hollow where her name used to live, scooped out sixty years ago and never refilled. The penance lying over everything like snow. And beneath it, buried deep, absurdly, stubbornly alive—the small warm thing.

Emrys sat back down on the crate. Slowly. As though their knees had argued with them and lost.

"If you'd told me," they whispered, "any time. Any single time in nineteen years. If you'd told me at Fenwick, or on the road, or at the camp—"

"I know."

"—instead of letting me trust you blind—"

"I know." Sable hopped closer, one step, and stopped, leaving the distance between them exactly as wide as Emrys had made it. "There is no good answer to that. Only this: every day I didn't tell you was a day I hoped you'd never have to know. And hoping that was selfish, because it was hope for *me*—that I could keep the one good thing I'd ever done without paying for the bad. Penance isn't penance if you get to choose when the bill arrives. So here it is. Arrived. Take it however you must."

Emrys stared at the floor. Outside, far below, a patrol's lanterns swayed along an avenue, and somewhere in the dark the Choir's hum pressed against the glass like a face.

"My mother," Emrys said at last. "Is she alive?"

"As of the last bird I questioned on the wind, three days ago—yes. The valley folk have gone to ground in the old quarry caves. The Choir's hounds circle the borders but haven't breached. Your mother is alive, and she knows you left to end this, because I sent a jackdaw with word before we reached Fenwick." Sable paused. "That also, you may weigh in the balance. I did it without asking you."

"Why?" Emrys's laugh was wet and terrible. "More lies?"

"One more kindness I decided you were allowed to have. Forgive me or don't. Add it to the ledger."

For a long moment Emrys said nothing. Then: "Get out of my sight tonight, Sable. Fly somewhere. The roof, the bell tower, I don't care. Because if you're standing there at dawn I'll either forgive you too fast or cast you out too hard, and I don't trust myself to know which. Let me sleep angry. Let me wake up and decide who I am with this knowledge in me."

Sable bowed her head until her beak nearly touched the crate. "That," she rasped, "is the wisest thing anyone has said to me in sixty years."

She flew up through the broken slats into the night. The city's false lamps burned beneath her; above them, the starless black went on forever. She circled the safehouse once, twice, and found a perch on the bell tower's cold stone, where she tucked her beak under her wing and did not sleep, and counted the hours until dawn the way she counted everything—

by their weight.

Dawn came gray and starless as every morning since midwinter. And with it came footsteps on the tower stair—heavy, uneven, urgent—and the door banged open, and Wren Calloway stood silhouetted against the graying sky with a scrap of paper crushed in her fist and a look on her face that Sable had seen only once before, the day the star-charts burned.

"They've moved the audience," Wren said, breathless. "The king. Tomorrow. And Emrys—Emrys is already gone from the safehouse, and Odile says no one saw them leave."
