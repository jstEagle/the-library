## 15. The Unbinding Rite

The scholars worked in a room beneath the spymaster's house that had no windows and too many candles, and they argued the way moths argue with glass — endlessly, desperately, against something they could not see.

Emrys watched them from a chair dragged into the corner three days ago and never moved since. There were four of them: Odile's people, plucked from universities and archives before the Choir's reach had closed around such places. A gaunt woman named Ilsette who smelled of ink and camphor; twin brothers, Perrin and Aldous, who finished each other's sentences and each other's doubts; and a young man called Tam, barely older than Emrys, who had wept openly when he first heard Emrys speak to Sable and had to leave the room.

They had spread across the long table every fragment of the old rite that could be found or bought or stolen. It was not much. It was, Odile said, everything.

Parchments scorched at the edges, as if pulled from a fire. A hymnal with its pages glued together by centuries of damp, pried apart only last winter. A rubbing taken from a stone in a ruined chapel in the eastern hills — charcoal on cloth, the letters half-swallowed by lichen. And one actual book, small enough to fit in a coat pocket, its cover scarred with what might have been claw marks or candle wax or both.

"Again," Ilsette said, not looking up. "From the beginning. Slowly."

Tam cleared his throat. His voice still shook when he read aloud; Emrys had noticed it did not shake when he whispered. "*'Let the speaker stand within the song's own threshold, where the stolen voice is kept. Let them call the bound one by their true name — not the name given, but the name sung. And when the two voices answer one another—'*"

"*—the bond may be cut*," the twins said together, as they always did. Then, separately, because they could not help themselves: "*May*," said Perrin. "*Not shall*," said Aldous.

"*May*," Tam agreed miserably, and looked at the table as though it had personally betrayed him.

Emrys turned the word over for perhaps the hundredth time. *May*. All of this — the fragments, the arguments, the candle smoke stinging their eyes — balanced on a word that meant nothing at all.

"And after?" Emrys asked. They knew the question was coming. They asked it every day, and every day the scholars gave a slightly different version of the same non-answer, like men circling a well and describing its water without drinking.

Ilsette set down her pen. She had kind eyes, Ilsette, the kind that made lying to her feel like kicking a dog.

"'And when the two voices answer one another,'" she recited, "'*let the speaker hold the thread between them until it parts. The speaker will hear the whole of the song while they hold it. What remains of the speaker after the cutting is a matter the old texts do not record.*'"

"A matter they do not *record*," Aldous repeated. "Which is not the same as a matter they did not know."

"They knew," Perrin said quietly. He was the gentler twin, or perhaps simply the more honest one. "Look at the marginalia. Look here—" he turned the little book toward Emrys, tapping a column of cramped annotations in the margin of the rite itself, written in a different hand, later, hurried. "Someone copied this out after the fact. After the Spire was bound the first time. And whoever it was wrote this." His finger stopped on a line so small Emrys had to lean forward, the candlelight swimming.

Emrys read it. Read it again. The words did not change.

*The warden who held the thread sang with us ever after. We buried her voice with her body, but the body would not stop singing.*

The room went very quiet. Even the twins stopped finishing each other's sentences.

"She held on too long," Tam whispered. "Or the song held onto her. Either way—" He swallowed. "She came out. Her body came out. But part of her stayed in the Choir."

"Part of her," Emrys said, "or all of her?"

No one answered. That was answer enough.

Sable shifted on the back of Emrys's chair, talons flexing against the wood — a small, familiar pressure, an anchor. Through the thin ribbon of awareness that connected them even in silence, Emrys felt her mood: coiled, watchful, unhappy. She had been unhappy for three days. She had been unhappy, Emrys suspected, since the moment she understood what these fragments meant, which meant she had understood before any of the scholars had.

*Crow,* Emrys thought at her. *You've read the margins.*

*I've read worse margins,* came the dry reply, but underneath it ran something else, something Emrys rarely heard from Sable: fear. Real fear, unarmored by sarcasm.

Emrys sat back. Around them the scholars resumed their argument — the direction of the threshold, whether the true name must be spoken aloud or sung, whether the rite required moonlight or merely starlight, and how either requirement could possibly be met in a sky with neither. Their voices blurred together. Emrys found they were no longer listening.

They were thinking about the drowned king. About the voice they'd heard beneath the decrees, faint and frantic, a man beating his fists against the inside of a locked door. *Help me. Please. I can hear you. Whoever you are — help me.*

Aldric had been drowning in that song for years. Every day Emrys waited was another day he drowned.

And the price of reaching him was written in the margin of a stolen book in a dead woman's handwriting: come out singing, or don't come out at all.

Emrys looked at their own hands, folded in their lap. Hands that had spent twenty-two years learning to be quiet. Hands that had finally, at the edge of the world, found something worth being loud for.

"All right," they said.

The scholars didn't hear. Emrys raised their voice — a strange, rusty feeling, using volume in a room of strangers.

"I said, all right. I'll do it."

Every head turned. Sable's talons bit through fabric into skin, a warning, and Emrys ignored it.

"The rite needs a beast-speaker. There are maybe a handful left in the world who aren't already vacant-eyed and obedient, and none of them are standing in this room. I'm what you have. So I'll do it."

"No."

The voice came from the doorway, and Emrys knew it before the word finished landing — knew it the way they knew rain coming, the way they knew a hawk's shadow crossing a field. Wren stood in the entrance with a lantern in one hand and a stack of Odile's requisition papers in the other, and her face had gone the color of old parchment.

"You'll do no such thing," she said.

"Wren—"

"Don't." She set the lantern down with exaggerated care, the way she did when her hands wanted to do something else entirely. "Don't stand there and tell me you've decided, based on three days of listening to frightened academics guess at a dead language. Don't— how much of it did they even translate? Half? Two-thirds?"

"Enough," Emrys said.

"Enough." Wren laughed, and it was an awful sound, hollow as a dropped pot. She crossed the room in five strides and snatched the little book off the table, turning pages with a cartographer's precision until she found what she was looking for. "Then you've read this part. 'The speaker will hear the whole of the song.' Do you understand what that means? Not the piece of it you heard under the palace. Not the edges. The *whole*. Years of it. Thousands of voices, all of them screaming, all of them beautiful, all of them *wanting you to join*—"

"I know what it means."

"Do you?" She slammed the book shut. Dust jumped in the candlelight. "Because I watched what the Choir does from the outside, Emrys. For two years I watched it happen to the cleverest, strongest people in the capital, one by one, and every single one of them believed right up until the end that they were the exception. My mentor included. He walked into the Spire's foundations with a theory and a prayer and I never saw him again, and I have spent every day since drawing maps trying to earn my way back to that door, and I will be damned — *damned*, Emrys — if I walk you to it and hand you over like a parcel."

"That's not what this is."

"Isn't it? Then what is it?"

"It's the only way!" Emrys was on their feet now, and their voice cracked out of them with all the force of twenty-two years of silence behind it. The candles guttered. Somewhere in the walls, a rat froze mid-scurry. "You want to kill the king instead? Go ahead. Ask Tam how the binding works — ask any of them! The Choir isn't *in* the king the way a worm is in an apple, it's woven through him, through the Spire, through half the machinery of the crown. Kill Aldric and the Choir doesn't die, it just loses its coat. It finds another. Maybe it finds someone closer than a stranger on a throne. Maybe it finds—"

They stopped. The sentence sat there, unfinished, and everyone in the room heard where it was going.

Wren's jaw tightened. "It won't find me."

"You don't know that."

"I don't know anything anymore," she said, quieter. "That's rather the point." She pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes for a moment. When she lowered them, she looked ten years older and infinitely tired. "Emrys. Listen to me. You are twenty-two years old. You grew up in a valley where you weren't allowed to speak above a whisper to anything with a heartbeat. Three months ago you'd never slept outdoors. You are brave beyond all sense and reason, and you are *not ready*, and the difference between those two things is going to get you killed."

"Then teach me," Emrys said. "That's what I'm asking. Not permission — preparation. Teach me to shield better. Teach me to hold a thread. Teach me everything your mentor taught you about the Spire, every corridor, every door. Give me the best possible chance, and then let me take it."

"And if the chance fails?"

"Then you'll still have your maps, and Odile will still have her spies, and the world will still have one fewer beast-speaker to persecute."

"Don't joke."

"I'm not joking." Emrys held her gaze. It cost something; Wren's stare had a weight to it, the weight of someone who had already lost everything once and catalogued the losses in her own precise hand. "I'm telling you the truth. I heard him, Wren. Underneath all of it — the ringing, the decrees, the thing wearing him like a coat — there's a man in there, and he's begging. Every day I wait, he drowns a little deeper. If I die doing this, at least I die having answered. That's more than silence ever gave anyone."

The scholars had gone very still, pretending to work. Sable hadn't moved at all.

Wren looked at Emrys for a long time. Something moved behind her eyes — Emrys couldn't name it, and didn't try. Some things were not for reading, even by someone who could talk to crows.

"You think this is courage," Wren said at last. "Walking into the song. It isn't. Courage is walking *out* again. Anyone can give themselves away — I've seen children do it, during the first purges, offering themselves up because they thought their gift was a debt the world had come to collect." Her voice roughened. "Your gift is not a debt, Emrys. It's not a curse and it's not a key and it's not a sacrifice waiting to happen. It's *you*. And I refuse — I absolutely refuse — to spend you."

"Then don't spend me." Emrys stepped closer. Close enough to see the candle flame reflected twice in her eyes, once in each. "Stand next to me instead. Hold the door. That's all I'm asking — the same thing you've done every step of this road. I climb. You anchor. If I start to slip—" they took a breath, "—pull."

"Pull," Wren repeated flatly. "Pull *how*? Pull *what*? The text doesn't say. The text says the old warden came out *singing*, Emrys. How exactly am I supposed to pull someone out of a song?"

"I don't know yet." Emrys glanced at the scholars. "But they've got three days of fragments and we've got however long it takes Odile to move us into position. Somebody smarter than all of us figured this out once, with less light and worse maps. We can figure it out again." They turned to Ilsette. "Start with the marginalia. Everything that woman wrote. Every note, every correction, every crossed-out line. If she learned the hard way, I want to learn from her."

Ilsette met Emrys's eyes, and nodded slowly, and something passed between them that needed no translation — scholar to subject, witness to volunteer, one keeper of dangerous words to another.

Wren stood rigid in the middle of the room. Emrys watched her weigh it, watched the arithmetic run behind her face: the odds, the alternatives, the drowned king, the vanished mentor, the girl from the valley who had walked into her life and refused, with mule-calming stubbornness, to walk out of it.

"You'll train," Wren said finally. It was not agreement. It was a treaty, signed under duress. "Every morning. Shielding, holding, breaking contact. Until I say you're ready — not until *you* say it. Me. And the moment I decide you're not ready, we burn these fragments and find another way, even if the other way is worse. Swear it."

"I swear."

"On what?"

Emrys thought about it. On the wards? Dead. On the stars? Dark. On Hollowmere, on home, on the letter folded into the lining of their pack that they could not bring themselves to finish?

"Sable," they said.

The crow lifted her head from the chair-back. In the candlelight her eye caught the flame and held it, black shot through with gold, ancient and amused and grieving all at once.

"On Sable," Emrys repeated. "She's the meanest, most honest creature I've ever known. If I break my word, she's permitted to peck me forever."

"She'll hold you to that," Wren said. And then, so quietly that Emrys almost missed it, almost missed the crack beneath it: "So will I."

She picked up her lantern and left, and the door closed behind her with a sound like a period at the end of a sentence.

* * *

That night Emrys could not sleep. They lay on the narrow cot in the room Odile had given them — a real bed, a real roof, luxuries that felt like borrowed clothes — and listened to the city breathe around them, and did not sleep.

Near midnight, Sable flew in through the gap beneath the shutters and settled on the bedpost, folding herself down into a shape like a small dark judgment.

*You're angry with me,* Emrys thought at her.

*Furious.*

*Say it, then.*

Sable was quiet for a while. Rain had started somewhere above them; Emrys could hear it ticking against the shutters, soft as fingernails.

*When I was young,* Sable said at last, *there was a sparrow in my flock. Foolish bird. Brave in the wrong direction. A hawk got into the hedgerow one morning, and instead of flying high like the rest of us, she dove straight at it. Straight at its eyes. Drove it off, saved six chicks in the nest below.* A pause. *The hawk killed her anyway. But slower. And the last thing she heard was the flock calling her name.*

*Is that a warning or a eulogy?*

*It's a memory,* Sable said. *I've carried it forty years and it hasn't gotten lighter. That's what memories do, girl. That's what they're *for*.* She hopped down to the footboard, closer, her head cocked so one bright eye fixed on Emrys in the darkness. *I won't tell you not to do this. You wouldn't listen. You have your mother's chin and your father's stubbornness and no sense whatsoever, and I love you for all three, which is the worst bargain I've ever struck.*

Emrys's throat went tight. *Sable—*

*But hear me.* The crow's voice dropped into something older than her usual dryness, something that sounded dredged up from very deep water. *When you go into that song — and you will go, I can see it in you the way I see weather coming — you hold one thing above all the rest. Not the king. Not the mission. Not even your own name. You hold the sound of my voice saying yours, and you do not let go of it, no matter how beautiful the other thing sounds. Promise me that.*

*How will that help?*

*Because,* Sable said, *a name sung by something that loves you is the strongest rope in the world. I've seen it pull birds out of fires.*

Emrys reached out and touched one feathered shoulder, gently, the way you touch something you're afraid might vanish. Sable allowed it, which was itself a kind of vow.

Outside, the rain thickened. And far away — miles distant, past the rooftops and the lamplit denial of the sleeping city, past the walls and the checkpoints and the posters promising stars that would never return on their own — Emrys felt it again, the way they had felt it every night since the palace: the Spire, humming. Patient. Hungry. Waiting for them with the terrible courtesy of a host who has already set your place at the table.

Beneath its hum, faint as a pulse under ice, a human voice was still singing.

Emrys closed their eyes and began, silently, to practice the only thing left worth practicing: holding on.
