## 16. What Stays Invisible

The scholars of Odile's circle had given Emrys a room in the topmost floor of the map-house, a narrow chamber that smelled of ink and dust and the tallow smoke of a hundred years of midnight work. From its single window, if Emrys pressed their forehead to the cold glass and looked straight up past the eaves, they could see a sliver of sky.

It was empty. It had been empty for months now, and every night Emrys looked anyway, the way a tongue returns to a missing tooth.

Tomorrow they would climb the Spire.

Tonight they sat on the edge of a borrowed bed with a sheet of good paper — real paper, rag-made, smooth as skin — and a pen that scratched too loudly in the quiet, and tried to write a letter to their mother.

*Dear Mama,* they wrote, and then stopped, because everything after that was either a lie or an accusation, and they loved her too much for either.

The lamp guttered. Somewhere below, Wren's boots crossed the courtyard, unhurried, pausing at the well, pausing again at the gate — her nightly circuit, counting the things she could still count. Sable was out in the dark somewhere, doing whatever Sable did when she thought no one was watching, which lately had been nothing Emrys could name without a knot forming under their ribs. The bargain was mended but the scar tissue was new; it pulled when Emrys moved wrong.

They looked down at the page. *Dear Mama.*

How did you write to someone whose whole life you had spent lying to her? Not cruelly — never cruelly. Hollowmere lies were gentle lies, structural lies, load-bearing lies. *I'm fine. I'm careful. I don't talk to the goats anymore, I promise.* Lies laid brick by brick until they became the walls of a house, and everyone lived inside them, warm and safe and slightly suffocated, and called it home.

Emrys dipped the pen again.

*I used to think hiding was the same as living,* they wrote, surprising themselves. The words came out crooked, half-cursive, the handwriting of someone who had learned letters from a peddler's almanac and a crow's dictation. *You taught me to be quiet so I would be safe. And it worked. I want you to know that first, before anything else: it worked. I am alive because you taught me how to be small.*

The lamp hissed. Down in the street a dog barked twice and then, hearing something Emrys couldn't hear — or could hear, faintly, always, like water behind a wall — went silent and slunk away.

*But being alive isn't the same as living, is it. You knew that too. I saw you know it, some mornings, when you stood at the garden fence looking at the pass out of the valley like a woman looking at the sea. We were both keeping the same secret from each other: that the silence was costing us something we couldn't name, and we'd decided not to name it because naming it would mean we'd have to do something about it.*

Emrys set the pen down. Their hand was shaking, only a little.

It was strange, this clarity. All their life, feeling had come to them sideways — through the animals, through the low constant murmur of beasts thinking their beast thoughts, anything rather than the raw unmediated noise of their own heart. They had gotten very good at translating themselves into other creatures' vocabularies. The fox felt fear; therefore Emrys felt fear, at one remove, manageable, furred over. But tonight there were no animals near except the city's few terrified remnants huddled in cellars, and so Emrys sat alone with the plain unfiltered fact of themselves, and it was louder than any beast they had ever heard.

Tomorrow they would walk into the song that ate names.

And the terrible thing — the thing they kept turning over like a stone with something alive underneath — was that part of them understood the Choir.

Not forgave it. Understood it. That was worse.

Because Emrys knew what it was to be lonely enough to want to dissolve into something larger. They had spent twenty years practicing a smaller version of the same surrender: making themselves invisible, unremarkable, a person shaped exactly like the space other people weren't looking at. It had been survival. It had also been a kind of vanishing, voluntary and slow, and no one had ever sung to them while it happened, and maybe that was the only difference. Maybe the Choir was just loneliness with power. Maybe the vacant-eyed obedient ones hadn't been dragged anywhere. Maybe some part of them had said *yes, finally, take the weight of being a self, I never wanted it.*

Emrys pressed the heels of their hands against their eyes until colors bloomed.

No. That was the song talking already, seeping in through the cracks the scholars' diagrams had opened in their mind. Odile's people had warned them: study the rite and the rite studies you back. Every hour with those fragments of pre-Choir script left Emrys humming something under their breath that they didn't remember learning.

They picked up the pen again because the alternative was listening.

*Here is what I know now, Mama, that I couldn't have told you at the garden fence:* the letter went. *Invisibility kept me safe and it kept me useless, and those two things were never separate. The valley needed people who could hear what I hear. The world needed it. I needed it. And I gave it up — we gave it up, all of us, generation after generation — for a quiet that was really just a very slow way of going extinct.*

*Wren says cartographers have a word for places left blank on maps. She says the old maps used to put monsters there, or dragons, or nothing at all, and that "nothing at all" was the most frightening thing you could draw, because it meant no one had dared to go and look. I was a blank place on my own map, Mama. Twenty years of terra incognita. Tomorrow I go and look.*

*If I don't come back —*

The pen hovered. Ink gathered at the nib in a dark swelling bead.

*If I don't come back, I need you to know that the last thing I will feel will not be fear. It will be the king's voice saying my name. You can't understand what that means yet — you will, when the stars come back, if they come back — but a voice saying your true name is the opposite of disappearing. Whatever happens in that tower, I will be more visible in one moment than I have been in my whole life. Don't grieve me as someone who hid. Grieve me — remember me — as someone who spoke.*

Emrys read it back. It wasn't finished. It might never be finished; some letters are less messages than mirrors. But it was true, all the way through, and truth was the one currency they had never been allowed to spend in Hollowmere, and it felt extravagant to spend it all at once here at the end.

They folded the paper along Wren's careful demonstration-creases — the cartographer folded everything in thirds, even her failures, especially her failures — and sealed it with a drop of wax and, after a moment's thought, pressed the pad of their thumb into the soft wax. A thumbprint. Not a signet, not a sigil. Just proof that a particular hand had existed, had touched this, had been *here*. The most ordinary signature in the world, and the first one Emrys had ever put to anything that mattered.

They addressed it simply: *Mara Ashgrove, Hollowmere, by whatever road remains.*

Then they sat holding it for a long time, because sending it was impossible and not sending it was unbearable, and they held it the way you hold a bird you intend to release but haven't yet found the courage to open your fingers around.

* * *

Sable came in through the window sometime past midnight, without announcement, the way she did everything. She landed on the desk, knocked the ink pot an inch toward the edge purely on principle, and regarded Emrys with one bright black eye.

"You're awake," she said. Her voice inside Emrys's skull was dry as seed husks. "Everyone's awake. Wren's sharpening things that were already sharp. The scholars are arguing about whether the seventh syllable of the rite is aspirated. The whole city is pretending to sleep the way fish pretend to be calm."

"Did you eat?" Emrys asked, because it was easier than the conversation they actually needed to have.

"I ate." Sable hopped closer, head tilting. "You wrote something."

"You can't read."

"I can't *read*," Sable agreed. "But I know what a letter looks like when it's written by someone who thinks they're going to die. Crows carry those sometimes. Off battlefields. Off plague houses. They all fold the same way." She tapped the sealed paper once with her beak, gently, for her. "Thirds. Like the map-woman taught you."

"Wren folds everything in thirds."

"Yes," Sable said. "She does."

Silence settled between them, the old familiar shape of it, but changed — ever since the confession, ever since *I led them to the valley's edge*, the silences between Emrys and Sable had a new grain to them, sanded rough. Emrys had forgiven her. Forgiveness, they were learning, was not a door you walked through once but a corridor you kept walking down, and some nights the corridor was longer than others.

"Sable." Emrys turned the letter over in their hands. "When you served them. The Choir. What did it feel like? Being part of the song?"

The crow went very still. Stillness, in a creature made almost entirely of motion, was its own kind of scream.

"Why do you ask?"

"Because tomorrow I'm going to stand in front of it, and I want to know what I'm walking into. Not the danger. The *temptation*. Everyone keeps telling me about the danger. Nobody tells me about the temptation."

Sable looked at the window, at the starless dark beyond it. When she answered, her voice had lost its dryness; it was young, suddenly, impossibly young, a fledgling's voice coming out of an old crow's throat.

"It felt like being known," she said. "Completely. Every ugly thing you've ever done, every small cowardice, every thought you've never said aloud — it already had them, and it didn't flinch, and it *sang to you anyway*. Do you understand? Everything I'd ever hidden, and it wanted me regardless. There was no performance left to maintain. No mask. You spend your whole life building a self out of what you show people, and the song takes the whole scaffolding down in one note, and underneath there's just — you. Small and naked and *held*." Her feathers rippled, a shudder passing through her like wind through wheat. "I have flown since across half the world trying to find anything that felt like that. Nothing does. That's the trap. That's the whole trap."

Emrys's throat was tight. "And you got out. How?"

"I didn't get out." Sable turned her head, and the lamplight slid across her eye like oil across water. "Something called me out. A sound from outside the song. One voice, one note, from a warden on the valley border, dying, singing a warning into the dark. It cut through everything. And I remembered that being completely known by a thing that loves nothing is not the same as being loved." She fixed Emrys with both eyes now, fierce and ancient. "Remember that tomorrow. When it knows everything about you — and it will, it will open you like a letter — remember that being read is not the same as being *answered*. The Choir reads. It has never once answered anyone. But a voice that says your name back—" she tapped the letter again, "—that's the thing it can't counterfeit. That's your rope out. Hold onto it."

Emrys nodded, and found they couldn't speak, and Sable had the grace — rare, expensive grace — to pretend not to notice.

* * *

Near dawn, footsteps on the stairs. Wren didn't knock; she had stopped knocking weeks ago, having concluded, in her precise way, that courtesy between people who intended to die for each other was a formality they could no longer afford.

She stood in the doorway in her travel coat, hair bound back, looking older than her years and steadier than her hands. In her arms she carried a leather tube, and Emrys knew what was in it before she said anything, because Wren carried her maps the way priests carried relics.

"You're up," Wren said.

"Everyone's up. Apparently."

"Mm." She crossed the room, glanced at the letter on the desk, and — with the delicacy of someone who had spent her life reading other people's borders without crossing them — looked away. "I've finished it," she said instead. "The route. Through the Spire's lower galleries. Come and see."

She unrolled the map on the desk, weighting the corners with the ink pot, a candlestick, Emrys's untouched supper roll. And there it was: the Spire rendered in Wren's faultless hand, every gallery, every stair, every choke-point marked in red, the ascent drawn as a single thread of ink climbing the tower's spine like a vein.

"The ritual begins at moonrise," Wren said, and her voice was flat in the way Emrys had learned meant she was holding herself together with cartographic discipline. "The Choir will pour most of itself upward into the working — that's our only mercy, that it must concentrate to feed. Odile's people create a diversion at the west postern. I hold the gallery door. Sable—"

"Sable flies interference. I know."

"And you climb." Wren's finger traced the thread of ink to its terminus: a small chamber at the crown of the Spire, marked with a symbol Emrys now knew intimately, the seven-pointed knot of the old wardens. "Two hundred and forty steps from the door to the crown. I counted them from the old plans. I want you to know that I counted them, because I need you to know that I've been sitting with that number for three days."

Emrys looked up at her. "Wren—"

"If you falter on step ninety, I can't come for you. If the door fails at step one hundred and fifty, I still can't come for you. My job ends where yours begins, and I hate it, and I've redrawn this map eleven times hoping the architecture would change." She exhaled slowly. "It hasn't. Maps are honest that way. It's why I love them and why I'll never forgive them."

Emrys reached out and put their hand over hers, there on the map, on the little crown-chamber, on the two hundred and forty steps. Wren's fingers were cold and ink-stained and trembling very slightly, and she let them tremble, because there was no one left in the room to perform steadiness for.

"When this is over," Emrys said, "you're going to draw the stars back onto the charts. All of them. In their right places."

"That's not how astronomy works, Ashgrove."

"I know. Draw them anyway. Wrong, if you have to. Draw them like a promise."

For a moment Wren's composure cracked clean through, and Emrys saw beneath it the disgraced woman who had watched a king go hollow and said nothing, who had spent years paying a debt she couldn't name, who had bet everything — Odile's bargain, her career's ghost, her life — on a valley-born child who talked to animals. Then she blinked it away, and rolled up the map, and was the cartographer again.

"Sleep," she said. "Or lie down and pretend. It's what the fish do."

She paused at the door, though. Turned halfway.

"For what it's worth," she said quietly, "the letter folds in thirds. That's the correct way. Whatever's in it — whoever it's for — it's the correct way to say it."

Then she was gone, her boots fading down the stairs, and Emrys was alone with the grey beginning of morning and the letter and the long day ahead.

They rose, and dressed in the dark clothes Odile's people had provided, and tucked the letter inside their coat, against their chest, over their heart — not to send, not yet, but to carry. Some letters you deliver yourself or not at all.

At the window, the sky was turning the color of ash. No stars. No sun yet either, just a bruised and sourceless light, the world lit by nothing, waiting.

Below in the courtyard, Wren was checking straps. On the gatepost, Sable sat preening, watching the eastern dark. Somewhere beneath the city, in foundations older than the kingdom, the wounded Choir waited too, gathering itself for moonrise.

Emrys took one breath, then another, and went down to meet the day — visible, at last, to anyone who cared to look.
