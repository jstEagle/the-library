## 17. Ascension

The Spire drank the sky.

Emrys saw it from three streets away, rising out of the palace district like a needle threaded through the world, and around its crown the darkness moved. Not clouds—clouds had edges, had weight, had the decency to drift. This was a slow rotation of nothing, a wheel of absent stars turning above the tower's tip as though the night itself were being wound onto a spool.

*They're taking them down one by one,* Sable said from Emrys's shoulder, her claws tightening through the coat's wool. In the weeks since the valley, since the wards and the screaming midwinter and the road that never seemed to end, Emrys had learned to read the crow's voice the way they'd once read weather: the flatness of it now was worse than fear. *I watched them do this in the capital before I fled south. The king stands at the top and sings, and where his song touches, the stars forget how to burn.*

"Then we climb while he's singing," Wren said.

She stood in the shadow of a chandler's awning with Odile's map spread across her forearm, the paper glowing faintly where she'd marked it with luminous ink—a cartographer's trick, Emrys had learned, for reading in the dark without a lantern's betrayal. Her finger rested on a narrow line running up the Spire's eastern face.

"The old maintenance stair. It predates the Choir's renovations by two centuries—it doesn't appear on any chart they've issued since Aldric changed. Odile's people swear it still exists behind a plastered-over arch in the counting house on Candlemaker's Row." She looked up, and in the borrowed lamplight her face was pale and set, the guilt that lived permanently behind her eyes burned down to something harder tonight. "Once we're inside, there are three doors between us and the ascent. I hold them. You run."

"And you?" Emrys asked, though they already knew.

"I hold them," Wren said again, patiently, the way she'd explained the shielding exercises on the road—again and again until Emrys stopped flinching at the land's grief pressing against their skull. "Odile's people can bar a door. What they can't do is know *which* door matters, or how long it will take the Choir's servants to find another way around, or what to sacrifice when the choice comes. That takes someone who understands the shape of a building. Someone who owes a debt." A thin smile. "Someone who drew the original floor plans of this tower and has spent six years hating herself for it."

"You could come with me," Emrys said. "To the top."

"To the top is the song, Ashgrove." Wren folded the map with a crispness that sounded like armor settling into place. "The song that eats names. You told us yourself what it did to the refugees—the ones who heard it went vacant before it ever touched their minds. I'm a drunk with a good memory for lines on paper. If I hear that song, I'll hand you over to it myself and smile while I do." She tapped the map once, hard. "No. My post is the door. Your post is the crown. We each spend what we have."

Sable ruffled her feathers—a sound like a small dry laugh. *She's right, fledgling. And I'll be above you the whole way. The Choir's spies in the walls are mostly rats and pigeons, and pigeons are idiots. I can buy you minutes. Minutes are all anyone gets tonight.*

Emrys looked from the crow to the cartographer and felt the strange arithmetic of it settle into their chest: everything either of them had, poured into buying one person one chance. They thought of their mother's kitchen in Hollowmere, of the silence they'd kept for twenty years, of the letter in their breast pocket that they might never send.

"All right," they said.

* * *

The counting house smelled of tallow and fear.

Odile's contact—a grey woman with ink-stained fingers who answered only to the name Mistress Quill—let them through the back and pulled aside a shelf of ledgers without ceremony. Behind it, exactly where Wren's memory said it would be, an arch stood half-drowned in old plaster, its keystone carved with a spiral that made Emrys's teeth ache to look at. The same spiral. The same mark that wound through every carving beneath the chapel floor, the work of the wardens who had bound the Spire long before the Choir ever wore a king.

*Beast-speakers built the way up,* Sable murmured. *Remember that, when your legs fail. The stairs were made for someone like you.*

Mistress Quill handed Wren a crowbar and a lantern she refused to light. "The servants change shift at the third bell. After that, the corridor below belongs to the pale ones' people. There were two guards posted here yesterday; today there are four. They're expecting something."

"They're expecting *him*," Wren said. "The gifted one the posters promise will be reported. Word travels even through Odile's channels." She turned to Emrys, and for a moment her composure slipped, showing the woman underneath—the one who had signed a bargain trading Emrys's freedom for help, and had hated herself a little more every day since. "Ashgrove. When you reach him—if you reach him—"

"I know."

"No, you don't. Listen." She gripped Emrys's shoulder, hard enough to bruise. "Everything we've done, everything Odile traded, everything Sable confessed, the hound we killed, the roads we bled on—it bought *this*. One window. Don't waste it being kind to me at the bottom of a staircase. Go up. Climb until your hands bleed if you have to. And whatever you hear at the top—" her voice cracked, just once, "—remember which voice is yours."

Then she kissed her own thumb and pressed it to the map's edge, an old cartographer's blessing, and pushed Emrys toward the dark.

* * *

Behind the plaster the air was cold and mineral, tasting of centuries.

The maintenance stair spiraled upward inside the Spire's outer wall, a throat of rough stone barely wide enough for shoulders, lit by nothing. Emrys climbed by touch and by the faint wrong-glow that seeped through cracks in the inner wall—the light of the ritual already beginning somewhere far above, bleeding down through the tower's bones like infection through flesh.

And the wall spoke.

Not in words at first. In pressure. As Emrys climbed, the ambient magic of the capital—which had pressed on them since Fenwick like deep water, which Wren had taught them to shield against hour by exhausting hour—thickened until their shielding felt like cupping hands against a flood. The Spire was not merely a tower. It was a wound in the world's skin, and through it the Choir's song poured ceaselessly into the earth, and everything living within a mile of it heard.

The stones themselves remembered being quarried. The mortar remembered fire. And beneath all of it, faint and rhythmic, came the pulse Emrys had first felt in the crypts: the old binding, the warden-work, holding—not failing, not yet, but straining like a rope around something vast.

*Talk to it,* Sable said. Above and outside now; her voice reached Emrys through the gift alone, thinned by stone but steady. *The tower. You speak to beasts and birds and hounds made of stolen screams. The stones are older than any of those. Speak.*

So Emrys did. Haltingly, in the wordless grammar of their gift, climbing all the while—they pressed one palm to the wall and gave the stone what they had. *I am a warden's heir. I carry the key. Let me pass. I am coming to unmake what was done.*

The answer shuddered up through their arm and their ribs and settled behind their sternum like a second heartbeat:

**LATE.**

One word, in a voice like a mountain deciding to be disappointed. But beneath the judgment, unmistakably: the spiral-carved stair shifted its angle by a fraction. A seam that had been sealed for two hundred years exhaled dust. The way opened.

Emrys laughed—a ragged, disbelieving sound swallowed by the dark—and climbed faster.

* * *

At the fourth landing, the song found them.

It came down the inner shaft like smoke, sweet and layered, a hundred voices braided into one melody that promised rest, belonging, an end to the terrible loneliness of having a self. Emrys staggered against the wall, palms flat to cold stone, and felt the shielding Wren had drilled into them begin to shred like wet paper.

*Emrys.* Sable's voice, sharp as a talon. *Listen to my voice instead. Ugly little bird. Selfish, thieving, magnificent bird. Say it back.*

"Sable," Emrys whispered. "Ugly. Thieving. Magnificent."

*Better. Keep climbing. The song wants you whole—that's its weakness. It cannot eat what refuses to be a meal.* A pause, and then, quieter, almost gentle: *Fledgling. Whatever happens above—whatever I am, whatever I was—I need you to know the years with you were the first thing I chose freely. Climb.*

Above, through the shaft, came a sound that froze the blood: voices shouting, boots, the groan of timber. Then, muffled by floors of stone, a crash—and Wren's voice, ringing clear and furious through the tower's throat:

"*The stair is closed for repairs!*"

A pause. A sound like a body hitting a wall.

"*REPAIRS!*" she screamed, and something heavy slammed home, and Emrys understood that the first door had been spent.

* * *

They ran after that. No more careful climbing—the song was rising, the ritual building toward some crescendo overhead, and every minute the stars wheeled thinner above the Spire's crown. On the fifth landing Emrys passed a window slit and saw the city spread below, ten thousand lamps burning defiant against the dark, and above them the hole in heaven growing wider, hungrier, closer to the ground than it had been in Hollowmere. Close enough, soon, to reach down and drink.

On the seventh landing the second door broke.

Emrys felt it rather than heard it—a vibration in the stone, a scream cut short, and then the song surged up the shaft with new force because the thing keeping it out was gone. They pressed both hands to the wall and begged in the old grammar: *Hold. Hold. Hold for me.*

**THE DOOR IS NOT MINE,** the tower answered. **BUT THE STAIR REMEMBERS ITS MAKERS.**

Somewhere above, stone ground against stone, and when Emrys burst onto the eighth landing they found the corridor beyond choked floor-to-ceiling with rubble—the tower itself collapsed a section of its own passage, spending its oldest bones as a second door. Dust hung in the air like held breath.

Beyond the rubble, faintly, came hammering. Voices that did not sound entirely human, calling in beautiful harmonies.

*That buys you time, not safety,* Sable said. She swept down through the shaft then, past Emrys, wings beating, and Emrys felt her purpose like a struck bell: *The pigeons on the upper roost are the Choir's eyes. Time to earn my penance.* And she was gone into the dark above, a black scrap against blackness, flying toward whatever waited at the top so that Emrys would have one fewer enemy to climb past.

Emrys climbed alone.

The last flights blurred together—legs burning, lungs full of dust and magic, the song no longer coming down the shaft but radiating from everywhere, from the very air, gorgeous and annihilating. Twice Emrys fell to their knees and had to claw their way back to their own name. *Emrys Ashgrove. Of Hollowmere. Speaker to beasts. Daughter-son-child of the valley that forgot. Warden's heir. Key.* The words like a rope thrown down a well.

At the top of the final flight stood a door unlike the others: tall, ancient, carved over every inch with spirals, and warm to the touch like the flank of a living animal.

Emrys laid both palms against it and gave it everything left.

*I am Emrys Ashgrove. The wards of Hollowmere were raised by my ancestors' voices. I heard the true king drowning in his stolen throat. Open for me. OPEN FOR ME.*

The door shuddered. Considered. And opened—not outward, not inward, but *away*, dissolving along its spiral seams into a ring of dust that hung in the starless air like a halo.

Wind hit Emrys full in the face.

They stepped through onto the crown of the Spire, onto a platform of white stone beneath the wheeling absence where the stars should have been—and the song, no longer filtered, no longer distant, took them the way the sea takes a swimmer.

King Aldric stood at the platform's center with his back turned, robes moving in wind that touched nothing else, arms raised to the hungry dark. His voice rang out over the city, beautiful and hollow, singing the stars down.

And beneath it—drowned, faint, frantic—Emrys heard it. The other voice. The true one.

*Ashgrove.* Cracked. Drowning. *Ashgrove—you came—you actually—*

Below, distantly, something exploded. The tower lurched. Somewhere in the dark above, feathers scattered against the void, and Sable screamed a warning Emrys couldn't yet parse.

But the king had begun to turn, and the thing wearing his face was smiling, and it knew Emrys's name.
