## 18. The Song That Eats Names

The Spire did not have stairs so much as it had a throat.

Emrys felt that with every step upward—the way the spiral passage narrowed and flexed around them, the walls warm and faintly damp, breathing in a rhythm too slow to be stone's own. Behind them, far below, Wren held the door they'd wedged with an iron bar and a prayer, and Sable was somewhere above in the dark, a scrap of shadow doing battle with things that had once been pigeons. The sounds drifted down: wings, shrieks, the wet percussion of something small and wrong being thrown against the wall.

*Keep climbing,* Sable had said, before she vanished into the dark. *Don't listen to anything until you have to.*

Emrys climbed. The bag on their shoulder grew heavier with every turn of the stair—Odile's scholars had packed it with chalk, salt-iron filings, a strip of vellum inscribed with the binding rite in three dead hands, and a knife that Wren had pressed into their palm at the bottom of the tower with a look that said everything her voice couldn't hold steady enough to say.

The air changed as they rose. It tasted of cold iron and old honey, sweet and metallic at once, and beneath it ran a vibration Emrys felt in their teeth before they heard it with their ears. A hum. A held note, sustained for years without breath.

They knew that note. They had been hearing its echo since midwinter night, when the stars went out and the animals screamed.

At the top of the stair there was no door. There was only an archway of black glass, and beyond it, the crown of the Spire opened like a flower cut from the sky—or rather, from where the sky used to be. The ceiling above was a dome of the same black glass, and where stars should have shown through it, there was nothing. Not cloud. Not night. An absence so complete it made Emrys's eyes water to look at it, a hole in the world shaped like heaven.

And standing in the center of that absence, on a dais of pale stone, his back to the archway, was King Aldric.

He was smaller than the coins made him look. That was Emrys's first thought, absurd and distant: *the king is smaller than his face.* He wore no armor, only a plain grey robe, and his hair was thinning at the crown, and he stood with his arms slightly away from his body, palms up, the posture of a man holding something invisible and very heavy. Around him the air shimmered with heat-haze that had no heat.

Emrys stopped at the edge of the archway, one hand flat against the cold glass frame, and did the thing they had spent twenty years learning not to do.

They listened.

* * *

It came all at once, the way a flood comes when the dam goes—not gradually but entirely, a wall of sound with no left or right, no near or far. The Choir did not sing *at* them. It sang *around* them, *through* them, as though Emrys had always been inside the song and had only now noticed the walls.

It was beautiful. That was the first betrayal of it, and Emrys understood in one white-hot instant why the refugees wept when they described it, why they described it at all, why some of them had walked back toward it. Every voice Emrys had ever heard was in it—their mother humming over the mending basket, Sable's dry rasp, the low complaint of Hollowmere's oxen at dusk, the whole animal chorus of their childhood—and woven through those, ten thousand voices more. Shepherds. Sailors. Children. Kings. All of them singing in harmony, all of them *glad*, and the gladness was the worst part, because it wasn't a lie. Emrys could hear that it wasn't a lie. Whatever the Choir took from people, it gave them this in exchange: a belonging so total that the word *alone* became meaningless, became a fossil-word from a dead language.

*You have been quiet so long,* the song said, and it said it in Emrys's own voice, which was somehow worse and better than if it had used another. *We heard you. Even behind the wards, even in the valley that forgets—we heard you, little key, little door, little voice that speaks to crows. Do you know how long we have listened for someone who could hear us back?*

Emrys gripped the glass archway. Their fingers were going numb; they couldn't tell if it was the cold or the song pulling feeling out of them thread by thread.

*I'm not listening,* they said aloud, and their voice sounded small and cracked in the vast dome, a pebble dropped into a cathedral.

*But you are,* the Choir sang, gently, the way a mother corrects a child who has denied taking the last of the bread while the crumbs are still on their chin. *You have always been listening. That is your whole self, isn't it? The valley told you it was a sickness. Hide it, hush it, starve it. But we know what it is. We remember what beast-speakers were, before the wardens were broken and scattered and taught to be ashamed. You are an ear. And an ear is not made for silence. An ear is made for music.*

The dais seemed closer. Emrys couldn't remember walking. Their boots whispered on black glass floor that hadn't been there a moment ago, or perhaps the room had moved around them, or perhaps distance simply didn't apply here, inside the song.

King Aldric stood ten feet away now, still with his palms up, still holding his invisible weight. His eyes were open. They were the color of skimmed milk, and they did not blink, and they did not see Emrys at all—or rather, they saw straight through, past, into whatever the Choir looked at when it looked at anything.

Behind the king's face, something else was happening. Emrys could see it now that they were close: a flicker under the skin, like a fish turning in shallow water. Like a man drowning behind glass, mouthing words no one reads.

*Look what we kept for you,* the Choir sang, and the milk-white eyes turned—not Aldric turning, but the thing wearing him, rotating his head with a smoothness no neck should allow. *A king. We know you have never wanted a kingdom—you are not built for wanting, are you? You are built for keeping. For tending. So tend this. A realm with no more hunger in it, no more hiding, no more child in a cellar whispering to mice and being told she is damned. Every gifted one, gathered. Every voice, joined. No one will ever again have to choose between their gift and their home.*

And oh, it knew. That was the horror Emrys would carry out of this room if they carried anything: the Choir had read them like a letter left open on a table. It knew about the nights they'd lain awake listening to the valley breathe and ached with the effort of not answering. It knew about their mother's face when Emrys was seven and asked, too loudly, why the goat was sad. It knew about every swallowed sentence, every bitten tongue, every year of a life spent folded up small enough to fit through the eye of Hollowmere's needle.

*Come,* the song said, and now it was not one voice but all of them, the whole drowned congregation, and they were singing Emrys's name—not the name their mother used, but the true one, the one Emrys had never spoken aloud because names have weight and this one had been too heavy to lift. *Emrys Ashgrove. Come home. You were never meant to live in a valley that forgets. You were meant to remember for everyone.*

Their hand was reaching out. They watched it reach, from somewhere slightly outside themselves, the way one watches weather. The king's raised palm was inches away. The skin of it was grey as ash, and beneath the skin the drowning thing thrashed—

*No.*

Not aloud. Not in any language with words. It came from inside the king, from the deep place behind the milk-white eyes, and it was not a shout. It was barely a sound at all. It was a thread of a voice, frayed nearly to nothing, worn down by years of screaming into a space no one entered.

It was singing.

One note. Cracked, wavering, almost gone—but placed with intention, bent with meaning, and Emrys's gift seized on it the way a drowning sailor seizes driftwood. Because it wasn't just a note. It was a name.

*Em-ris.*

Sung wrong. Sung by a stranger's ruined throat, in a dialect of the old warden-tongue that Emrys had only seen carved beneath chapel floors—but sung *to* them, aimed at them like an arrow loosed down a long dark hall, and it struck true.

The song faltered.

For one heartbeat—for one single suspended moment—the great harmony hiccupped, the way a choir falters when a soloist finds a note no one expected, and in that gap Emrys heard everything the Choir had been smothering: the king. Aldric. Not the hollowed puppet, not the smiling thing with too many teeth that Sable had fled from across half a kingdom, but the man underneath, the man who had been awake in there, *awake*, through all of it, through the burning of the star-charts and the unmaking of the heavens, holding himself together note by note the way a starving man hoards crumbs, waiting—hoping against hope—for an ear.

*You hear me.* The thread-voice shook with something that might have been laughter or weeping. *After all this time. You actually hear me.*

*Yes,* Emrys thought, or said, or sang—it hardly mattered which; inside the song they were the same. *I hear you.*

*Then listen carefully, warden's child, because I can only do this once.* The voice was gathering itself, spending itself, a candle burning down fast to give one last light. *It cannot be fought. Do you understand? Everything you push against, it takes into the song. Your fear, your anger, your grief—I fed it all of mine, years of it, and it only grew fatter. There is one thing it cannot digest.* The milk-white eyes flickered, and beneath them, briefly, human eyes looked out—brown, tired, terrified, alive. *Harmony. It eats every voice that stands apart. It has never met two voices that stand together.*

The Choir understood then. Emrys felt it understand—a shudder running through the dome, the black glass ceiling groaning, the beautiful song curdling at its edges into something with teeth. The king's body lurched. The invisible weight he'd been holding slammed down into his shoulders and he staggered, and the Choir poured itself forward through his mouth, a flood of every stolen voice at once:

*YOU ARE NOTHING ALONE. YOU HAVE ALWAYS BEEN NOTHING ALONE. COME BACK INTO US AND BE EVERYTHING—*

And Emrys stood in the flood, and for a moment—gods, for a long, shameless, sweet moment—they considered it.

They considered putting down the bag and the knife and the rite and the war. They considered letting go of the loneliness that had been their oldest companion, older than Sable, older than Wren, older than hope. The song opened before them like a valley, green and warm and lit, and every voice in it was saying *we saved you a place, we always saved you a place, why did you make yourself so small, why did you hide, come out, come out, you never have to be quiet again—*

It would be so easy. It would be like sleep.

*Em-ris.*

The second note came weaker than the first. The king was failing; Emrys could feel him guttering, a lamp with minutes of oil left. But the note landed, and this time Emrys heard what was wrapped inside it, what the dying man had spent his last strength to fold into the syllables: not a plea. An offering.

*Aldric,* Emrys sang back.

Their voice came out ragged, untrained, a valley-child's voice that had never sung anything but lullabies to frightened animals—and it didn't matter. In the old warden-tongue, in the language beneath chapel floors, the name meant *hearth-keeper*, and Emrys put every year of hiddenness into it, every swallowed word, every night they'd chosen silence and hated it, and they reached—not with their hand, though their hand found the king's cold fingers and gripped them—with everything they were.

Two notes. One throat that held two souls.

The Choir screamed.

It was not a beautiful scream. It was the sound of a thousand locked doors opening at once, of every stolen voice remembering itself, and the dome of black glass cracked from crown to foundation, and through the cracks Emrys saw—not stars, not yet, but the *places* where stars had been, wounds in the sky shaped exactly like missing light, waiting to be filled.

The king's grip crushed Emrys's fingers, bone grinding bone, and his mouth opened wide, wider, and out of it poured the Choir in a black torrent, tearing free, ripping loose, howling with a fury that shook the Spire to its roots—and Emrys held on, sang on, poured the rite off the vellum and into the wound between king and entity, salt-iron and chalk-dust and a name sung twice.

The torrent hit the floor and sank, gushing down between the pale stones, into the foundations, into the deep places of the Spire, wounded and shrieking and *not finished*—Emrys felt that with terrible clarity even as the last of it drained away. Not destroyed. Driven underground. Buried, furious, and patient as rot.

Then the king fell, and Emrys fell with him, and the song was gone so suddenly that the silence rang like a struck bell.

* * *

They lay together on the black glass, an old man and a valley-child, chests heaving. Above them the dome hung in shattered spiderwebs, and through the largest crack, faint and impossibly far away, something glimmered. One point of light. Small. Unsteady.

Like a candle deciding whether to stay lit.

"Is it—" Aldric's voice was a ruin, scraped raw, barely his own. He turned his head with visible effort, and his eyes—brown, human, flooded with tears—found Emrys's face. "Is it out?"

"It's out." Emrys's own voice was worse. "It's in the foundations. It's hurt. It's *angry*."

"Good," the king whispered, and began, horribly, to laugh—a broken, wheezing, unmistakably human laugh. "Let it be angry. I have been polite to it for six years."

Boots on the stair. Shouting—Wren's voice, ragged with relief and fury in equal measure, and the flutter of tired wings, and Sable landing hard on Emrys's chest, glaring down at them with one bright eye.

"You're bleeding," she said.

"I'm fine."

"You're *bleeding*, and the sky is cracking open, and something enormous just went *down* instead of *out*, which I do not like at all." Her claws pricked through Emrys's shirt, anchoring. "Also the king owes me eleven years of back payment and I intend to collect."

Below them, deep in the Spire's bones, something shifted. Settled. Began, very quietly, in a voice made of a thousand stolen throats, to sing again—softly, patiently, a lullaby it was teaching itself, a song it was rebuilding note by note in the dark.

Emrys closed their eyes and held on to the sound of Wren's boots, growing louder, coming for them, and tried to think of nothing at all but the climb back down.
