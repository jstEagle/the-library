## 19. Two Voices, One Throat

The song had Emrys by the throat before they reached the crown.

It came up through the Spire's bones like water through a drowned man's lungs—every stairwell, every seam of black stone humming with the same vast chord, and the chord had a shape, and the shape was *belonging*. Emrys clung to the railing of the final landing and felt their own name begin to loosen at its edges, like a knot worked at by patient fingers. *Emrys.* What was that, really? A sound a mother made in a valley that no longer existed on any map. A small thing. The Choir could hold so much more.

*Let go,* it sang, in a voice like ten thousand throats agreeing at once. *You have been alone your whole life. We are never alone.*

Somewhere below, iron screamed against iron—Wren, holding the door, holding it, holding it—and somewhere above, wings beat against air gone thick as tar. Sable. They were both spending themselves to buy this one chance, and Emrys stood on the threshold of the ritual chamber doing nothing but listening.

That was the trick of it. That was the trap.

The chamber opened around them like the inside of a bell: ribbed stone rising into darkness, and at its center, on a dais of fused glass where starlight should have pooled and did not, King Aldric stood with his arms raised toward the ceiling he was unmaking. The crown on his head was not gold. It was a lattice of pale filaments, root-thin and endless, running up through the stone, down through the stone, out through the walls into the world—the Choir's grip made visible at last, the way a current is only visible when something is caught in it.

Aldric's face was calm. That was the worst part. Not the too-many-teeth smile from Sable's telling, not the burning eyes of the posters—just calm, the terrible serenity of a man who believes himself saved.

Emrys took one step forward, and the song turned its full attention on them, and the world went white.

---

They were drowning again. They knew this drowning now; they had felt its edge in the audience hall, beneath the king's ringing decrees, a faint voice going under. But here at the source there was no edge. The song poured into every hollow Emrys had ever carried—the empty chair across the table, the words swallowed behind their teeth for twenty years, the valley that loved them and would have let them die rather than speak. The Choir filled those hollows like warm wax. *We will hold all of it,* it promised. *You need never carry yourself again.*

And Emrys felt themselves beginning to agree. Felt their name thinning. Felt the borders of their body going soft as a map left out in rain, coastlines dissolving, *Emrys Ashgrove of Hollowmere* becoming simply a note in a chord that had been sounding since before the first star was lit—

A voice said their name.

Not the Choir's voice. A human voice, cracked and small and impossibly far away, the way a candle is far away at the bottom of a well. It said *Emrys* the way their mother said it when she thought they were asleep—softly, without expectation, just to feel the shape of it in her mouth.

Aldric. What remained of Aldric, at the bottom of his own stolen body, still singing.

He wasn't begging anymore. Emrys realized that with a shock that cut through the white. In the audience hall he had begged. But down here, hearing another soul caught in the same net, the king had done what trapped things do: he had reached toward it. He was singing Emrys's name back to them, over and over, a thread thrown across dark water, and though the Choir's song was vast beyond reckoning, it had one flaw Emrys could see from inside it—it was a chord, and a chord is many voices pretending to be one, and a pretense can be harmonized with or pulled apart.

Their gift had never been a curse. It had never even been a key, not exactly. It was an ear. And an ear that truly listens does not fight the music.

*Oh,* Emrys thought, with a clarity that felt like falling. *Oh, we don't tear it out. We sing it loose.*

---

They found the note.

Not with their mind—with everything older than their mind, the place where beast-speech lived, the wordless grammar they'd spoken to mules and crows and terrified hounds made of screaming people. Every creature they had ever talked to had understood them because underneath all language there is one shared thing: the wish to be heard. The Choir had forgotten it. The Choir only spoke; it did not listen; it had grown so large that listening had become impossible, the way a king cannot hear a single subject.

But Aldric was listening. Aldric had been listening for years from the bottom of his own silence, and he had never once stopped.

Emrys opened their throat and matched him.

It was not a shout. It was barely a sound. They found the king's thread—that cracked, persistent singing of their name—and instead of pulling against it, they slid alongside it, a second voice a hair's breadth off, the way two bells an instant apart make a beat you can feel in your teeth. The dissonance was tiny. The dissonance was everywhere. Because the Choir's chord was built on perfect agreement, one false harmonic ran through it like a crack through ice, branching and branching, and every branch was a question the Choir had no answer for: *what if a voice belongs to itself?*

The white light shuddered.

*What are you doing,* the Choir sang, and for the first time the ten thousand throats were not quite together. *Stop. Stop. You are ours. You came to us. You wanted—*

"I wanted," Emrys said aloud, and their voice sounded enormous in the bell-shaped dark, "to be *heard*. Not held. There's a difference. You wouldn't know. You've never once listened."

They sang harder. Not louder—truer. They gave the harmony everything Hollowmere had taught them: twenty years of swallowed words, every conversation with Sable on the fence rail, every whispered comfort to spooked livestock, the whole secret vocabulary of a life spent listening when no one knew. And Aldric heard it—felt it arrive alongside his own thread—and the drowned man at the bottom of the well found, impossibly, a hand.

He began to climb.

Emrys felt him do it, felt the king's will gather itself out of years of fog, and it was not strong, it was ragged and half-dissolved and weeping without eyes, but it was *his*, and he set it against the Choir from the inside while Emrys pulled from the outside, and between them they found the seam.

The crown-filaments lit white. The whole Spire rang like a struck bell. Somewhere far below, Wren was shouting something—Emrys couldn't hear the words, only the fierce animal defiance in them—and Sable's wings tore through the dark overhead, and Emrys planted both feet on the glass dais, gripped the nearest filament with bare hands, and *sang the tearing note*.

It was not gentle. Nothing true ever is.

The Choir came loose the way a tooth comes loose—not cleanly, with a sound felt in the spine more than the ears, roots ripping free of bone they had grown into. Aldric screamed. Emrys screamed. For one endless moment they were the same scream, king and beast-speaker hanging in the bell of the world, and then the filaments snapped back through the stone like severed tendons, recoiling, retreating, pouring down into the Spire's foundations in a flood of pale light that dimmed as it went—wounded, howling in frequencies that cracked the glass dais, dragging its torn chord into the deep places under the city to lick its wounds in the dark.

The crown fell.

It hit the dais and shattered into ordinary gold, dull as a plow-blade, and King Aldric collapsed into Emrys's arms like a coat slipping off a hook.

---

For a long time there was only breathing. His, ragged and shallow. Theirs, wracking. The chamber was silent in a way Emrys had no memory of any place being silent—the song was gone, actually gone, and the absence rang louder than its presence ever had.

"Your Majesty." Emrys's voice came out ruined, sanded raw. They lowered him gently onto the broken dais. Up close, freed of the Choir's serenity, his face was a ruin of ordinary human feeling—confusion, grief, shame, arriving all at once after years of numbness. His eyes, when they opened, were wet and unfocused and *his own*, brown and tired and frightened.

"Hollowmere," he whispered.

Emrys went cold. "What?"

"The wards." His hand crawled across the glass and caught Emrys's sleeve with shocking strength. "I remember—I was in the Spire, the first night, I heard it choose, and it showed me—" He coughed, and the cough bent him double. "It showed me the map of everything it had already eaten. Stars. Warden-lines. Your valley was a hole in its web, girl, boy, *child*, whatever you are—it couldn't see in. So it waited. Years. It sent things to the border to worry the old wards loose, like a wolf circling a pen, and I watched, I *watched* from inside my own skull, and I could not—" His voice broke entirely. "I could not warn anyone. Do you understand? I have been awake in there the whole time."

Emrys understood. Oh, they understood better than he could know. They had spent a lifetime awake inside their own silence.

"The wards didn't fail," Aldric rasped. "They were *unmade*. Slowly. From outside. Which means—" He gripped harder. "Which means the thing knows where the warden-lines run. All of them. It has been mapping us for years, the way your kind once mapped it. And it is wounded now, but wounded things in the deep places do not die, they *burrow*."

Above them, wings. Sable dropped out of the dark and landed hard on the shattered dais, feathers scorched, one eye swollen shut, and fixed Emrys with the good one.

"You're alive," she croaked. "Huh. I had money on the big glowing horror, honestly."

"Sable—"

"Don't. Don't 'Sable' me yet." She hopped closer, and her voice dropped into something rawer than sarcasm. "You were gone. In the song. I saw your face—you weren't in it anymore. And then you were, and you were *singing*, of all the cursed things, and the whole Spire started coming apart like a wet loaf." She looked at the fallen crown, the dead gold, the cracks crawling up the chamber walls. "What did you do?"

"We didn't fight it." Emrys stared at their own hands. They were shaking, and faintly, faintly, at the very edge of hearing, something in their skull was still humming—a cold thread of the chord that had lodged there when they gripped the filaments, quiet now, patient, waiting. They pushed the awareness down where it couldn't reach their face. "We sang it out. Both of us. Together."

"Then it can be sung back in," Aldric whispered, "or sung elsewhere. It learns, beast-speaker. Faster than anything living." He tried to rise and failed, and laughed—a broken, astonished sound. "I can't feel my legs. Do you know, I think I'm *glad*. They haven't been mine in so long that numbness feels like getting them back."

Boots on the stairs—Wren, gray-faced, blood sheeting down one arm from a wound she clearly hadn't noticed, sword still drawn, taking the last steps at a stagger. She saw the king on the ground and stopped as if she'd hit glass.

"Your Majesty." Her voice did something complicated. She had served him; she had watched him change; she had spent years believing she'd failed him. She knelt, awkwardly, wincing, then seemed to realize kneeling was absurd with the man half-dead on a shattered altar, and stayed down anyway.

"Calloway." Aldric squinted at her. "You look terrible."

"You're one to talk, sir."

"They burned your maps." He said it suddenly, urgently, as if the thought had been waiting years for a mouth. "All of them. But I memorized the master index before it took my voice. Every warden-line, every node, every anchor-stone of the old network. It's all still in here—" he tapped his temple, and his hand trembled, "—in the part it couldn't reach. I can give it back. I can give you all of it. If someone writes while I speak. If I live long enough."

Wren looked at Emrys. Emrys looked at Wren. Somewhere beneath them, deep in the Spire's foundations, something vast shifted its weight in the dark and went still, listening.

Sable hopped onto Emrys's shoulder, leaned close, and murmured, so softly only a beast-speaker could have caught it:

"It's still in your head, isn't it. I can smell it on you. Cold and choral." A pause. "We'll deal with that next. One miracle at a time."

Below, the shifting came again—closer, or deeper, impossible to say—and the first crack of dawn light found its way through the broken ceiling and lay across the three of them like a thin gold blade.

Aldric closed his eyes. "Then write quickly," he said. "Before it finishes learning what we just taught it."
