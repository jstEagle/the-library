## 20. The Price of the Key

Emrys woke to a room that was not singing, and understood immediately that something was wrong.

It took them three heartbeats to find the source of the wrongness, and when they did, they almost wished they hadn't. The silence in the sickroom at the top of Odile's safehouse was real—the walls were stone, the windows shuttered against the smoke-hazed capital dawn, Wren asleep in a chair pulled close to the bed. But beneath the silence, threaded through it like cold wire through wool, there it was: a hum. Faint. Patient. A chord held by a thousand distant throats, so far away it might have been weather, might have been the sea.

Except Emrys knew what the sea sounded like now, and it did not sing.

They lay very still and listened to it breathe inside their own skull.

*You are ours,* said nothing, in no voice, and yet the meaning arrived whole, the way meanings always arrived in beast-speech—wordless, certain, warm as a hand on the back of the neck. *You heard us. You harmonized with us. What has once been sung cannot be unsung.*

*Watch me,* Emrys thought back, and the chorus rippled—not laughter exactly, but the place where laughter would be if laughter had been flayed of all joy.

The rite had worked. That was the thing they had to hold onto, the splinter of truth they could build a life around. The Choir was torn loose from King Aldric; the man who had worn the crown like an empty glove for two years had wept on the Spire floor, whispering his daughter's name over and over as though checking that his mouth still belonged to him. The Spire's foundations groaned and settled as something vast retreated into the deep places beneath the city, wounded, hungry, but *out*. The stars had not returned yet—Wren said the wardens' network would take seasons to rebuild—but last night, for the first time since midwinter, the sky above the capital had been merely dark instead of *devoured*, and dark sky was a wound, and wounds healed.

And the price of that healing lived behind Emrys's eyes.

They sat up slowly. Every muscle reported in, aching, as if they had climbed the Spire with their body instead of only their soul. Their hands were bandaged—when had that happened?—and the palms stung with the particular deep burn of magic spent past all sense of measure. On the bedside table, someone had left water, bread, and a sprig of dried feverfew arranged with a care that could only be Wren's.

Wren herself was asleep in the chair with her boots still on, her head tipped back, one hand resting open on her knee as though she had fallen asleep mid-gesture, mid-argument, mid-insistence that Emrys not do the foolish brave thing they had done anyway. There was ash ground into the creases of her knuckles and a fresh cut across her brow where a piece of the doorframe—her doorframe, the door she had held—had found her. She looked ten years older than she had in Fenwick, drunk and furious at her maps. She looked, Emrys thought, like someone who had paid a debt she'd been carrying for two years, and didn't yet know what to do with the lightness.

Emrys reached for the water, and the chorus stirred.

It wasn't loud. That was the horror of it. It was *quiet*—a lullaby-volume thing, a murmur under floors, and it rose gently whenever Emrys reached toward the world, as though it too wanted to reach, wanted to be included, wanted to help. *Let us carry it,* it murmured as Emrys's fingers closed around the cup. *We are strong. We are many. You will never be tired again. You will never be alone.*

Emrys set the cup down without drinking and pressed both bandaged palms flat against their temples, and did the thing Odile's oldest scholar had taught them yesterday, in a voice like dry leaves: find the edge of it. Find where you end.

*Where I end.* They closed their eyes and went looking.

Beast-speech had always taught them this, though they'd never had the words for it until now. To speak with a mule or a crow or a grieving hillside was first to know the shape of your own boundary—to stand inside yourself and feel where your skin of self met the world's great weather of other voices. For nineteen years that boundary had been a wall they hid behind. Now it had to become a blade.

They found the edge of themselves the way you find the edge of a stream in flood—by wading out until the current pushed back. And there, just past the boundary, coiled and waiting and *singing*, was the Choir's fragment: the part of the song that had poured into the open door of their mind during those endless minutes on the Spire's crown, when they and a dying king had harmonized against a god.

*Good,* the fragment sighed. *You found us. Now rest.*

"No," Emrys said aloud, in the small cracked voice of a person alone in a room, and pushed.

Pushing was not shouting. Pushing was not force. Pushing was what Sable did every morning when she preened—precise, unhurried, absolute attention applied feather by feather—and Emrys applied it now, gathering the edges of themselves inward, smoothing the boundary taut, and singing back.

Not the Choir's music. Never that. They sang the way the valley sang. The low patient drone of Hollowmere's hills in autumn. The gossip-clatter of sparrows in the hedgerows. Their mother's off-key humming over the washbasin. The particular grumble of old Bramble the mule, may he live forever, complaining about rain. Small sounds. Home sounds. Sounds that had never once asked Emrys to be anything but exactly what they were.

The chorus recoiled, hissing static, and then—this was the worst part—it *adjusted*. Softened. Wrapped itself smaller and quieter and waited, the way a hound waits at the edge of firelight.

Emrys opened their eyes, sweating, shaking slightly, and drank the water in four long swallows.

Every morning, then. Maybe every morning for the rest of their life. They would find the edge of themselves, and they would sing the valley into the space the Choir wanted to occupy, and the Choir would wait, and they would do it again tomorrow. Survival bought with silence had never been living—but survival bought with *song*, daily, deliberately, chosen—that, maybe, that they could work with.

Across the room, Wren stirred.

"You're doing it," she said, before her eyes were even fully open. Her voice was gravel and smoke. "The exercise. I can tell because you look like you're arm-wrestling something invisible."

"How long have you been awake?"

"Since you started arguing with the furniture." She hauled herself upright with a wince, crossed to the bed, and pressed the back of her hand to Emrys's forehead with the brisk tenderness of someone who had clearly been doing it all night. "No fever. Good. Eat the bread. All of it—I don't care that you're not hungry, you burned through more of yourself than any speaker in the archive records, and Odile's scholars keep using the word 'unprecedented' in tones that frighten me."

Emrys ate the bread. It was stale and wonderful. Wren dragged her chair closer, turned it around, and straddled it backward with her arms folded on the rest, studying Emrys with those cartographer's eyes that measured everything, that had once measured coastlines and now measured people.

"Tell me honestly," she said. "Scale of one to ten. How much of it is still in there?"

Emrys considered lying. Considered how easy it would be, how Wren had earned a lie if anyone had. Then they remembered the premise of everything—all of this, the flight from Hollowmere, the hound in the tall grass, the climb up the Spire while Sable's wings beat smoke into the faces of things that should not have faces. They had spent their whole life buying safety with silence. They were done purchasing.

"A three," they said. "This morning. Yesterday it was a five. Odile says it may settle lower. It may also—" they made themselves say it, "—worsen. If I'm careless. If I listen to it. If I ever let myself *harmonize*."

"And if you're careful?"

"Then I get to keep being me." They turned the bread crust in their fingers. "That's the price of the key, apparently. Wren, I unlocked the door, and the lock bit me, and now I carry its teeth."

Wren was quiet a moment. Outside the shutters, the city went about its borrowed-light business—carts, bells, a crier hoarsely announcing the king's recovery as though repeating it enough times would make the sky make sense again. When Wren spoke, her voice had gone rough in a different way.

"I watched him disappear for two years," she said. "Aldric. Sat across the council table from whatever was wearing him and drew maps of roads for it and told myself the king was just tired, just changed, just *kingly*, because the alternative was admitting I served a hollow thing and my mentor had been right and I'd helped bury her for saying so." Her jaw tightened. "You went into that Spire knowing what it cost. You came out with the king and with *that*." She nodded at Emrys's temple. "I've been sitting here all night trying to decide if I'm grateful or if I'm furious, and I've concluded I'm both, and that both is probably what caring about someone feels like when you're honest about it."

Emrys felt their throat go tight. "Furious at me?"

"At you. At the Choir. At the archive scholar who invented a rite with a warning label. At myself for holding that door instead of climbing it myself, which I couldn't have done, because I don't have your gift, which is precisely why you had to do it, which doesn't help at all." She exhaled. "Mostly at the world. The world deserves it."

Something loosened in Emrys's chest—a knot they'd been carrying since midwinter night, since their mother's face in the doorway, since the animals screaming in a language only their child understood. They hadn't realized how much they'd needed someone to be furious *with* them rather than *at* them.

"The letter I wrote my mother," they said quietly. "Before the ascent. Did it—"

"It's in my coat. I kept it safe." Wren patted the battered satchel leaning against the chair leg. "I also took the liberty of reading the address. Fenwick has courier lines running east again as of two days ago. When you're ready. No rush. Or—" a crooked half-smile, the first Emrys had seen on her face in what felt like a year, "—or we deliver it ourselves, eventually, and you watch her face, and I finally get to see the famous valley everyone's wards were so smug about."

*Home,* said the chorus softly, sliding toward the word like a hand toward an unguarded door. *We could show you home. We remember every valley. We are older than valleys—*

Emrys found their edge. Sang the hills. Sang the sparrows. Held, held, held.

Wren watched them do it and did not look away, which Emrys loved her for. Most people, they suspected, would learn to glance elsewhere during the mornings' silent struggle. Wren watched like she read maps: entirely, without flinching, memorizing terrain she intended to walk beside you through.

"There," Emrys breathed at last, wrung out. "Quiet again."

"For how long?"

"Until tomorrow."

"Then tomorrow we do it again." Wren said it simply, like scheduling a departure. "And the day after. However many tomorrows it takes. That's not a burden, Emrys. That's just a road. I've walked longer ones with worse company."

* * *

Sable arrived through the gap in the shutters at midmorning, in a slant of thin grey light, and landed on the bedpost with the delicacy of a duchess entering a sickroom she disapproved of.

"You look terrible," she observed.

"You're welcome," Emrys said.

"Don't thank me, I haven't finished judging you." But her head tilted, one bright black eye fixing on Emrys with an intensity that had nothing of jest in it, and when she spoke again her mind-voice had dropped its sharpness entirely. "Show me."

Emrys hesitated—then opened the boundary a crack, let her look. It was a strange intimacy, stranger than speech: Sable's awareness brushing along the edge of theirs, finding the cold coiled fragment, mapping it the way Wren mapped coastlines. She withdrew after a long moment.

"It's smaller than yesterday."

"You can tell that?"

"I led the Choir to the edge of your valley once, little speaker. I carried its song in my skull for a season. I know its weight the way you know your own feathers." She hopped down onto the blanket, closer, and began—with great ceremony—to preen a stray strand of Emrys's hair into order, which was as close as a crow came to an embrace. "It shrinks when you starve it. Feed it nothing. Not fear, not loneliness, not even curiosity. Curiosity especially. It dresses itself up as curiosity sometimes. As wonder. As *home*."

"I know."

"Do you? Because it wore Aldric for two years, and he is not a fool, and it still ate him." Her beak tugged gently at another strand. "I keep watch. That is my penance and my privilege and you do not get a vote in it. While you sleep, I sleep with one eye open. While you sing it quiet, I listen for whether it sings back. This is not negotiable. I have already informed the cartographer. She tried to give me orders. I let her believe they were accepted."

From the corner, Wren, without looking up from the map she was amending by lamplight: "I heard that."

"Wings have excellent hearing. It's the ears that are decorative."

Emrys laughed—a real laugh, startled out of them, and immediately regretted it, because the chorus stirred at the sound like a sleeper at noise, reaching, wanting, *give us that too, give us everything*—and they had to spend a long minute smoothing their boundary again while Sable stood guard on the blanket and glared at the inside of Emrys's skull as though she could peck eyes out through bone.

When it passed, Sable said quietly: "It will try to take your joys first. Remember that. Laughter, love, home—it wants the warm rooms of you. Guard them hardest."

"I know," Emrys said again, and this time meant it all the way through. "But here's the thing, Sable. It's in *my* house now. And in my house, we do things the Hollowmere way."

"Which is?"

"We're stubborn, we're quiet, and we outlast everything."

* * *

Evening came down over the capital like a held breath. From the sickroom window, unshuttered now by Wren's decree—"you need to see the sky, you fought for the damn sky"—Emrys watched the city kindle its ten thousand lamps, the borrowed light that had kept humanity alive through the starless season. Somewhere south, the Spire stood dark and quiet, its foundations humming with a wounded god. Somewhere north, a king slept in a bed he hadn't truly occupied in two years, waking hourly to touch his own hands and confirm they answered only to him.

Somewhere far east, past mountains and borders and a ward-line that no longer existed, a valley called Hollowmere was going about its evening without knowing its most silent child had broken the sky open and paid for it in cold coin.

Emrys touched their temple. Beneath the bandage, behind the bone, the fragment waited—patient, diminished, singing its small cold song to itself in the dark.

Tomorrow they would sing the valley back at it. Tomorrow, and the day after, however many it took.

They were still learning the shape of the new arithmetic of their life when Sable, roosting on the sill with the last light gilding her feathers, went suddenly rigid—and spoke in a voice stripped of all her usual armor:

"Emrys. There's a rider coming up the causeway. Fast. Palace colors."

Wren was already on her feet, hand going to her satchel, map forgotten. "At this hour? After everything—"

"She's carrying something white," Sable said. "A banner. And Emrys—" the crow turned, and her eye caught the lamplight and held it, burning, "—the horse is screaming. Not neighing. *Screaming.* In words. It's saying they've found another one. It's saying the Choir left something behind in the Spire, and it's awake."
