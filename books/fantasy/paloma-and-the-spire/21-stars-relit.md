## 21. Stars, Relit

* * *

**Wren**

The maps came back to her on the ninth day after the Spire, carried in a lacquered chest by a palace clerk who would not meet her eyes.

Every chart she had ever drawn for the Crown—the coastlines of the Sundered Reach, the river-roads of the interior, and above all the star-charts, forty years of them in her own hand and her mentor's hand before that—stacked and sorted and smelling faintly of the vault where they'd been locked away since the night King Aldric burned the copies in the courtyard. These were the originals. The ones Odile's people had smuggled out one at a time over three years, folded into cloak linings and hymnals and the false bottoms of wine crates, because someone had understood even then what Wren had only begun to suspect: that a kingdom that loses its stars loses its way home, and someone intended for the way home to survive.

She spread them across the long table in the map-room of the rebuilt chancellery, all of them, edge to edge, until the wood disappeared beneath ink and vellum and the ghost-light of a hundred candle stubs. Her hands shook. She told herself it was the cold.

"You're crying over paper," said Sable from the windowsill, where she had taken up residence as though the room belonged to her, which in a sense it now did. "I've seen you do this before. It was also over paper."

"It's not—" Wren pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes. "These are wrong."

"Wrong?"

"Look." She pulled a chart toward the light, one of her earliest, drawn when she was nineteen and certain of everything. The constellations marched across it in their old formations, the Antlered Hart, the Drowned Sisters, the Spindle and its thread of seven stars running north-south like a seam through the sky. "This is how they were. Before. But the Choir didn't just *put out* the stars—it ate them in a pattern. It started here—" her finger touched the Spindle, "—and worked outward, oldest lights first, because the oldest stars sit closest to whatever's underneath the Spire. Which means if we're going to relight them, we don't relight them all at once. We relight them in reverse order of its hunger. There's a sequence. There's always a sequence."

Sable ruffled her feathers, which was how she shrugged. "And you know the sequence because you spent three years mapping an absence."

"I mapped the wound," Wren said. "You can learn a great deal about a blade from the shape of the cut."

It was true, and it was also penance, and she no longer troubled herself to separate the two. For years she had believed her disgrace was failure—maps confiscated, mentor vanished, name struck from the rolls. Now she knew it had been worse than failure. She had *seen* the king change and said nothing loud enough. She had redrawn the star-charts without the missing constellations because she was ordered to, and her hand had obeyed as smoothly as if the sky itself had agreed to shrink. Every obedient line she had ever drawn had helped make the dark seem natural.

Never again. She dipped her pen.

Outside the map-room window, the capital went about its evening under lamplight as it had for two years—shop shutters closing, watchmen calling the hour, the great bells of the Chantry ringing flat and tuneless because their bronze had been recast during the occupation and no one could remember the original tones. A city of borrowed light. But beneath the borrowed light something else moved now: carts arriving from the valley provinces, wagons loaded not with grain but with people. Beast-speakers, summoned by royal decree, traveling openly on roads where two months ago their gift had been a death sentence.

Wren watched a family climb down from a hay cart in the square below—a mother, two children, a boy of perhaps eight with a goat following him like a shadow. The boy stopped in the middle of the square, tipped his head back, and listened to something no one else could hear. Then he laughed, and the goat laughed too, or made the sound goats make when they are laughing, and the mother put her hand on his hair and did not hush him.

*There,* Wren thought, and wrote the first line of the new chart, the one that would hang in every schoolhouse in the kingdom before spring: not a map of the sky as it had been, but of the sky as it would be, star by relit star, each one marked with the name of the warden whose voice would call it home.

Her mentor had taught her that maps were promises. She had forgotten. She was learning again.

* * *

**Sable**

The tower was warm, and the king's windowsill was the best windowsill in the world, and Sable did not intend to surrender it to any sparrow, crow, or archangel that might contest the claim.

She had earned it. She reminded herself of this hourly, because old habits died hard and the oldest habit of all was the whisper at the base of her skull that said: *you do not belong among the kept things. You belong to the cold. You led it to the valley's edge once, and only penance stands between what you were and what you'll be.*

Penance looked like this: sitting guard while a broken king slept, listening with the half of her that still remembered the Choir's grammar, so that if anything stirred in the Spire's foundations—if anything sang up through the stone—she would hear it first and scream it loudest.

The king slept badly. All his kind did now; the freed ones, the survivors, the ones who had been worn like coats by something older than hunger. He slept in short drowning intervals and woke with his hands closed around air, and twice he had called for his brother, who had been dead eleven years, and once he had called for his mother in a voice pitched high and young, and Sable had hopped along the sill and murmured crow-nothings until his breathing steadied. He never thanked her. He wasn't sure she was real. That was fine. Half the time she wasn't sure either.

Tonight he woke early, before the lamps were lit, and lay staring at the ceiling with those eyes that had been empty for two years and were now full of a terrible weather.

"Crow," he said.

"Sire."

"Do you dream? Birds. Do you dream?"

"We dream of carrion and shiny things and falling. Sometimes all three at once."

A sound escaped him that had once been a laugh and was working its way back. He sat up slowly, joints cracking like green wood, and looked out the window at the dark. Not at the city—at the sky above it, the vast unmarked dark where the stars had been, where for two years there had been nothing but the Choir's slow feeding and the black.

"The scholars came again," he said. "Your cartographer's sequence. They believe it will work. They say the wardens' network can be rebuilt—that the stones remember, that the old carvings show how it was done before the Choir ever wore a crown." His hands turned over in his lap, studying them as if they belonged to a stranger. "They need a speaker to lead it. The girl. Emrys."

"They need Emrys," Sable corrected. "There's a difference, and I'd thank you to learn it. People have needed that one their whole life. Nobody has asked what Emrys needs until quite recently."

"And does anyone know?" The question was soft, genuine, and it caught Sable off-guard, the way the king occasionally did—not Aldric the vessel, not the smiling thing with too many teeth, but the drowned man who had fought his way up through two years of song because a beast-speaker sang his own name back to him. "What they need?"

Sable thought of Emrys down in the chancellery archives, surrounded by scholars and scrolls, learning the old tongue of the wardens with an appetite that frightened everyone who loved them—including, especially, Sable. Learning it the way a person learns the shape of a door they intend to walk through.

"They need to finish something," she said at last. "That's enough for tonight."

Below the window, the city's borrowed lights burned in their patient thousands. And far to the north, past the walls, past the hills, on the spine of the mountain where the first warden-stone had been raised again out of rubble and memory, a single figure stood waiting for dusk with a crow's sister perched on the rocks nearby, watching, reporting, waiting for the word.

Soon, Sable thought. Soon the sky would owe them everything, and they would collect.

* * *

**Emrys**

The warden-stone was taller than Emrys had imagined, taller than the carvings beneath the chapel floor had promised, a finger of granite as wide as a cottage and scarred with channels where old light had once run like water. They had helped raise it—or rather, they had stood among forty masons and six beast-speakers and a very tired king's engineer and spoken to the stone in the deep grammar, and the stone had remembered its purpose the way an old dog remembers its name, shivering upright out of the rubble with a groan that everyone present felt in their teeth.

Now it waited. They all waited. Dusk pooled in the valleys below; the capital glimmered far to the south, a scatter of embers on the plain; and overhead the sky hung black and absolute, the way it had for two years, the way it had hung over Hollowmere on midwinter night when Emrys had packed a bag and walked out of the only home they'd ever known.

Their mother's letter was still folded inside their coat. Unsent. Perhaps unsayable. Some things you carried instead of delivering.

"You're doing the face," Sable said, dropping onto the stone beside them.

"What face?"

"The one where you're about to volunteer for something catastrophic and pretend it's a reasonable decision."

"This is a reasonable decision. The scholars checked the rite four times. The risk is contained."

"The risk involves your mind being used as a bridge between the world and the heavens, which the last person who attempted it did not survive, and 'contained' is a word invented by people who want other people to stop arguing." Sable preened, aggressively casual. "Also, Wren is climbing up here, and she has been rehearsing an argument for the last quarter mile, and I thought you'd want warning."

"Wren signed off on this."

"Wren signed a piece of paper. Wren's actual opinion is being carried up this hill alongside her actual boots."

Emrys almost smiled. Almost. Then Wren crested the ridge, breathless, map-case banging against her hip, and the smile died of natural causes, because Wren's expression was not the expression of a woman carrying an argument. It was the expression of a woman carrying news.

"The scouts," she said, before she'd fully caught her breath. "The ones watching the Spire. There's movement in the foundations—deep movement, the kind the hounds make when they're being called. And the stone—" she pressed her hand flat against the granite, as if she could read it by touch, "—it's been humming since sundown. Emrys says the stone's been humming since sundown. I felt it from the road."

Emrys had felt it too, and had been pretending otherwise, the way they had once pretended not to hear animals screaming, the way hiding had been the whole architecture of their life. They set their palm against the warden-stone and let themselves listen properly.

Beneath the hum: fear. Old, deep, mineral fear. The stone remembered the Choir. The stone remembered what it cost to hold heaven open, and it knew that tonight, when Emrys reached into the network and pulled the first star back into burning, everything wounded and furious in the Spire's foundations would feel the pull like a hook behind the eye—and come.

"Then we do it fast," Emrys said. "And we don't do it alone."

They looked at the gathering below: Wren with her charts and her guilt finally laid down; the six speakers, farmers and fisherfolk and a former smuggler, each of whom had spent a lifetime being told their voice was a sickness; Sable, who had served the darkness and chosen the light and never once since asked for forgiveness, only for work. And farther down the slope, riding up through the last light with an honor guard he had refused to leave behind, the king himself, moving slowly, wrapped in wool, determined to stand witness to the first dawn of the sky in two years even if it killed him, which his physicians assured him it might.

"No," Emrys said quietly, to the stone, to the dark, to the thing sleeping in the deep places that was already beginning to sing. "Not alone. Never alone again."

* * *

**Aldric**

He had stood on battlefields. He had stood at his father's funeral in the rain, and at his coronation before ten thousand strangers, and on the night the Choir took him he had stood in the heart of the Spire and watched his own hands begin to move without him. None of it had prepared him for standing on a cold mountainside, wrapped in a blanket like an invalid, watching a young person from a hidden valley speak to a rock.

Except that it was not speaking to a rock. He understood that now, in the way a man who has been underwater understands water. Emrys's voice slid into a register below language, and the stone answered—not in sound, in *light*, a slow pulse rising through the carved channels like sap rising in spring—and then the answering pulses came back from the other five stones strung across the hills, faint and far, a heartbeat finding its rhythm, a network waking after two years of death.

And above it all, the sky waited, black as a closed eye.

"Your Majesty." Wren Calloway stood at his shoulder with a chart in her hands, and he saw that her fingers trembled, and he remembered—God help him, he remembered—the day he had ordered those same charts burned, had smiled while they burned, had felt the Choir purring approval through his stolen throat. He had apologized to her once, badly. He suspected he would be apologizing to her for the rest of his life, and that this was correct.

"Cartographer," he said. "Tell me what happens."

"When the network closes, the wardens' voices go up through the stones into the old paths. The first star relights at the crown of the Spindle—the smallest one, the one the Choir ate last, because it's the easiest door to reopen." She swallowed. "After that, the rest follow, night by night, as fast as the speakers can bear it. Months. Perhaps a year. But the sky comes back, Your Majesty. Star by star. It comes back."

"And the Choir."

Wren was quiet a moment. Below them, Emrys raised both arms, and the six speakers joined hands around the stone's base, and the humming deepened into something that was nearly music, nearly grief, nearly joy.

"The Choir is wounded and hungry and it has just heard the sky begin to close over its head," Wren said. "What happens next depends entirely on whether we flinch."

Aldric looked at his hands. Steady. His own. He had been a puppet so long that owning his own hands still felt like a miracle granted on loan.

"Then we don't flinch," he said.

Emrys spoke a word that was older than the kingdom, older than the language of kings, and the stone drank it down, and the light ran the channels and leapt the gap between hills and answered stone to stone to stone, a chain of small suns along the dark earth—

—and above them, in the crown of the dead Spindle, in the exact place where the last star had gone out, a point of white heat opened in the blackness like an eye remembering how to see.

One star. Small. Stubborn. Burning.

The sound that rose from the mountainside—from the speakers, from the soldiers, from the king himself, who found he was weeping without shame—was not cheering. It was something older and rawer, the noise people make when a long-held breath finally leaves the body.

And far below, deep beneath the city, in the wounded roots of the Spire, something screamed—not with fury, Aldric thought, but with *hunger*, hearing heaven close its first door.

Emrys lowered their arms, swaying, blood threading from one nostril, grinning like the dawn.

"One down," they said. "Nine hundred and ninety-nine to go."
