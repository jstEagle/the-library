## 22. Home, Louder Than Before

The valley announced itself long before Emrys could see it.

They felt it first in their teeth—a loosening, a quieting, like a hand unclenching after years of holding something too tightly. Then the birds began to change. For three days out of Fenwick the crows had flown with them in loose, curious spirals, chattering news of roads and rivers and the great kindled star that now burned over the capital every night, bright enough to cast shadows. But as the road climbed into the hills above Hollowmere, the birds grew quieter. Not silent—never silent again, not for Emrys—but gentler. The way voices soften when they come within earshot of home.

*You're nearly there,* said a wren from a hawthorn hedge, and its voice was small and round as a berry. *The valley remembers you.*

Emrys stopped on the road and let that settle. Behind them, Wren Calloway reined the mule to a halt and squinted at the hills with a cartographer's hunger.

"That's it?" she said. "That's the famous valley? It looks like any other fold in the world."

"It's supposed to," Emrys said. "That was rather the point."

The wards were gone—the old bones of them, the forgetting-wards that had wrapped Hollowmere in silence for longer than anyone alive could remember. Emrys had braced themselves for the absence to feel like a wound. Instead it felt like taking off boots after a long march. Magic no longer pressed against their skull like deep water. The land spoke plainly here now: grass gossiping about rain, the river muttering its one long sentence, the pines humming their slow green patience. Loud, all of it. Louder than before. But Emrys had learned, over a winter that had cost them more than they could yet count, that loudness and pain were not the same thing.

Sable dropped out of the sky and landed on their shoulder with the particular weightlessness she performed when she wanted to seem casual and never quite managed.

"Well," she said. "It's smaller than I remembered."

"You've been here before?"

"I've been everywhere before. That's my whole tragic backstory." She preened her wing, deliberately offhand. "It's smaller. And greener. I expected somewhere this self-righteous about secrecy to be uglier."

Emrys smiled and did not answer, because down in the valley, someone had seen them.

A figure stood at the edge of the barley fields, small at this distance, still as a fencepost. Even without the gift, Emrys would have known the set of those shoulders. With it, they heard what they had been hearing faintly for miles, growing clearer with every step: their mother's heartbeat, quick and uneven, and beneath it a thread of song she didn't know she was singing—an old lullaby, the one she used to hum while kneading bread, back when Emrys was small enough to sit on the flour-dusted table and pretend not to listen.

*She practiced,* Sable murmured. *I heard her practicing your name. She got it wrong eleven times and then right twice and then cried into a dishcloth.*

*Sable.*

*I'm just saying. Dishcloths don't lie.*

Emrys walked down into the valley, and their mother walked up the lane to meet them, and for a long moment neither of them moved. Maren Ashgrove had gone greyer since midwinter; there were new lines around her mouth, carved by months of not knowing. Her hands, always so steady with dough and shears and sickles, wrung each other once, twice—and then she crossed the distance between them and held her child hard enough to leave an ache, and Emrys discovered that they were crying, and that they did not mind.

"You came back," she said into their hair. "The letter said you might. The letter said—I read it forty times. You wrote *teach*, Emrys. You wrote *I'm done hiding*. Do you know what I thought when I read that?"

"That you'd be ashamed?"

"I thought," she said, pulling back to look at them, her eyes fierce and wet, "that I finally had something to tell people about my child besides *they're quiet*. Come inside. There's bread. There's—" She stopped. She was looking past Emrys's shoulder at the woman leading a mule down the lane, maps strapped in oiled leather behind the saddle, a royal cartographer's badge worn openly now, polished, defiant. "And who is this?"

"Wren Calloway," Wren said, with a bow that was half mockery and entirely sincere. "Formerly of His Majesty's Survey Office, currently unemployed, permanently indebted to your child. I'm told there's bread."

"There is bread," Maren said, in the tone of a woman reassessing everything she'd assumed about her offspring's year away, "and you'll have some, and you'll tell me how they got that scar."

"Mama—"

"The one on the jaw, Emrys. I raised you. I know which scars are new."

*Oh, I like her,* Sable said, and hopped down onto the gatepost to supervise the procession to the house.

* * *

Hollowmere had changed less than Emrys feared and more than they expected.

The village itself was the same: the crooked lanes, the millpond, the elder's hall with its turf roof sagging like a tired shoulder. Old Bramwen still sat outside her door shelling peas and pretending not to see everything. But the quality of the place had shifted, the way a room shifts when a window is opened. People looked up when travelers passed. Children ran to the road to stare at Wren's maps. And everywhere, woven through it all, Emrys heard the thing that had been impossible all their life: neighbors talking openly. Not loudly. Not yet. But a shepherd by the millpond was telling his wife, in a low wondering voice, that his dog had *said* something to him last week, plain as day, and his wife was laughing—not cruelly, but with the shaky laugh of someone testing new ground—and saying perhaps he hadn't dreamed it after all, because hadn't the king himself stood on the palace steps and said the gifted were wardens, had always been wardens, and weren't we all taught wrong?

Word traveled slowly into valleys. But it traveled.

That first evening, Bramwen came to the house. She stood in the doorway with her shawl pulled tight, older than Emrys remembered, or perhaps Emrys had simply never looked properly before.

"They say you went to the Spire," she said. No greeting. Bramwen had never wasted words on greetings.

"I went to the Spire."

"They say the king fell down dead on his own altar and rose again a man." Her eyes, pale as winter water, searched Emrys's face. "They say a beast-speaker did it. They say the stars are coming back, one by one, lit by speakers standing at the old warden-stones. Is that true?"

"Mostly true. The details are worse and stranger than the rumors."

Bramwen nodded slowly. Then she did something Emrys had never seen her do: she bowed her head, briefly, the way one bows to a storm that has passed through and left the fields standing.

"When I was a girl," she said, "there was another one. Before you. A boy who talked to the bees. We sent him away. His family packed at night and no one ever said his name again, and I helped carry his mother's furniture to the wagon, and I was *proud*, because I thought we were keeping the valley safe." She lifted her head. "I am old, Emrys Ashgrove, and I would like, before I die, to stop being proud of the wrong things. So. What do you need?"

Emrys looked at Wren. Wren looked at the ceiling and became very interested in a beam. *Your valley, your call,* she mouthed.

"The schoolhouse," Emrys said. "The old one, by the yew. If the village will allow it. I want to find them, Bramwen. The children who hear things. There must be others like me—there always were, and we all learned to swallow it. I want to teach them what their voices are. What they're *for*. Before they learn to hate them."

The old woman was quiet for a long moment. Outside, the evening birdsong swelled and settled, and Emrys heard, threaded through it, the yew tree itself murmuring—*yes, yes, the schoolhouse, yes, it has waited*—and almost laughed at the eagerness in it.

"The village will allow it," Bramwen said at last. "The village will *sweep the floor*. Some of us have been waiting our whole lives to apologize to children who aren't even born yet." She turned to go, then paused. "There's a girl. Tamsin Cooper's youngest. Eight years old. She's been telling her mother the sheep are frightened of the north field, and the sheep *are* frightened of the north field, there's a sinkhole opening. Her mother doesn't know what to do with her. She cries at night and says the animals are too loud and she wishes they'd stop." Bramwen's voice roughened. "She says exactly what you used to say. Your mother told me. Years ago. I did nothing then either."

"Bring her tomorrow," Emrys said gently. "First student. Tell her the animals won't stop, but she'll learn to hear them without drowning."

Bramwen left. In the silence after, Maren put a hand on Emrys's arm and squeezed, and said nothing at all, which between the two of them had always been the loudest sentence available.

* * *

The schoolhouse smelled of dust and apples and, faintly, of decades of chalk.

Wren spent two days on the roof before she pronounced it sound, and then three more days doing things to it that made the local carpenter alternately delighted and offended. She drew, of course. She drew everywhere—on scraps, on slate, in the dirt with a stick, on the schoolroom wall until Emrys caught her at it and she had the grace to look only slightly unrepentant. But the map she was proudest of hung behind the teacher's desk now: the valley entire, rendered in her precise, impatient hand, and stitched across it in red thread, the old warden-stones.

"There are nine," she said, for perhaps the hundredth time, pacing before it while Emrys stacked benches. "We've confirmed four. The king's scholars think the network runs the whole spine of the mountains—every valley like this one, every place where people learned to hide. Which means every hidden valley has hidden children. Which means—" she tapped the map, "—this isn't a school, Emrys. It's the first stone of something much larger. Aldric wants a warden corps rebuilt within five years. Odile is already sniffing out candidates. The spymaster has *opinions* about curriculum, if you can believe it."

"I can believe anything about Odile."

"She asked me to tell you she's sorry about the knife incident. Those were her exact words. 'Tell the beast-speaker I regret the knife incident.' From Odile that's practically a sonnet."

"And Sable?"

"Sable," Wren said, "is on your roof, eating something she shouldn't."

Sable was, indeed, on the roof, and it was a mouse, and she showed no remorse whatsoever when Emrys climbed up to join her. Together they sat on the warm slates and watched the valley turn gold toward evening. Below, lamplight was beginning to prick out of windows. Somewhere a dog barked a long, complicated complaint about cats, and Emrys translated it absently, smiling, the way one hums a familiar tune.

"So," Sable said. "You're staying, then. Really staying."

"For now. Wren and I will travel to the other stones when the school is running—there are students who'll need finding, and stones that need kindling, and the Choir is wounded but not dead, and someone should keep an eye on the foundations of the Spire. But this is home base. This is where the letters come." Emrys paused. "Where I come back to."

"Hm." Sable ruffled her feathers. When she spoke again, her voice had lost its armor. "I keep waiting for you to bring it up. The bargain. What I did. You forgave me too easily, and I don't trust easy forgiveness—it goes bad, like milk."

Emrys was quiet a moment. The cold chorus stirred at the edge of their mind—the fragment of the Choir's song lodged there since the Spire, the daily discipline of setting it aside, note by note, like folding away wet clothes. They had gotten good at it. They would get better. But it meant they understood, now, in a way they wished they didn't, what it was to carry something inside you that whispered you belonged elsewhere.

"I didn't forgive you easily," Emrys said. "I forgave you the way I want the world to forgive people like us. Slowly, and on purpose, and every day again. You led the Choir to the valley's edge once. And then you spent years standing guard against it. That's not nothing, Sable. That's the whole arithmetic of a life." They held out their arm, and the crow stepped onto it, light as a secret. "Besides. You're going to help me teach. Who better to warn children about listening to beautiful voices than someone who knows exactly how they lie?"

Sable made a sound that was either a laugh or a cough. "I'm going to be *terrible* at it. I'll frighten them."

"Good. Fear honestly earned is worth ten comfortable lies. That's going on the wall, actually. Don't let Wren erase it."

* * *

Tamsin Cooper arrived three days later, dragged by the elbow by a mother whose face was a battlefield of shame and hope, and stood in the doorway of the schoolhouse with her chin tucked down and her fists balled, radiating the specific fury of a child who has been told all her life that the truest thing about her is a sickness.

Emrys knelt so they were eye level.

"Hello, Tamsin. I'm Emrys. I hear them too."

The girl's eyes flicked up—quick, suspicious, hungry. "All of them?"

"All of them. Right now the oak by the well is complaining about a woodpecker, the millpond is bragging about how deep it is, which is a lie, it's barely deeper than your waist, and there's a fox under the floor of the Cooper barn who thinks your mother's hens are very rude." Emrys tilted their head. "Am I close?"

Tamsin's breath caught. Her fists unclenched, finger by finger, like a flower deciding to open. "The fox," she whispered, "sleeps in the *south* corner."

"The south corner," Emrys agreed gravely. "You're right. I stand corrected. That's the first lesson, by the way—you'll always hear things I can't, and I'll always hear things you can't. Every voice is different. That's not the curse part. That's the *gift* part, and it takes most people eight years and one terrible winter to believe it, so consider yourself ahead of schedule."

Behind them, Wren had gone very still over her maps. Sable, perched on the windowsill, cocked her head at the girl and said, in her driest register, "Well? Sit down, small human. School's begun."

Tamsin sat down. And outside the open windows the whole valley went on speaking—grass and river and crow and wind, the yew tree murmuring its long patience, the distant stones humming their half-lit vigil—and none of it needed to be silenced, and none of it needed to be feared, and Emrys Ashgrove stood in the middle of all that glorious noise, in the valley that had taught them to hide, and began, at last, to teach.
