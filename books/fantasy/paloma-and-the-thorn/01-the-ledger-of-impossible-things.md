## 1. The Ledger of Impossible Things

The trouble with trading in impossible things, Quentin Nakamura had learned over eleven years at the Gilded Thorn, was that the impossible arrived at inconvenient hours and never once apologized.

It was a quarter past midnight on a Tuesday in late autumn, and the impossible had come in the form of a widow named Marta Voss, who sat across the appraisal table clutching a bottle the size of a plum to her chest as though someone might pry it from her cold dead hands, which, to be fair, was roughly the business model of everyone else in the room.

"Take your time," Quentin said, which was a lie, because she had four more appointments before dawn and the candle was already down to its last knuckle of wax. But Rule One of the Gilded Thorn — *the client's grief sets the pace* — was carved into the lintel above the door in letters that glowed faintly when anyone tried to rush it. The guildmaster claimed the carving was decorative. Quentin suspected it bit.

Marta Voss took her time. She turned the bottle over twice, breathed on the glass, and finally set it down between them with the tenderness of a woman laying an infant in a cradle.

"It's his laugh," she said. "My husband's. From before."

Quentin nodded and reached for her loupe. Bottled grief came in varieties the way wine did — vintages, bodies, finishes. A laugh-from-before was a good year. She held the bottle up to the lamp and watched the light move through it: amber-gold, shot through with fine bright threads, the way sound looked when you caught it properly. Amateur bottlers left bubbles. This one was clean work.

"Where was it bottled?" she asked.

"The Laughing Priestess, in Corrow. The good one, not the one by the docks."

"The one by the docks bottles grief into regret," Quentin said. "Different product entirely." She set the loupe down. "You want to sell or to visit?"

Marta blinked. "I'm sorry?"

"Visit. Some clients open the bottle once a season. Listen for a minute, put the cork back, go home lighter. It keeps better that way, if you don't shake it. Grief, like anything alive, does poorly in a house where no one visits." Quentin slid a small card across the table. "Selling is permanent. Visiting is a relationship. I'm obliged to tell you both options exist, because Rule Four says so, and because the guild has a reputation, and the reputation is that we are monsters, which is unfair but useful, so we protect it by occasionally being decent when nobody's watching."

The widow stared at her for a long moment. Then, unexpectedly, she laughed — a small, cracked thing, but real — and Quentin thought, *there it is*, because they always laughed, eventually, in this chair. It was practically a signature on the contract.

"I'll visit," Marta said.

"Sensible. Twelve silver a year for the storage shelf, climate-controlled, away from the hope inventory — hope corrodes glass, don't ask me why, nobody knows why, half this building runs on things nobody knows why." Quentin wrote the entry in her ledger with a fountain pen that never needed refilling, another item on the long list of guild mysteries she'd stopped investigating around year three. "Second shelf from the top. Visit whenever you like. If you ever change your mind about selling, the offer stands, but—" she capped the pen, "—between us, off the books, don't."

Marta Voss left at half past midnight holding her receipt like a love letter, and Quentin noted the transaction, initialed it, and felt the particular nothing that followed every closing of a deal. Not satisfaction exactly. More like a door shutting quietly in a large empty house.

She was alone in the appraisal room now, which was how she liked it, which was also, if she were honest with herself — and she made a professional habit of honesty with herself only during daylight hours — how she didn't.

* * *

The second appointment of the night was a debt settlement, and debts were Quentin's favorite part of the trade, because debts had rules, and rules were the closest thing the world offered to kindness.

The debtor was a fidgety young man named Oskar Bell, a clockmaker's apprentice who had borrowed six years from the guild two winters ago to finish his masterwork — a clock that told not the hour but the *mood* of the household, which sounded useless until you learned that three marriages in Corrow had been saved by it, and one poisoning prevented, so perhaps not useless after all.

He'd come to repay. That alone made him remarkable; most borrowers spent their borrowed years and then spent twenty years avoiding the guild's collectors, as though the Thorn would forget. The Thorn forgot nothing. The Thorn's memory was the single most reliable object in the city, and Quentin was its keeper.

"Borrowed years are repaid with interest," she said, opening the ledger to his page. "Six years borrowed at the standard rate comes to seven years, four months, eleven days. You may pay in kind — years of your own life, drawn off the end, painlessly, you won't feel a thing until your hair goes grey slightly ahead of schedule — or in equivalent value. Guild-approved equivalents include memories, names, firstborn promises, and certain categories of luck."

Oskar went pale. "Certain categories?"

"We don't deal in bad luck," Quentin said. "Bad luck is volatile. It leaks."

He swallowed. "And if I pay in years? What happens to them?"

"They go into escrow." She tapped the ledger. "Until the guild needs them."

"For what?"

Quentin paused. It was a fair question, and she realized, with a small cold turn in her stomach, that she did not know the answer. There was a page in the oldest ledger — the Black Ledger, kept in the vault, written in ink that predated the kingdom — labeled simply *HOLDING*. Every borrowed year the guild collected went onto that page. In eleven years she had never seen a withdrawal.

"That's above my station," she said, which was true, and which was also the sentence she used most often in this building, and which had begun lately to taste like ash.

They settled it in years. Oskar signed, the pen drew a little of his life out through his fingertips the way it always did — Quentin had signed enough contracts to know the feeling, a cool thread unspooling from somewhere behind the ribs — and he walked out seven years poorer and visibly relieved, thanking her twice, which was another thing people did in that chair, and another small quiet nothing she filed under *order maintained*.

After he left, she sat for a moment with her hand flat on the ledger, feeling the faint warmth of the paper. Ledgers at the Thorn were alive in the way old dogs are alive: mostly asleep, loyal, aware of far more than they let on. Her ledger liked her. She could tell because it never snapped its pages shut on her fingers, whereas Tam's ledger — Tam being the guild's forger, a nervous brilliant man who could counterfeit any document except an honest one, ink refusing to hold his lies — had bitten him twice last month.

"Order," Quentin said aloud to the empty room, and lit a fresh candle, and waited for the third appointment, which did not come.

That happened sometimes. The impossible stood people up. She used the hour to do inventory, which was her version of prayer, if prayer involved counting forty-one jars of bottled homesickness and noting that the jar of a king's forgotten coronation oath had gone cloudy again and would need re-corking by month's end.

* * *

At the end of the night, as she always did, Quentin climbed the narrow stair to the shrine.

Every guild had a patron. Most chose practical gods — gods of coin, of doors, of favorable weather for shipping. The Gilded Thorn had inherited Veth, the Silent One, god of ledgers and unspoken words, and no one remembered why, because the records explaining it were themselves silent, which Quentin considered either elegant irony or divine laziness.

Veth had no mouth on its statue. That was the whole iconography: a tall figure robed in slate, hands open, face smooth as an unwritten page. Worshippers said the silence meant listening. Skeptics said it meant absence. Quentin, kneeling on the worn stone with her ledger open on her lap out of habit, had spent three years unable to decide, and the not-deciding had begun to wear grooves in her.

Because she prayed. She'd deny it under torture, but she prayed — not with words, exactly, since what use were words to a mouthless god, but with entries. Each night she opened her ledger to the back page, the one she kept blank, and she held it up, and she thought her day at the god as clearly as she could: the widow, the clockmaker, the cloudy oath-jar. *Here,* she thought. *Here is what I kept. Here is what I balanced. Is it enough? Are you there?*

For the first eight years, something had answered. Not words — Veth had none — but a feeling, a pressure behind the sternum, warm and vast and attentive, like standing near a great hearth in a cold house. The god read her pages. She'd have staked her contract on it.

Three years ago, the hearth went cold.

No explanation. No gradual fading. Just one night the presence was there, and the next night it wasn't, and every night since. Quentin had checked everything — the shrine stone, the offerings (Veth accepted blank paper, which Quentin found touching), the alignment of the ledgers, the phase of the moon, the possibility that she'd done something unforgivable, though her sins ran to dry wit and occasional contraband tea rather than anything the gods traditionally smite. Nothing. Silence, total and absolute, from a god whose entire nature was silence anyway, which made diagnosis impossible. How did you distinguish a silent god ignoring you from a silent god simply being itself?

She'd asked Guildmaster Corvane Dusk about it once, early on. He'd been polishing his spectacles at the time, and he'd said, without looking up, "Gods keep their own books, Nakamura. Worry about yours." And she had, because he was right, because that was the job, because order was the thing she had instead of whatever other people had — families, faiths, futures.

But tonight, kneeling on the cold stone with the blank page held up to the smooth-faced statue, Quentin noticed something she had never noticed in eleven years.

The statue's hands were not open.

They had been open — she was sure of it, sure the way you're sure of your own name. Open palms, receiving. Now the fingers were curled inward, almost closed, like a man gripping something to his chest. Like a debtor hiding assets. Like—

Like someone holding a door shut against a wind on the other side.

Quentin lowered the ledger slowly. The candle flame leaned, though there was no draft. Somewhere below her, deep in the vaults, the Black Ledger turned a page by itself — she heard it, a soft dry whisper like snow beginning to fall.

"Three years," she said to the statue. Her voice sounded very small in the shrine. "You could at least leave a note."

The statue, naturally, said nothing. But as she gathered her ledger and pinched out the candle, Quentin felt — just once, just at the edge of perception, the way you feel eyes on your back — the sensation of being *read*. Fiercely. Hurriedly. The way a man reads a letter he already knows is bad news, searching for the part that tells him how much time he has left.

She stood in the dark doorway a moment longer than she meant to.

Then she went down to her rooms, locked her ledger in its drawer, and lay awake listening to the wind rise off the mountains, colder than it had any right to be in autumn, colder than the almanacs promised, colder than it had been in living memory.

Outside her window, on the sill, the first frost of the season was forming — weeks early, in patterns too regular to be natural, like handwriting.

Quentin fell asleep telling herself it was nothing. She was very good at that. It was, after all, the same skill the god had mastered.
