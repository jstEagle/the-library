## 3. What the Guild Owes

The Gilded Thorn had rules for everything. There was a rule for how to shelve bottled grief (upright, away from sunlight, never beside joy, which curdled it), a rule for how to count borrowed years (always aloud, always twice), and a rule—Quentin had written it herself in her first month as ledger-keeper—for how to handle items that arrived without provenance: *catalogue nothing until you know what it is.*

Guildmaster Corvane Dusk broke that rule before breakfast.

"Appraise her," he said, standing at the window of his office with his back to Quentin, watching the frost climb the glass like it had an appointment. "Full appraisal. Provenance, condition, market value."

"She's a child," Quentin said.

"She's an anomaly of significant interest." Corvane turned. He was a narrow man, all elbows and ledger-lines, with silver threading through his dark hair so precisely it looked installed. He wore his authority the way he wore his rings—one on every finger, none of them warm. "Anomalies are our stock in trade, Nakamura. You keep the books. You know that better than anyone."

"I know we trade in impossible things," Quentin said. "Grief in bottles. Years on loan. Names that haven't been spoken yet." She kept her voice level, the way she kept her ledgers: balanced, line by line, no smudges. "We don't make children one of them."

"Not yet." Corvane smiled, which was worse than not smiling. "But she walked out of a blizzard speaking a language that predates every kingdom on this continent, and every ledger in this building opened itself to the same blank page. You told me that yourself. You wrote it down." He tapped the report on his desk—the one Quentin had filed at dawn, because a thing unwritten was a thing that hadn't happened, and Quentin did not deal in things that hadn't happened. "Blank pages are either debts or assets. I intend to find out which."

Quentin looked at the report. Her own handwriting stared back at her, neat and damning. *All 214 volumes open to page one. Page one is empty. The child slept in the kitchen and did not wake when I moved a candle six inches from her face.*

"The guild owes her," Quentin said. "That's what she is. A debt. Someone knocked on our door in a dead season, barefoot, and the house answered. That's not inventory, Guildmaster. That's an obligation."

Corvane considered her for a long moment. He had a way of considering people that made them feel like entries awaiting correction.

"You've been praying again," he said. "To your silent god."

It was not a non sequitur. With Corvane, nothing ever was; every remark was a column in some private ledger Quentin couldn't see. "I light a candle," she said. "Veth doesn't answer. That's between me and Veth."

"Three years of candles." He came around the desk and stood close enough that she could smell the ink on him—guildmaster's ink, the expensive kind mixed with ash so that whatever he signed could never be forged. "And in three years, has anything happened? Has the god so much as cleared its throat?"

"No."

"And now a child arrives who makes the ledgers open themselves, and you want to call it coincidence. Sentiment." He shook his head, almost gently. "You're a good bookkeeper, Nakamura. The best I've had. But faith makes sloppy arithmetic. It rounds up. It assumes the universe owes us meaning." He gestured toward the door. "The child stays in the east room. Locked. Fed, clothed, comfortable—we're not monsters—but no one speaks to her, no one teaches her, and no one, above all, tells her anything about this building. If she's what I think she might be, she is the most valuable item this guild has held since its founding. And if she isn't—" a small shrug, "—then we've inconvenienced a lost child for a fortnight, which the world will forgive us."

*The world,* Quentin thought, *has forgiven this guild considerably worse.*

"Yes, Guildmaster," she said, because she was still under contract, and the contract said obey, and the contract was signed in her blood and countersigned by a god who no longer spoke.

She took the stairs down to the kitchen two at a time, which for Quentin constituted running.

* * *

Hesper had made porridge.

This was notable because Hesper made everything into something else. She was a broad, flour-dusted woman who had come to the guild twenty years ago as a cook and stayed as something more complicated: she flavored memories. A spoonful of her honey could make you taste your grandmother's kitchen. Her black bread carried the smell of rain on a summer road. The guild paid her extravagantly and pretended she was staff, and Hesper let them pretend, mostly because the kitchen had a good stove and she liked feeding people who didn't know they were hungry for something.

The child sat on a crate by the hearth, wrapped in a blanket that had been mended so many times it was more darning than wool, holding a bowl of porridge in both hands like it might be a trap.

"It's just oats," Hesper was saying, in the loud, slow voice people use for foreigners and the hard of hearing, which was unkind in both cases. "Oats! From the market! Nothing strange in it!"

"She speaks a language older than kingdoms," Quentin said from the doorway. "I don't think 'slowly' is the problem."

"Well, what do I do, then? Mime?" Hesper demonstrated miming *eat*, which involved pointing at the bowl and making an expression of such aggressive encouragement that the child flinched.

Quentin crouched down to the child's level. Up close, Paloma—all right, the child; nobody had named her yet, though Quentin had privately been calling her Paloma since last night, after the dove-gray of her eyes, and was aware this was exactly the sort of sentiment Corvane would round up against her—looked perhaps seven years old, maybe eight. Frost-bitten toes, now bandaged. Hair the color of wet sand. A small silver chain around her neck with a pendant shaped like a bell that made no sound, which Quentin had catalogued overnight as *item unknown, do not touch* and then immediately touched.

"Eat," Quentin tried, in three languages. Then, feeling foolish, she pointed at the bowl, pointed at her own mouth, and ate an imaginary spoonful.

The child watched this performance with the grave courtesy of someone being kind to an idiot. Then she lifted her own spoon, copied the motion exactly, and ate a real spoonful, maintaining eye contact the entire time, as if to say: *there. Like that. Was it so difficult?*

Hesper barked a laugh. "Oh, I like her."

The child ate. Quentin knelt there on the cold flagstones and felt something in her chest shift out of alignment—a column that had been straight for three years listing, very slightly, sideways.

"Corvane wants her locked in the east room," Quentin said quietly.

Hesper's ladle stopped mid-stir. "Locked."

"Fed, clothed, comfortable. No monsters. Just... catalogued."

"He called her an *item*?"

"He called her an anomaly of significant interest."

"Hm." Hesper resumed stirring with the particular violence of a woman converting anger into breakfast. "You know what I put in this porridge? Nothing. Plain oats. Because she's been walking through snow barefoot and her stomach doesn't need a memory, it needs calories." She glanced at the child, then at Quentin, and lowered her voice. "You going to lock her up?"

"I'm going to carry out my instructions," Quentin said. "Exactly. To the letter."

Hesper raised an eyebrow. Twenty years of cooking for a guild of liars, forgers, and memory-thieves had given her a fine ear for loopholes.

"To the letter," Quentin repeated. "He said the east room. He said fed and clothed. He said no one speaks to her." She paused. "He didn't say anything about the kitchen."

* * *

The east room, as it happened, was through the kitchen.

This was an architectural accident of the old building—the guildhouse had been a merchant's home once, then a counting-house, then a temple briefly, during a period Quentin's ledgers described only as *"the regrettable decade"*—and the shortest route from the kitchen to the east room passed directly by the stove, the bread rack, and the crate by the hearth where a certain child was eating porridge.

Quentin walked that route slowly. She stopped at the bread rack, because a good clerk documents everything, and selected a heel of yesterday's loaf. She stopped at the jar of plum preserves, because inventory must be checked. She stopped by the hearth and set both items on the crate beside the child, and then, because rule forty-one required verification of item condition, she checked the bandages on the small feet, and found them dry and properly tied, because Hesper had done them at dawn while Quentin was filing reports.

The child looked up at her. And said something.

It was a short phrase, four syllables, rising at the end like a question. Quentin knew perhaps forty words of the pre-kingdom tongue—not from study but from the ledgers themselves, from the oldest entries, the ones written before the guild had standardized its script, when clerks had still annotated contracts in the old tongue the way scholars annotate in Latin. *Four syllables, rising.* She turned them over.

"Say it again?"

The child said it again, patient, and pointed—at Quentin, at the bread, at the preserves, back at Quentin—and then pressed both hands flat over her heart.

*Thank you,* Quentin translated, with a certainty that arrived from somewhere below thought. *For the food. Thank you.*

"You're welcome," Quentin said, and then, absurdly, in the old tongue, dredging the word up from a margin note in Volume One: "*Setha.*"

The child's whole face changed. It was like watching frost reverse itself—like watching spring rehearse. "*Setha!*" she repeated, delighted, and said four more words, fast, and grabbed Quentin's hand with both of hers, small and warm and sticky with porridge.

Every rule Quentin had ever written said this was where she stood up, stepped back, and became a clerk again.

Quentin stayed kneeling for eleven more seconds. She counted them. She counted everything.

Then she rose, informed the child that the east room had a window and a lockable door and that Hesper would bring lunch, and escorted her there with the detached professionalism of a woman moving a valuable asset to secure storage, and absolutely not the demeanor of someone who had just been thanked in a dead language by a barefoot miracle and had liked it.

* * *

The trouble with being the ledger-keeper of the Gilded Thorn, Quentin reflected that evening, is that you know exactly what everything costs.

She sat in her office above the counting floor with the day's entries spread before her, and she read her own hand and did not like the arithmetic. *Item: one child, unknown provenance, secured east room.* Below it, in the space where a responsible clerk noted discrepancies: *Item: one heel of bread, one jar plum preserves, unlogged.* Below that: *Item: eleven seconds, unaccounted.*

Eleven seconds. In three years of keeping perfect books, she had never once failed to account for time. Time was the guild's whole business; you learned to hold it carefully, like water.

And beneath all of it, the entry she'd been avoiding all day, the one her pen kept skirting the way a tongue finds a broken tooth:

*Item: one prayer, unanswered, third year running.*

She lit the candle anyway. Small, tallow, set in the niche by the window where the little brass mark of Veth hung—a circle with a horizontal line through it, the sign of the ledger-god, the god of debts honestly weighed and words that mean what they say. Merchants' god. Clerks' god. Hers, once, by contract: she had sworn her service to Veth at nineteen, and the god had sealed the oath with a touch like cold ink drying, and then, three years ago, gone silent. Not absent—Quentin still felt the seal sometimes, faint, like a scar that aches before rain. Silent. As if the god were holding its breath.

Or as if something were holding the god's breath for it.

"Three years," Quentin said to the candle. "A word. Any word. Even a late fee notice. I'd take a late fee notice."

The flame bent, very slightly, toward the east room. Toward the child.

Quentin sat very still for a long moment, telling herself it was a draft. The windows were latched. She checked. She was the sort of person who checked.

Downstairs, faintly, through the floorboards, she heard Hesper singing to herself in the kitchen, and under Hesper's song—no, woven through it, impossible, coming from behind a locked door on a corridor with no other exit—a child's voice, humming the same tune back, note for note, in a language that had no business knowing Hesper's grandmother's lullaby.

Quentin closed her ledger.

On the desk, unbidden, unlogged, all 214 volumes of the guild archive lay open to page one. Blank. Waiting.

And in the morning, when Corvane summoned her to announce that the appraiser would arrive within the week—a specialist, he said, an expert in extinct tongues and rare acquisitions, and Quentin should prepare the item for examination—Quentin heard herself say, "Yes, Guildmaster," in her flattest clerk's voice, while somewhere below her ribs a different ledger opened, and the first entry wrote itself in handwriting she almost recognized:

*Item: one conscience. Condition: previously dormant. Provenance: unclear. Value:* 

Her pen hovered over the page.

*Value: pending.*
