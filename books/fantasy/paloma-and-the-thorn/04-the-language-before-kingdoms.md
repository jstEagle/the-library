## 4. The Language Before Kingdoms

*Warm first. Warm is the true thing.*

Paloma remembered warmth the way other children remembered their mothers' faces: completely, and without needing to try.

She remembered a country where the stones stood in rings like old men holding hands, and the grass between them grew gold-green and soft as sleeping. She remembered that the stones hummed—not a sound you heard with your ears but a sound your bones made back, a low glad note that meant *you are here, you are counted, you are kept*. Her grandmother had pressed her small palm against the tallest stone once, on the day of the naming-feast, and said words in the old tongue that Paloma had known before she could walk:

*The season turns because we sing it. The singing does not stop because we sleep.*

That was the whole of the covenant, really. That was everything. A song passed hand to hand like bread.

And there had been a Voice—oh, there had been a Voice. Not the stones' humming, which was the song's body, but the singer itself: vast and patient and so full of delight that when it sang the spring into place, the snow didn't melt so much as *laugh itself away*, sliding off the hillsides in bright ropes of water while every green thing pushed up through the mud like children late for a feast. The Voice had no face. The Voice had never needed one. When it sang near, Paloma felt it in her teeth, in the roots of her baby teeth especially, which her grandmother said was correct, because the covenant lived in what was still growing.

*The god sings. We keep time. That is the bargain, little heir. Nothing more, nothing less, and it has held since before kingdoms had names.*

Then—

Then white.

White was not silence. White was loud. That was the thing no one who hadn't been there would ever understand: the endless winter wasn't quiet at all. It roared. It roared in a register below hearing, a pressure like the sky pressing its whole weight down to whisper *mine, mine, mine* into the marrow of the world. The warm country had gone under it the way a candle goes under a cupped hand. One moment the stones were humming. The next they were only cold shapes, and the song in them was smothered mid-note, and Paloma's grandmother was pushing her out the door with both hands and saying the last words she would ever say in the old tongue:

*Run toward the lowlands. Run toward the ones who keep ledgers. They owe us. They have always owed us.*

*They owe us.* Paloma had carried those three words south through the white like a coal cupped in bare hands. She did not know yet what ledgers were. She did not know who *they* were. But her grandmother's voice had gone hard in a way it never went, hard the way a door goes when it is being barred, and so Paloma ran.

* * *

Now there was a city, and the city was loud in the ordinary way—carts and bells and men shouting about fish—and underneath the ordinary noise the white roar went on and on, and nobody else seemed to hear it. That was the loneliest part. She would stand in a street full of people and feel the winter's great dumb pressure humming through the cobbles into her feet, into her bones, into the roots of her teeth, and everyone around her haggled and laughed as if the world were still being sung.

Her feet hurt. Someone had told her—no, someone had *not* told her; she had gathered it from the shape of their mouths and the pointing of their fingers—that she should not have come barefoot through the snow. She looked down at her own feet now, wrapped in somebody's cast-off wool that smelled of onions, and thought: *the frost took my shoes the second night. The frost takes whatever it likes. That is what it means to be winter instead of a season.*

Because that was the wrongness at the center of everything, the wrongness she had no words for in any tongue, old or new: winter was supposed to be a *season*. It was supposed to arrive, sing its cold clean verse, and then bow and let the next singer take the stage. This winter did not bow. This winter had stopped the music and refused to leave the stage and was standing there breathing on everyone.

Somewhere, far north, something was holding the stage by force.

And somewhere closer than that—she felt it even now, faint as a heartbeat through a wall—the Voice was trying to sing and getting no further than a breath.

*It gave too much,* she thought, and did not know how she knew it, only that the knowing sat in her chest like a swallowed stone. *It sang and sang against the white until it had nothing left to sing with, and then it—*

Then it what? She turned the question over. It was like turning over a broken pot: beautiful on one side, empty on the other.

* * *

The building they had brought her to was strange and wonderful and smelled of dust and secrets and, faintly, of honey.

There were shelves. There were so many shelves, and on them sat bottles—thousands of bottles—each one holding a color that moved like weather. She pressed her nose to one shelf and looked into a little glass bottle of grey-blue swirling, and grief looked back at her, bottled and labeled in a script she couldn't read, and Paloma thought: *these people put feelings in jars. These people are either very wise or very foolish, and I think perhaps both.*

A woman worked nearby at a desk heaped with books. Tall, sharp-eyed, with ink on her fingers and a look on her face like a locked drawer. The others in the building spoke the new tongue around her, quick and clipped, but this woman spoke less than anyone, and when she did speak it was precise as a knife laid flat on a table.

The woman had a mark on the back of her right hand.

Paloma saw it and forgot how to breathe.

It was a small tattooed sigil, dark as old iron: a vertical line crossed by three horizontal bars, like a ladder half-built, or a staff with rungs, or—

Or a stave. Half of a stave. The first half of the oldest notation in the world, the notation the standing stones themselves wore carved into their flanks, the notation in which the Voice's songs had been written down so that the people could keep time with them.

*Half,* Paloma thought, her heart hammering. *It is half of something. Where is the other half? Who broke it? Why would anyone break a song?*

She crept closer while the woman was bent over her books. The mark was careful work, loving work, the kind of work done by someone who had copied it from somewhere older without understanding what they copied. Paloma knew the shape the way she knew her own heartbeat. In the warm country, every keeper of the stones bore the full sigil: the descending stave and beneath it the answering stave, call and response, god and people, the two halves leaning together like a couple at a fire.

This woman carried the call alone. Just the top half. Just the god's line, with nothing beneath it to answer.

*Oh,* Paloma thought, and her eyes stung suddenly, and she didn't fully know why except that it was like seeing a chair with no one in it, set at a table, waiting. *You are marked as an answerer and there is no one left to answer. You pray to a singer who cannot sing. You have been talking into a silence for years, haven't you. I can see it in your shoulders. You hold yourself like someone listening very hard and hearing nothing.*

The woman glanced up. Their eyes met. Paloma, who had crossed a hundred miles of roaring white alone, felt her cheeks go hot as if she'd been caught stealing.

The woman said something in the new tongue. Her voice was dry and even, but her hand—the marked hand—had curled slightly closed, the way a hand curls around a thing it doesn't want examined.

Paloma didn't understand the words. But she understood the tone, because some tones are the same in every language: *Who are you, and why are you looking at me like that?*

So Paloma did the only honest thing. She reached out and touched the back of the woman's marked hand, gently, one fingertip on the lowest bar of the broken stave, and she sang.

Not loudly. Barely above breath. Three notes of the answering stave—the response-line, the people's half, the half that was missing from the woman's skin—sung the way her grandmother had taught her, sung into the hollow place where the harmony should have lived.

The notes came out thin and cracked. She was tired and hungry and her throat was raw from the cold country. But the third note caught on something. For half a heartbeat—a single half-heartbeat—the air above the desk *leaned*. The lamp flame stood up straighter. The bottled griefs on the shelves all swirled the same direction at once, like grass before wind, like a congregation turning to face the singer.

And far off, so far it might have been imagination, something vast drew a breath.

Then it was gone. The flame flickered normally. The bottles resumed their private weathers. The woman stared at her hand as though it had spoken, and then at Paloma, and her locked-drawer face had come open just a crack, and behind it was something so nakedly hungry and hopeful that Paloma's chest ached with recognition.

*I know you,* Paloma thought fiercely, in the old tongue, knowing the woman couldn't understand a word and not caring. *I know what you are. You are a keeper whose stone was taken away. You are the answering half of a broken song, and you have been standing at attention for three years waiting for orders from a god who traded its voice away to keep the winter off us all—and the trade is failing, keeper, the trade is failing, I can hear it failing, I can hear the white coming up the road like a debt collector.*

*I am the heir of the covenant. My grandmother told me to find the ledger-keepers, because they owe us. And you—you are owed something too. I think maybe we are owed the same thing.*

The woman was saying something again, carefully, slowly, the way people speak to the very young or the very lost. Paloma caught none of it. But the woman had not pulled her hand away. That was the important thing. The marked hand lay open on the desk now, palm half-turned up, the broken stave facing the ceiling as if waiting for rain.

Paloma sat down on the floor beside the desk, because her legs had finally admitted what they'd known for a hundred miles, and she leaned her shoulder against the woman's knee—the way you lean against a standing stone, the way you lean against anything that hums—and she began, very quietly, to teach.

One word at a time. The first word of the old tongue, the word that opened every song, the word that meant *listen*.

The woman watched her mouth. She was the kind of person, Paloma could already tell, who learned things properly or not at all.

Outside, the unseasonal frost climbed the window glass in long white feathers, and beneath the city's ordinary noise the winter's roar went on and on, patient as a creditor.

But inside, in a room full of bottled feelings, a child sang one small word to a woman with half a god written on her hand—

*Listen. Listen. Listen.*

—and for the first time since the warm country went under the white, Paloma felt the coal in her hands give off heat.

* * *

She dreamed, that night, of the stones.

In the dream they stood in their ring on the gold-green hill, humming their low glad note, and the grass was warm, and her grandmother's laugh came from somewhere behind her like sun through a window. But when Paloma turned to find her, the dream shifted the way dreams do, and she was standing instead at the edge of a great white field, and in the middle of the field knelt an enormous shape—vaster than a hill, vaster than weather—bent double, mouth open wide, singing with everything it had.

No sound came out.

The shape sang and sang and the field drank every note before it was born, and the shape's shoulders shook with the effort, and Paloma understood, in the way of dreams, that it was not silent because it had abandoned them. It was silent because it had *paid*. It had poured its whole voice out against the white, year after year, and the white had taken it all, and still it knelt there mouthing the song, refusing to stop, refusing to leave the stage, holding the seasons' place the way a sentry holds a gate with a broken sword.

*Hold on,* Paloma tried to tell it. *Hold on. I found one of them. The one with the mark. I'm going to find the rest.*

The great shape turned its head—or seemed to—and though there was nothing so small as eyes anywhere on it, Paloma felt herself *seen*, wholly and kindly, the way the stones used to see her.

And then, faint across the white field, so faint it was almost not there: not a sound, but the *shape* of a sound. The ghost of a melody. Two notes, rising.

Call. Waiting for answer.

Paloma woke in the strange warm room with her heart full and her fists clenched, and downstairs, muffled through floors, she could hear the ledger-woman's voice—low, dry, precise—arguing with someone about her. Arguing *for* her, she decided. She decided it the way you decide the sun will rise: stubbornly, and without proof.

She hugged her knees and listened to the building settle, and she practiced the new tongue's few words under her breath like counting beads, and outside the window the frost feathers grew and grew.

*Three days,* the white roar seemed to say, in its pressure below hearing. *Perhaps four. Then I finish collecting.*

*Not if I can help it,* Paloma answered, in the language before kingdoms, in the language the winter could not hear and therefore could not refuse.

And far below the city, deeper than cellars, older than ledgers, something vast and voiceless drew another breath—and waited, with the patience of everything that has already given everything, to see what the small singing creature would do next.
