## 5. Winter Has a Creditor

The frost came inside on the third morning, the way creditors do.

Quentin noticed it first in the ledger room, where she kept the guild's oldest books behind a door with three locks and one superstition. The ink in the open ledger had gone stiff overnight, each numeral furred white at the edges, as though the page itself had grown old. She breathed on it. The frost did not melt so much as flinch, drawing back from her breath like a cat deciding whether to be petted.

"That's new," she told the empty room.

The empty room declined to comment. Most of her conversations went that way.

She had taken to talking aloud more since Paloma arrived—a bad habit, or possibly a good one, depending on whether you believed that people who lived alone with ledgers ought to practice being people. The child was upstairs now, in the kitchen, where Hesper had annexed her with the ruthless efficiency of a woman who considered an underfed child a personal insult to her profession. Quentin could hear, faintly, the sound of Paloma laughing at something Tam had drawn on a scrap of paper. It was a strange sound in the Gilded Thorn, laughter. Like birdsong in a countinghouse. Everyone kept pausing to listen for it and then pretending they hadn't.

Quentin dipped her pen, warmed it over the candle stub, and got back to work. There was always work. That was the mercy of ledgers: they did not care about the weather.

She was halfway through reconciling the quarterly interest on a jar of bottled grief—compound melancholy, the client had insisted, and the guild's rates on compound melancholy were frankly punitive—when the cold came down the stairs like a decision.

Not a draft. Drafts wander. This came straight down the corridor, purposeful as a bailiff, and it carried a smell Quentin knew from her mercenary years: the smell of the far north, of ice that had never once been sorry about anything. Her breath plumed. The candle guttered, stood back up out of what she could only describe as professional obligation, and died anyway.

In the doorway of the ledger room stood a figure made mostly of winter.

He was tall in the way that doorframes are tall—present, structural, impossible to argue with. His coat was grey as the sky before snow, long enough that its hem seemed still to be arriving. Frost bloomed across his shoulders in patterns too regular to be natural: not crystals but script, row upon row of fine written characters, legible only if you were the kind of person who could read weather. His face was pale and correct and utterly without malice, which was somehow worse than if it had been cruel. Cruelty you could plan around. He looked like a man who had never once wanted anything, only collected it.

"Good evening," he said. His voice was the sound of a lake freezing—one long note, very quiet, going on longer than any note should. "I am looking for the office of record."

"This is the Gilded Thorn," Quentin said. "We're closed to solicitation."

"I am not soliciting." He produced, from his coat, a document. It was the size of a door and he handled it the way other men handle letters. "I am collecting."

Quentin looked at the document. Every instinct she owned—the ones that had kept her alive through eleven campaigns and one disastrous marriage—stood up and asked to leave by the window. She stayed seated. Running looked worse on the ledger.

"The office of record," she said, "is me. Quentin Nakamura, keeper of ledgers, bound servant of Veth." She said the god's name the way she always did, out of three years of habit and no reply: steadily, without hope, the way you say a word you've decided not to cry about. "State your business."

Something happened to the Advocate's face. It might have been a smile. On him, expressions arrived like distant weather reports—technically accurate, arriving late, concerning somewhere else entirely.

"You are bound to Veth," he repeated. "How convenient. Then you may witness on its behalf." He laid the document on the table between them, and the wood whitened beneath it, and Quentin read.

* * *

She had read contracts all her adult life. She had read contracts written in blood, in ash, in the sweat of men who did not know they were signing away their grandmothers' recipes. She had a professional appreciation for a well-drafted instrument, the way a soldier can appreciate a well-made sword even while it is being used on him.

This was the finest contract she had ever seen, and reading it felt like drowning in clean, cold water.

It was old. Not old the way the guild's oldest ledgers were old—vellum and dust—but old the way mountains are old, the way the language underneath kingdoms is old. The script moved. Not animated, exactly; it simply refused to hold still, because it had been drafted before stillness was invented. But the terms resolved themselves in her mind the way numbers do, cleanly, terribly:

*Item the first: The voice of the god Veth, received into escrow by the undersigned office, to be held against fulfillment of the Covenant of Seasons.*

*Item the second: Should the Covenant fail of renewal at its appointed term, the escrowed voice shall be deemed forfeit, and title shall pass to the Creditor of Record, together with all appurtenances thereof, including but not limited to: spring, thaw, the green year, and the waking of seeds.*

*Item the third: Notice of collection shall be served upon the world entire, in the form most legible to the debtor.*

"In the form most legible," Quentin said slowly. "You mean the winter."

"I mean the winter," the Hoarfrost Advocate agreed, with something that in a human face would have been called satisfaction and in his was merely accuracy. "Your god has not renewed. Your god has not answered. Your god—" and here he paused, and the frost-script on his shoulders rearranged itself, and Quentin understood, with a lurch, that he was checking something, consulting some vast frozen index of his own, "—has not spoken in three years, two months, and eleven days. Default is established. Collection commences."

Three years, two months, and eleven days. Quentin's hands were steady on the table. She noted, distantly, that this was the kind of thing her hands did instead of shaking, and that someday she would probably die of it.

"You have the wrong office," she said. "I'm a servant of Veth, not its agent. I don't hold its voice. I've spent three years praying at it and getting nothing but my own echo. If you want the debtor, go find the god."

The Advocate regarded her. Snow began, very gently, indoors.

"I have found the debtor," he said. "Veth stands where it has always stood. It cannot come to the negotiation. It cannot send word. It cannot plead, petition, or explain." He tilted his head, and for one moment something almost like pity crossed his correct pale face, the way light crosses ice. "That is what forfeit means, keeper of ledgers. You have been praying to a god with no mouth. Did you truly believe the silence was abandonment?"

The question went through her like a splinter finding its depth.

Three years. Three years of prayers spoken into a silence she had catalogued, privately, as neglect. She had built a whole architecture around it—*the god is distant, the god is testing me, the gods of small offices are forgotten first*—and every beam of that architecture had just been quietly removed, and she discovered she was standing on nothing, and that nothing, impossibly, was better than what she'd been standing on before. Because silence-as-abandonment meant she had been alone. Silence-as-bargain meant—

"It gave its voice away," she said. "On purpose. As payment."

"As security," the Advocate corrected. "A voice held in escrow against the renewal of the Covenant of Seasons. A god may not swear an oath in its own name; that would be a god vouching for itself, and no court in creation will honor it. So Veth pledged the only collateral it possessed that could not lie." He gathered his document, and the frost retreated from the table like a tide. "Its voice. Every word it would ever speak, every prayer it would ever answer aloud, every song it would ever sing the seasons into place—all of it, held in trust, forfeit upon failure."

"And the Covenant failed."

"The Covenant was not renewed," the Advocate said precisely. "There is a difference. Renewal requires certain instruments. Certain stones. Certain—" he paused again, that terrible consultation happening behind his eyes, "—speakers. When the instruments scattered, renewal became impossible, and default became inevitable, and I became busy." He settled his coat, and the hem finished arriving. "I am not your enemy, keeper. I am arithmetic. Do not hate the sum for totaling."

"What happens when you collect?"

"Spring passes to my principal. Thaw. The green year. The waking of seeds." He said it the way she would read a list of seized assets. "The winter that falls will not be a season. Seasons end. This will be a condition. The world will keep, the way things keep in a vault: perfectly, permanently, and dead."

Quentin thought of Paloma upstairs, laughing at Tam's drawings. She thought of the child barefoot in the snow, speaking a language older than kingdoms, and how every ledger in the building had opened itself to the same blank page—as if the books themselves had recognized something arriving.

"How long," she said, "do we have?"

"Collection is a process, not an event. The frost advances as the paperwork proceeds." He moved toward the door, then stopped, and turned, and looked at her with those eyes that held no want and no mercy and, buried very deep, something that a generous soul might have called respect. "You keep honest books. That is rare. So I will tell you what the honest books would tell you." He raised one hand, and in it, briefly, appeared a single line of frost-script, hanging in the air like a signature waiting for a page. "Default is established but not finalized. A debt in escrow may yet be redeemed—if the covenant is reforged before collection concludes. Instruments exist. Stones. Speakers." His gaze flicked upward, toward the kitchen, toward the laughter. "You have one of each already. How remarkable. Almost as though someone planned it."

"Who holds the stones?"

"They were sold," said the Hoarfrost Advocate. "By the office that held them in trust. Sold, over the centuries, one by one, to buyers whose names are written in your own ledgers." And he smiled, finally, properly, and it was the worst thing she had seen all winter. "Audit yourself, keeper of ledgers. Then decide whom you serve."

* * *

He left no footprints, which Quentin took personally. Everyone left footprints; it was practically the social contract. But the corridor where he'd walked bore only a thin rime of frost in the shape of absence, already fading, and the candle relit itself as though embarrassed to have been involved.

She sat for a long time at the table with the ghost of the contract still cold in her mind, doing what she did best, which was arithmetic.

Veth had not abandoned her. Veth had *paid*. Had handed over its voice—the voice that sang the seasons into place, the voice Paloma remembered from a warm country of standing stones—and gotten, in exchange, nothing but time. Time bought for the world at the price of a god's silence. And she had spent three years resenting the god for the purchase, the way a child resents a parent who works late.

"Well," she said aloud, to the empty room, and her voice cracked on the second word, and she let it. "Damn you anyway. For not telling me."

But gods with no mouths couldn't tell anyone anything. That was rather the point.

She wiped her face with the heel of her hand, efficiently, the way she closed a ledger at the end of a long night, and went upstairs to audit herself.

The kitchen was warm and gold-lit and full of smells Hesper had no business producing from turnips. Tam was showing Paloma how to draw a lopsided cat. Hesper was threatening soup. And Paloma looked up as Quentin came in, and said something in the old tongue—soft, questioning, her head tipped—and pointed, with the unerring instinct of children and auditors, directly at the place where Quentin's heart had just broken and reassembled itself slightly wrong.

"No," Quentin said, and then, because lying to the child felt like writing a false entry, amended: "Not yet. But I know what's owed now. And I know who owes it."

She looked around the kitchen—at Hesper's spoon frozen mid-stir, at Tam's pen dripping, at Paloma's patient, stubborn, hopeful face—and heard the Advocate's parting words again. *Sold, by the office that held them in trust. Written in your own ledgers.*

"Tam," she said. "How would you feel about pulling the oldest ledgers in the archive? The ones nobody's opened since before Corvane's time?"

Tam's pen stopped dripping. "Define 'nobody.'"

"Everyone. Ever. Including the parts the guild would very much prefer stayed unread." Quentin pulled out a chair and sat, heavily, among the warmth, a woman with a creditor's deadline and a god's silence and, for the first time in three years, an actual job description. "Because it turns out our guild sold the pieces of the world's oldest promise. One stone at a time. To buyers whose signatures we keep."

Hesper set down the ladle. Paloma reached across the table and put her small hand flat on Quentin's ledger-callused fingers, and said, in the tongue before kingdoms, a word that needed no translation.

Outside, the frost climbed another inch up the window, patient as paperwork.

Quentin looked at it, and at the child, and at the blank page waiting in every book downstairs, and understood that the audit had already begun—and that the first entry under *liabilities* was going to be everything the Gilded Thorn had ever sold.
