## 6. Oath-Stones

The Gilded Thorn kept its oldest ledgers in a room that smelled of dust, tallow, and old decisions.

Quentin had worked in that room for eleven years and still found it unsettling, though she would have bitten off her own tongue before admitting it. The Archive—capital A, because Corvane insisted on the capital—occupied the guildhouse's lowest floor, below the vaults, below even the wine cellar, which everyone agreed was the true foundation of any respectable institution. The shelves went back further than the building did. That was not a figure of speech. Quentin had once measured the east wall against the blueprints and come up short by nine feet, as if the Archive had quietly eaten its way into the earth out of simple appetite.

She lit three lamps, set her tea where it could not possibly spill onto anything older than her grandmother, and pulled the first ledger of the First Shelf down with both hands.

It weighed as much as a sleeping dog. The cover was vellum gone the color of weak tea, and someone—some clerk dead four hundred years, probably—had titled it in faded iron-gall ink: *Accounts of the Covenant, Being a True and Faithful Record.* Quentin snorted. No ledger ever written was either true or faithful. Ledgers were like their keepers: they told you what they wanted you to know, and you had to read the margins to learn the rest.

She had been reading margins for two days now.

Ever since the Hoarfrost Advocate had stood in the front hall with his breath fogging in summer-warm air and said the words *held in escrow*, Quentin had not slept properly. She was a mercenary before she was a bookkeeper, and mercenaries knew what escrow meant. It meant something was being kept safe for a third party. It meant the thing had an owner who had never given it up. It meant that someday, someone would come to collect.

Veth's voice sat in some account somewhere, accruing interest. And the winter outside the windows—the frost in the first week of harvest, the ice on the gutters, the child half-dead on their doorstep speaking a language that made every ledger in the building open itself to blank pages—that winter was not weather.

That winter was a *notice*.

"Right," Quentin said aloud, to the empty Archive, because talking to herself was cheaper than talking to a god who didn't answer. "Let's find out who's been robbing us."

* * *

The early entries were beautiful, in the way that very old things are beautiful: everything slightly wrong, all the conventions not yet invented. The first clerks of the Gilded Thorn hadn't used numbers. They'd used knots—actual knotted cords bound into the spine alongside the pages—and each knot was annotated in that same pre-kingdom script Quentin had last seen coming out of a six-year-old's mouth.

Except it wasn't quite the same. Paloma's tongue moved when she spoke it, lived, bent itself around small wants and large fears. The script on these pages was stiff. Ceremonial. The difference between a mother singing and a priest intoning.

Quentin copied out a passage anyway, sounding the syllables under her breath, feeling foolish. She got perhaps one word in ten by guesswork, and one of those guesses was almost certainly wrong, because it translated in her head as either "*oath*" or "*onion*," and no covenant in history had ever been sealed with onions.

Then she found the stones.

They appeared first as inventory. Entry 114, in a hand so precise it looked printed: *Four (4) oath-stones, grey-blue, uncut, humming. Condition: excellent. Provenance: the Covenant of the Four Realms, being the terms under which voice is lent and season is pledged. Location: one per realm, set at the boundary-stones. Purpose: anchor. Do not move. DO NOT MOVE.*

Someone had underlined the last instruction twice, and then, several decades later in a different, shakier hand, added in the margin: *We moved them.*

Quentin sat back. Her tea had gone cold. She drank it anyway, because cold tea was a punishment she understood and the thing unfolding in front of her was not.

She turned pages. The stones recurred through the ledgers like a refrain—inspected, insured, occasionally *humming louder than usual, see attached complaint from neighbors*. And then, in the ledger for the year the kingdom of Vail was founded, a new entry, in a hand Quentin recognized.

Corvane Dusk had been guildmaster for thirty years. But his handwriting had not changed since he was a junior clerk; Quentin had seen his earliest apprenticeship samples in the personnel files, back when she'd been bored enough to snoop. The neat, upright, faintly smug letters were unmistakable.

Only—they weren't his. Not yet. The year on this entry predated Corvane's birth by two hundred years.

Which meant the Dusk family had been writing the same way, in the same hand, for centuries. Trained to it. Or *choosing* it. Quentin filed that thought away in the drawer where she kept things she wasn't ready to look at directly.

Entry 1,902: *Oath-stone the first (Northrealm), sold to the Chapel of the Drowned Saint, price: remission of the guild's debt in kind. Signed, Guildmaster Aldous Dusk. Note: the stone wished to go. It hummed at the chapel bells until they agreed.*

Entry 2,317: *Oath-stone the second (Eastrealm), sold to the Vault of the House of Ninefold Interest, price: forty years of good harvest, held in trust. Signed, Guildmaster Sera Dusk. Note: the buyer does not understand what it anchors. This is acceptable. Understanding is not required of collateral.*

Entry 3,044: *Oath-stone the third (Southrealm), sold privately. Price: [redacted]. Buyer: [redacted]. Signed, Guildmaster [redacted]. Note: I am sorry. I am sorry. I am sorry.*

Three lines of *I am sorry*, each one fainter than the last, as if the pen were running out of ink or the writer out of conviction.

Quentin put the ledger down and pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes.

Her guild. Her ridiculous, beloved, infuriating guild, with its rules about tea and its ledgers of bottled grief and its policy of never asking where the impossible things came from. They hadn't just traded in the leftovers of the First Covenant. They had *dismantled* it. Sold it off piece by piece across five centuries, one solvent transaction at a time, each guildmaster presumably telling themselves what Quentin told herself every time she balanced a cruel column: *it's just business, the alternative is worse, someone else would have done it anyway.*

And the fourth stone?

She searched. She searched the way she'd once tracked a debtor through three cities and a false beard: methodically, vindictively, cross-referencing every mention, every margin note, every stray knot in the binding cords. Oath-stone the fourth, Westrealm. It appeared in the early inventories. It appeared in inspection reports—it was always reported as the quietest of the four, *"hums only when spoken to."*

And then, in the ledger of the year the long cold began—the year Veth went silent, the year the prayers stopped being answered—there was a single entry.

*Oath-stone the fourth (Westrealm): status amended. Location: [no entry]. Custodian: [no entry]. Value: [no entry].*

Every other field in the ledger was filled in. Every other field, in five hundred years of obsessive record-keeping, had been filled in. But the fourth stone's line simply ended, and after it, in the margin, in a hand Quentin did not recognize—sharper than the Dusks', slanted, urgent, like writing done while running—

*It was never sold. It was spent.*

Below that, smaller, almost illegible:

*Ask the god. If it will speak. It will not speak. That is the whole of the tragedy. We took the payment and the god took the silence and neither of us read the full contract. — a clerk who loved this house*

Quentin read it four times.

Then she did what she always did when the world refused to make sense: she made a list.

*Known:* One stone in a drowned chapel up north. One stone in a moneylender's vault, location uncertain but the House of Ninefold Interest had branches in every city worth robbing. One stone sold privately, redacted, which meant someone powerful had bought it, which meant, statistically, someone in this city, because the powerful never traveled farther than their own vanity required.

*Unknown:* The fourth stone. Spent, not sold. Whatever that meant.

*Also unknown:* how much time they had before the Hoarfrost Advocate came back with his receipt.

She wrote it all out fair, in her best ledger hand, and then she looked at the page and laughed—one short, ugly bark that echoed off the shelves and came back sounding accusing.

Because here was the joke, and it was a good one, the kind the gods told when they'd been drinking: the guild had spent five hundred years selling pieces of the covenant to pay its debts, and now the debt had come due, and the only assets left in the account were a silent god, a stolen child, and Quentin Nakamura, whose entire fortune consisted of one mercenary contract, one sword, and a teacup with a chip in it shaped vaguely like a fish.

"We're insolvent," she informed the Archive. "We've been insolvent for centuries. We just had very good handwriting."

* * *

She climbed the stairs slowly, the copied pages folded inside her coat, next to her heart like contraband, which in a sense they were. Knowledge was inventory at the Thorn. Knowledge about the guild's own crimes was inventory nobody was supposed to possess.

The kitchen was the one room in the guildhouse that operated on a different economy. Everywhere else, everything was owed. In the kitchen, Hesper gave things away—flavors, mostly, and second helpings, and opinions nobody had asked for. Quentin smelled tonight's work from the landing: bread, and under the bread something stranger, the ghost-taste of a summer afternoon, the specific golden light of a day you'd forgotten you'd had. Hesper cooked memories into food the way other cooks used salt. It was technically against guild policy. Hesper had been technically against guild policy for twenty years and the guild had learned to live with it, the way you learn to live with weather.

Paloma was sitting on the counter.

This was also against policy. Paloma had been at the Thorn for nine days now, hidden—Corvane's word was *appraised*, and he said it the way a man says *pending*—and in nine days she had annexed the kitchen the way small conquerors do: completely, permanently, and without anyone quite remembering agreeing to it. She was wrapped in one of Tam's old coats, sleeves rolled six times, swinging her feet, watching Hesper knead dough with the total absorption most people reserved for executions.

And she was talking. To Hesper. In the old tongue, a stream of it, and Hesper—Hesper, who spoke Common, Kitchen, and enough Thieves' Cant to insult a customs officer—was nodding along, answering in Common, and somehow the conversation was working. Quentin had watched this happen for days. It should not have worked. Language was supposed to be a wall.

But Hesper flavored memories, and memory, it turned out, was the older language. Paloma said a word that meant, as far as Quentin could guess, *warm*, and Hesper handed her the heel of the loaf fresh from the oven, and the word landed. Paloma said a longer word, urgent, pointing at the window where the frost ferned up the glass, and Hesper's face did something complicated, and she said, "I know, little one. I know it's wrong, the cold coming this early. I know."

Not understanding. *Answering.* There was a difference, and Quentin felt the difference land in her chest like a key turning.

Paloma saw her then. The child went quiet—not afraid, exactly. Watchful. She had taken to studying Quentin with a particular intensity, and Quentin had a bad feeling she knew why. On the second night, half-asleep by the kitchen fire, Paloma had reached out and touched the back of Quentin's hand, right over the small grey mark every sworn servant of Veth carried—a circle with a line through it, the sigil of the ledger-god, the brand of the bound. The child had traced it twice, frowning, and said one word.

Quentin hadn't asked anyone what it meant. She suspected she already knew. Half of something broken, that was her guess. A sigil cut in two, waiting for its missing piece.

"The bread's for you," Hesper said now, nodding at a plate. "Sit. You've got your research face on. You look like a woman who's found out her family's been robbing graves."

"My family's been robbing *everything*," Quentin said, taking the stool. "Graves would be an improvement."

She ate. The bread tasted like a market day from when she was eight—her mother's hand around hers, sun on cobbles, nothing owed to anyone. Hesper watched her eat it with the satisfied expression of a smuggler whose cargo has cleared customs.

"Found something?" Hesper asked, too casually.

Quentin hesitated. Rule one of the Thorn: information is inventory. Rule two: inventory is leverage. She had lived by both rules for eleven years, and they had kept her safe and fed and alone.

She looked at Paloma, who was watching her over the collar of Tam's enormous coat, patient as a stone, humming—actually humming, a low grey-blue note that Quentin felt in her teeth more than heard, the same note, she realized with a lurch, that the ledgers described the oath-stones making.

*Hums only when spoken to,* the old entry said.

The child had spoken to her mark. Maybe that was why the ledgers opened themselves. Maybe the child had been speaking to them too.

"Found something," Quentin said. "Three somethings. Sold, all of them, by my illustrious predecessors. And a fourth that—" She stopped. Started again. "A fourth that isn't anywhere. Which means the only way to fix what's coming is to go get the three that are left, and I don't know where two of them are, and the third one's in a chapel at the bottom of a lake."

Silence in the kitchen. Even the fire seemed to lean in.

"Well," said Hesper, wiping flour off her hands with the air of a woman settling a long-standing account, "good thing you know people who are good at finding things that can't be found."

"You cook memories."

"And Tam," Hesper said, "forges documents better than the government that invented them, and hasn't been able to tell a lie in ink since the day he was born, which makes him the only honest man in this building. And you—" she pointed the rolling pin at Quentin like a scepter "—you are going to stand right there and tell us the whole story, start to finish, while I make soup. Because that child has been listening to you worry for nine days, Quentin Nakamura, and children can't do anything about worrying except catch it."

Quentin opened her mouth to say that this was guild business, that it was dangerous, that civilians should stay out of it.

What came out instead, to her considerable surprise, was the truth. All of it. The stones, the sales, the redactions, the desperate clerk's marginal confession, the whole insolvent history of the house she'd sworn to. Hesper stirred soup and made small outraged noises in the appropriate places. Paloma listened without understanding a single word and yet leaned forward at every turn, as if the shape of the story alone were nourishment.

And when Quentin finally ran out of words and sat there hollowed out and lighter than she'd felt in years, Paloma slid off the counter, padded across the flagstones, and put one small hand flat on the folded pages sticking out of Quentin's coat.

She said a word. Just one. The same word she'd said that night over Quentin's mark.

Then she pointed north.

Toward the drowned chapel. Toward the first stone.

Toward whatever was coming for all of them, walking down out of the white with a receipt in its hand.
