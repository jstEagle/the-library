## 7. The Cook, the Forger, and the Child

Hesper had been flavoring memories for thirty-one years, and in all that time she had never once been asked to cook for a child.

She stood in her kitchen at the bottom of the Gilded Thorn—a room the guild's founders had built generous and warm, back when they still believed comfort was a commodity worth stocking—and she looked at the tray Corvane's steward had sent down with its instructions, and she felt something in her chest go tight and wrong.

*One portion. Bland. Nothing that might affect the appraisal.*

The appraisal. As if the child were a jar of bottled grief to be shelved until some buyer came along with coin enough for the impossible.

"Nothing that might affect the appraisal," Hesper repeated aloud, to the kitchen at large. Her pots, which knew her moods better than most people did, rattled on their hooks in sympathy.

The trouble with being the guild's memory-cook was that Hesper could not make a bland meal if her life depended on it. Every dish she made carried something of what it was made from, and what it was made *for*. A soup simmered with patience came out patient; you ate it and found yourself willing to wait an extra hour for a late friend without resentment. Bread kneaded with courage gave shy people spines for an evening. She had once made a forgiveness-stew for two brothers who hadn't spoken in nine years, and they'd wept into their bowls and left arm in arm, and Hesper had cried too, alone in her kitchen, because that was the other thing about her cooking: it always left a little of itself in the cook.

So when she went up the back stairs that night—past the locked door on the third landing where the child was kept, past the guard who pretended not to see her because everyone pretended not to see Hesper, that was the arrangement—she carried a tray that was many things, but bland was not one of them.

Barley soup with honey stirred in at the end, cooked slow, cooked kind: it would taste like being looked after. Fresh bread with rosemary, because rosemary was for remembering, and whatever else this child had lost, Hesper wanted her to keep her memories. And a small pot of plum preserves made last summer from fruit grown in the guild's neglected courtyard garden—the one thing about the Gilded Thorn that Hesper refused to let die—which would taste, faintly and stubbornly, like summer. Like proof that summer existed. That it came back.

The guard stepped aside. "Corvane said—"

"I know what Corvane said." Hesper was a wide woman with flour permanently dusted in the creases of her knuckles and a voice like a hearth settling. "Corvane says a great many things. He also says the kitchen budget is fine, and I've seen the books."

She knocked softly, then opened the door.

The child was sitting in the corner farthest from the window, knees drawn up, watching the door with the flat, unblinking attention of a small animal deciding whether the thing entering the den was weather or wolf. Bare feet. The guild had provided slippers; the slippers sat untouched by the bed, arranged with suspicious neatness, as though the child had considered them and filed them under *traps*.

"Hello, little bird," Hesper said, and set the tray down on the floor between them, and backed off two steps, and sat down herself, heavily, on the cold flagstones, because you didn't feed a frightened creature from above. You got down where it lived.

The child stared at the soup. Steam curled up off it, carrying rosemary and honey and the ghost of plums.

Something happened to the child's face. It was very quick, and it was gone almost before Hesper caught it—but she caught it. The rigid wariness cracked, just for a heartbeat, into something younger and hungrier and infinitely sadder. The look of someone smelling a kitchen from a country that no longer existed.

"You know," Hester said conversationally, as the child crept forward inch by inch, "I've fed a lot of strange appetites in this house. There was a man who paid us to bottle his grief, and before he left he asked me for one last meal, and do you know what he wanted? Boiled mutton. Plain boiled mutton, the way his mother ruined it. Worst thing I ever cooked on purpose." The child reached the tray. Small hands closed around the bowl. "Grief wants what it remembers, even when what it remembers is bad. That's how you know it's grief."

The child ate like the winter outside had taught her exactly how long food could be trusted to stay food. But halfway through the bowl she stopped, spoon suspended, and looked at Hesper with her head tipped, and said a short string of words in that language no one in the guild could parse—liquid, singing, older than the stones of the building—and pressed one hand flat against her own chest, and then gestured at the bowl, and then at Hesper, and then made a sound that needed no translation at all, because it was the same in every tongue ever spoken by anyone:

*Mm.*

"That's right, little bird," Hesper said, her throat suddenly inconvenient. "That's right."

She stayed until the bowl was empty and the bread was crumbs and the child's eyes were drooping despite herself, fighting sleep like it owed her money. On the way out, Hesper paused at the door.

"Tomorrow I'll bring the good spoons," she said. "And we'll see about those slippers."

Behind her, in the dark, the child said one more word. Just one. Hesper didn't understand it, but she understood it perfectly anyway, the way you understand warmth.

It sounded like *stay.*

* * *

Tam Voss could not lie in ink.

This was, professionally speaking, a catastrophe. Tam was the Gilded Thorn's forger, and a forger who couldn't lie was like a locksmith afraid of doors. His documents were flawless—hand, seal, watermark, the correct tea-stain of age on the vellum—and every single one of them told the truth. He had forged a merchant's travel writ so perfect that the merchant himself later used it, and it worked, because the merchant really was a merchant really traveling really north. He had authenticated deeds, wills, letters of credit, and every forgery he'd ever produced had turned out, upon inspection, to be accurate down to the last copper. The guild kept him because his work never failed. The guild mocked him because his work never failed *honestly*, which everyone agreed rather missed the point of forgery.

Tam had a private theory about why. Ink, in his experience, remembered. You put words down and they sank in and held their shape forever, and somewhere in that holding, they became accountable. Paper was a witness that never blinked. He had tried, early in his career, to forge a lie—a bill of sale for a horse that had died, sold to a buyer who didn't exist—and his hand had simply refused. The pen had dragged. The ink had come out gray. In the end he'd written the truth instead: *this horse is dead, and I am sorry, and here is what it was worth while it lived.* The client had framed it.

So it was Tam, naturally, whom Quentin Nakamura came to see on the fourth night of the frost, moving through the scriptorium's lamplight like a woman with stolen goods in her pockets, which, in a manner of speaking, she did.

"I need you to look at something," Quentin said, "and tell no one."

"I can't lie in ink," Tam said, "but out loud I'm a fountain of deceit. Ask anyone. No one will confirm it."

Quentin set a page on his desk. It was a rubbing—charcoal on paper—of a mark she'd taken from the child's doorframe, or perhaps the child had left it there; Tam gathered the details were complicated and classified. The mark was a spiral broken by a straight line through its heart, half of something, clearly, the way a key is half of a lock.

Tam looked at it, and the hair on his arms stood up, because he *knew* this mark. Not from life. From work.

"Wait," he said. "Wait, wait." He was already pulling ledgers, three years back, five years back, ten, his fingers walking the spines the way other men walked streets they'd grown up on. "I authenticated a deed once—I don't authenticate many things anymore, they mostly have Quentin for that, but this one came through my desk because it involved land grants and old script, pre-kingdom script, and I'm the only one who reads it—" He found the ledger. Found the entry. Found the copy of the deed folded into the record, yellowed, official, stamped with the seal of a guildmaster two reigns back.

And there, in the corner of the deed, beside the signature of the seller, was the mark. The same spiral. The same break. Except on the deed, the mark was whole—or rather, it was the *other* half. The mirror of the child's.

"What was sold?" Quentin asked quietly.

Tam read. His mouth went dry. "A stone," he said. "An oath-stone. From the First Covenant. Sold to a private buyer, name redacted, price listed as—" he squinted— "'one favor, uncashed, in perpetuity.' Who sells a stone for a favor?"

"A guildmaster," Quentin said, "apparently. Several of them. How many of these have you authenticated, Tam?"

He didn't want to count. He counted anyway, because his hands were honest even when his mouth wasn't. Three deeds. Three stones. Three sales spanning sixty years, each one signed, sealed, and made true by the only forger in the building who couldn't lie.

"I didn't know," he said. His voice came out small. "I read the script. I checked the seals. I never— it was land records, it was *boring*, Quentin, nobody tells the forger what the boring things mean—"

"I know." Quentin took the page back. Her face was doing something careful and controlled that Tam recognized as her version of fury. "That's rather the point of boring things."

He sat with it after she left. The lamp burned low. Outside, the frost ticked against the windows like a clerk counting coins.

He should have felt worse, Tam thought. He had, in the smallest and most technical sense, helped sell the pieces of whatever the child was heir to. But what he actually felt, sitting there in the honest lamplight, was not guilt exactly. It was direction. For the first time in his careful, truthful, useless career, Tam Voss knew exactly which document he needed to find next—and exactly why.

He got a fresh sheet of paper, and wrote at the top of it, in his beautiful, incorruptible hand: *Things I Have Made True That Should Never Have Been True.* Then he began the list.

It had three items. He intended to cross them all off.

* * *

By the ninth day of the frost, the child had a name—not her real one, which no one could pronounce, but a working title—and the working title was Mouse, bestowed by Hesper on the grounds that the girl moved through the guild's corridors the way mice move through walls: silently, along the edges, appearing in doorways when least expected and vanishing the moment anyone looked directly at her.

Mouse appeared in the kitchen on the morning of the ninth day, which was not supposed to be possible, because the kitchen was four floors down and two locked doors away from where Mouse was kept, and yet there she stood in the doorway at dawn, barefoot on Hesper's warm flagstones, having defeated the locks through means no one ever fully established. (Tam, when consulted, examined the locks and pronounced them "unpicked, unbribed, and unbothered," and suggested, with a straight face, that the child had simply asked the doors politely in the old tongue, and the doors, being older than the guild, had agreed.)

Hesper didn't ask. Hesper put her to work.

It began with potatoes. Mouse could not be allowed to wander the guild—Corvane's orders were explicit, and the corridors above were full of clerks with appraising eyes—but the kitchen was below the guild's attention, the way cellars are below everything, and a child peeling potatoes was furniture. So Mouse peeled. She peeled badly at first, removing more potato than peel, and Hesper corrected her hands gently, wrapping her own broad ones around the small ones, and by the third day Mouse could peel a potato into a smooth pale spiral with a single unbroken turn, which was either talent or witchcraft, and Hesper chose to call it talent because witchcraft got children appraised.

They built a language out of necessity. Hesper named things—*potato, knife, fire, salt*—and Mouse repeated the sounds in her accent that bent every vowel like sunlight through water, and then, inevitably, she answered in her own tongue, giving each word its older, deeper twin, so that within two weeks Hesper's kitchen vocabulary had become bilingual and Hesper had begun dreaming in a language she didn't speak. *Fire,* Mouse said, pointing at the oven, and then said the old word, and the old word made the flames lean slightly toward her, and both of them decided, by mutual unspoken treaty, not to mention that.

Tam started coming down in the evenings. Officially he came to eat, which was true, because Hesper's cooking was the only honest pleasure in the building. Unofficially he came with paper, because he had conceived a scholar's passion for the old tongue and a forger's discipline for learning it, and Mouse was a patient teacher in the way of children everywhere: merciless about pronunciation, delighted by error.

"No," she would say—she had learned *no* first, and wielded it with relish—"no," and take the pen from Tam's hand, and draw the character properly, a flowing spiral-with-a-break, and say the word again, and Tam would try again, and get it wrong again, and she would press both hands over her face in theatrical despair while Hesper laughed hard enough to shake the hanging pots.

"She's laughing at you," Hesper translated.

"I'm aware," Tam said. "In any language, I'm aware."

But he kept the practice sheets. All of them. Even the failures. Especially the failures. There was one sheet—his first attempt at writing her real name, the one she had finally offered, whispering it like a secret worth more than the guild's entire catalogue—where he had gotten every stroke wrong, and instead of crumpling it, Mouse had taken it, studied his mangled version of her own dear name, and smiled, and touched the page, and touched her chest, and said the old word for *gift.*

Tam kept that sheet in the inner pocket of his coat, over his heart, where important documents belonged.

The kitchen changed. This was the thing nobody planned and everybody noticed. The Gilded Thorn upstairs was all ledgers and locks and the hush of valuable things, but downstairs the kettle sang, and the bread proved, and a child's laughter—rare at first, then less rare, then, on one miraculous evening involving a collapsed soufflé and Tam wearing most of it, frequent—warmed the flagstones better than the ovens did. Clerks began finding reasons to deliver messages personally to the kitchen. The night guard on the third landing started leaving his boots outside Mouse's door, wordlessly, because a growing child shouldn't be barefoot, and Mouse left them alone for six days and then wore them, and the guard walked around grinning for a week.

Hesper cooked bolder now. If the stew tasted like courage, well. If the morning porridge carried a note of *you are safe here, you are safe here, you are safe here*—well. Corvane had ordered bland. Hesper had been flavoring memories for thirty-one years, and she flavored this one the way she wanted it remembered: warm, and golden, and full.

On the twelfth night of the frost, the three of them sat at the scarred kitchen table after close—soup, bread, the good spoons, as promised—and Mouse drew on the table in spilled flour, because paper was precious and flour was free. She drew the spiral-and-break, Quentin's mark, the god's mark, and then she drew its mirror, and then she fit them together, and tapped the whole shape twice.

Then she looked up at them with an expression that needed no translation, and pointed upward—through four floors, through the ledgers, through the locked rooms—toward the guild above, and the guildmaster at the top of it.

Tam felt the list in his inner pocket. Hesper felt the stew going cold in the pot. Outside, the frost ticked at the windows, patient as a creditor.

And upstairs, in the catalogue room, Guildmaster Corvane Dusk dipped his pen and wrote, in the solstice auction ledger, beneath a fresh entry, two words that would burn through the whole warm kitchen below like frost through glass:

*Lot One.*
