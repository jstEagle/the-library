## 8. Sold at Auction

The solstice catalogue arrived on Quentin's desk the way bad news always did at the Gilded Thorn: bound in good leather, typeset with care, and smelling faintly of money changing hands.

She had been proofing entries since sundown. Lot 14: a jar of a widow's grief over her second husband, lighter than most, notes of rosemary. Lot 15: eleven borrowed years from a man who had run out of things to live for and wanted them back by spring. Lot 16: a name not yet spoken, still warm, still deciding what it wanted to be when it grew up.

Then she turned to Lot 17.

*One (1) child, female, approx. seven years. Origin: unknown. Provenance: found at guild door, first frost. Speaks the pre-kingdom tongue of the First Covenant — believed to be the last living speaker. Appraised as: irreplaceable. Reserve: none. The guild accepts no liability for seasonal phenomena associated with this lot.*

Quentin read it three times. The third time was not because the words were difficult. It was because she kept hoping they would rearrange themselves into something else.

"Corvane," she said to the empty office, in the tone she usually reserved for misfiled ledgers, "you absolute *ledger-entry*."

She took the stairs two at a time, which for Quentin Nakamura constituted open weeping.

* * *

Corvane Dusk received her in his study, where he sat behind a desk older than most kingdoms, doing sums in a hand so neat it looked printed. He was a long, grey man, precise as a closing bracket, and he had once been kind — Quentin had seen the fossil record of it in the way he still fed the courtyard crows, though he'd deny it under oath.

"You've seen the catalogue," he said, without looking up.

"I've seen an abomination with a lot number."

"It's inventory management, Quentin."

"She is a *child*."

"She is the last speaker of a dead language, currently eating my kitchen out of house and home, attracting frost that has no business existing this far south, and drawing the attention of collectors who make the Hoarfrost Advocate look like a friendly neighborhood moneylender." He set down his pen at last. "I am keeping her safe the only way I know how. Behind glass. With a price tag."

"That's not safety, that's display."

"In thirty years of running this guild, no item behind glass with a price tag has ever been stolen, harmed, or eaten by winter." He folded his hands. "Name me one better system."

"A family."

"The guild *is* a family. A family with excellent accounting."

Quentin stared at him. Somewhere below them, faintly, Hesper's kitchen was producing smells that could reconcile sworn enemies, and Tam was presumably teaching Paloma to draw letters in flour, correcting himself every time his pen-hand tried to embellish. A family with excellent accounting. She almost laughed. It would have come out wrong.

"Corvane," she said quietly, "what happens if someone buys her?"

"Someone reputable. Someone vetted." He resumed his sums. "Scholars. Museums. Possibly a very wealthy language enthusiast."

"And if the Hoarfrost Advocate bids?"

The pen did not stop moving, but it slowed — one stroke, half a heartbeat longer than it should have been. Quentin had spent nine years reading Corvane Dusk the way other people read contracts: for the hesitations.

"He won't," Corvane said. "Winter doesn't buy. Winter collects."

"Winter is going to be very interested in Lot 17."

"Then I suggest," said the guildmaster, "that you place a bid."

It took her a moment to understand that he had just done something almost like kindness, wrapped in the language of commerce the way everything in this building was. He was telling her the only way to keep the child was to win her. Fairly. Openly. On the books.

He was also, Quentin suspected, testing exactly how much she was willing to lose.

* * *

The solstice auction was held in the Long Gallery, which had been built to display the guild's grandest acquisitions and now mostly displayed dust sheets and one extremely smug taxidermied griffin. Candles burned low and blue — the guild bought its candlelight wholesale from a woman in the fen country, and it always gave buyers that flattering, slightly drowned look that loosened purses.

Quentin arrived early and stood at the back, cataloguing exits out of habit. Hesper materialized beside her carrying a tray of spiced wine nobody had ordered, which was how Hesper attended everything.

"You look terrible," Hesper said fondly. "Here. It's got courage in it. Actual bottled courage, third shelf, don't tell Tam, he thinks I've been watering it down."

"Hesper. If I don't come back from this—"

"You'll come back. You're too stubborn to be sold against." She peered at the catalogue over Quentin's arm and went very still. "Lot 17. Oh, that *bastard*."

"He says it's inventory management."

"He flavors memories for a living, Quentin, I know a lie of omission when I smell one." Her voice dropped. "What are you going to do?"

Quentin looked down at the catalogue. At the neat entry. *Believed to be the last living speaker.* At the word *irreplaceable*, which Corvane had chosen, she realized, deliberately — because irreplaceable things drew the highest bidders, and the highest bidder tonight needed to be her.

"I'm going to bid," she said.

"On what? Your savings wouldn't cover the jar of widow-grief."

"I know." Quentin turned the catalogue over in her hands. "That's why I brought everything else."

Tam found them ten minutes later, pale and clutching his hat. "They're saying there's a stranger upstairs. Grey coat. Frost on the shoulders indoors. The doorman let him in because he showed credentials and the doorman's eyebrows are currently frozen at 'alarmed.'"

"The Advocate," Quentin said.

"At a *guild auction*. That's like death showing up at a wedding."

"Death shows up at weddings all the time. It's called marriage." Hesper pressed the wine into Quentin's hands. "Drink. Then go get your daughter."

"She's not my—" Quentin began, and stopped, because the lie refused to form even in her own head, and she was standing next to a forger who couldn't abide untruthful ink and a cook who could taste one at forty paces. "...Fine. Yes. Drink first."

* * *

Lots 1 through 16 went smoothly, in the way that auctions go smoothly when everyone present is professionally comfortable trading in human sorrow. The widow's grief fetched a respectable sum. The borrowed years were contested hotly between a physician and a playwright, which told you everything about both professions.

Then the auctioneer — a reedy man named Pellwick whose voice had been appraised and insured by the guild itself — tapped the lectern.

"Lot seventeen."

The room changed temperature. Not metaphorically; several candles guttered, and the man beside Quentin stopped chewing his mint.

Pellwick read the entry aloud in his insured voice, and Quentin watched the audience the way she watched ledgers: for hesitations. Scholars leaned forward. A museum consortium murmured. In the third row, a woman wearing furs that were moving slightly despite being dead wrote a number on her card that made her escort inhale.

And at the back, by the doors, stood a figure in a grey coat, frost blooming across his shoulders like slow white ivy, holding a bidding card that appeared to be made of ice and appeared, also, to be unnecessary.

"Opening bid," said Pellwick, "two hundred crowns."

"Two-fifty," said the fur woman.

"Three hundred," said the museum consortium.

"Five hundred," said the grey coat, without raising anything, and his voice came out the way a lake sounds when it freezes overnight.

The room went quiet in the specific way rooms go quiet when people realize the game has changed. Pellwick's professional smile developed a crack down the middle.

Quentin raised her card. "Six hundred."

Every head turned. She felt the attention land on her like weather. Hesper, somewhere behind her, whispered "*yes*."

"Seven hundred," said the fur woman gamely.

"One thousand," said the grey coat.

Quentin closed her eyes for exactly one breath. Then she opened the ledger she had brought — her personal ledger, the one containing everything Quentin Nakamura owned in this world — and began to spend it.

"One thousand five hundred," she said, "and my townhouse on Candlemaker's Row."

Murmurs. Pellwick blinked. "The... the house is included in the bid?"

"The house, fully owned, deed in hand, no liens. One thousand five hundred and the house."

"Two thousand," said the grey coat, and the frost on his shoulders crept down his sleeves.

"Two thousand five hundred," said Quentin, "the house, and my commission collection — fourteen completed mercenary contracts, all paid, all documented, worth roughly four thousand crowns in resale."

Someone in the crowd actually gasped. Selling a completed contract was close to selling a piece of your own history; collectors loved them, the way people love locks of hair.

"Three thousand," said the grey coat. His voice had begun to leave frost on the air itself, little clouds that hung and did not disperse.

Hesper's hand found Quentin's shoulder. "Stop," she breathed. "You'll have nothing."

"I know what I'll have," Quentin said, and raised her card again.

"Three thousand five hundred," she said, "the house, the commissions, and my guild shares."

"*Quentin*—"

"Four thousand," said the grey coat.

And here was the thing about Quentin Nakamura that the Gilded Thorn had never quite understood, though the ledgers should have told them: she had never once in nine years bid on anything for herself. She appraised. She recorded. She kept other people's treasures safe and wanted none of them. Her savings were large and her life was small and the gap between those two numbers had always looked, to her, like loneliness dressed up as discipline.

Paloma ate breakfast with flour on her nose. Paloma called the ledger-god mark on Quentin's wrist "half of something broken" and had tried, twice, to sing it whole. Paloma had learned the word "home" four days ago and used it, casually, devastatingly, while drying dishes.

There was nothing in the world Quentin owned that was worth more than that. So it was all, therefore, worth exactly that.

"Four thousand," Quentin said, "the house, the commissions, the shares — and my contract."

The silence this time was total. Even the grey coat stilled.

"My mercenary contract," she clarified, into the hush, because clarity mattered in commerce. "Sworn service to the god Veth, held in trust by this guild, nine years standing, non-transferable by guild law." She paused. "Transferable by covenant law, however, upon voluntary surrender before witnesses. Which I am offering. Voluntarily. Before witnesses."

Corvane Dusk, seated in the shadowed box reserved for the guildmaster, went very still. It was the stillness of a man watching a number appear in his ledger that he had not written and could not have predicted, and finding, somewhere beneath the alarm, a strange and unwelcome respect.

"Miss Nakamura," Pellwick said carefully, "a sworn contract cannot simply be—"

"It can," said the grey coat.

Everyone turned. He stepped forward into the candlelight, and up close the frost on him wasn't decoration; it was *growing*, delicate white ferns unfurling along his lapels as he moved. His face was pleasant the way a frozen pond is pleasant, right up until you fall through it.

"Covenant law permits transfer of a sworn contract," the Hoarfrost Advocate said, "if freely offered and freely accepted. And I accept." He smiled. It did not reach anywhere near his eyes, which were the color of the sky before a blizzard. "In fact, mercenary, I find your bid *fascinating*. Do you know what you're offering? Do you know what your god took in exchange for its silence? Do you know what happens to the collateral when the debt comes due?"

"No," Quentin said evenly. "But I know what I'm buying."

"A child."

"A person. Who eats too much bread and sings in a language older than kingdoms and calls my god's mark broken." She met the Advocate's eyes. "And I'm telling you, whatever winter is owed — it isn't her."

Something flickered across the frozen face. Not warmth. Recognition, maybe — the look of a collector encountering an item rarer than any in the catalogue.

"Four thousand five hundred," said the Advocate.

"Five thousand," said Quentin, "and everything else I have, which after this bid is my boots, my pen, and approximately eleven crowns."

A ripple of laughter went through the gallery — nervous, but real. Hesper squeezed her shoulder hard enough to bruise. In the guildmaster's box, Corvane Dusk slowly, deliberately, shook his head once: *stop*.

Quentin did not stop.

"Five thousand," repeated the Advocate, softly, "and I will take the child north, where she will be *safe*, mercenary. Safer than a guild full of sentimental accountants can keep her. Winter is coming for that girl whether you win her or not. The only question is whether it arrives as a buyer or a collector."

"Then I'll keep winning," Quentin said, "every time."

For a long moment the only sound was the tick of frost spreading across the floorboards around the Advocate's shoes.

Then he inclined his head, precisely, like a clerk closing a file. "Withdrawn," he said. "Sold, then — to the mercenary. For five thousand crowns, one townhouse, fourteen commissions, all guild shares, and one sworn contract." He turned toward the doors, and paused. "You should know the terms of what you've surrendered. Contracts voided by their holders forfeit protection. Protection forfeited leaves the holder... *unmarked goods*."

He looked back at her, and the smile returned, thin as ice on a puddle.

"I will see you soon, Quentin Nakamura. Winter keeps excellent records."

The doors closed behind him. The candles steadied. Somewhere in the gallery, the fur woman's dead furs finally stopped moving.

"Sold!" Pellwick croaked, banging the gavel with the enthusiasm of a man who wanted very much for this evening to be over. "Lot seventeen, sold to Miss Quentin Nakamura!"

* * *

They brought Paloma up from the kitchens afterward, because Hesper insisted the child should hear it properly and not from gossip, and because — though Hesper would deny this too — she had been waiting behind the serving door with a celebration cake since lot twelve.

"They sold me?" Paloma asked, blinking up at Quentin. She had the word now, *sold*, along with *auction* and *expensive* and, from Tam, a somewhat garbled understanding of *reserve price*.

"Bought," Quentin corrected. "I bought you. It's different."

"What it cost?"

Quentin considered lying. Then she considered the company she was in — a cook who tasted lies, a forger who couldn't write them, a guildmaster watching from the shadows — and decided honesty was simply the cheaper option.

"Everything," she said. "The house, the commissions, my shares, and my contract with Veth."

Paloma's brow furrowed. She reached out and took Quentin's wrist, turning it over to look at the small dark mark of the ledger-god, the one she had once called half of something broken. Her thumb traced it gently.

"Gone?" she asked.

"Not gone. Given." Quentin flexed her fingers. "There's a difference. Apparently an important one."

Paloma studied the mark a moment longer. Then she looked up, and there was something in her face that made Quentin's chest hurt — the fierce, uncomplicated gravity children reserve for moments adults have failed to notice are important.

"You gave everything," Paloma said slowly, in trade-tongue, hunting each word, "for me."

"Yes."

"Why?"

And Quentin, who had spent nine years recording other people's prices, who knew the exact market value of grief and years and names, opened her mouth to give some dry, defensible, ledger-appropriate answer —

—and the candles went out.

All of them. Every blue flame in the Long Gallery, snuffed at once, and the cold that came in behind the darkness was not the polite cold of a winter evening but the *other* kind, the kind with intent. Frost raced across the floor in a straight line, deliberate as ink from a steady pen, and stopped in a perfect ring around Quentin's feet.

On the back of her wrist, where Veth's mark had been, the skin went cold as riverbed stone. And in the silence, clear as a bell struck once, every person in the room heard the same sound:

A page turning.

Somewhere far off, in whatever ledger holds the debts of gods, an entry had been made. *Protection: forfeited. Holder: marked. Collection: pending.*

"Well," said Hesper into the dark, with forced brightness, "anyone else's cake suddenly feel premature?"

Paloma's small hand found Quentin's in the blackness and gripped it, and the child spoke into the freezing air — one word, in the old tongue, sharp and bright as a blade being drawn.

Quentin didn't know what it meant.

But the frost, for one heartbeat, flinched.
