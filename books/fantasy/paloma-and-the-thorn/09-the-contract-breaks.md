## 9. The Contract Breaks

The auction hammer had not yet finished falling when the ledger began to bleed.

I don't mean that poetically. I mean that the great book chained to my desk—the Ledger of Impossible Things, in which I had recorded eleven years of bottled griefs and borrowed years and names not yet spoken—opened itself to a page I had never written on, and red ink welled up out of the paper like blood through a bandage. The letters assembled themselves in a hand that was not mine, though it knew mine intimately. It was Veth's hand. Or it had been, three years ago, before the god went silent and left me praying into a well with no bottom.

*CONTRACT VOID*, the page said.

Then, beneath it, in smaller script, almost apologetic:

*MARKED FOR COLLECTION.*

"Well," I said aloud, to an empty office, "that's that, then."

I had known it would come to this. I want that on the record, wherever records go when ledgers burn: I knew. When I stood up in the auction hall and bid everything I owned—my savings, my weapons, my mercenary contract itself, the sworn bond between me and the silent god—I did the arithmetic first. Quentin Nakamura does not make bids she cannot cover. The sum of my life came to one child, barefoot, asleep against Hesper's kitchen bench upstairs, speaking the language before kingdoms. It was, by any honest appraisal, a fair trade. I would have been embarrassed to offer less.

But knowing a thing and watching its consequences ink themselves onto your own ledger are two different weathers entirely.

* * *

The contract-breaking took all night, which surprised me. I'd assumed severance from a god would be quick—a snap, a cold wind, done. Instead it happened the way a long marriage ends: slowly, item by item, while you sit there cataloguing what's leaving.

At midnight, the mercenary brand on my left wrist—the small silver mark of Veth, the closed mouth, the folded hands—went from warm to merely skin-temperature. At one, every ward I'd ever paid for on my old boarding room expired at once; I felt them go like teeth loosening. At two, the little protections I'd stopped noticing years ago announced themselves by their absence. The stairwell lamp that never flickered flickered. The draft under the door found me for the first time in a decade. My tea went cold faster than it should have, and I sat there holding the cup anyway, because my hands wanted something to hold that wasn't the ledger.

At three, the mark faded to a ghost of silver, and I understood what I'd actually purchased at that auction.

Not freedom. Not exactly. I'd bought Paloma out of Corvane Dusk's catalogue with the only currency I had left, and the currency had been protection—the god's quiet, constant, unglamorous shelter, the thing that had let me walk through eleven years of the impossible trades without being eaten by any of them. Three years of silence, and I'd still been spending it. Faith is like that. You keep drawing on an account long after you've stopped believing anyone's home.

Now the account was empty, and something out in the snow had been notified.

*COLLECTION PENDING*, the ledger added, helpfully, in fresh red.

"Thank you," I told it. "You've always had a gift for reassurance."

* * *

Hesper found me at dawn, or what passed for dawn in the deepening season—which lately meant a gray light like dishwater poured over the world. She carried two cups of her memory-tea, the kind flavored with other people's better days, and set one in front of me without asking. It tasted of someone's wedding, summer, a garden I didn't recognize. I drank it anyway. Free happiness is free happiness.

"You look terrible," she said.

"I've been voided."

"Mm." She sat down across from me, a broad woman built like a bread oven, with flour in her eyebrows at all hours as if it grew there. "And how does that feel, then? Being voided."

I considered the question honestly, because Hesper could tell when I lied—she said lies tasted like burnt onions, even secondhand. "Like taking off a coat I forgot I was wearing," I said. "Cold. And lighter than I expected. And stupid, because I liked the coat."

"And the girl?"

"Asleep. Tam's sitting with her. He's teaching her to write her letters—or teaching himself to read hers, more accurately. He copied down half her alphabet last night and cried twice."

"He cries at everything."

"He cried at a receipt once. A *receipt*, Hesper."

She smiled, but it didn't hold. Her hands wrapped around her cup, and the steam off it smelled like somebody's childhood, and she looked at me with the expression of a woman deciding whether to say a hard thing. Hesper always decided yes, eventually. It was one of the things I loved about her and one of the things I forgave her for, in roughly equal measure.

"The Advocate was seen on Lantern Row last night," she said. "Standing in the snow. Not moving. Just standing, facing the guild, for four hours. Folks thought it was a statue until it turned its head."

I nodded, because nodding was cheaper than screaming.

The Hoarfrost Advocate. I had met it once, formally, in Chapter Five of this disaster—though nothing about that meeting had chapters in it, just cold. It was a collector, in the oldest sense: not a monster but a clerk, tall and thin and precise, wearing winter the way other functionaries wore livery. It did not hate me. That was the worst part. Hatred would have been a comfort, some warmth to push against. The Advocate regarded me the way I regarded an overdue account: without malice, without mercy, with a schedule.

It had come for Veth's voice, held in escrow since the First Covenant. The winter falling on us now, unseasonal and deepening, was the collection notice. And now that I had broken my contract—now that I had stepped outside the god's shelter to pull a child inside it—I was, legally speaking, collateral.

"They'll come for me," I said.

"Yes."

"Not for Paloma. For me. The debt's mine now."

"That's how I read it." Hesper reached over and put her flour-dusted hand flat on my ledger, right over the red ink, as if she could smother it. "So here's my question, Quentin Nakamura, and think before you answer, because I'll know if you lie. Are you sorry?"

I thought about it. I thought about the auction hall, Corvane's face going smooth and furious as my bid landed, the child upstairs who had fallen asleep mid-word, her head tipping against Tam's shoulder like trust was a language she'd never forgotten how to speak. I thought about three years of prayers into silence, and how the silence had turned out not to be abandonment after all but a bargain—and how a bargain made by someone else can still be honored by you, if you're fool enough, if you're brave enough, if you're the sort of person who reads the fine print of the world and signs anyway.

"No," I said. "I'm not sorry. I'm terrified. Those are different rooms."

"I know," Hesper said. "I just wanted to hear you say it."

* * *

Leaving the Gilded Thorn took less time than I expected, because I had never owned much. A mercenary's discipline: keep your goods countable, keep your exits memorized, love nothing you can't carry. I packed in twenty minutes. Two shirts, a knife, a spare knife, the satchel where I kept the guild's oldest records—the pages I'd torn out over three sleepless nights of research, the ones naming the four oath-stones of the First Covenant, three sold, one missing. Ink, pens, a flint. The ledger itself stayed chained to the desk, because the ledger belonged to the guild, and I no longer did.

That last realization hit me at the door, oddly. Eleven years. I had walked past that door ten thousand times thinking of it as mine. Now it was just a door, and I was weather on the other side of it.

Tam was waiting in the entry hall with Paloma bundled in a coat three sizes too big—Hesper's doing; everything warm in that building was Hesper's doing—and a stack of papers he thrust at me with the particular shame-faced energy of a man handing over contraband.

"Travel documents," he said. "Forged. Well—not forged." He winced. "You know how I am. I can't lie in ink. So they're real documents, technically. I just... wrote them into existence. There's a difference. Legally there's probably not a difference. Don't look at me like that."

"Thank you, Tam."

"It says you're a widow named Marta Ostrova traveling with your niece to see her grandmother in the northern hills. I gave the niece a name too. I hope that's all right. Children should have names on paper even if—" He stopped, because Paloma was tugging his sleeve, and saying something in the old tongue, soft and serious, and whatever it was made him go pink to his ears.

"What did she say?" I asked.

"She—" Tam cleared his throat. "She said thank you. For the name. She said names are doors, in her language. She said you give someone a name and you're telling the world: this person may enter."

I looked at the child. Six years old, maybe seven, dark-eyed, solemn as a bank holiday, wearing a coat that could have housed a smaller barn. She looked back at me with the patience of someone who has already waited out an empire.

"Doors," I repeated.

She nodded once, gravely, and held up her hand.

I took it. Of course I took it. Some transactions don't need a ledger.

* * *

Corvane was waiting outside.

I should have expected it. Guildmasters do not become guildmasters by letting people leave through the front door unobserved. He stood at the top of the guild steps in his gray coat, hands clasped behind his back, watching the snow come down with the expression of a man auditing the sky and finding it irregular.

"Quentin," he said.

"Guildmaster."

"You understand," he said, without turning, "that I would have sold her for the guild's good. And you understand that I believe you've doomed us all. Both of those things are true, and I hold them both, and I will hold them until one of us is proven wrong." Now he turned. His eyes were tired in a way that no amount of power fixes. "I watched the last winter war, Nakamura. I was nine years old. I saw what the cold does when it isn't bargained with. Everything you're doing feels like courage from where you stand. From where I stand it looks like a woman tearing open a door to check if the storm is real."

"It's real," I said. "I'm going to close it properly. With the stones. With the covenant reforged—not crueler. Truer."

"That's what everyone says before the frost takes the census." He sighed, and something in him—something old, something that had once been a nine-year-old boy counting frozen neighbors—flickered and went out again. "Go, then. I won't stop you. The guild's books show you were dismissed for cause; your severance is forfeit; the child was never listed." A pause. "I falsified the catalogue entry after you won it. Struck her from the record. As far as the guild is concerned, Paloma does not exist."

I stared at him. "Why?"

"Because a collector can only collect what's written down," Corvane Dusk said, "and I am very good with ledgers, Nakamura. It's the only kindness I have left in stock, so take it and go." He turned back to the snow. "And Quentin—if you find the fourth stone—"

He didn't finish. Maybe he couldn't. Maybe the sentence was a door he wasn't ready to walk through either. I left him standing there, an old man auditing a white sky, and went down the steps into the cold with a child's hand in mine.

* * *

The city was beautiful, which felt like an insult.

Snow does that—it commits atrocities and arranges itself gorgeously over the evidence. Lantern Row wore its frost like jewelry. The gutters sang with ice. Somewhere a baker was up early, and the smell of bread fought the cold at the doorway of his shop and lost, but honorably.

Paloma walked beside me, small boots crunching, and every few steps she stopped and crouched and touched something: a fence post furred white, a dead weed standing in ice like a glass sculpture, the iron lion on a banker's doorstep. Where her fingers rested, briefly, the frost bloomed—little flowers of it, six-petaled, opening and closing like breath.

"You shouldn't do that where people can see," I murmured.

She looked up at me and said something long and liquid in the pre-kingdom tongue, and then, carefully, in the trade-speech Tam had been teaching her, word by word like coins counted out:

"The *cold* is listening. Not the people. The cold wants to know... who speaks."

"And what happens when it knows?"

She considered this with the gravity of a judge. Then she pointed north, past the rooftops, past the walls, toward the hills where the drowned chapel waited and the first oath-stone slept under water and a revenant clerk guarded it, demanding equivalent exchange.

"We knock," she said.

We walked. The snow deepened. Behind us the Gilded Thorn shrank to a lit window and a gray figure on the steps, and ahead of us the road north unspooled white and silent, and somewhere in that silence—patient, punctual, carrying its little book of debts—the Hoarfrost Advocate was waiting for me to be somewhere with no witnesses.

My wrist, where the god's mark had faded to a ghost, ached like a tooth remembering itself.

I was terrified. I want that written plainly, in my own hand, in the honest ledger I intended to start keeping the moment I could afford paper: I was afraid all the way down, the way you're afraid in a dream where the floor has gone soft. But underneath the fear, in the place where faith used to live before I learned it had been spent on someone else's bargain, there was a new thing growing. Lighter than the coat I'd taken off. Warmer than I expected.

A child's hand in mine. A road north. Four stones, three found-or-findable, one missing.

And a name on forged papers that said I might enter.

"Marta Ostrova," I said to the snow, testing it. Paloma giggled—the first time I'd heard her do it—and corrected me in her own tongue, and whatever she said, it made the frost-flowers bloom along the whole fence line as we passed, six-petaled, opening like small white bells ringing without sound.

Behind us, far back down the white road, something tall and thin and patient stepped out of the treeline and began, unhurried, to follow.
