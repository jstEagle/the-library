## 10. Road of White Bells

The world had too many words in it now.

That was the first thing Paloma understood about leaving the guild: the city behind them had been loud with words that were not hers—*coin* and *contract* and *appraised*, words like locked boxes—and the road ahead was quiet, and into that quiet her own language was waking up, one word at a time, like lamps being lit in a long dark house.

She rode in front of Quentin on the borrowed mule, wrapped in a coat that smelled of kitchen smoke and Hesper's rosemary, and she did not know how to say thank you in the loud-tongue yet. So she said it in hers.

*Ammelath.*

Quentin went very still behind her.

"What did you just say?"

Paloma pointed north, where the hills were white. Then she pointed at the mule's ears, because they were funny. Then she pointed at Quentin's heart, which was harder to point at, so she ended up patting the coat over it, twice, firmly, the way you'd settle a bird on a perch.

"That's not an answer," Quentin said. But her voice had gone strange and soft, the way it did when she thought Paloma wasn't listening. "Say it again."

*Ammelath.* Thank-you-that-is-not-a-debt. The old tongue had a whole family of words for thanks, and none of them meant owing. That was the thing nobody in the city seemed to understand: you could give a thing away all the way to the bottom of it, and no ledger anywhere needed to know.

Behind them, faintly, a bell rang. Not a church bell. A colder sound, thin as ice on a puddle.

Quentin turned the mule off the road and into the trees without a word.

* * *

They traveled north for three days, and Paloma began keeping count of the words that came back to her.

They arrived at odd hours. She would be chewing on hard bread—or watching Quentin chew on hard bread while pretending it was a fine meal, which made Paloma laugh every time, silently, inside—and suddenly a word would surface like a fish rising, whole and gleaming, and she would know what it meant the way you know your own hand.

*Sennavar:* the smell of snow before it falls. She taught it to Quentin by pointing at the sky at dusk, when the air went clean and metallic.

"Sennavar," Quentin repeated. Her accent was terrible. It was wonderful. "Snow-smell." She frowned at the horizon. "We should find shelter."

They found shelter in the hollow of an old barrow-mound, and that night Paloma dreamed of standing stones, and when she woke, frost-flowers had bloomed in the dirt beside her sleeping roll.

Not frost on things. Frost *as* things—six-petaled, deliberate, each one different, arranged around her head like a crown laid down by someone careful. Quentin crouched over them for a long time without touching.

"In the ledgers," she said finally, "there's a category we never use. Entries that write themselves. Nobody knows what triggers them. We call them *unbidden*." She looked up. Her breath hung white between them. "I think I'm looking at one."

Paloma reached out and touched one of the flowers. It did not melt. It chimed—a tiny sound, like a spoon against a cup—and the note went up through her fingers and settled somewhere behind her ribs, warm.

*I know this,* she thought. *I knew this before I knew anything else.*

She didn't have the word yet. But she could feel it coming, far off, like sennavar.

* * *

On the fifth day the bells caught up with them.

There were three of them, or maybe three was only how many she could hear—the rest were below hearing, a pressure in the teeth. They came across the snowfield at dusk: shapes like bell-towers walking, tall and jointed wrong, their clappers swinging free and silent, ringing only when they stepped, and each step rang out a name.

Paloma heard the names. That was the worst part. Each toll was somebody's name, spoken once and then taken, folded into the walker's cold body like a coin into a purse.

"Off the ridge," Quentin breathed, hauling the mule around. "Now. Quietly."

But the lead bell swung toward them anyway, and its clapper struck, and the name it rang was:

*Oxenfree-of-the-Nakamura-Line, called Quentin, debtor.*

Paloma felt Quentin flinch through the mule's back, a small full-body shiver, there and mastered.

"They can't take me," Quentin said, low, mostly to herself. "Not yet. The mark's voided but the debt's still... pending. There's procedure. Even winter has procedure." She said it like a prayer. Maybe it was one.

The bell-walker stopped at the edge of the ridge. It did not cross. Snow hissed off its shoulders in a long sigh, and Paloma understood—it was reading something written on Quentin that she couldn't see, checking the terms, finding them not yet due.

Then it turned its bell-mouth toward *her.*

And Paloma, who had been frightened since the night the white came for her country, who had run barefoot through snow while her whole world sang itself shut behind her—Paloma stood up in the stirrups, filled her lungs with sennavar-cold air, and spoke.

"*Neth ammaro veth-ka.*"

You are late. Your account is closed.

She didn't decide to say it. The words decided her; they came up from wherever the fish rose from, and they came out ringing, and where they fell, the snow bloomed—white bells of frost, dozens of them, opening along the drifts between her and the walker like a road being paved.

The bell-walker looked at the flowers for a long moment.

Then it bowed. Deeply. The way you bow to a house you have entered by mistake. And it walked away north, ringing other people's names, growing fainter, until the last toll was just weather.

Quentin stared at her.

"What," Quentin said, "did you tell it."

Paloma considered. She patted the coat over Quentin's heart again, twice, and then pointed north, and then, after some thought, pointed at herself and drew a circle in the air: everything inside this, safe.

"Right," Quentin said faintly. "Of course. Perfectly clear." She rubbed her face with both hands, and Paloma saw—because she was learning to read this person the way she was relearning to read the world—that under the fear there was something else, something that looked almost like pride, wearing fear's coat as a disguise.

"You're going to be the death of my professional composure," Quentin told her, and turned the mule north, following the road of frost-bells that Paloma's voice had opened.

For the first time, they were going where *she* led.

It felt like a door opening in a wall she'd stopped noticing was there.

* * *

That night the shelter was a drowned chapel—Quentin said the maps called it the Chapel of Saint Aldhelm-in-the-Marsh, but the water had taken the marsh and the saint together, and now only the roofline stood above the ice like the back of a sleeping animal. They made camp in the bell tower, which had somehow stayed dry, as if the tower were holding its breath.

Paloma could not sleep. The words were too awake.

She sat by the little fire and listened to them come. *Vesselar*: the warmth of a fire seen from outside a window. *Orrenneth*: the specific silence of two people choosing not to talk. *Kethrava*—

*Kethrava* arrived and she went still, because kethrava was not a small word. Kethrava was the word for what the stones were. Not oath-stone, not anchor-stone—those were the loud-tongue's flat approximations, the way you might call the sun "the bright thing." Kethrava meant: *a promise given a body so it can stand guard while you sleep.*

The First Covenant hadn't built walls. It had built friends.

"Can't sleep either?" Quentin had appeared with the tea Hesper had packed, pressed into cakes like dark coins. She handed one over. "Fair warning, it tastes like punishment. Hesper calls it 'character.'"

Paloma drank it. It tasted like punishment. She drank more.

Then, slowly, using snow and spilled tea on the tower floor, she drew: four stones, standing in a ring. Around the ring she drew small figures—people, animals, seasons. Then she erased one stone with her sleeve. Drew another being carried away, small lines beneath it meaning *sold*. Erased a third. The fourth she circled, and tapped, and looked at Quentin with a question in her face.

Quentin watched the whole performance without breathing.

"The stones," she said quietly. "You're telling me about the stones." She knelt, took Paloma's hand, and turned it palm-up, and traced in the spilled tea a shape Paloma knew better than her own heartbeat: the mark of Veth, the ledger-god, the silent one—the same mark burned into Quentin's wrist, the same mark half-glimpsed in her oldest memories of the singing country.

Except Quentin drew it differently. Or rather, she drew it *half* of it. The vertical stroke and the left curve. Nothing more.

"This is the god's mark," Quentin said. "Every mercenary sworn to Veth carries it. Every ledger in the Thorn opens with it." She paused. "Why do you keep looking at it like that?"

Paloma took Quentin's wrist gently, turned it in the firelight, and then completed the drawing in the tea on the floor: the right curve, the closing arc, the base-line that turned two broken strokes into a single sign.

A door. A door drawn shut.

Or drawn *closed from the inside*, if you knew how to read it—which she did, suddenly, fully, the knowledge arriving like the fish rising, like the lamp lighting, and with it came a word so large she nearly dropped the tea:

*Ammaveth.* The-one-who-holds-the-door.

Her mouth went dry. She looked at the mark on Quentin's wrist. Half a door. She looked at the fourth stone in her mind's eye, the one that had gone missing from every record, the one even the guild's oldest ledgers lost track of.

*Oh,* she thought. *Oh, you poor enormous quiet thing.*

"Paloma." Quentin's voice had gone careful, the way it did around dangerous inventory. "What did you just figure out? Because you've got the same face Tam gets when he's spotted a forgery, and frankly it's terrifying on someone that small."

Paloma didn't answer. She couldn't—not in the loud-tongue, and not yet in hers, because the word for *your god is the fourth stone* was a word that changed everything it touched, and she wanted to hold it a moment longer before the world rearranged itself around it.

Instead she finished her terrible tea, and lay down on her bedroll, and let herself sleep.

Where she slept, the frost-flowers bloomed—up through the tower floorboards, up the cracked stone walls, dozens of them, ringing softly whenever the wind moved, and if you listened the way the old country had listened, they were not random. They were a lullaby. The same one the standing stones used to sing.

Quentin sat watch by the fire, and Paloma drifted off to the sound of her humming it—badly, half-remembering, having no idea where she'd learned it.

* * *

They crossed into the high fells on the ninth day, and that was where the road of white bells ended—not faded, not abandoned, but *ended*, deliberately, the way a sentence ends.

Every frost-flower they had followed converged here, on a shelf of rock above a frozen lake, and grew together into a single great blossom the size of a cart wheel, and inside it, held like a fly in amber, was a sign.

Not letters. Paloma's language didn't waste promises on letters when it could use the thing itself. The sign was a feeling shaped into ice: *beyond this point, the road must be walked by the one it belongs to.*

"It means you go on alone," Quentin translated grimly, though Paloma suspected she was guessing from the shape of it rather than the words. "Doesn't it. Of course it does. Everything in this covenant is a test with a fee schedule."

But Paloma was already looking past the great blossom, down at the lake, and past the lake to where the fells climbed into a pass that glowed faintly, warmly, gold-under-white, like a coal wrapped in wool.

And rising from the pass, faint but unmistakable, carrying across miles of frozen air—

Singing.

Stone singing. A kethrava singing to itself in the dark of the long winter, patient as a promise, waiting for someone who could answer in kind.

Paloma took Quentin's hand. She pointed at the pass. Then she pressed Quentin's palm flat over her own heart, twice, firmly, settling the bird on the perch.

"Ammelath," she said. And then, because the word had finally risen, whole and gleaming, she gave Quentin the biggest one she had:

"*Amma.*"

Keeper. The-one-who-holds.

Quentin made a sound that she would deny for the rest of her life, and squeezed her hand hard, and said, hoarsely, "Go on, then. I'll be right behind you. That's not a contract. It's just true."

Paloma smiled, and stepped past the great white blossom, and walked down toward the singing alone—

—and the singing stopped.

Mid-note. Cut clean, the way a throat is cut.

In the sudden silence, from somewhere beyond the pass, came a sound she knew from the worst night of her life: the slow, deliberate crunch of footsteps that weigh too much, walking through snow that has learned to be afraid.

Something had reached the stone first.
