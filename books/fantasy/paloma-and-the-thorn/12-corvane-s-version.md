## 12. Corvane's Version

Corvane Dusk had been awake for thirty-one hours, and he intended to be awake for at least thirty-one more. Sleep was a luxury for men whose ledgers balanced.

He stood in his private office above the auction floor, hands clasped behind his back, watching the frost climb the window glass in slow, deliberate patterns—like a creditor writing out its terms in a hand too elegant to refuse. The frost had reached the third pane now. In autumn. Before solstice. He had measured it every morning with a brass rule he kept on the sill for exactly that purpose, and he had recorded the measurements in a slim black book that no one else in the Gilded Thorn knew existed.

Three years ago, the frost had taken eleven days to cross one pane.

Last winter: four.

This morning: it had crossed before he finished his tea.

"Master Dusk?" His secretary, a nervous young man named Pell whose entire function was to knock on doors and be disappointed, stood in the doorway holding a tray. "Your tea. And the appraisal report on the—the item."

"The child," Corvane said, without turning around.

"Yes, Master Dusk. The child."

Pell set the tray down with the exaggerated care of a man who had once dropped an entire inventory of bottled courage and still woke up screaming about it. Corvane waited until the door clicked shut behind him before he picked up the report.

It was thin. Quentin Nakamura's handwriting was all over the margins, which meant she had read it, which meant she disagreed with it, which meant it was probably accurate. She had a gift for disagreeing only with things that were true.

*Subject: female child, approx. seven years. Language: unclassified; partial cognates with pre-kingdom registers. No measurable trade-value in grief, memory, or time. Appraisal inconclusive. Recommend reclassification as person.*

*Recommendation appended by Q. Nakamura against protocol.*

Corvane almost smiled. *Against protocol.* Quentin wrote her objections like a woman signing her own arrest warrant, neatly, in advance, so that no one could later claim she hadn't known what it would cost her. He had watched her do it for nine years. It was one of the reasons he trusted her with the ledgers and one of the reasons he would never tell her anything that mattered.

He set the report down and poured his tea. It had gone cold, but cold tea was a discipline. A man who needed his comforts warm was a man who could be bought with a hearthfire.

On his desk, beneath a paperweight shaped like a raven mid-scream, lay the catalogue page for the solstice auction. He had written the entry himself, in his best hand:

*Lot 47. One speaker of the First Tongue, living, intact. Provenance: found. Reserve: none. This lot will not be sold twice.*

The last line was not commerce. It was a warning, and he meant it for exactly one reader.

*You cannot have her, Quentin,* he thought, sipping the cold tea. *Not because I am cruel. Because you are kind, and kindness is how the last age died.*

* * *

People assumed Corvane Dusk loved money. It was a reasonable assumption. He dressed in grey wool when black silk would do, ate plain food, kept no mistresses, gambled nothing he couldn't calculate, and yet had built the Gilded Thorn into the most feared house of impossible trade in the known world. Greed without appetite confused people. They decided it must be greed of a purer, more concentrated kind.

They were wrong, but he had stopped correcting them. Correcting people required conversation, and conversation required admitting you wanted something from them, and wanting was a debt.

What Corvane actually loved was arithmetic. Specifically, he loved the arithmetic of survival. Everything else—guilds, gods, kingdoms, children—was a column in that sum.

And the sum, this season, was coming up short.

He unlocked the bottom drawer of his desk—the one with three locks, two of which were real—and took out the oldest thing he owned. Not the deed to the guild. Not the black book of frost-measurements. Something older than both: a child's drawing, done in charcoal on the back of a debt notice, sixty-one years ago.

It showed a village. Crooked houses, a crooked well, a sun drawn with rays like a startled hedgehog. In the corner, small and careful, a signature: *Corry, age 6.*

There was no village anymore. There was no well. There was a valley called the Hollow of Bells, because when the wind moved through it, the frozen branches of the trees rang against one another like a funeral that had never quite finished. Sixty-one years ago, Corvane had stood in the snow with charcoal-stained fingers and watched the soldiers of the Winter War march past his father's door, and he had drawn the village because some instinct told him to record it while it still existed.

The instinct had been correct. That was the first lesson of his life: *record things while they exist.* It was also, in a sense, the founding principle of the guild.

He had been the only survivor from the lower village. Not because he was brave—he had hidden in a root cellar with three pigs and a dying woman who sang to him in a language he did not know, a language that made the cold feel, briefly, like something with manners. The woman had died on the fourth day. The pigs had lasted longer. Corvane had crawled out into a world gone white and silent, and he had walked eleven miles through snow to the nearest garrison, and when they asked him his name he said it clearly, twice, because he understood even then that a name was the one possession no one could take from you unless you gave it away.

He had never given his away. Remember that, he told himself, whenever the temptation came. *Remember what you are keeping it for.*

* * *

The trouble with Quentin—and he did think of it as *the trouble with Quentin*, the way other men thought of *the trouble with my knee*—was that she believed silence was absence.

She prayed to Veth every night. He knew this because he had once been young enough to check, and old habits keep better than wine. She prayed in the small shrine off the ledger room, kneeling on the cold stone with her soldier's posture, and she got nothing back, and she kept praying anyway, and she called that faith.

Corvane had knelt in that same shrine forty years ago, back when he was merely the guild's youngest appraiser and Veth's silence was merely a mystery rather than a policy. He had asked the god a single question—the question every apprentice eventually asked, drunk or desperate or both:

*Why won't you speak?*

And Veth had answered him.

Not in words. Veth does not deal in words; words are currency, and the god had already spent all of its own. It had answered him the way the guild answers a client who cannot pay: with terms. A vision, brief and vast, of a bargain struck before kingdoms, before calendars—a voice traded away, escrowed, held against a debt so old that the collateral had become the world itself. *I gave up my voice so your springs would come,* the silence said. *But debts mature. And when mine does, I will need either payment or a new covenant. And the old covenant is broken, and its stones are scattered, and its heir is a rumor.*

Corvane had walked out of that shrine knowing two things. First: the winter was not weather. It was an invoice.

Second: someone would have to write the new covenant, and it would not be the god, because the god had nothing left to sign with. It would be a person. A person with clean books and unsentimental arithmetic and the stomach to pay a price in full rather than let the whole world default.

He had spent forty years becoming that person. He had sold the oath-stones—not out of treachery, whatever Quentin's researches were whispering to her, but out of *dispersal*. You do not leave all the pieces of a weapon in one vault. You scatter them where no one will think to assemble them, and you wait, and you buy them back when the hour comes. Two were already recovered. The third sat beneath his feet. The fourth—

The fourth was the problem. The fourth had never been found, and lately Corvane had begun to suspect that there was no fourth stone at all, that the covenant had always required something else, something the old realms had possessed and the new world did not.

And then a barefoot child had knocked on the guild door speaking the language of the woman in the root cellar, and every ledger in the building had opened itself to the same blank page, and Corvane Dusk had felt, for the first time in sixty-one years, something he refused to call hope and insisted on calling *a favorable market position.*

* * *

He went down to see her at dusk, as he did every evening. Not because he was fond of her. Fondness was a leak in the hull. He went because you inspect your assets daily, and because she was the most important asset the world had produced in a thousand years, and because—though he would have denied it under torture, and had, once, professionally—the child looked at him sometimes with an expression that reminded him of the dying woman's song, and it cost him nothing to stand in a doorway for a few minutes being reminded.

She was in the kitchen. Of course she was in the kitchen. Hesper had annexed the child within a day, the way Hesper annexed everything edible or sad, and Tam had followed the way Tam followed anything that let him avoid the forgery room, and now the three of them occupied the kitchen every evening like a small warm rebellion with flour on its hands.

Corvane paused outside the door and listened, which he told himself was surveillance.

Hesper was teaching Paloma the names of spices. "This one's cinnamon. Cinn-a-mon. It tastes the way a hug feels."

"Cinn-a-mon," Paloma repeated, and then said something long and liquid in the old tongue, and Hesper laughed and said, "I'll take that as a compliment," though neither of them understood the other, which seemed, Corvane noted grimly, to be no obstacle whatsoever to their friendship.

Tam was showing her a trick with a quill—making ink bloom into shapes on wet paper. He drew a lopsided bird. Paloma took the quill and drew, in three strokes, a bird so perfect it looked taxonomically useful, and Tam put his hand over his heart as if wounded.

"She's showing me up," Tam said. "Seven years old. I've been forging documents for twenty years and I can't draw a bird that doesn't look like it owes money."

Paloma giggled. Then she looked toward the door—straight at the gap where Corvane stood, though the light was wrong for seeing—and said something soft in the old tongue. Just one word. But the word landed in the kitchen like a coin dropped in a quiet church, and Hesper went still, and Tam went stiller, and Corvane felt the word go through the door and through his grey wool coat and into the place where he kept the arithmetic.

He didn't know what it meant. But he knew tone. Tone was universal; it was the one language that needed no translation. And the tone of that word had been the tone of the dying woman's song. The tone of *stay.*

He stepped into the kitchen.

The warmth hit him like an accusation. Hesper straightened up with the expression of a cook caught feeding strays, which was accurate. Tam knocked over the ink. Paloma simply looked at him, calm as a cat, cinnamon on her chin.

"Master Dusk," Hesper said. "We were just—"

"Educating the asset. I can see that. Continue." He let his gaze rest on each of them a moment longer than comfort allowed. "A reminder, since the season makes us sentimental. The child is catalogued. Lot forty-seven. She is not a mascot, a daughter, or a cause. She is the difference between one more winter and the last winter, and every hour she spends learning spice-names is an hour the frost gains on us. Am I understood?"

"Yes, Master Dusk," said Hesper, in the tone of a woman who understood perfectly and intended to ignore it.

"Master Dusk," said Tam, in the tone of a man rehearsing for a confession he hadn't committed yet. Corvane filed that tone away. Tam was up to something. Tam was always up to something, but usually it was small—skipped shifts, watered ink. This was a different size of guilt. He would look into it after solstice.

"And you," he said to Paloma.

She looked up at him. And then she did the thing she did every evening, the thing he came down for and would have cut off his own hand before admitting: she held out her hand, palm up, the way you offer something to a bird.

In her palm lay a cinnamon stick.

"For you," Hesper translated uselessly. "I think it's for you."

Corvane Dusk, guildmaster of the Gilded Thorn, breaker of strikes, unmaker of three rival houses, stared at a stick of cinnamon as if it were a loaded crossbow.

"I don't eat sweets," he said.

Paloma pushed it an inch closer. Her expression did not change. It was the expression of a creditor, patient and absolute, and Corvane recognized it because he invented it.

He took the cinnamon stick. He did not eat it. He carried it upstairs in his closed fist like a coal, and set it on his desk next to the child's drawing of the village, and hated himself with great efficiency for ten full seconds before returning to work.

*Sentiment,* he told himself. *A leak in the hull. Patch it.*

He did not patch it. He simply stopped looking at that side of the desk.

* * *

That night he opened the black book and made the entry.

*Day 214 of the early frost. Pane three, fully crossed by dawn. The Advocate has been seen at the city gates—twice this week, asking after the guild by name. Quentin has begun researching the old covenant entries; she believes I don't know. Let her. Her conscience will make her useful and then make her predictable, and I can plan around predictable.*

*The child speaks more each day. The ledgers respond to her. Yesterday the blank pages showed frost-flowers blooming where she slept, which means the covenant recognizes her blood before it recognizes my gold, which is inconvenient and confirms everything.*

*Solstice is in nineteen days. At solstice I list her. At solstice I sell her—to myself, through three blind accounts, so the transaction is legal, witnessed, and irreversible. Ownership is the oldest magic there is. Once the deed is signed, no Advocate, no god, no mercenary with a good heart and bad judgment can touch her.*

*Then we begin the rite. A new covenant. A cruel one, yes—I will not lie in my own ledger. The old covenant asked the god for its voice. Mine will ask the heir for her fire, her tongue, her years, all of it, fed into the stones until the debt is paid in full and the winters come on schedule like honest taxes. It will hurt her. I know it will hurt her. I have priced that hurt, entered it, and accepted it, and I will carry the entry for the rest of my life, which I expect to be short, because men who balance such books rarely die comfortably.*

*But the alternative is the Hollow of Bells forever. Every village. Every well. Every child with charcoal hands. I have seen the last winter. I am the only appraiser alive who has seen it, and I am telling you the price of it, and the price is a girl, and I would rather damn myself precisely than let the world default vaguely.*

*Let them call me a monster. Monsters are what survivors look like from inside the warm houses.*

He closed the book. Below the window, the frost had finished the third pane and started the fourth, elegant and unhurried, a collector who knew the debt was good.

Nineteen days. He turned down the lamp—

—and stopped.

On his desk, beside the drawing and the cinnamon stick, lay a piece of paper that had not been there when he lit the lamp. He knew every object on that desk the way he knew his own teeth. This was new.

It was a scrap of good rag-paper, and across it, in ink still faintly wet, ran a single line in a hand he knew intimately—because he had trained it, corrected it, praised it exactly twice in twenty years.

Tam's hand. Tam's *honest* hand, the one the man could not forge with, the one that shook when it lied.

The note read: *"Master — I authenticated the deed that sold the third stone. I'm the reason you have it. I'm going north with them to undo my name. Come stop me if you can. — T."*

Corvane stood very still in the dark office, listening to the frost tick against the glass like a clerk counting coins.

So. The forger had finally written something true.

He reached for his coat.
