## 13. Tam's Ink

The trouble with being a forger who cannot lie in ink was that Tam Quill had to do all his lying out loud, which was the one thing he was terrible at.

He had discovered this affliction at eleven years old, when he first dipped a pen into his master's inkwell and copied a merchant's signature so perfectly that the merchant himself, shown the forgery an hour later, swore on his mother's grave it was his own hand. The ink had settled onto the page with a small, contented sigh—the sound good ink makes when it believes what it's doing—and young Tam had felt the truth travel up through the nib and into his wrist: *this is real now.* Whatever he wrote became true on paper. Not metaphorically. Actually. The ledger didn't record what happened; in some quiet, stubborn way, the ledger decided.

It was why the Gilded Thorn kept him. Every guild that trades in impossible things needs someone who can make a document say something impossible without the document objecting. And it was why Tam had spent nine years sleeping badly, because there was exactly one document in the guild's archive that he could not stop thinking about, and its ink had sighed the wrong way, and he had heard it, and he had said nothing.

Now he stood outside a drowned chapel three days north of the city, snow to his knees, holding a leather tube of forged travel papers against his chest like a hot coal, trying to rehearse a confession.

*Hello Quentin. Hello, child whose name I only learned yesterday from Hesper, who hit me with a ladle. Nine years ago I authenticated a deed, and I'm afraid I've come to un-sign myself.*

No. Too neat. Confessions were never neat; that was the whole problem with them.

The chapel rose out of the whiteness ahead—half-sunk in the frozen shallows of a lake that had once, according to every map worth forging, been dry land. Bells hung in its leaning tower, white with frost, silent as everything else these days. Somewhere inside or beneath it was the first oath-stone, and somewhere inside or beneath it also was Quentin Nakamura, mercenary of the Thorn, keeper of ledgers, woman who could kill you six ways with a fountain pen, and currently the only person in the world Tam trusted more than he feared.

Also present: a child who spoke the language before kingdoms, and who had, by all reports, made frost-flowers bloom where she slept.

Tam took a breath that hurt going in, and waded toward the door.

* * *

Quentin met him at the threshold with a knife held casually, the way other people hold teacups.

"Tam." Her eyes went over him the way they went over an inventory: boots, frostbitten ears, leather tube, no weapon, guilt visible from orbit. "You're a long way from your desk."

"I brought papers," he said. "Travel documents. For you and—" He fumbled the tube open with fingers that wouldn't behave. "For Paloma. Passes for the northern tolls, a letter of safe conduct under the Ashford Road Compact, and a bill of manumission backdated past Corvane's auction filing, because technically the sale was never properly notarized, and I have decided, retroactively and permanently, that it wasn't."

Quentin took the papers. She read fast—he'd seen her read contracts faster than most people read insults—and her eyebrows did the thing where one stayed professional and the other admitted surprise.

"These are flawless."

"They're true," Tam said, and then, because the words had started and once his mouth started it was worse than his pen, "that's the whole problem with me, actually. Everything I write comes true on paper. So I can't forge anything false. I can only find the smallest true thing in a situation and write it so large it covers everything else. The sale wasn't notarized. That's true. So the bill of manumission is true. It just required me to be very precise about which truths I fed it." He swallowed. "Paloma is free on paper now. Legally, cosmically, in every register my ink recognizes. Free."

Behind him, a small voice said something in the old tongue—soft, questioning.

"Free," Quentin repeated, translating it into plain words for both of them, and Tam watched her face do something complicated and private before it went back to being a mercenary's face. "Thank you, Tam. This was dangerous. Corvane will know your hand."

"He'll know worse than my hand." Tam looked down at the snow. Here it came, then, whether he'd rehearsed it or not. "Can we sit? It's—it's a sitting confession. Standing confessions are for theft and adultery. Mine has architecture."

* * *

They sat inside the chapel on a fallen beam near the drowned nave, where the water had frozen into a floor of green glass and Paloma could crouch at the edge and watch fish move under it, slow and dreaming, their shadows drifting across the ice like handwriting.

Tam held the confession in his chest the way he held bad news in a ledger: sealed, heavy, waiting for the moment the seal had to break.

"You know what my gift is," he began. "You've seen it. Ink tells truth through me. What you don't know is how the guild uses it. You keep the ledgers, Quentin—you see the entries. But you don't see the deeds. The transfers. The bills of sale that move things nobody's supposed to be able to own."

"I know Corvane moves things," Quentin said carefully. "That's the trade."

"There's a difference between moving a thing and making a thing movable." Tam turned his hands over. Ink stains in every crease, nine years deep, maybe deeper—they went all the way back to the first well. "A bottle of grief moves because grief can be owned; it's already a possession, just misfiled. Borrowed years move because years are currency. But some things aren't property. Some things are promises. And a promise can't be sold unless somebody first writes it down as though it can—and here's the trick, here's the whole filthy trick—if a man with my gift writes it, the paper believes him. The world reads the paper. And then the promise *is* property, and it always was, and everyone remembers it that way."

Paloma had stopped watching the fish. She was looking at him now with those dark, patient eyes, and Tam found it easier to confess to her than to Quentin, which was strange, but confessions are strange.

"The oath-stones," he said. "The First Covenant. Four stones anchoring four realms, holding winter and spring in balance—that's the old story Hesper tells better than me. What the story doesn't say is that stones don't get sold by thieves in the night. They get sold by guildmasters with paperwork. Three times, over two hundred years, a guildmaster of the Gilded Thorn decided an oath was inventory. And each time, the deed needed authentication. A true signature. A hand the covenant itself would believe."

Quentin had gone very still. "Three deeds," she said. "Three authentications."

"Two before my time. Master Aldous Fenwick signed the second one—I found his mark in the deep archive, the night I went looking, after Hesper hit me with the ladle and told me about Paloma and the Advocate and the god's voice. But the third stone." His voice cracked and he let it. "The third stone, Quentin. Sold fourteen years ago. To a buyer whose name was written as a cipher, because even then somebody knew to be ashamed. And the deed came across my desk with Guildmaster Dusk's seal and my master's instructions, and I read it, and I felt the ink sigh."

"What did it say?" Quentin asked quietly. "The deed. What did your ink think was true?"

"That an oath-stone of the First Covenant, being guild property per prior instruments, was transferred to the named buyer for consideration received, all liens discharged, covenant obligations transferring with title." He recited it the way you recite a scar. "Covenant obligations transferring with title. Do you understand what that means? Whoever bought the third stone bought the *debt attached to it*. Bought a piece of the bargain Veth made. And my ink confirmed it was true, and because it was true, the covenant bent around it. Maybe that's why the bargain is failing. Maybe it isn't just that Veth traded its voice. Maybe pieces of the promise itself got sold out from under the god, and the whole agreement has been coming apart ever since, one authenticated line at a time."

Silence. The bells overhead ticked as ice shifted in them—a sound like a clock deciding whether to start again.

"Fourteen years ago," Quentin said slowly. "I was already at the guild. I was already keeping ledgers."

"You weren't on the deed floor. Nobody honest is allowed on the deed floor."

"Whose cipher was it?" Quentin asked. "On the third stone. The buyer."

Tam shook his head. "I've spent nine nights trying to crack it and I can't—not fully. But I'll tell you what I can read, because my ink wrote the cipher's frame and the frame remembers. The buyer paid in years. Decades of them. Someone gave up a huge portion of their life to own a piece of the old covenant." He laughed, and it came out awful. "Who does that? Who spends decades of life on a rock?"

"A collector," Quentin said. "Or someone hiding it from a worse buyer." She rubbed her eyes. "The Market of Borrowed Years. If the second stone's in a moneylender's vault there, maybe the third—"

"Maybe. I don't know yet. That's why I'm here instead of dead in a ditch, which was the other option." Tam straightened up on the beam, and now came the part he'd rehearsed forty times on the road, and got wrong forty times, and finally understood couldn't be rehearsed at all. "Here's my confession, and then I'll take whatever sentence you give me. I authenticated that deed. My hand. My gift. I made an oath into property, and the property into a wound, and I went back to my desk and sharpened my pens and said nothing for fourteen years because Corvane pays me, and the work was warm, and shame is a room you can furnish if you stay busy enough." His eyes stung. He let them. "I can't unsign it. Believe me, I've tried—I've sat with that deed and every pen I own and the ink won't take a retraction, because retraction isn't true; you can't un-ring a bell, only answer it. But I can follow the debt to its owner. I can put my gift against the chain of title until the whole rotten instrument comes apart. And when we find whoever holds the third stone, I want to be the one standing there saying, on behalf of the guild, on behalf of my own cursed honest hand: this was never yours. We sold you something we had no right to sell, and the sale is void, and I am the proof."

Paloma moved. Before anyone could stop her, the child crossed the frozen nave, took Tam's ink-stained right hand in both of hers, and turned it palm-up, studying it. Then she said a word in the old tongue—short, round, certain—and pressed his stained fingers closed around it.

"What did she say?" Tam whispered.

"She does that," Quentin said. "She names things." A pause, and then Quentin translated, and her voice was not quite steady. "She called you *maker-of-true-marks.* And then—" he saw Quentin listening to something in the girl's next words, weighing them, "—she said the second word means *mender.* Maker-of-true-marks who mends. That's you now. That's what she says you are."

Tam looked down at his closed fist, at the ink stains that went all the way back to the first well, and felt something in his chest unlock that had been locked so long he'd mistaken it for his ribcage.

"Well," he managed. "I've been called worse by shorter people."

* * *

They worked through the afternoon, because grief and purpose both go down easier with labor.

Tam spread his papers on a pew that jutted from the green ice like the rib of a drowned giant and showed Quentin what he'd built. The travel documents first—she tested each one the way she tested everything, reading for seams, finding none, scowling as though flawless work were a personal affront.

"The Ashford compact pass gets you through the northern tolls," Tam explained. "The toll-keepers there are strict but literal; show them true paper and they wave you through, show them false paper and they hang you, show them *ambiguous* paper and they debate philosophy for a week while you freeze. Yours are true. Paloma's—" he hesitated, "—Paloma's was harder. There's no registry she belongs to. No kingdom claims her; she predates kingdoms. So I couldn't write 'subject of' anything. I had to find a true category."

"And?"

"I wrote her in as *property of herself.* Full ownership, self-held, no liens, provenance: original." He shrugged, embarrassed. "My ink loved it. Sighed like a cat in the sun. Apparently self-ownership is the oldest and truest kind. The compact's legal framework barely supports it, but the paper believes it, and the toll-keepers will believe the paper."

Quentin stared at him for a long moment. "You made a child legally impossible," she said, "in order to make her free."

"I made her *accurately impossible,*" Tam corrected. "There's a difference. It's the difference I'm for."

Then the harder work. He pulled out his working copies of the third deed—all fourteen years of memory poured back into fresh ink—and walked Quentin through the chain of title the way a surgeon walks an apprentice through a body. Seal here. Authentication there. Ciphered buyer. Consideration paid in years, routed through a broker, and the broker's mark—

He stopped.

"What?" Quentin said.

"The broker's mark." Tam leaned close, heart starting to hammer. "I copied it a hundred times over the years for practice and never thought about it. It's a stylized hourglass with a coin balanced in the neck. That's a market sigil, Quentin. That's a *booth sign.* There are maybe six brokers in the world who use an hourglass-and-coin, and five of them operate out of one place."

"The Market of Borrowed Years," Quentin breathed.

"The same. Whoever bought the third stone bought it through the black market in life-years—which means the stone's still within reach of that market's economy. Debts there don't die; they circulate. If we can trace the consideration forward instead of backward—follow the years instead of the buyer—"

"—we find the stone," Quentin finished. She was already pulling out her own notes, the research she'd done in the ledgers' oldest entries, and laying them beside his deed copies, and Tam watched two kinds of paper truth meet on a pew in a drowned chapel and fit together with a click he felt in his teeth.

From the edge of the ice, Paloma spoke. Neither of them understood the words—but they both understood the tone, because it was the tone of a child telling adults something obvious. She was pointing north, past the broken tower, past the white bells, to where the sky had gone the color of skimmed milk.

And in the milk-white distance, faint but unmistakable, something was glowing. Not lightning. Not dawn. A vertical seam of pale gold light, far away, hanging above the horizon like a crack in the day itself.

"My ink wants to write about that," Tam said faintly.

"What's it want to say?"

"It's not sure yet." He uncapped his pen, held it over a blank page, and waited—the old habit, letting truth arrive before chasing it. The nib trembled. Then the ink sighed, soft and certain, and his hand moved, and he looked down at what he'd written:

*The market is awake.*

Quentin read it twice. "Then we'd better be too," she said, and began packing the satchel with the first recovered oath-stone, and Tam—maker-of-true-marks, mender, forger of accurate impossibilities—folded his deeds away and followed a mercenary and a child north into the whitening world, toward the one signature he intended to spend the rest of his life undoing.
