## 14. The Market of Borrowed Years

The city of Vell had no name on any map worth trusting, which was how it liked it. Maps were a kind of promise, and Vell kept none. It sat in a fold of the mountains like a coin slipped between mattress and wall—found only by people who already knew to look, and lost again the moment they stopped.

We came down into it at dusk, three travelers and one satchel that clinked when I walked, which in Vell was roughly equivalent to ringing a dinner bell.

"Smaller than I imagined," Tam said, squinting at the terraced rooftops glowing with lamplight below us.

"Everything honest is," I said. "That's why they built this place dishonest."

Paloma rode on my shoulders with her heels drumming my collarbone in a rhythm she'd invented somewhere on the Road of White Bells and refused to explain. She'd been doing it for two days. I'd decided it meant *hurry up* rather than *your neck is bony*, because I preferred that interpretation and I have always been a generous appraiser of my own qualities.

Below us, Vell burned gold against the snow. Somewhere in that tangle of crooked streets and stairways was a moneylender named Ossory Vane who kept an oath-stone in his vault the way other men keep a lucky coin—and charged interest on it.

"Remind me," Tam said, "of the plan."

"The plan is we go in, I negotiate, you stand there looking harmless."

"I am harmless."

"You're a forger who can't lie in ink. You're the most harmless man alive. That's why you're here." I adjusted Paloma's weight. "And if negotiation fails, we improvise."

"That's not a plan, that's a mood."

"In Vell, moods are legal tender."

He laughed despite himself, and we went down into the light.

* * *

Vell smelled the way all black markets smell: hot oil, wet wool, and the particular metallic sweetness of magic being spent too fast. The main street—called the Ledger, because even criminals are sentimental about their metaphors—was lined with stalls selling things that should not have been for sale. Bottled arguments. A cage of secondhand courage, chirping like crickets. A woman selling yesterday, decanted into little clay cups, guaranteed fresh.

Paloma stared at everything with her mouth open, which I found reassuring. A child who could still be astonished by commerce was a child the world hadn't finished ruining.

A vendor pressed a cup into her hands before I could stop him. "On the house, little miss. Yesterday last spring. Best vintage in years."

She looked up at me for permission. I nodded once. She drank, and her whole face changed—the particular softening of someone tasting something warm from very far away.

"What did you see?" Tam asked gently.

Her answer came slow, in the broken common tongue she was assembling word by word, like a mason building a wall out of river stones. "Grass. And... someone singing. Not words. Just glad."

I paid the vendor anyway, because nothing in Vell was free and I didn't want the debt accruing interest in directions I couldn't see. He took the coin and gave me a look of professional respect. In Vell, paying for a gift marked you as either rich or careful, and either way, people left you alone.

Ossory Vane's establishment stood at the top of the Ledger, behind a door of black iron worked with scales—not the weighing kind, but the fish kind, which told you everything about the man's sense of humor. Inside was a parlor of red velvet and low lamps, and Vane himself: small, round, immaculate, with the eyes of a man who had counted everything he'd ever seen and found most of it wanting.

"Mistress Nakamura," he said, before I'd said a word. "Or should I say, formerly of the Gilded Thorn? Word travels. Word always travels; it's the only thing in Vell that travels free."

"Then you know why I'm here."

"The stone." He steepled his fingers. "Yes. Sit. Drink. We'll discuss terms like civilized people, which is to say, like thieves with better handwriting."

We sat. Tam poured tea with the nervous precision of a man defusing something. Paloma curled against my side and pretended to sleep, which fooled no one, least of all Vane, whose eyes flicked to her twice with the expression of a man adding figures.

"The stone came to me forty years ago," Vane said. "From a guild courier with more debt than sense. Collateral on a loan he never repaid. I've held it since. It hums, you know. At night, when the vault is quiet, it hums like a kettle deciding whether to sing. My wife hated it. My wife left. I have sometimes wondered if those two facts are related."

"It anchors a covenant," I said. "It belongs in a circle with its brothers."

"Everything belongs somewhere, mistress. That is what collateral *is*. The question is what it will cost to move it." He opened a ledger—I felt Tam stiffen beside me at the sight, poor man, ledgers were his sacrament—and turned it around. "Not coin. Coin I have. Not favors. Favors rot. Years, mistress Nakamura. The stone was bought with time, and time it shall cost. One year per decade of its exile. Four years of life, freely given, willingly paid. I have a device for the extraction. It's quite painless. Most clients say so afterward, and most clients are in no condition to lie."

Four years. I did the arithmetic the way I do all arithmetic, coldly and instantly, and then I did it again the way I do the arithmetic that matters, which is to say while thinking about who would be waiting for me at the end of it.

Four years off my life. I was thirty-one. Mercenaries don't usually see fifty anyway; the profession has opinions about longevity. Four years was cheap.

But Vane watched me calculate, and smiled, and I understood one heartbeat too late.

"For you," he said softly, "the price is different. You carry stolen goods, mistress. Three stones in that satchel, if rumor serves, and rumor in Vell is never wrong, merely early. For a thief, the rate doubles. Eight years. And there is the matter of the child."

Paloma's breathing changed against my side.

"The child is not for sale," I said.

"Everyone is for sale in Vell. It's our founding principle. But set her aside—set her aside, I insist, I'm a family man in the sense that I had a family once. Eight years, freely given. Or—" he spread his hands, a gesture of terrible reasonableness— "you may leave, and I will sell the stone to the next interested party. And I will tell you, as a courtesy, that the next interested party has white eyes and arrives with frost, and does not negotiate so much as *foreclose*."

The Hoarfrost Advocate. Of course. The collection notice had found the collateral before I had.

Tam's teacup rattled against its saucer. Paloma's hand found mine under the table and gripped it, small and fierce, and I thought about chapter and verse, contract and clause, eight years and a marked woman and a child who needed me alive and *present*, not eight years thinner—

And that was when the door banged open, letting in a gust of snow and the smell of browned butter.

* * *

Hesper Quill filled doorways the way weather fills valleys: completely, and without apology. She stood in Vane's parlor steaming slightly, travel-cloaked, flour still somehow dusting one sleeve, and surveyed the room with the expression of a cook inspecting a kitchen she intended to take over.

"Hesper?" Tam's voice cracked in the middle, which for Tam was practically a aria.

"You left," she said, "a note. On the *larder door*. Written in *gravy*. 'Gone north, back soon.' Back soon! It's been six weeks! Six weeks of Corvane sulking, the solstice auction looming, and me feeding a guild full of vultures with nobody to appreciate the difference between thyme and regret!" She advanced on the table. "So I closed the kitchen, I hired my niece, and I followed the trail of gossip, which led to Vell, which led to a fishmonger who mentioned a mercenary woman asking after Vane, and here we are. Hello, poppet." This last to Paloma, who had launched herself across the floor and attached to Hesper's legs like a barnacle with opinions.

"You can't be here," I said. "Vell isn't safe for—"

"Neither is your cooking, and yet you survive." She unclasped her cloak, sat down in the fourth chair as though it had been reserved, and looked at Vane. "Now. You're the moneylender. Good. I've dealt with your kind before. What's the price of the rock?"

"Madam—"

"Quill. Hesper Quill. Cook, formerly of the Gilded Thorn, currently of nowhere in particular and everywhere I'm needed. The price."

Vane, to his credit, recognized quality when it sat down across from him. "Years of life. Eight, given the circumstances of acquisition."

"And the extraction hurts?"

"Painless, I'm told."

"They always say that." She rolled up her sleeve, revealing forearms like ship's cable. "Fine. Do it."

"No," I said.

"Yes," said Hesper.

"*No.*" I heard my own voice come out flat and hard, the guild voice, the appraisal voice, and I hated it. "Hesper. Eight years. You're older than me. Those aren't spare years, they're—"

"They're *mine*, girl, that's the point of them. That's the entire point of anything you own—you get to spend it." She leaned forward, and her voice dropped into the register she used when bread was burning or children were crying, the register that brooked no argument because argument required oxygen and she'd consumed all of it. "You want to lecture me about debts? Let's do sums. Who fed that child when the guild had her locked in a storeroom? Me. Who taught Tam to read faces so he'd stop signing things blind? Me. Who kept a pot of soup on for three years because a certain mercenary looked thin and lonely and too proud to say so? *Me.* You think I don't know what a debt is? I've been keeping your accounts since before you knew you had any. This isn't charity. This is *collection*."

The parlor was very quiet. Somewhere below, the market murmured. Paloma had gone still against Hesper's side, watching us both with the grave attention of a child who understands that adults are weather and must simply be survived.

"Eight years," I said finally, quieter. "You'd be giving up eight years for a rock."

"I'm not giving up eight years for a rock." Hesper reached over and tapped the satchel, and the stones inside hummed—one note, then another, then a third, harmonizing like kettles agreeing on supper. "I'm giving up eight years so that poppet gets her spring. So Tam stops flinching at his own signature. So you stop praying to a god that can't answer and start hearing one that can. That's not a rock, Quentin Nakamura. That's the down payment on everyone getting to grow old together. And I intend to be insufferable at every single one of your birthdays, so you'd best make sure I live long enough."

Vane cleared his throat. "This is very touching. I'll add it to the invoice."

"Do," said Hesper, "and I'll salt your tea."

* * *

The extraction happened in the back room, which was less a chamber of horrors than a very tidy office with a brass apparatus like a cross between a loom and an orrery. Vane was, I'll grant him, professional. He explained every step. He offered tea. He let Tam hold Hesper's hand, and let Paloma sit on my lap where she could see, because Hesper insisted the child not be sheltered from it—"she's going to spend her whole life learning what things cost; she may as well start with something worth buying."

Hesper sat in the chair with her sleeve rolled up and her chin high, like a woman waiting for a haircut she'd already decided to enjoy.

The machine sang—a low chord, almost the same note the stones made—and light came out of Hesper's wrist. Not blood-light. Warmer. Hearth-light, harvest-light, the color of late afternoon through a kitchen window. It coiled up into the brass arms of the device and gathered there, eight bright threads, each one flickering with something I couldn't see but could feel: a wedding feast, maybe, or a grandchild not yet born, or just long mornings with bread rising and nowhere to be.

Paloma made a sound. Small. Then she stood up out of my lap, walked to the machine, and put her hand flat against the brass.

The threads stilled. Bowed, almost, the way wheat bows to wind.

And Paloma spoke—in the old tongue, the pre-kingdom language, four syllables soft and clear—and though I didn't know the words, I knew the shape of them, because I'd been raised on ledgers and I know a receipt when I hear one.

Vane went pale. "What did she say?"

"She said," Tam translated, haltingly, his scholar's ear catching what mine couldn't, "*paid in full, witnessed and sealed.*" His voice shook. "Mistress Quill—those aren't just years now. They're *sanctified*. Whatever she just did, the covenant itself countersigned it. Nothing can take them back. Nothing can cheat you of them. When those years would have ended, you'll simply... continue. Guaranteed. By the First Covenant."

Hesper blinked. Then, being Hesper: "Well. It's about time somebody in this outfit honored a warranty."

Even Vane laughed. Even Vane.

The stone came out of the vault wrapped in velvet, humming its kettle-hum, and when Hesper took it in both hands it quieted against her skin like a cat recognizing the person who feeds it. She tucked it into the satchel beside its brothers, and the three notes resolved into something almost like a chord.

Almost. There was a gap in it still. A rest where a fourth voice should be.

* * *

We ate that night in a tavern called the Second Chance, which served stew of ambiguous provenance and wine of no provenance whatsoever, and Hesper held court, and Tam cried into his cup twice and denied it both times, and Paloma fell asleep mid-sentence against Hesper's arm with her hand still curled around a heel of bread.

I sat across from them and did the arithmetic again, the kind that matters.

Three stones. One missing. A guildmaster building a crueler covenant. A collector with white eyes closing the distance. And a cook who had just bought us all a future with eight years of her own, and would never once, I knew with absolute certainty, mention it again unless it was to complain about the interest.

"Hesper," I said, when the others were settling into rooms upstairs. "Why? Truly. You had a life. You walked away from it."

She was wiping down the table out of pure habit, though it wasn't hers. She considered the question the way she considered seasoning: seriously, and without hurry.

"When I was young," she said, "my mother told me food is love made edible. Took me thirty years to understand she meant it as instructions, not philosophy." She wrung out the cloth. "You three are the first thing I ever cooked for that needed more than soup. So I'm finishing the dish. That's all. Now go to bed, Nakamura, you look like week-old pastry."

I went. But at the stairs I paused, because Paloma had stirred in her sleep, and was talking—dream-talking, in the old tongue, her small voice rising and falling like something being sung from very far away.

And beneath her words, faint but unmistakable, the three stones in the satchel by the door began to hum along.

They weren't humming at her.

They were humming *toward* something. Northward. Like dogs pointing.

Like kettles, at last, beginning to sing.
