## 15. What Veth Cannot Say

Paloma had decided that she did not like sleeping in strange beds, and this was her third one in as many weeks.

The first had been the guild's cot, which smelled of cedar oil and someone else's dreams. The second had been the floor of a waystation inn, where she'd slept curled against Quentin's back because the bed itself was suspicious — too soft, too wide, a bed that expected things of you. This third one was in the loft above a drover's barn on the north road, under a roof that ticked and settled like an old man counting coins, with wool blankets that smelled of sheep and, faintly, of Hesper's kitchen, because Hesper had packed them a bundle of travel bread and had apparently seasoned the wrapping cloth itself with *home*, the memory of it, warm and buttered.

Tam had laughed at that. "She flavored the *cloth*."

"She flavors everything," Quentin had said, in that flat voice Paloma had learned meant she was pleased but would rather be buried alive than admit it.

Paloma had fallen asleep thinking about home, which was dangerous, because home was a country she could not find on any map Tam owned, and Tam owned a great many maps, including two that were lies he'd drawn himself for practice.

So it was perhaps not surprising that her dream began with a door.

It stood alone in a field of white — no wall around it, no house, just a door standing upright in the snow like a tooth left behind by some enormous smiling thing. It was made of dark wood, and it was very old, and it had a knocker shaped like a bell with its tongue removed.

Paloma knew doors. The Gilded Thorn had forty-one of them, and she had counted, because counting kept her calm when the world spoke too fast. Doors were how you got inside places, and inside was where people kept the warm things: ovens, ledgers, other children, dogs.

This door, though. This door she knew the way you know your own name before anyone has told it to you.

She walked to it through snow that didn't bite. That was the first wrongness — snow here was polite, snow here only wanted to hold her up. She reached for the knocker, and the bell-shaped thing trembled under her hand like something that had been waiting a long time to be touched, and she thought: *you can't ring, can you? Someone took your tongue too.*

She pushed the door open herself.

* * *

Inside there was no room. Inside there was winter the size of everything.

Not the cruel winter — not the one that had chased her barefoot down a mountain road, the one with teeth. This was winter as it must have been before anyone taught it to be hungry: vast, slow, silver, patient. A plain of white light going out forever in every direction, and above it a sky like the inside of a shell, pearlescent, humming with a sound she could not hear but could *feel*, the way you feel a drum through a floor.

And in the middle of it all stood the shape.

Paloma stopped. Her body wanted to run; the old animal part of her, the part that had survived the white road, screamed at her legs. But she was also a child of the First Covenant, even if nobody had told her so yet in words, and children of the Covenant are taught — in their bones, in their milk-teeth, in the language they dream in — that you do not run from a god. You bow. Or you laugh. Depending on the god.

This one, she somehow knew, you could do either.

The shape was enormous and difficult to look at directly, the way the sun is difficult, or the truth. It had the suggestion of shoulders like hills. It had antlers, or branches, or rivers seen from very high up — branching things, silver-dark, spreading wide enough to hold a sky. Its hide was the color of deep water under ice. And it had a mouth — she was sure it had a mouth, though when she looked straight at it the mouth seemed to be everywhere and nowhere — and the mouth was moving.

Moving, moving, moving. Saying something. Saying something with its whole self, with its shoulders and its branch-antlers and the pearl-shell sky, saying something so large that sound could not carry it, the way a river cannot fit in a cup.

No sound came out. Of course no sound came out.

*You're Veth,* Paloma said, and her voice in the dream was her own voice but older, or truer, or simply unafraid. She said it in the old tongue, the language that lived in her mouth without permission, the language the scholars' books called dead while it went on living in her like a heartbeat. *You're the ledger-god. You're the one Quentin prays to every night even though you never answer.*

The vast shape went still.

Then it leaned down.

Down and down and down, the way a mountain would lean if a mountain were lonely, until its face — and yes, there was a face, a face like weather, like the first frost making pictures on a window — hung above her, close enough that she could see that its eyes were not eyes but openings, apertures, little round windows into somewhere warmer.

And it spoke at her, silently, with everything it had.

* * *

Here is what Paloma understood, standing in the shell-colored winter, looking up into a god's silent shouting face:

She could almost hear it.

Not with her ears. With something behind her ears, something older than ears, some door in her that everyone else's had bricked shut generations ago. The words arrived the way warmth arrives through a window — not the thing itself, but the undeniable evidence of it.

*I never left,* said the silence. *I never abandoned anyone. I am still here. I have been here every single night, kneeling beside every prayer I could not answer.*

Paloma's throat hurt. "Then why won't you talk?" she asked, and her voice cracked in the middle like green wood, because she was seven-going-on-eight and gods are frightening even when they love you. "Quentin talks to you every night. Three years. She thinks you don't listen. She thinks—" She stopped, because saying it out loud made it real: *she thinks her god gave up on her.* "She stands at her window and she says goodnight to you and you say nothing."

The god flinched.

A god, flinching. The whole silver plain shuddered like the surface of a struck lake, and somewhere far away in the dream-snow, frost-flowers bloomed and died and bloomed again, quick as heartbeats.

*I cannot,* the silence said, and now Paloma could feel the shape of the grief in it, grief with edges, grief like a ledger entry that will not balance no matter how many times you recopy the numbers. *I sold my voice, little speaker. No — worse. I put it in escrow. Do you know what escrow is?*

"I live with accountants," Paloma said. "I know what escrow is."

Something happened in the god's face that would have been a smile, on anything smaller. The frost-flowers bloomed pink for a moment, all of them at once, like a field blushing.

*Then you understand. I did not give my voice away. I *held* it hostage against myself. There is a difference, and the difference is everything, and no one has read the terms in a thousand years except the ones who profited from misreading them.*

The god sank down — folded, the way a heron folds, all those impossible angles coming to rest — until it crouched before her, and its window-eyes were level with hers, and Paloma saw that they were wet. Gods should not be able to cry, she thought. Somebody should write a rule.

*Listen,* said Veth, who could not speak, and made her listen anyway. *Before kingdoms, there was the Covenant, and the Covenant was a song with four verses, and each verse was sung into a stone, and the stones held the seasons in their places like pegs holding a tapestry taut. Spring knew where to go because the stones told it. Winter knew when to leave.*

"But winter isn't leaving," Paloma whispered. "Winter is eating everything. It followed me. It ate my whole—"

*Because I broke the song.*

The words landed in her chest like stones dropped into still water.

*A war came. The last winter war — the one your books call the Long Dark, the one the guildmasters' grandfathers watched from behind locked doors. Winter did not come that time to pass through. It came to stay. It came with creditors. It came with contracts. And I looked at the world I loved, all of it, every singing valley, and I understood that spring would lose.*

The god raised one vast limb — a branch, a river, an arm — and gestured at the endless white plain around them, and Paloma understood suddenly that this place was not a dream-country. This place was *inside*. This was where Veth lived now, had always lived since the bargain: in the space between prayers, in the pause after goodnight, in the held breath of three years of silence.

*So I made a trade. The worst trade ever made by anyone who loved what they traded. I took my voice — the voice that sings the seasons into their places — and I sealed it away as payment, as surety, as the collateral against winter's claim. As long as my voice stayed forfeit, winter could advance but never finish falling. A debt in dispute cannot be collected. So I disputed it. For a thousand years I disputed it, little one. Every spring you have ever seen — every thaw, every green shoot, every foolish bird singing in March — that was me, arguing. That was my silence, holding the line.*

Paloma was crying. She noticed distantly, the way you notice rain starting. "But it's not working anymore."

*No,* said Veth, gently, the way you confirm a wound. *The Advocate has found new clerks. New readings. Old debts wake up hungry, and mine has woken. The dispute is failing. When it fails entirely, the collection comes due, and the collection is—*

"An endless winter," Paloma said. "Corvane wants to make a new covenant. A crueler one. He wants to use me to hold it."

The god went very still, the way the air goes still before snow.

*Yes. He is wrong, and he is right, which is the most dangerous shape a man can be. A covenant can hold back winter. But a covenant needs four things, and he means to force the fourth instead of receiving it freely, and a stolen verse does not sing. It only screams. And the world would survive on screaming, for a while. The world is good at surviving. But nothing would ever be green again out of joy. Only out of fear.*

Paloma wiped her nose on her sleeve, because even in dreams before gods, you are who you are. "The fourth thing," she said. "Quentin has three stones. In her satchel. She carries them like they're eggs." She hesitated, then asked the question that had been sitting in her chest since the drowned chapel, since she'd watched Quentin come out of that grey water with her eyes emptied of her happiest day and not know why she felt lighter and sadder at once. "What's the fourth thing?"

The god looked at her for a long time.

Window-eyes, full of somewhere warmer.

*That,* said Veth, *is the one thing I cannot tell you. Not will not. Cannot. My voice is forfeit; my knowledge of the terms died with my ability to speak them. I can show you only what fits through silence: feelings, shapes, the pressure of a truth against the inside of a locked box.* The great head bowed. *But you — you are the heir of the ones who wrote the terms. The last speaker. The words live in you unbought and unspent. Somewhere in your blood is the sentence that finishes the covenant, and I cannot read it for you, and neither can Corvane, and thank every mercy that he cannot.*

"So we're both mute," Paloma said. "You can't say it and I don't know it yet."

*We are both mute,* agreed the god, with unmistakable, heartbreaking humor, *which makes us excellent company for each other.*

Paloma laughed — a wet, hiccupping, furious laugh, the kind children produce when awe and grief arrive in the same delivery. And then, because she was who she was, she did the thing that no supplicant in a thousand years had done.

She stepped forward and put her arms around the god.

Around whatever part of the god was nearest — a limb like a frozen river, cold and enormous and trembling slightly, like a dog that has been kicked by everyone it ever trusted and cannot believe a hand has come down gently. She pressed her cheek against the ice-deep hide and felt, rather than heard, the hum of the unheard song underneath, the voice in escrow, still there, still whole, still singing to itself in the dark of its own silence like a child humming in a locked room.

"You never left," she said into the cold. "You've been here the whole time. Saying goodnight back. She couldn't hear you."

*Every night,* the silence said. *Tell her — no. You cannot tell her. She would not believe a child's dream, and I would not blame her; belief is expensive in this guild-ridden age. But when the moment comes — and it is coming soon, little speaker, sooner than any of you want — when the moment comes, remember what I showed you tonight. Remember that the fourth thing must be given, never taken. Remember that I did not abandon her. Remember that the silence was never empty.*

"It was full," Paloma said. "It was full the whole time."

*It was full,* said Veth, *of everything I could not say.*

The white plain was beginning to thin around the edges, going soft and grey and wool-smelling, the barn loft pulling her back up through the layers of sleep. The god rose, unfolding, becoming weather again, becoming horizon.

Wait, Paloma almost said. Don't go. I just found you.

But the god bent down one last time, and where its mouth would be, it pressed something against her forehead — not a kiss exactly, more like a seal, like wax on a letter, like the period at the end of a sentence — and into that touch it poured one final silent word, and this one she heard clearly, perfectly, the way you hear your own name:

*Spring,* it said. *I have been saving it for you. All of you. Go and collect it.*

* * *

She woke in the sheep-smelling dark with frost-flowers blooming on the blanket over her chest, small white blossoms opening in neat rows like lines of writing, and she lay still and let herself cry for a while — quietly, the way Quentin cried, she'd noticed, when she thought no one could see, which was one of approximately ten thousand things Paloma had learned by watching her.

Then she climbed down the loft ladder, careful on the fourth rung that wobbled, and crossed the cold floor of the drover's barn, and found Quentin asleep sitting up against a hay bale with her sword across her knees and her ledger open on her lap, because Quentin Nakamura slept the way she did everything else: as a compromise between duty and exhaustion.

Paloma looked at her for a long moment. At the mercenary mark on her wrist, the half of something broken. At the woman who prayed every night into a silence that had been answering all along.

*Soon,* she thought. *Soon I'll figure out the fourth thing, and you'll stop being sad in secret, and we'll get the third stone back from that horrible beautiful vault, and the god will sing, and spring will come like it's supposed to.*

She tucked the frost-flowered blanket around Quentin's shoulders, picked up the ledger-quill, and — because she was a child of accountants now, and because the dream had left her hands steady — she opened the ledger to the current page and wrote, in her careful borrowed alphabet, beneath Quentin's neat columns of expenses:

*Night 19. Dreamed of the god. It says: not abandoned. It says: soon. It says the fourth thing must be given freely. P.S. Quentin owes me one (1) honey roll for emotional labor.*

She capped the ink, closed the book, and was halfway back to the ladder when Quentin stirred, blinked down at the open ledger in her lap, and read the page.

Paloma froze on the ladder, caught, guilty, grinning.

Quentin stared at the frost-flowers on the blanket. Then at the entry. Then at Paloma. And her face did something complicated — the face of a woman whose entire cosmology had just been annotated by an eight-year-old in purple ink.

"Paloma," she said slowly, "what exactly," and outside, far off across the white fields, something howled that was not a wolf, and every horse in the barn began at once to scream.
