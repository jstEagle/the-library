## 17. Fuel for a Crueler Covenant

The vault did not have a door so much as it had an absence of everything else.

Paloma had learned the guild by its sounds—the kitchen's clatter like rain on tin, Tam's pen scratching truth into paper, Hesper humming in a language of butter and salt. The vault had none of those sounds. It had a silence with weight to it, a silence that pressed against her ears the way deep water presses against a swimmer's chest.

She sat on a chair that was too tall for her, in a room lit by one lamp that burned without flickering, because even fire was afraid down here.

*Cold country,* she thought, in the old tongue, which was the only tongue her fear spoke fluently. *Loud white cold country. I want the stones. I want the standing places. I want—*

She wanted Quentin. She did not know the word for that yet. She suspected there wasn't one, or if there was, it was a word people were too shy to say out loud, the way they were too shy to say *I am afraid* and *do not leave* and *you are my winter now, the good kind, the kind that means sleep and bread and someone watching over you.*

The lamp guttered once, as if it had heard her thinking.

Then the man came back.

Corvane Dusk moved through the vault the way a ledger moves through a house—flatly, thoroughly, leaving nothing unaccounted for. He carried no lantern. The shadows made room for him, which Paloma thought was very rude of them. He wore his grief the way other men wore coats: buttoned up, well-tailored, and clearly older than he was.

He set down a case. From the case he lifted things one at a time, laying them out on a cloth as carefully as a cook lays out knives: a bowl of black water. A ring of iron keys that hummed when they touched each other. A strip of parchment covered in writing that hurt to look at—not because it was ugly, but because it was *old*, old in the way mountains are old, old in the way her grandmother's stories were old before anyone wrote them down.

Paloma knew that writing.

Her whole body knew it before her mind caught up. It was the script of the First Covenant, the letters that had been carved into the standing stones of her warm country, the letters the voice had sung into place when the world was new and the seasons still needed teaching their manners.

"You recognize it," Corvane said. Not a question. He had the voice of a man who had stopped asking questions years ago and now simply announced conclusions. "Of course you do. That is rather the entire point of you."

He said it without cruelty. That was the worst part. Hesper had told her, once, kneading dough with flour to her elbows, that the most dangerous people in the world were not the ones who enjoyed hurting others but the ones who had found a reason that made hurting feel like arithmetic. Necessary sums. Balanced columns.

"I watched the last winter war," Corvane said, arranging his terrible cutlery of ceremony. "You would not remember it. You weren't born. Neither was I, not quite—but my mother was, and she remembered it for both of us, every day of her life, in the way she flinched at snow." He adjusted the bowl of black water a half-inch to the left. Perfectionist. "The first covenant held for an age because it was built on sacrifice freely given. Do you understand what that means? It means it was always going to fail. Freely given things can be freely withheld. The god Veth understood this eventually—it gave its voice rather than let the covenant collapse entirely. Noble. And doomed. A loan against a future that never agreed to pay."

Paloma hugged her knees. She understood perhaps one word in three, but she understood the shape of him, the shape men got when they decided a child was a column in a ledger.

"So we will build a better one," Corvane said. "A covenant that does not depend on willingness. One bound in flesh that cannot withdraw its consent, because it was never asked for any." He looked at her then, and his eyes were tired and certain and terribly sad. "You are not a prisoner, child. You are a foundation stone. In a hundred years, no one will curse me. They will be alive to curse anything at all, which is more than the last covenant managed."

He began to speak the old words.

And here is what no one in the Gilded Thorn had understood, not Corvane with all his ledgers, not even Quentin with her careful mercenary heart: the words were not foreign to Paloma. They were *hers*. They were the first thing she had ever heard, sung over her cradle by women whose names the kingdoms had erased. When Corvane pronounced them, he pronounced them like a merchant reading a bill of sale—correctly, precisely, and completely without love—and every syllable landed wrong, like a bell struck with a hammer instead of a clapper.

It was the ugliest sound Paloma had ever heard. Her grandmother's prayers spoken by a man balancing books.

The rite took hold anyway. Old words do not require love; that was the whole horror of them. Cold crawled up from the floor, up through the legs of the too-tall chair, up into her bones, and she felt it begin to write itself into her, line by line, clause by clause—a contract being inked directly onto a living child. Somewhere above her, impossibly far away, she felt the weather change. Felt the sky lean closer. Felt winter, the real winter, the endless one, sit up from its long crouch and take notice.

*No,* she said.

She said it in the old tongue, and it came out small, a mouse of a word in a cathedral of a room.

Corvane did not pause. Why would he? What power did a child's refusal have against a rite older than refusal?

But Paloma had been raised—or half-raised, or dreamed-raised—in a country of standing stones, and her grandmother had told her the secret that the kingdoms forgot: the First Covenant had never been between the god and the realms. That was the second covenant, the polite one, the one with paperwork. The first covenant, the true one, had been between the god and *everyone who spoke to it*. Every voice counted. Every voice, however small. Especially the small ones, because the god had always liked the small ones best—they were closest to the ground where the spring came up.

*No,* Paloma said again, and this time she stood up on the rungs of the too-tall chair, and she put her whole self into it, her fear and her fury and her homesickness and her stubborn hope, all of it, everything the white loud cold country had not managed to grind out of her.

*I refuse. I am not your stone. I am not your column. I am Paloma, and my name is mine, and my voice is mine, and I was sung into the world by women who are dust now but who are STILL ANGRY, and I am angry on their behalf, and I REFUSE.*

The lamp went out.

For one heartbeat the vault was dark as the inside of a closed book.

Then the satchel answered.

Quentin's satchel—left behind, confiscated, dumped carelessly in the corner of the vault by a guard who had not known what he was carrying—burst open like a seed pod. Three oath-stones rose into the air, and they were *singing*. Not loudly. Not dramatically. They sang the way her grandmother had hummed while shelling beans: low, unhurried, absolutely certain. The first stone, bought with Quentin's happiest memory, sang in a voice like morning. The second, bought with Hesper's decades, sang in a voice like a kitchen. The third, pried from Corvane's own vault by hands that shook, sang in a voice like a door finally opening after a long illness.

They sang in the old tongue. And what they sang was her name.

Not the name Corvane would have carved into her. Her actual name. The one the frost-flowers knew. The one the road of white bells had learned. The stones had been listening the whole journey—listening to her sleep-talk, her half-words, her growing fluency—and they had taken her name into themselves the way soil takes rain, and now they gave it back to her, ringing, *anchored*, a name with foundations under it.

The cold writing itself into her bones stopped. Recoiled. Old words required consent, it turned out—true consent, freely given—and a freely given *refusal*, spoken in the covenant's own tongue by its own heir, was worth ten thousand rites.

Corvane stumbled back a step. For the first time since Paloma had met him, the guildmaster looked like a man reading a ledger and finding the columns did not balance.

"That is not possible," he said. "The stones were inert. They have been inert for centuries. They require—" He stopped. Paloma watched him arrive at the answer, watched it land on him like snow off a roof. "They require a speaker."

*Yes,* said the stones, in the old tongue, in Paloma's name.

The black water in the bowl boiled away to nothing. The humming keys cracked, one by one, like ice letting go of a branch in spring. The strip of ancient parchment curled at its edges, and the terrible old writing lifted off it like smoke and dispersed, un-written, un-bought, returned to the air where words belonged.

Paloma climbed down from the chair. Her legs shook. She walked to the corner and picked up the satchel herself, because it was Quentin's, and Quentin would want it, and holding it was almost like holding Quentin's sleeve, which was the next best thing to holding Quentin's hand.

The stones settled into the leather, still warm, still murmuring. Above her—she could feel it now, feel it the way you feel a storm two valleys over—the sky was whitening. Corvane's rite had not finished, but it had not wholly failed either; something had gotten loose, some fraction of the endless winter had slipped its leash and begun to fall. She felt the city above begin to freeze mid-breath. She felt a thousand hearts slow toward stillness.

And underneath all of that, faint but unmistakable, coming up through the floors like warmth from a kitchen:

Footsteps. One set. Unhurried. Unprotected. Marked and mortal and walking straight toward the vault with no plan that Paloma could hear, no cleverness, no leverage—

Only love, which the old tongue had a word for after all. Paloma had just learned it. It sounded like her name.

The vault door, cracked and sealed and absent-of-everything-else, began, very slowly, to thaw.

* * *

Corvane recovered faster than she expected. That was the thing about men who balanced ledgers: a loss on one page only sent them hunting for credit on another.

"Enough," he said, and his voice had gone quiet, which was worse than loud. "You have delayed the rite. Delayed is not defeated. The winter above us does not need your consent, child—it only needs time, and time is the one commodity this guild has never failed to collect." He drew himself up, and the shadows made room for him again, and Paloma hated how much space he could take up just by standing still. "When the city freezes, the covenant completes itself around whatever anchor remains. You. Or the mercenary, if she reaches us—her mark makes her a vessel too. Either will serve. I take no pleasure in it."

*Liar,* said the stones, softly, in her name. *You take every pleasure you permit yourself, which is none, which is why you are doing this.*

Paloma almost laughed. Grandmother would have loved these stones.

She backed toward the thawing door, satchel hugged to her chest, and she said the only thing left worth saying, in the old tongue, clear as a bell struck at dawn:

"Then you should know the terms have changed. The covenant isn't yours anymore. It's listening to *me*."

The door opened—not unlocked, not forced, simply *melted*—and beyond it the corridor glowed with a strange white light, the light of a sky finishing its fall, and in that light, small and steady and walking as though the frozen world were a hallway in her own house, came Quentin Nakamura, carrying three empty hands and a face that had already chosen what it would spend.

Behind Paloma, the three stones began to sing louder.

Ahead of her, Quentin saw her, and mouthed something across the freezing air—no sound, the cold had eaten all the sound—but Paloma read her lips easily enough, because some words need no language at all.

*I'm here.*
