## 19. Reforging

### Quentin

The frozen city did not echo. That was the first thing Quentin noticed as she walked through it, and the thing that frightened her most.

Sound had always been the world's way of admitting it existed—the rattle of shutters, the argument of gulls, the groan of cart wheels on old stone. Now her boots made no noise at all on the snowed streets. Her breath made no fog. Even the satchel of oath-stones against her hip had gone quiet, though she could feel them humming like bees in a jar, three small hearts beating out of time with each other and all of them, somehow, keeping pace with hers.

Above her, a baker stood in his doorway with a tray of loaves half-lifted toward the sky, frost climbing his sleeves like ivy. A cat was mid-leap between two windowsills, suspended in white air. Somewhere a church bell hung silent at the top of its swing, holding its note forever, or refusing to begin it—Quentin couldn't decide which was worse.

*Corvane,* she thought, and the name tasted like an unpaid invoice.

She had no plan. She wanted to be honest with herself about that, because lying to yourself was the one trade the Gilded Thorn forbade even in its worst years, and she had been a member of that guild long enough for the rule to get into her blood. She had three stones, a mark on her wrist where Veth's protection used to sit—a raw pink scar now, like a healed burn—and no contract, no god, no legal standing whatsoever. The Hoarfrost Advocate could collect her at any moment, and by every law she knew, would be *right* to.

But Paloma was in the vault under the guild, and Corvane Dusk was doing something to her that made the sky go white, and there were some debts you paid without checking the terms.

"Keep up," she told the stones, because talking to objects was either madness or comfort and she'd never learned to tell the difference. "We're going home."

The Gilded Thorn rose ahead of her, its gilded sign furred with frost, its door standing open. Of course it was open. Corvane wanted her to come. He'd left the door open the way you leave a trap baited, and Quentin Nakamura, who had spent eleven years appraising traps for a living, walked straight into it anyway.

Some things you didn't appraise. Some things you just paid.

* * *

### Paloma

The vault was cold in the wrong way.

Paloma had known cold before—the cold of the road, the cold of the snow that chased her across half the world—but this was different. This cold came from underneath, from the floor, from the circle Corvane had chalked around her feet with symbols she almost understood and wished she didn't. It was the cold of something being *taken*, and it climbed her legs like water filling a boot.

"Be still," Corvane said. He knelt at the edge of the circle, hands steady, face kind. That was the terrible part. His face was so kind. "I know it frightens you. I'm frightened too. I have been since I was younger than you."

He held the third oath-stone in both palms. It was black as a closed eye.

"You don't understand what I saw," he went on, and his voice dropped low, the voice of a man telling a story he'd told himself ten thousand times. "The last winter war. I was a clerk's boy. I counted the dead because someone had to and the clerks were dying faster than anyone. Whole villages, gone quiet. And do you know what stopped it? Not armies. Not heroes. A covenant. A bargain. Someone gave something up, and spring came back." He looked at her, and his eyes were wet, and Paloma hated that it made her want to believe him. "I am not cruel, child. I am arithmetic. One life weighed against every life. Any honest ledger balances the same way."

Paloma pressed her hand flat against her chest, over her heart, the way her mother had taught her. *When you cannot say it, mean it.*

Then she refused him. In the old tongue, in the language of standing stones and singing seasons, she said: *No. You are not arithmetic. Arithmetic does not weep. You are afraid, and fear is not a number.*

She didn't know if he understood. But the satchel-songs in her blood—the two stones she'd carried so far north they felt like family—suddenly answered her from somewhere above, somewhere close, humming up through the vault's stone bones like warmth rising through a floor.

And Corvane heard it too. She watched his hands begin, very slightly, to shake.

* * *

### Quentin

The stairs down to the deep vault were lined with candles that had frozen mid-flame, each one a small orange sculpture of itself. Quentin took them two at a time, which was foolish for a woman her age with no divine protection and a bad knee, and did it anyway.

She burst through the vault door with all the dramatic entrance of a mercenary who had rehearsed speeches on the walk over and found, as usual, that reality had not read the script.

Corvane stood in a ring of chalk with the third stone raised. Paloma stood inside the ring, chin up, fists balled, radiating the specific fury of a seven-year-old interrupted mid-defiance. The air between them bent, whitened, thickened like breath on glass.

"Quentin," Corvane said, without turning. "You're late. You were always late with your ledgers. I forgave it because your columns balanced."

"Put the stone down, Corvane."

"I can't." And here was the awful thing: he sounded *relieved*. Like a man setting down a weight he'd carried so long he'd forgotten his shoulders were supposed to be straight. "Don't you see? That's the whole point. No one person can put it down. That's why it has to be a covenant. That's why it has to cost. If it cost nothing, it would hold nothing."

"The old covenant cost a voice," Quentin said. She stepped closer. The stones in her satchel were singing now, actually singing, three notes searching for a fourth. "Veth gave its voice so the world would keep its springs. That was the bargain. That was the *terms*, Corvane—I've read them, they're in the oldest ledger, in the pre-kingdom script, and Tam finally translated them last month while drunk on Hesper's memory-wine, which is the only reason any of us know—" She stopped. Breathed. "You're not balancing a ledger. You're forging one. You're writing in a debt that was already paid."

Corvane's jaw tightened. "The winter is falling again. Whatever the terms were, they're failing."

"Because the covenant is *broken*," Quentin said. "Three stones sold. One missing. A god struck mute and left holding a bargain alone. You don't fix a broken contract by doubling down on it. You refound it. You renegotiate." She lifted the satchel off her shoulder. "And renegotiation requires a new signature."

"There is no fourth stone," Corvane said. "I've searched for thirty years. It doesn't exist. The covenant can't be reforged, only replaced, and replacement requires fuel—"

"That's where you're wrong," said Quentin, "and I say this with love, Guildmaster, because you taught me everything I know about impossible things: the fourth anchor was never a stone."

She opened the satchel. Three stones rose into the air, slow as bubbles in honey, and arranged themselves in a triangle above the chalk ring, humming.

"It was always meant to be given," Quentin said. "Freely. By a willing party. That's why the old realms kept it vague in the records—because you can't inventory a gift. You can't appraise it. Believe me, I've tried." Her mouth did something that was almost a smile. "It's a name. A true name, spoken into the covenant, freely surrendered. And I happen to have one."

"No," said Corvane.

"Yes."

"*No.* Quentin Nakamura, I forbid—"

"You can't forbid me," she said gently. "I broke my contract, remember? I'm not yours anymore. I'm not anyone's. Which makes me," and here her voice caught, just slightly, "the freest person in this room. Freer than you. Freer than her. Free enough to give this away."

She looked at Paloma then, really looked at her—small and barefoot and stubborn, the child who had knocked on the guild door with winter at her heels and every ledger opening to a blank page—and Quentin thought: *this is what I was for. Eleven years of ledgers and prayers into silence, and this is what all of it was for.*

"Paloma," she said. "Cover your ears if you want. But I'd rather you heard it."

* * *

### Paloma

Paloma did not cover her ears.

She watched Quentin kneel at the edge of the chalk circle, inside the ring of floating stones, and she understood—not all the words, but the shape of them, the way you understand rain coming before it falls.

"Veth," Quentin said, to the empty air, to the silence that had lasted three years. "Old friend. Bad correspondent. I'm going to speak now, and for once in your endless quiet, I need you to listen."

She closed her eyes.

"My name is Quentin Nakamura. Mercenary of the Gilded Thorn, keeper of its ledgers, sworn once to your service and unsworn by my own choice, which I regret exactly none of. I give my name to the covenant. Freely. Without price, without escrow, without terms." A pause, and then, softer, with the ghost of her dry humor clinging to it like frost: "Though if you're taking requests, I'd like spring back. And I'd like her to grow up warm. Consider it a tip."

She spoke her name into the silence.

Not said it—*gave* it. Paloma felt the difference in her teeth, in her bones, in the standing-stone song behind her heart. The name left Quentin the way heat leaves a cup into cold air, visible for a moment as a shimmer, and then gone, and where it had been there was nothing, a smooth blank space like a page waiting.

The three stones flared white.

The chalk ring burned gold and then silver and then a color Paloma had no word for yet but knew she would learn, a color like the moment before dawn when anything might still happen. The fourth anchor settled into place—not a stone, a name, freely given—and the covenant closed around it like a hand closing around another hand.

And the silence broke.

* * *

### Quentin

It began as a hum. Then a chord. Then a voice—*the* voice, vast and cracked with disuse and unmistakably, achingly glad, pouring up through the vault floor and the guild foundations and the frozen city above like water finding a crack in a dam.

Veth sang.

It was not a beautiful voice, at first. It was rusty and enormous and slightly off-key, the voice of a god who had been mute for centuries and had a great deal saved up, and Quentin laughed—actually laughed, tears freezing on her cheeks—as the song rolled through the vault and the frost on the walls bloomed backward into water. The suspended bell above the city finished its swing. Somewhere overhead, faintly, impossibly, the baker's loaves completed their journey to the windowsill.

The winter did not vanish. But it *stopped*. The white pressure on the sky eased like a fist unclenching, and through the vault's high vent-slit came the first gray light of a morning that intended, eventually, to be blue.

In the center of it all, the three stones sank gently to the floor and fused—seam melting into seam, song folding into song—into a single pillar the height of a child, warm to the touch, humming the covenant's new terms in a language only one person present could read.

Paloma read it. Her lips moved. Then she looked up at Quentin with an expression of such catastrophic delight that Quentin's heart nearly failed on the spot.

"What does it say?" Quentin asked. "The terms. What did I sign?"

Paloma considered. Then, carefully, in accented but flawless Common: "It says the keeper's name is held in trust. Not lost. Held. Until the covenant is old enough to give it back." She frowned, concentrating. "Also it says—" she squinted— "*thank you for the tip.*"

Quentin sat down abruptly on the cold floor, which was no longer cold, and laughed until she cried, and discovered she could not remember her own name, and found that this felt strangely, enormously like being forgiven.

Which left only Corvane.

He stood at the edge of the ruined chalk circle, the black third stone fallen from his hands, staring at the singing pillar with the face of a man reading a ledger that has just informed him he has been rich his whole life and never once checked. Thirty years of arithmetic. One life against every life. All of it built on the belief that the covenant demanded cruelty—and here were the true terms, glowing on warm stone, demanding only that someone give freely what no one can be forced to surrender.

"I didn't know," he said. His voice was very small for a man so large. "The records were sealed. I thought—"

"You thought the world ran on payment," Quentin said. She got to her feet, nameless and light as an empty ledger. "It runs on gifts, Corvane. Payments are just what we make when we've forgotten how."

Corvane Dusk, guildmaster of the Gilded Thorn, who had witnessed the last winter war and prepared thirty years for the next one, looked at the child, the nameless woman, and the singing stone—and faltered. His knees actually bent. He sat down hard among the melted chalk, and put his face in his hands, and above his head the frost retreated up the walls like a tide going out.

Paloma tugged Quentin's sleeve.

"He's crying," she whispered.

"Yes," Quentin said.

"Are we going to be nice to him?"

Quentin considered the man who had sold a god's voice, catalogued a child, and frozen a city mid-breath—and who was now weeping on the floor of his own vault like a clerk whose columns had finally, catastrophically, balanced wrong.

"We're going to be fair," she said. "It's worse."

Above them, muffled by stone and distance, came a sound the frozen city had not made in weeks: a knock at the guild's front door. Measured. Patient. Cold as an appointment.

Paloma and Quentin looked at each other.

"The Advocate," Paloma whispered.

Quentin straightened, brushed off her knees, and found—to her considerable surprise—that she wasn't afraid. Marked, unprotected, nameless, and utterly without assets, she walked toward the stairs with a child's hand in hers and a god's song underfoot.

"Well," she said. "Let's see what the terms are."
