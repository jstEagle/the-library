## 20. Spring, Annotated

The first thing the thaw did was knock over a cart.

I heard it from the guild steps—a crack of wood, a horse's indignant scream, and then the sound of a thousand small things happening at once: icicles letting go of eaves in glittering avalanches, gutters waking with a groan, water finding every gutter and drainpipe and cellar crack it had been denied for a season and taking all of them at once. The city exhaled. You could hear it breathing again, that great wet animal breath of drains and rivers and ten thousand chimneys, and underneath it, faint as a coin dropped in another room, something singing.

The stone in my satchel was singing. All three of them were.

I sat down on the guild steps because my legs had decided the matter without consulting me, and I watched the water come.

It came the way debts come—first a trickle, then a stream, then a brown joyful flood down the Corridor of Weavers, carrying a season's worth of ash and grit and one very surprised boot toward the harbor. People emerged from doorways like creatures testing whether the world still counted as real. A woman two doors down burst into tears at the sight of her own reflection in a puddle, which I understood completely. There had been weeks when none of us had been sure we had reflections anymore. When the Long Winter finished falling, the city had frozen mid-breath—mid-argument, mid-prayer, mid-cup-of-tea—and what Corvane's half-finished rite had started, Veth's returning voice had undone, but undoing takes longer than doing. The ice had gone reluctantly, grudgingly, like a tenant being evicted.

Now it was going all at once, and the city was loud, and I was sitting on cold wet stone holding a satchel full of singing rocks and I had no name.

That last part deserves some attention, because I kept circling back to it the way a tongue returns to a missing tooth.

*Quentin Nakamura.* I said it in my head out of habit and felt nothing answer. Not silence exactly—the silence of Veth had been a specific silence, a held-breath silence, a silence with weight behind it—but absence. For eleven years my name had been a hook, and contracts had hung from it: my mercenary oath, my guild bond, the debt I'd carried since I was nineteen and stupid and alive mostly by accident. Every paper with my signature on it had reached down through that name and held me. Now the papers were just papers. Somewhere in the guild archive there was a contract with *Nakamura, Q.* written across it in my own careful hand, and it was as dead as last winter's fire. I was, legally speaking, nobody. Unownable. Unbindable. Free in the way that a boat cut loose from its moorings is free, bobbing in a flooded street, going wherever the thaw took it.

I found I didn't know yet whether that was a blessing or a drowning. Both, probably. Most true things are both.

"Excuse me."

A man had stopped on the steps below me, dripping, holding his hat in both hands like an offering. "Are you—" He squinted. "You're the one who walked through the freeze. Everyone saw you. Walking through the middle of it when everything was stopped." His face did something complicated. "We thought you were dead. Or worse. And then the singing started."

"I'm not either," I said. "Dead or worse."

"No," he agreed, looking at me strangely, and I realized why. He was trying to think of my name. Everyone would be, for a while. The woman who walked through the stillness, the mercenary who gave her name to a god—and no one would be able to say it, because there was nothing to say. I was going to be a story with a hole in it. Stories with holes in them grow strange fast. By midsummer I'd probably have three heads and a sword of dawnlight, if the city's usual standards held.

"Thank you," the man said finally, inadequately, and went splashing away, and I sat there being grateful to be thanked by someone who couldn't tell me who he was thanking.

* * *

Inside the Gilded Thorn, the thaw had gotten there first.

Water had come through the roof above the appraisal room and dripped, all night, onto the ledger of bottled griefs, swelling it to twice its size so that it now looked less like a book than like a loaf that had been left to rise. Hesper stood over it with a wooden spoon in one hand and an expression I'd never seen on her before, which took me a moment to identify as uncertainty.

"It'll dry," she said, before I could ask. "Everything dries. That's the whole trouble with grief, isn't it? It keeps." She turned the spoon over. "Some of these bottles cracked in the freeze, you know. Grief got out. Down in the cellars there's a corner that's been weeping since Tuesday and nobody can get near it without crying about their mother."

"Has anyone tried apologizing to it?"

"I tried feeding it," she said. "Don't laugh. It worked on the frost-flowers."

It had, actually. Paloma's frost-flowers—the white blossoms that bloomed where she slept, that had bloomed all down the road north and all the way back again—had taken to Hesper's cooking the way everything took to Hesper's cooking, which was completely and immediately. She'd been leaving bowls of stewed pears in the cellar corner on a hunch, and the weeping had gotten, in her words, "more dignified."

The guild hall itself was transformed, and not only by the water stains. With Corvane gone—exiled by his own surrender, walking north two days ago with a single trunk and the whole of the guild's oldest ledgers balanced on his back like penance made physical—the Thorn belonged, provisionally and improbably, to Hesper. She hadn't wanted it. She'd accepted it the way she accepted most things, with a shrug and the observation that someone had to keep everyone fed, and that governance was mostly keeping people fed if you did it right. Already the auction catalogue had been pulled from circulation. Already the vault where Paloma had been held was being aired out, scrubbed, and—on Tam's insistence—converted into a reading room, "so the worst room in this building becomes the best one, and the building has to live with the irony."

Tam himself was at the long table with ink to his elbows, drafting the new charter. He wrote the way he forged: slowly, beautifully, and unable to lie. This was proving a complication. Every draft so far had contained a clause he couldn't honestly include, and he kept striking them out with little sighs.

"We can't promise the guild will never trade in impossible things again," he told me, not looking up. "Ink won't hold it. Half of what we do is impossible. What I can write is that we'll never trade in *people*, and never sell what wasn't ours to sell, and never again hold a god's voice in escrow like a pawned watch." He dipped his pen. "Which is more than the old charter ever promised. The old charter promised nothing. It just assumed everyone would behave, which is how we got Corvane."

"And how do we make people behave?"

"We don't," Tam said. "We feed them, and we write down what they owe, and we forgive small debts loudly so big ones feel ashamed." He glanced up, and his eyes went soft and awkward in the way they always did when he was about to say something sincere and couldn't find a forgery for it. "Also we don't let our best clerk walk out the door just because she's technically unemployable. No name means no contract, Quentin—or whatever I'm meant to call you now. But it doesn't mean no work. Work doesn't need your name. Work just needs you."

I opened my mouth to give the sensible answer—that I had a child to look after, that I needed to learn how to be a person instead of a contract, that gratitude made my skin itch like wool—and what came out instead was:

"I'll think about it."

Hesper snorted from the doorway. Tam smiled at his ink. Some things, apparently, survived even the end of the world.

* * *

Paloma found me at dusk on the roof.

She climbed the ladder the way she did everything now—quickly, certainly, with the particular confidence of a six-year-old who has recently discovered she is important and has decided to be important *sideways*, on her own terms, mostly in kitchens. The frost-flowers bloomed in her wake along the rooftop gravel, small white stars opening and closing like breathing. In the weeks since the reforging they'd begun appearing wherever she was happy, which meant the guild had developed a strange new weather system of joy, and the younger clerks had started leaving notes for each other in the patterns. *She laughed at breakfast. Look at the east wall.*

She sat down next to me without asking, which was also new. Before, she had asked permission for everything, in the old tongue first and then, haltingly, in ours—*may I, may I, may I*—as though permission were a currency she'd once been short of. Now she sat like she owned the roof. In fairness, she sort of did. The covenant ran through her like a river under a street; you couldn't see it, but everything above it was built on the sound.

Below us the city glittered and dripped. Somewhere a fiddler had come out onto a balcony, playing badly and joyfully, and people were shouting encouragement and insults up at him in equal measure, which is the traditional way cities say *we missed you* to music.

"The stone sings louder at evening," Paloma said. Her Common was better every day, but she still chose her moments, spending words like a miser spending gold. "Did you notice?"

"I noticed."

"It's not singing to us." She hugged her knees. "It's singing to the other stones. Counting them. One, two, three—" she tapped my satchel, "—and four." She pointed down, through the roof, through the floors, to the vault-turned-reading-room where the fourth stone now stood in a bed of river gravel and Hesper's pear bowls, reforged, whole, humming its low note into the bones of the building. "It counts and it's happy. Stones are bad at hiding happy."

"You're an expert now?"

"I'm the First Speaker," she said, with the magnificent gravity only a child can deploy without irony, and then ruined it entirely by adding, "also I had a pear."

We sat while the light went amber and then violet. The fiddler below switched to a slow tune, something old, and I found my chest doing a complicated thing around it, because I knew the tune—it was a marching song from the winter wars, the ones Corvane had survived and been bent by, the ones my grandmother had hummed while she mended nets—and it sounded different played into a warming street. Like a letter read aloud after the person it was about has been forgiven.

"Quentin."

My chest did the complicated thing harder. She'd been saying it for weeks—my old name, the hole in the story—and every time it landed wrong, like a key turning in a lock that opens onto air. Not because I regretted it. I would trade the name again tomorrow, and the day after, gladly, forever; I had seen what Veth's voice did when it came home, had heard the covenant take it and sing, had felt the winter let go of the world like a fist unclenching after years. No price was too high. But a price paid still hurts, and nobody tells you that the hurting doesn't cancel the rightness. They coexist. They share a room. They bicker.

"You don't have a name," Paloma said. "And names are how the world holds on to you." She said it in the tone of someone quoting, and I realized she was—quoting me, from some fireside night on the road, some piece of road-wisdom I'd handed her without knowing I was handing it over. Children bank everything. They spend it back years later, with interest.

"That's true," I said carefully.

"So." She turned to face me fully, cross-legged, grave, and produced from her sleeve a strip of birch bark, curled like a scroll, marked with lines of the old tongue in charcoal—her own writing, round and fierce, letters that predated kingdoms and had outlasted several of them. "I asked the stone. The stone asked the god. The god—" she paused, hunting for the word, found it, "—*agreed*. Which the god almost never does, Tam says. So it's official. Officialer than Tam's ink."

"Paloma—"

"It means *keeper*." She laid the bark in my hands like she was laying a crown, or a debt, or both. "Not keeper like a jail. Keeper like—" she frowned, searching our shared language for the shape of it, "—like the person who keeps the fire. Who keeps the ledgers. Who keeps the *people*. The one who stays awake so everyone else can sleep." She looked up at me, and her eyes were the exact grey-green of the first thaw water, and absolutely certain. "That's what you are. You kept me. So now the world gets to keep *you*, and it needs something to hold on to."

I looked down at the bark. The characters seemed to move slightly, the way the frost-flowers breathed, and I understood that this was not a nickname. It was a name in the oldest sense—a true one, witnessed by a stone and a god and a six-year-old with pear on her chin. Contracts couldn't hold me. But this wasn't a contract. Nothing in any ledger I'd ever kept had a column for this.

"When people say it," I managed, "it'll mean all that? Every time?"

"Every time," Paloma said. "Even when you burn the bread."

"I don't burn the bread. That was one time, and the oven was grieving."

"Even then." She grinned, gap-toothed, and the east wall of the guildhall, far below, quietly bloomed.

I said the name aloud. It came out rough the first time, smoother the second, and on the third try it fit the way a key fits—not the old key, not the contract-key, but something older and stranger, a key to a door I hadn't known the house had. *Keeper.* The one who stays awake. Somewhere beneath us the fourth stone hummed a note of agreement, and I felt the world's grip close gently around me, not a chain this time but a hand.

Paloma leaned her head against my arm, watching the lamps come on across the drowned, delighted city.

"There's one more thing," she said, in the tone of a child remembering homework. "The god wants to see you. Tomorrow. It says—" she listened to something I couldn't hear, head tilted, "—it says it owes you a conversation. Three years of prayers, it says. It's been *keeping track*." A pause. "Gods are bad at apologies. Tam's helping it write one."

I looked at the child, at the singing city, at the birch-bark name warming in my hands like a coal.

"Well," I said. "Then I suppose I'd better be there."
