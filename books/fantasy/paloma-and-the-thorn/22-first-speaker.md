## 22. First Speaker

The classroom smelled of chalk and woodsmoke and, faintly, of Hesper's honey-bread, because Hesper had never once in her life sent a child anywhere without provisions.

Paloma stood at the front of the room and held up the stone.

It was not an oath-stone — those were far away and sleeping their long sleep — but a teaching stone, one Tam had cut and polished until it was smooth as a river pebble, then inked with the first letter of the old tongue. The letter had no name in any kingdom's alphabet. It looked like a door seen from the side, or a person standing very still, listening.

"This one," Paloma said, "is called *vess*. Can anyone say it?"

Twenty children said it twenty different ways. One of them said it correctly, a small girl in the second row with flour still on her sleeve from helping Hesper that morning, and Paloma felt the word land in her chest like a bell being struck. *Vess.* The sound of standing still and listening. The first sound her mother had ever taught her, back in the warm country, back before the white and the loud.

"Again," she said, and they said it again, and outside the window the autumn light came down gold through the garden where Quentin was working.

* * *

The school had been Tam's idea, though he would deny it under oath, which was a funny thing to say about a forger who could not lie in ink. He had drawn up the plans the way he drew everything — precisely, obsessively, with little annotations in the margins — and then he had pretended the whole thing was Hesper's idea so that no one would thank him. Hesper had accepted the thanks graciously and then made him peel four hundred apples as payment.

The building sat at the edge of the guild quarter, which had changed more in seven years than in the seven hundred before it. Under Hesper's stewardship the Gilded Thorn still traded in impossible things, but the catalogue had been rewritten in Hesper's own hand, and the new rules were posted by the door in letters even a child could read:

*We do not buy grief from the grieving.*
*We do not sell years to the desperate.*
*We do not appraise children, or anything that can say no.*

Business was slower now. Business was also, everyone agreed, better.

Paloma finished the lesson with the song — she always ended with the song, the one that sang the seasons into place, the one she had carried out of the white country inside her ribs like an ember in cupped hands — and the children sang it badly and beautifully, the way all first speakers sing, and when the bell rang they poured out into the yard like water finding its level.

She stayed behind to straighten the benches, which was not necessary but was quiet, and quiet was still something she collected the way other people collected coins.

Through the window she watched Quentin in the garden.

* * *

Quentin did not know she was the keeper of the singing stone. That is: she knew it, in the way she knew the ledgers balanced, but she would have laughed at the word *keeper*, would have waved it off with one dry hand and gone back to her pruning. She had been nameless once — Paloma remembered that year, the year after the vault, the year the thaw came — and the world had not known what to do with a woman whose contracts could not hold her. Debts slid off her like rain off oiled cloth. Landlords forgot she existed. Once, a tax collector had looked directly at her and wept without knowing why.

Then Paloma had taken her hand in the frozen street, in the breathless white hush after the rite broke, and given her the word.

*Kepa.*

Keeper. The one who holds the door open. The one who tends what others abandon.

Quentin had cried, later, privately, in the way she did everything — efficiently, resentfully, once. But she had kept the name. She wore it the way she had once worn her mercenary contract: close against the skin, where it could not be traded away.

Now she moved down the garden rows with her sleeves rolled, talking to the plants in the low, exasperated voice she used for everything she loved. The singing stone stood at the garden's heart, half-sunk in the earth where they had set it, and it hummed — always, faintly, a note below hearing that you felt in your teeth rather than your ears. Roses grew around it in a riot that no gardener should have permitted, and yet every rose bloomed out of season, stubbornly, as if the stone were still keeping its old bargain in miniature: *you will have spring; I will see to it personally.*

Paloma watched Quentin stop beside the stone, as she did every day, and rest her hand on it for a moment. Listening. She would deny that too.

Seven years, and Veth had never spoken a word aloud. But the god was not silent anymore. That was the thing people got wrong, the thing the ballads got wrong — and there were ballads now, terrible ones, full of howling winds and heroic sacrifices, and Tam had forged a polite letter to every bard in the city asking them to please stop. Veth was not silent. Veth simply no longer needed to shout. Its voice lived in the turning of the seasons now, in the exactness of them, in winter arriving on schedule and leaving on schedule, in the first frost-flower opening on the same morning every year like a merchant honoring a contract to the letter.

Some gods thundered. Theirs kept books.

Paloma understood this completely. She had, after all, been raised by one.

* * *

At midday she walked out into the garden, and Quentin handed her a basket without looking up, because this too was ritual.

"The tomatoes are sulking," Quentin said.

"Everything sulks around you. You scold them."

"They're *underperforming*."

"You told the lettuce it was a disappointment."

"The lettuce heard what it needed to hear." But the corner of her mouth had moved, the small tectonic shift that, in Quentin Nakamura — in *Kepa*, rather — constituted open laughter. She straightened, pressed her hand to the small of her back, and looked at the stone. "It sang last night."

"I know. I heard it."

"It woke me at the third hour. Sang the same phrase over and over." Quentin frowned, and Paloma recognized the frown — it was the ledger frown, the one that meant an entry refused to balance. "I couldn't make out the words."

"I could," Paloma said quietly.

Quentin turned to look at her fully then, and waited, because she had learned — slowly, painfully, over years — that some things could not be hurried, least of all the old tongue, which arrived in Paloma like weather rather than like speech.

"It's the closing line," Paloma said. "Of the covenant-song. The part my mother never taught me because I was too young, and the part the god couldn't finish because—" she gestured, helplessly, at seven hundred years of history, "—because of everything. It's a promise. It says: *what was broken is not ended; it is waiting to be sung by new voices.*"

Quentin was quiet for a moment. A leaf came down off the pear tree, unhurried, right on schedule.

"That sounds," she said at last, "suspiciously like something a guildmaster would put in a prospectus."

"It sounds like something you'd put in a ledger."

"I put nothing so sentimental in ledgers." She picked up her shears. Then, gruffly, not looking at Paloma: "New voices. Well. You've got twenty of them in there mangling *vess* every morning. Sounds like the covenant's getting its money's worth."

* * *

That evening there was supper at Hesper's, because there was supper at Hesper's every evening, because Hesper had decided decades' worth of borrowed years bought her the right to feed everyone she loved whether they liked it or not.

Tam was there, arguing cheerfully with a merchant about a deed while simultaneously proving, with documents, that the deed was a forgery — his favorite party trick, the confession and the crime in one motion. Hesper moved through the kitchen like weather of a warmer kind, seasoning the stew with a pinch of courage and a whisper of patience, flavors she kept in jars now, labeled in her own hand. Corvane's chair sat empty at the far end of the table, as it always did, though no one had removed it. He had taken his exile north two winters ago, carrying his guilt the way he'd promised — like a ledger he intended to balance — and twice a year a parcel arrived containing no letter, only restitution: coin, sometimes; once, impossibly, the deed to a chapel that had been drowned for three centuries and was now, somehow, dry. They had never written back. But they had never thrown anything away either.

After supper, the children who hadn't gone home yet crowded around Paloma demanding the story again — the real one, not the ballad version — and she told it the way she always told it, starting not with the winter or the god or the mercenary, but with a knock on a door.

"There was a child," she said, "barefoot in the snow, who spoke a language nobody knew. And behind a desk inside, there was a woman with a ledger and a dry voice and a mark of her god on her wrist. And the child looked at that mark and thought—"

"What?" asked the flour-sleeved girl, always first, always hungry.

"*Half,*" Paloma said. "She thought: that looks like half of something broken. And she was right. And the woman took her in, and argued with her guildmaster, and bid everything she owned at an auction, and broke her own sacred contract, and paid a revenant clerk the happiest day of her life, and walked through a frozen city with no plan but love —"

"—that's not how *she* tells it," someone said, and the whole table snickered, because everyone knew Quentin's version began with *"the paperwork was irregular"* and ended with *"and then I fixed it."*

"—no," Paloma agreed, smiling. "That's not how she tells it. But it's how it happened. And in the end, when the covenant needed a fourth thing, it didn't need a stone. It needed a name, freely given. And she gave hers. And the god got its voice back, and the winter ended, and a child who had lost everything was given a home, and a woman who had given up everything was given a name."

She looked across the table at Quentin, who was pretending to read a shipping manifest and fooling no one.

"And that," Paloma said, "is why we start every lesson with *vess*. Standing still. Listening. Because the most important things don't shout."

* * *

Later, when the dishes were done and the children gone home and Tam had fallen asleep sitting up over his drafting table and been covered with a blanket by Hesper with the tenderness of a woman hiding contraband, Paloma walked out into the garden one last time.

Frost was coming. She could feel it the way she felt all the seasons now — not as threat, but as appointment. Winter would arrive tomorrow night, exactly on time, and stay its allotted season, and leave. It had done so for seven years. It would do so for seven hundred more. Somewhere far north, in a country of standing stones she would visit someday, when she was ready, the other oath-stones slept in their recovered places, humming to each other across the distances like old friends who no longer needed to talk to know they were heard.

She knelt by the singing stone and put both hands flat on its cool surface.

"Goodnight," she said in the old tongue. And then, because she was the First Speaker and could afford to be generous, she said it again in the trade-tongue, and once more just in plain words, the kind anyone could understand.

The stone hummed beneath her palms. Not the closing line tonight. Something smaller. A lullaby-phrase, maybe, or a greeting, or — she smiled — a complaint about the roses.

Behind her, the kitchen door opened, and Quentin came out with a lantern and two cups of Hesper's cider, and sat down on the cold bench beside her without comment, because that was what keepers did: they showed up, on schedule, and stayed.

"Winter tomorrow," Quentin said.

"On schedule."

"Hm." She sipped her cider. "Never thought I'd say this, but I've grown fond of the stuff. Winter, I mean. It keeps its accounts current."

Paloma laughed, and leaned against her shoulder, and above them the first snowflake of the season came down — early by a single hour, the only thing in the world that dared run ahead of its contract — and landed on the singing stone, and did not melt, and glittered there like a signature on a page.

And in the classroom behind them, on the chalkboard Paloma had forgotten to erase, the first letter of the old tongue waited in the dark for morning:

a door, seen from the side.

open.
