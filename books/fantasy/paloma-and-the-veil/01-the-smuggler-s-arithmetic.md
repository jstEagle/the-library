## 1. The Smuggler's Arithmetic

The goats knew before she did.

Ines Winterbourne stood in the blue hour before dawn and watched the herd come down the scree in single file, hooves finding purchase where no human eye could see a path. Eleven nannies and a belligerent old buck named Cistern, all of them heavy with the particular resentment of animals who had been woken too early for a purpose they considered beneath them.

*Cold,* said the lead nanny, whose name was Sorrel because her ears were the color of autumn oak leaves. *The stones are cold and my feet are cold and I am cold.*

*You will be warm by midmorning,* Ines told her, and felt the words settle into the goat's mind like a pebble dropped into still water. *There is clover on the far side of the pass. Sweet clover, three days grazed and growing again.*

*Liar,* said Sorrel, without heat. Goats appreciated a good lie told with confidence. It was one of the many things Ines liked about them.

She shouldered her pack — rope, dried fish, a coil of salted hide, the small iron kettle that had been her mother's and that she had never once used for anything but carrying — and started up after them. Behind her, the cabin sat dark against the pines, its chimney cold, its shutters drawn. Anyone passing would see an abandoned homestead, one of dozens scattered through the lower Thornfells since the Crown had taxed the high valleys into emptiness. That was the point of it. Ines had spent twenty years learning how to be overlooked, and she had gotten so good at it that sometimes she wondered whether she would notice herself, if she ever passed her own door.

The arithmetic of the morning was simple, as smuggler's arithmetic always was. Eleven goats bought in the lowland market at four coppers the head, driven over the Saddle Road to the mining camps at Grayhollow where meat was scarce and law was scarcer, sold at nine. Minus feed, minus the toll Tam Verrick's people charged at the river ford — two coppers a head, or a favor of equal weight, and favors were the only currency Ines dealt in gladly. Minus risk, which could not be counted but was always paid.

Forty-one coppers, if nothing went wrong. Enough for flour, lamp oil, the good beeswax from the widow Anselm, and a small purse of silver set aside against winters like the last one. It was not wealth. It was not even comfort, exactly. But it was hers, earned in the space between the Crown's maps, where no soldier bothered to look because there was nothing there worth taxing and no one worth conscripting.

That was the other half of the arithmetic, the part she never spoke aloud even to herself: invisibility was worth more than gold. A woman who talked to beasts could not exactly advertise. The last person who had seen her do it plainly — a drover named Hesk, eleven years ago, drunk enough to be honest and sober enough to remember — had looked at her afterward the way men look at a snake in a woodpile. He hadn't reported her. There was no law against talking to animals; the magistrates would have laughed him out of the assizes. But he had crossed the street when he saw her coming for the rest of his life, and Ines had learned that laughter was only the first kind of fear. The second kind wore uniforms.

So she kept her mouth shut around people and let the animals carry her conversations instead, and the arrangement had held for twenty years, and if it was lonely — well. Loneliness was also a kind of arithmetic. She had subtracted it long ago and lived with the remainder.

* * *

The Saddle Road climbed through larch forest gone gold at the edges, the first bite of autumn already in the air. Ines walked behind the herd and listened to the mountain talk, because the mountain was always talking; most people simply couldn't hear it over themselves.

The pikas under the talus slopes were quarreling about hay caches. A marten somewhere above was complaining about a wolverine who had raided its larder, in the aggrieved tones of a merchant describing a burglary. And threading through it all, the low constant murmur of the stone itself — not words, exactly, more like the memory of words, the way a shell holds the sound of the sea. The Thornfells were old mountains, older than any kingdom that claimed them, and Ines had long suspected they remembered things no living creature did. When she pressed her palm to the rock on quiet days, she almost heard it. Almost. Like a voice through a wall.

*Two-legs with iron,* said Cistern suddenly, from the head of the column. *Smell of iron and oil and many boots.*

Ines stopped. The herd stopped with her, eleven faces turning back with the synchronized insolence only goats could manage.

*Where?*

*Behind. Below. The road we are not taking.* Cistern flicked an ear toward the valley floor, where the King's Highway ran along the river, straight and wide and patrolled. *Many boots.*

She knelt and put her hand flat on the trail dust, as if checking for vibration, which was nonsense — she wasn't listening with her hands. But it gave her a moment to think while she pretended to be an ordinary woman resting an ordinary herd.

Soldiers on the King's Highway was unremarkable. Soldiers moved up and down it all summer, escorting ore wagons, shaking down toll-payers, reminding the high valleys who owned them. But Cistern said *many*, and Cistern had been a military goat once — requisitioned by a supply company, escaped at the first opportunity — and his nose for soldiers was better than any scout's.

*I'll look,* she told him. *Sorrel, take them into the trees. Graze slowly. Do not go over the skyline.*

*We were going to graze slowly anyway,* said Sorrel, and led the herd off through the larches with the air of one granting a great favor.

Ines left the trail and climbed. There was a shelf of granite below the ridge crest, half-hidden by mountain ash, where a person could lie flat and see the whole sweep of the valley without being seen from it. She'd found it years ago and thought of it, privately, as her window. She reached it breathing hard — she was thirty-four and the mountains kept their own accounts of age — and settled onto the cold stone, and looked down.

The King's Highway glittered in the early light like a brown ribbon. And on it, moving south to north in a column that took a full minute to pass beneath her, marched soldiers.

Not the usual garrison drift. These moved in ranks, in step, with supply carts and remounts and a wagon covered in oiled canvas that rode heavy, the way wagons ride when they carry something the owners don't want jostled. She counted banners: none. No colors, no heraldry. Unmarked troops, and unmarked troops meant one thing in the Thornfells.

Veilbreakers.

The word arrived in her mind with a taste attached, bitter as yew. Everyone in the high country knew the stories. The Veilbreakers were the Crown's answer to a question nobody had asked — the question of what lay beneath the mountains, behind the old veil between worlds, the one the covenant-keepers had tended in the time of grandmothers' grandmothers. Most folk said the Veil was a fable, a story to keep children from wandering into mine shafts. But the Crown had built an entire order on it, and orders built on fables did not march forty strong through empty valleys.

They were heading north. Toward Grayhollow. Toward the passes.

Toward, though she could not have said why the thought landed so coldly in her chest, *her*.

Ines lay on the granite a long time, watching the column dwindle to dust, and made herself count what she knew. One: soldiers, unmarked, well-supplied, moving with purpose. Two: they were on the King's Highway, which she had avoided, as she always did, and which meant they weren't looking for a goat-smuggler on the Saddle Road. Three: the covered wagon. Heavy wagons carried instruments, and instruments meant surveying, and surveying meant someone was making maps.

Maps of what? The Thornfells had been mapped a dozen times over. Every pass, every mine, every ruined chapel had its place on some clerk's chart in the capital.

Unless someone was mapping something that moved.

Her own tracks, for instance. Her own routes. The pattern of a woman who crossed the high passes eleven times a year like clockwork, who bought stock at the same markets, who drank at the same ford. Twenty years of habit, laid out plain for anyone patient enough to read it. She had always told herself her routines were camouflage — that doing the expected thing was safer than doing the strange thing. But habits were also signatures. And she had just watched forty men walk north along a road that would put them, by nightfall, within sight of every trail she used.

Below her, faint on the wind, came a cry. Not a pika, not a jay. A hawk — the tiercel who hunted the ridge thermals, a sharp-tempered little broadwing she'd known since he was a branchling. He circled twice over the ash trees and screamed again, and this time she caught the shape of it:

*Riders. Riders off the road. Coming up the sheep track. Slow, quiet, looking at the ground.*

Looking at the ground. Reading it.

Ines went down the mountain faster than she had come up, her heart keeping time with her boots.

* * *

*Sorrel. We're leaving. Now.*

The nanny lifted her head from a patch of bittercress, chewing, unimpressed. *You said clover.*

*And I said graze slowly, and you've been slow, and now we go. There are men on the sheep track reading the ground like a letter.*

Goats, as a rule, cared nothing for human danger. But they cared deeply about strangers with dogs and iron, and something in the way Ines said it — or perhaps something the mountain itself murmured through the soles of their hooves — turned the herd's collective mood from sullen to urgent. They fell into file without another complaint, and Ines took them off the Saddle Road entirely, west, onto a route that existed on no map because it barely existed on the ground: a seam of broken limestone below the crags, passable only in dry weather, invisible from any distance unless you knew to look for the cairn shaped like a sitting hare.

She had learned it from a chamois, six years ago, in exchange for pulling thorns from his hoof. It was the kind of debt the wild called in casually and repaid the same way, and it was the whole architecture of her life: favor stacked on favor, conversation stacked on conversation, a web of small kindnesses holding up one hidden woman.

By noon they were across the seam and descending into the next valley, and the hawk found her again, riding the thermals above the crags.

*They stopped,* he reported, landing briefly on a boulder to regard her with one furious yellow eye. *At your trail. Where your herd came up this morning. The one who walks in front crouched and touched the ground where you knelt. Then they argued. Then they went back down.*

*Argued about what?*

*About whether to follow. The one in front wanted to. The one with the white sash said no. Said—* the hawk cocked his head, hunting for the human words, which he pronounced the way a child pronounces a foreign tongue, carefully and with distaste — *said "she'll run true to form." Then they laughed.*

*She'll run true to form.* Ines stood in the wind with the goats cropping the thin grass around her and felt the sentence turn over and over like a stone in a stream. True to form. As if she were a known quantity. As if someone had written her down.

Twenty years of careful arithmetic, and somewhere in it she had become a sum someone else was calculating.

*Thank you,* she told the hawk. *Will you watch them?*

*I am always watching,* he said, which was hawk for yes, and launched himself off the boulder into the blue.

* * *

Grayhollow smelled of coal smoke and mutton fat, as it always did, and the mining camp's single street was loud with the evening shift. Ines sold the goats to a foreman named Brakka — a woman built like a mine prop, with forearms that could crack walnuts and a heart she hid behind a permanent scowl — and haggled the price up to ten coppers a head on the strength of Sorrel's evident excellent health, which Sorrel supported by standing very straight and looking dignified.

*You sold me for ten,* Sorrel observed, as Brakka led the herd away. *I am worth twelve.*

*Buy yourself back someday and charge what you like.*

It was an old joke between them, worn smooth as a river stone, and it was the closest thing Ines had to banter with anyone. She stood a moment watching the goats disappear into Brakka's paddock, and the loneliness rose in her the way ground fog rises at dusk — not suddenly, not dramatically, just everywhere at once. Twenty years of conversations, and not one of them with a face like hers. Not one voice that could say *how are you* and mean it in the human way, with all the terrible weight humans loaded onto those three small words.

She bought her flour and her oil and her beeswax. She bought a twist of candied ginger, because Paloma—

No. Because *she* liked candied ginger, and there was no one else to buy it for, and a woman alone was allowed her small vanities.

She was counting out coppers at the widow Anselm's stall when the ravens started.

Grayhollow's ravens were camp followers, fat and cynical, experts in discarded sausage. They did not speak to Ines — she was not their sort of people, they had made clear decades ago — but they spoke *around* her, loudly, the way courtiers gossip near a queen. And what they were saying, in the ragged chorus of the eaves, was this: *soldiers, soldiers, asking questions, asking at the assay office, asking at the tavern, showing a paper, showing a picture—*

A picture.

Ines left her change on the counter and walked out into the street, and the crowd parted around her the way it always did, not because they feared her but because she had spent twenty years teaching them not to quite see her. She drifted toward the tavern on the tide of her own invisibility and stopped in the shadow of the woodshed across the lane.

Two men stood by the tavern door. They wore no colors, but they wore their swords the way the column on the highway had worn theirs, and the taller one held a sheet of paper that he showed to each miner who passed. She was too far to see what was drawn on it. She didn't need to see it. She watched the miners shake their heads, one after another, and she watched the tall man fold the paper away with the unhurried patience of a man who had all season.

*Ask the drovers,* he said to his companion, in a voice pitched to carry no farther than the door. *Someone moves stock over the high passes. Someone always does. Find me the someone.*

Ines stood very still in the shadow of the woodshed, and the arithmetic rearranged itself in her head with a click she could almost hear. Forty men on the highway. Officers reading her trails. A picture shown in every market town between here and the river. They weren't mapping her movements after all.

They were hunting something. Something small enough to draw on a piece of paper. Something lost.

And every road she had ever walked, every habit she had ever trusted, now pointed straight at her door.

She shouldered her pack and walked north out of Grayhollow without looking back, and behind her, high on the tavern roof, the ravens watched her go and said nothing at all — which, from ravens, was the loudest warning there was.
