## 2. A Child at the Door

The storm came down off the high shoulders of Thornfell like something with a grudge.

Ines had watched it build all afternoon—a bruise-colored wall crawling over the western peaks, swallowing the sun in slow, deliberate bites—and she had spent those hours the way she spent most hours: working, and listening. The goats needed bedding before the weather broke. The woodpile needed covering. And the mountain itself needed attending to, because Thornfell always said what it meant, and tonight it was saying *shelter*.

She heard it first from the marmots.

They lived in the scree field above her cabin, a chattering colony she'd known for eleven years, and by now their alarm calls were as legible to her as any human tongue. *Big wind coming,* they'd been saying since noon, sharp two-note whistles flung from rock to rock like thrown stones. *Big wind. Big wind. Go down. Go down.*

By dusk they had stopped saying it entirely, which was worse. Marmots who went silent were marmots already underground, and marmots only went underground early when the thing coming was bigger than wind.

So Ines worked fast. She dragged the last split pine under the lean-to, weighted the tarp with stones, checked the goat shed's latch twice because the brown doe—Bramble, opinionated and enormous—had learned to lift it with her lip and considered this a fine joke. The goats pressed close while she worked, breathing their warm hay-and-rain smell against her hands.

*Storm,* Bramble said, in the way goats say things, which is less words than a pushing of meaning against the inside of your skull. *Bad one.*

"I know," Ines said aloud. She almost never spoke to people, but to animals she spoke constantly, low and easy, a running commentary she hadn't permitted herself around humans since she was nine years old. "I know. You'll be warm. Nothing's getting through that roof."

*Cold,* said the youngest kid, who had no opinions yet beyond temperature and milk.

"Yes. Cold." She scratched his ears. "Sleep."

The first thunder came as she latched her own door, a long rolling growl off the northern crags, and then the rain arrived all at once—not falling so much as arriving, a gray curtain drawn across the world. Ines lit her lamp, set water to heat, and settled into the evening ritual: beans, bread gone hard enough to need soaking, the mending basket she would ignore. Outside, the wind found its voice, a low moan through the pass that rose and fell like something breathing.

She was halfway through the beans when the hawk spoke.

That was wrong, for a start. Hawks did not speak in storms; hawks roosted through storms, feathers puffed, dignity intact, waiting out the weather with the patience of creatures who had never once doubted the sky would clear. But the voice came anyway, thin and strained through the rain-hammered dark, from the old pine at the treeline:

*Small one. Small one walking. Alone.*

Ines set down her spoon.

*Coming here,* the hawk said, and there was something in the tone she had never heard from a hawk before—something that in a person she would have called awe. *Walking through this. Walking toward you.*

"Nothing walks through this," Ines said to the empty room. "Nothing small."

*It does,* said the hawk. *Come and see.*

She told herself she wouldn't go. She told herself it was a trick of wind on feathers, a bird half-dreaming, that whatever moved out there in the white roar was a lost deer or a desperate fox and none of it was her business, because nothing on Thornfell was ever her business, that was the whole architecture of her life, built stone by careful stone over twenty years: trusted by outlaws, invisible to the Crown, fluent only with beasts, beholden to no one.

She was pulling on her oilskin coat before the thought finished.

The cold outside hit like a hand. Rain came sideways, needling, and the wind shoved her three steps toward the treeline before she'd decided to move. She kept to the path by memory, by the white blazes she'd cut into the pines years ago, and the storm erased everything past arm's reach so thoroughly that when the shape finally resolved out of the gray it seemed to have been conjured rather than approached.

A child.

A girl, small—eight years old, maybe, though it was hard to tell with the soaked wool hanging off her—standing in the open not thirty feet away, facing the cabin as if she'd simply been waiting for the door to be noticed. Her hair was plastered black to her skull. Her lips were blue. And clutched against her chest with both hands, held the way other children hold a doll or a wounded bird, was a broken stone whistle: a fist-sized piece of carved granite, snapped clean across, its mouthpiece shattered.

The girl looked at Ines.

Ines looked at the girl.

And the girl opened her mouth and said something.

No sound reached Ines through the storm—or perhaps sound reached her and her mind refused it, the way an eye refuses a light too bright. What she felt instead was the words themselves, landing somewhere behind her sternum: syllables in a cadence she did not know and yet understood the shape of, the way you understand the shape of grief or dawn. It was not Lowland speech. It was not the border cant the smugglers used, nor the clipped Crown-tongue of magistrates, nor anything from the valley tongues her mother had once hummed over the wash.

It was older. It was *much* older. And every hair on Ines's arms stood up beneath the oilskin, because she knew—she knew the way she knew rain from blood—that she was hearing the Old Speech, the language of the covenant, the tongue her mother had died rather than surrender, the language that had not been spoken aloud in the mountains since before kingdoms had names.

From a child standing barefoot in a flood.

"Inside," Ines said, and her voice cracked on it. "Inside, now."

The girl didn't move. She said the words again, patiently, as if Ines were the one being difficult, and this time Ines caught fragments—the sense arriving sideways, half-glimpsed, like fish under ice. Something about *finding*. Something about *the one*. Something about *told me*, and *never stop*, and underneath it all, threaded through every syllable like wire through cloth, a desperation so vast it made the storm feel small.

"I don't—" Ines started, and stopped, because the girl had turned her head.

Not toward Ines. Toward the treeline. And out of the treeline, absurdly, impossibly, came the fox.

Ines knew this fox. A vixen, three winters old, clever and shy, who would not come within fifty feet of the cabin even for the salted scraps Ines sometimes left. The vixen crossed the open ground through the worst of the storm as if the storm were a rumor, trotted directly to the girl, and sat down at her side in the flooded mud, looking up at her with an expression Ines had seen only once before in her life—once, twenty years ago, on the face of a wolf looking at her mother.

Reverence.

The girl put her free hand on the fox's head. The fox leaned into it. And the girl spoke again—those same impossible syllables, softer now—and the fox *answered*, a short chuffing bark, and Ines felt the meaning of it land in her own chest too, uninvited, unmistakable:

*Here. Safe. This one will keep you.*

Ines stood in the rain and felt twenty years of careful, load-bearing silence shift on its foundations.

"No," she said quietly, to the girl, to the fox, to the storm, to whatever had sent this. "No, no, no. Whatever you think I am—"

The girl swayed.

It was small, just a tilt, a puppet string going slack—but Ines was moving before her mind caught up, crossing the mud, catching the child as she folded. The girl weighed nothing. She was ice-cold, lips gray now instead of blue, and the broken whistle left a wet stone-shaped print against Ines's chest where the girl's grip finally failed. The fox trotted alongside them the whole way to the door, uninvited, unhurried, and sat on the threshold looking up until Ines—feeling like a madwoman—stepped back to let it in.

* * *

The cabin had never held two people. It barely held one and a goat's worth of gear. Ines stripped the girl out of the ruined wool, wrapped her in every blanket she owned, and set her by the fire, rubbing warmth back into hands and feet gone waxy-white with cold. The girl surfaced once, eyes opening—dark eyes, unnervingly steady for a child half-dead—and fixed on Ines with an expression that was not fear. That was the strangest part. There was no fear anywhere in her, only a kind of exhausted certainty, as if she had walked through a mountain storm on the strength of instructions and intended to keep following them.

Her lips moved. Faint, cracked, half-conscious: the Old Speech again, a few syllables, and the fire popped, and the kettle on its hook began—Ines would swear later—to steam upward more eagerly than kettles ought to.

Then she slept.

Ines sat across from her until well past midnight, drinking tea she didn't taste, watching the blanket rise and fall.

The fox had curled up against the girl's side like it had been assigned there, and slept too, one ear cocked at Ines in a way she chose to find insulting.

What she should do was obvious. Come morning, she should walk the girl down to the lowland road, hand her to the first caravan, wash her hands of the whole affair. Children belonged with people. People were Tam's department, the market's department, the Crown's department—let the Crown have her, let someone whose life wasn't built on being overlooked deal with a foundling who spoke in dead languages.

Except.

Except the Veilbreakers existed, and everyone in the high country knew what they hunted, even if nobody said it aloud. Except her mother had spoken this same tongue, and had burned for it on a magistrate's pyre in a square full of polite people who watched with their hats in their hands. Except the girl had been *sent*—*told me*, the words had said, *never stop*—and things that were sent were usually fleeing something, and things that fled to the top of Thornfell in a storm were fleeing something very large indeed.

Except the fox.

Ines looked at the fox, and the fox looked back, and between them passed the oldest conversation there is: *Well?*

"Well what?" Ines muttered. "You saw what I saw. She speaks it. She *speaks* it, and the mountain answers her like she's—"

Like she was royalty. Like she was a queen in exile, and every beast in Thornfell her kneeling court. Ines had spent her whole life as the only creature in these mountains who could hear them, a secret kept so long it had grown into her skeleton, and now in one night a silent child had arrived carrying a broken whistle and a dead language, and the whole order of Ines's world had tilted like a table with a broken leg.

The girl stirred. Her hand crept out from the blankets, searching, and closed around the broken whistle where it lay on the floor beside her. Even asleep, she did not let go.

Ines pulled her own blanket around her shoulders, sat back down, and gave up on sleep entirely.

Outside, the storm raged on, and somewhere beneath it—she could feel it now, faint as a toothache, a pressure behind the world—the mountain hummed. Not wind. Not water. Something older, waking slowly, turning over in its long sleep like a man hearing his name called after years.

Toward dawn, the ravens came to the window ledge, four of them, hunched against the rain, and they did not caw. They watched the sleeping girl with their flat black eyes, and then the eldest turned its head and regarded Ines, and spoke a single word in the voice ravens use for matters of weight:

*Soon.*

Ines looked at the child, at the whistle, at the fox, at the birds.

"What's soon?" she asked. "What's coming?"

But the ravens only pressed closer to the glass, out of the rain, and waited with her for morning—as if they, too, had been told to find her, and never stop talking, and had simply gotten there first.
