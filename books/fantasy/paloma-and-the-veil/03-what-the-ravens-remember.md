## 3. What the Ravens Remember

The ravens kept their parliament on the black spine of Carrion Crag, three miles east of Ines's cabin as the hawk flew and six as the goat stumbled. It was a journey she made perhaps twice a year, and never gladly, because ravens did not gossip so much as they *audited*. Everything you told them went into the great ledger of their remembering, and everything they told you came out at interest.

She left Paloma asleep under the wolf-pelt with a bowl of barley porridge covered by a cloth, and she left Bracken—the surly billy who had appointed himself the child's guardian within a day of her arrival—standing in the yard like a hired knife. The other goats watched her go with expressions of profound betrayal. She was used to that.

The morning was cold enough to make her breath hang in rags. Frost had come early this year, silvering the juniper and stiffening the mud into ridges like old scar tissue. Ines climbed with her collar up and her thoughts down, which was where they preferred to live anyway.

*She spoke to the marmots,* one of the goats had said last night, chewing. *In the dark. Not words like yours. Words like the mountain makes when it's deciding whether to fall.*

Ines had pretended not to hear. This was a skill she had perfected over twenty years: pretending not to hear things said in voices only she could hear.

But there was no pretending away what had happened at the stream two days past. Paloma had knelt at the water's edge, silent as always, and a trout had risen—risen, in October, into air cold enough to kill it—to hold itself before the child's face as though presenting itself for inspection. And Paloma had made a sound. Not a word. A shape of sound, round and old, like something rolled downhill from the bottom of time, and the trout had turned belly-up in an attitude that could only be called *reverence*, and then righted itself and gone.

Ines had stood in the shallows with her boots filling and felt the first real fear since the child had appeared on her doorstep clutching a broken stone whistle. Not fear *of* the girl. Fear of what the girl was a door into.

There was only one authority on doors beneath the mountains, and it lived on Carrion Crag, and it would want to be paid.

* * *

> *We remember the door below.*
>
> *We remember when it was loud.*

The parliament assembled as she climbed the last pitch—first one raven on the leaning stone, then five, then twenty, dropping out of the grey sky like pieces of a shattered thing reassembling. They took the rocks around her in a rough ring, feathers puffed against the cold, eyes like wet obsidian.

"Grandmother," Ines said, inclining her head to the eldest—a ragged female missing half a wing-feather, who had been old when Ines's mother was young, if ravens could be believed about anything, and they usually could be believed about exactly one thing each. "I've brought nothing. I'm asking a debt."

*You always bring nothing,* Grandmother said. *That is why we talk to you. The ones who bring things want answers shaped like the things they brought.* She sidled closer, head cocked. *Ask.*

"There's a child. Came out of the high passes in a storm, eight years old or near it, mute—or I thought mute. She speaks something. Old Speech, maybe. Older." Ines heard her own voice go careful, the way it went when she walked on scree. "The beasts answer her like she's born above them. And the mountain—" She stopped.

*Go on,* said a young male, too eagerly. *Say the frightening part. We like the frightening part.*

"The mountain feels different," Ines finished. "Thinner. Like ice over water in thaw."

For a long moment the only sound was wind combing through the crag and the click of raven claws shifting on stone. Then Grandmother laughed—not a pretty sound; raven laughter was a dry tearing, like cloth giving way—and said:

*So it begins again.*

"What begins?"

*The door below was loud once,* Grandmother said, and around her the parliament settled into the stillness they kept for old things, wings folded, heads low, an audience older than any audience Ines had ever stood before. *Before your kingdoms had names. Before the first king stole his crown from a river. There were keepers then, human women and men who held the words that kept the door quiet. They lived in the high valleys and they spoke the Loud Language softly, and beneath the mountains the veiled ones slept, or dreamed, or whatever it is such things do instead of sleeping.*

"And then?" Ines knew some of this. Her mother had known more, and had died for knowing it. But her mother's stories had been given to a child in fragments, in whispers, in the grammar of hush, and half of them Ines had spent twenty years trying to forget.

*And then the keepers grew fewer,* Grandmother said simply. *They always do. Humans burn what reminds them they are small. The words got scattered. Some went into stones. Some went into whistles.* Her eye fixed on Ines with terrible gentleness. *Some went into bloodlines, thin as frost.*

Ines said nothing.

*A girl,* said the young male, hopping twice along the rock. *A girl came up from the deep valleys two turns of the moon ago. We saw her people's smoke from the ridge. Then we saw the other kind of smoke, the kind that eats itself. Soldiers in grey with white stitched on their sleeves. They burned a sanctuary. We have seen sanctuaries burn before. This is not new. What is new—*

*What is new,* Grandmother interrupted, because young males narrated like magpies hoarded, taking every bright thing for themselves, *is that one walked out. One carried the words out in her mouth. And the words are waking up in her, word by word, and every word she finds, the door below hears it.*

"She's unsealing something," Ines said. It wasn't a question.

*Your mother asked us this same question,* Grandmother said, *in this same spot, in this same tone, twenty-two winters ago. About herself. About what was waking in her. We told her what we will tell you.* She drew herself up, ragged wing quivering. *The child has a name. The beasts already gave it to her, and the name is true: Paloma. Dove-of-the-ruin. Last Voice. She carries what the burned ones saved, and she does not know how heavy it is, and she cannot put it down.*

"Can the words be put back? Quieted? If I—if someone taught her silence—"

Grandmother's laugh again, that dry tearing.

*You cannot teach the sea to unremember rain. Every word she keeps is a plank pulled off the door. Every word she loses is a plank nailed back—but the losing must happen the right way, at the right place, or the door comes off its frame entire.* She paused, and her voice dropped into a register Ines had rarely heard from a raven: something almost like awe, almost like pity. *There is a place. The First Threshold, your kind named it long ago, when naming still meant something. If the covenant is to be reforged—if the door is to be made quiet again—it happens there, willingly given, freely spoken. Or it breaks everywhere at once, and everything beneath the mountains comes up into your valleys looking for room.*

*Everything beneath,* echoed the young male, with relish. *The horned ones. The pale herds. The things your grandmother's grandmother called the neighbors.*

"They're real," Ines said quietly. "My mother said they were real."

*Your mother said a great many true things,* Grandmother said. *That was her whole misfortune.*

Wind gusted across the crag, flattening the grass to bronze. Ines wrapped her arms around herself and made herself ask the question underneath all the others.

"Why tell me this? Your court doesn't involve itself in human debts. You said so to my mother too."

*We tell you because you asked,* Grandmother said. *No one else asks anymore. The goats don't ask. The wolves ask only about meat and borders. Humans stopped asking us anything except how to avoid us.* Her head tilted, that alien sideways tenderness. *Also, because the girl fed a trout from her own hand to a heron yesterday, and the heron is my granddaughter's mate, and family looks after family. Even ours. Even yours.*

*Paloma,* the young male said again, tasting the name. *Last Voice. Door-opener. Word-bearer.* He fluffed himself enormous. *We will watch her. We watch everyone. But her especially now.*

"One more thing," Ines said. "Grey soldiers. White stitching. Have you seen more of them?"

The parliament went very still, and Ines understood, with a sinking she felt in her back teeth, that she'd asked the wrong question last instead of first.

*Two days ago,* Grandmother said, *a man in grey came up the valley road on a horse too fine for the road. He stopped at the shrine-stone where the paths fork and he tied a strip of cloth to it. White. With red thread. He asked the ferryman's boy questions and paid him with a coin the boy bit and the boy lied to him and the man smiled like a man who collects lies.* Her eye glittered. *He asked about a missing child. He described her wrong on purpose, so that anyone who had seen her would correct him, and in correcting him, confess.*

The frost inside Ines had nothing to do with the weather.

"How far behind her is he?" she asked. "How many?"

*One man is here,* said Grandmother. *One man is always the first finger of a hand. Where the finger goes, the hand follows. Fly home, Speaker. Fly home and count your stores and decide what you are carrying, because the road south through Thornfell closes with the snow, and snow is early this year, and you have until it falls to choose what kind of story you and the dove will be when they find you.*

*Fly home,* chorused the parliament, in the flat unison they used when a matter was concluded, and twenty black heads turned toward the valley at once, and Ines felt the weight of their attention lift from her shoulders like a hand withdrawn.

*We remember the door below,* the young male said softly, as she turned to go. *We would prefer to go on remembering it shut.*

* * *

She ran most of the way down, which was foolish on scree and she did it anyway, and the goats complained at length about being left, and Bracken butted her hip hard enough to bruise as payment, and none of it touched the cold core of what Grandmother had said.

Paloma was in the yard when Ines came around the cabin, sitting on the chopping block with her broken whistle in both hands, turning it over and over. When she saw Ines she stood, and something in her posture—the way she squared herself, chin up, feet planted—made her look suddenly less like a child waiting for supper and more like a soldier awaiting orders.

The marmots had gone quiet along the rock wall. That was new. Marmots were never quiet. Ines looked at the rock wall and the marmots looked back with their small brown faces and did not whistle, and the silence had a listening quality to it, like a held breath.

"You know," Ines said aloud, in human speech, uselessly, and then closed her eyes and opened the other door, the one she kept barred. *I went to the ravens.*

Paloma's head tilted—raven-quick, beast-court quick—and her hands moved, the whistle rising, and she made a sound. Two sounds. Shapes of sound, round and old, and Ines did not need translation to feel them land like stones dropped down a well: *the door* and *the loud.*

"Yes," Ines said. "You know what you are."

Paloma's face did something complicated—young and old at once, a child's grief wearing an heirloom too large for it. She pressed the broken whistle against her sternum, hard, the way one presses a wound, and shook her head. *No*, said the gesture. *Not all. Not yet.*

From the ridge above the cabin, faint on the wind, came a sound Ines had been listening for all her life without knowing it: hoofbeats on the valley road, unhurried, official, wrong. Grey-coated. Too fine a horse for the road.

Ines put her body between the child and the sound without deciding to, the way a wolf puts itself between a den and a scent.

"Inside," she said, in every language she had, including the ones without words. "Whatever happens—inside, and quiet."

Paloma looked at her for one long moment. Then she nodded once, a small precise nod, and slipped through the doorway like water finding its level, and Ines turned to face the road with her hands empty and her heart going like a trapped bird, while somewhere on the ridge the ravens rose all at once into the grey sky, twenty black marks against the cloud, following the rider down into the valley like a verdict learning to fly.

The man in grey reached the fork at the shrine-stone and stopped, and looked up at the cabin, and smiled the smile of a man who collects lies.

Ines began composing hers.
