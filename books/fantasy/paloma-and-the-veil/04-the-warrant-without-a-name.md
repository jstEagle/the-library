## 4. The Warrant Without a Name

The lowland market at Millbrook smelled of wet wool, woodsmoke, and other people's lies. Ines had come down for salt and lamp oil, the two things a mountain cabin could not make of itself, and she had come alone—Paloma left behind with the goats and strict instructions delivered not in words but in gesture: stay inside, bar the door, feed the brown ewe first because she sulked otherwise. The girl had watched her go with those enormous dark eyes that never seemed to blink when they ought to, and had pressed one small hand flat against the doorframe as Ines walked away, as if touching the wood could keep her tethered.

Ines told herself the tightness in her chest was only the altitude change.

Millbrook sat where the Thornfell foothills gave up pretending to be mountains and became merely stubborn hills, and twice a month it filled with everyone who had something to sell and everyone who wanted to pretend they hadn't bought it stolen. Ines knew which stalls to visit and in what order, which farmers overcharged and which smugglers underreported, and how long she could stand in the crowd before her silence stopped reading as rustic reserve and started reading as suspicion. Twenty years of practice had made her invisible here. She moved through the market like smoke through a crack in a shutter.

She was haggling over lamp oil with a widow who charged by mood rather than measure when she felt it: a wrongness moving through the crowd, the way water feels a stone dropped upstream before it sees it. People were turning their heads. Conversations were folding themselves shut like moths closing wings.

A magistrate's party was coming up the high street.

Ines set three coppers on the oil barrel without waiting for change and drifted toward the farrier's awning, where shadow pooled deep and horses made better company than people anyway. From there, half-hidden behind a bay gelding that kept trying to lip her sleeve, she watched.

There were six of them. Two soldiers in Crown grey with the veiled-lantern badge of the Veilbreakers stitched at the collar—new, that badge, or at least new to these parts; she'd have remembered it from last season's runs. A clerk with ink-stained fingers clutching a ledger. And at the center, a magistrate: a narrow man in dove-grey robes gone travel-worn at the hem, with the particular pallor of someone who had ridden hard and hated every mile of it. He climbed onto the customs platform beneath the market oak, unfolded himself to his full unimpressive height, and held up a scroll.

The crowd quieted the way crowds do—not all at once, but in a spreading hush, like frost crossing a pond.

"By order of the Crown," he read, "a warrant is issued for the recovery of a missing child."

Ines went very still. The bay gelding shifted its weight and she put a hand on its neck without thinking, and the horse—sensible creature—stopped shifting.

"The child is female, aged approximately seven to nine years, small of stature, dark of eye." The magistrate's voice carried flatly across the square, worn smooth by repetition; he had clearly said this in a dozen towns already. "She may be traveling alone or in company. She does not speak any known tongue. She should be considered—"

He paused. Ines realized he was consulting his clerk, and the clerk was shaking his head slightly, and something about the whole exchange struck her as rehearsed badly rather than not at all.

"—considered lost," the magistrate finished, "and in danger. Any person rendering aid to this child performs a service to the Crown. Any person found concealing her—" and here his voice changed, took on an edge that had been absent from everything before it, "—commits treason against the realm."

Then he did the thing that made the cold slide down Ines's spine like meltwater finding a crack in the roof.

He turned the scroll around so the crowd could see it.

Even from the farrier's awning, Ines could see the warrant was blank. Below the seal and the royal flourishes, where a name should be written, there was only empty vellum—and beside it, in fresh ink, a line of instruction: *To be completed by the finder.*

"You will notice," the magistrate said, and now he smiled, thin and unpleasant, a man who thought smiles were tools and had brought only the one, "that no name appears upon this warrant. This is deliberate. The Crown does not know who harbors this child. The Crown does not need to know." He let the scroll roll closed. "Whoever finds her writes their own name into the law's favor. Whoever shelters her—we fill in the name afterward. Either way, the name gets written. The only question is whether you write it yourselves, or whether we do it for you."

He stepped down. The soldiers began working the crowd in pairs, unhurried, showing a small painted likeness of a child to stall-keepers and idlers alike. The likeness was good. Too good. It showed a girl of perhaps eight with dark eyes and a serious mouth, hair cut raggedly as if by someone in a hurry, and Ines felt her stomach drop through the floorboards of the world, because she had looked at that exact face across her own breakfast table for eleven days now.

*She does not speak any known tongue.* They knew that much. Which meant they had asked villages full of questions, and gotten answers from people who had seen Paloma pass through—or flee through—or been burned out around her. Ines thought of chapter five of nothing, of nothing at all, of the girl's own broken account: fire, running, a whistle snapped in half, *find the one who talks to beasts and never stop talking.* The Veilbreakers had burned a sanctuary village and were now walking its survivor's description from market to market like peddlers selling fear.

The painted likeness came closer. Ines turned her shoulder to it, bent to examine the farrier's work on a cart horse's shoe that needed no examining, and made herself breathe.

That was when she saw Tam Verrick leaning against the well at the square's edge, arms crossed, watching her watch the soldiers, with the expression of a man who had been waiting for her to notice him for some time.

* * *

Tam Verrick ran goods along the river roads south of the Thornfells—salt one direction, wool the other, and certain unlisted items in whichever direction paid best. He was broad as a doorway, gray at the temples, with a laugh that could be heard across three fields and a temper that could too, and he had known Ines since she was sixteen and stealing back her own confiscated goats from a Crown impound lot with nothing but patience and an uncanny way with the animals. He'd never once asked how she did it. That was the foundation of their friendship, more than any affection: Tam asked nothing, and Ines, starved of human speech the way a cave plant is starved of sun, had slowly learned to give him everything else.

They met, by old habit, at the back of Brannoc's alehouse, in the yard where the barrels were stacked and nobody respectable had business.

"You look terrible," Tam said warmly.

"You look rich."

"I am rich. There's a difference between looking and being, and you've never learned it." He studied her a moment longer, and the humor drained out of his face the way it always did eventually, leaving the shrewd riverman underneath. "You heard the magistrate."

"Everyone heard the magistrate."

"Everyone heard the words. You heard the meaning." He kicked a barrel upright and sat on it, which for Tam was settling in for a long conversation. "Blank warrant. That's not how warrants work, Ines. That's how traps work. They're not hunting a child. They're fishing. They've thrown a net across six counties and they're dragging it slow, and anything that moves gets a name written on it."

"I know how nets work, Tam. I live above them."

"Do you?" He said it gently enough, which was worse than saying it cruelly. "Because I've been doing my own arithmetic since midwinter. Your runs have shifted. You used to cross at Hollow Ford like a sensible woman, and then suddenly you're going around by the scree path like a madwoman, adding two days to every trip. Then the hawk business—you know Marta at the tollhouse swears your hawk follows you the whole length of the pass? People talk, Ines. Smugglers especially. Talking is most of what we do."

"Talking is what you do. I carry goats."

"And now you're carrying something else." He leaned forward, elbows on knees, and his voice dropped to the register he used for prices and ambushes. "I'm not asking what. I'm telling you what I know. Three weeks back, a Veilbreaker scout came down the river road asking after a mute child, and two weeks back there were two of them, and yesterday there's a magistrate with a blank warrant, and today you walk into my market wearing a face like a woman carrying a lit fuse through a powder store." He spread his hands. "I can count, Ines. I just can't divide. So here's my offer, plain: I'm heading south day after tomorrow with a wool run. I can take two passengers as far as Marrow's Bend, off the books, past every checkpoint, because I know the checkpoints better than the men who staff them. You and whatever it is you're hauling. South, where the Crown's reach goes soft."

Ines felt hope rise in her like water in a dry well—and immediately, suspiciously, like water in a dry well, she wondered what was filling it.

"What's the price?"

"No price. A question." Tam's eyes were steady and kind and absolutely immovable. "What are you carrying?"

"Goats."

"Ines."

"Salt. Lamp oil. Goats."

"Ines." He said her name the third time softly, and that softness was the sound of twenty years of friendship being weighed on a merchant's scale. "You forget who taught you the river roads. I've watched you stand in a thunderstorm arguing with a mule, and the mule apologizing. I've seen wolves step aside for you on the ford road like you were late for an appointment. I don't care. Do you understand me? Whatever you've got—a fugitive, a curse, a talking animal, a demon in a sack—I don't care, and I won't ask again. But I need to know if it's going to burn my boat, because I've got eleven men whose necks ride on my judgment, and right now my judgment says yes and my gut says ask. So ask I did. What are you carrying?"

And here was the trouble with secrets, Ines thought later, and thought often in the days that followed: they corrode exactly the things they were built to protect. She had lied to magistrates easily, to soldiers easily, to the entire human race with almost no effort at all, because lying to people required only that they listen with their ears, and ears were easy. But Tam listened with everything. And she had spent twenty years out of practice at deceiving a friend.

"A child," she said.

Tam nodded slowly. "The child."

"Not necessarily."

"Ines Winterbourne." He laughed—one short, unhappy bark. "You lie like a goat walks a rope bridge. Badly, and everybody watching holds their breath."

"It isn't—" She stopped. Started again. "It's complicated."

"Everything worth smuggling is." He stood up off the barrel, and something in his face had settled, decided. "Here's what happens now. Day after tomorrow, dawn, the wool wagons roll south. If you're standing at the mill bridge with your cargo, you're aboard, and I don't look in the sacks, and I'll swear to the Veilbreakers myself that you're just the strange goat woman and I barely know you. If you're not there—" he shrugged, huge and helpless, "—then you're not there, and I'll spend the rest of my life wondering if I buried you, because that's where this road ends if you take it alone. Blank warrants get filled in, Ines. That's the whole point of them. Somebody's name goes on that paper. I'd very much like it not to be yours."

He walked away across the barrel yard, and Ines stood among the shadows of stacked oak and iron hoops and understood that she had just done the arithmetic of friendship, and the sum was ugly: she could protect Paloma with secrecy, or she could protect them both with trust, and every year of her life she had chosen secrecy, and every year it had cost her another piece of the world.

Behind her, in the square, a soldier was holding up the little painted likeness to a fruit-seller's children, asking have you seen her, sweetling, look carefully now. One of the children shook her head. The other stared at the picture a long moment with the frank, terrible honesty of the very young, and then said, loud enough to reach the barrel yard:

"That's the lady's girl. The goat lady. She comes down from the mountain with her."

* * *

Ines bought nothing else. She walked out of Millbrook at a pace just short of running, took the drover's track north rather than the high road, and did not stop to rest until the town was a smudge of chimney smoke behind her and the foothills had risen around her like familiar shoulders.

The hawk found her at the second ridge, dropping out of a hard blue sky to land on a fence post ahead, head cocked, golden eyes furious.

*I know,* Ines said silently, wearily, the way she spoke to birds, a pressure of meaning rather than words. *I know. Faster.*

The hawk launched and led her home along the ridgeline, and Ines followed, and all the while her mind turned the same stone over and over: *the lady's girl.* A child in a market square had named her in front of a Veilbreaker soldier. By tomorrow morning, maybe tonight, grey riders would be climbing the mountain track toward a cabin that sat alone in a bowl of pine with one road in and no road out.

She crested the final rise as the light went long and copper, and saw the cabin below with smoke rising straight from its chimney—good, obedient, careful—and felt relief pour through her so fierce it was almost pain.

Then she saw the second thing, and the relief died in her throat.

Down in the valley, on the lowland road where it crossed the river flats, small and slow and patient as weather, a line of riders was already making for the foothills. Six of them. Grey cloaks.

Ahead of them, trotting down the track toward the riders with her hand raised in greeting, as friendly as you please, went a figure Ines knew by silhouette alone: broad as a doorway, gray at the temples.

Tam Verrick was going to meet the soldiers halfway.

And Ines, alone on the ridge with the wind pulling at her coat and a hundred heartbeats of distance between herself and either man, had no way of knowing whether her oldest friend was riding down to lie for her—

or to write her name on a blank page.
