## 5. Paloma Speaks

The storm had spent itself by morning, and the world outside the cabin lay rinsed and glittering, snow-heavy pines bowing under a sky scrubbed blue. But inside, in the warm dark that smelled of woodsmoke and dried herbs and goat, Paloma sat on the floor beside the hearth with her hands folded in her lap like a small stone monument, and for the first time since her village had burned, she opened her mouth and let the words come.

They did not come as the lowland people's words came. They came the way water comes from beneath a mountain—cold, old, tasting of iron and root. The Old Speech. The tongue her grandmother had called *the first listening*, the language that was not made by people but had made itself, or perhaps had been made by whatever lived below the Veil before anyone thought to build a door between worlds. Every syllable of it carried weight. Every word, once spoken, could not be unspoken.

The woman called Ines sat across from her on a three-legged stool, very still, her weathered hands wrapped around a clay mug she had long stopped drinking from. Her eyes were wide. Not frightened—not exactly—but wide the way eyes go when a person sees something they have spent their whole life pretending not to believe in.

"Speak," Ines said softly, in the flat human tongue, and then, wonder cracking her voice open: "No. Go on. I can hear you."

And Paloma, who had not spoken a full sentence to another living soul in forty-one days—who had counted them, each one a small gray stone added to a pile in her chest—began to tell her everything.

* * *

*I am called Paloma,* she said. *That is the name my mother gave me at the well-house, in the old way, whispering it into the water so the mountain would know me. It means dove. It means the bird that comes back.*

She watched Ines flinch slightly at the cadence of it—the Old Speech moved strangely through a human mouth, all consonants worn smooth, vowels held too long, like stones in a riverbed. But the woman understood. Paloma could see the understanding land in her face like birds settling one by one onto a wire.

*I am eight years old. I was born in Serramonte, which your maps do not have, because we did not want them to.* A breath. The hearth popped. Outside, somewhere down the slope, one of Ines's goats bleated, and even that small sound seemed to lean closer to listen. *Serramonte was a sanctuary. Do you know what that word means in the Speech? It does not mean safe. Safe is a wall. Sanctuary means a place where something is kept alive on purpose. We kept the covenant alive. My grandmother did, and her mother before her, back to the time when the Threshold was not a story but a place you could walk to.*

Ines's knuckles whitened around the mug. She knew. Paloma could see that she knew—or half-knew—and that the knowing hurt her, and that she had built her whole silent life on top of that wound like a cabin built over a grave.

*We were not many,* Paloma went on. *Forty-one souls. Forty-one mouths that could speak the Speech, though most of us only spoke it small—blessing the goats, calming the bees, asking the river to be kind to our washing. Only the elders carried the true words. My grandmother carried the most. She was the Voice of Serramonte, and when the covenant-keepers of the other sanctuaries died out—one here, one there, sickness, forgetting, the Crown's quiet questions—her voice became one of the last threads holding the Veil shut beneath the mountains. She told me once, when I asked why the words mattered so much, that the Veil is not a wall either. It is a seam. And seams are held by stitching. And every stitch is a word spoken by someone who means it.*

Her throat tightened. She made herself keep going. That was the instruction, the last instruction, the only one that mattered now: *never stop talking*. But it was hard. Speaking meant remembering, and remembering meant the fire again, and the fire again meant—

She spoke anyway.

*The Veilbreakers came on the night of the autumn market moon. I know because Grandmother had sent me up to the high pasture with the last of the goats, an errand she invented, I think now, because she knew. Elders always know. The wind had been wrong for three days. The ravens would not roost in our valley—they circled and screamed in a tongue even older than ours, and Grandmother stood in the doorway listening to them with her face like carved wood.*

*From the pasture I saw the lights. Not torches—Veilbreakers do not carry torches, they carry lamps with white glass, lamps that burn without heat, because fire is honest and they are not. The lights came down into the valley from three sides at once, quiet as frost. And then the shouting started, and then the screaming, and then the burning.*

Paloma's hands had begun to tremble in her lap. She pressed them flat against her knees. Ines set down the mug and leaned forward, slowly, the way one leans toward a wounded animal—not to touch, just to be near.

*They did not rob us,* Paloma said. *Robbers want things. They wanted only one thing: the voices. They went house to house and they made people speak—made them recite, made them sing, made the children say the blessing-words over the bread—and those who could speak the Speech truly, they took away in wagons with covered tops. Those who could not, they left standing in the square, ash falling on them like terrible snow. My mother could speak. She pushed me behind the well-house and put her shawl over my head and told me to count the stones until the counting was done. I counted four hundred and six stones. When I looked up she was gone, and the well-house was burning, and the white lamps were moving toward the elder house where Grandmother kept the tablets.*

*My grandmother did not run.* Here Paloma's voice changed—it dropped lower, steadier, the way a river goes deep and calm right before the fall. *I saw her from the pasture ridge. She walked out into the square with her arms open and she began to speak the Long Blessing, the one that takes an hour, the one that names every living thing in the valley and asks the Veil to hold. She got through the mountains and the rivers and the pines. The Veilbreaker captain—a woman with a scarred lip and kind eyes, which is worse than cruel eyes, cruel eyes tell you the truth—ordered her to stop. Grandmother did not stop. She got through the beasts of the field and the birds of the air. They struck her with a stave of black metal and the words came out of her broken, half-said, and the ground under the square groaned. I heard it. Everyone heard it. Something far below us turned over in its sleep, and the well water went black for a day afterward.*

*Then they took her too. And the captain stood in the middle of our burning sanctuary and she picked up Grandmother's fallen shawl, folded it neatly, and set it on the well rim like a flag of conquest. Like she was sorry. Sorry is the cruelest word there is.*

* * *

The fire had settled to embers. Somewhere in the loft above, a raven had arrived at the smoke-hole and sat there in silence, head cocked, listening. Paloma noticed it and drew strength from it—ravens were witnesses; ravens remembered; if a raven was listening, then nothing she said here would ever be entirely lost.

Ines had risen at some point without seeming to move and now crouched a few feet away, level with the child, her elbows on her knees. Up close her face was a map of years lived alone: lines like game trails running everywhere and meeting nowhere. Her lips were parted. She was breathing carefully, deliberately, the way people breathe when they are keeping themselves from making noise.

*Why are you alive?* she asked, and it was almost a whisper, and she said it in the Old Speech—clumsy, accented, but real. Paloma's heart cracked a little wider, and warmer. The woman could speak some of it. Had always been able to. Of course she could. That was why.

*Because of the whistle,* Paloma said, and lifted the cord from around her neck. The broken stone whistle lay in her palm, split down its length, its mouthpiece chipped away. *It was mine, but it was made by hands much older than mine, from Threshold-stone, and it does not make sound the way other whistles do. It makes the Sound underneath sounds. When I blew it, everything with ears turned toward me. When the wagons rolled out of the valley at dawn, I followed them to the tree line, and Grandmother's friend the fox found me there—weeping, lost, stupid with grief. And the fox put its muzzle against my ear and gave me the message. The message was already waiting, do you understand? She had sent it ahead of her, days ago, when the wind first went wrong. She knew what was coming and she prepared my road while she still could.*

*The message was this:* Paloma closed her eyes and repeated it exactly, the way the fox had given it, the way it had been given to the fox, word for word down a chain of ears older than any kingdom:

*Go south along the spine of the world. Trust no roof that has a chimney smoking at dawn. Cross the water where the willows kneel. And find the one who talks to beasts — the woman alone in the Thornfell, whose name the animals know and men do not. Give her your voice. Never stop talking. Not for fear, not for kindness, not for sleep. The moment you fall silent longer than a day and a night, the seam begins to unravel, and what is beneath the mountains will feel it pulling loose, and it will rise to see what happened to its stitching.*

Paloma opened her eyes. Ines had gone the color of old tallow.

*A day and a night,* the woman said in her rough human tongue, and then, catching herself, in the Speech: *How long since the village? How long have you been walking?*

*Thirty-nine days,* Paloma said. *I spoke to myself the whole way. To the trees, to the stones, to the ghosts of my goats. I sang the blessing over bread I didn't have. Twice I slept more than half a day and woke with my heart hammering because I felt it—I felt the seam pull. Like a thread dragging across the back of the world. Like something down there rolling toward the light of a keyhole.* She swallowed. *On the thirty-ninth night I reached your valley, and I saw the hawk circling your chimney, and I heard it say your name—it doesn't have a shape in the human tongue, it sounds like wind through a specific crack in a specific rock—and I knew I had found you. And then I was so tired. And I knocked. And I couldn't anymore. I fell silent for nearly two days, and I am sorry, I am so sorry, I tried, but my body closed like a fist—*

*Two days,* Ines breathed. *Child. Two days of silence. What did you feel?*

And Paloma told her, because there was no longer any reason not to tell her anything at all.

*I felt the mountain dream loudly. All night, under the snow, under the rock, something vast shifted its weight and murmured in its sleep, and every beast in the valley stood facing the same direction at midnight, facing down, facing the deep places. Your goats would not bed down. The ravens flew circles till dawn. And I felt a word leave me—a small one, a stitch-word, the word for thanking the rain—and I could not call it back. It went down into the stone like water into sand. And I don't think it's coming back.*

Silence filled the cabin, but it was a different silence now—not the silence of a mute child and a mute woman circling each other across a table, but the loaded silence after a bell has been struck. Even the raven in the smoke-hole made no sound. Even the embers seemed to hush their popping, listening.

* * *

Ines stood. She walked to the window, pushed aside the hide curtain, and looked out at her mountains—at Thornfell, her fortress, her hiding place, twenty years of deliberate invisibility—and Paloma watched her shoulders change. Watched the wariness rearrange itself into something harder and sadder and, impossibly, taller.

When she turned around, her jaw was set the way Paloma's grandmother's jaw used to set before festivals, before the Long Blessing, before work that mattered.

*Tell me about the tablets,* Ines said in the Speech, and her accent was better now, warming, loosening, as if the language were a room in her that had been locked for decades and was being opened shutter by shutter. *Your grandmother's tablets. Did the Veilbreakers take all of them?*

*Not all.* Paloma's heart began, very quietly, to beat faster—not with fear this time. With something that had lain dormant in her since the well-house, since the four hundred and six stones: purpose, waking stiffly, like a creature coming out of winter. *There were three fragments of the First Covenant. Two the elders kept. One—the oldest, the Threshold fragment—was never kept anywhere. It was given away, generation to generation, to whoever among the children showed the deepest ear for the Speech. It was hidden in the giving. Nobody could take it by burning a house, because it wasn't in the house. It was in a person.*

Ines went very still. The firelight moved on her face.

*Grandmother gave it to me the morning of the market moon,* Paloma said. *She taught me eleven lines of it and made me swear to teach myself the rest by speaking them aloud every day, whether or not anyone listened. Whether or not I understood them. Especially when I did not understand them. She said understanding would come later, the way spring comes later than the thaw. She said the words would keep me alive and keep something else asleep, both at once, and that someday I would stand in front of a stone that had been waiting for those words since before there were kingdoms, and on that day I would finally understand what I have been carrying.*

She touched her own chest, over her heart, where the broken whistle hung.

*What am I carrying?* she asked Ines—in the Speech, in the old tongue, in a child's clear voice that shook only a little. *You know things. You talk to hawks and your name is a crack in a rock. Tell me what the words in me are for. Tell me why they burned my home to get at voices. Tell me what happens if I ever stop talking for good.*

Outside, the morning wind came down off the peaks and moved through the pines, and beneath it—faint, distant, rising from somewhere far below the snow and the stone and the roots of the world—came a sound that neither of them could mistake. A long, slow exhalation. The sigh of something immense turning in its sleep, dreaming, perhaps, of doors.

The raven on the smoke-hole spoke then, one flat croak of warning, and Ines crossed the room in three strides and blew out the lamp that wasn't lit and stood in the bright cold window-light with her hand on her knife, listening to a road only she could see.

*Riders,* she said, in the human tongue, and her voice had gone flat and hard as slate. *Three of them, coming up the valley road. Wearing white-lamped lanterns at their belts.*

Paloma's blood turned to snowmelt. She knew that light. She had watched it eat her whole world.

Ines looked back at her over her shoulder, and her eyes were no longer wide with wonder. They were narrow with decision, already made, already finished, the way a mountain decides things.

*Get your boots on, little Voice,* she said. *You're going to have to keep talking while we run.*
