## 6. The Beast Court at Hollow Ford

They walked north for two days before Ines allowed herself to believe the ravens' directions.

Hollow Ford was not a place one found so much as a place that permitted itself to be found. It lay in a fold of the mountains where the river split around a spine of black stone and reassembled itself on the far side, quieter than it had any right to be. The trees there grew wrong—too straight, too old, their roots gripping the banks like fingers holding down a page. Ines had been three times in her twenty years, each time summoned rather than invited, each time with her heart hammering like a trapped bird and her mouth full of words she did not dare waste.

This time she brought a child who could not stop speaking.

"Slowly," Ines murmured, for the tenth time since dawn. "The court will hear you whether you whisper or shout. Save your voice."

Paloma looked up at her with those gray eyes that seemed to hold weather, and answered in the Old Speech—a soft run of syllables that made the birches along the path shiver though there was no wind. Ines caught perhaps one word in five. *Threshold.* *Below.* A word she thought might mean *hungry*, which at least was universal.

"I know. I'm hungry too." She dug the last of the smoked trout from her pack and split it. The girl ate like a small wolf, hunched over her portion, guarding it, and something in the sight twisted an old ache loose in Ines's chest. Twenty years alone made a person forget that watching someone eat could hurt.

The ravens had gone ahead at first light—all six of them, lifting off the cabin's eaves in a ragged black ribbon—and Ines had not needed their speech to understand the summons. The court had heard about the child. Of course it had. The mountain heard everything now; the mountain had been listening ever since Paloma arrived, its silence gone strange and attentive, like a sickroom.

*The door below is loud,* the eldest raven had said before they left. *Come before it grows louder.*

* * *

They reached the ford at dusk.

Ines felt the change before she saw anything: the hush deepening, the river's noise flattening as if a hand had been laid over it. Paloma stopped mid-sentence—the first time Ines had known her to stop—and went rigid, her small hand finding Ines's sleeve and gripping it.

"They're here," Ines said quietly. "Don't be afraid. Whatever happens, don't run. Running is an answer, and it's the wrong one."

She led the girl down through the alder thicket to the gravel bar where the black stone spine rose from the splitting water. And there they were.

Wolves first—seven of them, arranged in a crescent on the near bank, still as carvings, their eyes catching the last light like coins at the bottom of a well. Behind them, on the higher ground among the alders, stood the elk: nine great gray-brown shapes with antlers like winter branches, motionless in a way no living thing should manage. And above, filling every branch of every tree along both banks, the ravens—hundreds of them, silent, which was the most unnatural thing of all. A wood full of ravens makes a sound like the world arguing with itself. This wood said nothing.

Ines knelt, slowly, and pressed her palm flat to the cold gravel. It was the old courtesy. *I come low. I come unarmed. I ask to be heard.*

A wolf detached from the crescent. Not the largest—that honor belonged to a grizzled male at the crescent's center—but a lean gray female with a torn ear and eyes the color of wet slate. She crossed the gravel without hurry and stopped an arm's length away, close enough that Ines could smell the pine and blood-smell of her coat.

*You bring the loud thing,* the wolf said. Her mind-voice was dry as bark, unimpressed.

"I bring a child," Ines answered aloud, because before the court one spoke in one's own throat or not at all. "Her name is Paloma."

*We know what she is called.* The wolf's gaze slid past Ines to the girl. *The ravens named her before your fire was cold. We know also what she carries. The whole mountain knows. The fish know. The worms under the stones know.* Her lip lifted slightly from her teeth—not quite a snarl, not quite anything else. *The door below has not been this loud since my grandmother's grandmother howled at the breaking of the first seal.*

Paloma stepped forward.

Ines's hand shot out to hold her back and missed. The girl walked past her, small boots crunching on gravel, and planted herself between the wolf and the water, chin up, and began to speak.

It poured out of her—the Old Speech, rapid and ringing, syllables that seemed to bend the air. The ravens shifted on their branches in a single ripple, like wind moving through wheat. The elk lowered their great heads. And beneath everything, faint but unmistakable, Ines felt the ground hum, a vibration rising up through her knees, as if the mountain itself were leaning in to listen.

She caught fragments. *Seal.* *Burned.* *Sanctuary.* A word repeated again and again that she did not know but that made the wolves' ears flatten each time it came, a word like a struck bell.

When Paloma finally stopped, breathless, the silence afterward had weight. Then the torn-eared wolf did something Ines had never seen a wolf do before the court.

She sat down.

*Last Voice,* she said, and the words carried—not just to Ines but outward, taken up by the ravens and passed bank to bank, tree to tree, until the whole darkening wood held the phrase like a cupped flame. *Last Voice. So the court names her.*

The elk stamped once, all together, a sound like a door closing.

"What does that mean?" Ines asked. Her voice came out smaller than she intended. "What is she the last voice *of*?"

The old male wolf rose from the crescent's center and came forward at last, stiff in the shoulder, gray-muzzled, ancient. When he spoke, his mind-voice was deep enough that Ines felt it in her sternum.

*Before your kind built its first wall, there was a pact. You have heard its name in half-remembered songs. The world below the mountains and the world above were not always divided. There was a door, and keepers of the door, and the keepers spoke the Old Speech so that both sides could hear and be bound.* He paused, and his good ear twitched toward Paloma. *Your Crown burned the sanctuaries. Your Veilbreakers hunted the speakers. And now one child remains who carries the tongue of the covenant, and her words are pulling at a knot that has held for a thousand years. Every sentence she speaks loosens it further. Every day she lives untaught, the door strains.*

"So we should teach her," Ines said. "Quickly. Before—"

*Before,* the old wolf agreed, *or after. The court has debated it since the ravens first cried your name across three valleys.* His eyes, pale gold, fixed on Ines with terrible patience. *But hear the judgment first, Speaker-to-beasts, before you argue terms.*

He turned back to Paloma and inclined his head—an inch, no more, but from him it was a bow that would have made kings weep.

*The court tests her now. Stand aside.*

* * *

What followed, Ines would remember for the rest of her life, in the way one remembers a fever.

The ravens came down first. They did not attack—they swirled around the child in a spinning column, hundreds of wings, a black tornado with Paloma at its eye, and through the roar of feathers Ines heard them speaking all at once, a hundred voices layered over a hundred questions in the Old Speech, fast and overlapping, a storm of language designed to drown. Any ordinary mind would have shattered. Ines felt the edges of it even standing outside the column, a pressure like deep water.

Inside, Paloma stood with her arms at her sides and answered.

Not all of it. Not even most of it. But she answered—voice rising clear through the feather-storm, steady, refusing to be silenced, and where her answers landed the nearest birds fell quiet, then quieted the ones beside them, the storm unraveling from within. In less time than Ines could have counted a hundred heartbeats, the ravens settled onto the gravel around the girl in a ring, heads cocked, and one of them—the eldest, the one who had first warned Ines of soldiers on the ridge road—hopped forward and touched its beak, gently, to the broken stone whistle still hanging at Paloma's neck.

Then the river tested her.

The water rose. Ines saw it happen and did not believe it: the ford's current gathering itself against the black stone spine, climbing, forming a shape—a long-necked, finned suggestion of a creature older than the river it lived in, made of spray and shadow. It loomed over the child and roared, a soundless roaring that Ines felt in her teeth, a question asked in pressure and cold.

Paloma reached out and put her hand into the water-shape's crest.

And laughed.

It was the first time Ines had heard her laugh. It was a small, cracked, astonishing sound, eight years old and starved for joy, and the river-creature shivered apart into ordinary spray, and the ford ran quiet again, and somewhere in the alders the elk breathed out all together like a congregation at the end of a hard sermon.

The old wolf spoke last.

*She hears us all,* he said. *Not as you hear us, beast-speaker—you, who learned our tongues like a merchant learns coin. She hears us as the first keepers heard, without effort, without border. The Old Speech is not learned. It is remembered. She remembers what your whole kind forgot.* He let that settle, and then his voice hardened. *Therefore the court declares her the Last Voice, keeper of the covenant's tongue, and claims for her the protection of every claw and wing under Thornfell. No Veilbreaker hand shall touch her while we draw breath.*

Relief hit Ines so hard she nearly sagged. An escort. Wolves and ravens and elk between Paloma and Serane Voss's hunters—

"But," she said, because the old wolf's tone had not softened, and twenty years of reading beasts had taught her that a declaration with a *therefore* was never finished.

*But,* the wolf agreed, grim as a landslide, *the court will not escort her to the Threshold.*

The word dropped into Ines's stomach like a stone into a well.

"The ravens said—" she began.

*The ravens say many things. Ravens are gossips with wings.* A rumble moved through the crescent of wolves—not quite laughter, not quite censure. *Hear me, Ines Winterbourne. The covenant is not ours to mend. Long ago, when the door was first sealed, it was human hands that broke the trust—your kings, your hunger, your fear of what lived below. The debt was written in your tongue and laid upon your kind. If beasts reforged it, the mending would not hold, for the pact would remember that the debtors did not pay.* His pale eyes held hers. *The Last Voice may speak the words. But the words require a Speaker—one who gives freely, of their own will, what cannot be taken. That is the old law. That is the only law.*

"And if no Speaker comes?"

*Then the Veil fails. Everything below dies screaming, and everything above learns why the dark was kept behind a door.* He said it without drama, the way one reports weather, and somehow that was worse.

Ines looked at Paloma. The girl was watching her, gray-eyed, unblinking, and Ines realized with a lurch that the child had understood every word—not the wolf's mind-voice, perhaps, but the shape of it, the weight. Children always understood the weight.

"She's eight years old," Ines said, and hated how her voice shook. "She watched her village burn. She walked out of the ashes alone and found my door in a storm. She has carried this alone since before I knew her name." The anger came up in her now, hot and unexpected, twenty years of hoarded solitude igniting all at once. "You sit here in your parliament older than kingdoms and you declare her, you test her, you *name* her—and then you hand her to me like a parcel and wash your paws of the road?"

The old wolf did not flinch. *Yes.*

"Why?"

*Because mercy that costs the giver nothing is not mercy. It is decoration.* He stepped closer, and this close Ines could see the frost of age around his muzzle, the old scars mapping his flanks like a history of the mountain itself. *You think we do not care, beast-speaker? We have cared for a thousand years. We cared when your mother was taken—we sent the foxes to warn her, and she would not run, because she too believed the debt was hers. We cared when the sanctuaries burned; our ravens pulled three children from the smoke, and two of them died anyway, because humans hunt what ravens save.* His voice dropped, and for a moment it was not hard at all. *The court refuses escort because escort would make this ours to fail. It is not ours. It was never ours. It is yours, Ines Winterbourne, daughter of Sera Winterbourne, who stood where you stand and paid what the stone asked. The question is whether you are your mother's daughter or merely her survivor.*

The gravel bar was very quiet. The river ran. High above, the first stars were pricking through the blue-black, and the ravens sat in their thousands like a congregation waiting for the verdict.

Ines knelt, slowly, in front of Paloma. Up close the girl looked impossibly small—smoke-dark circles under her eyes, a scratch healing on her chin, the broken whistle glinting at her throat. Eight years old. The last voice of a thousand-year covenant, wearing boots two sizes too big.

"They won't come with us," Ines told her, in plain human speech, because some things deserved plainness. "To the place we have to go. It has to be you and me."

Paloma considered this with the grave attention she gave everything. Then she reached out and took Ines's hand—hers small and rough, chapped from the cold—and spoke a single line of the Old Speech, soft, questioning.

Ines didn't know the words. But she knew the shape of a question asked when someone is afraid, because she had spent twenty years not asking it herself.

"Yes," she said. "Together. I promise."

And because promises made before the court were binding in ways human law had never managed, she turned back to the old wolf and spoke loudly enough for every ear on the bar.

"I accept the burden. Alone, if alone is what the law demands. I'll take her to the First Threshold. I'll find what the stone asks for, and I'll pay it." Her jaw tightened. "But hear this too, old one: I'm not doing it for your law, or the Crown's, or my dead mother's ghost. I'm doing it because a burned child walked through a storm to my door, and somebody has to finish walking her out of it."

For a long moment the wolf simply looked at her. Then he did the second unprecedented thing of the evening: he laughed, a low rolling sound that set the whole crescent's tails moving.

*Good,* he said. *Anger keeps better than obedience on a long road.* He turned, and the crescent of wolves rose with him, and the elk swung their antlered heads toward the darkening passes. *One gift before you go, since gifts are free tonight. The ravens will watch. They will not help—watching is not helping, remember that when you curse them—but what they see, the mountain sees. And the mountain, beast-speaker, has begun to hope. That is more dangerous than despair. Despair sits still. Hope moves.*

He padded to the edge of the gravel and paused, his torn-eared lieutenant falling in beside him.

*Go south through the smuggler roads. Voss's net is closing on the valley already—our foxes counted her fires last night, and there were more fires than stars.* His gaze flicked once to Paloma, and something almost gentle passed through it. *Keep her talking, Ines Winterbourne. Every word she speaks holds the door another hour. The day she falls silent is the day you will wish you had never taught her caution.*

Then the court dissolved the way weather dissolves—wolves flowing into the treeline, elk fading among the alders, the ravens going up all at once in a black spiral that scattered into ordinary night birds, and the ford was only a ford again, a river splitting around a stone, and the hush lifted, and the world resumed its usual indifferent noise.

Ines stood a moment longer with Paloma's hand in hers, looking south, where beyond the black ridgelines the lowlands smoldered faintly with the lights of the enemy.

"Well," she said to the child, to the mountain, to no one. "Alone, then."

Beside her, Paloma began, very softly, to talk—to herself, to the dark, to the door below—in a language older than any kingdom, keeping the world shut with every word.

And Ines tightened her grip on the small hand and started walking.
