## 7. Roads That Lie

The smuggler roads of Thornfell were not roads at all, not in the way the Crown understood roads. They had no surveyors' marks, no milestone stones, no names cut into granite by some clerk who had never felt frost in his boots. They were habits. Old ones. A deer trail that widened each autumn because Tam's people drove goats along it; a dry creek bed that only held water three weeks out of the year and was therefore safe for exactly those three weeks; a shelf of limestone beneath an overhang where no snow settled because the mountain breathed warm air through a crack nobody had ever bothered to find.

Ines had learned them the way she'd learned everything that mattered—by listening. The foxes knew which passes the soldiers watched. The choughs counted campfires from the thermals and reported them in their flat, gossiping voices. Even the marmots, fat and self-important, kept a running tally of boots on the high trails, because boots meant dogs, and dogs meant trouble for everyone with a burrow.

For twenty years this knowledge had been her livelihood and her armor. Now it was a child's only shield, and Ines felt the weight of that every time Paloma stumbled on the scree and Ines caught her elbow without looking, the way you catch something you have already decided never to drop.

They were four days south of Hollow Ford when the hawk found them.

She came down through the pines in a long slanting dive, wings folded, and pulled up short of Ines's shoulder with a shriek that needed no translation: *smoke, many men, the low road, moving fast.*

"How many?" Ines asked, keeping her voice low even though they were alone under the trees. Alone except for Paloma, who was watching the hawk with an expression Ines had come to dread—a kind of recognition, as if the bird were an old acquaintance greeting a princess in exile.

The hawk ruffled her feathers. *More than the fingers of both hands. Less than the stars.* Hawks were precise about prey and vague about everything else.

"Which way are they angled?"

*Like a net,* the hawk said, and spread one wing to show it—sweeping, closing, patient. *Like men who have been told where to stand.*

Ines stood very still among the pines. Told where to stand. That was the phrase that mattered, and it sat in her stomach like a swallowed stone. Soldiers blundered; that was normal, that was almost endearing. But a net meant information, and information about these roads meant someone had been mapping her movements—the goat runs, the seasonal shifts, the creek beds—and feeding the map to the Crown.

She thought of Tam, and pushed the thought away before it could finish forming.

"We go east," she said aloud. "The Widow's Stair."

Paloma tugged her sleeve. The girl didn't speak—not in any tongue Ines knew—but she had ways of asking questions: a tilt of the head, a finger pointed, eyebrows raised in silent arithmetic. She pointed east, then made a face like she'd bitten something sour.

"Yes," Ines said. "It's ugly. Ugly is good right now."

The Widow's Stair was a series of switchbacks up a cliff face that most maps marked as unclimbable, because most maps were drawn by men who measured with chains and sightlines rather than with the memory of their own hands. It was ugly. It was also invisible from below, screened by a curtain of birch that grew sideways out of the rock, and by the time anyone thought to look up, Ines intended to be over the shoulder of the mountain and gone.

Paloma climbed like a goat, which Ines supposed should not have surprised her. The girl was eight, maybe nine, small for it, all elbows and dark eyes, with hair that hadn't been cut since whatever village had burned. She climbed without complaint, without sound, and that silence was the strangest thing about her—stranger than the broken stone whistle she carried knotted in a scrap of cloth, stranger than the way wolves dipped their heads to her across a valley. Most children Ines had met filled silence the way water fills a hole. Paloma simply was it.

On the third switchback, Ines heard it.

Not with her ears—with the older listening, the one that lived somewhere behind her sternum. A thread of unease running through the birdsong above them, thin as wire. The choughs had gone quiet on the eastern ridge, and choughs did not go quiet unless something with wings bigger than theirs was passing, or something with boots.

She pressed herself against the cliff and pulled Paloma in beside her, one hand flat over the girl's mouth out of pure reflex, though Paloma had never once made a sound that wasn't asked for.

Through the birch screen, far below on the low road, came the column.

Ines had seen Crown soldiers before—tax collectors' escorts, mostly, miserable men in grey wool who hated the mountains and were hated back by every living thing in them. These were different. These moved like the hawk's description: like a net being drawn. No stragglers, no camp noise, no singing. Grey-and-white livery she didn't recognize, and at the head of them, riding a mountain-bred mare that clearly despised her, a woman in a black half-cloak with silver braid at the collar.

Even at that distance, Ines felt the woman's attention like a physical touch. The officer reined in, turned her face upward toward the cliff—toward the birch screen, toward the exact stretch of rock where two figures stood motionless—and smiled.

It was too far to see the smile properly. Ines saw it anyway. Some smiles carry.

Then the column moved on, south along the low road, unhurried, and the choughs resumed their gossiping overhead, and Ines discovered she had been holding her breath long enough to make her vision spark.

Paloma pulled Ines's hand gently away from her mouth and looked up at her. Then she did something she had done only twice before: she reached up and touched Ines's cheek, once, with cold fingers, and let her hand fall.

*You know her,* the touch said, though Ines couldn't have explained how she knew that was what it said.

"No," Ines whispered. "But I think she knows me."

* * *

They crossed the mountain's shoulder that night under a sky scoured clean by wind, and came down into a country Ines loved with the specific love one reserves for places that have saved your life repeatedly: the Liar Roads.

That was what Tam's people called them, anyway. Officially they were nothing. Unofficially they were a lattice of trails through the southern foothills that rearranged themselves twice a year—not by magic, though newcomers always assumed magic, but by something more reliable: mud, meltwater, deadfall, and the stubborn annual migrations of everyone who used them. A route that was open in June would be a river in May and a wall of alder by September. The smugglers didn't fight this; they memorized it, season by season, generation by generation, until the whole network lived in perhaps forty heads on the entire continent, and half of those belonged to Tam Verrick.

Ines knew maybe a quarter of it. Enough. For now.

"The trick," she told Paloma as they walked, because talking to the girl—even wordless, even unanswered—had become a habit she neither examined nor resisted, "is that there is no trick. You just have to be willing to look stupid. Every sensible road leads somewhere a soldier can follow. So we take the stupid ones."

Paloma considered this gravely, then nodded once, the nod of a co-conspirator accepting terms.

They took the stupid ones. A sheep track that ended at a shepherd's ruined bothy and continued, impossibly, through its collapsed back wall into a hollow full of hazel. A ford across a stream so shallow it barely wet the ankles, chosen not for depth but because the banks on either side were soft peat that recorded every boot print for a week—they crossed midstream on exposed stones instead, and Ines spent ten minutes afterward brushing their tracks off the stones with a pine bough. A night march along a ridge whose only virtue was that the wind screamed across it constantly, drowning footsteps and erasing nerve.

The beasts helped where they could. A family of badgers reported the state of a bridge. A sleepy owl noted that three men were drinking at the ferry inn and had been drinking since noon, which meant the ferry crossing was safe until morning. Foxes left sign for them like courteous hosts, scat and prints arranged along the safer forks as if marking a path for honored guests.

But the net kept tightening anyway. That was the thing Ines couldn't shake. On the fifth day, a woodcutter's hut they'd planned to shelter in had been visited—recently, politely, by someone who'd paid for eggs and asked questions about travelers. On the sixth day, they doubled back along their own trail and found a paper nailed to a tree at child height, weatherproofed with wax, bearing the Crown's seal and a single line of print:

**LOST CHILD. REWARD FOR SAFE RETURN. SHE IS NOT IN TROUBLE.**

Below it, in smaller type: *Report sightings to Captain S. Voss, Veilbreaker Company.*

"She is not in trouble," Ines read aloud, and heard how the words curdled. "Well. Someone has been to school."

Paloma read it too—or looked at it, at least, for a long moment, her jaw tight. Then she spat on the ground beside the tree, a small, precise, utterly unchildlike gesture, and walked on.

* * *

They were seven days out of Hollow Ford when the woman stopped pretending.

Ines knew it first from the ravens, who came to her at dusk in a bare field of stubble where they'd paused to eat stolen turnips, and delivered their news in the flat, formal cadence they used for things they considered important.

*A rider comes alone,* the eldest raven said. *One horse, one woman, no sword drawn. She carries bread and salt and a paper with a ribbon on it. She says she comes under parley.*

"Parley," Ines repeated. "With whom?"

*With the one who talks to beasts,* the raven said, and hopped closer, head cocked, one eye fixed on her with terrible interest. *Those are the words she used. She learned them from someone who learned them from a burning house.*

The field went very still around Ines. The turnip in her hand suddenly weighed nothing at all.

From a burning house. From Paloma's village, then—from the sanctuary of covenant-keepers, from the people who had known enough to send a child to find "the one who talks to beasts." Which meant the Veilbreakers had survivors, or prisoners, or papers, or all three, and Captain Serane Voss had done her homework with a thoroughness that made Ines's skin crawl.

She could refuse the parley. Run now, in the dusk, lose the rider in the hills. But the rider was alone, and alone meant confident, and confident meant the net was already closed everywhere else. Running from a closed net just meant choosing which knot to die in.

Besides—and this was the thought Ines refused to examine too closely—she wanted to see the woman's face.

"Where?" she asked the raven.

*The millstone field. Where the old mill burned and only the stones remain. She will wait one hour.*

Ines left Paloma hidden in a drainage culvert with strict instructions—in gesture, in the few words of trade-speech the girl seemed to half-understand—to stay put, to stay silent, to run east if Ines wasn't back by moonrise. Paloma took her hands and squeezed them hard, once, and Ines pretended her own hands weren't shaking.

The millstone field was exactly as grim as its history demanded: two great circular stones lying in the weeds like fallen moons, the foundation walls of the old mill ankle-high and moss-eaten, the stream that had once turned the wheel now diverted and sullen. The rider was already there, standing beside her grazing mare, and she had built a small fire, which was either arrogance or invitation, and knowing what Ines already knew, probably both.

Captain Serane Voss was younger than Ines expected. That was the first obscenity of her. Thirty, perhaps thirty-five, with a broad, open face built for smiling, brown hair cropped sensibly short, and the easy stance of someone who had grown up around horses and never learned to fear anything that couldn't kick. Her grey-and-white uniform was immaculate in a way that suggested she changed it precisely to unsettle people who expected monsters to look like monsters.

"Ines Winterbourne," the captain said warmly, as if greeting a cousin at a wedding. "Goat-runner, beast-speaker, ghost of the high passes. Do you know, I've been chasing rumors of you for two years? Everyone said you didn't exist. I'm delighted to be proved wrong." She gestured at the fire. "Sit. I brought bread. Actual bread, not army biscuit—I raided a bakery in Millbrook this morning. I promise you it's the best thing you'll eat this month."

Ines stayed standing, out of arm's reach, downwind. "You know my name."

"I know lots of names." Voss crouched by the fire and broke the loaf, and the smell of it—crust, wheat, warmth—drifted over deliberately. "Names are my trade. Here's mine, freely given: Serane Voss, Captain, Veilbreaker Company, Third Charter. And here's what I want, equally freely given: I want you to give me the girl, and I want to give you your life back."

"My life," Ines said, "isn't lost."

"Isn't it?" Voss looked up, and her smile was gentle, genuinely gentle, which was the second obscenity of her. "Twenty years in the mountains, Ines. Twenty years talking to goats and crows because people—" she caught herself, adjusted, "—because the world made people difficult. I've read what the Crown has on you. It's thin. A few smuggling charges, decades old. Do you know what amnesty means? It means all of it burns. Clean slate. Land grant, even—the lowlands, good soil, a cottage with a door that locks. You'd never have to hide again. Never have to run. All you have to do is walk away from one child who isn't yours, was never yours, and who is currently sitting in a ditch wondering if you're coming back."

The last sentence landed like a hook behind the breastbone. Ines kept her face still. "You know where she is."

"I know where you'd leave her. Culvert, east field, line of sight to the millstones. I've read your file, Ines; I've also read you. You're careful, not cruel. You wouldn't take her into a parley." Voss shrugged, easy as shrugging off a cloak. "I could have taken her an hour ago. I didn't. That should tell you something about how I work. I don't burn sanctuaries. I don't put children in cells. I want the Veil *understood*—controlled, studied, kept safe—before it tears open and drowns half the valleys in things out of the old stories. That girl is the key to the biggest door in the world, and she's eight years old and carrying it through winter with no shoes worth the name. You call that protecting her? Because I call it negligence with a halo."

And here was the trap, Ines thought distantly, watching steam rise off the shared fire: the trap wasn't the threat. There had been no threat. The trap was that Voss was *right about some of it*. Paloma was exhausted. Paloma's feet were cracked. Paloma flinched at smoke. Every league south was a league of danger bought with a child's small body, and here was a woman offering warm bread and locked doors and an ending to the running—

"You'd keep her safe," Ines said slowly. "In a Crown facility. Under Crown study."

"Under Crown *care*."

"They burned her village."

"Some people in my company burned her village," Voss said, and for one flickering instant something moved behind the charm—something older and rawer, quickly shuttered. "I won't pretend otherwise. I'll also tell you that the man who gave that order answers to me now, and that the Third Charter exists specifically because the First Company were animals. Reform, Ines. It's slow and it's ugly and it's the only thing that works. Give me the girl and I will spend the rest of my career making sure what happened to her village never happens again. Keep her, and we'll both watch the Veil fail, and neither of us will be able to protect anyone from what comes through."

The fire crackled. Somewhere a curlew called across the darkening fields, long and falling, like grief practicing.

And Ines—God help her—wavered. Not because she believed in the Crown. She had twenty years of reasons not to believe in the Crown. She wavered because she was tired, bone-tired, tired in the place where mothers get tired, though she was no one's mother, and because the bread smelled like civilization and the offer sounded like mercy and because Serane Voss had the rare, terrible gift of meaning at least half of what she said.

She was opening her mouth—she would remember this forever, that she was actually opening her mouth, forming the shape of some negotiated half-yes—when the wind shifted.

And on the wind, faint, from the direction of the east field, from the drainage culvert where a silent child waited in the dark:

Humming.

A lullaby. Four notes rising, three notes falling, a little hitch in the middle where the melody bent like a branch under snow. Ines's whole body went cold and then hot. She knew that song. She had known it before she knew her own name. Her grandmother had sung it over the cookfire in a dialect nobody else in the valley spoke, patting the blanket down over Ines's shoulders, saying *sleep, little wren, the mountain holds you, sleep, little wren, the door below is kind*—

Her grandmother, who had died whispering Old Speech into Ines's hair while Crown soldiers drank her stew in the next room.

The same song. Note for note. From a child born in a sanctuary village a hundred leagues away, who had never met Ines's grandmother, who could not possibly have known—

Unless the song was older than both of them. Unless it had come down through covenant-keeper lines like blood, like a birthmark, like a key passed hand to hand in the dark. Unless everything Voss had just offered—the files, the charters, the reform, the *control*—was a stranger's argument against a truth written in a lullaby.

Voss had heard it too. Ines watched the captain's head turn toward the east field, watched the warmth drain out of her face by degrees, replaced by something hungry and calculating and, worst of all, sad.

"That song," Voss said quietly. "Where does she know that song?"

"Same place I do," Ines said, and heard her own voice come out steady at last, iron poured into the wavering shape. "From the people you burned."

She stepped back from the fire. "Tell your company the roads lie, Captain. Tell them the goat-witch is going south with the Last Voice, and every mile they close, we'll be a mile they haven't thought of yet. And tell whoever wrote *she is not in trouble*—" Ines picked up the wax-sealed notice she'd torn from the tree that morning and let it fall into the fire, where it curled and blackened like a dying moth. "—that trouble is the only honest thing anyone's offered that child in weeks."

Voss didn't move to stop her. She stood by her fire with the bread going cold in her hands, and she said, softly, almost to herself: "My mother sang that song too."

Ines stopped walking.

For a heartbeat the field held its breath—the two women, the dying fire, the curlew crying somewhere in the dark, and between them the impossible arithmetic of a shared lullaby: two daughters of two destroyed houses, standing on opposite sides of a burning question.

Then Ines turned and walked into the dark, toward the culvert, toward the small humming voice that was still singing, patiently, faithfully, the song of the door below being kind.

Behind her, she heard Voss mount her horse. Heard the hoofbeats turn—not north, toward the net, but south, unhurried, parallel to their own path.

Following. Waiting. Believing, still, that patience was on her side.

And ahead of Ines, in the culvert, Paloma fell silent mid-verse—which meant, Ines realized with a chill that had nothing to do with the wind, that the girl had heard everything, including the part where Ines had nearly said yes.

There would be a conversation coming. There would be trust to rebuild, and roads that lied, and a captain who sang her mother's songs while sharpening the Crown's knives.

Ines knelt at the edge of the culvert and held out her hand in the dark.

"Come on, little wren," she said, using the old words without deciding to. "The mountain holds us. Let's not make it hold us here."

Paloma took her hand—and did not let go for eleven miles.
