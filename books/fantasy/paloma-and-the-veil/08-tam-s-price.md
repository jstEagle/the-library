## 8. Tam's Price

The river knew Tam Verrin before he knew himself. It had carried his father's flatboats, and his grandfather's, and it had taught him the first lesson of every smuggler's life: water does not lie, but it will carry liars gladly, so long as they pay the toll in caution.

He paid it now, poling the shallow barge up the Graywater with the lantern hooded to a sliver of amber light, watching the banks for the signal Ines had promised—a knot of red wool tied to an alder branch, high enough that flood season wouldn't take it. He found it a mile past the old ferry stones, exactly where she'd said, and felt the familiar loosening in his chest that meant a plan was still holding.

*She trusts you,* said the small voice at the back of his skull, the one that sounded like his mother. *That's the trouble.*

He poled on.

The cave mouth opened behind a curtain of willow on the river's north bank, wide enough for the barge, tall enough for a man to stand upright in the dark beyond. Tam had used it three times in ten years—twice to hide contraband brandy from the excise men, once to hide himself from a husband with a grievance and a crossbow. It was dry-floored, deep-running, and invisible from the water unless you already knew to look. Which was precisely why it had seemed like such a good idea, four days ago, when Serane Voss had sat across a tavern table from him with her officer's coat unbuttoned and her voice soft as banked coals, and asked him where he would take them.

He tied off the barge, shouldered two sacks of provisions, and went in.

They were waiting by a fire no larger than a cupped hand—Ines Winterbourne crouched with her back to the stone, and the girl beside her, asleep sitting up, her head tipped against Ines's shoulder like a fledgling that had decided this particular branch was home. The sight of it did something to Tam's chest that he did not enjoy. He had known Ines twenty years. He had never once seen her let anything lean on her.

"You're late," Ines said, without looking up.

"River's low. Had to walk the barge through the shallows below the mill." He set down the sacks: flour, salt pork, dried apples, a wheel of hard cheese wrapped in cloth, a jar of honey because the girl had looked at honey in the market the way other children looked at festival drums. "And I had company on the road. Crown riders, heading south. Two patrols."

Ines's head came up. Her eyes were the gray of slate under rain, and they went through him the way her eyes always did—not searching, exactly. Weighing. "Which roads?"

"The ridge road and the ford road both. Moving toward the pass country." He crouched by the fire, held his hands over it though it gave little heat. "They're not patrolling. They're casting. Casting a net."

"And you came upriver anyway."

"I came upriver anyway."

"Through their net."

"Rivers don't have checkpoints," Tam said. "Yet."

Silence, except for the fire's small chewing and the river talking to itself outside. The girl stirred, murmured something in her sleep—that language again, the one that made the hair on Tam's arms stand up when he'd first heard it, words that sounded less like speech than like weather, like geology, like the sound a mountain would make if it could remember being young. Even asleep, half-swallowed, it filled the cave the way water fills a footprint.

"She talks in her sleep," Tam said quietly.

"She never stops," Ines said. There was something in her voice he'd never heard before—not the wariness, not the flinty competence. Something rawer. "Tam. Before we go further. What did it cost you to come?"

The question landed in his stomach like a swallowed hook.

"What makes you think it cost anything?"

"Because you won't look at me. You've been looking at the fire since you walked in. Twenty years, you've lied to magistrates, customs men, wives, and me, and you've always done it looking straight at whoever you were lying to, because you believe a lie told eye-to-eye is half forgiven before it's finished." She turned fully toward him then. "You're not looking at me, Tam."

The fire popped. Outside, the river kept its endless low argument with the stones.

Tam Verrin had faced down excise cutters, bandit crews, a winter avalanche, and one genuinely memorable bear, and none of it had prepared him for the simple arithmetic of a friend asking a fair question. He was, by trade and temperament, a man who believed every problem had a price, and every price could be paid, and the only sin was pretending the ledger balanced when it didn't.

So he did what he had never once done in twenty years of smuggling.

He told the truth.

"They have my crew," he said.

Ines went very still.

"Six of them. Brann, Olla, the twins, young Piet, and Marta—who's nineteen, Ines, she was eleven when she started running my deck lines. Veilbreakers took them at Coldspring nine days ago. No charges. No warrant. Just a cell under the magistrate's house and a message sent to me." His voice came out level, which surprised him; he'd expected it to crack. "Come find me when you're ready to talk about routes, the message said. Signed S.V."

"Sera—"

"Captain Serane Voss." He laughed, and it came out ugly. "You know what the worst part is? She was kind about it. Sat me down, poured me wine, asked after my mother's health. Told me all she wanted was information. Where would Ines Winterbourne run, with a child the Crown wants? What roads? What caves? What friends?" He spread his hands. "I told her I'd think on it. She smiled like she already knew the answer, because she did, because there was only ever one answer, wasn't there? You'd come to me. Everyone comes to me. I'm the road, Ines. That's all I am. And they've put a knife against the road."

The girl shifted in her sleep. Ines eased her down onto a folded blanket with a gentleness that seemed to cost her something, and then she stood, and Tam stood too, because some instincts are older than thought.

"You sold us," Ines said. Not a question.

"Not yet." His jaw worked. "I haven't told her anything. But I'm going to have to, Ines. Or they'll hang them—or worse, they'll just keep them, rot them in that cell until there's nothing left to hang. Six lives. I know how that sounds laid next to one child, I do, and I hate myself for doing the arithmetic at all, but I can't—" His voice finally broke, and he hated that more. "I can't let them die for me. For any of it."

The silence stretched long enough that Tam heard things he'd never noticed in this cave in ten years: water seeping somewhere in the dark, the tick of cooling stone, his own heart going like a mallet on a hull.

When Ines spoke, her voice was quiet and terrible and calm.

"When did you tell her the cave?"

His breath stopped. "I—"

"Don't lie to me now. Not here. Not with her sleeping ten feet away."

"Three days ago," he whispered. "The route. The river. This cave. Everything up to the falls. I drew it for her. On her own paper, in my own hand." He sank down onto a supply crate, all the strength gone out of his legs. "I'm sorry. Gods, Ines, I am sorry in a way I don't have words for. I'll get on my knees if it helps. I'll row you both down to the sea myself. I'll—"

"Stop."

He stopped.

Ines stood looking at him for a long moment, and Tam watched something move across her face that he couldn't read—grief, maybe, or the death of an assumption she'd carried for twenty years. Then she crouched, took a stick from the firewood pile, and began to draw in the ash at the edge of the firelight.

"Get over here," she said. "And listen very carefully, because we're only going to do this once."

* * *

What she drew was the river country as Tam knew it—but changed.

"Voss has your crew," she said, tapping the stick where the magistrate's house would be, down in Coldspring. "Which means Voss believes your loyalty is bought and settled. Which means everything you told her, she believes." The stick moved. "So we make it true—and then we make it useless."

Tam stared. "I don't follow."

"You told her we'd come upriver to this cave. Fine. We *did*. We slept here tonight. Ash, footprints, a cold fire—she'll find all of it, and it will confirm every word you said, and it will buy your crew another day of breathing, because a handler trusts intelligence that verifies." The stick dragged a line south. "But look. The river forks above the falls. Everyone forgets the fork, because the north channel dries to a trickle by midsummer and everyone assumes it's dead. It isn't dead. It's *narrow*. A barge won't fit, but two people on foot can walk the channel bed for six miles, under the willow cover, and come out at the old kiln road below the quarry—and from there, south, away from every road you drew for her."

"She'll figure it out."

"She'll figure it out tomorrow, or the day after, when her scouts walk this cave and find it empty but warm. And by then we're a full day gone, and her net is cast for the river, and the river is where we aren't." Ines looked up at him, and her eyes were flint again, but there was something underneath the flint now, something almost gentle, which frightened him more than anger would have. "You gave her truth, Tam. So we bury her in it. Every word you sold her stays true right up until the moment it stops mattering."

"And my crew?" His voice cracked on the word. "This doesn't free them. The moment she learns I played her—"

"Your crew is alive because you're useful, and you stay useful as long as she thinks she owns you." Ines set down the stick. "So you go back to her. Tomorrow. You tell her we passed through, that we took the south road toward the shrine passes—the western route, the hard one, the one that adds five days. You give her that trail and you weep while you do it, and she'll believe you, because you've earned belief with the cave and the ashes and the fact that you came to warn me tonight instead of selling me outright."

"But I *did* sell you."

"Yes." Flat. Final. "You did. And I'm not going to pretend otherwise, and I'm not going to forgive it tonight, and maybe not for a long time, because trust isn't a rope you can splice back the same once it's parted." She held his gaze, and Tam felt the words land like ballast stones. "But I'm not going to hang you for it either. Because you came here first. Because you told me. Because mercy given freely is worth more than vengeance taken cheaply—and because I need you useful, and a dead friend is useful to no one."

Tam's eyes burned. He scrubbed a sleeve across his face and laughed, wet and broken. "Twenty years I've watched you talk to goats and never say ten words to people, and it turns out when you finally speak, it's to hand me my life and my shame in the same sentence."

"That's what speech is for," Ines said. "Mostly."

Behind them, the girl stirred again. And this time she didn't murmur—she sat up, blinking in the ember-light, and looked at Tam with eyes too old for her face, and said something in that impossible language, three soft syllables that fell into the cave like stones into deep water.

Then she reached out and touched Tam's hand, briefly, the way you'd touch a wound to see how deep it went.

And the ravens outside the cave mouth—three of them, perched in the willows where no raven had any business being at midnight—all spoke at once, in voices like torn silk:

*"The debt is weighed. The debt remains."*

Tam didn't understand a word of it. But he understood the weight of it, and he understood that whatever he had set in motion by putting ink to Voss's paper, it was bigger than six lives in a cell, bigger than one child, bigger maybe than the Crown itself.

"I'll go at first light," he said hoarsely. "I'll feed Voss the western trail. And then I'll come back for you both, Ines. Whatever it costs. I swear it on the river."

Ines looked at him a long moment. Then she nodded once—the smallest nod, the kind you give not to forgive but to release.

"First light," she said. "And Tam—when you sit across from her, and she pours you that wine, and asks you so kindly where we've gone?" Her mouth curved, and it was not a kind curve. "Look her straight in the eye when you lie. It's half forgiven before it's finished."

Outside, the river ran on in the dark, carrying its liars gladly—and far downstream, in a cell beneath the magistrate's house at Coldspring, six prisoners slept without knowing that their lives now hung on a false trail drawn in ash, a smuggler's practiced tears, and the last living voice of a covenant older than any kingdom.

And on the willow branch above the cave, the three ravens waited without sleeping, watching the water flow south toward the falls—

—where, come morning, someone else was already waiting.
