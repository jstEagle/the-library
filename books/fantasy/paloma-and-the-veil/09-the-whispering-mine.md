## 9. The Whispering Mine

The mine mouth opened in the hillside like a wound that had never learned to close.

Ines stood before it with the wind pulling at her coat and Paloma pressed close against her side, and she felt the old dread rise in her throat—not fear of the dark, which she had long since made her peace with, but fear of what this particular dark remembered. The Thornfell range was riddled with such places. Silver had drawn men into these mountains a century ago, and silver had abandoned them just as quickly when the veins ran thin and the tunnels began to whisper. The smugglers called them dead holes. The beasts refused to enter them at all.

That was the part that frightened her. In twenty years of mountain living, Ines Winterbourne had never once known an animal to refuse anything out of superstition. Wolves did not believe in ghosts. Ravens did not fear the dark. But three winters past, she had watched a fox stop dead at the treeline above this very mine, hackles lifted, and turn back without so much as a glance in her direction—as if the animal had heard something beneath the earth that no honest creature should have to hear.

"Stay close," she said, though Paloma could not have understood the words. The girl looked up at her anyway, grey-eyed and solemn, and nodded as if she had understood perfectly. Perhaps she had. Ines was beginning to suspect that the child understood far more than any of them gave her credit for—understood tone, perhaps, or intention, or whatever older language lived underneath all languages, the one that humans had forgotten and beasts had never needed.

She lit the lantern. Its glow seemed small and apologetic against the black rectangle of the tunnel, and for a moment neither of them moved. Then Paloma stepped forward first, small hand finding the hem of Ines's coat, and Ines followed her in.

* * *

The dark swallowed them by degrees.

First it took the sky, then the ridgeline, then the pines, until there was nothing but the circle of lamplight moving across wet stone and the sound of their own breathing. The tunnel sloped gently downward, its walls ribbed with the marks of ancient picks, and water dripped somewhere ahead with the patience of something that had been counting the years since the last human voice.

Ines counted her steps the way she always did in enclosed places—one hundred, two hundred, three hundred—and tried not to think about how much stone hung above her head. She had crossed the mountains a hundred times by a hundred routes, but she had always avoided the mines. There were roads that lied, as Tam liked to say, and there were roads that told truths no one wanted to hear. The mines belonged to the second kind.

At four hundred steps, Paloma stopped walking.

The girl's grip tightened on her coat. Her head tilted—the same listening tilt Ines had seen her give to ravens, to wolves, to the wind itself—and her lips parted slightly, as though she were tasting the air.

Then Ines heard it too.

A voice. Faint, warped, coming from deep within the rock—or from everywhere at once. It was speaking, and the shape of the words was familiar in a way that made the hair on Ines's arms stand upright, because she had spent six days listening to that shape. It was Paloma's language. The Old Speech. But it came back wrong—stretched, hollowed, like a song played underwater, like a name called down a well by someone who had never quite learned it.

The walls were echoing the child's words. Words she hadn't spoken.

Paloma said nothing. She only stood there in the lamplight, her small chest rising and falling quickly, and the mine whispered back at her in her own stolen tongue.

"It knows you," Ines said quietly, and hated how her voice shook. "Whatever this is—it knows you."

They walked on. There was no other way through; the pass above was crawling with Veilbreaker patrols, and Tam's false trail would buy them perhaps two days, three if the weather held against Voss. The mine was the only road south that no soldier would think to guard, because no soldier who knew the stories would enter it. Ines had chosen it precisely because it frightened her. Fear, she had learned, was an excellent mapmaker. It never sent you where everyone else was looking.

But the whispers grew worse.

By five hundred steps they were constant—a low murmur threading through the drip of water, rising and falling like breathing. And they were not random. Ines began to catch fragments, whole phrases surfacing and sinking again: words that sounded like blessings, like lullabies, like the opening lines of some vast recitation that had been going on since before the silver-seekers came and would go on after the last stone fell. Once, unmistakably, she heard her own name come back at her out of the dark—*Winterbourne*—warped almost past recognition, drawn out long and mournful, the way a wolf draws out a howl.

Her hand found Paloma's shoulder and stayed there. The girl did not flinch. If anything, she leaned into the touch, and Ines realized with a strange, quiet shock that the child was comforting *her*.

*You're trembling,* the girl's eyes said. *I know. I know what it is to be afraid of your own voice.*

* * *

They rested in a side chamber where the ceiling rose high enough to stand, and Ines built a small fire because the alternative was sitting in the dark with the whispers, and she was not strong enough for that. The flame threw their shadows huge against the ribbed stone, two shapes huddled together at the bottom of the world.

Paloma ate the dried plums Ines gave her with the deliberate slowness of a child who had learned not to waste food, and watched her over the fire with those grave grey eyes. And Ines found herself talking—which was itself remarkable, because she did not talk, had not talked, not really, in twenty years. To people, at least. Words came out of her stiff and rusted, one at a time, like stones pulled from a frozen riverbed.

"There was a woman," she said. "Before me. Before any of this."

Paloma went still.

"She talked to beasts." The fire cracked and settled. Somewhere in the dark, the mine murmured its distorted agreement. "Not the way you do. Not—royal. Not holy. She talked to them the way you'd talk to a neighbor over a fence. She'd argue with the crows about whose berries were whose. She scolded the mule. She sang to the goats when the storms came, and they'd press up against the walls of the pen to be near her voice." Ines stared into the flame. "I thought every mother could do that. I was four years old. I didn't know it was a gift. I didn't know it was anything."

The whispers in the walls had gone quiet, or nearly. As if the mine, too, were listening.

"They came in winter. Men in Crown colors, with papers and a brazier and a rope." Ines's voice stayed flat; she had practiced this flatness for twenty years without knowing why, and now, at last, the practice revealed its purpose. "They read out charges. I don't remember the words. I remember my mother's face while they read them. She wasn't afraid. That's the part I've never managed to forgive her for, or myself for wanting her to be. She looked at me—just once, across the square—and she smiled, and she said something under her breath, and the crows went mad. Every crow in the valley, screaming at once, until the men couldn't hear their own charges anymore."

Paloma had crept closer. Ines let her.

"They hanged her anyway. And the crows followed the cart out of town, all day, all the way to the treeline. And I was taken in by an aunt who taught me very quickly and very thoroughly that girls who talk to animals get their mothers killed. So I stopped. With people, I mean. I kept the other half—I couldn't keep from keeping that half—but I buried it. I made myself invisible. Twenty years, I made myself a ghost with a goat herd, and I told myself it was safety." She laughed, and the laugh came out wrong. "It wasn't safety. It was just a smaller cage."

The girl reached out and touched her hand.

Just that. Small fingers, chapped from the cold, closing over Ines's knuckles with a gravity far beyond eight years old. And Ines understood, in the way one understands things in the dark and in the deep places of the world, that Paloma was saying something—that this was speech, real speech, translated not through sound but through touch. *I know,* the hand said. *They burned my village for the same reason. We are the same inheritance, you and I. They are still killing us for the crime of hearing.*

Ines turned her hand over and held on.

For a long time neither of them spoke, and the mine did not speak either, and the silence between them was the first silence in six days that had not felt like dread. It felt like shelter. Ines thought about her mother's smile across the square, about the crows screaming, about the twenty years of careful wordlessness that had led her here—to a flooded hole in a mountain, holding hands with a child who spoke the language the Crown wanted to cut out of the world.

"I used to think," she said at last, "that if I was quiet enough, they'd leave me alone. That the warrant would pass over my door. But warrants don't work that way, do they? They fill themselves in eventually. Someone's name goes on the line sooner or later." She looked at the child. "Better it should be mine. Better it should be someone who's had twenty years to practice being nobody."

Paloma squeezed her hand once, hard, and shook her head with a fierceness that needed no translation. *No.* The gesture said it plainly. *Not nobody. Never nobody.*

And then the mine answered.

* * *

It came from deeper in the tunnel—a swell of whispering louder than anything before it, dozens of voices braided into one, and this time the distortion was thinner, the words clearer, and Ines felt her blood turn to cold water as she recognized what they were saying. Not fragments now. A warning. The same phrase, repeated over and over, rising like floodwater:

*The door below is waking. The door below is waking. The little voice must not stop. The little voice must not stop.*

Paloma was on her feet, gripping Ines's hand, her eyes wide and shining in the firelight—and she was mouthing the words along with the walls, matching them exactly, syllable for syllable, as though the mine were not echoing her at all but *teaching* her, feeding her the covenant line by line like a nurse feeding medicine to a sick child.

The lantern guttered. The fire bent sideways, though there was no wind.

And from far below them—from depths no silver-seeker had ever dug—came a sound that was not a whisper at all. A low, vast exhalation, like the mountains themselves drawing breath, like a door of impossible size shifting in its frame after a hundred years of stillness.

The Veil was thinning. Here, now, beneath their feet.

Ines snuffed the fire with her boot, took up the lantern, and took the child's hand.

"We move," she said. "Now. South end of the tunnel, fast and quiet, and we don't stop for anything the dark says to us."

Paloma nodded—and then paused, and looked back into the black depth of the mine, and lifted her free hand in a small, solemn wave, as if thanking it. Or promising it.

Behind them, as they hurried into the southern dark, the whispers followed—not menacing now, but urgent, sorrowful, a chorus of buried voices chanting them onward toward the light:

*Hurry, hurry, keeper's daughter. Hurry, hurry, speaker's friend. The door is waking, and the hunters are already at the mouth of the world.*
