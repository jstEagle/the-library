## 10. Voss Closes the Net

Serane Voss had learned to read mountains the way other officers read ledgers, and the mountain before her was lying.

The Whispering Mine sat in its fold of grey scree like a mouth pretending to be closed. Officially it had been dead for sixty years—copper, then nothing, then the Crown's surveyors marking it *exhausted* on maps that no one consulted twice. Unofficially, the mine breathed. Her outriders reported a warmth at its mouth in deep frost, a faint hum that made horses refuse the trail, and now, this morning, a scatter of small footprints in old snow: a woman's boots, long-strided and deliberate, and beside them the prints of a child, half-erased by drifting, still fresh enough to make Serane's heart beat faster than she permitted it to show.

"There," said Lieutenant Hask, pointing with his chin rather than his hand, because he had learned that too—from her. A thread of woodsmoke rose from somewhere below the mine's collapsed upper galleries, thin and blue and confident. Someone had built a fire inside a mountain and trusted it not to kill them.

"Company stays here," Serane said. "Twelve men at the treeline. Four with me to the mouth."

"Captain—" Hask hesitated. He was a good officer, which meant he asked the questions she would have asked. "If it's the Winterbourne woman, the reports say she has a way with animals. The bears up there—"

"I know what the reports say." She said it mildly, and Hask closed his mouth. He did not know what else the reports said, because she had burned those pages herself, three years ago, in a brazier in the Veilbreaker garrison at Cardun Hollow, and had watched her mother's name curl into ash without feeling anything she could name.

Her mother's name. There was a thing she carried the way soldiers carried shrapnel—too deep to cut out, too close to the bone to forget.

*Marisol Voss.* Covenant-keeper. Burned in the square at Aldenmere when Serane was nine years old, for the crime of teaching Old Speech to children who were not her own. Serane remembered the fire less than she remembered the weeks before it: her mother kneeling in their kitchen, tracing letters in spilled flour while Serane laughed at how the words seemed to *lean*, how the flour itself appeared to listen. She remembered her mother saying, *One day you'll understand what these are worth,* and she remembered thinking, even at nine, even then, with the terrible clarity of children: *Then why do we hide them?*

That question had built her entire life. The Crown had only given it a uniform.

She climbed toward the mine mouth through snow that squeaked like new leather, four soldiers behind her, her breath pluming white. The Veilbreakers wore no colors on operations like this—the Crown preferred its deniability stitched tight—but every one of them carried the grey-lacquered compasses that didn't point north. They pointed at breaches. At thin places. And all six of the compasses in her company, she knew without looking, were trembling toward the dark opening ahead like dogs straining at a scent.

The child was making the Veil thin just by existing. Just by *talking.*

Serane had read the interrogations from the burned sanctuary at Corren Vale—what little the survivors' informants had gathered before the fire. A village of covenant-keepers, two generations deep, raising a girl on words older than any kingdom. And when the Veilbreakers came, the girl had been sent away with one instruction, passed down like an heirloom: *find the one who talks to beasts, and never stop talking.*

Never stop talking. Serane almost admired it. It was, after all, exactly what her mother had done—kept speaking, kept teaching, right up until the Crown proved that speech could be stopped with rope and pitch. The difference between Marisol Voss and the child Paloma was only this: Serane intended to reach the girl before the Crown's clumsier hands did. Before another square, another fire, another waste.

Because that was what no one above her rank understood, what Magistrate Ellery-Doone with his blank warrants would never grasp: the covenant could not be *destroyed*. You could burn every keeper, silence every voice, and the Veil would simply rot on its own—a dam failing slowly, drowning valleys, spilling things that belonged beneath the mountains into the world of men. She had seen the first of it already, in the spring: the black elk standing in a barley field at dawn, staring at farmers who stared back, neither knowing anymore which side of the world they were on.

But if the Veil could be *held*—if the last Voice could be taken alive, kept, studied, bound to the Crown's service instead of hunted to extinction—then the door beneath the mountains need not fall at all. It could be owned. Guarded. Governed.

Power, yes. But power as a wall around everything soft and living. Power as protection. Her mother had hidden the covenant and died for nothing. Serane would hold it openly, and no one would ever burn again for what they knew.

She believed this the way she believed in the weight of her own sword. Which is to say: completely, and without examining where the belief had come from, or what it cost to carry.

* * *

The mine mouth was taller than a man and half-choked with fallen rock, and the warmth came out of it like the breath of something sleeping. Twenty paces out, Serane raised her fist and halted her four.

Because the bears were awake.

There were three of them, denned somewhere in the galleries—she'd known from the outriders' reports, and had chosen to come anyway—but she hadn't expected them to be *here*, ranged across the scree before the entrance like a wall that had opinions. A sow big as a haywagon, and two yearlings flanking her, all of them standing on hind legs, all of them looking—not at the soldiers, but at *her*, with an attention that had nothing animal in it.

"Hold," Serane said quietly. "Nobody draws."

Hask's breathing had gone shallow. "Captain, that's—bears don't—"

"They do," Serane said, "when someone asks them to."

And there it was again, the old knowledge rising in her like water into a well shaft: her mother's voice, the flour letters, the way the world leaned toward certain words. She had spent twenty years building a fortress against that inheritance, stone by stone, rank by rank, and still it seeped through the mortar. The beasts were listening to the woman in the mountain. Of course they were. Ines Winterbourne, smuggler, hermit, ghost of the Thornfell passes—the file on her was thin because everyone who might have thickened it had a habit of forgetting what they'd seen.

"Captain Winterbourne!" Serane called, and her voice carried flat and clear across the scree, trained to cut wind and distance both. "I know you can hear me. I know the mountain tells you everything. Come out and talk. I'm not here to burn anything."

A long silence. Snow ticked down off the rocks. Then, from the darkness of the mouth, a voice—low, dry, unhurried, the voice of a woman who had negotiated with wolves and found them more reasonable than magistrates.

"You brought forty men to a conversation."

"Thirty-six. And I left them at the trees." Serane stepped forward, alone, palms open and away from her sword—an old duelist's courtesy, and also a true one. "You have my word none of them advance unless I call."

"Your word." Ines Winterbourne emerged into the grey light with the child behind her, one small hand gripped tight in hers, and Serane felt something twist under her ribs at the sight of the girl—small, dark-eyed, watchful, a smudge of soot on one cheek. So ordinary. So utterly unordinary. "I've read your Crown's word, Captain. It's printed on blank paper."

"The warrant isn't mine." Serane kept her tone level, almost conversational. Two soldiers, she noted, were edging despite orders; she silenced them with a glance. "Ellery-Doone's paper. I don't serve the magistrate. I serve the Veil."

That landed. She watched it land—in the slight stillness of the woman's shoulders, the flicker of the child's eyes. Good. Let them wonder.

"You serve the Crown," Ines said. "Whatever you tell yourself at night."

"I serve the idea that your mother's generation got wrong." Serane took another step, and the sow bear shifted, and she stopped, respecting it. "They hid. They whispered in kitchens and taught in cellars, and when the Crown came—and the Crown always comes—they burned alone, one at a time, and the covenant got smaller and weaker and nobody was any safer. Hiding failed, Captain. For twenty years it has been failing. The Veil is thinning whether anyone speaks or not. Things are coming through. Villages are starting to notice. And when the panic starts—when the first town burns because something with too many antlers walked out of a hillside—you'll wish there had been someone strong enough to stand at that door and *hold it shut*."

"And that someone is the Crown."

"That someone could be," Serane said. "With the child. Alive. Protected. Under my command, not the magistrate's—I give you my word on that too. No cells. No interrogation. A garrison, teachers, guards who answer to me. She keeps her voice. The realm keeps its door. Everyone keeps their lives." She let it soften then, deliberately, because she had read the file and knew what this woman was: alone two decades, fluent in everything except people. "You've carried her this far on goat trails and stolen sleep. How much farther can you carry her? To the Threshold? Through winter? Past every company I won't be able to call off next time?"

She saw the temptation work in Ines Winterbourne like weather works on stone—not dramatically, but inevitably. And Serane hated herself a little for how good she was at this, and told herself, as always, that hating the necessity was proof she wasn't cruel. Cruelty was letting the dam fail. Cruelty was another fire in another square.

Behind Ines, the child tugged her hand and said something.

It was three syllables, maybe four, in a language Serane had not heard since she was nine years old kneeling in flour—and the whole mountain *answered*. Not echoed. Answered. The sound went into the rock and came back changed, warmer, and the sow bear dropped to all fours and turned its head toward the mine mouth as if called by name, and every hair on Serane's arms stood up beneath her oiled leather, because the words had *leaned*, the way flour had leaned once in a kitchen in Aldenmere, and twenty years of fortifications cracked straight down the middle.

*One day you'll understand what these are worth.*

"Yes," Serane heard herself say, barely above the wind. "That. That's what I'm offering to protect."

Ines had gone very still. When she spoke, her voice had changed—no longer the smuggler's flat caution but something older and colder underneath. "You knew that language. Once."

"My mother spoke it." Let her have that. It was true, and truth was the only coin Serane had never counterfeited. "She died for it. In a square, in front of a crowd, when I was nine. So don't lecture me about what the Crown does to keepers, Captain Winterbourne. I was there. I'm the reason there's a different way."

"A different way," Ines repeated. "Owned by the same hands that held the torches."

"Hands change. Systems endure." It sounded hollow even as she said it, and she pressed on before the hollowness could echo. "Come down with me. Both of you. Today, willingly, and I swear on my mother's ashes you'll be treated as guests."

The child looked up at Ines. Said nothing aloud—but Serane saw the small mouth move, shaping silent syllables, and saw Ines's jaw tighten as though the words themselves were arguing.

"No," Ines said.

"Captain—"

"I said no." And then Ines Winterbourne did something Serane would think about for years afterward, in the way one thinks about a door closing that one believed was a wall. She turned her head slightly, toward the dark of the mine, toward the sow and her yearlings, toward the whole listening mountain, and she *spoke*—a low run of sound, half-whisper, half-song, in no language Serane's training had ever catalogued.

The mountain answered.

It began as a rumble, high on the eastern face above the mine mouth—a sound Serane's soldier's mind identified in one cold instant: *rockslide, natural, unavoidable, except that it isn't.* Boulders the size of cottages let go of the slope and came down in a slow, thunderous cascade, precisely, impossibly, along the line between Serane's position and the treeline where her thirty-two waiting soldiers stood. The scree road vanished under a hundred tons of stone. The path of retreat became a wall. Dust rolled down and hung in the freezing air like a curtain drawn across the world.

Through it, unhurried, came the bears—sow first, yearlings flanking—walking a narrow untouched lane of snow directly toward the Veilbreakers, and stopping, and sitting, enormous and patient, between the soldiers and their captain.

"Nobody moves," Serane said, and her voice did not shake, because she would not permit it. "Nobody draws."

Hask was gray-faced. "Captain, the road—"

"I can see the road, Lieutenant."

She looked up the slope, through the settling dust, to where Ines Winterbourne stood with the child's hand in hers, silhouetted against the mine's dark breath. Their eyes met across fifty yards of ruined ground.

"This is what holding the door looks like," Ines called, not cruelly. Almost gently. "Not owning it. Asking it. Remember the difference, Captain. Your mother did."

Then they were gone into the mountain, and the bears rose, and ambled back into the dark after them, and Serane Voss stood alone in the dust with her sword undrawn and her conviction bleeding quietly from a wound she could not yet locate.

*Asking it.* As if the mountain had a vote. As if protection were a favor begged from beasts instead of a structure built by will.

But her compass, when she finally drew it, was trembling harder than before—pointing past the mine, down-slope, south and west, toward the drowned country where the old chapels slept under floodwater. They were running for the fragments. Of course they were. The Voice, seeking the Words.

"Break camp," she told Hask. "Send riders to the river road. Every ford, every ferry. And get me Tam Osgood's crew list—I want to know which smuggler captains owe the Crown favors, and which ones owe the Crown *fear*."

Somewhere south, a woman who talked to beasts and a child who spoke the world's first language were moving toward the First Threshold. Serane Voss flexed her sword-hand, once, and followed—because doors did not hold themselves, and if no one else would stand at this one and *own* it, then she would, alone if she must, all the way down into the dark.

She did not yet know about the chapel under the lake. But the compass knew. And it was pulling her, like a hooked fish, toward the water.
