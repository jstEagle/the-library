## 11. The Drowned Chapel

The mine spat them out on the far side of the ridge two days later, and the world they came out into was not the one they had left.

Ines felt it first in her feet. Twenty years of walking Thornfell had taught her soles to read the ground the way scholars read scripture—the particular hush of old stone, the tremor of water moving where it shouldn't. Here, beneath the pine duff and the frost-hardened mud, something was humming. Not a sound she heard with her ears. A sound her bones reported, low and patient, like a bell that had been ringing since before there were ears to hear it.

Paloma felt it too. The girl had gone quiet in a new way—not the silence of someone who had no words, but the silence of someone holding them behind her teeth like a mouthful of river stones. She walked with her hand pressed flat against every tree they passed, and the trees leaned toward her, very slightly, the way wheat leans toward sun.

*She's listening for it,* Ines thought. *And it's listening back.*

They descended through a forest of black pines into a valley the maps called Hollowmere and the smugglers called the Drown, because the whole basin had once been a lake before some ancient convulsion drained it overnight, leaving behind a floor of gray silt and standing water and things that had drowned mid-motion—a chapel among them, its bell tower rising out of the shallows like a finger raised in warning or blessing; no one alive knew which.

Tam had told her about it once, years ago, over cheap brandy in a lowland tavern: *There's a chapel under the water at Hollowmere, Wren-girl. Old beyond old. Smugglers use it to store cargo because the Crown won't go near it. Soldiers say the place whispers. Priests say worse.* He'd laughed then, slapping the table. *You'd love it. Nothing down there talks back.*

Nothing down there talked back. But something down there was waiting, and both of them knew it, and neither of them said so, because Paloma could not and Ines would not.

* * *

They reached the chapel at dusk, wading the last hundred yards through water that came to their thighs and was warm—unnaturally, terribly warm, as if the valley sat atop a sleeping animal's blood. Reeds parted before Paloma without being touched. A heron stood in the shallows watching them pass, and when Ines reached toward it with the small silent asking she had carried all her life—*friend? danger? which way?*—the bird did not answer. It only turned its head and looked at Paloma, and bowed.

Ines stopped wading.

In twenty years, no wild thing had ever bowed to her. She spoke to beasts and beasts spoke back, but it had always been a conversation between equals—or between equals and something slightly less than equal, because she was human, and humans had burned their side of an old bargain so thoroughly that even the animals who liked her kept a certain careful distance. She had never minded. Distance was what she wanted. Distance was the whole architecture of her life.

But the heron bowed to Paloma, and the reeds made way, and the warm water rose around them like a congregation standing for a bride.

"Come on," Ines said aloud, because her voice sounded better than the thoughts underneath it. "Before dark."

The chapel doors stood open above the waterline, oak gone black as bog-iron, carved with figures worn nearly smooth. Ines hauled herself up onto the threshold and pulled Paloma after her, and they stood dripping in the nave of a church that had been dead longer than any kingdom they knew.

It was vast inside—far vaster than the outside suggested, which should have been impossible and was simply one more thing about this valley that declined to obey. Moonlight fell through a broken roof in pale columns and struck the floodwater below, so that the whole floor of the chapel glowed like a sheet of hammered tin. Pillars marched away into shadow. Somewhere, water dripped with the patience of centuries. And at the far end, where an altar would have stood, stood a slab of black stone taller than Ines herself, upright, freestanding, its surface crawling faintly—faintly—with light, like embers remembered rather than burning.

Paloma made a sound. It was not a word. It was the sound a person makes when they come home to a house they had been told was destroyed.

She ran. Her wet boots slapped the flooded floor and sent rings of silver across the water, and Ines lunged after her—"Paloma, wait, we don't know—" but the girl was already there, already pressed against the black stone with both palms flat, her forehead touching its cold face, and the embers beneath the surface flared at her touch like coals breathed upon.

Ines caught up and stopped, breathing hard, water streaming off her coat. Up close she could see the stone was covered in writing—not carved, exactly. Grown. The letters rose from the surface the way crystals grow, geometric and patient, and they were the same letters Paloma scratched in the dirt at night when she thought Ines wasn't watching. The same letters she traced on fogged glass, on spilled flour, on Ines's own forearm once, absent-mindedly, while they waited out rain.

Old Speech. The language older than kingdoms. The language the ravens remembered from when the door below was loud.

"Is this it?" Ines asked quietly. "The first fragment?"

Paloma turned. In the ember-light her face was stripped of everything childish, and what remained was ancient and frightened and certain, all three at once. She nodded. Then she pointed at the stone, and then at her own throat, and then—her hands hesitating for the first time—at Ines.

*I can read it,* the gesture said. *I can speak it. But I don't know if I should.*

And there it was, the question Ines had been outrunning since the night a silent child appeared at her door with a broken whistle in her fist. Because she knew what speaking meant now. She had known since the mine, since the walls had echoed the girl's sleep-talk back distorted, since the Veil had thinned enough that she could feel it fluttering like a torn banner. Every word Paloma spoke in the Old Speech moved something. Opened something. Or sealed something—if the words were the right ones, in the right place, spoken by the right voice.

Maren's voice came back to her across the miles they'd put behind them: *A Voice and a Speaker, willing, at the First Threshold. Anything less is just noise leaking through a crack.*

This wasn't the Threshold. This was a fragment, a piece of the covenant's text lying drowned in a smuggler's storeroom. Reading it here would be—

Would be what?

Ines looked at the stone. The embers pulsed, slow, like a heart deciding whether to keep beating.

"We have to know what it says," she said finally. "Whatever happens next, we have to know. If Voss gets here first—" She didn't finish. They both carried the image of Veilbreaker sappers chiseling fragments like this one out of chapels and mines and barrows, hauling them to some Crown vault to be studied by men who thought a covenant was a kind of weapon.

Paloma searched her face. Whatever she found there must have been enough, because she turned back to the stone, drew a breath that seemed to come up from her boots, and began to read.

* * *

The first syllable changed the temperature of the room.

Not the air—the room. Ines felt it in her teeth, in the scar tissue of an old burn on her shoulder, in the water below, which stopped rippling all at once, everywhere, as if the whole flooded nave had been struck still by a hand. The moonlight columns thickened. The drips ceased. Even the hum in Ines's bones went silent, the way a forest goes silent before a wolf speaks.

Then Paloma's voice filled the silence, and Ines understood that everything she had heard from the child until now—the hummed lullabies, the half-words, the sounds she made talking to foxes—had been a stream. This was the river.

The Old Speech did not sound like language. It sounded like the thing language was invented to describe. Each syllable landed with weight, with texture; Ines could feel them the way you feel thunder through a floor. And the stone answered. With every line Paloma spoke, the grown letters brightened, and the light climbed off the surface of the slab and into the air itself, hanging in the ruined nave in slow golden threads, weaving, braiding, building something that looked like script and moved like weather.

Paloma's voice cracked on the third stanza and did not stop. Tears ran down her face and she read through them, louder, and Ines realized the girl wasn't performing the text—she was grieving it. These were names. Ines couldn't understand the words, but she understood the shape of them, the way you understand a funeral in any tongue. Names of keepers. Names of sanctuaries. Names, she understood with a cold spreading certainty, of villages like Paloma's, written into the covenant's own body so that they could not be forgotten—and every one of them a name the fire had answered.

The light finished weaving. For one suspended moment the whole chapel hung inside a lattice of gold, every pillar outlined, every drop of floodwater lit from within, and Ines saw—she would swear until her dying day she saw—the shapes of people in the light. Hundreds of them. Standing in the water where the pews had been, facing the altar, heads bowed. The congregation of a church that had been dead for a thousand years, present for one verse more.

Then Paloma spoke the final line, and the lattice collapsed inward—into them.

It didn't hurt. That was the shocking part. Ines braced for fire and got warmth instead, a warmth like the one memory she had of her mother's arms, sudden and total and gone before she could hold it. The light poured through her skin and wrote something there, she knew it did, she could feel the letters settling into her like seeds into soil—into her wrists, her throat, the backs of her hands. Across the nave, Paloma glowed the same way, the marks blooming along her small arms like vines of dawn.

And then it was over. The moonlight was ordinary again. The water dripped. The heron outside shifted in the reeds with a croak.

Paloma swayed. Ines caught her, and the girl clung to her coat and shook with silent sobs, and Ines held her and looked down at her own forearms in the dimness, where faint lines now traced the insides of her wrists—thin, silver, unmistakable. Not ink. Not scar. Script. She could feel it when she touched it, the way you can feel a bruise before you can see it.

"Oh," she said softly. "Oh, you foolish, brave girl."

Because she understood now, all at once, what Maren had tried to tell her and what she had refused to hear: the covenant did not distinguish between reading and reforging, between a fragment and the whole. The mark was the mark. The bond was the bond. By speaking the fragment aloud, Paloma hadn't merely learned the covenant's terms.

She had signed them.

And whatever marked them bound them—together, and to the Veil, and to every ear in the world that knew how to listen for the covenant's frequency. Ines had spent twenty years perfecting invisibility. She had built her entire survival out of silence, misdirection, the fine art of being nobody worth a second glance. And in the space of ten minutes, a mute child had painted a beacon on both of them that any Veilbreaker tracker could read from across a kingdom.

She should be furious. She was, distantly, the way you are furious at weather. But mostly, looking down at the girl shaking against her chest, she felt something worse: tenderness, and beneath the tenderness, the terrible arithmetic of what came next.

"They'll know," she murmured into Paloma's hair. "The moment those marks settled, someone in a Crown uniform felt a tug on a line, somewhere south of here. Maybe several someones." She closed her eyes. "We just stopped being findable and started being visible. There's a difference, little one, and it's the difference between a stone in a river and a lantern on a hill."

Paloma pulled back and looked up at her, tear-streaked, luminous. And then she did something she had never done. She took Ines's marked wrist in both her small hands, turned it over, and pressed her own marked wrist against it—skin to skin, script to script.

The warmth flared again. And through it, without a single word, Ines felt the girl's meaning arrive whole, the way meanings arrived from wolves and hawks, but deeper, clearer, closer:

*Now you're marked too. Now they hunt us together. Good. I was tired of being hunted alone.*

Ines laughed—a rough, startled sound that echoed strangely in the dead nave, and somewhere overhead, in the broken belfry, something stirred and rustled and settled, as if the chapel itself had chuckled.

"All right," she said. "Together."

* * *

They did not stay the night. Ines had survived this long by knowing when a shelter became a trap, and a glowing beacon in a valley full of warm water was a trap with a view. They gathered what they could from the smugglers' caches along the gallery—dried fish, a coil of good rope, a hooded lantern with oil still in it—and Paloma, before they left, laid her palm on the black stone one last time.

The embers pulsed once, gently, like a heartbeat saying goodbye.

They waded out under the stars. Behind them the bell tower stood in the shallows, and as they reached the far shore Ines could have sworn she heard it toll—once, faintly, though the bell had rotted out of it a century ago, and the sound came not through the air but up through the soles of her boots, through the marked script in her wrists, through the new-woven thread that now ran between her heart and a child's.

East, the thread said. South and east. Toward the high passes. Toward the First Threshold.

They made camp at midnight in a hollow of granite above the Drown, and Ines was laying the meager fire when the night went wrong.

It began with the birds. Every roosting thing in the valley lifted at once—a black eruption of wings against the stars, thousands of them, wheeling east in absolute silence. No calls. No alarm cries. Just departure, total and coordinated, the way a household flees a house before the walls come down.

Ines stood very still. She reached out with the old asking, the lifelong whisper—*what is it, what do you see*—and got nothing, because the birds were gone, all of them, the sky scoured clean.

But Paloma had gone rigid at the edge of the hollow, her head cocked, listening to something Ines could not hear. Slowly the girl raised one marked arm and pointed south, down the valley, toward the lowland road.

Toward the point of light that had just appeared there. One lantern. Then two. Then a dozen, strung out along the road like beads on a wire, moving with the unhurried discipline of soldiers who knew exactly where they were going and did not need to search.

Captain Serane Voss did not need ravens anymore. She did not need Tam's sold secrets, or informers, or scent-hounds. She had felt the fragment spoken, felt the covenant flare like a struck match in a dark country, and she had simply turned her company toward the light.

Paloma's hand found Ines's in the dark. The marks on their wrists pulsed in unison, soft and traitorous as two hearts beating in one chest.

"Up," Ines whispered, kicking dirt over the fire. "We walk tonight. We walk until the marks stop glowing."

And they climbed, two small marked figures ascending into the black spine of the mountains, while below them the lanterns left the road and began, patiently, to follow.
