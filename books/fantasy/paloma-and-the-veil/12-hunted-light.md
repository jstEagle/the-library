## 12. Hunted Light

The marks came alive at dusk.

Ines felt them first as warmth—two small coins of heat, one beneath her collarbone, one over Paloma's heart, glowing through cloth like embers wrapped in linen. By full dark they had brightened to a soft gold that no blanket could smother, and when Ines looked down at the girl walking beside her on the game trail, she saw the light leaking between Paloma's fingers where the child had pressed her hand to her own chest.

"Does it hurt?" Ines asked.

Paloma shook her head. Then, in her careful, chiming Old Speech—the words still strange and lovely and terrible in Ines's ears—she said: *It sings. It wants to be found.*

"That's rather the problem," Ines muttered.

They had come down out of the Whispering Mine two days past with Voss's riders at their backs and the taste of rock dust in their throats, and every hour since, the light had grown stronger. It was a beautiful thing and a fatal thing: a beacon stitched into their skin by a chapel older than any kingdom, marking them for every Veilbreaker within fifty miles. Ines had tried smearing ash over it, then mud, then a poultice of yarrow and charcoal that Maren's name had been worth remembering for. The light shone through all of it, patient as moonrise.

The ravens had stopped following them an hour ago. That was the other problem. Ravens followed Ines the way weather followed mountains; their absence was a silence shaped like a warning.

"We need walls," Ines said, more to herself than to the child. "Stone. Something old enough to have opinions."

Paloma tugged her sleeve and pointed north, up a fold of the mountain where the pines gave way to grey scree and a thread of smoke rose thin and straight against the last violet of the sky.

Ines stared at it. A fire that vertical meant a chimney, and a chimney that high on a bare shoulder of Thornfell meant someone who did not wish to be seen and did not much care who knew it.

"I know that smoke," she said slowly. "I've known it since I was smaller than you."

*Whose?*

"A woman my mother trusted." Ines adjusted the strap of her pack and began to climb. "Which is either very good news or the end of us both."

* * *

Maren's house grew out of the mountain like something the mountain had dreamed—a squat tower of dry-stacked granite furred with lichen, roofed in old slate, its single window burning amber against the dark. There was no doorbell, because there was barely a door; Ines knocked once on the iron-banded oak and heard, from inside, the deliberate scrape of a chair and the slow percussion of a cane.

The door opened on a face like a riverbed: lined, grey, and full of hidden water. Maren was smaller than Ines remembered, which meant Ines had been very young indeed when she'd last stood here. Her eyes went from Ines to Paloma to the glow at both their chests, and her expression did something complicated—grief and recognition and a kind of grim arithmetic.

"Ysolde's daughter," she said. Not a question. "And you've gone and gotten yourself *marked*."

"Maren—"

"Don't stand on my threshold broadcasting like a festival bonfire. Inside. Quickly. And you—" she looked down at Paloma, and her voice roughened, "—come in out of the cold, little voice. You look hungry enough to eat my whole garden, and I'd let you."

The interior was warm and close and smelled of dried herbs, woodsmoke, and beeswax. Bundles hung from the rafters in braided columns; jars glowed dull green and amber on every shelf; a kettle already sang on the hob, though Ines had heard no one approach a fire. The floor was strewn with rushes, and under the rushes, faintly, Ines could feel it—the hum of old warding laid down in another century, thrumming against her boots like a sleeping cat's purr.

Maren bolted the door, drew a circle of salt across it with her heel, and only then turned around.

"Now," she said. "Sit. Both of you. Eat while I look at those marks, because I'll want you fed before I say what I have to say. Bad news goes down easier over stew. My mother's rule. Probably somebody's mother's rule going back to the First Threshold itself."

She ladled out barley stew thick with root vegetables and a rabbit Ines chose not to ask about. Paloma ate the way children eat after long fear—fiercely, silently, watching the door between spoonfuls. Ines ate too, though her stomach was a knot, because Maren was right: bad news went down easier fed.

While they ate, the old witch knelt before Paloma with a groan and held her palm above the child's glowing chest. She did not touch it. Her lips moved; sweat broke on her brow; and the light beneath Paloma's shirt pulsed once, twice, like a heart answering a knock.

"Well," Maren said, sitting back heavily on the hearthstone. "That's a covenant-mark. True one. Haven't seen one since—" She stopped. Looked at Ines with those riverbed eyes. "Since your mother wore one. Did you know that? Ysolde carried the mark of a Speaker. Half-formed, half-taught, but real. They burned her with it showing. I always wondered if that was the crime or just the excuse."

Ines set down her spoon. The stew sat in her like a stone. "I was six," she said. "I remember the light coming out of her sleeves. I thought everyone's mother glowed."

"No," Maren said gently. "Only the ones carrying a world under their ribs."

* * *

Later, with Paloma asleep on a cot near the fire—truly asleep, finally, her breathing deep and even, the mark at her chest dimmed to a drowsing coal—Maren poured two cups of something sharp and resinous and pushed one across the table at Ines.

"Tell me everything," Maren said. "From the night she arrived. Leave nothing out because you think an old woman will blush. I buried three husbands and outlived two inquisitions. Nothing blushes in me anymore."

So Ines told it. The storm, the broken whistle, the beasts bowing to a mute child. The ravens and their memory of the loud door below. The blank warrant. Hollow Ford and the beast court naming Paloma the Last Voice. Voss in her charming grey coat, offering amnesty with one hand and holding chains in the other. Tam's betrayal and the false trail. The mine where the walls spoke back wrong. The drowned chapel, the tablet, the flood of old light—and the moment the light sank into their skin and stayed.

When she finished, the fire had burned low and Maren had not interrupted once. Now she stared into her cup for a long time.

"You understand what you're carrying," she said at last. It wasn't really a question either, but Ines answered it anyway.

"The Veil is failing. Paloma's words are unsealing it—or finishing its unraveling, I don't know which. The Crown wants to seize her and own whatever power comes through. If they do—"

"If they do, the Veil won't fall so much as be *torn*," Maren said. "There's a difference, girl, and it matters. A falling Veil is a door closing badly. A torn Veil is a wound that never closes. Everything beneath the mountains—every sanctuary, every beast-court hall, every drowned chapel and buried garden and all the old folk who never left—everything dies. Slowly, if we're lucky. And the sickness doesn't stay politely below. It comes up. You've seen the mine walls. That's a pinprick. A tear would be a hemorrhage."

"And if the Veil simply fails on its own?"

Maren's jaw tightened. "Then it falls clean, but it falls *all the way*, and the covenant dies with it, and the two realms forget each other forever—which sounds merciful until you understand that the realms are not two things that can be separated. They're one thing folded over. Pull the fold flat and you don't get two worlds. You get none." She drank. "Your mother knew the terms. So do I, because Ysolde made me learn them, God rest her stubborn soul, in case she didn't live long enough to teach them to you herself. Which she didn't."

Ines's hands were shaking slightly. She wrapped them around the warm cup. "Say it anyway. All of it. I'm tired of learning this story in fragments."

Maren nodded slowly, and when she spoke, her voice took on the cadence of recitation—the cadence Ines had begun to recognize as the shape of Old Speech wearing human grammar like a borrowed coat.

"The covenant is a promise made at the First Threshold, in the first winter of the first kingdom. It binds the veiled world and the sunlit world into one household. It has never been reforged because it has never needed to be—until now. To reforge it requires two souls standing together at the Threshold stone: a Voice, who speaks the words of the old pact, and a Speaker, who answers them—not with memorized ritual, mind you, but with a true offer. The stone asks each of them what they will give. And it must be given *willingly*. Freely. With open eyes and no coercion and no bargain struck beforehand. That's the whole law, Ines. Power hoarded breaks the covenant. Justice freely given remakes it." She paused. "The stone knows the difference. Don't ever think you can fool it. Your mother told me people always believe they can. People are wrong."

"And if no one stands at the Threshold?" Ines asked quietly. "If we fail? If Voss takes her first?"

"Then the Veil thins to gauze and then to nothing, and everything below the mountains dies, and the dying climbs." Maren set down her cup with a click that sounded terribly final in the quiet room. "How long do we have? That depends on how many words your girl remembers. Every phrase of the Old Speech she loses is a stitch pulled. I felt the Veil from here three nights ago—it moaned like wind in a chimney. It's thinner than it's been in nine hundred years."

Outside, far off toward the pass, something howled—not wolf. Wolves howled with music in them. This was flatter, lonelier, wrong-angled. Maren and Ines looked at each other across the table and neither pretended it was a dog.

"They're tracking the light," Ines said. "Voss will have seen it by now. Everyone will have seen it. We're a bonfire walking."

"You're worse than a bonfire. Bonfires burn out." Maren levered herself up with her cane and went to a chest in the corner, rummaging through it with the fierce efficiency of a woman who had been preparing for disasters for decades and intended to keep her hand in. She came back with a folded length of black wool shot through with threads of dull silver. "Warding cloth. My own weaving. It won't hide you—nothing hides a covenant-mark, that's rather the point of a covenant—but it will muffle you. Turn a shout into a whisper. Buy you hours instead of minutes. Wear it until you reach the Threshold, and not one step past, because the stone must see you plainly when you arrive."

"Thank you." Ines took the cloth. It was heavier than it looked, and warmer, as if the silver threads remembered sunlight.

"Don't thank me yet." Maren lowered herself back into her chair and was silent a moment, looking into the banked fire. When she spoke again, her voice had changed—smaller, older, sadder. "I need to tell you something about your mother, since you were too young to carry it yourself, and it's been sitting in me thirty years like a swallowed stone."

Ines went very still.

"She came here, the winter before they took her. Sat where you're sitting. She knew what was coming—she'd read the signs, same as anyone with sense could. And she asked me a question. Asked whether a person could give the covenant too *much*. Whether the gift of speech with beasts was hers to spend, or a debt she owed forward." Maren's eyes lifted to Ines's face. "I told her I didn't know. I've regretted that answer for thirty years, so let me do better by you now: the stone will ask you what you'll give, and it will mean it, and whatever you answer, it will take you at your word. Think about that before you're standing there with no time left to think. Promise me you'll think about it."

Ines thought about her mother's sleeves full of light. About the hawk on her wrist that morning twenty years running, the wolves' low counsel at Hollow Ford, the ravens' gossip, the whole chattering invisible nation that had been her only country since childhood. Something in her chest tightened and would not be named.

"I'll think about it," she said.

"Good." Maren rose, stiffly, and touched Ines's hair—an old woman's gesture, brief and gruff and unbearably gentle. "You have her face, you know. When you're frightened. Exactly her face."

* * *

Ines did not sleep. She sat by the fire through the small hours with the warded cloak across her knees and watched Paloma breathe.

Sometime past midnight the child stirred, whimpered—a small, cracked sound—and surfaced halfway from sleep, eyes unfocused, searching the shadows. Ines moved to the cot's edge and took her hand.

"You're safe," she murmured. "Old woman's wards. Stronger than my temper, and that's saying something."

Paloma blinked at her. Then, sleep-slurred, in the Old Speech that rang like struck glass even at a whisper: *You are afraid.*

"I am."

*Of the soldiers?*

"No." Ines hesitated. Lying to adults had never come easily to her; lying to this child seemed suddenly impossible. "Of what the road asks. Of what the stone at the end of it will ask harder."

Paloma considered this with the grave attention of the very young and the very old. Then she scooted sideways on the narrow cot, making a space, an invitation as plain as language. Ines looked at her a long moment—this small, marked, impossible creature the whole world was converging to crush—and lay down beside her, boots and all, the way you lie down next to someone when the storm is too large to fight and the only honest act left is company.

Paloma pressed her forehead against Ines's shoulder and began, very softly, to talk herself back to sleep: fragments of the Old Speech, tumbling and sweet, a litany of ordinary holy things—*bread, ember, sister, door, bread, ember, dawn*—each word a stitch in the world, each stitch a little brighter for the saying. Ines listened until the words slowed, and softened, and became breath.

Above the shuttered window, the warding hummed. Beyond the wards, on some dark slope, the wrong-angled howl rose again, closer now, and was not answered by any living throat.

Three days' hard walk to the First Threshold, Maren had said. Two, if the weather held. The weather would not hold.

Ines closed her eyes and let the child's small voice carry her to the edge of sleep, knowing that at dawn they would wrap themselves in shadow-cloth and walk into the teeth of the hunt—and that somewhere behind them, patient as winter, Serane Voss was already reading the light.
