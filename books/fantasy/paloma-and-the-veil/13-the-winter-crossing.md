## 13. The Winter Crossing

The mountain did not care that they were running out of time.

Ines had known this crossing her whole life—had run it twice in autumn with goats bleating behind her and once in early spring when the snow still held a knife's edge—but she had never attempted it in the dying weeks of the year, and never with a child on her back who was more precious than any contraband the Crown had ever dreamed of taxing. The pass above Hollow Ford was called the Shepherd's Tooth by the smugglers, because it bit, and because only fools and shepherds tried to get past it.

She was, she supposed, both.

They had left Maren's hearth at first light with what the witch could spare: a sack of smoked hare, a twist of dried bilberries, half a wheel of hard cheese wrapped in waxed cloth, and a jar of pine salve that smelled like Ines's childhood. Maren had pressed it into her hands at the door and held them there a moment too long.

"Your mother crossed in winter once," the old woman had said. "She came through my door after, blue to the elbow, laughing. Remember that. Laughing."

But Ines could not imagine her mother laughing, not anymore. Her mother lived now only as a shape in the mine's echoing dark, a name spoken by ravens, a warning. She crossed in winter and she died for it, and here was Ines doing the same arithmetic with a different sum.

"Up," Paloma said.

Not in words, of course. The girl had learned to say *up* with her whole body—arms lifted, chin tipped, eyes certain—and Ines bent without argument and let the child climb onto her shoulders, then shifted her hips until Paloma rode high against her pack like a small warm stone.

Above them, the sky was the color of an old scar.

* * *

The foxes found them at midday, or perhaps Ines found the foxes; with her gift the distinction had always been blurry, a conversation that simply began wherever two minds happened to touch. Three of them, red as embers against the grey scree, sitting in a row on a shelf of stone as though they had been waiting for an appointment.

*"You are late,"* said the eldest, a vixen with a torn ear and the particular impatience of a creature who has raised too many kits to suffer fools.

"We're going to the Threshold," Ines said aloud, because speaking aloud kept her anchored, kept the words from spilling out of her like water from a cracked jug. "The First Threshold. You know the way."

*"We know the way the way knows itself,"* the vixen replied, which was fox logic and therefore useless and therefore true. *"Follow. Do not stop when I stop. Stop when I do not."*

Paloma laughed—a real laugh, bright and startling, the first one in days—and the youngest fox yipped in answer, delighted, and bounded up the slope. Ines felt something in her chest loosen and immediately resented it. There was no room on this mountain for softness. Softness froze.

They climbed.

The Shepherd's Tooth earned its name by the second hour. The path narrowed to a spine of wind-scoured rock with air on both sides, and Ines went along it the way her body remembered rather than the way her mind preferred: weight low, steps short, hands finding holds before her eyes did. Paloma, bless her, understood instinctively. The girl went quiet and still, her arms looped around Ines's neck, her breath measured, as though she too had crossed impossible places before—as though, Ines thought with a chill that had nothing to do with cold, some older Paloma had done exactly this, generations ago, carrying something heavier than a child carries.

Below them, far below, the valley they'd fled lay under its first snow. Somewhere down there, Voss was regrouping. Somewhere down there, Tam was either holding his silence or breaking it, and Ines had stopped trying to guess which. The mountain allowed no room for guessing. The mountain only allowed climbing.

By late afternoon the light had gone thin and coppery, and the wind had begun to speak in the register Ines feared—not the ordinary complaint of gusts over stone but a low sustained moan, the sound the peaks made when weather was being born somewhere out of sight.

The vixen stopped on a shelf beneath an overhang of blue ice.

*"Shelter,"* she said. *"Tonight. The sky will close tomorrow and it will not open kindly."*

"How long will it stay closed?"

*"Long enough,"* the fox said, which meant *I don't know,* which meant everything.

They scraped out a hollow beneath the ice lip, stacked stones against the wind, ate half the smoked hare and all of nothing else. Ines gave Paloma the larger share and lied about having eaten on the climb. Paloma looked at her with those grave dark eyes—the eyes of a child who had watched her village burn and had decided, somewhere in the ashes, that lies told out of love were still permitted—and took Ines's hand and turned it palm-up, inspecting it, as if the truth might be written there.

Then she did something new.

She took Ines's forefinger and traced on it, slowly, deliberately: three strokes down, a curve, two strokes across. A word written in skin instead of air. Ines frowned, puzzled, and Paloma traced it again, patient, insistent, and on the third repetition something old stirred in Ines—the shape of a letter from a script she'd seen once, scratched into her mother's hidden things, a script the Crown had burned along with everyone who could read it.

"What does it say?" Ines whispered.

Paloma smiled, small and sad, and traced it one more time, and pressed Ines's closed hand against her own small chest.

*Mother*, Ines thought, and could not say it, and turned away to feed the fire so the child would not see what her face was doing.

That night she slept badly, dreaming of a door below the mountains that swung on rusted hinges, and every swing looser than the last.

* * *

The storm came the next morning exactly as promised, and it came like a punishment.

It did not arrive; it *descended*—the whole sky letting go at once, snow falling so thick and dry that it hissed against the rocks, the world contracting to a circle ten feet wide and shrinking. The wind hit from the north with fists. Ines roped herself and Paloma together with the coil from her pack—smuggler's habit, good rope, better knots—and they moved into the whiteout with the foxes as their only compass.

Or rather, with one fox. The other two had vanished into the storm within minutes, but the torn-eared vixen stayed ahead, materializing out of the white like a thought, sitting, waiting, moving on. Ines learned the rhythm of her: sit means rest is possible, move means move *now*. Once the fox led them sharply left around a cornice that Ines, stumbling blind, would have walked straight onto—a slope of wind-loaded snow hanging over nothing, waiting for exactly such a footstep to let go.

*"Thank you,"* Ines said, gasping.

*"Do not thank me. Count your steps,"* the vixen answered. *"Counting keeps the mind above the snow."*

So Ines counted. Four hundred steps to the col. Six hundred more along the ridge with the wind shoving her sideways like a drunk giant. Somewhere past eight hundred, the counting became a prayer, and the prayer became just numbers, and the numbers were all that existed: her boots, the rope's tension, the small weight of Paloma clinging to her back, the ember-red flicker ahead.

The supplies failed quietly, the way supplies do. The cheese went first—frozen to iron, then lost, she suspected, to a marten that raided the pack during the second night's miserable bivouac in a rock crevice. The bilberries lasted two days. The smoked hare, eked and rationed, lasted four, and on the fifth morning Ines shook the sack and heard only the sound of empty cloth, and stood very still in the grey dawn light while the arithmetic of her own body presented itself: she could go hungry for a long time. Children could not.

There were mountain hares. She knew it, felt them the way she felt all living things, faint warm presences under the snow like coals. But hunting meant stopping, and stopping meant freezing, and the vixen pressed them onward with an urgency that Ines trusted more than her own hunger.

On the sixth day, Paloma stumbled and did not get up.

* * *

She didn't collapse dramatically. That was the terrible part. She simply sat down in the snow, mid-stride, with the mild expression of someone setting down a burden, and looked at Ines with polite apology, as though asking permission to be finished.

"No," Ines said. "No. Get up, little one. We're nearly—"

She didn't know what they were nearly. The fox had gone ahead and not returned. The whiteout had thinned to a grinding grey haze but the ridge stretched on, featureless, endless. They had eaten nothing but snow-melt and a handful of lichen Ines had pried off the rocks and pretended was food for a full day. And Paloma sat in the snow, swaying, her lips blue-grey, and did not get up.

Something happened in Ines then that she would spend the rest of her life failing to describe. It was not decision. Decision is weighed and chosen. This was older and simpler—the thing in her that had carried the girl up the Shepherd's Tooth simply refused, absolutely and without debate, to accept the snow's answer. She knelt, bundled Paloma against her chest inside her coat, cinched the rope around them both until they were less two people than one clumsy animal, and stood.

"I've got you," she said. Her voice came out strange, roughened, almost tender. "You don't have to walk. I'll walk for both of us."

And Paloma, half-conscious against her collarbone, began to speak.

Not Old Speech—something smaller, threadbare, the way a candle gutters. Words in the tongue of her burning village, murmured over and over, a litany, a rope of sound she was hauling herself up hand over hand. Ines couldn't understand a syllable of it and understood all of it anyway. It was a counting. The child was counting her words the way Ines counted her steps, keeping her mind above the snow, holding on to the covenant one syllable at a time.

Then, mid-litany, Paloma faltered. Searched. Went silent.

And Ines *felt* it.

There was no other word. Through the soles of her boots, through the marrow of her, she felt a note drop out of the world's music—a thread parting somewhere far beneath the mountains, a door swinging a degree wider on its broken hinge. The Veil thinned, and the thinning passed through her like a cold wire. She staggered, caught herself, kept walking.

"Say it again," she begged. "Whatever you lost—say the ones you have left. Say them all. Don't stop."

Paloma obeyed. Weakly, wandering, sometimes losing the thread and beginning again—but she talked, and each recovered phrase seemed to knit something back, faint and fraying, and Ines climbed with the child's voice in her ear like a pulse she was keeping alive by hand.

Maren's voice surfaced in memory: *Each forgotten phrase weakens it further.* The witch had explained it beside her fire, turning her cup: the covenant was not a document, not a stone tablet alone—it was a living recitation, carried in keepers' mouths, renewed by speech. Paloma was the last mouth. Every word she lost to exhaustion, to cold, to grief, was a word struck from the pact forever. Time was not merely passing. Time was being *unspoken*.

So Ines walked, and Paloma talked, and the mountain took its toll of both.

* * *

The fox returned at dusk—or what passed for dusk, a dimming of the grey—and led them off the exposed ridge into a fold of the mountainside where the wind broke apart against standing stones. Ancient stones, Ines realized, as her vision swam into focus: a ring of them, tilted and furred with frost, carved with spirals worn nearly smooth. Waymarkers. Covenant-road markers. Her mother's people had walked here, generations of them, and the stones still remembered enough to hold a pocket of stillness against the storm.

She got Paloma inside the ring, got the shelter-cloth up between two monoliths, got snow melting in the tin cup over a flame the size of her thumbnail. The last of the pine salve she rubbed into the girl's frost-nipped fingers and cheeks, working the blood back with her thumbs, and Paloma watched her through half-closed eyes with an expression that undid Ines completely—not gratitude, not trust, something more unguarded than either. The look of a child watching her mother work.

Neither of them named it. Neither of them ever would. But it filled the little ring of stones like warmth fills a room, and Ines sat in the middle of it, exhausted beyond tears, holding the small chapped hands.

"You lost another word today," Ines said quietly. "I felt it."

Paloma nodded. Then she took Ines's finger again and traced the letter-shape from the first night—three strokes down, a curve, two across—and then traced several more, slower, teaching her, letter by letter, a whole phrase. Ines sounded it out in her mind, clumsy, halting. When she finally assembled it, her throat closed.

*Don't be afraid of the quiet parts.*

"The quiet parts are coming, aren't they," Ines said. "For me. After the Threshold."

Paloma didn't flinch and didn't lie. She traced the letters again, and then added one more stroke—a small mark beneath the phrase—and pressed Ines's hand to her heart a second time, harder, insistent, as if to say: *but look where it stays.*

Outside the stones, the storm screamed and found no purchase. Inside, Ines pulled the girl against her side beneath the shelter-cloth and felt the child's heartbeat slow toward sleep, and made herself a promise with no witnesses but the frost-furred spirals: whatever the stone asked at the Threshold, whatever price it named, she would pay it gladly, and this small warm weight against her ribs would walk out of the mountains into a world that was whole.

She slept. For the first time in six days, she truly slept.

* * *

She woke to silence of the wrong kind.

The storm had blown itself out in the night; the sky above the ring of stones was rinsed clean and violently blue, the snow blazing. Beautiful. Deadly. And the vixen stood at the edge of the ring facing downslope, ears forward, every hair on her raised spine standing like hackles on a much larger animal.

Ines was awake and reaching for her knife before her eyes fully opened.

*"Fox?"* she breathed. *"What is it? Storm's passed—we should move—"*

The vixen did not turn her head. When she spoke, her mind-voice had none of its usual wry impatience. It was flat and cold as the ice overhanging them.

*"The mountain has changed its mind about you, Speaker."*

"What do you mean?"

*"I mean the pass ahead is open,"* the fox said. *"Too open. No wolf crosses it. No bird flies it. Since moonset, the wind comes down that valley smelling of iron and men."*

Ines rose slowly, Paloma stirring against her side, and followed the fox's gaze down the long white throat of the pass toward the First Threshold—and saw, tiny with distance but unmistakable, a line of dark figures moving up the snowfield in perfect disciplined order, leaving a stitched trail of boot-prints behind them.

A company. Climbing toward the same door they were.

And at its head, even at this distance, a figure in officer's grey who paused, turned, and looked up the mountain directly at the ring of stones—as if she knew precisely where they slept.

Voss.

Paloma's small hand found Ines's and gripped it, and the girl began, very softly, to speak—to fill the morning with every word she had left, hoarding nothing, spending everything, while below them the Crown came climbing toward the end of the world.
