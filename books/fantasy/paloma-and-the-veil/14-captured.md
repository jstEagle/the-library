## 14. Captured

The pass betrayed them the way winter betrays a traveler who has grown too fond of the sun: slowly, then all at once.

Paloma felt it before she saw it. The mountain had been murmuring to her all morning—not in words, not yet, but in that low hum beneath the snow, the sound of stone remembering older weights. Then the murmur changed. It thinned. Stopped. As if someone had laid a hand over the mountain's mouth.

She stopped walking and pressed her palm flat against the drift beside the trail.

*What is it?* Ines asked without asking. Twenty days on the road had taught them both this language of shoulders and glances, though Paloma's true answers still came out in the Old Speech, syllables like river-stones turning over, and Ines had learned to read their shape if not their meaning.

*Men,* Paloma said. *Many men. Below the ice.*

Ines went very still, the way she did when hawks spoke to her, her whole body becoming an ear. "Voss," she breathed. Not a question.

The ravens had warned them at dawn—*iron comes, iron comes, iron smells of the low country*—but they had thought the warning meant the valley road, the wide way east. Tam's route was supposed to be secret. Tam's route was carved into no map, marked by no cairn, known only to smugglers and the foxes they bribed with salt pork.

Tam's route, which Tam had sold once already, and which Ines had turned into a false trail, and which Voss had therefore learned twice over—the second time by putting Tam's crew in irons again and letting him watch them shiver.

Paloma understood all of this only later, pieced together from Ines's curses and the wolves' bitter testimony. In the moment, there was only the mountain going quiet, and then the snow ahead of them standing up.

That was how it looked. The white slope to their left simply rose, shedding drifts, and became men—twenty of them, thirty, gray-cloaked and gray-hooded, so long buried in their hiding places that ice crusted their shoulders like scales. Veilbreakers. Their crossbows were already raised, already steady, as if they had been aiming for hours at the exact place two travelers would finally stop walking.

"Captain says no arrows unless she asks for them." The voice came from everywhere and nowhere, bounced off the ice walls of the pass. Then Serane Voss herself stepped out from behind a spur of black rock, unhurried, her sword sheathed, her hands folded like a woman arriving at a dinner party. Her breath made small neat clouds. "Good morning, Wren Quintana. Good morning, little Voice."

Paloma did not answer. She had learned that much from Ines: silence is a wall you can stand behind.

But Voss smiled at her silence the way a locksmith smiles at a lock. "You see?" she said to her soldiers, almost warmly. "This is why we do not harm the child. A child who can be taught when to speak—and when not to—is worth ten thousand swords."

They took Ines first. That was the worst of it—not the ropes, not the cold, but watching Ines let it happen. Watching the woman who spoke with bears and bargained with rockslides go quiet and still, her eyes finding Paloma's over the soldiers' shoulders with a message in them that needed no Old Speech to translate:

*Live. Endure. I will find you.*

Then a hood came down over Paloma's head, smelling of wool and lamp oil, and the world became footsteps and rope-jingle and the long groan of the mountain under many boots.

* * *

They kept her hooded through the descent, which told Paloma something: even Voss's own soldiers were not permitted to look too long at the child whose words unmade doors. She counted turns by the feel of the ground—down, always down, out of the wind, into pine shadow, onto old paving stones that rang hollow beneath her boots. A road built by people who had believed the mountains would love them forever.

When the hood came off, she stood in a courtyard walled in raw granite, a fortress grown into the pass itself like a tooth into a jaw. Torchlight. Iron braziers. And at the center, a chair, and in the chair, Serane Voss, waiting as though Paloma had kept *her* waiting.

There was a table between them. On the table: bread, cheese, a cup of milk still steaming. Someone had thought about what a child would want. Someone had been instructed to think about it.

"Sit, please," Voss said.

Paloma sat. She was hungry enough that her stomach embarrassed her, but she did not touch the food. She had watched Ines refuse gifts from strangers on principle, and some principles, Paloma had decided, were worth borrowing even when you didn't understand them.

"You're angry," Voss observed. "Or frightened. Or both. I want you to know that neither is necessary." She leaned forward, elbows on knees, and her voice dropped into something almost gentle. "Do you know what happened to your village, Paloma?"

The name struck like a hand across the face. Paloma flinched before she could stop herself, and hated the flinch, and knew Voss had seen it.

"Yes," Voss continued softly. "I know your name. I know your village sang the covenant-keeping songs for nine generations. I know your grandmother taught you the Threshold verses before you could tie your own boots. I know because I have spent three years reading everything the Crown's archives hold about people like you." A pause, delicate as a knife laid flat. "People like my mother."

Paloma stared at her.

"Oh yes." Voss's smile was thin and joyless. "You didn't think the Crown sends just anyone to hunt its own oldest secrets? My mother kept a sanctuary in the eastern hills. Smaller than yours. She taught me four words of the Old Speech before the Veilbreakers came for her, and I learned the rest of my education from the men who burned her sanctuary down." She spread her hands. "So understand me, child. I am not your enemy because I hate what you are. I am here because I am the only one in this army who knows exactly what will be lost if the Crown's fools get their way—and I intend to be the one holding you when that moment comes. Protection, not possession. There is a difference."

Paloma thought of the mine walls whispering distorted echoes. She thought of the drowned chapel flooding with light. She thought of Maren's hands shaking as she explained what happens when the covenant fails: *everything beneath the mountains dies, child, and everything above it goes mad with grief.*

And she thought: *this woman believes her own kindness.* That was the terrible thing. Voss was not lying. She was simply wrong in the way mountains are wrong when they decide a valley belongs to them.

Paloma said nothing.

Voss waited. The torches crackled. Somewhere above, wind worried at the battlements.

"You'll speak eventually," Voss said, rising. "Everyone does. But I'll tell you a secret, since we're going to be family now." She paused at the door, and her voice lost its warmth entirely, became something clinical and cold. "My scholars tell me the covenant lives in your voice. Every word you speak in the Old Speech strengthens the Veil; every word withheld weakens it. So consider, tonight, in whatever cell we give you, whether your silence is really defiance—or whether it is surrender wearing defiance's clothes."

The door closed behind her. The bread went cold on the table.

* * *

The cell was not cruel. That was its cruelty. A pallet, a blanket, a bucket, a high slit of window showing a stripe of evening sky. They wanted her comfortable. Comfortable children talk.

Paloma sat on the pallet and hugged her knees and did the arithmetic Voss had given her, because she could not help doing it. It ran through her head in her grandmother's cadence, the way all hard lessons returned home eventually:

*Every word strengthens. Every silence weakens.*

Was it true? She reached out with the sense Ines had been teaching her to trust—that listening-place behind the breastbone where the world's older voices gathered. And she heard it. Faint, frayed, but unmistakable: the Veil beneath the fortress, stretched like wet cloth over a drum. And every minute she sat mute, she felt another thread thin. Not metaphor. Thread. She could hear threads snapping somewhere far below, tiny sounds like ice crystals collapsing in warm air, and each snap sent a tremor through the deep places where things slept that should keep sleeping.

Her silence was tearing it. Her silence, chosen as armor, was doing the Veilbreakers' work for them.

She almost laughed. It would have been a wild, frightened laugh, the kind Ines called *mountain laughter*, heard most often from travelers three days lost. All those years of her village teaching her that speech was sacred, that words were load-bearing stones in the arch of the world—and she had survived the burning by learning to be silent, and now silence itself was the fire.

*Very well,* she thought. *You want me to talk?*

She began with her grandmother's evening blessing, the one that opened every supper in the sanctuary, spoken soft enough to fit inside her own cupped hands:

*"Stone remember. River remember. Root and wing and sleeping fur, remember us, for we remember you."*

The thread-snap sounds stopped.

It was small, what happened next. No light flooded the cell. No guards came running. But behind her breastbone, the listening-place filled like a cup held under a spring, and far below the fortress, Paloma felt the Veil draw one slow breath and hold it. Strengthening. One word at a time.

So she kept talking.

She talked the way the lonely talk—quietly, continuously, to no audience but the dark. She recited the Threshold verses Maren had drilled into her until her throat was raw. She named every villager she could remember, all forty-one, saying each name twice because her grandmother said names deserved to be said twice: *Maren-of-the-sanctuary, Joss-the-bellringer, Auntie Sef who burned the good bread, little Corvin who chased goats.* She described the drowned chapel to the darkness, and the beast court at Hollow Ford, and the exact weight of Ines's hand on her hair during the whiteout crossing, when neither of them had said anything and nothing needed saying.

She talked about Ines. That was the well she returned to most often, when the verses ran dry and the names ran out. How Ines lied badly and loved carefully. How Ines had carried her through snow that ate grown men, humming a lullaby through gritted teeth, refusing to set her down even when Paloma begged her to save herself. How Ines had looked at her over the soldiers' shoulders in the pass, and how that look was a promise, and promises made by people who talk to beasts are not like other promises.

Somewhere past midnight, boots passed her door. Paused. Moved on. Once, very faintly, through the stone, she heard a dog whine—a jailer's dog, big-pawed and unhappy, pacing a corridor below. Paloma sent a thread of the Old Speech after it, barely a breath: *"Good dog. Sad dog. Wait and watch, faithful heart."*

The whining stopped. The paws settled outside her door, and stayed.

Near dawn, exhausted, hoarse, she arrived at the last verse her grandmother ever taught her—the one for endings, the one Maren said could only be spoken truly by someone who meant it:

*"What is broken is not ended. What is ended is not lost. What is lost is waiting, patient as seed under snow, for the hand that planted it to come back for the harvest."*

She whispered it into her knees, and meant every word, and felt the Veil beneath the fortress settle like a great animal curling up to wait.

Let Voss come back with her gentle voice and her cold arithmetic. Let the scholars measure and the soldiers guard. They had put the last Voice of the covenant in a stone room alone with nothing but her own words—and they had handed her the one weapon no army could confiscate.

They had given her time to speak.

Paloma closed her eyes, kept the verse turning softly under her breath like a hearth-fire banked for morning, and listened—to the dog breathing beyond her door, to the threads knitting themselves whole below, and, faint and far and unmistakable, to something moving through the high passes toward the fortress with the sure, silent tread of someone who knew every smuggler road the Crown had never mapped.

Someone was coming for her.

Paloma smiled into the dark and did not stop talking.
