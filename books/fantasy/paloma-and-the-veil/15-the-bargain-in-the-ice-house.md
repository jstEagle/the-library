## 15. The Bargain in the Ice House

The cold found Ines first.

It came up through the stone floor in slow waves, the way cold does when it has been waiting a long time for company—patient, proprietary, as though the room had been keeping itself frozen on spec. She sat with her back against the wall and her wrists raw from the manacles, which had been removed an hour ago or a day ago; time moved strangely in a place with no windows. The walls sweated. The ceiling was ribbed ice, ancient and refrozen so many times it had gone grey as old bone.

An ice house. Of course. Somewhere beneath the Crown garrison at the pass, some enterprising quartermaster had carved storage chambers into the mountain's shoulder, and now one of them held her instead of winter mutton. She almost laughed at that. Almost.

She had been counting her breaths to keep track of hours, and she had reached somewhere past four thousand when the door opened.

Serane Voss came down the stairs alone, carrying a lantern and two tin cups. She had changed out of her riding leathers into a plain officer's coat, unadorned, the blue of it faded to something like the color of dusk. Without the sword and the smile she wore like armor, she looked younger. Tired. A woman who had been awake too long thinking about things she did not want to think about.

"Tea," Voss said, setting one cup within reach of Ines's chained hands. "It's terrible. The garrison boils everything twice."

Ines looked at the cup and did not touch it.

"I didn't poison it," Voss said. "If I wanted you dead, you'd be dead, and I'd have saved myself a great deal of paperwork."

"If you wanted me *talked to*," Ines said, "you'd have sent someone who could lie better."

Voss laughed—a short, genuine sound that seemed to surprise even her. She lowered herself onto an upturned crate across the cell, knees apart, elbows on thighs, a soldier's posture worn soft at the edges. Up close, in lantern light, Ines could see the fine lines around her eyes and the way her gaze kept flicking, once, toward the ceiling—as if listening for something above the ice.

"You're not what I expected," Voss said.

"No one ever is. That's rather the point of expectations."

"The woman who talks to beasts." Voss turned her cup slowly in her hands. "I've read the file. It's thin. Twenty years of nothing—no taxes paid under any name we can find, no crimes charged except smuggling, no family, no history before Thornfell. You're a hole in the Crown's ledger, Ines Winterbourne. Do you know how much that frightens people?"

"Good," Ines said. "Let them be frightened. It's honest work."

Voss smiled again, but this time it did not reach anywhere near her eyes. She set the cup down and leaned forward.

"Here's what I've come to offer. Not amnesty—that was for the road, when I still thought you might be a fool. This is larger." Her voice dropped, went careful, the way people speak when they are about to say something they have rehearsed and half-believe. "Joint custody of the reforging. Crown and wild alike. The girl performs the rite at the First Threshold, the Veil is sealed, and the covenant is held in trust by both parties. We get order. They get protection. Nobody burns, nobody starves, nobody's village goes up in smoke because some magistrate three hundred miles away got nervous." She spread her hands. "It's the only sane solution left. I'm offering you a seat at the table."

Ines let the silence stretch until it creaked.

Then: "You said 'we get order.' Which we?"

Voss blinked. "The Crown."

"And 'they get protection.' The beasts. The veiled folk. Everything under the mountain." Ines shifted forward, chains clinking, and fixed the captain with a look she had learned from wolves—the flat, patient stare of something deciding whether you were prey. "Captain, in twenty years of listening to animals, do you know what I have never once heard? A wolf ask another creature for permission. Protection is what the strong call it when they decide where the weak will live. That's not a covenant. That's a kennel."

"It's *survival*," Voss said, and there it was—the crack in the officer's voice, the thing underneath. "You've seen what's coming. The Veil is thinning. Things are already spilling through—my scouts found a valley last week where the deer have antlers of glass and won't die. Villages are panicking. If this isn't managed—if it isn't *held*—there will be slaughter, and it won't be the beasts doing most of it. I'm trying to prevent a war."

"So am I."

"Then take the bargain!"

"I would," Ines said quietly, "if it were real."

The words landed. She watched them land—watched Voss go very still, the way a person does when a truth they've been carrying sideways is suddenly set upright in front of them.

"Explain that," Voss said.

"Your warrant had no name on it. Your orders, I'd wager, don't either—not really. So tell me plainly, Captain, and I'll know if you're lying because your face isn't good enough yet: when the reforging is done, who holds the seal? Who decides, ten years from now, fifty years from now, whether the Veil opens or stays shut? Is it a table with two chairs?" Ines leaned back against the sweating stone. "Or is it a vault with one key?"

For a long moment Voss said nothing. The lantern hissed. Somewhere far above, boots crossed a floor, unhurried.

And then Serane Voss did something Ines had not expected. She told the truth.

"There's a chamber beneath the palace in Carreth," she said. "I've seen the plans. I wasn't meant to see them—I was meant to be the arm, not the eye—but I read them anyway, because I'm the one who has to live with what my hands do." Her voice had gone flat, reciting. "The First Threshold isn't the only place a covenant can be anchored. There are older stones. Deeper ones. The plan is to take the child after the reforging, take the Words out of her mouth and into a written form—we have scholars working on transliteration already—and re-anchor the whole pact to a stone of our choosing. Under guard. Under law." She met Ines's eyes. "Under lock."

"A vault," Ines said.

"A vault."

"Not a table with two chairs."

"No," Voss said. "Not a table with two chairs."

Ines nodded slowly, and something in her chest that had been straining all night—hope, probably, that stubborn animal—finally lay down and died, and its death made room for something harder and cleaner. She had spent her whole life believing that people, like beasts, mostly wanted the same simple things: warmth, food, their young kept safe. She had built her mercy on that belief. Tam had tested it. Now Voss had broken it open entirely, because Voss was not cruel, and that was the worst part. Voss believed every word. Voss thought she was offering rescue.

"You were a covenant-keeper's daughter," Ines said.

Something flickered across the captain's face—grief, quickly shuttered. "My mother sang the Old Speech to me as a lullaby. When the purges came, she hid me in a root cellar and told me to count to a thousand, and when I came out the house was ash and she was gone, and the men who did it wore the same uniform I wear now." She looked at her own hands. "I joined the Crown because I decided that hiding in cellars gets children killed, and laws keep them alive. I have spent twenty years making that true. Don't you dare tell me it was the wrong choice."

"I'm not telling you anything," Ines said. "I'm just sitting here, in your ice house, watching you choose."

"Choose?"

"You know what happens to Paloma after the vault. You've read the plans. A child who carries living words doesn't survive having them cut out, Captain. Whatever's left afterward won't be a girl. It'll be a page." Ines's voice did not rise. It never needed to. "You know it. You came down here knowing it, hoping I'd give you a reason to believe otherwise, and I can't, because there isn't one. There's no deal to be had. There never was. You just needed to hear it from someone who wasn't holding the pen."

Voss stood abruptly. The crate scraped. For a heartbeat Ines thought the captain might strike her, and then she saw the truth instead: Voss's hand was shaking, and she pressed it flat against her thigh to hide it, and her eyes were wet and furious about being wet.

"Enjoy the tea," Voss said hoarsely. "It's terrible."

She climbed the stairs. The door boomed shut behind her, and the bar fell with a sound like a knuckle cracking, and Ines sat alone in the cold with two cups of terrible tea and the shape of the future standing over her, unmistakable now, no longer wearing anyone else's face.

* * *

She gave herself one hour to despair. She measured it in breaths, as she measured everything, and she used it well—letting herself feel the full weight of the bars, the distance to Paloma (somewhere above, in the garrison proper, guarded by strangers), the memory of her mother's hands teaching her the names of hawks before the Crown took her mother's tongue and then her life. Despair was honest. Despair deserved its hour. But it did not get two.

When the hour ended, Ines began to work.

The manacles were iron, old iron, pitted with rust from the dripping walls—and rust, as any smuggler knew, was a friend. She worked her right wrist against the shackle's rough edge in slow, steady strokes, pausing whenever boots passed overhead, and while she worked she listened.

That was the other thing. The Veil was thinning everywhere, the ravens had told her, and thin places leaked. Even here, under a hundred feet of garrison stone, she could feel it: a faint pressure behind her ears, like the moment before a storm breaks, and threaded through it—faint as a smell of rain—the presence of something alive and warm and near.

A dog. There was a dog somewhere close. She could feel its attention like a candle flame felt by someone walking home in the dark.

She turned her mind toward it, gently, the way she had done ten thousand times with hawks and foxes and one memorable badger, and spoke without sound, in the wordless grammar of image and need.

*I am below. I am caught. I am small and sad.*

The attention sharpened. Curious. Then—she felt it arrive like sunlight through a crack—sympathy.

The dog came to her in pieces, through the stone: the scrape of claws on wood, a wet nose testing the gap under a door, the long patient sigh of an animal settling down to wait outside a cell it had decided belonged to a friend. It was a big dog. She could tell by the weight of its breathing. Some kind of mastiff or herd-guard, the jailer's own by the collar-jingle, and it stayed all night, pressed against the far side of the wall, sharing its warmth through six feet of rock as though warmth were a thing that could be posted through stone.

Near dawn—it must have been near dawn, though the ice house kept no calendar—the rust gave way.

Her right hand slipped free with a sound no louder than a sigh, and she went to work on the left with renewed patience, and above her the dog began, very softly, to whine.

*No,* she told it. *Quiet. Quiet, good heart. Wait.*

But the dog had heard something she hadn't: footsteps on the stairs, early, hurried. The bar scraped. The door swung open on a young jailer with sleep still in his eyes and a ring of keys in his fist—and stopped dead, staring at the empty shackles, at Ines standing free in the middle of the cell with her ruined wrists at her sides.

They looked at each other for the length of one breath.

Ines saw it all in that breath: the boy's fear, his youth, the dog's leash hooked to his belt, the way his eyes went first not to his prisoner but to the floor—to the place where his dog slept nights, as if checking whether she had heard it too. He was maybe nineteen. He had a mother somewhere. He had signed on to feed prisoners, not to fight women who talked to wolves.

Behind him, filling the doorway, the mastiff pushed past his legs and sat down at Ines's side like a boulder rolling to rest, and leaned its enormous warm weight against her hip, and looked up at the boy with an expression of profound, immovable expectation.

"Bramble," the boy said helplessly. "Bramble, no."

"She's a good judge of character," Ines said. "Better than the Crown, I think."

The boy's hand hovered over his keys. Over his knife. Over the alarm rope that would bring forty soldiers down the stair. And Ines did the only thing she had left, the thing that had cost her everything once already and would cost her more before the end: she spoke to him, human to human, plainly, with nothing hidden.

"They're going to hollow out a little girl to fill a vault," she said. "You've seen her. The child they brought in yesterday. You've brought her food—you have flour on your sleeve from the morning bread. Look at your sleeve, son, and then look at your dog, and then choose which of us you want to be when this story gets told."

The boy looked at his sleeve. At Bramble. At the dark stairwell behind him, where Captain Voss's terrible tea and terrible plans waited in the grey morning light.

His hand closed—not on the rope, not on the knife, but on the keys—and he pulled the largest one free and held it out with a shaking arm, and Bramble's tail swept the icy floor once, twice, like a slow metronome counting down to something.

Above them, faint through a hundred feet of stone, a bell began to ring.

Not the alarm bell. The other one—the deep, cracked bell that garrisons rang only when the world outside had come undone. And beneath its tolling, rising from the valley below, Ines heard a sound she knew better than her own heartbeat, a sound she had not heard since her childhood and had mourned in advance for twenty years:

Wolves. Hundreds of them. Singing the mountains awake.
