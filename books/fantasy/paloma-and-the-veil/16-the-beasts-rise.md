## 16. The Beasts Rise

The cage rolled south through a country that had stopped pretending to be at peace.

Paloma felt it before she saw it—the way the air itself had begun to fray, like cloth rubbed thin at the elbow. Every mile the Veil weakened, and every mile she felt the loosening in her own mouth. Words that had lived in her since before memory were slipping loose like teeth. That morning she had reached for the word for *west*—a word with wind in it, a word her mother had used to call the geese—and found only a smooth blank place where it had been.

She pressed her tongue to the gap and made herself speak anyway. Quietly, into the straw of the wagon bed, so the guards on the bench would not hear.

*I am the last voice. I will not be quiet.*

The words came out small. But they came out.

* * *

In the village of Harrow's Bend, three days' walk below the pass, the first of them came down out of the treeline at dusk.

It was an elk—but no elk any of them had seen, its antlers grown wrong, branching like frost ferns, its coat the grey-white of old birch bark, its eyes lit from within by a pale green that was not reflection but *source*. It walked down the high street without hurry while dogs went mad behind their fences and a smith dropped his hammer and a woman pulled her children indoors and bolted the door with hands that shook.

The elk stopped at the well. It looked at the water for a long time. Then it looked up at the shuttered windows, at the village entire, and it made a sound—not a bellow, not a whistle, but something between, something that carried meaning the way a bell carries sound through walls.

Everyone who heard it understood the same thing, though none could say how.

*You did this.*

Then it turned and walked back into the trees, and Harrow's Bend did not sleep that night, and in the morning the magistrate's men found hoofprints in the churchyard mud that steamed until noon.

By week's end there were others. A white fox trotting through the market at Dunlow with a pheasant in its jaws, ignoring the screaming, as if the market were simply forest now. A bear swimming the Alderwater in broad daylight while fishermen rowed for shore. Owls sitting on rooftops in daylight, dozens of them, silent, watching the roads. Watching the soldiers on the roads.

The Crown's criers called it plague. The priests called it judgment. The smugglers, who had always known what lived above the snowline, called it what it was.

The door below was open, and the things behind it were coming out, and they were not raging. That was the worst of it, people said afterward. They weren't raging. They were *arriving*, patient as creditors, as if the valleys had always belonged to them and everyone else had merely been borrowing.

* * *

In the foothills west of the Alder, Ines Winterbourne walked through a war that had not decided yet whose war it was.

She had come down out of the high country alone, which was its own kind of wound. After the ice house she had moved at night, sleeping in culverts and shepherd bothies, following the ravens because they were the only ones still speaking to her—and even their speech had changed. Where once they'd brought her gossip and grievance in equal measure, now their voices came clipped and urgent, stripped down to essentials, as if they too were rationing what remained.

*Road. Soldiers. Road clear. Go.*

She went.

Below her, the valley roads crawled with columns. She counted banners through the raven's eye—a raven perched on a supply wagon could see farther than Ines ever would, and she had learned to borrow its sight the way she'd once borrowed a hawk's patience. Voss had pulled companies out of the garrisons. Not to hunt a child anymore. To hold the valleys against what was rising out of the mountains. The net meant for two fugitives had become a cordon meant for the world's edge, and it was failing, because you cannot cordon weather.

*They are afraid,* the raven said, riding her shoulder as she picked along a streambed. The bird's feathers smelled of smoke; half the lowland rookeries had burned when panicked villages torched their own granaries. *Two-legs are all afraid. Fear makes them stupid.*

"Fear makes them dangerous," Ines said. "Stupid is just what happens after."

The raven clicked its beak—the closest it came to laughter these days. But there was no real humor left in it. When Ines asked what the courts were saying, the bird fell silent for a long moment, gliding ahead to a dead pine and waiting for her to catch up, which was itself an answer. Ravens did not wait for anyone.

"The High Court has broken silence," it finally said. "You understand what that means."

"I understand nobody breaks silence in eight hundred years over nothing."

"They blame your kind." The raven preened, not looking at her. "Not you. Not the child. Your *kind*. The covenant-keepers burned, the Speakers scattered, the Voices silenced—and now the debt comes due, and the whole species owes it. The wolves wanted to close every road. The elk wanted to walk through every village until the two-legs knelt. There was talk of—" it hesitated, and ravens did not hesitate—"of letting the Veil fall entirely. Letting everything beneath come through. Starting again without humans in the ledger."

Ines stood very still among the alders. "And what stopped them?"

"You did." The raven cocked one bright eye at her. "A wolf stood up in the court—young, scarred, the one who scouted your cabin two winters past—and said he had known a human who kept faith when keeping faith cost everything. He named no names. But every creature in that hollow knew whose den he meant." The raven shifted. "The court did not forgive. Do not mistake it. But it *waited*. And waiting, for a court that predates your kingdoms, is a kind of miracle."

"And the child?"

"The two-legs move her south. Caged. Toward the capital." The raven's voice flattened. "There are those in the court who say let the Crown keep her. Let the Veil fall and be done with the bargain forever."

"And the wolf?"

"The wolf," said the raven, "has already flown."

* * *

The prison wagon had four guards, and Paloma had spent six days learning all of them the way she had learned animals: by watching.

The young one with the chapped hands fed her first and always gave her the heel of the loaf rather than the soft middle, and looked away while she ate. The old sergeant slept badly and muttered about his daughter. The two on the bench spoke of pay and never of home, which told her they had none worth speaking of. None of them were cruel. That should have made it easier, she thought, and instead it made it harder, because cruelty she could hate, and these men she could only pity, and pity was heavier.

On the seventh night they camped in a river bend where the Alder ran wide and slow, and Paloma heard the wolves before any of the guards did.

Not howling. Howling would have woken them. This was lower—a long thread of sound under the river-noise, almost too deep for human ears, a frequency that lived in the chest. Paloma sat up in the cage. Her heart began to hammer, and she did not know whether it was fear or recognition, and then the thread of sound resolved into pattern and she knew.

It was a summons. The old gathering-call, the one that pulled packs together across forty miles of dark. And woven through it, repeated over and over, a single phrase in the Old Speech:

*Little sister. Little sister. We come.*

She pressed her face to the bars. "Here," she whispered. "I'm here. Here, here—"

One of the bench-guards stirred. She went silent, curled small, breathing slow the way the does breathed when they froze in bracken. The guard grumbled and settled. Above the black line of the firs, the clouds tore open, and moonlight spilled down the river like milk.

The attack, when it came, was over in less time than it takes to tell.

The horses went first—that was the way of it, always the horses, because a pack that can unmake your flight has already won. They came out of the treeline in a wedge, huge and pale-eyed, and they did not snarl and they did not bite. They *herded*. They split the camp like a stream splitting around a stone, drove the screaming guards into a knot against the wagon's wheel, and held them there with a wall of bodies and breath, and the message in that containment was unmistakable: *we could, and we choose not to, and understand the difference.*

The big one—the scarred one, grey-muzzled though he moved like a young animal—came to the cage. Up close his eyes were the green of deep water, and when he looked at Paloma she felt herself being read, thoroughly and without shame, the way she had been read once by the beast court at Hollow Ford.

"Last Voice," he said. His speech was accented, rough-edged, a mouth built for other sounds. "The court sends its apology for the wait."

"You came," Paloma said, and her voice cracked on it, and she hated that and didn't care. "The court wouldn't escort me. You said it was our debt. You said—"

"The debt is yours." The wolf lowered his head so their eyes were level. "The rescue is ours. A court may refuse to carry a stranger's burden. It may not watch a cub die in a cage while owing her mother's blood." His lip lifted slightly, not quite a snarl, not quite anything else. "Your sanctuary burned. Its keepers died speaking. We do not forget the ones who die speaking."

He took the lock in his jaws. It was iron and good workmanship and it lasted four seconds.

"Where?" Paloma asked as the door swung. Her legs shook when she hit the ground. "Where do I go? They said the Threshold, but I don't—I lost the word for west, I lost—"

"North and east. Above the falls where the river remembers being rain." The wolf turned his head, and beyond him the pack had begun to flow back toward the treeline, unhurried, the guards huddled and untouched and weeping with relief they didn't understand. "Ravens will find you. They are finding another, even now—one who walks the foothills with grief in her pockets." His eyes came back to Paloma, and in them was something fierce and old. "Run, little sister. Run like the debt depends on you. Because it does."

She ran. Behind her she heard the sergeant shouting orders into the dark, and the dark gave back nothing, because by then the wolves were shadows and the shadows were gone.

* * *

The ravens found Ines at dawn, in numbers.

They came down out of a sky the color of dirty wool—first two, then ten, then thirty, wheeling over the alder stand where she'd sheltered, their cries carrying a cadence she hadn't heard since before the ice house. Urgency, but not alarm. Direction.

"She's free," the lead raven said, landing on her knee, and Ines's breath left her as if she'd been struck. "The wolves took her off the south road two nights past. The Crown is lying about it—there are notices in every village, a girl fled her captors, reward for return, the usual ink. But she is free, and she moves north, toward the falls."

"Alone?"

"With a pack around her like a wall of teeth. For now." The raven hop-stepped along her knee. "But the Crown is not chasing her with patrols anymore. Voss has stopped being a captain. She is taking everything—garrisons emptied, roads seized, a column moving north with wagons and chains and fire-pots. Whatever she means to do at the Threshold, she means to do it with an army."

Ines rose. Her body had opinions about rising—six weeks of cold and hunger and grief had filed them all down to a single dull complaint—but she rose, and the ravens rose with her, thirty wings beating the mist apart.

"Which way to the falls?"

"*We* go," the raven said. "All of us. The court broke its silence for this. Did you think we'd send you walking?" It lifted off her knee and wheeled overhead, and its cry came down sharp and clear. "The foothills are burning, Speaker. Two-legs fighting beasts, beasts fleeing two-legs, villages against villages. The law is a rumor. The roads are a lie. So we will do what we have always done when your kind fails—we will make a road ourselves. Fly when we fly. Sleep when we sleep. Trust nothing with a banner."

They flew, and Ines followed, north and east through a country coming apart.

She saw the war from above, borrowed through a hundred raven eyes: a mill town where farmers had formed a ring around their fields with pitchforks and prayer while a herd of strange elk walked the boundary line all night, neither attacking nor leaving, until at dawn the farmers understood they were being *shown* something—the old boundary stones under the weeds, the covenant-marks worn nearly smooth—and the eldest farmer knelt in the mud and wept. A bridge where soldiers had fired on a white fox and the arrows had passed through it like light through light. A valley where the Veil had thinned so far that the air itself shimmered, and through the shimmer things moved that had no names Ines knew, vast and slow and sorrowful, and the people who saw them put down their weapons and sat down in the grass.

Everywhere, the same shape underneath the chaos: the old order failing not with a roar but with an unveiling, lies burning off like morning fog, and what remained when the fog lifted was simply true. The beasts had kept faith longer than the kings. The smallest voices had been the load-bearing ones. Justice had gone on living in the wild places, outside every courtroom, waiting for humans to remember where they'd put it.

On the third day the ravens brought her to the river gorge above the falls, and the mist rose up to meet her, cold and enormous, and somewhere inside that mist—she knew it the way she knew her own heartbeat, the way she had known it since a storm-night knock at her door—was a child.

The lead raven landed on a stone beside her, feathers ragged, eyes bright.

"She is near," it said. "But so is the army. One day, perhaps two. Choose quickly, Speaker."

Ines looked north, into the falling water and the roar, and began to climb.
