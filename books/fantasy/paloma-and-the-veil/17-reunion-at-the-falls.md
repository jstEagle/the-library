## 17. Reunion at the Falls

The ravens found her before dawn.

They came down through the freezing fog in ones and twos, settling on the black branches above the trail like a parliament convened in mourning, and their voices were ragged with distance. *Up. Up. The small voice climbs. The iron men climb faster.*

Ines stood in the shallows of a stream that had no name on any Crown map, her boots half-frozen, her breath tearing out of her in white rags, and listened to the sky tell her that Paloma was alive.

She had not let herself believe it for eleven days. Eleven days since Voss's soldiers had taken them both at the pass; nine since she had gone over the wall of the ice house with nothing but a jailer's dog barking cover and a stolen knife; seven since she had learned from a chattering magpie that the child had been moved north, toward the Threshold, because Serane Voss wanted the reforging done under Crown eyes or not at all. Seven days of climbing alone through country that was coming apart at its seams—valleys where the Veil had thinned so far that stags walked openly through barley fields and farmers burned their own barns to keep the things inside them out.

*Where?* she asked the ravens. *Where is she now?*

*Above the falling water,* they said, all together, a sound like torn silk. *Above the falls. She sings to keep the door from forgetting. But Ines Winterbourne—the iron men are behind her, and the pass will not hold long against what comes up it.*

Ines closed her eyes. Above the falls meant the last shelf of mountain before the First Threshold—a place she had known about for twenty years and never dared approach, because approaching it meant admitting she remembered her mother's hands shaping the Old Speech into the dark of their cabin, and remembering that meant grieving it, and she had spent two decades building her whole life on top of that grief like a house built on a grave.

Now there was no time to grieve anything.

"How far?" she asked aloud, because speaking to birds in front of witnesses had stopped mattering somewhere around the burning of Paloma's village.

*A day,* said the oldest raven, the one with the scarred beak who had first told her the door below was loud. *A day for you. Half a day for the soldiers. And behind them, Ines—behind them comes something worse than soldiers. The Veil is failing. What sleeps beneath the Threshold has begun to turn over in its sleep.*

"Then I'd better run," Ines said, and ran.

* * *

She climbed through the morning like a woman being chased by her own heartbeat.

The smuggler roads were gone—washed out, snowed under, or simply wrong, bending away from her when she needed them straight, as if the mountains themselves had begun to lie. Twice she had to trust foxes to find her footing across scree fields that shifted like water. Once she crossed a frozen lake where something vast moved beneath the ice, slow as a thought, and she did not stop to learn what it was.

Her body was failing her. She knew it the way an old rope knows its own fraying: the cough that wouldn't leave her chest, the hands that shook when she ate, the way the world went silver at the edges when she climbed too fast. Twenty years of winters in the Thornfell had been spending her coin all along, and now she was making withdrawals she couldn't cover.

It didn't matter. None of it mattered. There was a child above a waterfall singing words older than kingdoms to keep a door shut, and every hour Ines wasted was an hour the door swung wider.

Near midday she came over a shoulder of granite and saw the falls.

They fell out of a hanging valley in three white ribbons, thundering down two hundred feet of black stone into a pool that steamed with cold. Above them the mountain rose in terraces toward a saddle of bare rock—and there, tiny against the snow, a figure sat cross-legged on the highest terrace, small and still, and even at this distance Ines could feel the words coming off her like heat off a forge.

Paloma's voice carried through the roar of the water the way light carries through glass. Ines couldn't make out the words—not in her own ears—but every living thing on the mountainside could. Ravens wheeled above the terrace in a slow protective spiral. A family of ibex stood motionless on an impossible ledge, facing the child like sentries. And along the trail below her, winding up through the switchbacks, came the soldiers.

Thirty of them. Maybe more. Red-and-iron livery of the Veilbreakers, moving fast and grim, with Captain Serane Voss riding at their head on a gray mare that picked its way over the rocks with uncanny sureness. Behind the column, further down, Ines saw the thing the ravens had warned her of: a darkness in the air itself, a shimmering wrongness where the Veil had worn through, and shapes moving inside it that had no business in daylight.

There was no way past thirty soldiers. There was no way around them. The only trail to the terrace went directly through their line.

Ines crouched in the rocks and did the arithmetic she'd been doing her whole life—goats and contraband and routes and risk—and for the first time the numbers refused to come out. Every path ended with her dead on the switchbacks and Paloma dragged to the Threshold in chains, and the covenant reforged as property, or not reforged at all.

That was when she heard the whistle.

Three notes, low and rough, echoing up the valley from the south—the old smuggler signal, the one that meant *friend coming, hold your fire*, the one Tam Vetch had invented himself because he could never remember the official ones. Ines turned so fast she nearly lost her footing.

Down the southern trail, strung out along the ridgeline like beads on a crooked wire, came men and women with crossbows and billhooks and the particular rolling walk of people who had spent their lives carrying other people's secrets over passes. At their head rode Tam Vetch on a shaggy hill pony, his beard full of frost, his left arm bound up in a sling, grinning like a man arriving late to his own funeral.

"Ines Winterbourne!" he bellowed, in a voice built for shouting across market squares. "You look terrible! Get down here before I cry!"

* * *

He embraced her with one arm and held on too long, the way men do when they've decided not to explain why.

"You're supposed to be in chains," Ines said into his shoulder.

"I escaped." He pulled back. His grin flickered, and underneath it she saw the truth: exhaustion, guilt, and something rawer. "Mostly. Voss took my crew at the river cave—you know that part. What you don't know is what happened after. They marched us east toward the lowland jails, and my people—" His jaw worked. "My people started singing. All of them. At once. Songs their grandmothers sang, songs nobody remembers teaching them. And the horses went mad, and a bridge that has stood four hundred years came down in the night, and by the time the Veilbreakers dug themselves out of the river, half my crew had vanished into the hills like smoke." He looked up at the falls, at the small figure on the terrace. "Her words are coming loose everywhere, Ines. Like seeds on the wind. Things are waking up that forgot how to wake."

"The rest of your crew?"

"Holding the southern ridge. Forty-one souls, give or take, armed with whatever we could pull out of my caches." He patted the sling. "I got this from a Veilbreaker sergeant who made the mistake of being between me and a friend of mine."

Ines felt something crack open in her chest that she didn't have a name for. Twenty years she had kept everyone at the length of a transaction—goats for grain, silence for passage, favors banked like coins. She had believed that was wisdom. Standing in the frost with Tam Vetch's blood on his sleeve and his crew on the ridge for her sake, she understood it had only ever been fear.

"Tam," she said. "Voss sold you to me once. I forgave you for it. You don't owe me—"

"I know what I owe." He cut her off gently. "I owed a debt to a woman who showed mercy when mercy cost her something. That's not the kind of debt a man walks away from." He turned and looked up the switchbacks, at the red-and-iron column crawling toward the falls, and his face went hard and professional. "Voss has thirty on the trail and another twenty holding the pass behind us. We can't beat them in the open. But this is my country, Ines. These are smuggler roads. And there's a thing about smuggler roads that the Crown has never learned."

"What's that?"

"They only have to work once." He grinned again, and this time it was the old grin, the wolfish one, the one that had laughed at magistrates for thirty years. "Get yourself up that trail. My people and I will arrange for the Crown to take the scenic route."

* * *

The plan was simple, which was how Ines knew it would nearly kill them all.

Tam's crew would hit the Veilbreaker column at the third switchback, where the trail narrowed to single file against a drop of eighty feet. Crossbows from above, rockfall from the cliffs, chaos in the ranks—and while the soldiers were busy dying slowly on a ledge, Ines would climb the goat track on the eastern face, the one that wasn't a track so much as a rumor, straight up to the terrace above the falls.

"It's suicide," Tam said cheerfully. "Which is why it'll work. Nobody guards a wall that can't be climbed."

"I've climbed it," Ines said. "When I was nineteen. With better knees."

"Then you'll climb it slower this time." He caught her wrist as she turned to go. His grip was strong despite the sling. "Ines. Whatever happens up there—whatever the stone asks of you—I want you to hear it from someone who's watched you for twenty years pretending to be nothing. You were never nothing. Not one day. Not to us."

She couldn't answer. She squeezed his hand once, hard, and climbed.

* * *

The eastern face was exactly as cruel as she remembered.

Forty minutes of frozen handholds and lying rock, her lungs burning, the cough doubling her over twice until she tasted copper. Below her the battle began—she heard it more than saw it: the snap of crossbow strings, the crash of stone, Tam's voice rising over everything in a bawled verse of some obscene marching song that half his crew seemed to know. The Veilbreaker column stopped advancing and started surviving.

And then, cutting through it all, a sound that froze Ines to the rock face.

Horns. Deep, brazen, disciplined—the Crown's signal for *company arms*, for a real fight, and beneath them Voss's voice ringing clear off the cliffs: *"Second company, wheel left! Loose at will!"*

They had reserves. Of course they had reserves. Twenty soldiers holding the pass, and Voss had fed them into the flank, and now Tam's crew—forty-one smugglers with hunting bows and farm tools—was caught between hammer and cliff.

Ines hung on the frozen wall of the mountain and heard her friends begin to die.

*Move.* She climbed. Hand over hand, boot over boot, blood cracking from her split fingertips onto the stone, the cold biting through her gloves until her fingers stopped reporting anything at all. *Climb. Climb. Everything depends on climb.*

She hauled herself over the lip of the terrace as an arrow buried itself in the rock where her head had been.

* * *

Paloma was waiting for her.

The girl stood at the center of the terrace with her back to the abyss, and the snow around her had melted in a perfect circle, and the air itself hummed. She was thinner than Ines remembered—days in chains, days without enough food—but her eyes were the same: enormous, ancient, eight years old and older than eight years old. When she saw Ines she made a sound that was not any language at all, just a child's cry, and then she was running, and then Ines was on her knees in the slush with her arms full of her.

Small fists gripped her coat. A small face pressed into her neck. And through the bond that had grown between them in mines and chapels and winter crossings, Ines felt the flood of everything the girl couldn't say in words Ines knew: *you came, you came, they said you wouldn't, they said you left me, you came.*

"I came," Ines said into her hair. "I'm sorry I'm late. Traffic on the mountain."

Paloma laughed—a wet, broken, glorious laugh—and pulled back and signed rapidly, the quick gestures they'd built between them on the road. *The stone. It's ready. It's been asking for hours. It wants a Speaker. It won't wait much longer.*

Behind her, at the terrace's heart, stood the First Threshold.

Ines had imagined it for twenty years and gotten it wrong. It was not a gate or an arch. It was a single standing stone, taller than two men, black as the inside of a closed eye, veined with lines of faint gold that pulsed like a sleeping pulse. The air around it bent, the way air bends over summer road, and through the bend Ines could see—just barely—shadows moving. The world beneath the mountains, looking out.

The stone was humming. And the humming had a question in it. Ines could feel it pressing against her mind like a hand against a door: *What will you give? What will you give? What will you give?*

"There," Paloma signed, pointing at the stone, and then her hand dropped, and her face changed, and she pointed past Ines instead—at the head of the trail.

Serane Voss climbed onto the terrace alone.

Her company stayed below, holding the switchbacks against Tam's battered line, but the captain herself came up the last stretch of trail unhurried, sword sheathed, helmet off, frost in her close-cropped hair. She stopped ten paces away and looked at the standing stone with an expression Ines had never expected to see on that composed face.

Reverence. And grief.

"Captain," Ines said, putting herself between Voss and Paloma, though her legs could barely hold her.

"Don't." Voss's voice was quiet. "Don't waste your breath defending her from me, Ines. I'm past that." She looked at the stone again, and her hand rose halfway toward it and stopped, as if the air near it burned. "My mother sang those words. Did you know that? Before they took her. Before I learned that the Crown's way was the only way a clever woman survives." Her jaw tightened. "I told myself seizing the covenant was protection. Control it and no one burns for it again. I told myself that all the way up this mountain." She drew a long breath. "And then your smuggler friends started singing on my bridge, and my soldiers' horses knelt to a child, and I watched the Veil tear open over the pass, and I have to ask you something, Ines Winterbourne, and I need you to answer honestly, because everything depends on it."

Below them, horns. Screaming. Arrows. Tam's line buckling.

"What?" Ines asked.

"The stone asks for something irreplaceable." Voss's eyes came up and fixed on hers, and in them was the wavering of a woman standing on the edge of everything she'd built her life on. "What are you going to give it?"

Before Ines could answer, the arrow took Tam Vetch in the shoulder, and the pass began to fall, and the stone beneath the mountain opened one golden vein like an eye, and asked its question again—louder this time, in a voice that shook snow from the peaks:

*What will you give?*
