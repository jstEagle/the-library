## 18. The First Threshold

The last stretch of the mountain did not want them there.

Ines felt it in her knees before she felt it anywhere else—the way the stone tilted against each step, the way the wind came down the face of the ridge not as weather but as refusal. Even the snow behaved strangely here, lying in long pale ribbons that seemed to have been combed into place by some enormous deliberate hand, as though the mountain itself had been making its bed for a thousand years and did not appreciate visitors.

Paloma climbed ahead of her, small boots finding holds Ines could barely see. The girl had not stopped speaking since the falls—not loudly, not to anyone in particular, but continuously, a low thread of Old Speech running beneath her breath like water under ice. Ines had learned to stop listening for meaning in it. The words were not for her. They were for the mountain, for the Veil, for whatever thin membrane still held between this world and the one beneath—and every hour that thread grew thinner, because Paloma was losing words even as she spent them.

She had lost *morath* two days ago. Ines knew because the foxes had told her, their voices suddenly muffled in her mind where they had once been bright as struck flint. She had lost *selun* at dawn. Each word that slipped away from the child took something with it—a color gone from the world, a warmth gone from the air, a note dropped from a song that had been playing so long no one remembered when it began.

And now, cresting the final shoulder of rock, Ines saw what all of it had been for.

* * *

The First Threshold was not what she had imagined. She had imagined—she admitted it to herself now, standing in the wind with her lungs burning—a door. A gate, perhaps, or an arch of worked stone, something with hinges and intention. Something built.

Instead there was only a hollow in the mountaintop, a bowl of bare granite perhaps fifty paces across, ringed by spires of ice-glazed rock like the teeth of some drowned giant. And in the center of the hollow lay the covenant-stone: a single slab of black basalt, longer than Tam's river boat, half-sunk in the earth as though the mountain had swallowed it up to the chin and then thought better of it and left it there, waiting.

It was covered in writing.

Not carved—Ines saw that immediately, though her eyes watered in the wind and she had to blink twice to be sure. The letters stood proud of the surface, raised like scar tissue, and they were moving. Slowly. The way glaciers move. The way continents move. Lines of Old Speech crawling over the black stone with the patience of something that had been saying the same sentence for three thousand years and would finish it eventually, whether or not anyone remained alive to hear the end.

Paloma made a sound Ines had never heard from her—not a word, not a cry, but something older than either. A recognition. The sound of a child seeing, for the first time, the house her mother had described to her a hundred times and never been able to show her.

The girl ran.

"Paloma—" Ines lunged after her, boots skidding on glazed stone, heart slamming against her ribs—but the hollow was smooth and the child was light and fast, and by the time Ines reached the center Paloma was already standing at the edge of the covenant-stone with both mittened hands pressed flat against the basalt, her lips moving, reading.

No. Not reading. *Answering.*

The words coming out of her were the words written in the stone, Ines realized. The same slow sentences, spoken at the speed of a child instead of the speed of geology. The stone said a line; Paloma said it back; and where her voice touched the writing, the letters brightened—faint gold rising through black, like embers breathing under ash.

And the Veil—

Ines felt it the way she felt weather in old wounds. The whole mountainside shuddered, gently, the way a sleeping animal shudders in dream. Somewhere far below—farther than any mine, deeper than any chapel—something vast turned over and listened.

Then the gold dimmed. The letters cooled. And Paloma stumbled back from the stone, gasping, her small face grey with exhaustion, and Ines caught her before she fell.

"It asks," Paloma whispered. Her voice was hoarse from days of unbroken speech. "It keeps asking. It won't take just the words."

"I know." Ines pulled the girl against her side, shielding her from the wind, and looked out over the ring of ice-toothed spires—at the narrow pass beyond them, at the dark thread of the smuggler road far below, at the place where, any hour now, a company of Veilbreakers would come pouring through with Serane Voss at their head.

The ritual needed a Speaker. Maren had told them that much, in the witch's warm low room while the marked light pulsed under their skin like a second heartbeat: *a Voice and a Speaker, both willing, both giving.* Paloma was the Voice. That had always been clear. But the stone wanted more than recitation. It wanted payment. It wanted something irreplaceable, given freely, from the one who stood beside the Voice and chose to stand there.

Ines had known, in the way one knows the weight of an axe one has carried for years without looking at it, what the stone would ask of her.

She had simply hoped, until this moment, to arrive without knowing.

* * *

They waited.

There was nothing else to do. Tam and his crew were holding the pass below—Tam, who had sold them twice and bled for them once, who had looked at Ines in the firelight two nights ago and said, *I can't give you back what I cost you, Wren, but I can make sure nobody else adds to the bill*—and every rattle of distant musket-fire or scream of gulls told her the debt was being paid in full. The ascent behind them was closed. For now.

So they waited in the hollow, and Ines built a fire in the lee of the great stone because Paloma's hands were shaking, and fed the girl dried elk and snowmelt, and watched the light change over the ringed horizon as the sun crawled west.

It was strange, she thought, how quiet the top of the world was. No trees. No birdsong. Only wind, and the faint subsonic murmur of the stone, and Paloma's ceaseless thread of Old Speech, which she had resumed the moment her hands stopped trembling—as if she understood, better than any adult Ines had ever known, that silence was the true enemy.

"You're thinking about giving it your voice," Paloma said suddenly, in the common tongue, not looking up from the fire. "Aren't you."

Ines went very still. Twenty years of practice kept her face flat. "What makes you say that?"

"The wolves talk about you." The girl poked the fire with a stick, matter-of-fact. "When they think I'm asleep. They call you *the one who listens*. They say you've never once asked them for anything. They say—" she hesitated, and her voice went smaller, "—they say you're lonely in a language nobody else can hear."

The wind moved through the spires and made a sound like slow breathing.

"They're right," Ines said at last. It felt enormous, saying it aloud. It felt like setting down a pack she'd forgotten she was wearing. "I was four years old when I first heard a hawk speak. My mother laughed and said I'd been given a door most people don't get. She didn't tell me the door only opens one way—that I'd spend my whole life on the wrong side of it, understanding everything and understood by nothing." She fed another stick to the fire. "My gift isn't a gift, little one. It's a room. And I've lived in it alone for twenty years."

Paloma set down her stick. She crawled across the space between them—an eight-year-old's graceless, determined crawl—and put her arms around Ines's middle, and pressed her face into the wool of her coat.

"Don't decide yet," she said into the fabric. "The stone hasn't asked."

"How do you know?"

"Because I can feel it thinking." A pause. "It's very old. It doesn't waste questions."

Ines put her hand on the child's hair and looked out past the ring of spires, at the darkening sky, and let herself believe—for one measured breath—that they might have until morning.

They did not.

* * *

Voss came at dusk.

Ines heard her before she saw her—not the company, not yet, but the woman herself, climbing the final pitch alone with her sword still sheathed and her hands empty and visible, the way a hunter approaches a snare it does not wish to spring. Behind her, far down the switchbacks, torchlight moved in a slow constellation: the last Veilbreaker company, held at the treeline by something. By Tam. By the pass. By whatever bargain Voss had made with her own soldiers to come up this mountain the way she was coming—with no arrows loosed, no net thrown.

Ines rose and put herself between Voss and Paloma, and the old reflexes sang through her—the hawk's warning cry gathering somewhere in her chest, the wolf's hackles, twenty years of beasts ready to answer if she called. Except the beasts were silent tonight. Far, far away. The Veil's failing had thinned everything, even her.

Serane Voss stopped ten paces off and stood in the last red light, and Ines saw that she was not the same woman who had offered amnesty on the lying roads. Something had been burned out of her—or burned clean. Her uniform was torn at the shoulder where a raven had clearly done its work. There was blood on her cheek that did not appear to be hers.

"I'm not here to fight," Voss said.

"No," Ines agreed. "You couldn't. Your company is pinned at the treeline and we both know it."

"My company is pinned because I ordered them pinned." Voss's voice was level, but her eyes went past Ines—to the stone, to the crawling gold letters, to the small figure wrapped in a blanket at the fire. Something moved across her face that Ines recognized, because she had seen it in mirrors for twenty years: the look of a person standing at the edge of a room they have lived in too long. "I wanted to see it. Before the end. Whatever the end is."

"Then watch," Ines said.

"I read my mother's papers." The words came out of Voss abruptly, as if they'd been waiting years for the door to open. "After the chapel. After the marks. I went back through everything the Crown confiscated from the sanctuary villages, everything I signed the burn orders for, and I read it. Do you know what the covenant actually says, Winterbourne? Not what the Veilbreakers' doctrine says. What it *says*?"

"Tell me."

"That power beneath the mountains was never meant to be owned." Voss's jaw tightened. "That it was *lent*. Freely. To people who gave freely back. And that every attempt to seize it—in my grandmother's time, in mine—hasn't weakened the Veil because the Veil is fragile." She shook her head slowly. "It's weakened because the Veil is *offended*."

Paloma stirred at the fire. "You sound like the ravens," she said.

Something cracked in Voss's expression—briefly, like ice underfoot. "I sound like a woman who burned a sanctuary village and told herself it was protection." She looked at Ines directly now, and her hand did not go to her sword. "I'm not asking forgiveness. I'm telling you why I'm standing here instead of down there with the men who follow me. If the stone takes what it needs—if it seals—I want to witness it. And if it doesn't—" her voice hardened, "—if it fails, then everything I believed was right, and I'll do what I came to do. Both of us live in one of those worlds. I'd like to know which one before dark."

Ines studied her for a long moment. Captain Serane Voss, who had hunted them across three hundred miles of lying road. Who had cornered them at the mine mouth. Who had, Ines now understood, been chasing not power but her own mother's ghost—the ghost of a covenant-keeper who had chosen the wrong side, or the Crown had chosen it for her, and whose daughter had spent her whole life building a fortress around that wound.

"We could use a witness," Ines said finally. "Someone the Crown will believe."

Voss almost smiled. It was a terrible, tired thing. "The Crown believes nothing. But yes. I'll witness."

* * *

Full dark came, and with it the stars—brutally clear at this altitude, cold as nailheads—and with the stars, the stone woke fully.

There was no gradual brightening this time. The gold letters flared all at once along the whole length of the basalt, and the hollow filled with light that cast no shadows, and the murmur that had run beneath the mountain for three thousand years became, unmistakably, a *voice*. Not male, not female. Old the way bedrock is old. It spoke in Old Speech, and Paloma translated as it spoke, her own voice rising to meet it, and Ines felt the hairs on her arms lift and her eyes sting and her heart hammer with a terror that was somehow also relief, because at last, at last, the thing that had been chasing them since a storm-night knock at her cabin door had arrived, and it was not the Veilbreakers.

*"The Voice speaks,"* the stone said, through Paloma's mouth and its own. *"The Voice has spoken since the burning. The Voice is weary, and the door sags on its hinge, and the things below press their faces to the membrane and wait."*

Paloma's translation wavered. She steadied it.

*"One more word is required. One more voice. The covenant was forged by many and broken by few, and it can be remade only by one who stands beside the Voice not by commandment, not by duty, not by fear—but by choice, and pays from her own hand."*

The light gathered. It gathered toward Ines. She felt it the way she felt the hawk's warning, the wolf's counsel, the ravens' gossip—a pressure, a presence, an attention vast and patient turning itself upon her like a searchlight.

*"Speaker,"* the stone said. *"What will you give?"*

And the hollow went utterly still.

The wind died. Paloma's thread of speech fell silent. Even Voss, standing rigid at the rim of the light with her hand finally, unconsciously, resting on her sword hilt—even Voss seemed to stop breathing. The stars burned. The letters glowed. The question hung in the frozen air between the stone and the woman, and Ines understood that it was not rhetorical, that it was not a test with a prepared answer, that the stone genuinely did not know—and that whatever she said next, in the next handful of heartbeats, would be the truth of her entire life distilled into a single offering.

She thought of the hawk on the ridge road, twenty years of mornings. Of the wolves calling her *the one who listens*. Of her mother's laugh, and the door that opened only one way, and the room she had lived in alone.

She thought of Paloma's arms around her middle, and the words *don't decide yet*, and how the deciding had already happened, long ago, on a night she hadn't recognized—maybe the night she'd taken the child in, maybe the night at Hollow Ford when she'd accepted the burden alone, maybe the night in the whispering mine when a small hand had found hers in the dark and neither of them had let go.

The stone waited. The light waited. Paloma looked up at her with eyes that held the whole veiled world underneath them, and did not say *don't*, and did not say *please*, and Ines loved her for that more than she could ever have said.

Ines opened her mouth.

Behind Voss, at the rim of the hollow, torchlight broke over the spires—the pass had fallen, or been abandoned, and the last Veilbreaker company was coming—and Ines stepped forward anyway, toward the stone, toward the question, and answered.
