## 19. What Ines Gives

The wind at the First Threshold did not howl. It waited.

Ines had climbed through arrow-fire and thinning air to reach this shelf of stone above the drowned world, and now that she stood upon it, the mountain itself seemed to hold its breath. The covenant-stone rose from the summit like a tooth of the earth—taller than three men, older than the oldest raven's memory, its surface veined with channels that glowed a faint, failing amber. Where once those veins had run like rivers of light, they now flickered in patches, guttering, the way a hearth gutters when the wood is spent. Paloma's words had been feeding it for days, each syllable drawn out of her like thread from a spool, and still it was not enough.

The stone needed more than speech. It needed a price.

Ines knew this the way she knew weather coming—some deep animal part of her had known since Maren's kitchen, since the old witch had said *a Voice and a Speaker, both willing*, and Ines had felt the word *willing* settle into her chest like a stone dropped into a well. She had spent the whole winter crossing telling herself there would be another way. There was always another way; she had built twenty years of survival on finding them. But the stone asked its question now, and the question had no clever answer.

*What will you give?*

It did not speak aloud. It spoke the way beasts spoke to her—a pressure behind the eyes, a meaning arriving without language—and Paloma heard nothing of it, only watched the dying light with her small fists pressed against the cold stone, whispering fragments of Old Speech into the veins as if she could breathe a fire back to life one ember at a time.

Behind them, down the switchback of the pass, Tam held the narrows with his freed crew and whatever gunpowder he'd hoarded across two decades of smuggling. The crack of muskets came up the mountain in flat echoes, spaced and patient. Voss's company was climbing anyway. They always climbed anyway. That was what made Veilbreakers Veilbreakers: not cruelty, but refusal to stop.

And Voss herself—Serane Voss, captain, collaborator, daughter of a burned covenant—was already on the summit shelf.

She had come up the eastern face with six soldiers, and Tam's men had let her through, because Ines had asked them to. She walked now with her sword drawn but pointed at the ground, snow crunching under her boots, her breath steaming around a face that looked less like a hunter's and more like a woman arriving late to a funeral she'd been avoiding for years. Her soldiers hung back at the edge of the shelf, uncertain, their orders and their eyes at war.

"Step away from the stone," Voss said, and her voice cracked on the last word, which surprised her more than it surprised Ines.

Ines did not step away. She knelt beside Paloma instead and tucked a strand of hair behind the girl's ear, fingers numb, and Paloma leaned into the touch without looking up from her whispering.

"She can't stop," Ines said quietly. "You know that by now. If she stops, the Veil comes down tonight, and everything beneath these mountains—the beast courts, the old sanctuaries, every living thing your Crown has spent a century pretending isn't real—it all spills out or dies out. Probably both."

"The Crown doesn't want it to die." Voss's jaw tightened. "The Crown wants to *hold* it. A door with a lock is worth ten thousand open ones. Give me the child, Winterbourne. I'll put her somewhere safe. Comfortable. She'll want for nothing except—" she faltered, "—except everything."

"That's the arithmetic of a gaol," Ines said. "I've lived by arithmetic my whole life. Goats over passes, contraband through fog, always counting what a thing costs against what it brings. And I'll tell you what the sums never taught me." She rose, knees protesting, and turned to face the captain fully. "Some things are only worth what you lose by keeping them."

The stone pulsed faintly. The question pressed again against the inside of Ines's skull, patient as tide.

She thought about her mother then—she could not help it. A kitchen in a lowland village, a woman singing to the geese and having the geese sing back, and neighbors who noticed, and men who came before dawn. Ines had been four years old. She remembered being lifted onto a horse by strangers whose faces she had never seen again, and looking back at the burning house, and hearing—not screaming, not anything human—just the geese, calling her mother's name in their flat iron voices, over and over, because they had loved her too and had no way to follow.

Every gift Ines had ever used to survive had been bought with that fire. She had hidden inside it like a fox in a den. She had spoken to hawks and wolves and ravens and never once, in twenty years, said a true thing to a person if she could avoid it. The gift had been her shield, her livelihood, her entire self. *The one who talks to beasts.* Take it away and there was no Ines Winterbourne at all—only a woman alone in a silent world, ordinary as bread.

The stone asked again.

And Ines, who had lied badly to friends and brilliantly to magistrates, who had rerouted her life around every danger, found that for the first time in her memory she did not want another way.

"I know what it wants," she said.

Paloma's head came up. The girl's dark eyes went wide, and she shook her head hard, and the Old Speech tumbled out of her in a rush—incomprehensible to any human ear, and yet Ines understood every word, because the beasts had taught her the shape beneath all languages: *No. No, not you, it will take your voice, it will take everything, I was told to find you, I won't—*

"Hush," Ines said gently. "Listen to me, little dove. You were told to find the one who talks to beasts. You found me. Do you know why it had to be me?" She crouched so their eyes were level. "Because a covenant is a promise between equals, and equals must give freely. Your people gave their voices. My mother gave her life. Everyone in this long chain gave something they couldn't take back. If the Crown takes you—if anyone *takes* anything—the covenant stays broken, because a seized gift is no gift at all. It has to be given. Freely. By someone who could walk away and doesn't."

Voss had gone very still. "Winterbourne. Whatever you're about to do—"

"You should watch," Ines said. "Really. You especially. You've spent your whole life thinking power is something you seize or it seizes you. Watch someone set theirs down."

She turned to the stone. She laid both palms flat against its frozen surface, and the failing amber light crept up her wrists like water finding a channel, and the question came one final time—not as pressure now but almost as tenderness, the way an old wolf might nose a dying friend.

*What will you give?*

"My gift," Ines said aloud, and her voice carried strangely far in the thin air, and every bird on the mountain went silent to listen. "All of it. Every word I've ever shared with hawk and wolf and raven and bear and the small secret things under stones. Twenty years of counsel and warning and friendship. I give the silence of my mother's geese back to the covenant. I give the voice I never earned and always used." Her throat tightened. "And with it, I give what the gift made me. The invisibility. The wildness. The self that never belonged anywhere and so could belong everywhere. Let the realms knit closed. Let the beasts keep their own counsel, as is just—they are not ours, and they never were. And let this child be the bridge, whole and free and guarded, because she is the future and I am only the price."

The stone drank her words.

For a heartbeat, nothing. The wind resumed, indifferent. Voss shifted her weight, sword half-raising—

Then the veins of light ignited.

Amber flooded upward through the covenant-stone like dawn breaking inside rock, and the light leapt from the stone's crown into the sky in a column visible, Ines knew, from every valley in the range. Beneath their feet, deep in the mountain, something vast exhaled—a sound felt rather than heard, the great slow breathing of the veiled world settling back into rhythm. The air itself changed texture, thickened, became whole. All along the summit ridge, frost flowers bloomed where no moisture was. Far below, muffled by stone and distance, Ines heard—or remembered hearing—the roar of the beast court at Hollow Ford, a hundred voices rising at once, and she understood that they were singing, and that this was the last time she would ever understand them, and that they knew it too.

Because then the silence began.

It came gently, the way dusk comes. One by one, the voices left her. Not taken—*returned*. She felt the hawks fold their sharp bright speech back into themselves like wings closing. The wolves' long low counsel dimmed to mere sound, then to nothing at all. Somewhere near the shelf's edge a raven sat watching her, and she looked into its eye and saw—nothing looking back. Just a bird. Black feathers, quick clever brain, a stranger. Twenty years of friendship ended between one heartbeat and the next, and the raven did not even know they had been friends, and Ines's legs nearly folded under the weight of it.

She made a sound. She didn't mean to. It was small and human and it hurt coming out, and Paloma caught her around the waist and held on, and Ines held the girl back just as hard, and the two of them knelt together on the summit while the light poured heavenward and the world was made whole at the cost of one woman's whole self.

Grief moved through her like floodwater through a valley. She let it. She had spent twenty years damming everything—grief for her mother, grief for her solitude, grief for a childhood stolen by fire—and she was done with dams. So she wept, openly, on a mountaintop, in front of a Crown officer, and discovered that the tears did not unmake her. Something else was happening underneath the grief, quiet as snowfall: peace. Not the peace of safety. The peace of an account finally balanced. Of a debt paid in full. Of a woman who had hoarded her one treasure all her life discovering, in the moment of losing it, that she had never once been poor.

When the light faded and the column dissolved into the clouds, the mountain was simply a mountain again. Beautiful. Silent. Ordinary.

Ines got up.

Voss was staring at her. The captain's sword point had drifted to the snow sometime during the sealing, and now she looked down at it as though she'd forgotten what it was for. Her soldiers stood slack at the treeline, muskets lowered, watching the afterglow fade off the stone like watchers leaving midnight mass.

"You gave it up," Voss said. It was not a question. Her voice sounded scraped hollow. "You just—you had it in your hands, the only leverage that mattered, and you handed it to a rock."

"It wasn't leverage," Ines said. Her voice sounded strange to her own ears—thinner, plainer, a voice with nothing behind it anymore. "That's rather the point."

"They sent me to seize a weapon." Voss looked at Paloma—at the girl standing protectively at Ines's side, chin up, unafraid—and something in the captain's face broke and reassembled itself into an older expression, one worn by a daughter of burned sanctuaries before she learned better than to wear it. "My whole career, I told myself the Crown would protect the Veil better than the keepers had. That control was mercy. That taking was a kind of saving." She laughed, short and joyless, breath steaming. "And you've undone it in an afternoon by *giving*. Do you know what they'll do to me when I report this?"

"Report it honestly," Ines said. "It's the first order anyone's given you that costs nothing."

Voss studied her for a long moment. Then she sheathed her sword—a clean, deliberate sound, final as a door closing—and raised one hand, and her soldiers turned and began to descend the eastern face without a single protest, as if they too had seen something at the stone that made warrants feel very small.

"Go south, Winterbourne," Voss said, pausing at the edge of the shelf. She did not look back. "There's a magistrate in the lowlands with a blank warrant and your name half-written on it. I can't tear it for you." A pause, and her voice roughened. "But I find I've lost my appetite for filling in blanks."

She went down into the snow, and Ines watched her go, and the mountain kept its silence around them like a held note.

Paloma slipped her small hand into Ines's. The girl looked up with eyes that held the whole reforged covenant in them now—old light banked to embers—and squeezed once. She said nothing. She didn't need to; some languages needed no gift at all.

Together they started down the southern slope, toward Tam and the pass and whatever justice or judgment the lowlands meant to hand them. Behind them the covenant-stone glowed steady and warm, tended by no one, needing no one, a promise finally kept.

Ines walked into a world that had gone utterly, permanently mute to her—and found, to her astonishment, that she was not walking alone, and that for the first time in twenty years, the silence felt less like a wall and more like a door.

But three days later, at the edge of the foothills, when a magistrate's riders came down the road ahead of them with paper in hand, Ines would learn that some debts, once paid, are merely transferred—and that the trial nobody called was already gathering witnesses.
