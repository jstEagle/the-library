## 20. After the Silence

The first morning of the new world, Ines woke to nothing.

Not the absence of dreams—she had dreamed, strangely and sweetly, of her mother's kitchen, of bread and woodsmoke and a voice she could not quite hear. Not the absence of sound, either. The cabin creaked as cabins do. Wind moved through the pines above the roofline in long, slow breaths. Somewhere down the slope a stream ran loud with snowmelt, chattering over stones like a child who had just learned to talk.

It was the *other* silence that woke her. The silence where twenty years of voices used to be.

For two decades she had risen to a running commentary no other human being had ever heard: the goats complaining about their knees, the jays filing their grievances against the magpies, the old mule delivering his dawn opinion on the quality of the hay. The hawk who nested on the crag above the pass would already be reporting traffic on the ridge road, itemized and indifferent. And beneath it all, always, the low murmur of the mountain itself—not words exactly, but a pressure, a presence, the sense of being *known* by something vast and patient. Ines had hated it once, when she was small enough to still remember her mother's hands and the men who came for her. She had called it a curse in the years before she learned to call it a life.

Now it was gone, and the morning was enormous with its absence.

She lay still under the wool blanket and listened to the nothing where everything used to be, and understood, with the clarity that grief sometimes grants, that she had never once been lonely in all those years alone. Lonely was what you were when there was no one to talk to. Ines had talked constantly. She had simply done it in a language no court recognized and no warrant could name.

She got up, dressed, built the fire, put water on. Her hands knew the work. That was the mercy of mornings—they gave you tasks before they gave you thoughts.

Outside, the goats were waiting at the fence line, and this was the moment she had been dreading without admitting she dreaded it. Twenty years of greeting them by name and complaint. She opened the gate and said, "Morning," the way any woman says it to any animals, and the goats looked at her with their horizontal pupils and did not answer, because they had never answered, not really, not in words—and yet the silence between them now was a different animal entirely. Before, there had been a bridge. Now there was only the river.

One of the nannies—Bramble, the one with the crooked ear whom Ines had argued with about fencing for fifteen years—pressed her blunt warm face against Ines's palm and stood there, breathing. Goats are not sentimental creatures. But Bramble stayed longer than goats stay, and Ines scratched the coarse ruff under her jaw and felt the tears come without permission, hot and fast, dripping off her chin into the goat's fur while the goat went on chewing the air with her peculiar sideways patience.

"I know," Ines told her, uselessly, in the only language she had left. "I know."

* * *

Paloma found her there an hour later, sitting on the chopping block with an untouched axe beside her and a look on her face that Ines suspected was visible from the valley floor.

The girl was nine now—or nearly; they had stopped counting precisely after the Threshold, the way you stop counting anything that has become larger than numbers. She had grown through the winter and spring like a green shoot finding light, taller, steadier, her silence long since broken open into speech. But not the speech of the lowlands. Paloma spoke the Old Speech first and the common tongue second, the way some children grow up bilingual, and when she spoke the older language the air around her changed—a faint pressure, a smell like rain on hot stone, the sense of a door standing ajar somewhere just behind the world.

She came up the path from the waystation with an armful of kindling she didn't need, because Paloma had learned that Ines's hands needed occupation before her heart would open, and set it down by the door with the exaggerated care of a child performing helpfulness.

"You didn't come to breakfast," she said.

"I'm not hungry."

"You're sad." Paloma said it without accusation, which was somehow worse. She climbed onto the chopping block beside Ines, uninvited, entitled, taking up the space the way she took up every space now—as if the world had been arranged for her convenience, which, in fairness to the world, it somewhat had. "You're sad and you're pretending it's about the goats."

"It is about the goats."

"It is not about the goats." Paloma swung her feet. Below them, the new waystation roof gleamed with fresh pitch—Tam's crew had helped raise it in the spring, Tam himself directing operations from a chair with his leg propped up and opinions flying like sparks off a grindstone. Travelers came now, real ones, peddlers and pilgrims and the occasional lost Veilbreaker deserter looking for a meal and absolution, and Ines fed them all and asked no questions, because asking no questions was the one skill twenty years of smuggling had perfected. "The ravens say you sit out here every morning and don't chop anything."

"The ravens," Ines said carefully, "talk to you."

"Everything talks to me." Paloma said it simply, without pride, the way you'd say *the sun comes up*. It was true. Since the covenant had been reforged—since Ines had poured her gift into the stone at the First Threshold like wine into the earth—the whole veiled architecture of the world had become legible to the girl. She heard the beast court convening in its hollow places. She heard the deep doors below the mountains, quiet now but not dead, humming their long contentment. She was nine years old and she was a bridge, and the beast court had assigned her guardians the way a kingdom assigns an heir its honor guard: two white wolves who shadowed the tree line at all hours, and a raven named—Paloma insisted he had a name, though he refused to say it to anyone but her—who perched on the waystation signpost each evening like a small black magistrate.

"They asked me to tell you something," Paloma said. "The court. They made me memorize it so I wouldn't get it wrong."

Ines looked out at the mountains. "I don't want to hear it."

"You do."

"I don't."

"You do," Paloma repeated, with the terrible confidence of a child who has been right about adults before, "because you've been waiting for it since the morning you woke up quiet. They said—" and here she closed her eyes, reciting, and when she spoke the Old Speech the syllables came out round and heavy as river stones, and even untranslated Ines felt them land in her chest like footsteps on a frozen lake:

*"She who gave her voice to the beasts is not forgotten among the beasts. What is given freely is not taken away. It is planted. And what is planted waits."*

Silence. Snowmelt chattering in the stream. Bramble, down in the paddock, watching them both.

"What does that mean?" Ines said at last. "Exactly. Word for word. Don't soften it."

Paloma opened her eyes. "It means they miss you too," she said, and her voice cracked on the last word, because she was nine, whatever else she was, and grief was still new to her even if old to everyone else. "It means the wolves ask about you. Every time the court meets, someone asks if you're well. It means—" She stopped, pressed her lips together, tried again. "It means you're not punished. You're *remembered*. There's a difference. Maren explained it to me. Punished is when they take something and keep it. Remembered is when they take something and hold it for you."

Ines looked at her hands. They were ordinary hands, chapped at the knuckles, scarred across the palm from a wire fence in another life. Hands that could no longer ask a hawk what moved on the ridge road. Hands that had held a silent child through a storm and been, without knowing it, holding the last seal of the world.

"Tell them," she said finally, and her voice came out rougher than she intended, "tell them thank you. Tell them—" She stopped. Started again. This was the trouble with having only one language left; it held so little. "Tell them I'm not sorry. Tell them I'd do it again. And tell them—" the tears were back, damn them, "—tell them the door stays open on my side too. Whatever that's worth."

Paloma slid off the chopping block and wrapped both arms around Ines's middle, face pressed into her coat, and Ines held her and looked out over the bright hard beauty of the Thornfells and grieved, openly, thoroughly, the way you grieve the dead—with gratitude underneath, because the dead had lived, and the gift had been real, and twenty years of voices was not nothing. It was everything. It had been everything, and she had spent it well.

* * *

Spring turned toward summer the way it does in high country—all at once, overnight, as if the mountains had merely been waiting for permission.

The waystation filled and emptied, filled and emptied. Tam came through twice with cargo he described as "perfectly legal" in a tone that convinced no one, and sat on Ines's porch telling lies about his crew's exploits until dark. Maren sent down jars of salve and bundles of herbs with instructions written in a hand like spiderwebs. Once, far off on the southern road, Ines saw a rider in grey pause at the fork, look up at the waystation for a long moment, and ride on—and she understood that Serane Voss was alive, and watching, and had chosen, for now, to let the world stand. Some debts are paid in inaction.

And Paloma grew into her strange office the way a tree grows into its crown.

Ines watched it happen daily and marveled. The girl who had arrived mute at her door now kept appointments. Twice a month she walked up to the high hollow where the beast court convened, flanked by her white wolves, and came back in the evening with news that sounded like nonsense and wasn't: that the elk had agreed to let the lowland farmers cut winter browse on the south slopes in exchange for leaving the birch stands alone; that something old and blind in the deepest cave system had turned over in its sleep and murmured a blessing on the Threshold-keeper; that the ravens had settled a border dispute with the hawks without bloodshed, which, according to Paloma, was unprecedented in four hundred years and would be spoken of for centuries.

"And what do you say at these meetings?" Ines asked her one evening, turning spits of trout over the fire.

"I listen," Paloma said. "Mostly. Then I translate. That's the whole job, Ines. Nobody down here understands what the beasts are saying, and nobody up there understands why we do what we do. Somebody has to stand in the middle and say *both of you are saying the same thing*." She shrugged, eight years old in her bones and ancient in her office. "My village did it for a thousand years. I'm just the last one, so I do it alone. But I'm not—" she glanced sideways at Ines, quick and shy, "—I'm not actually alone."

"No," Ines said. "You're not."

* * *

On midsummer's eve, Ines walked up past the treeline alone.

She hadn't planned it. But the evening was gold and windless, and her legs carried her the old way, the smuggler's way, up the switchbacks toward the First Threshold as if the mountain itself remembered her feet. She climbed until the air thinned and the world fell away below her—the waystation a toy, the valley a green wrinkle, the smoke of hearths rising straight up in the stillness.

At the crest, the covenant-stone stood in its ring of standing rocks, catching the last light. It looked the same as it always had: grey, split, unremarkable, unless you knew. But Ines could feel it now in a new way—not hearing it, never hearing it again, but *knowing* it, the way you know a grave holds someone you loved. Something of hers was in there. Twenty years of hawk-warnings and wolf-counsel and goat-complaints, her mother's inheritance, her shield, her secret self—all of it planted in the stone, rooted, alive in whatever way such things stay alive.

She sat down beside it with her back against the warm rock, the way she had once sat beside her mother, and said aloud, to the stone, to the mountain, to whatever listened:

"I'm not sorry."

The wind moved through the standing rocks and made a low note, the way wind will, nothing more. Or perhaps something more. Ines chose, deliberately, deliberately, to believe it was nothing more—and found that choosing was its own kind of peace.

She was still sitting there when the raven arrived.

It came up over the eastern ridge with the sun behind it, wings wide, riding the updraft in slow circles, and landed on the covenant-stone not three feet from her. It was a big bird, glossy-black, with the particular insolent tilt of the head that Ines had catalogued across twenty years of raven conversation, and it regarded her with one bright eye.

Ines held her breath. Old habit. Waiting for words.

None came. Of course none came. The raven was just a raven—clever, wild, unburdened by covenant or court. It picked at the lichen on the stone, eyed her boots, and croaked once, harsh and ordinary, the plain ugly music of its kind.

But then it did something no ordinary raven did. It hopped closer, deliberate, across the warm rock, until it stood within arm's reach. And it bowed—low, formal, wings folded, head dipped—the precise bow, Ines realized with a jolt that went through her like cold water, that ravens of the court gave to a Voice. To Paloma. The bow that said *we know what you are*.

Then it straightened, shook its feathers, and flew west toward the deepening gold of the sky, leaving Ines alone on the mountaintop with her heart hammering and her eyes stinging and a question rising in her that she could not put down:

What is planted, and waits—and what, exactly, is beginning to grow?

Below her, far down the valley, a lamp flickered alight in the waystation window, and a small figure stepped out onto the porch and raised one arm—waving, or signaling, or simply letting her know she was seen.
