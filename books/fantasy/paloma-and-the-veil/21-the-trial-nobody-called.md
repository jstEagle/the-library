## 21. The Trial Nobody Called

The summons came on the first morning of thaw, when the gutters of Lowmarket ran loud with snowmelt and the whole town smelled of wet wool and woodsmoke.

Ines was mending a harness strap outside Tam's warehouse when the Crown riders came down Cooper's Row—six of them, blue-and-silver, their horses shod so loudly on the cobbles that every conversation in the street died at once. She knew before they stopped what they wanted. She had known since the pass, since Voss lowered her sword and let two small figures walk away from the First Threshold into the falling snow. Mercy at the top of a mountain is not the same as mercy in a capital. Somewhere in a marble office, a clerk had opened a file with her name half-written in it, and the ink had finally dried.

The officer in front dismounted. He was young, precise, and uncomfortable in a way that Ines recognized: a man who had been given an order he did not like and had not yet learned to refuse it gracefully.

"Ines Winterbourne." He said it as a statement, not a question. "By order of the Magistracy of the Southern Reaches, you are detained for questioning regarding violations of the Veil Statutes, trafficking in contraband livestock, obstruction of Crown officers, and—" he squinted at the paper, as if hoping it had rewritten itself "—consorting with beasts in a manner prejudicial to the security of the realm."

Behind him, one of his soldiers snorted and turned it into a cough.

Ines set down the harness strap. Her hands were steady. Twenty years of talking to animals and staying silent around people had taught her that most human authority was noise, and noise could be waited out.

"Am I to come quietly?" she asked.

"You're to come," the officer said. "Quietly would be appreciated."

* * *

They did not take her to a jail. They took her to the market square.

She understood why the moment she saw it: someone in the Magistracy had decided that a public proceeding would restore order's reputation after a winter in which order had been badly embarrassed. The Veil had thinned and knitted closed again while Crown soldiers stood watching; creatures had poured into human valleys and been sent back not by soldiers but by wolves who seemed, disturbingly, to be following instructions; and a child no taller than a fence post had walked out of the mountains flanked by elk. The Crown needed a story with itself in it, standing upright.

So they built a platform in the square, draped it in blue cloth, and set a magistrate behind a table with the royal seal stamped into the wood like a wax wound. A crowd gathered the way crowds do—not summoned, not organized, simply present, the way weather is present. Ines was seated on a plain chair below the platform, wrists bound loosely in front of her, and she spent the first ten minutes doing something no one in the square could see: listening.

There were sparrows under the eaves of the chandlery. There was a dog tied by the well, a big brindled thing with a farmer's knot and a farmer's patience. And above the square, riding the thaw wind in slow circles, there were ravens—more ravens than a market town had any business hosting, black as punctuation against the pale sky.

*Wait,* said the eldest raven, the one with the torn left wing who had guided her through the foothills during the war-torn weeks. *We are here. The court is here. Speak if you can. If you cannot, we will speak.*

*I can't understand you anymore.* It still caught in her chest, saying it even inside her own skull where no one could hear—the silence where the beasts' voices used to live was a missing tooth her tongue kept finding. *I gave it up. You know I gave it up.*

*We know,* the raven said, and its voice was already going strange and thin, becoming just a bird's caw, becoming nothing but sound without meaning. *That is why we came.*

Then it was only a raven, wheeling, crying, beautiful and unintelligible, and Ines sat alone among humans for the first time in twenty years and discovered that she was not, actually, alone.

* * *

The magistrate was a woman named Aldessa Cray, and she was not a fool, which made everything harder.

She began properly, reading the charges in a voice trained to carry across courtyards: the smuggling, twenty years of it, itemized with a thoroughness that made Tam, standing at the edge of the crowd, wince theatrically at each count. The harboring of an unregistered child. The obstruction of Captain Serane Voss. The traversal of sealed ways beneath the mountain. And last, read slowly, the charge that had clearly been added by someone higher up: *the unauthorized exercise of covenant speech, in violation of the Crown's sole claim to the power of the Veil.*

"Unauthorized," Ines repeated, when invited to respond.

"The covenant is Crown property," Magistrate Cray said. "Its reforging was conducted without license."

"Its reforging was conducted," Ines said, "because if it hadn't been, everything under the mountains would have died, and everything over them shortly after. I wasn't aware death required a permit."

A murmur went through the crowd. Cray let it run out, unhurried. "You misunderstand me, Winterbourne. I am not here to punish the outcome. I am here to establish ownership of it. The Crown claims the covenant. You performed the rite. Therefore the covenant's power passed through your hands unlawfully, and the child—who is, by every account, now the living seat of that power—is Crown ward. These are the facts I must record." She folded her hands. "Unless facts are brought forward that alter them. This is a hearing, not a trial. Witnesses may speak."

And that, Ines thought later, was the mistake. Not cruelty—Cray had little enough of that—but procedure. The belief that a square full of people was furniture.

Tam stepped forward first.

He looked terrible, in the way of a man who had been drinking less rather than more, which for Tam was far worse. He wore his good coat, the one with the brass buttons he saved for funerals and customs inspections, and he walked to the witness mark like a man walking a plank he'd built himself.

"Captain Tamris Vole, of the river boat *Kestrel's Debt*," Cray read. "You are yourself under indictment for smuggling, sedition, and the sale of information to a Crown officer without license. You understand testimony here may weigh against you?"

"I understand I'm hanged either way, ma'am," Tam said cheerfully, "so I'll spend my words where they do some good." He turned to face the square rather than the magistrate, which was technically improper and obviously deliberate. "I've known Ines Winterbourne twenty years. She moved my goats over passes that killed better men than me. She never took a coin she didn't earn and never told a secret she was keeping for somebody else—even when keeping it cost her." His voice roughened. "Last winter I sold her route to Captain Voss. Not for gold—for my crew's lives. And do you know what she did? She forgave me. Then she used my betrayal to build a false trail and save us all. I have been bribed, threatened, and arrested by the Crown, and never once has the Crown forgiven me anything. That's my testimony. Charge me however you like after."

He walked back. No soldier moved to stop him, because the crowd had shifted—had leaned, the way a hillside leans before it decides to slide—and everyone felt it.

* * *

They came one after another after that, and none of them had been called.

The widow Brannoc, whose son had been taken by Veilbreakers two winters past and returned hollow-eyed and silent, testified that Ines Winterbourne had sat with the boy three nights running until he slept without screaming. The cooper testified that during the breach, when the Veil spilled its creatures into the valley and the town militia had formed a line to shoot them, it was Ines who walked out unarmed between the muskets and the wolves and—here he faltered, because he had no words for what he'd seen—*talked*. Not with sound. With something older. And the wolves had turned and gone home, and no one in Lowmarket had died that night, though three towns downriver could not say the same.

An old drover named Hesk spoke about the goats, of all things, and made the crowd laugh and then go quiet: "Twenty years she brought stock through Thornfell and never lost one to cold or crag or cat. You want to know how? Ask yourselves why the mountain liked her. Ask what kind of person the mountain likes." He pointed a gnarled finger at her, bound in her chair. "That kind."

Magistrate Cray wrote steadily through all of it, but Ines watched her, and saw the thing she had been sent here to prevent assembling itself in front of her anyway: a story in which the Crown appeared nowhere except as the danger everyone had been protected from.

Then the animals spoke.

Not in words—never in words, not anymore, not for her—but the brindled dog by the well began to sing. It was a low, rising, deliberate sound, nothing like barking, and one by one other dogs in the square joined it, and then the horses of the Crown detachment themselves shifted and blew and added their voices, and overhead the ravens came down out of the pale sky in a spiral, dozens of them, landing on the chandlery roof, on the well, on the empty witness mark itself, and the whole square filled with a sound that no one present had ever heard and everyone present somehow understood: grief, and gratitude, and the long memory of things that do not forget a debt.

The soldiers did not know what to do. Their training contained no page for this.

"They're testifying," said a voice, high and clear.

Paloma came through the crowd the way water finds a channel—people simply moving aside before they knew they meant to. She was nine now, or nearly; she wore a grey cloak and the mended stone whistle on a cord at her throat, and she walked to the witness mark and stood beside the ravens as if standing beside ravens were the most ordinary act in the world, which, for her, it was.

Magistrate Cray looked down at her for a long moment. "Child. Do you know what this proceeding is?"

"Yes," Paloma said. Her voice was quiet and carried anyway, the way the Old Speech always carried, even now that she wore it in trade tongue. "It's a place where people decide who belongs to whom. But nobody called the ones who actually belong to her." She gestured—at the ravens, at the singing dog, at Tam, at the widow, at the whole leaning crowd. "So they called themselves."

"The child is Crown ward," Cray said, but her voice had lost its courtroom floor. "Under the statutes—"

"Read the covenant," Paloma said.

"There is no copy of the covenant in this jurisdiction—"

"I'm the copy."

Silence, total, the kind that has weight. Paloma reached up and touched the stone whistle at her throat, and when she spoke again it was not the trade tongue. It was the Old Speech, rolling out across the square like water over stones, and every soul there felt it in the sternum—a language older than kingdoms, older than the Crown whose seal sat on the magistrate's table, a language that had been spoken when the door below the mountains was first shut and would be spoken when every kingdom above it was dust. She recited, Ines realized, the first fragment of the covenant, the drowned chapel's tablet, the words that had flooded a hidden chamber with light: the terms of the old bargain, the promise of the Threshold, the sentence that named not a crown but *keepers*—voices and speakers, willing, plural, free.

When she finished, the ravens lifted as one body off the rooftops and wheeled twice over the square, and the dogs went quiet, and the snowmelt in the gutters sounded suddenly very loud.

Paloma switched back to words people owned. "The covenant doesn't belong to the Crown. It never did. It belongs to whoever keeps it. She kept it." She pointed at Ines. "With everything she had. Even the part of herself she loved best. Do you know what she gave so the world wouldn't end? She gave the voices. All of them. She can't hear the birds anymore. She can't hear the wolves. She gave up the only friends she had for twenty years, for strangers like you." Her chin trembled and she steadied it, eight years old in a burned village, nine years old in a market square, ancient in both. "So arrest her if you want. But you'll have to explain to all of us"—she spread her arms to include the crowd, the birds, the sky—"why keeping the world alive was a crime."

* * *

Magistrate Cray sat for a long time.

Later, in taverns, people would argue about what passed across her face in those minutes—the clerk's discipline fighting something older and less convenient. What was recorded, because half the square saw it plainly, was this: she looked at the warrant in front of her, the one with the blank space where a name had been filled in by hand, *Ines Winterbourne*, in the careful script of some capital functionary who had never seen the Thornfells and never heard a wolf decide not to kill.

She picked it up.

She tore it. Once, lengthwise, unhurried, the way one tears bread rather than paper. Then again, and again, until the pieces were too small to mean anything, and she let them fall from the platform like the season's last dirty snow.

"The charges are dismissed," she said, in her carrying courthouse voice, and then, quieter, almost to herself, in the voice of a tired woman at the end of a long winter: "All of them. This court finds nothing here worth punishing and much worth—" she paused, chose the word carefully, "—*keeping*."

The square did not cheer, exactly. It exhaled. Two hundred people breathed out at once, and the sound was better than cheering, and Ines sat in her chair with her bound hands and felt the ropes suddenly absurd, a joke someone had forgotten to finish. A cooper's apprentice climbed onto the platform uninvited and cut them with a skinning knife, grinning.

Tam cried openly and blamed the wind. There was no wind.

* * *

They walked her home through streets that had become, in the space of an afternoon, a gauntlet of the opposite of hostility—bread pressed into her hands, children darting out to touch her sleeve, the widow Brannoc kissing her cheek with whisky-breath ferocity. Paloma walked beside her the whole way, shoulder brushing her hip, saying nothing, which between the two of them had always been its own complete language.

At the door of the waystation, Ines stopped. Above the eaves, the ravens had settled along the ridgepole in a row, ragged and patient, like a jury adjourning forever.

"I can't hear you," she told them. Her voice cracked on it, twenty years of silence and one afternoon of justice pressing on the same bruise. "I don't know what you're saying anymore. I don't know if you know that."

The eldest raven—the torn-wing one—hopped down to the fence rail and regarded her with one dark eye. And though no voice came, though there was nothing in the caw it gave but bird and wind, Ines found she understood it anyway, the way one understands a fire is warm or a door is open: not through gift, but through having loved a thing long enough.

*We stayed,* the caw said, or seemed to say, or her heart said for it. *Some doors stay open after all.*

Inside, Paloma was already lighting the stove, and on the table, where the girl had left it that morning, lay a letter bearing the seal of the beast court of Hollow Ford—the first message the wolves had ever sent down to a human town, delivered by elk hoof to the waystation door, addressed in claw-scored bark-paper to *the Keeper and the Speaker, together*.

Paloma held it out, eyes bright with a mischief Ines had learned to fear and love in equal measure.

"You'll want to hear this one," she said. "It's about the Threshold. Someone's coming up the mountain—and they're asking for you by name."
