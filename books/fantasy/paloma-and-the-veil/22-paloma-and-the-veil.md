## 22. Paloma and the Veil

The Threshold had learned her footsteps.

That was how Paloma thought of it, anyway—though the stone beneath her palms was older than learning, older than kingdoms, older than the first word anyone had ever spoken into the dark beneath the mountains. Three autumns had passed since the day Ines Winterbourne had knelt on this same shelf of granite and given away everything she was, and in three autumns the stone had grown warm under Paloma's hands the way a sleeping animal grows warm, slowly, trustingly, as if it had decided at last that no one here meant it harm.

She came every morning before the sun cleared the eastern ridge. This was not duty, exactly. Duty was a word from the human tongue, with its flat taste of obligation, and what Paloma felt was nothing so flat. It was closer to what she imagined a daughter felt toward a mother who could no longer speak for herself—the daily tending, the checking of breath, the small ceremonies that said *I am still here, I have not forgotten you.*

She swept the moss from the covenant-stone's base with a broom of birch twigs. She poured water from the spring into the hairline crack where the old light had once flooded out, and watched it vanish downward, feeding whatever roots the Veil still put down into the deep places. And then she sat cross-legged on the warm stone and spoke to it—not the Old Speech, not usually, though she kept every word of it now, hoarded like coins against a famine that would never come again—but simply spoke, in the plain trade-tongue of the valleys, telling the Threshold about the weather, about the travelers on the road below, about Ines.

*Ines burned the porridge again,* she might say. *She blamed the stove. The stove did not answer, because stoves cannot talk, but I saw its smokehole puff in what I choose to believe was laughter.*

The stone never answered either. That was the bargain's shape, and Paloma had made her peace with its shape: the realms were knit closed, the door below sealed by a sacrifice freely given, and silence was the seal's other name. But silence, Paloma had learned, was not the same as absence. When she pressed her palm flat to the granite she felt something there—a patience, vast and slow, like the attention of a thing that breathed once a century. The world beneath the mountains was alive. It was only quiet now. Quiet, and safe, and hers to guard.

She rose, brushed off her skirt, and took the switchback path down toward the road.

* * *

The waystation stood where the smuggler road bent around the shoulder of Thornfell, a low stone house with a sod roof and a stable that smelled honestly of hay and less honestly of goats. Ines Winterbourne had built it with her own hands over two summers, hauling stones that no mule could have carried, and when travelers asked her how a woman of her size managed such a thing she only shrugged and said the mountain helped. Which was true, though not in any way they could have understood. The mountain remembered what she had given. The whole mountain remembered.

Paloma came down through the pines in the gold end of the afternoon and found the yard full of life: a tinker's cart, two pilgrims arguing cheerfully about saints, Tam's youngest crewman flirting disastrously with the tinker's daughter, and Ines herself at the fence rail, arms crossed, watching a hawk.

Paloma stopped.

It was an ordinary hawk. She knew hawks the way she knew rain—intimately, from the years when they had been her scouts and sentries and sometimes her salvation—and this one was common as bread. A red-shouldered hen, young, her feathers still carrying the brown softness of her first year. She sat on the top rail with her head cocked, regarding Ines with the flat yellow interest of any wild thing assessing a strange large animal.

But Ines was standing very still. Still in the particular way she went still, spine straight, hands loose, the posture of a woman listening to something no one else could hear. Paloma had seen her do it a hundred times in the first months after the Threshold, before the habit broke against the wall of silence—reaching, always reaching, for voices that no longer came. She hadn't done it in over a year. Paloma had thought—had hoped—that the reaching was over.

"Ines," she said softly, coming across the yard.

"I know." Ines didn't turn. Her voice was rough. "It's nothing, little bird. Just a hawk."

"It's just a hawk," Paloma agreed, and came to stand beside her at the rail.

The hawk watched them. Travelers moved around the yard below, hammering a loose wheel, trading salt for stories, and the hawk ignored all of it with the magnificent contempt of her kind. She watched only the two of them—or rather, Paloma thought, watching closely, she watched Ines, with her head tilted at an angle that was not quite the angle of hunger.

"She landed ten minutes ago," Ines said. "Right there. Out of a clear sky."

"Hawks do that."

"Not at me. Not anymore." Ines's jaw tightened. "They used to land at me everywhere. Ridge lines, fence posts, my own windowsill. Half my life, I had wings reporting to me like I was some kind of—some kind of general." She laughed, short and without much humor in it. "You'd think I'd be grateful for the quiet. Some days I am. Some days it's like being deaf in a house where music used to play."

Paloma reached over and took her hand. Ines's fingers were scarred and strong and cold from the mountain air, and they closed around Paloma's hand the way they always did—completely, without hesitation, as if twenty years of solitude had been only a long walk toward this.

"You gave it up," Paloma said. "All of it. For the door. For everyone under the mountain, and everyone above it, and for me."

"For you most of all," Ines said, "though don't let it go to your head."

"And the bargain was that the silence would be yours forever. That's what the stone asked. That's what you paid." Paloma turned to look at the hawk directly. "So if something is reaching back across that silence—if even one small wing remembers you—then maybe the door isn't locked, Ines. Maybe it's just closed. And maybe someone left it on the latch."

Ines made a sound that was half laugh and half something else entirely, something that lived deeper down. "You sound like Maren."

"Maren says I sound like your mother."

"Maren says a lot of things." But Ines's grip on her hand tightened, and she did not look away from the hawk, and Paloma saw that her eyes were bright in the way she hated to be seen crying.

For a long moment none of them moved: the woman who had given up the speech of beasts, the girl who carried what remained of the Old Speech in her mouth like a lit coal, and the hawk on the rail between them, ordinary and impossible.

Then the hawk dipped her head.

Not a preening motion. Not the sideways flick of a bird adjusting her view. She bowed—slowly, deliberately, folding her neck so that her beak pointed at the ground before Ines's boots, and held it there for the length of three heartbeats, and raised it again.

The yard noise went on. The tinker hammered. The pilgrims argued. Somewhere in the stable a goat complained about the philosophy of fences. None of them saw it. Only Paloma saw it, and Ines, who made a small broken sound and pressed her free hand over her mouth.

"There," Paloma whispered. "Doors stay open after all."

* * *

That evening the waystation filled with the smell of stew and woodsmoke, and Paloma worked the kitchen beside Ines in the easy rhythm they had built over three years—chopping, stirring, passing, their elbows speaking a language older than words. The travelers ate and told lies about the pass and paid in copper and news, and Ines collected both with the shrewd good humor that had once made her the most trusted smuggler on the mountain and now made her the most trusted innkeeper on it, which was nearly the same profession with better soup.

Tam arrived after dark, snow already dusting his shoulders though winter was weeks off, which meant he'd come over the high route, which meant he wanted something. He always wanted something. But he also brought honey wine and a carved wooden fox for Paloma's collection, and he kissed the top of her head like an uncle, because that was what he was now, whether the word had ever been spoken or not.

"Roads are quiet," he reported to Ines over the wine. "Too quiet for the season. And there's talk in the lowland markets—magistrates asking questions again. Not about you," he added quickly, seeing her face. "About the Threshold. Whether it's guarded. Whether the Crown ought to post its own... keeper."

"They tried that," Paloma said. "The magistrate tore up the warrant himself, in front of four hundred people."

"Magistrates retire. Warrants get rewritten. Crowns have long memories and longer ledgers." Tam turned his cup in his hands. "I'm not saying trouble's coming, Wren. I'm saying the mountain feels like it did before. Like the year the ravens went quiet and started counting soldiers."

Ines looked at him for a long moment. Then she looked at Paloma, and something passed between them that needed no translation at all.

"We'll keep watch," Ines said simply. "We've had practice."

After Tam slept off the wine, Paloma climbed to her room under the eaves, where the wooden foxes lined the sill and the window faced east, toward the high shoulder where the covenant-stone waited in the dark. She lit no candle. She stood at the glass and let her eyes adjust until the mountain resolved itself out of the blackness, a shape she knew better than her own hands.

Far up on the ridge, faint as breath on a mirror, a thread of light moved along the stone.

Paloma went very still.

It might have been starlight. It might have been the moon finding a seam of quartz. It might have been any ordinary thing at all. But the light pulsed—once, twice—slow and deliberate, like the attention of a thing that breathed once a century, like a heartbeat remembering its rhythm. Like a door, somewhere far beneath the mountains, being tested gently from the inside by someone who hoped it was still on the latch.

Paloma pressed her hand to the cold glass, and smiled, and began—to herself, quietly, in the language older than any kingdom—to speak.
