## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The valley forgot everything that came into it, and Marguerite had learned to be grateful for that.

Magic could not cross the ridgeline. Whatever old grief had hollowed the place out — the villagers said a god died here once, and the ground drank so much of its power that nothing else could grow in the soil but turnips, barley, and silence — it made the valley a blind spot. A hole in the world's memory. For eleven years she had lived inside it, letting it eat her name.

She was Marguerite Voss to no one now. To the village of Hollowmere she was simply *the carver*, the woman up past the mill who worked bone the way other folk worked wood. They brought her their dead — a grandfather's knucklebones, a stillborn child's tiny wrist-bone, the skull of a beloved hound — and she carved them into keepsakes: birds, moons, little running horses. And while her knife moved, she listened.

Because the bones spoke. They always had.

Not words, exactly. More like weather. Pressure behind her eyes, a taste like copper rain, and then impressions: a woman's hands kneading bread, a man's terror of deep water, a dog's whole-hearted devotion to one particular child. The dead did not speak in sentences. They spoke in *longing*. And Marguerite took that longing and folded it into the carving — a wing shaped just so, a mane flowing at an angle that would make a widow's breath catch without her knowing why — and gave the grief back to the living wearing something beautiful.

It was honest work, if you didn't look too closely. She never told them what she heard. She never told anyone anything. In the valley, the gift went quiet enough that she could almost pretend it wasn't there, the way you can almost forget a tooth that aches only when the wind blows from the north.

Almost.

* * *

On the morning the riders came, Marguerite was carving a sparrow from the femur of a man named Oskar Brandt, who had spent sixty years wanting to fly and had settled, in death, for being given wings.

The dead man's longing sat warm against her palm as the knife whispered through the bone. Outside her window, October light lay flat and gold on the stubble fields, and Tamsin was down at the well doing what Tamsin always did on market mornings: talking too loudly, laughing too easily, drawing every eye in Hollowmere the way a struck flint draws sparks.

Tamsin was seventeen, all elbows and freckles and terrible secrets kept badly. She could make flowers open out of season. Nothing grand — a crocus in the frost, a rose in November — small magics, pretty ones, the kind that got girls burned in the low country but that the valley mostly forgave, because the valley forgot. Marguerite had found her nine years ago, half-starved on the ridge road with frostbitten feet and a stolen loaf, and had taken her in the way you take in a stray: reluctantly, then completely.

"Marguerite!" Tamsin's voice came up the path ahead of her, bright and urgent. "Marguerite, there's riders — crown riders — coming up the valley road—"

Marguerite set down the knife. The sparrow's unfinished wing caught the light, half-made, half-hope.

Through the window she saw them: six riders in the king's grey-and-gold, moving at a walk, unhurried, the way men move when they own everything they survey. At their head rode a tall officer with a face like a closed door and eyes the color of river ice. He carried a scroll banded in black wax.

Her hands went cold around the bone.

*They cannot find us,* she thought, absurdly, uselessly. *The valley forgets.*

But valleys forget magic. They do not forget proclamations.

* * *

They gathered everyone in the square by the well, the whole of Hollowmere — forty-odd souls, farmers and millers and the smith, breath steaming in the thin cold. The officer unrolled his scroll and read in a voice trained for parade grounds:

"By decree of His Majesty Aldric Vane, Lord Protector of the Realm and Captain of the King's Hunt: let it be known that the age of hiding is ended. All persons gifted with unnatural arts shall present themselves for assessment within thirty days. Those who shelter the gifted share their guilt. Those who surrender them share the realm's gratitude." He rolled the scroll shut. "Assessment begins today. This valley has been remiss."

A murmur ran through the crowd like wind through barley. Marguerite stood at the edge of it, ordinary as a fencepost, her carver's smock dusted with bone meal, and thought: *be invisible, be nothing, be the woman with the knife and the dust.*

Then a sergeant seized Tamsin by the arm.

It happened fast. One of the riders had seen — gods knew how, perhaps a rose blooming out of season in someone's kitchen garden, perhaps a whisper, perhaps nothing more than a girl whose face was too alive to be trusted — and he swung down from his horse and took her wrist, and Tamsin cried out, and a crocus burst up through the frozen mud at her feet in sheer terrified reflex, purple and impossible against the grey morning.

"There," the sergeant said. "There's your witch."

"No—" Tamsin's voice cracked. "No, please, I didn't mean—"

The officer turned his ice-colored eyes on her. "Name?"

"Tamsin. Please, sir, it's nothing, it's just flowers, it doesn't hurt anyone—"

"It isn't about hurting," the officer said, almost gently, which was worse. "It's about order."

They were binding her wrists. The crowd looked at the mud, at their boots, at the sky — anywhere but at the girl. Forty people who had eaten her bread and laughed at her jokes and been given roses in November, and every single one of them suddenly fascinated by the weather.

And Marguerite felt something break in her chest — not loudly, not dramatically, but with the quiet finality of a bone snapping under a knife.

Eleven years of invisibility. Eleven years of being no one, of folding the dead's longings into sparrows and moons and saying nothing, saying *nothing*, keeping her head down and her gift buried because the valley forgot and that was supposed to be safety, that was the whole bargain—

They were putting Tamsin on a horse.

"Wait," Marguerite heard herself say.

Her voice came out small. She cleared her throat and stepped forward, out of the crowd, into the open space where everyone could see her, which was the one thing she had sworn never to do again.

"The girl is nothing," she said. "Flowers, yes — a trick, barely a trick. But if you want the real thing—" She held up her hands, bone-dust still clinging to the creases of her fingers. "I hear the dead. Every bone I carve, I hear what's in it. I've been doing it in this valley for eleven years. Take me. Leave her."

The square went very quiet. Even the wind seemed to hold its breath.

The officer studied her for a long moment, those pale eyes moving over her face like a man reading a ledger. "You confess freely?"

"I confess freely."

"And the girl?"

"Is a gardener," Marguerite said, "with a green thumb and a guilty conscience. Nothing the Hunt needs."

Something flickered across the officer's face — not kindness, exactly, but the recognition of a useful transaction. He gestured, and the sergeant released Tamsin's wrists.

Tamsin stared at her. "Marguerite, no—"

"Hush." Marguerite smiled at her, and was surprised to find the smile steady. "Water the roses."

They bound her hands this time — hers, not Tamsin's — and lifted her onto a grey horse, and the last thing she saw of Hollowmere was Tamsin standing in the trampled frost with a broken purple crocus at her feet, crying without any sound at all.

* * *

They rode east, out of the valley, up the switchback road toward the ridge.

And as they climbed, as the valley fell away below her like a cup emptying of light, Marguerite felt something she had not felt in eleven years.

The bones in her saddlebag began to whisper.

All of them at once — Oskar Brandt's unfinished sparrow, the widow's moon, the hound's skull she'd never delivered — waking as the magic of the wide world touched them again beyond the ridgeline. Not the soft weather-murmur she knew. Something sharper. Urgent. Afraid.

And beneath the whispers, rising like a stone dropped into deep water, one word repeated over and over in a voice that belonged to no one living:

*The Wolf stirs. The Wolf stirs. The Wolf stirs.*
