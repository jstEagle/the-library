## 2. What the Bones Remember

The road to the capital was paved with the dead.

Marguerite felt them through the wagon's floorboards, through the iron of her shackles — a low murmur rising from the earth itself, like water seeping into a cellar. Every mile east, the voices thickened. In the valley they had been faint things, muffled by the magic-dead soil, whispers she could almost pretend were her own thoughts. Here the land remembered everything: plagues, massacres, a battle so old its name had rotted away and only the screaming remained.

She kept her eyes on her hands. They had taken her tools but not her habits; her fingers moved against her palms, tracing the ghost of grain in bone, the patience of carving. It was how she prayed. It was how she stayed quiet.

Across the wagon bed, Tamsin wept without sound. They had let the girl go — Marguerite had made them, trading herself for her with a single raised hand and three words spoken aloud for the first time in six years: *I'm what you're looking for.* Now Tamsin was a shrinking figure on the valley road behind them, and Marguerite carried the image of her like a stone under the ribs.

*You shouldn't have,* said the skull fragment sewn into her hem. An old woman's voice, dry as seed husks. *Better you than the child, though. Better anyone.*

"Shut up," Marguerite murmured.

*Never could,* said the bone, fondly.

* * *

Aldric Vane rode at the head of the column and did not look back at the prisoner wagon, because looking would mean thinking, and thinking was a luxury the crown had priced out of his reach.

Twelve years a witch-hunter. He had burned the gifted and drowned them and hanged them, and he had learned early that the ones who frightened him were never the screamers. They were the quiet ones. The carver woman had not screamed when he took her. She had looked at him with gray eyes that saw too much, and she had said nothing at all, and that silence had followed him into camp like a dog.

His second, Bren, reined alongside. "She didn't use it," he said. "The gift. Not once on the road. You'd think she'd try something."

"She's saving it." Aldric watched the tree line where the wild country began, black pines marching down from the hills like an army waiting for orders. "They always save it."

"For what?"

Aldric didn't answer. In his saddlebag, wrapped in oilcloth, lay the proclamation scroll and beneath it a private letter bearing the chancellor's seal: *Deliver the bone-carver alive. The vault-masters require one who hears.* He had read it eleven times. He had told no one. Orders that only make sense to the man who wrote them are orders that get soldiers killed.

Ahead, the road bent toward the river ford, and the pines leaned closer, and Aldric felt — absurdly, superstitiously, the way he had not felt since boyhood — that the forest was holding its breath.

* * *

They came for her on the third night, and the dead told her first.

Marguerite woke in the wagon with every bone in her sewing humming at once, a chord of warning, and thirty heartbeats later the sentry's throat opened in the dark. She did not hesitate. Six years of hiding had taught her exactly one lesson worth keeping: when the moment comes, move before your courage can argue.

She rolled off the tailboard, hit the ground, and ran for the trees with shackles still on her wrists, chains singing at every stride. Behind her the camp erupted — horns, torchlight, Bren's voice cracking with alarm. Arrows whispered past. The pines swallowed her.

She ran until her lungs tore, guided by the dead, who knew these woods better than any living tracker. *Left,* said a soldier dead two hundred years. *Down the ravine,* said a shepherd. *Under the fallen oak,* said a child, and Marguerite crawled under the root-cellar dark of it and lay in the cold loam while boots crashed past above her, and waited.

Near dawn, a hand closed over her ankle.

She bit through the man's glove before his other hand clamped her mouth. "Quiet," he breathed. "I'm pulling you *out*, you feral little — there. Good." He was gaunt, gray-stubbled, with a burn scar crawling up his neck like ivy. He cut her shackles with a knife that shimmered wrong in the dimness. "Name's Osk. Can you walk?"

"I've been walking since they took me."

"Then walk faster. They brought dogs."

* * *

Osk led her through the hills for two days, and on the third evening the trees opened onto a hollow where firelight glowed through a screen of woven branches — a camp built to be invisible, smoke channeled underground, tents the color of bark.

There were nine of them. A woman with river-gray eyes who introduced herself as Sefa and never blinked quite enough. Twin boys who finished each other's sentences and each other's dreams. A huge, gentle man called Brannoc whose shadow moved a half-second after he did. And apart from all of them, at the edge of the fire, a man who did not introduce himself at all.

He sat like a held breath. Big through the shoulders, dark-haired, with a face that might have been handsome if it ever committed to an expression. When Marguerite entered the circle, he looked up, and something passed across his features — recognition, or its echo — and was gone.

"You're the bone-carver," Sefa said. "From the valley."

"The valley doesn't exist," Marguerite said. "That's rather the point of it."

Someone laughed. The tension cracked, just slightly, and Osk pushed her down onto a log and pressed a bowl of barley stew into her hands as if feeding strays were his calling in life. She ate. It was the first hot food in four days, and she had to concentrate very hard on the taste to keep from crying.

The silent man spoke once, near midnight, when the others slept. He crouched by her bedroll, and his voice was low and roughened, like a door unused for years.

"The bones you carry. What do they say tonight?"

Marguerite studied him. "Why do you want to know?"

"Because they've been saying my name for three days," he said, "and I'd like to know why."

She should have lied. She was good at lying; she'd had practice. But exhaustion stripped her, and the bones in her hem were already murmuring, and she heard their words clearly for the first time since the valley:

*The Wolf stirs. The Wolf stirs. Wake not, wake not, wake—*

She looked at the man by the fire, at the firelight that seemed to bend around him rather than touch him, and understood nothing except that she was afraid.

"They say a wolf is stirring," she said carefully.

Something in his face closed like a shutter. "Sleep, carver," he said, and walked into the dark, and did not come back until dawn.

* * *

In the captain's tent, Aldric laid the map flat and weighted its corners with stones.

Bren stood over it, jaw tight. "Dogs lost her in the ravine. But we found tracks joining hers — at least two, maybe more. There's a whole nest of them out there."

"There is." Aldric traced the wild country with one finger. "And I know where they'll run."

"How?"

"Because I'd run there myself." He tapped the map where the mountains folded into a hidden green fold of land, marked on no crown survey: the ruins of the old shrine at Vaelmoor. "Prey goes to ground where the hunting was worst. Nobody searches a graveyard."

Bren hesitated. "Sir — the letter. The chancellor's. Are we delivering her to the capital, or—"

"We're delivering her alive," Aldric said, "because dead women don't hear anything." He rolled up the map, and his hand was steady, and he hated it. "Break camp. We ride for Vaelmoor."

* * *

The fugitives moved by night and hid by day, and slowly, without her permission, the hollow became a home.

Sefa taught her which mushrooms wouldn't lie to you. The twins adopted her with the ruthless efficiency of children, demanding stories and offering gossip about everyone in camp — *Rurik sleeps sitting up, Rurik doesn't eat fish, Rurik hasn't bled since Osk saw him take a crossbow bolt at Marrow Ford, right through the arm, and there wasn't even a scar—*

"He doesn't dream either," Sefa said one evening, quietly, watching the man in question stand watch on the ridge. "Brannoc shares dreams with half the camp, the boys dream together, even I dream — bad ones, but mine. Rurik sleeps like a stone in a wall. Nothing gets in. Nothing comes out." She glanced at Marguerite. "You hear the dead. Does he feel like dead to you?"

Marguerite considered the question honestly. "No," she said. "He feels like something older than dying."

It became habit, almost without deciding: she carved again. Osk stole her a knife and a knucklebone of elk, and she worked by firelight while the camp drowsed around her, shaping the bone into a small running figure — a wolf, though she hadn't meant it to be. Her hands decided. Her hands often knew first.

And every night, the old bones in her sewing said the same thing, louder, more urgent, a litany worn smooth by centuries of repetition:

*The Wolf stirs.*

*Wake not. Wake not. Do not let it wake.*

On the ninth night, Rurik sat down across the fire from her, uninvited, and nodded at the little carved wolf in her palm.

"That's the third one you've made," he said.

"It's not for you."

"No," he agreed. "I don't think it is." His eyes caught the firelight and held it too long, the way a predator's eyes do — not reflecting, considering. "Carver. When the hunters come for us — and they will come — promise me something."

"I don't make promises to men who won't tell me their names."

"Rurik," he said. "My name is Rurik." And then, softer, almost to himself: "It used to be something else."

Before she could ask, the night split open.

A horn sounded from the southern ridge — long, brazen, horribly familiar. Torches bloomed among the trees like a rash of fever. The camp erupted around them, Osk shouting, Sefa dragging the twins toward the ravine, Brannoc's lagging shadow stretching enormous against the flames.

And down through the burning treeline came the hunters of the crown, and at their head, on a black horse, Captain Aldric Vane, his sword already drawn, his eyes finding Marguerite across the fire with terrible certainty.

"Vaelmoor," he said, to no one, to everyone. "Just like the map said."

The bones in Marguerite's hem went silent — utterly, dreadfully silent — and then, in a voice that was not one voice but thousands, they screamed:

*THE WOLF STIRS.*
