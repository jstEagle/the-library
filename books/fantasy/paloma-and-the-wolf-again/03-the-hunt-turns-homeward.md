## 3. The Hunt Turns Homeward

Rurik had not dreamed in three hundred years, and so he did not know the shrine was coming until he smelled it — old stone and older blood, soaked so deep into the earth that even rain could not wash it clean.

He stopped at the tree line with the others strung out behind him: Sefa with her fire cupped in both palms like a bird she was afraid to let fly, old Bram limping on a staff of rowan wood, the twins pressed shoulder to shoulder, and Marguerite last of all, her carved bones clicking softly in their pouch at her hip. She had stopped sleeping under blankets. She said the dead were louder now, and they kept her warm enough.

"The shrine of Vael," Rurik said.

"You know it?" Sefa asked.

"I knew it when it wasn't ruined."

He did not tell them more. He never told them more. That was the shape of his life — a man built around an absence, a hollow where his history should have been, filled instead with things he could not say. He knew the shrine because he had watched it raised stone by stone by people who prayed to something older than their gods. He knew the blood because some of it was his.

Marguerite came to stand beside him. Up close, her eyes were the grey of river ice, and they saw too much.

"They're in there," she said quietly. "The hunters. I've been hearing them since dawn — Aldric's dead soldiers whispering to my bones about where he means to stand."

"How many?"

"Twelve. Maybe fourteen." Her jaw tightened. "And one more voice. A living one, talking to Aldric. Familiar."

Rurik felt it before she said the name — a cold sliding down his spine like water finding a crack in a wall.

"Bram," he said.

She nodded once.

* * *

They confronted him at the edge of the camp, while the twins gathered wood and Sefa pretended not to listen. Bram sat on a fallen log with his hands folded on his rowan staff, and he did not deny it. That was the worst part. He looked up at Rurik with eyes gone milky and tired, and he said, "You were going to get us all killed anyway."

"What did you tell them?"

"Where we'd cross. Where we'd sleep." The old man's voice was flat as slate. "Aldric promised pardons. Real ones, sealed with the crown's wax. My wife's name cleared. My daughter's." He laughed, a dry terrible sound. "I'm eighty years old, boy. I have spent forty of them running. I want to die in a bed."

"There are no beds for people like us," Rurik said. "Only graves the crown digs and fills and calls mercy."

Bram's gaze slid past him, to Marguerite, and something flickered there — shame, maybe, or its corpse. "And what does she hear in your bones, Wolf? Has she told you yet? Has she told you what you are?"

The word landed like a dropped stone. *Wolf.* Not an insult. A name.

Rurik turned slowly. Marguerite stood very still, her hand resting on her bone-pouch, and her face was the face of someone who has been carrying a weight too long and can no longer remember when she picked it up.

"I was going to tell you," she whispered.

"When?"

"Soon."

"When, Marguerite?"

"After we were safe. After—" She stopped. Breathed. "The dead don't say *Rurik*. They've never once said your name. They say *the Wolf stirs*. They say *the Wolf wakes*. They say it over and over, in voices from places that aren't on any map I know. And yesterday, one of them finally said more." Her voice dropped. "It said: *the crown knows what sleeps beneath the shrine. The crown means to kill the guardian and drink the war.*"

The forest went quiet around them. Even the wind seemed to hold its breath.

"Drink the war," Rurik repeated.

"That's what it said. That's all it said." Marguerite stepped closer, close enough that he could see her hands trembling. "Rurik. What sleeps beneath the shrine?"

And there it was — the moment he had been running from for three hundred years, arriving at last with the inevitability of weather. He looked at her, this woman who heard the dead and loved them anyway, who had given up everything to save a girl who wasn't hers, and he understood that he could lie to her. He could lie and run again and keep running until the crown's knives found him or the world ended, whichever came first.

Or he could tell the truth, here, in the smell of pine and coming rain, and let it cost what it cost.

"Me," he said. "What sleeps beneath the shrine is me. Or the part of me I buried there, a long time ago, when burying it was the only way to keep a war from eating the world." He heard his own voice go strange and low, layered, like two speakers sharing one throat. "There was a war, Marguerite. Before your kings. Before your gods had names. It was fought with magic so vast it cracked the sky, and it would have unmade everything, and the only way to stop it was to seal it — to bind the whole burning thing beneath the earth and set a guardian over the door." His hands opened. They were shaking too. "I am the door. I am the lock. And I have been walking away from myself for three centuries, pretending to be a man."

Nobody spoke. Somewhere behind them, the twins had stopped gathering wood. Sefa's little flame had gone out.

Then Bram's staff cracked against the ground as he rose. "You see?" the old man rasped. "You see what walks among us? We're not fugitives anymore. We're kindling. Every hunter, every witch-finder, every ambitious lord — they'll come for us because of *him*, and—"

The horn sounded from the direction of the shrine.

* * *

It was a hunting horn, deep and brazen, and it was answered by another from the east, and another from the south. Rurik counted them with a sick, cold clarity: they were not being chased into the shrine. They were being driven into it. Bram had sold them the crossing, and Aldric had built a yard around it.

"To me!" Rurik roared, and the word came out doubled, tripled, a wolf-voice wearing a man's throat, and the fugitives broke toward him through the trees as the first arrows came whistling out of the green dark.

Sefa's fire went up in a wall between them and the eastern archers. The twins ran with their hands linked, weaving a fog so thick the hunters' shouts turned to confusion inside it. Rurik put himself at the front, because he was the front, because he had been a wall against worse things than arrows, and he felt the old shape rising in him like floodwater — fur and fang and the vast slow heartbeat of something that remembered when mountains were young.

"No," Marguerite gasped beside him, grabbing his arm. "Not here. If you change here, they'll see. They'll *know*. The whole plan depends on knowing—"

"It doesn't matter anymore."

"It matters! Rurik, if they take you—"

"If they take me, they'll try to kill me at the shrine and fail, because I cannot die while the seal holds, and then they'll learn what I am and spend a hundred years trying to open me like a letter." He caught her face in his hands. Her skin was cold. Her eyes were terrified and furious and brave, all three at once, and he thought: *this is what I fought the war for. This. People willing to be afraid for each other.* "Marguerite. Listen to me. Whatever happens — whatever you hear the dead saying — do not wake me. Do you understand? Promise me. Let me die before you let them use me."

"You just said you can't die."

"*Promise me.*"

The fog tore open. Hunters poured through, silver-badged, crossbows leveled, and at their center rode Captain Aldric on a grey mare, his face grim and unreadable, his sword already drawn.

"Rurik of nowhere," Aldric called, "in the name of the crown, surrender the guardian."

So they knew. All of them, they already knew.

Rurik stepped in front of Marguerite and let the change take him.

It was not gentle. It was never gentle. His bones lengthened and broke and knitted longer; his cloak split along the spine as the great grey mass of him shouldered up out of the man-shape like a mountain rising from a valley floor. The hunters screamed. Horses screamed. And the Wolf of the old war stood in the ruined shrine's shadow with the light of a dying age in his eyes, and he roared, and the roar shook acorns down from trees a mile distant.

The battle was short and brutal and everywhere. Fire and fog, arrows and teeth. Rurik fought to drive them back, not to kill — every death fed the thing beneath the earth, and he could feel it stirring against its chains, drunk on blood, whispering in a language that predated speech. *Open, open, open.*

He did not see Bram fall. He heard it — the old man's cry, cut short — and some traitorous part of him noted that Bram had gotten his wish after all: he would not die in a bed, but he would not die running either.

Then a net of blessed iron came out of the smoke, weighted with prayer-knots, thrown by four men at once, and it took the Wolf across the shoulders and dragged him down into the mud of the shrine yard, and the world went white and screaming.

Through it, dimly, he heard Marguerite.

She was fighting — small, fierce, hopeless, swinging her bone-pouch like a flail, the dead howling out of it in a chorus that made hardened soldiers drop their weapons and cover their ears. She reached him. She fell to her knees in the mud beside his great shuddering head, and her hands found his fur, and her tears hit his nose like rain.

"Don't," he managed, in the wrecked half-voice left to him. "Don't wake me. Let them—"

"I promised you nothing," she said.

"You promised—"

"I promised I'd tell you the truth. Here it is." She leaned close, her forehead against his, and her voice broke and held. "I love you. And I will not watch them kill you. Whatever it costs — whatever *I* have to give — I will not watch them kill you."

Boots thundered. Hands pulled her up and back, her nails raking furrows in the mud, reaching for him. Aldric himself took her arm, and his face, above her, was not cruel — that was somehow worse. It was the face of a man doing arithmetic.

"Take the bone-witch to the capital," Aldric ordered. "The vault-masters will want her gift intact." He looked down at the pinned Wolf, at the ancient eyes blazing in the mud, and added, almost gently: "Kill the beast at the shrine. Then dig."

The net cinched. The iron burned. And the last thing Rurik saw before the white pain took him was Marguerite's face over the hunters' shoulders — grey-eyed, tear-streaked, and absolutely resolved — growing smaller as they carried her away toward the city where the dead waited in their thousands, whispering her name, whispering *the Wolf*, whispering:

*wake, wake, wake.*
