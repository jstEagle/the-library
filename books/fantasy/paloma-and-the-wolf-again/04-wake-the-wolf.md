## 4. Wake the Wolf

The bone-vault lay beneath the capital like a second heart, and it beat wrong.

Marguerite felt it through the soles of her boots as they led her down — a slow, arrhythmic pulse in the stone, as though the mountain itself were dreaming badly. The walls around her were lined with shelves, and the shelves with bones. Thousands of them. Tens of thousands. Femurs stacked like cordwood, skulls arranged by dynasty, ribs fanned out in patterns that might have been devotion or might have been arithmetic. Every one of them was singing.

She had heard bones sing her whole life, but never like this. Never all at once. It was not voices here; it was a choir, and the choir was screaming.

"Stop walking," she said.

The hunter behind her did not stop. His gauntlet closed on her shoulder, iron biting through wool, and he steered her onward past a shelf of finger-bones that whispered *she can hear us, she can hear us* in a hundred dead tongues.

Aldric waited at the vault's center, where the ceiling opened into a shaft of grey daylight and a dais of black stone rose from the floor. On the dais, chained at wrist and throat and ankle with bands of pale metal that drank the light, knelt Rurik.

He looked smaller than she had ever seen him. Not diminished — *contained*, the way a river is contained by a dam, the way a forest fire is contained by a trench dug in desperate haste. His head hung forward. When he lifted it and found her eyes, there was no surprise in his face, only grief.

"I told you to run," he said.

"You tell me a great many things." Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "I've stopped listening."

Aldric stood beside the dais with a ledger open under his arm, and he wore the expression of a man about to conduct an experiment he has already decided will succeed. "Marguerite Voss," he said. "Bone-speaker. Do you know what this is?" He gestured at the vault, the shelves, the singing dark. "Every gifted bone the crown has taken in three hundred years. We have been building toward this longer than you've been alive. The thing beneath this city — the war your friend's kind buried — it is power beyond anything the world remembers. And he—" a nod at Rurik, almost tender, "—is the lock."

"He's the guardian," Marguerite said.

"He's a key that thinks it's a wall." Aldric turned a page. "We don't need him willing. We need him open. And for that we need someone who speaks to what's inside him." He smiled, and it was the smile of a man who believed himself reasonable. "You're going to wake him, Mistress Voss. Gently, if you prefer. Either way."

* * *

They left her alone with him. That was their mistake, or perhaps their arrogance — Aldric genuinely believed she would comply, because Aldric genuinely believed everyone, given sufficient pressure, chose survival. She had watched him work on the march south. He was not cruel for pleasure. He was cruel the way a carpenter is precise: because the shape required it.

Rurik watched her cross the floor between the shelves, chains dragging as he shifted against them.

"There's a way," he said quietly. "You know there's a way. You could refuse. They'd kill you, and the dead would go quiet for you forever, and I would stay asleep enough to hold the seal until they find another speaker. There are always other speakers."

"Not like me." She knelt in front of him, close enough to see the old scar at his jaw, the one he'd claimed came from a plough. A lie. Everything about him was a beautiful lie wearing a man's face. "And not like this. Rurik — listen to them. Really listen."

He frowned. "I don't hear—"

"The bones do. All of them. They're not warning anymore." She took his chained hand in both of hers. His skin was cold as river-stone. "They're grieving. Do you understand? Three hundred years of gifted dead, harvested and shelved, and every single one of them knows what's coming. They've been trying to reach me since the valley. Since before the valley. *Wake him,* they say. *Wake him before they cut him open.*"

"If I wake fully," he said, and his voice cracked along its length like ice on a pond, "the war wakes with me. You've heard it. You know what's down there."

"I know." Her thumb moved over his knuckles. Beneath the cold she could feel it now — the pulse from the deep stone, matching itself to his heartbeat, two rhythms arguing. "And I know what I am, Rurik. I'm the woman who spent thirty years pretending she couldn't hear the dead because hearing them made her strange. I hid in a hole in the world and carved pretty trinkets and called it a life." She looked up at him. "I'm done calling it a life."

"Marguerite—"

"They'll take Tamsin next. You know they will. And after her, every gifted child in every valley they haven't found yet. There is no version of this where staying quiet saves anyone." She rose. "So I'm going to choose truth over safety. One last time. And then I'm going to spend everything I have making sure the truth doesn't kill us all."

For a long moment he only looked at her — the ancient thing behind the tired eyes measuring her, weighing her, the way he must once have weighed kings and monsters and the slow turning of centuries.

"All right," he said at last. "But when it starts, whatever you hear — however loud it gets — remember my voice. Hold onto my voice. Promise me."

"I promise," said Marguerite Voss, who had broken every promise she'd ever made to herself and kept every one she'd made to someone else.

Then she put her palms flat against the black stone of the dais, closed her eyes, and opened every door inside her at once.

* * *

The dead came like a flood.

Not the polite whispers of her carving-table, not the murmurs she'd spent a lifetime damming back — this was the whole ocean of them, every voice in every shelf, pouring through her hands into the stone, into the chains, into the sleeping shape beneath the city. She gave them passage and they took it, roaring, and somewhere above her Aldric was shouting orders she couldn't parse, and somewhere below her something vast turned over in its sleep and began, slowly, to open its eyes.

*Rise,* sang the dead. *Rise, Wolf. Your watch is ended.*

The seal broke like a held breath released.

Light came up through the floor in veins. The chains on Rurik's wrists glowed white and sloughed away like frost in sunlight, and he stood, and he was not standing — he was *unfolding*, taller than the vault, taller than memory, fur like storm-cloud and eyes like the last coals of a fire that had burned since before the first king drew breath. The Wolf filled the shaft of daylight and howled, and the sound was not sound but the end of an age, and far beneath them Marguerite felt the sealed war strain against its grave — feel the old hatreds stir, the buried armies, the hunger that had slept three hundred years and woken ravenous.

It reached for the surface. She felt it climb.

And Marguerite, kneeling in the ruin of the dais with blood running freely from her nose and ears, did the only thing left to do.

She poured herself into the seal.

Not her gift as a tool — her gift as substance, as flesh, as the whole thirty years of listening she had hoarded and hidden and resented. She gave it the whispers and the warnings, the lullabies the dead mothers sang, the last words of ten thousand soldiers, every voice she had ever carried. She packed the wound in the world with everything she was and pressed down, and the war screamed against her, and she screamed back, and the dead — her dead, her endless chorus — screamed *with* her, lending her their weight, until the grave closed over the war like water closing over a stone.

The silence, when it came, was total.

Marguerite knelt on cold stone in a vault full of bones, and for the first time in her life, she heard nothing at all.

* * *

They walked out of the capital at dawn, two days later, while the city still argued about what had happened beneath it. Aldric was alive — she'd insisted on that, over Rurik's low growl — stripped of rank and left to explain to his crown why the vault stood empty and the Wolf unchained. The shelves of bones they had carried out themselves, all of them, and given to the earth outside the walls, each one spoken over with a name where a name could be found. Marguerite had done the speaking. Her lips had moved. No voice had answered, and she had wept anyway, and found even the weeping clean.

Rurik walked beside her as a man. Mortal now, the guardianship spent, the centuries burned off him like fog in sun. He bled when he stumbled on the road and swore with genuine astonishment, and laughed at his own swearing, and Marguerite thought she had never seen anything so young as an ancient creature discovering scraped knees.

At the crossroads where the north road split toward the coast, he stopped.

"I keep thinking I should go somewhere far," he said. "Somewhere with no history. Start being a person from scratch." He glanced at her sideways. "But I'm told I'm terrible at starting things alone."

"The valley's gone," Marguerite said. "The magic's awake everywhere now. Nowhere hides anyone." She shouldered her pack — lighter than it had ever been, empty of carvings, empty of voices. "Which means we get to build instead of hide. Tamsin's waiting at the coast with the others. Sera says there's an abandoned lighthouse. Says it needs people."

"A lighthouse." He tested the word like a new tooth. "Guarding ships instead of kingdoms."

"Someone has to."

He smiled — the real one, the one she'd caught only glimpses of across campfires — and turned north with her, and they walked together into a country waking from its long quiet, toward salt wind and found family and a life with nothing whispering underneath it.

Behind them, far to the south, the bells of the capital began to ring.

Neither of them looked back.
