## 1. The Road That Remembers

The mountain had been trying to tell Astrid something for three days, and they were too tired to listen.

It started small, the way most dangerous things do. A switchback that should have cost them an hour of climbing instead bent gently around a shoulder of granite, as if the rock itself had decided to be reasonable. A scree slope that had killed two mules in Astrid's twenty years of running this route now held its stones like cupped hands, each footing solid, each step sure. And yesterday, at the fork above the Greywater falls—where the trail split toward the salt road and the long drop into the ravine—the way had simply *opened*, a gap in the thornbrush wide enough for a loaded pack-mule, exactly where Astrid needed one.

They'd stopped then, hand on the mule's bridle, and looked back the way they'd come. The path behind them was already closing, thorns knitting together with a soft creak of living wood, erasing their passage like water smoothing over a thrown stone.

"Superstition," Astrid said aloud, because saying it aloud made it true. "Weather. Thaw-rot. The mountain shifting."

The mountain did not answer. Mountains rarely did. But the wind came down off the high snows and moved through the pass with a sound almost like breathing, and Astrid stood there a moment too long before shouldering the lead rope and walking on.

Thirty years. Thirty years of carrying other people's cargo through the Thornfell spine—salt out of the eastern pans, iron ingots, letters sealed with wax and lies, once a whole choir of fugitive nuns packed into a smuggler's wagon like herring in a barrel. Thirty years of knowing every goat-track and deadfall, every place the ice bridged the river in winter and every place it didn't. They had built a life on being unremarkable: a weathered face, a neutral accent sanded down from the northern vowels of their childhood, a name that wasn't quite theirs anymore. Hartwell was a common enough surname in the borderlands if you didn't look too closely at it. Most people didn't. Looking closely was how you got dead in Veyric's kingdom, and Astrid had gotten very good at not being looked at.

The mule—Bramble, ill-tempered, gray, and smarter than half the people Astrid had ever worked for—plodded along behind them with the resigned air of a creature who had heard all of her owner's conversations with herself and found them wanting.

"Don't give me that look," Astrid told her. "You ate my spare boot in March. You don't get opinions."

Bramble flicked one ear, which from her was practically a sermon.

The pass narrowed ahead, walls of black stone rising on either side, and here Astrid's mood darkened in the old familiar way, because this stretch was called the Widow's Teeth and it deserved the name. Rockfall country. Twice in thirty years Astrid had crossed it with their heart in their throat, listening to the mountain groan overhead. They knew the safe window after a freeze-thaw cycle, knew to cross at dawn when the ice held the loose stone together, knew the sound a slope makes just before it lets go—a deep, grinding sigh, like something enormous turning over in its sleep.

They crossed at midday, because the schedule demanded it, and nothing fell.

Astrid noticed. Of course they noticed. They stood in the middle of the Teeth with sunlight pouring down and the silence pressing close, and they counted the seconds the way you count after lightning, waiting for thunder. Nothing. The scree fields clung to their slopes. The talus cones sat where they had sat for a century. It was as if the mountain had drawn a breath and decided, deliberately, to hold it until they passed.

"You're getting soft," Astrid muttered to the stone. "Or I am."

Neither possibility sat well. Softness got smugglers killed. So did noticing things you couldn't explain, because explanations led to questions, and questions in the Thornfell borderlands these days led to witch-finders, and witch-finders led to fires. King Veyric's men had been burning things further south all spring—not just shrines, but *stories*. Songs about the old gods, pulled out of tavern repertoires. Grandmothers' charms against fever, confiscated and catalogued. A woodcutter near Lowmark, so the rumor went, hanged for carving a spiral into his doorpost, the kind of spiral farmers had carved for four hundred years without anyone calling it treason.

Astrid kept their head down and their cargo moving and told themselves the world's madness would stay south of the mountains, where it belonged.

* * *

They made camp that night in a hollow below the tree line, a spot Astrid had used perhaps forty times over the decades—a wind scoop behind a fallen fir, dry ground, a stream fifty paces off. It was a good camp. It had always been a good camp.

Tonight it was perfect.

That was what unsettled them. The firewood lay stacked nearby in driftwood-dry lengths, though Astrid hadn't gathered any yet. The stream ran clear where it usually ran brown with spring silt. Even Bramble seemed calmer, grazing the hollow's grass with the contentment of a beast in a stable rather than a beast on a mountainside full of wolves.

Astrid sat by the fire they'd built and turned their knife over in their hands and let themselves think the thought they'd been refusing for three days.

*The paths are changing.*

Not degrading—that would have been normal, that would have been weather. Changing. *Favoring.* Every obstacle between them and Lowmark had softened or shifted or stepped aside, and every shortcut they'd half-remembered from their youth had proven real, as though the mountain were consulting some old map of itself and finding routes it had forgotten it owned.

There was a word for that. There was an old word, taught to children in the years before the purges, whispered by grandmothers who remembered their own grandmothers: *the land remembers its friends.*

Astrid put the thought down the way you'd put down a hot coal.

Because there was another thing grandmothers used to say, and it was this: the old gods weren't gone. They were *sleeping*. And the oaths sworn to them—the swordspeaker oaths, the blood-oaths, all of it—hadn't been broken when the temples fell. They'd only gone quiet, like banked coals, waiting.

Astrid's grandmother had said that once, holding their small hands in hers, tracing the lines of their palms. *The oath sleeps in the blood, little wolf. Sleep is not death.* Then she had died, and the temple soldiers had come, and Astrid had learned the wiser lesson: sleep is not death, but pretending is survival, and survival was the only prayer worth keeping.

They fed the fire another branch and watched the sparks climb. Somewhere far off, a night bird called, and the sound carried strangely far, as if the air itself had grown more attentive.

"Nothing's waking up," Astrid told the flames. "Nothing remembers anything. I'm tired, the thaw's playing tricks, and tomorrow I sell salt to a man who cheats me and buy news from a woman who lies to me, and that's the whole shape of the world."

The fire crackled. Bramble sighed. The mountain breathed around them, patient as stone, and if it was listening, it gave no sign.

But Astrid slept badly anyway, and dreamed—not of anything they could hold onto by morning, only of a voice, low and vast and very old, saying a word they couldn't remember but somehow knew was their name.

* * *

Lowmark announced itself by smell before sight: woodsmoke, tanneries, the sour reek of the stock pens, and under it all the clean mineral bite of the salt flats to the east. The town sat where the Thornfell foothills gave up and became plain, a sprawl of slate roofs and muddy streets strung along the old trade road like beads on a dirty string. It was not beautiful. It was useful, which in Astrid's experience was rarer and more durable.

They came down the last mile of trail in the grey of early morning, leading Bramble through the birch scrub, and passed the roadside shrine.

Everyone passing into Lowmark passed the roadside shrine. It stood where the trail met the cart road, a knee-high cairn of stacked stones crowned with a flat slab, cracked clean through by some winter frost years ago. Nobody knew which god it had been built for—Ondrys, maybe, the storm-father, or Vessa of the crossings, or none of them, just *the old ones, whoever's listening*. Farmers still left offerings sometimes, in the careless way of people who aren't sure they believe but can't afford to be wrong: a twist of bread, a copper bit, a ribbon faded to grey. Since Veyric's edicts had begun creeping north, even those offerings had grown furtive. You left your bread and walked on quickly, eyes forward, the way you'd slip a coin to a beggar the constable was watching.

Astrid had walked past it ten thousand times. This morning they almost didn't notice it at all—which was why they noticed.

The crack in the slab was full of flowers.

Small white ones, star-shaped, the kind that bloomed in the high meadows in June and were weeks away from blooming anywhere near Lowmark. Dozens of them, packed into the fissure, fresh, dew still on the petals, though the morning was dry and the nearest meadow of that flower was two days' hard climb behind them.

Astrid stopped. Bramble stopped. For a long moment the only sound was wind in the birches and the distant clang of Lowmark waking up.

Someone had put them there. Someone always put flowers there; that was the mundane explanation, and Astrid reached for it gratefully. Pilgrims. Children. Some old woman clinging to the old ways, climbing into the hills for blossoms out of season because faith doesn't check the calendar.

Except the dew. Except the season. Except the fact that Astrid had come down this trail in the dark and seen no one.

"Superstition," they said again, quieter this time, and it sounded less like a fact and more like a wish.

They led Bramble past the shrine without touching it. But their eyes caught on the crack as they went by, and the crack seemed—this was absurd, they told themselves later, this was thaw-rot and bad sleep—to catch on them in return. Just for a heartbeat. Just the way a sleeping man's breathing changes when someone enters the room.

Then the road bent toward town, and the ordinary noise of Lowmark swallowed everything, and Astrid let it.

* * *

The salt sold quickly, because Astrid's salt was always good and always on time, and in the borderlands reliability was worth more than charm. The buyer, a fat and cheerful cheat named Odo Pell, haggled out of habit rather than hope, paid within a copper of fair price, and pressed a folded paper across the table with the coins.

"News," he said, lowering his voice. "Worth more than the salt, this time."

Astrid read it standing right there, because Odo didn't sell secrets twice. Witch-finders in Marrowdale, a week south. Three shrines pulled down in the river towns. And at the bottom, in Odo's careful merchant's hand: *They're asking after names. Old names. Someone in the capital dug up a list.*

"What list?" Astrid asked, keeping their voice bored.

"How would I know? But a factor of mine swears the finders are paying silver for family records. Baptismal rolls. Anything with bloodlines in it." Odo shrugged, vast shoulders rolling. "Bad season for people with long memories."

Astrid finished their business, bought grain and lamp oil, and went looking for the other kind of purchase. Maren the ferry-woman kept a lean-to by the docks where information changed hands along with rope and tar, and Maren owed Astrid twenty years of mutual non-betrayal, which in the trade was nearly love.

She confirmed what Odo had sold. She added one detail, free, and the detail was the hook that catches beneath the skin.

"They took a priestess yesterday," Maren said, coiling rope, not looking up. "Old Mother Anthe, from the almshouse chapel. Not one of the loud ones—she just kept a candle lit, that's all she ever did. Kept a candle lit for forty years." The rope paused. "They made her kneel in the square and renounce. She wouldn't. So they burned her *candle* first, in front of her, and then—" Maren's hands resumed, steady, deliberate. "Well. Then they burned what was left."

The dock gulls screamed. Somewhere a hammer rang on an anvil, bright and indifferent.

"And the town?" Astrid asked. "The town watched?"

"The town watched." Maren finally looked up, and her eyes were flat and tired. "What else does a town do? You've been gone a while, Hartwell. The world's getting smaller. Everything that doesn't fit inside the king's fist, he's burning." She tied off the coil. "You want my advice? Finish your business and get back over the mountains. Whatever's coming, it isn't going to improve."

Astrid wanted to say that they intended exactly that, that they'd spent thirty years learning the shape of safety and intended to keep it. But what came out instead was a question they hadn't planned:

"Why now? After all these years—why is he doing this *now*?"

Maren's mouth twisted. "Because something scared him," she said. "That's the only reason powerful men burn things. Something scared him, and he can't reach it, so he burns whatever he can."

* * *

Astrid meant to leave that evening. Instead they lingered, checking Bramble's shoes, topping off supplies, telling themselves the delay was prudence and not the strange reluctance of a person standing at the edge of a change they can feel coming through the floor. They took a room at the Drowned Goose because it was cheap and anonymous, and lay awake under the eaves listening to the town settle, and somewhere past midnight they heard it.

Singing.

Faint, from the direction of the river—a woman's voice, rising and falling in a pattern too old to be a song and too rhythmic to be weeping. Astrid knew the shape of it without knowing the words. Everyone over a certain age knew the shape of it, the way you know a lullaby you haven't heard since childhood. It was a prayer-cadence. The kind sung at crossings and births and funerals, back when such things were sung openly.

It lasted perhaps a minute. Then it stopped, cut off cleanly, as if the singer had remembered herself—or been reminded.

Astrid lay in the dark with their heart beating too fast and told themselves it was a drunk. A mourner. Wind in a chimney.

In the morning, crossing the market square with their hood up and their business done, they saw the crowd before they understood it. Gathered tight around the fountain, a hundred townsfolk standing in the particular frozen silence of people witnessing something they will spend the rest of their lives describing. At the center, a woman knelt on the wet cobbles, and in her arms—

Astrid pushed forward despite every instinct screaming otherwise. Curiosity was a smuggler's occupational hazard; they'd never fully cured it.

The child in the woman's arms was dying. That much was visible even to someone who tried not to see: the grey lips, the sweat-soaked hair, the terrible slack weight of a body that has stopped fighting. Fever, by the look of it, the lung-fever that swept the river towns every few years and took the young first. Astrid had seen it take a whole family once, up in the mining camps. There was no cure. There was only the end of it, sooner or later, and everyone watching knew which end this was.

The mother was praying. Not quietly. Loudly, brokenly, in the old cadence Astrid had heard through the inn wall the night before, words worn smooth by centuries of mouths, addressed to no god in particular because all the named ones were banned and the unnamed ones were all that was left. *Whoever listens. Whoever remembers. Whoever is left.*

And the child breathed.

Not a rattle. A breath—deep, easy, the breath of a sleeping child in summer—and then another, and the grey drained out of the lips and the flush of ordinary life came flooding back, and the small body in the mother's arms arched and coughed and *cried*, a thin furious newborn wail, and the crowd made a sound Astrid had never heard a crowd make. Not cheering. Something older and more frightened than cheering. A hundred people inhaling at once, a sound like the sea drawing back from a shore before the wave comes.

The mother lifted her child up like an offering, sobbing, laughing, and the crowd did not step forward. It stepped *back*, all at once, a slow ripple of retreat, mothers pulling children close, men making signs against evil that hadn't been made openly in a generation. Joy and terror in the same faces, indistinguishable. Astrid stood among them, hood shadowing their face, and felt the same two things tearing through their own chest, and hated both.

Because Astrid knew what this was. Knew it in the blood, in the place their grandmother's hands had traced thirty years ago. Prayers were being answered. The old words still worked. Something on the far side of the world's long silence had opened one eye.

And the king was burning everything that didn't fit in his fist.

Astrid slipped backward out of the crowd before the witch-finders—who would hear of this by nightfall, who were probably already riding—could begin their work of looking closely at faces. They collected Bramble from the stable yard with hands that were almost steady, and led her out through the west gate as the bells began to ring, fast and arrhythmic, the alarm pattern nobody had used in years.

Behind them, Lowmark seethed with wonder and fear. Ahead, the Thornfell passes waited, high and silent and—Astrid could not shake the thought—*attentive*.

The mountain paths had favored them for six days now. The shrine had bloomed out of season. A dead child had breathed. And somewhere south, a frightened king was sending men with lists of old names into the borderlands, asking after bloodlines.

Astrid clicked their tongue and turned Bramble toward the high country, and the wind came down off the snows to meet them, warm where it should have been cold, carrying a scent of white flowers that had no business existing in this season.

"Superstition," Astrid said one last time, to no one.

The mountain, very softly, did not agree.
