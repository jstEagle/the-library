## 2. Prayers Answered

The trouble with border towns, Astrid had learned over thirty years of passing through them, was that they remembered faces too well and asked questions too rarely. Lowmark was the worst of both worlds: a sprawl of timber houses and mud streets huddled against the last foothills of Thornfell like a beggar against a wall, close enough to the mountains to smell their weather, far enough from any king's road to have forgotten what law felt like. Everyone in Lowmark knew everyone's business. No one in Lowmark reported anyone's business to anyone who mattered.

That was why Astrid came back twice a year, no more, and never stayed longer than a market week.

They came down out of the high passes on the fourth morning of Frostwane, when the light came thin and silver through the pines and the frost on the bracken held until nearly midday. The salt was sold—every last measure of it, at prices that would keep Astrid fed through the thaw—and in its place the pack on their shoulders carried bolts of Vaelporti linen, a small keg of southern brandy, three pounds of iron nails, and a letter sewn into the lining of their coat that they had not read and did not intend to. That was the trade. Goods moved one way, secrets moved the other, and Astrid Hartwell stood precisely in the middle of the current and let neither touch them.

The town smelled of woodsmoke and wet wool and, beneath it, the particular sourness of fear that had been sitting too long without being named. Astrid noticed it before they reached the first house. Fear had a scent; thirty years of survival had taught Astrid's nose the difference between the sharp tang of a town expecting raiders and this slower, sourer thing—a whole community holding its breath.

*Weather*, Astrid told themselves. *A hard winter coming. Wolves driven low by snow.* There was always a reason, if you wanted one badly enough.

The shrine stood where the mountain track met the Lowmark road, as it had stood for as long as anyone could remember—which is to say, longer than anyone could remember, because that was how shrines worked. It was nothing much. A waist-high cairn of field stones, cracked down one side by some old frost or older grief, crowned with a flat capstone worn smooth by centuries of hands. Once there had been hundreds of such shrines along every road in the nine valleys, each one dedicated to whichever of the old gods had claimed that stretch of earth. This one had lost whatever name it carried. The carving on the capstone had weathered to a suggestion of lines, a face or a sun or a wave, impossible now to say. Travelers still touched it, the way people touch doorframes for luck, without thinking, without believing. Belief had been wrung out of the world a generation ago, thoroughly and officially, and what remained was habit.

Astrid touched it too. They always did. They told themselves it was camouflage—an outlaw smuggler showing proper disrespect-adjacent indifference, one more gesture among thousands—but their hand found the stone before their mind gave permission, and their fingers lingered a half-second longer than habit required.

They were three steps past it when the screaming started.

Not the scream of an attack. Astrid knew that sound intimately, the way a sailor knows the sea's different roars. This was higher, rawer, ragged with something worse than terror. Grief. The specific, animal grief of a mother.

Astrid turned.

A woman was kneeling at the shrine with a child in her arms—a girl, six or seven years old, wrapped in a blanket gone grey from washing. The woman's hair had come loose from its braid and hung around her face like a curtain she couldn't be bothered with. Behind her stood a knot of townsfolk, maybe twenty of them, drawn out of doorways and off the road, frozen in the peculiar paralysis of witnesses. An old man held his hat in both hands. A boy of perhaps ten stood on his toes to see over the shoulders of the grown folk.

"The fever took her in the night," someone whispered, and the whisper traveled backward through the crowd like wind through wheat. "The gray fever. She's been cold since dawn."

Astrid should have walked on. Every instinct honed across three decades of staying alive said *walk on, walk on, you are a shadow and shadows do not stop*. Their boots even took a step in that direction, gravel crunching, and then stopped, because the mother began to pray.

It was not a fine prayer. It was not a prayer at all, really, in the way priests would have understood it—no liturgy, no invocation, none of the careful forms that had been scrubbed from the world along with everything else. It was a woman talking to a stone because there was nowhere else left to talk.

"You can't have her." Her voice cracked on the second word and she kept going anyway, pressing her daughter's body against the capstone as though she could push the child through it into wherever the gods went. "Do you hear me? I know you're not supposed to listen anymore. I know they said you're dead, they burned the temples and they said you're dead and we all agreed, we all nodded, we kept our heads down, we did what we were told—" Her breath hitched. "But you were here first. You were here before the kings and you'll be here after them, and I am asking you, I am *begging* you, take me instead. Take my hands. Take my eyes. Take anything of mine you want, but give her back."

Silence. The kind of silence that has weight, that presses on the ears. Even the wind seemed to hold itself back from the little clearing. Somewhere behind Astrid, a horse shifted and stamped, and its rider hushed it with a sound that trembled.

Nothing happened.

Of course nothing happened. The gods were dead. Everyone knew the gods were dead; King Veyric's grandfather had made very sure of it, and Veyric's father had swept up the ashes, and Veyric himself kept the broom oiled and hanging by the door. Thirty years ago the last of the oath-bound swordspeakers had been hunted down and broken, their names struck from every record, their shrines toppled into ravines, and the world had settled into the long gray quiet of a house after a funeral. Astrid had built a life inside that quiet. Had built walls of silence and routine so thick around themselves that sometimes, on good days, they almost believed the quiet was real.

The mother knelt with her dead child and the crowd began, gently, horribly, to disperse—the way people disperse from funerals, from accidents, from anything that asks more feeling than they have budgeted for the day. The old man put his hat back on. The boy was pulled away by his sleeve.

And then the crack in the shrine began to glow.

It started as a thread of gold in the fissure running down the cairn's flank, thin as spider silk, faint as the first star at dusk. Astrid saw it because they were looking at the shrine instead of away from it—because some part of them, the part they kept locked in a cellar and fed only on dark nights, had never once stopped watching. For a moment they thought it was a trick of the light, sun through breaking cloud. Then the cloud shifted and the glow did not.

"Mother," the crowd breathed, all together, without meaning to.

The gold spread. It crept along the crack like fire along a fuse, slow and deliberate, and where it passed the old stone did not burn but *brightened*, the lichen on it flushing green, the frost on the capstone melting into beads of water that caught the light like scattered coins. And the child—

The child drew breath.

It was not a gentle thing. It was a convulsion, a drowning swimmer surfacing, her small back arching against her mother's arms with a force that made the woman cry out and nearly drop her. The girl gasped, and gasped again, and color came flooding back into her face—not gradually, not the way fever breaks, but all at once, like ink dropped in water, pink blooming across cheeks that had been gray since dawn. Her eyes opened. They were brown, ordinary, a child's eyes, and they blinked up at her mother with the mild confusion of someone woken too early from a nap.

"Mama," she said. "I'm hungry."

The sound the mother made was not a word. It was the noise a person makes when something too large to survive passes through them—joy and horror fused into a single note. She clutched her daughter and rocked her and laughed and sobbed in the same breath, and the crowd did not cheer. That was the thing Astrid would remember afterward, the thing that sat in their chest like a swallowed stone: *the crowd did not cheer*. Twenty people watched a dead child live, and what moved across their faces was not joy.

It was fear.

Awe and terror in equal measure, braided so tightly they were indistinguishable. A woman near the front crossed herself with the new rite, the king's rite, her fingers shaking. The old man with the hat backed away two steps, then three, as though the shrine might notice him. Someone said, very quietly, "It shouldn't have answered," and someone else said, quieter still, "We have to tell someone," and a third voice, barely audible: "No. No, we don't. We don't have to tell anyone."

That last voice was the truest thing spoken in Lowmark that morning, and everyone knew it, and everyone also knew it wouldn't matter. News like this was a flood; you could not tell it where to go.

Astrid stood at the edge of the gathering, half in shadow of the pines, and felt the ground tilt beneath a life they had spent thirty years building.

Because Astrid knew what answered prayers meant. Not in the way the townsfolk knew it, not as rumor and dread, but in the way a soldier knows the meaning of distant drums. Prayers answered meant the old powers were stirring. And the old powers stirring meant the hunt would begin again—the purge, the pyres, the witch-finders with their iron question-marks and their lists of names. It had happened before. Astrid had been young when it happened before, young enough to watch from hiding places while people with their surname were dragged into squares and made examples of, young enough to learn the lesson that had shaped every day since:

*When the gods wake, the first thing kings do is look for those who served them.*

And the Hartwells had served. However far the name had fallen, however carefully Astrid had buried it under thirty years of anonymity and salt dust and careful silence, the blood in their veins was the blood the old oaths had been sworn on. If the gods were waking—if prayers were being answered at cracked roadside shrines by whatever fragment of divinity still clung to the stones—then somewhere, sooner or later, someone would start asking which families had been spared when the swordspeakers fell. Which names had survived in the mountains. Whose grandmothers had whispered the old words over cradles.

Whose blood still remembered.

The child was sitting up now, demanding bread, and her mother was feeding her pieces of a honey roll from her own pocket with hands that would not stop trembling. The crowd kept its distance, the way one keeps distance from a lightning-struck tree. The gold light in the shrine's crack had faded, but Astrid could still see it if they looked—could see, or imagined they saw, a faint warmth lingering in the stone, patient as an ember under ash.

*You were here before the kings,* the woman had said, and the stone had believed her.

Astrid turned and walked, fast, toward the town, because walking away from the shrine in full view of twenty witnesses would draw more eyes than walking toward the market like any other traveler with goods to sell. Their heart hammered against their ribs. Their hands, shoved deep in their coat pockets, had gone cold despite the climbing sun.

In the market square, the ordinary machinery of Lowmark churned on—fishwives arguing over eel traps, a smith's hammer ringing, children chasing a dog between the stalls—and Astrid bought a room at the Drowned Stag, bought bread and hard cheese, bought a whetstone for a blade that did not need sharpening, and did all of it with the mechanical competence of a body whose mind was elsewhere. Everywhere, fragments of conversation rose around them like sparks.

"—the Maren girl, cold as a corpse since moonset—"

"—touched the stone myself when I was a boy, nothing ever—"

"—my cousin in Harrowdale says the well water ran sweet again, after forty years of bitter—"

Harrowdale. Two days' ride east. The well water. Small things. Little mercies scattered along the borders of the world like the first flakes of a storm that has not yet decided its size.

Astrid climbed to their rented room, barred the door, and sat on the narrow bed with their elbows on their knees and their head in their hands, and let themselves feel, for exactly sixty heartbeats, the thing they could not afford to feel in the street.

Wonder. Terrible, reluctant wonder.

They had spent their whole adult life telling themselves the gods were dead, and they had mostly succeeded, the way you succeed at anything you repeat often enough. But some cellar-locked part of them had never believed it—had listened to the wind in Thornfell's high passes and heard something listening back, had touched the waystones and felt a pulse too slow to be anything living and too steady to be nothing. That part of them was awake now, and singing, and Astrid hated it.

Because wonder was a door, and doors opened from both sides.

Sixty heartbeats. Then Astrid straightened, wiped their face, and began to plan like the survivor they had made themselves: leave tomorrow, ahead of the news, take the western passes, avoid Harrowdale entirely, sell the linen in a town that hadn't seen miracles yet. Simple. Manageable. A list of ordinary things to drown out the extraordinary one.

Below the window, in the square, the mother was carrying her living daughter home, and the crowd parted for them the way crowds part for royalty—or for plague survivors. At the edge of the square, Astrid noticed, a man in a plain gray coat was watching. Not watching the miracle. Watching the crowd. Writing in a small book.

He wore no livery, but his boots were government boots, and he rode a government horse, and when he closed his book he did it with the crisp finality of a man who has finished a task and knows exactly where the next one begins.

Astrid stepped back from the window, slowly, so the curtain did not move.

The gods were waking. And the king's ears were already open.

* * *

They did not sleep that night. Toward the small hours, Astrid gave up pretending, dressed, and sat by the cold hearth with their sword across their knees—an old blade, honest steel, no runes, nothing that any witch-finder could point to and name. Just a tool. Just a smuggler's insurance.

But as the sky beyond the shutters went from black to bruise, Astrid found their thumb moving over the blade's flat, tracing a line they had never been taught and had known forever: the sign of Ondrys, god of the long road and the keeping word. The gesture their grandmother had made over their cradle. The gesture that had gotten people burned.

Astrid snatched their hand back as if the steel had bitten.

Outside, far off in the hills above Lowmark, where no shrine stood that any map recorded, something ancient and patient turned over in its sleep—and the mountain paths, come morning, would be arranged a little differently than any mapmaker remembered drawing them.

Astrid did not know that yet. But they packed their bag anyway, three hours before dawn, and stood by the door with their hand on the latch, listening to a town hold its breath, waiting for the knock that had not yet come but already, in some way they could not name, had.
