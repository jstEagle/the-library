## 3. The Usurper's Edict

The news came to Lowmark on the back of a half-dead horse.

Astrid was in the yard of the Salt Cellar, checking the stitching on a pack strap that had been threatening to give out since the last crossing, when the rider clattered through the gate. They heard him before they saw him—the ragged rhythm of hooves that had been driven too long without rest—and looked up with the mild irritation of anyone whose morning is being interrupted by someone else's emergency.

Then they saw his face.

He was young, a courier's badge on a coat still bearing the dust of the king's road, and he was shouting before his boots hit the mud. "Edict! Edict from Veyric's own hand!" He hauled a scroll from his satchel and thrust it at the innkeeper, who took it the way a man takes a snake—carefully, and wishing he hadn't.

Astrid did not move from the wall where they leaned. But their hands went still on the strap.

They listened as the innkeeper read it aloud, voice growing uncertain in the middle, because the crowd in the yard had gone very quiet, the particular quiet of people listening to something they will pretend afterward they never heard.

*By order of Veyric, first of his name, true protector of the realm: all shrines, waystones, standing circles, and places of offering devoted to the gods of the old covenant are hereby declared forfeit. They are to be broken, buried, or burned. Any who tend them, any who pray at them, any who carry the marks or words of the old oaths are to be reported to the crown's witch-finders. Reward offered for such report. Punishment for concealment: forfeiture of goods, and worse.*

*Worse.* The phrase had the polished vagueness of law written by frightened men.

The yard stayed silent after the innkeeper finished. Then, one by one, people turned back to their business—haggling, hauling, feeding animals—with the exaggerated care of actors who have forgotten their lines. No one looked at the roadside shrine across the lane, the cracked little stone house where, four days ago, a dying girl had stood up out of her own fever while her mother wept into the mud.

No one looked at it, and everyone knew it was there.

Astrid finished the strap. Their fingers were steady. Thirty years of practice will do that; thirty years of keeping your face like a closed door, your voice like gravel, your name like nothing at all. They had been Hartwell to no one but themselves for so long that the word had worn smooth, a pebble carried in the pocket and never shown.

But the edict changed the arithmetic. *Any who carry the marks or words of the old oaths.* Astrid carried both, inked into skin under their sleeves and folded into memory behind their teeth, and until this morning those things had been relics, worthless, the dead language of a dead covenant. Now they were contraband. Now Astrid Hartwell was worth reward money.

They should leave Lowmark today. Every instinct said so.

Instead they found themselves walking across the lane, when the yard had emptied, to stand before the cracked shrine.

It was a humble thing, older than the town, one of the thousand small waystones the old faith had seeded along every road in the mountains—a stone roof over a carved niche, the figure inside weathered nearly faceless. Ondrys, once. Or one of his kin. Generations of travelers had left bread crusts and copper bits and knotted ribbons, and generations of priests of the new covenant had pretended not to see, because the little shrines were as unremarkable as fence posts.

Someone had already come with a hammer. Astrid could see the fresh chips at the base, pale wounds in grey stone, and the work abandoned halfway—the hammer lying in the weeds, its owner having lost his nerve, or gained some sense.

Astrid crouched and touched the chipped stone. It was warm.

Not sun-warm. The day was overcast, the light flat as pewter, and the stone beneath their palm held a heat like a sleeping animal, faint and patient. Astrid snatched their hand back and stood, heart tapping quick against their ribs.

*Weather,* they told themselves. *Geothermal springs. The mountain does strange things.*

The mountain had been doing strange things for two weeks now, ever since the girl in the fever. Paths that should have washed out in the spring melt holding firm under Astrid's boots. A rockslide that had blocked the high pass for a decade—Astrid had walked around its wreckage every season for years—found cleared, as if the mountain had swallowed its own debris overnight. Fog that parted ahead of them like a curtain drawn by a considerate servant.

Astrid had made a career out of reading country that didn't lie, and the country was lying to them now, or flattering them, and either way they didn't like it. Flattery from the world itself was not a thing that happened to smugglers. Smugglers got weather, bad luck, and occasionally a knife between the shoulder blades.

"Best not touch it," said a voice behind them. "Not anymore."

Old Maren, the tanner's widow, wrapped in her shawl despite the mild air, watching Astrid with eyes that had seen seventy winters and remembered most of them.

"I wasn't touching it," Astrid said. "Looking."

"Mm." Maren came closer, lowered her voice. "You were at the Wexley girl's healing. I saw you there, at the edge. You left before the cheering started." Her gaze was sharp as an awl. "Most folks ran toward it. You backed away slow, like from a fire."

"Fires are sensible things to back away from."

"So are miracles, maybe." Maren glanced at the shrine, then away from it quickly, superstitious even now—or especially now. "My grandmother used to say the gods don't come back all at once. They come back like water finding a crack. First a drip. Then a flood." She pulled the shawl tighter. "King's men broke the big circle at Harrow Ford yesterday. Twelve stones. Took them till dark and the men were sick after, sick to their bellies, wouldn't say why. And the witch-finders are coming here. To Lowmark. My cousin's boy saw their outriders on the south road, grey cloaks, riding two by two."

"When?"

"Soon. Days, maybe less." Maren fixed Astrid with a long look. "You're leaving, then."

It wasn't a question. Everyone in Lowmark knew Astrid left often and came back sometimes, and no one asked where, because Astrid paid in good silver and asked no questions either. That was the arrangement, and it had kept them all comfortable for years.

"I've a run scheduled," Astrid said.

"Take the north track, whatever you do. Not the south road." Maren hesitated, then added, quieter: "There was a man asking questions at my stall two days past. Before the edict even came. About guides through Thornfell. About—" she paused, and Astrid felt the ground tilt a half-inch beneath them— "about whether there were any Hartwells left in these parts."

The cold that went through Astrid then had nothing to do with the weather.

"Hartwell's a common enough name," they said, and their voice did not shake, which was the finest piece of craftsmanship they'd done all year.

"Not up here it isn't." Maren shrugged. "I told him nothing. But he wrote things down. Grey cloak under his traveling leathers, if you ask me. They always write things down."

She shuffled off toward her stall, and Astrid stood alone in the lane beside the warm, wounded shrine, doing sums they did not like.

Thirty years. Thirty years of being nobody, and in two days the name had surfaced twice—once in a stranger's mouth via Maren's stall, and once in the king's own edict, that phrase about old oaths. Coincidence was a coin Astrid had spent too often to trust it now.

They went to the Salt Cellar, paid their tab, bought three more days of provisions than the run required, and told no one when they were leaving.

* * *

They left at dusk, taking the north track as Maren advised, and the mountain received them the way it had been receiving them lately—with an ease that felt less like luck than like intention. The fog lifted off the trail ahead of them. The footing, treacherous scree in ordinary years, held like paved road.

Astrid hated it. They would have traded all of it—the clear paths, the kind weather—for one honest stretch of miserable trail.

On the second evening, camped in a hollow below the tree line with a small fire screened by stones, they heard the bells.

Lowmark's chapel bell, thin with distance, ringing out of pattern—not the hour, not the alarm, but the rapid repeated peal that meant *come and see*. And beneath it, fainter, another sound Astrid had no name for at first, and then did: shouting. Many voices. And a deep undertone, rhythmic, like mauls striking stone.

They climbed the shoulder above their camp in the last grey light and looked back the way they'd come. Far below, the town was a scatter of ember-colored points against the darkening valley, and from somewhere near its edge rose a glow too large and steady to be a cooking fire. The hammering sound carried up the mountain on the wind: the witch-finders, working already, breaking the shrine on the low road. Perhaps others. Perhaps all of them.

Maren's cousin's boy had been right about the schedule. Astrid had gotten out by a day, maybe two. Maybe hours.

They stood there longer than was wise, watching the little lights of a town where they had bought bread and mended straps and been left alone for eleven years. Something in their chest that they refused to examine tightened like a fist.

"They'll salt the ground next," said Astrid aloud, to no one, because saying things aloud to no one was a habit loneliness breeds and kills slowly. "Burning's for churches. Salt's what you use when you're afraid it grows back."

The mountain wind moved through the pines below, a long exhale, almost a sigh.

"Don't," Astrid told it. "Whatever you're doing—all of it—the paths, the warmth, the listening. Don't. I didn't ask. I don't want it."

The wind died. The silence that followed was somehow worse.

* * *

Two days north, at the crossing of the Weep and the old drover's road, Astrid stopped at the waystation to trade for arrowheads and heard the news that made the edict feel small.

Veyric hadn't just dispatched witch-finders. He'd issued a second decree, read aloud in every market square in the lowlands: the Thornfell passes were to be sealed. Trade suspended. All traffic through the mountains subject to search and charter, effective within the month. And with the decree, the usual rumor-mill freight: witch-finder companies moving west in strength, a hundred strong, maybe more, under a captain named Dreven, who had burned nine people in Harrow Ford and was said to sleep only two hours a night and weep in his sleep the other twenty-two. Who was said—this from a drover who claimed a cousin among the outriders—to keep a list. A list of names. Old names. Names out of the covenant rolls.

Astrid bought the arrowheads, kept their face bored, and asked, in the flat tone of a man discussing tariffs, "This Dreven. He have a reason to come to the mountains? Nothing up here but goats and smugglers."

The trader shrugged. "Reason enough, they say. There's a bloodline the king wants found. Sword-oath folk, from the war times. The Hartwells." A pause, a spit into the dust. "King means to end them root and branch. Says they're vessels. Whatever that means."

"Means nothing to me," Astrid said. "Never met a Hartwell."

"Didn't say you had." The trader counted change, incurious. Incurious was the great gift of the lowlands, and Astrid accepted it gratefully and walked out with their pulse loud in their ears.

Root and branch. Vessels. Someone in Veyric's court knew what the swordspeakers had been, and was hunting the bloodline by name—which meant the king's fear had a source, a book or a prisoner or a memory, and that source had produced Astrid's family name like a physician producing a diagnosis.

And Astrid had spent thirty years being the last of it.

They camped that night higher up, in the rocks, no fire, chewing dried meat in the dark and listening to the mountain breathe. Somewhere below, on the roads they'd traveled all their life, men with grey cloaks and lists were riding toward the same empty cabin, the same cold hearth, the same hollow where a mentor had vanished years ago without a grave.

Sleep didn't come. Around midnight, Astrid gave up, sat up, and spoke to the darkness again, because the habit was there and the dark was listening.

"If you're real," they said, "if any of you are real—if you're waking up in shrines and moving stones for me like I'm somebody's favorite child—then understand me. I don't want saving. I don't want crowns or quests or purpose. I want to be left alone. That's the whole of my prayer. Leave me alone."

The stars did not answer. But far down the valley, at the edge of hearing, a wolf began to howl, long and low, and though wolves were rare in Thornfell these days—rare for decades—Astrid could not shake the feeling that it was not hunting anything. That it was calling. That it was answered, faintly, from another ridge, and another, a chain of voices passing something along the dark like a message traveling hand to hand.

Astrid lay back down and stared at the stars and did not sleep.

* * *

They came down out of the high country five days later, circling wide of Lowmark, aiming for the western trails and the border towns beyond, where a person with a plain face and plain goods could be plain and be paid. At the ford of the Weep, where the water ran shallow over red stones, they found the road already occupied.

A rider waited there. A woman, alone, mounted on a good grey mare, dressed in travel leathers of better cut than any drover's, with a sword at her hip worn the way soldiers wear swords—like part of the body. She had a diplomat's composure and a hawk's attention, and when her eyes found Astrid across the ford, they did not glance away the way strangers' eyes usually did. They assessed. Catalogued. Settled.

"You're the guide called Rook," she said. Not quite a question. Astrid's working name, passed along the smuggler's grapevine, sold to whoever needed a path through Thornfell and could pay.

"I'm whoever paid the fee," Astrid said, wading toward the near bank. "You looking to cross?"

"Eventually. First I'm looking for a guide who knows the high passes in early thaw, can move quietly, and doesn't ask what's in the cargo." She dismounted, unhurried, and Astrid marked the economy of the movement—no wasted motion anywhere in the woman. "Sela Voss. Envoy of the Free Cities of the Vael Concord. I'm authorized to retain you for the full season."

"The full season's expensive."

"The free cities are wealthy and frightened. An ugly combination for their enemies and a profitable one for their friends." She reached into her coat—not fast, deliberately slow, letting Astrid watch—and drew out a letter, sealed with the Concord's interlocked-rings stamp. "Terms in writing. Half in advance."

Astrid didn't take the letter. "Free cities sending envoys over the Weep instead of the southern roads? Southern roads are safer this time of year."

"Southern roads are watched. Yours aren't. Yet." Sela's gaze was steady, and there was something underneath the professionalism, some pressure held carefully in check. "Thornfell's being sealed, Master Rook. King Veyric has decided the mountains belong to him now. My council wants routes that don't appear on his maps. I'm told you're the only guide alive who knows paths that don't appear on any maps."

"And who told you that?"

"A man in Lowmark. Before it became unhealthy to talk to him." Something flickered across her face, grief filed away for later. "The witch-finders reached the town the night after I left. I'm told they're making lists there now. Names."

The water talked over the red stones between them. Astrid stood very still.

"Names of what?" they asked. "Smugglers?"

"Names of an old bloodline." Sela watched them with those hawk's eyes, and Astrid understood, with the slow cold certainty of a trap closing, that this conversation had never been about cargo or passes at all. "The envoy before me carried a genealogy. Covenant rolls, copied from archives in Vaelport. Rolls of the swordspeaker families who served the old gods—the oath-bound, the god-touched. Most lines are extinct. Burned out, married out, faded." She paused. "One line isn't. One line survived in the Thornfell high country, under another name, doing work no honest person asks about."

The river was very loud. Astrid's hand rested at their side, a hand's width from the knife they hoped to God they wouldn't need—hoping to a God they'd spent thirty years insisting was dead.

"I don't know what you've heard, Envoy," they said, "but Rook's my only name. Born under a rock. No family."

"No," Sela agreed quietly. "You'd say that. Anyone would, after thirty years." She looked at Astrid then without calculation, and what was in her face was worse than suspicion—it was recognition, the look of a woman seeing a legend step out of a story she'd only read. "Hartwell."

The name landed between them like a dropped blade.

The river ran. On the ridge above the ford, unnoticed by either of them, a raven lifted off a dead pine and flew west, and the wind that followed it down the valley smelled, impossibly, faintly, of woodsmoke and snowmelt and something older—something like incense burning in a temple that had been ash for a century.

Astrid did not deny it. Denial was a door, and doors could be forced; silence was stone. They simply stood in the shallows of the Weep with the water pulling at their boots, thirty years of invisibility dissolving around them like frost in sunlight, and understood that whatever happened next, the long pretending was over.

"How," they said finally, and their voice sounded strange to them, rusted, like a hinge unused. "How long have you known."

"Since the letter described the survivor. Age, build, habits, a scar on the left forearm from a bear-grease burn." Sela's mouth twitched, not quite a smile. "You've been hiding in the one place in the world your name is a legend, Master Hartwell. The mountains remember you even if you've forgotten yourself."

Above them, far up Thornfell's shoulders, the peaks caught the afternoon light and held it, patient as a held breath. Somewhere in that white silence was a cabin Astrid hadn't visited in six years, and a grave that had never been dug, and answers Halvard had died rather than give.

Astrid looked at the envoy, at the sealed letter, at the road south where grey-cloaked men were writing down names.

"What exactly," they said, "do your free cities think I am?"
