## 4. What the Mentor Buried

The mountain let Astrid come home.

That was the only way to describe it. Three days out of Lowmark, moving by the old smuggler's routes that existed on no map and in no honest memory, and every fork in the trail had opened the way they needed to go before Astrid had even decided which way that was. The cairns were standing where they should have fallen decades ago. The scree slope below Raven's Tooth had a fresh path cut through it, neat as a stair, where last autumn there had been nothing but a throat of loose stone.

Astrid did not like it. Astrid liked it so little that they had spent half a day deliberately taking the wrong route—the hard one, the one that required rope and bleeding knuckles—just to prove the mountain couldn't simply *hand* them things.

It hadn't mattered. The wrong route had turned out to be passable too, in ways it shouldn't have been, and Astrid had arrived at the cabin's hidden valley with rope burns on their palms and a cold knot of dread sitting behind their sternum like a swallowed stone.

Smoke was rising from the chimney.

Astrid stopped at the treeline and stood very still for a long time, letting the wind move through them, cataloguing everything the way Halvard had taught them thirty years ago: *smoke first, then sound, then scent, then sight.* The smoke was thin and gray-green, pine boughs thrown on the fire to hide its smell—a trick only two people alive knew, and one of them was standing in this valley. There was no horse at the tethering post. No tracks but one set of boot prints, old and dragging, heel-scuffed in the way of a man who no longer lifted his feet all the way.

"Halvard," Astrid said aloud, to nobody, and the word came out cracked down the middle.

They had buried him eleven years ago. They had carried him up the mountain themselves, wrapped him in oiled canvas because there wasn't money for shroud-cloth, and laid him under the leaning stone above the winter creek with a sword across his chest that they'd stolen back from a collector in Vaelport at considerable personal risk. They had spoken the closing words of the swordspeaker rite over the grave even though speaking them made their skin crawl, even though the gods those words served were supposed to be dead, because Halvard deserved them and dead men could not be embarrassed by sentiment.

And now his smoke was rising from his own chimney.

Astrid's hand found the hilt of the short blade at their hip—an ordinary blade, bought honestly, used for skinning game and once, badly, for cutting a man's throat in Lowmark—and they walked down into the valley with it drawn, because whatever waited in that cabin, it was not going to find them surprised.

* * *

The door was unlocked. That alone nearly stopped them; Halvard had never in his life left a door unlocked, had drilled into Astrid that a locked door buys you three seconds and three seconds is a life.

Inside, the cabin smelled of woodsmoke and something else—something sweetish and wrong, like flowers rotting in a closed room. The single room was just as Astrid remembered it: the iron stove, the shelf of jars, the bed in the corner with its wool blanket worn thin at the fold. A pot of water sat on the stove, not quite boiling. Two cups stood on the table. One was full and steaming. The other was empty, waiting.

Halvard sat in the chair by the window.

He looked *terrible*. That was the first coherent thought Astrid's mind produced, absurdly, after the initial white shock: he looks terrible, worse than terrible, he looks like a man who has been dying for weeks and has finally gotten around to admitting it. He had been a big man once, broad as a door, with hands that could bend an iron nail straight. Now he sat folded into himself like a coat hung on a hook, his skin the color of tallow, his white beard straggling uncombed down his chest. His eyes, though. His eyes were the same—pale gray, sharp as chipped flint, and they found Astrid in the doorway the way they always had, missing nothing, approving of less.

"You drew your blade on an old man's chimney," Halvard said. His voice was gravel dragged over gravel. "I taught you better manners."

"You're dead," Astrid said. Their voice sounded far away to their own ears. "I buried you. I know the spot. I can take you to it."

"And I thank you for the sword, by the way. Vaelport steel. You always did have expensive taste with other people's coin." He coughed then, a long wet tearing cough that bent him double, and Astrid crossed the room without deciding to, sheathed the blade, and put a hand on his back the way they had when they were nine years old and fevered and frightened of the dark. His spine was a ridge of knots beneath their palm. He weighed nothing. When the coughing passed he slumped against their arm and breathed in shallow, whistling pulls.

"Water," he managed.

Astrid brought him the waiting cup. Their hands were not steady. They noticed this with a kind of detached irritation, the way one notices weather.

"How," Astrid said. "Eleven years, Halvard. I dug your grave. I wept on it. I—" They stopped, because the next part was shameful: *I talked to it.* For years, on the nights the loneliness got teeth, Astrid had sat on the leaning stone above the winter creek and told their dead mentor about salt prices and weather and the dreams they wouldn't say aloud. "Who did I bury?"

"A pox victim from the lowlands. Unclaimed. It seemed kindest." He drank, wincing. "You were young. You grieved clean and quick, the way I wanted. Grief is a fine teacher but a poor companion, and I needed you moving, not mourning. So I gave you a grave and I went east."

"East." Astrid pulled the second chair around and sat, slowly, never taking their eyes off him. "East is Veyric's country. East is where witch-finders burn people."

"Yes."

"You went *east*. Into the purge. With a face every shrine-keeper in three kingdoms would know." Astrid heard their own voice climbing and forced it flat. "Why?"

Halvard set the cup down. His hand trembled doing it, and he looked at the tremble with distaste, as if it belonged to someone else. Then he looked up, and what was in his face made Astrid's stomach drop—not guilt exactly, though there was guilt in it, but something older and more tired than guilt. The look of a man who has been carrying a load so long his shoulders have grown around it.

"Because the prayers started being answered again," he said. "Three years ago. In a village east of the Serpent's Spine, a well that had gone bitter came sweet overnight. Then a child in Karthen fell from a hayloft and stood up without a mark. Small things. Things people explained away, at first. But I'm old, Astrid, and old men remember what the world was like before the Silence, from our grandmothers' stories. I knew the taste of it." He smiled, and it was a terrible smile. "So I went to see how bad it was. And it's worse than bad. It's beginning."

"The girl in Lowmark." Astrid's mouth had gone dry. "The shrine. Yesterday."

"You heard already?"

"I was *there*." And then it all came out of them in a rush, the words they hadn't said to any living soul: the cracked roadside shrine, the mother kneeling in the mud, the light like honey through the crack in the stone, the child breathing. The crowd's faces. The way the whole town had rippled with terror and hunger at once, like a pond struck by a stone. "Half of them wanted to kneel and half of them wanted to run and I don't think a single one of them knew which. And the witch-finders were already riding in before the mother finished thanking her."

Halvard nodded, unsurprised, unsurprising. Of course he knew. He had probably been in Lowmark's shadows watching the same crowd.

"And the mountain," Astrid said, quieter now. "The paths. They've been... helping me. Moving for me. Cairns standing where they fell. A road through Raven's Tooth that didn't exist last year." They laughed, and it came out wrong. "I told myself it was weather. I've been telling myself it's weather for two months."

"It isn't weather."

"No," Astrid said. "I know it isn't weather."

Silence filled the cabin, thick as the rot-sweet smell. Outside, wind moved through the pines with a long sighing sound, and Astrid thought, absurdly, *it sounds like breathing.*

"Halvard." They leaned forward, elbows on knees, the way they had leaned a thousand times across this same table while he taught them cipher and star-reading and which mushrooms lied. "Tell me why you're here. Tell me why you crawled back up my mountain to die in my cabin instead of dying warm somewhere, because I know you, and you don't do anything without a reason, and you especially don't fake your own death and abandon a grieving child without a reason that would crack stone."

The old man was quiet for a long moment. When he spoke, his voice had changed—lower, careful, each word placed like a foot on river ice.

"Do you remember the oath?" he asked. "The real one. Not the words I let you speak over my grave. The one from the old days, the one the swordspeakers swore to Ondrys and his kin."

"I remember you made me memorize it and then told me it was dead history and never to say it aloud where anyone could hear."

"And why did I tell you that?"

Astrid frowned. "Because the kings burned the orders. Because saying it marked you."

"Partly." Halvard's eyes held theirs, flint-pale, unblinking. "Say it now. Just to me. Here, where no one living can hear."

Something in Astrid's chest went tight and cold. "Halvard—"

"*Say it.*"

They said it. They hadn't spoken the full rite in over a decade, but it lived in them the way a childhood song lives in the body, deeper than memory. The words came out rough and reluctant:

*"Blade and breath and bounden blood, I give what was given to me. While the mountain stands and the river runs, my word is the god's word, and the god's word is my word, until the last stone forgets the last name."*

The moment the last syllable left their lips, three things happened at once.

The fire in the stove leapt—actually leapt, flames standing tall and burning suddenly blue-white at their hearts, throwing wild shadows up the walls. The wind outside stopped mid-breath, the whole valley going silent as a held lung. And Astrid felt something turn over inside them, deep beneath the breastbone, in a place that had no name—a vast slow shifting, like a whale rolling over in dark water, like a mountain settling in its sleep. Warmth flooded up their arms and into their hands, and for one vertiginous instant they could feel the cabin entire: every beam and nail, the iron of the stove, the silver thread of the creek beyond the wall, all of it bright and *known*, mapped in their blood—

Then it passed. The fire settled. The wind resumed. Astrid sat shaking in the chair, their heart slamming, their hands flat on their thighs to keep them still.

"What," they said, "was that."

"That," Halvard said softly, "is why I faked my death and left you alone for eleven years, and I will spend what's left of my eternity apologizing for it." He gripped the arms of his chair and hauled himself upright, swaying, and Astrid moved to steady him and he waved them off with a flicker of his old imperiousness. He shuffled to the shelf, to the row of clay jars, and took down the third from the left—the one Astrid had always assumed held dried yarrow—and broke its seal. Inside was a bundle of oilcloth, and inside that, a book. Small, leather-bound, scarred, its cover stamped with a sigil Astrid knew intimately: the mountain-and-river mark of the swordspeaker order, worn nearly smooth by centuries of thumbs.

"The oaths never died," Halvard said. He turned the book in his ruined hands almost tenderly. "We told ourselves they did, because it was safer. The kings burned the shrines and scattered the order and called the gods silent, and we let them believe it, because a sleeping thing is best left looking like a corpse. But an oath sworn in blood doesn't die when the tongue stops saying it, Astrid. It goes down the bloodline. It sleeps in the blood. Grandmother to daughter, father to son, quiet as a coal under ash. Most lines thinned out. Most descendants are just people, good people, who feel a strange pull toward high places and old stones and never know why." He looked up. "But some lines didn't thin."

Astrid's mouth was very dry. "Mine."

"Yours." Halvard lowered himself back into the chair by degrees, like a man descending into cold water. "Your grandmother's line runs back to the last High Speaker of Thornfell. I chose you, boy—I *found* you, do you understand? I didn't take in a starving orphan out of charity. I took in the last living vessel of an oath three kingdoms old, and I trained you, and I hid you, and I let you believe the worst lie of your life: that you were nobody. That hiding was safety." His voice frayed. "Hiding was never safety. Hiding was a delay. And delays end."

Outside, the wind had died completely. The silence pressing against the cabin walls felt attentive.

"The answered prayers," Astrid said slowly. Each word cost effort. "The mountain moving. The shrine in Lowmark. The—the thing that just happened when I spoke the oath." They looked at their own hands as if they belonged to a stranger. "That's me. That's coming from me."

"You carry more than the oath." Halvard's gaze dropped to the book in his lap, then rose again, and Astrid saw clearly for the first time that the old man was afraid. Halvard, who had faced down witch-finders and kings and the slow betrayal of his own body without blinking, was afraid, and it was for Astrid, and somehow that was the most frightening thing in the room. "When the order fell, the last High Speaker did something desperate. Something the books barely whisper about. She couldn't destroy what she carried—no one can destroy it—but she could hide it, the way we hid the oath. She sealed it into her own blood and let it sink down the generations, thinner and thinner, a candle flame cupped in a fist, hoping it would gutter out." He paused. "It didn't gutter out. It slept. And it slept in her line, down and down, until it slept in you."

"Carried *what*?" Astrid's voice came out barely above a whisper.

"A fragment," Halvard said. "Of Ondrys himself. The last living piece of the Wolf of the Mountain. Sealed in Hartwell blood for two hundred years." He drew a ragged breath. "And now the prayers are being answered and the shrines are waking and the mountain itself is turning toward you like a flower toward sun, because the fragment knows the age of Silence is ending, and it is *waking up*, Astrid. It's been waking for months. Every strange kindness of the trails, every dream you never told me about—don't look surprised, I raised you, I know your face—it's all the same thing. The god in your blood, stirring. Growing. Reaching for the surface."

The cabin tilted. Or Astrid did. They gripped the table's edge and the wood bit their palms and the pain helped, anchored them, kept the roaring in their ears from becoming a scream. *A god. In me. In my blood, in my bones, in the body I have guarded and hidden and kept my own for forty years—*

"You knew." The words came out flat and deadly. "You've known since you found me. Since I was a *child*. You watched me learn to walk these mountains, watched me build this life, this small safe invisible life, and you never—"

"If I had told you at nine years old that a god lived in your veins, what would you have done?" Halvard's voice cracked like a whip, and then crumbled. "What would I have done at nine? I'd have gone looking. I'd have wanted to *meet* it. And there are things east of the Spine that listen for exactly that wanting—Veyric's witch-finders have instruments, Astrid, rites, interrogations of the blood. A child who knows what she carries shines like a beacon. A child who doesn't is just a smuggler with a rude mouth and good boots." He sagged. "I hid the truth to keep you hidden. It worked for thirty years. I'd do it again. Gods forgive me, I'd do it again."

"And now?"

"Now it's ending either way." He pushed the book across the table toward Astrid with two fingers, as if it might bite. "Everything the order knew about the fragment, about the sealing, about what wakes and how and what it wants—my master wrote it, and his master before him. It's yours. It should have been yours at twenty, and I stole those years from you, and I'll answer for that somewhere, someday." His breath caught, held, released. "There's more you need to know. About what the fragment does when it fully wakes. About what the High Speaker saw that made her seal it rather than use it. About the choice that's coming for you, sooner than—"

He stopped.

Not paused. *Stopped.* Mid-word, mid-breath, his mouth still shaped around the next syllable, and Astrid watched the color leave his face in a wave, from the jaw upward, like frost claiming a window.

"Halvard?"

He slid sideways in the chair. Astrid caught him—caught all ninety pounds of him—and eased him down onto the floor, onto their own cloak, shouting his name, pressing fingers to his throat where the pulse fluttered like a moth in a jar and then didn't flutter at all.

"No." They bent close, shook him, pressed their ear to his chest. "No, no—you don't get to—you don't get to come back from the dead twice and die in the middle of a *sentence*—"

His eyes opened. Once. Just enough. The flint-gray found Astrid's face, and his hand twitched against the floorboards, and with the last air in his lungs he made a sound that might have been a word—*blood*, or *below*, or maybe only a breath shaped like grief—and then Halvard Eriksen, last master of the swordspeaker order, the only father Astrid Hartwell had ever had, died for the second and final time in a cabin that smelled of woodsmoke and rotting flowers.

Astrid knelt over him for a long time. The fire ticked. The wind stayed silent, as if the whole valley were listening.

When they finally moved, they moved like a smuggler: efficiently, precisely, hands busy so the rest of them didn't have to be. They washed him. They wrapped him in the good blanket, the one from the bed, because there was no one left to scold them for wasting it. They carried him out into the blue hour of evening and up to the leaning stone above the winter creek, and they dug beside the false grave with their bare hands when the spade handle snapped, and they laid him down next to the stranger who had worn his name for eleven years, and they spoke the closing words of the rite with a voice that did not break until the very last line.

Then they went back inside, and sat at the table, and looked at the book.

The sigil on its cover caught the firelight. Beneath it, in faded ink, someone had written a title in the old hand: *On the Fragment and Its Keeping.*

Astrid reached out and touched it, and deep beneath their breastbone, in the nameless place, something vast rolled over in its sleep—and this time, unmistakably, it was reaching *back*.

Far to the east, down in the lowlands, torches were moving along the King's Road. Astrid couldn't see them. But some part of them, some part that was not entirely Astrid anymore, felt them coming anyway.
