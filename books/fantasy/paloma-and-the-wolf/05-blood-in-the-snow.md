## 5. Blood in the Snow

The snow came down in fat, silent flakes that swallowed sound and turned the world to a held breath. Astrid moved through it the way they had moved through thirty winters of Thornfell: low, quick, reading the mountain's moods in the tilt of branches and the weight of wind against their back.

They had left Lowmark two days ahead of the witch-finders. Two days was nothing. Two days was a margin so thin it could be eaten by one wrong turn, one frozen stream crossing, one night's fire that smoked too green. But Halvard's words were still lodged under Astrid's ribs like a splinter working toward the heart—*the oaths never died; they sleep in bloodlines*—and they could not think clearly in a town full of people who might already be counting Hartwells on their fingers.

So they ran. Not because running would help, necessarily. Because standing still meant thinking, and thinking meant feeling, and feeling meant grief, and grief was a luxury with witch-finders on the road behind them.

The old shepherd's trail climbed out of Lowmark's valley through pine shadow, then broke above the treeline onto the shoulder of the mountain where nothing grew but lichen and stubborn stone. The waystones began there. They always did—grey fingers of rock taller than a man, each one carved with spirals so worn by weather that only the deepest grooves still held their shape. Astrid had passed them ten thousand times without ceremony. Salt-carriers didn't pray at waystones. Praying got people noticed, and being noticed got people burned.

But today, passing the third stone, Astrid felt something like attention settle over them. Not eyes exactly. More like the particular silence of a room when someone has just stopped speaking because you entered it.

"Superstition," they muttered, and their breath came out white and ragged. "Weather and superstition."

The lie tasted thinner every time they said it.

* * *

Dreven caught up to them at dusk on the second day.

Astrid knew before they saw him—the way you know a storm is coming by the ache in old scars. They crested a rise above a frozen hollow where a stream ran black beneath its ice, and there they were: six riders fanning out along the lower slope, moving with the unhurried confidence of hunters who have never once been prey. Their lanterns glowed yellow through the snowfall. At their center rode a broad figure in a grey cloak trimmed with iron-grey fur, and even at this distance Astrid knew the set of those shoulders.

Captain Dreven. Veyric's hound. The man who had burned three shrines in Lowmark's outlying farms and salted the earth where the ash fell, and who had done it all with the mild, patient expression of a schoolmaster correcting sums.

Astrid dropped flat behind the ridge and did arithmetic. Six riders. One crossbow visible, maybe two. Open ground between here and the tree line—a quarter mile of scree and drifts, no cover worth the name. And behind Astrid, the waystones marched up the mountain like a fence of sleeping giants.

There was no path that didn't pass them. There never had been. That was what waystones were for, Halvard had said once, drunk enough to be honest: *they mark where the old agreements were signed, girl. You don't walk past an agreement. You walk under it.*

Astrid hadn't asked what he meant. Asking questions about the old gods was how you ended up dead or worse, and Halvard had taught them that lesson with bruises before he ever taught it with words.

Now six riders were climbing toward them through the snow, and Astrid drew their knife—not the smuggler's blade they used for cutting rope and occasionally throats, but the other one. The sword they'd sworn never to carry openly again. It had lived wrapped in oilcloth at the bottom of their pack for thirty years, and their hands remembered its weight the way a tongue remembers a lost tooth: constantly, painfully, uselessly.

They started downslope, angling for the stream, meaning to break ice and wade—cold kills scent, cold kills pursuit, cold kills everything eventually—and the snow chose that exact moment to betray them.

The crust gave. Astrid went down hard into a hidden crevasse mouth, sliding, scrabbling, and the knife—of course, the sword, the oath-blade—tore free of its wrapping and opened their palm from heel to finger as they caught themselves on rock.

Blood hit the snow. Bright, shocking red, absurdly vivid against all that white.

And the mountain woke up.

* * *

It began as warmth. Astrid felt it spread outward from the spilled blood like ink dropped in water—not heat exactly, but *recognition*, a vast slow attention turning toward them from somewhere below the stone, below the roots, below the deep places where things slept that had no business sleeping anywhere near a smuggler's route.

The nearest waystone began to glow.

Not light, precisely. It was more that the darkness inside the carved spirals reversed itself, became luminous, became *hungry*. Frost crawled off the stone in curling sheets. Snow around its base slid away as if shoved aside by hands. And from within it—from within all of them, five stones down the hollow, six, seven—came a sound like a long inhalation drawn through granite.

The horses screamed first. Animals always knew. Three of them reared and bolted, dumping their riders into the drifts, and the remaining witch-finders sat frozen in their saddles with the particular stillness of men who have just realized the story they tell others has become the story told about them.

Dreven's voice carried up the slope, steady, commanding: "Form line. Whatever this is, it bleeds and it burns. Form *line*."

He was braver than his men. Astrid would give him that much, later, when they had time to give anyone anything. He wheeled his horse and leveled his crossbow at the glowing stone as if faith in king and kindling could substitute for understanding.

The guardian came out of the ground.

That was the only honest word for it. It did not climb out; the ground simply stopped pretending to hold it, and it rose—a shape like a man the way a landslide is like walking. Twenty feet of stone and root and packed earth, its body studded with waystone shards that glowed in place of eyes, its arms ending not in hands but in something like blades grown from living rock. Where its chest should have been, there was a spiral—the same spiral as on the stones, turning slowly, and Astrid understood with sick certainty that it was turning toward them. Toward their blood. Toward the signature their blood had just written on the snow.

It looked at Astrid.

It *knew* them.

"*Oathsworn,*" it said, and its voice was an avalanche given grammar, and Astrid's whole body flinched as though struck. "*Called. Answered.*"

"I didn't—" Astrid's voice cracked. "I didn't call anything. I'm nobody. I'm a *carrier*, I move salt—"

The guardian was already moving. It ignored Astrid completely, the way a hammer ignores the hand that drops it once the falling starts, and it went down the slope toward the riders with a speed that should have been impossible for a thing made of mountain.

What followed took less time than Astrid could bear to remember afterward.

The crossbow bolts hit it and simply sank, swallowed, absorbed—the stone closing over them like water. The first rider tried to wheel away and the guardian's arm swept through him and his horse both, and there was no scream because there wasn't time for one, just a wet crunch and then shapes in the snow that had been a man moments before. The second and third riders made it three steps before the ground itself betrayed them, opening beneath their horses, folding them down into dark. The fourth got a torch lit—one small human defiance, flame guttering orange against all that blue-black glow—and the guardian paused, tilted its head almost curiously, and then exhaled a breath of grave-cold air that snuffed the torch and flash-froze the man's beard to his face before the stone arms finished the work.

Astrid watched. That was the worst part—they watched, kneeling in the snow with their cut hand pressed against their thigh, unable to look away, unable to intervene, unable to do anything but witness what their blood had summoned. What *they* had summoned. However accidentally, however unwillingly, the distinction felt obscene. A thing had died twenty feet from them because their palm had bled, and some buried part of them had understood the guardian's first word—*oathsworn*—and had not contradicted it.

Dreven alone survived longer. He dismounted when his horse threw him, drew steel, and actually landed a blow—a real strike across the guardian's torso that chipped it, that made it stagger. For one heartbeat Astrid thought: *he can hurt it, it can be hurt, maybe—*

Then the wound closed, stone flowing smooth like ice melting in reverse, and Dreven stood alone in a field of his dead with snow falling on his upturned face, and he looked—not at the guardian—but up the slope, directly at Astrid.

Even at that distance, in that light, Astrid saw his expression change. Saw the moment the schoolmaster's patience curdled into something else. Saw him understand exactly what he had found, and what it would mean for him to bring it back to Veyric alive.

"*Hartwell,*" he mouthed, or maybe said aloud; the wind stole it either way.

The guardian raised its arm.

"No!" Astrid was moving before they knew it, sliding down the slope on their heels, shouting into the wind. "No—leave him—I didn't ask for this—*leave him!*"

The guardian paused. Turned its burning spiral-face toward them, and for a moment—just a moment—it seemed to listen. Seemed almost gentle, in the way a flood is gentle when it considers whether to rise another inch.

"*Oathsworn commands?*"

"I command you to stop." Astrid's voice shook. "I command you to—to go back. To sleep. I never wanted any of this."

A silence. The snow fell between them, each flake catching the blue glow.

"*Too late for wanting,*" the guardian said, and there was something in its tone that Astrid would spend many sleepless nights trying to classify—pity, perhaps, or the terrible tenderness of old things for young ones who don't yet know what they carry. "*The blood remembers what the mind forgets. Sleep now, little oath. I will keep your door.*"

And it folded down into the earth like water sinking into sand, taking its glow with it, and the waystones went dark one after another up the mountainside like windows being shuttered, and the hollow was suddenly ordinary—just snow, just corpses cooling toward the temperature of the ground, just one man on his knees in the middle of it staring at Astrid like they were the monster.

Maybe they were.

Dreven didn't run. Astrid would remember that too. He gathered himself slowly, one hand pressed to a gash on his brow, and he looked at the bodies of his men, and then at Astrid, and he said, quietly, in the voice of a man reciting scripture: "The King will want to know your face. So I'll leave it in his dreams instead of mine." Then he whistled, and somewhere in the dark one panicked horse answered, and he was gone into the snowfall, limping, leaving a thin thread of red behind him that the snow was already erasing.

Astrid let him go.

They stood in the wreck of the hollow with their ruined hand dripping and their breath coming in sobs they couldn't seem to stop, and they counted the bodies because someone had to, because counting was the last ordinary thing left in the world. Five. Five men dead in under a minute, and the killing had been so *easy*, so effortless, that the true horror arrived late and sideways: not that the guardian had slaughtered them, but that Astrid's blood had called it up as casually as striking a match, and that some part of Astrid—some part older and deeper than the smuggler, deeper than thirty careful years of invisibility—had *recognized* it. Had known its name before it spoke. Had felt, for one shameful instant as it waded through armored men like a scythe through wheat:

Satisfaction.

Astrid knelt and was sick into the snow.

* * *

They walked most of the night, because stopping meant sleeping and sleeping meant dreaming and they could not bear to find out what waited behind their eyelids now. The hand they bound with strips of shirt, tight and clumsy, one-handed, and the pain kept them anchored—good pain, honest pain, the pain of an ordinary body doing ordinary damage. Not the other thing. Never the other thing again.

By grey dawn they reached the cabin.

Halvard's cabin—no. Their cabin now, whatever Halvard had been to them, mentor and jailer and father and liar. Smoke curled from its chimney, thin and grey against the pines, and Astrid stopped dead at the treeline with their heart slamming.

No one knew this place. No one had known it in thirty years except Halvard, and Halvard was dead—had been dying when Astrid left Lowmark four days ago, coughing blood into a cloth in a rented room, gripping Astrid's wrist with surprising strength to gasp out his confession about sleeping oaths and bloodlines before the fever took him under.

Except the smoke was rising. And Halvard's fever had been the kind you don't get up from.

Astrid approached with the drawn sword—when had they drawn it?—and pushed open the door with their boot, and there in the chair by the hearth, wrapped in three blankets and looking like a man who had already died and was merely waiting for his body to be informed, sat Halvard Eriksen.

Alive. Impossible. Alive.

"You're bleeding," Halvard rasped. His eyes, sunken and yellowed, fixed on Astrid's bound hand with an expression that was not surprise at all. "On the waystone, weren't you. I felt it wake from here. Forty years since I felt one wake." He coughed, wet and long, and smiled at whatever he saw in Astrid's face. "You have questions, child. Sit down. We haven't much time, and I've lied to you for thirty years, and the truth takes longer than lying does."

Outside, the snow kept falling on the waystones, patient as memory.

Inside, Astrid sheathed the sword, pulled the door shut against the cold, and sat.
