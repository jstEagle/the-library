## 6. Sela's Bargain

Sela Voss had followed the smuggler's trail for nine days, and in all that time she had learned exactly three things about Astrid Hartwell.

First: they were better at this than anyone she had ever tracked. The trail did not so much vanish as *refuse to exist*—scree swept clean by wind that had no reason to blow, streams forded at points where the water ran shallow as if it had been asked politely. Twice she lost them entirely and found them again only because she had paid a herdsman in Lowmark to describe the way Hartwell walked, and even then it was less pursuit than faith.

Second: they were alone, and being alone was costing them. She saw it in the camps—cold fires built small and buried early, one bedroll, one cup, one set of everything. Thirty years of solitude pressed into the geometry of a campsite.

Third—and this was the thing that made her stop on the third morning, breath steaming, hand resting on the pommel of a sword she hadn't drawn in years—they were being *followed too*. Not by her. By something else.

She had found the sign at dawn: boot prints in old snow, six sets, moving in a spread pattern that no honest traveler used. Witch-finders worked in threes; this was two teams, or one team doubled. And they were a day behind her, closing.

Which meant her window was shrinking, and Sela Voss had never been a woman who wasted windows.

She stood on the shoulder of the mountain with the wind trying to peel her off it and looked up at Thornfell—the real Thornfell, above the treeline, where the peaks went white and blind and the passes threaded through like stitches in a wound. Somewhere up there was the last of the swordspeakers. Somewhere up there was the only person alive who could walk these mountains without dying, and the free cities' last hope of not burning.

And somewhere behind her were men whose trade was finding people like that and putting them out like candles.

"Right," she said to the wind, and climbed.

* * *

She caught them at dusk on the fourth day of her climb, at a place where the trail narrowed between two walls of granite and a frozen waterfall hung like a curtain of dirty glass. She didn't try to be quiet about it. Quiet would have been an insult, and worse—a provocation. Instead she stopped twenty paces back, let her boots crunch deliberately on the crust of snow, and said, "I'll want a fire tonight. My hands are half-frozen."

The figure ahead did not turn around. For a long moment there was only the wind and the creak of ice, and then Astrid Hartwell said, without inflection, "There's a cave half a mile on. You can freeze there instead."

"I'd rather freeze near company."

"Company costs money."

"So does everything." Sela started walking again, matching their pace, close enough to see the grey threading the dark hair at their temples, the scar that split one eyebrow, the way their right hand never strayed far from the long knife at their hip—not a swordspeaker's blade, she noticed. A smuggler's knife. Practical. Deniable. "You're Astrid Hartwell."

"I'm nobody."

"You're the third person I've said that name to in Lowmark, and the first two flinched when I said it. That makes you somebody." She kept her voice level, conversational, the voice of a woman discussing the price of oats. "Hartwell is an oath-name. Old oaths. Swordspeaker oaths."

Astrid stopped walking.

They turned slowly, and Sela got her first good look at their face in full light—or what light remained, the sky gone the color of pewter behind the eastern peaks. It was a weathered face, a face that had been handsome once and had decided long ago that beauty was a liability. The eyes were the striking part: pale grey-green, watchful, and profoundly tired in a way that had nothing to do with sleep.

"You have me confused with a dead family," Astrid said.

"Families don't die. They hide." Sela held up both hands, palms out, showing them empty—an old gesture, older than the free cities. "I'm not witch-finder. You've been watching my approach for three days; if I were, you'd have known by now, and I'd be dead. I'm Sela Voss. Envoy of the Concordance of Free Cities. Vaelport, Marrowgate, Cindral, and the rest of the proud, terrified, squabbling collection of merchant republics that King Veyric intends to swallow after he finishes burning his own kingdom clean."

"I know what the free cities are."

"I know you do. You carry their salt past his tollmen twice a season." She smiled thinly. "Yes, I asked about you in Lowmark. Yes, that was dangerous for both of us. No, I didn't have a better way."

Above them, somewhere high on the ridge, a bird called once—a thin, wild sound—and fell silent. Astrid's gaze flicked upward, tracking it, and Sela saw something move behind those tired eyes. Not fear exactly. Recognition. As if the mountain itself had spoken and they had understood it.

"The witch-finders," Astrid said quietly. "How many?"

"Six, when they left Lowmark. Possibly fewer now. Possibly more." She watched them absorb that. "They're not following me. They're following the same rumor I am—that someone with the old blood came down from Thornfell to sell salt, and that a dead child got up off a shrine-stone and walked while the whole town watched."

"That wasn't me."

"No," Sela agreed. "That was a mother praying. But Veyric doesn't care about mothers. He cares about names, and bloodlines, and anything that might be a door." She took a slow breath, and now she let the envoy fall away, let the other voice come through—the one she used rarely, the one that had cost her things she didn't name. "So here it is, plainly, because you strike me as a person who hates being handled. The free cities need a swordspeaker. There are perhaps four people alive who remember the rites, and three of them are lying in unmarked graves. If Veyric's purge reaches the coast—if he seals the passes and turns his witch-finders loose on our shrines the way he's turned them loose here—we will burn alone, city by city, and no one will come. Unless someone comes first. Unless someone stands up and says *the old oaths live*, and means it."

The silence stretched. Snow began to fall, fine and dry, catching in Astrid's hair.

"And you think that's me," Astrid said at last.

"I think you're the last Hartwell. I think your grandmother signed the Concordance charter with a swordspeaker's mark. I think the gods are waking up whether we like it or not, and when they do, someone will have to decide what humanity does about it—and I would very much prefer that someone be a person who has spent thirty years keeping their head down rather than a king who burns his own brother."

Something flickered across Astrid's face at that—fast, quickly buried, but Sela had spent twenty years reading faces across negotiating tables, and she caught it. Grief. Old, private grief, and under it a thread of something rawer.

"You knew about Halvard," Astrid said.

"I know a great many things. It's my one talent." She softened it with a shrug. "He trained you. He hid you. He died—recently, I think, or recently enough. And now you're up here alone, carrying whatever he left you, waiting for the world to forget you again." She shook her head. "It won't. That's the truth I've climbed a mountain to tell you. The world is done forgetting. The only question left is who finds you first—me, or Dreven."

At the name, Astrid's whole body changed. The tiredness didn't leave, but something gathered underneath it, coiled and ready, and Sela understood suddenly why the witch-finders traveled in teams of six.

"You know Captain Dreven," Astrid said.

"By reputation. He burned a shrine-keeper in Marrowgate two winters ago. An old man. They made the congregation watch." She met their eyes steadily. "I'm offering you protection, Hartwell. Vaelport walls, a place behind real stone, people who will stand between you and him. And purpose, if you ever want it. Purpose is optional. Staying alive isn't."

For a long moment Astrid said nothing. The snow thickened around them, softening the edges of the world, and Sela waited with the patience of a woman who had once sat through eleven hours of tariff negotiation without standing up.

Then Astrid laughed—one short, bitter note, breath smoking in the cold.

"Protection," they said. "From Vaelport. I've seen what happens to people the free cities decide to protect. They get statues. After."

"That's fair."

"And purpose." Astrid's voice dropped, and there was something almost gentle in it, which was somehow worse than anger. "Envoy. Look at me. I move salt and iron and occasionally people who need to disappear. I have spent thirty years being *no one*, and every year of it I chose, and every year of it was worth it, because the last time someone decided I had a purpose, everyone I loved ended up in the ground." They turned away, shouldering their pack. "No. Find another miracle."

"There isn't another one," Sela said to their back. "That's rather the point."

Astrid kept walking. Toward the cave, Sela realized. Half a mile on. They hadn't told her to leave.

She smiled, just slightly, and followed.

* * *

**Astrid**

The cave was dry, deep, and smelled of cold stone and old smoke—Astrid's own smoke, from a fire built here two seasons ago and never quite forgotten by the rock. They got a flame going with the efficiency of long habit, small and shielded, and tried very hard not to notice how much easier the trail had been today. How the scree had held firm under their boots. How the wind had died at exactly the moment they needed it to die.

Halvard's voice, in memory: *The land remembers its servants, child. That's not a blessing. That's a debt.*

They pushed the thought down with the rest of them.

The woman sat across the fire with her hands out to the heat, and Astrid studied her the way they studied everything now—with the particular attention of a person who had learned that the world's kindness always arrived with a bill attached. Sela Voss. Envoy. She wore travel-worn leathers over what had once been expensive clothes, and a sword she carried like something she remembered how to use but hadn't used in years. Her hands were scarred at the knuckles. Negotiator's hands, or soldier's. Both, maybe.

"You're still here," Astrid said.

"You haven't told me to leave."

"I'm deciding whether to bother."

"Mm." Sela warmed her fingers. "Take your time. I've slept in worse than this. There was a warehouse in Cindral with a rat problem I still have nightmares about."

Despite themselves—despite *everything*—Astrid felt the corner of their mouth twitch. They killed it immediately. Thirty years of practice at killing such things.

"Why do you care?" they asked. "Truly. Not the speech. The speech was for the council that sent you. Why does *Sela Voss* climb a frozen mountain for a stranger?"

The envoy was quiet for a moment, watching the fire.

"Because I was in Marrowgate," she said finally, "when they burned the shrine-keeper. I stood in that crowd with my commission papers in my pocket and did nothing, because doing nothing was my job." Her voice stayed level, but her hands had gone still. "There was a girl in the front row, maybe ten years old, screaming for her grandfather, and two hundred adults stood there and let it happen because we were all so busy being *reasonable*. Being pragmatic. Being survivors." She looked up, and her eyes caught the firelight. "I'm done surviving, Hartwell. It's a coward's word for losing slowly. So yes—I climbed a frozen mountain for a stranger. Because the stranger is the only person alive who might make the next burning impossible."

Astrid fed a stick to the fire and said nothing for a long time.

They wanted to hate her. It would have been simpler. But the words had landed somewhere true, and the girl in the crowd sounded horribly like Yara—no. Like no one. Like a child Astrid had never known, because knowing children was a thing they had given up along with everything else.

"I won't rally anyone," they said at last. "I won't stand on walls and raise the old banner. Whatever Halvard told your council, whatever the stories say—the oaths are ash. The order is ash. What's left is me, and I'm a smuggler with bad knees."

"All right."

Astrid blinked. "All right?"

"You won't rally anyone. Accepted." Sela leaned forward. "But you know these mountains better than any living soul, and I have to cross them to reach Vaelport before the pass closes, and there are six witch-finders behind us who would very much like to meet you. So here is my revised offer: guide me through Thornfell. That's all. No banners, no oaths, no purpose. Just a road."

"And your protection in exchange."

"My protection, my discretion, and—" she reached into her coat and produced a purse, setting it on the stone between them with a clink that was frankly obscene in its weight, "—two hundred crowns. Half now, half at the wall of Vaelport."

Astrid stared at the purse. Two hundred crowns was three years of salt runs. Two hundred crowns was a cabin with a new roof, stores for winter, a life bought back outright.

Two hundred crowns was also, they understood perfectly well, the hook inside the bait.

"And if I say no?"

"Then I'll pay you fifty to point me at the pass and wish me luck, and Dreven will find me somewhere on the ice, and he'll be polite about it for an hour before he isn't." She shrugged. "I'd rather not. I've grown attached to not."

Astrid looked at the fire. At the purse. At the woman who had named Dreven's methods without flinching and called survival a coward's word, which was either the most foolish thing Astrid had heard in thirty years or the most honest.

*Everyone I loved ended up in the ground.* They could hear their own voice saying it, and hear, beneath it, the lie it half was. Some of them hadn't died. Some of them had simply been left—left in villages, left in safehouses, left behind on purpose so that loving Astrid Hartwell wouldn't cost them anything. And it had cost them anyway, eventually, because everything did.

The mountain shifted outside the cave mouth. Not literally—but the wind changed, swinging round to the north, and Astrid felt it against their skin like a hand turning them. Favoring them. Pointing them somewhere.

Toward Vaelport, if they read it right.

*Damn you, Halvard,* they thought, without much heat. *Even dead, you're spending my money.*

"One condition," Astrid said.

Sela raised an eyebrow.

"We go the long way. Through the Hollow Shrine valley." They held up a hand before she could object. "Yes, it's ruined, yes, it's old-god ground, and yes, that's precisely why we're going. Witch-finders won't enter it. Their own superstitions will keep them out longer than any army could. We shelter there tomorrow night, and we're across the high pass before Dreven figures out where the road went."

Something moved behind Sela's eyes—quick, calculating, gone in an instant. Astrid catalogued it and filed it away. This woman was not merely an envoy. This woman was doing sums, and some of the numbers were people.

"Agreed," Sela said. "The long way."

"Good." Astrid kicked the purse back across the stone. "Half now, like you said. And Voss—one more thing."

"Name it."

"If anyone travels with us—if you've got soldiers stashed in a fold of the map, a second team, anyone—you tell me tonight. Because I will find out on the ice, and the mountain doesn't give second chances to liars." They met her eyes across the fire, and let her see, for just a moment, the thing under the smuggler's weariness: thirty years of the old training, banked but not cold. "I've buried people who lied to me about smaller things."

For the first time since the trail, Sela Voss looked genuinely uncertain. It lasted perhaps two seconds. Then she inclined her head, graceful as a duelist acknowledging a touch.

"Fair terms," she said. "No one else. Just me, my tongue, and my terrible gratitude."

Astrid nodded slowly and turned to bank the fire, and neither of them spoke for a while after that. The storm built outside, and the cave held its small warmth, and two survivors sat sizing each other up across the flames like the last two pieces on a board neither had chosen to play.

It was much later—deep in the night, when Sela's breathing had gone slow and even—that Astrid lay awake staring at the ceiling of stone and felt it again. That presence. Patient. Vast. Closer than it had been in Lowmark, closer than it had ever been, curled somewhere behind their ribs like a second heartbeat learning the rhythm.

*Guide her,* it seemed to say, in a voice that was not a voice. *Take the long road. Take her to the shrine.*

Astrid closed their eyes and told themselves it was the wind.

In the morning, the snow had erased every track behind them—including, somehow, Sela's—and the path ahead lay open and clear, leading east, toward the Hollow Shrine, exactly as something had wanted.

Astrid looked at it for a long time.

Then they picked up their pack and led the way.
