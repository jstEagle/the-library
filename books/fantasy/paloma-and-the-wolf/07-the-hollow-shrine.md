## 7. The Hollow Shrine

The storm found them an hour past dusk, rolling down out of the high passes like something with intent, and Astrid made the call they had been dreading all day: shelter.

"There's a ruin ahead," they said, raising their voice over the wind. "Old shrine. Roof's mostly intact."

Sela Voss pulled her hood back just far enough to look at them. Rain ran off the leather of her travel coat in bright threads. "A shrine to which god?"

"The dead ones." Astrid turned uphill before she could see their face. "Does it matter?"

Behind her, Tam clutched his satchel of stolen witch-finder records to his chest as though the papers might drown. Yara stumbled along in Brannic's wake, the girl's small hand fisted in the soldier's cloak, and Brannic—big, disgraced, gentle Brannic—walked with one arm out behind him like a mother hen who had once been a battering ram. They were four days out from Lowmark's ashes and none of them had spoken much since. Grief sat on the company like wet wool.

The path climbed through black pines whose branches combed the rain into long cold fingers. And then, without warning, the trees stopped.

Astrid knew the place even before they saw it, the way you know a scar under your own shirt. A ring of standing stones, each taller than two men, their surfaces worn smooth except where old carvings still showed: spirals, antlered figures, rivers of tiny stars. Beyond the stones, half-swallowed by the hillside itself, stood the temple—a low building of grey granite that looked less built than grown, its roofline sagging but whole, its doorway a tall dark mouth framed in carved wolves.

The Thornfell paths had been favoring them again. Astrid had told themselves for thirty years that this was luck, or skill, or simply that they knew these mountains better than anyone alive. But today the trail had bent east when it should have gone west, had carried them around a rockslide that would have cost them half a day, and had delivered them here—to the one roof within ten miles—with the efficiency of a hand dealing cards to a marked player.

*Weather,* Astrid told themselves. *Superstition.* The same words they'd been using for weeks now, wearing thinner every time.

"Inside," they said. "Everyone inside."

The temple's interior was one great vaulted room, dry and cold-smelling, lit faintly by the last grey light through a smoke-hole in the roof. Astrid's lantern found what they remembered finding the first time, twenty years ago, when they'd sheltered here half-dead and alone: a floor of polished stone inlaid with a vast mosaic—the wolf-god Ondrys rendered in a thousand tesserae of slate and bone-white quartz, lying curled around a mountain the way a dog curls around a hearth. His eyes were chips of black glass. In the lantern light they seemed to follow.

Yara stopped dead just inside the door.

"It's warm," the girl said.

"It's not." Astrid set the lantern down. "Stone holds the day's heat. That's all."

But Yara was crouching, pressing her palm flat against the mosaic, over the stone wolf's flank, and her face had gone strange and young and frightened. "It's warm *here*, Astrid. Right here. Like—" She snatched her hand back. "Like skin."

Brannic got a fire going in the old fire-pit, which drew Yara away at last, and Tam collapsed against a pillar and was asleep almost before he finished sitting down, the way only the truly exhausted can sleep. Sela walked the perimeter with a soldier's thoroughness, checking sightlines, testing walls, and Astrid took first watch because taking first watch meant not sleeping, and not sleeping meant not dreaming, and lately the dreams had been—

Lately the dreams had been full of a voice.

They sat with their back against a pillar, sword across their knees, and watched the fire, and tried very hard to think about nothing at all. Salt contracts. Grain prices in Lowmark before Lowmark burned. The weight of Halvard's body in their arms, three weeks ago now, lighter than it should have been. *The oaths never died,* he'd said. *They sleep in bloodlines. You carry something more.* And then he'd died with the rest of it locked behind his teeth, and Astrid had been carrying the sentence's missing half ever since, like a stone in the boot.

The fire settled. The storm hammered the smoke-hole. Somewhere in the walls, water found channels it had been finding for centuries.

"You're doing it wrong," said the voice.

Astrid did not move. Every muscle in their body went to iron, because the voice had come from *inside*—not from the doorway, not from the shadows behind the pillars, but from the warm wet dark of their own skull, the way a thought comes, except it was not their thought. It was low and unhurried and amused, the voice of someone watching a child struggle with a knot.

They counted to ten. They checked the room: Sela asleep near the fire, wrapped in her coat; Brannic snoring; Yara curled small; Tam motionless. No one awake. No one speaking.

"I'm dreaming," Astrid breathed. "I fell asleep on watch."

"You don't sleep on watch. You haven't slept properly in twenty years. I would know. I've been counting." A pause, and then, with terrible gentleness: "You're holding your sword like a farmer holds a scythe. Wrist straight. Elbow soft. You knew this once. Your hands remember even when you refuse to."

Astrid looked down at their own grip and hated themselves for adjusting it.

"What are you?" Their whisper barely stirred the air.

The silence that followed was long enough that Astrid almost convinced themselves they'd imagined it. Then, warm as breath on the back of the neck:

"You know my name. Your people carved it on every mountain from here to the sea. Say it."

"No."

"No," the voice repeated, tasting the word. "Thirty years of no. Do you know how that feels, little sword? To be carried in the blood of the last one who could speak to me, and be refused even a greeting?" Something shifted in the tone—not anger. Something worse. Patience. "Your mentor knew. He felt me wake in you when you were nine years old, the night you cut your palm on the waystone and didn't tell him. He spent his whole life after that teaching you to be deaf. Teaching you to be *small*. I do not blame him. He loved you. But love makes cowards of careful men."

"Halvard protected me from you."

"He protected you *for* me. There is a difference, and you will learn it." The voice softened further, went almost kind, and that kindness was the most frightening thing Astrid had ever heard. "I am not asking for anything tonight. Sleep if you can. Walk tomorrow. But know this, Astrid Hartwell: the road brought you here. Not luck. Not skill. The mountain knows its master's house, and it has been carrying you home for a very long time."

And then it was gone—not faded, not withdrawn, simply *absent*, the way a hand lifts from a shoulder, leaving the skin cold where it had been warm.

Astrid sat unmoving until the fire burned down to coals. Their hands shook. They pressed them flat against the cold floor until they stopped.

At some point Yara woke, padded silently across the mosaic, and sat down beside them without asking. She looked at the stone wolf, then at Astrid, then back at the wolf.

"He talks to you," she said. It wasn't a question.

"Go back to sleep, Yara."

"He's been talking to you for a while, hasn't he. I thought so. On the pass, when you fought—" She stopped herself. Her jaw tightened. "You should know something. When we prayed together in Lowmark, before it burned—all those people, all those voices going up at once—I could hear the gods answering. All of them. Except one." She turned to look at Astrid, and her eyes in the dying firelight were ancient and sorry. "One voice was already *here*. Down among us. Close enough to touch."

She went back to her blanket. Astrid did not sleep. Above them, the storm wore itself out against the mountains, and the black glass eyes of the mosaic wolf held the ember-light, patient as winter.

* * *

Sela Voss woke before dawn out of habit and lay still beneath her coat, listening to the temple breathe.

She had slept in worse places. Barracks in Vaelport during the flood year. A ditch outside Karsk with an arrowhead still in her shoulder. But she had never slept in a place that felt *watched*, and this one did—not the ordinary watchfulness of ruins, where wind through broken windows plays tricks, but something deliberate. Attentive. Twice in the night she had surfaced from shallow sleep with the absolute conviction that someone had just finished saying her name.

She rose without sound and walked the circuit of the room, cataloguing.

Tam, asleep sitting up, arms around his satchel like a man hugging a lifeboat. Guilty, terrified, useful—he'd given her three pages of Dreven's operational orders before Lowmark burned, and she had them memorized. Brannic, sprawled, snoring, his sword within reach even unconscious; a good soldier ruined by politics, worth more than the council that broke him. Yara, curled tight, brow furrowed even in sleep, murmuring something too soft to catch—and Sela had noticed, over four days, how often the girl's head turned toward Astrid, tracking, listening, as if Astrid broadcast on a frequency only she received.

And Astrid. Awake. Of course awake—had been awake when Sela closed her eyes and was awake now, seated against the pillar with the sword across their knees, staring into dead coals like a woman staring at her own grave.

Sela drifted toward the fire and crouched beside it, feeding it two sticks, buying herself a reason to be close. She studied Astrid sidelong while pretending to study the flames.

Here is what Sela Voss knew, and had known since the pass:

She knew the histories. Not the peasant version—the real ones, the ones the free-city archives kept under lock, purchased at obscene expense from monasteries that no longer existed. She knew that the swordspeaker orders had numbered nine hundred at their height, and that every one of them had carried a fragment of a god sealed in their blood, and that the fragments were what let them work the old battle-rites: the impossible speed, the reading of terrain like text, the calling of things that lived under waystones. She knew the order had been exterminated—or believed exterminated—in Veyric's grandfather's time. And she knew, because a dying archivist had sold her the knowledge along with a warning, that the Hartwell line had been oath-bound to Ondrys specifically, and that the binding did not die with the order. It slept. It waited in bloodlines like a seed in frozen ground.

And she had watched Astrid Hartwell, four days ago in the Pass of Teeth, fight eleven witch-finders in a space wide enough for three, moving like water poured downhill, wearing an expression Sela had seen exactly once before—on the face of a Vaelport duelist who had taken moonpoppy resin before a match and killed six men laughing.

Astrid had enjoyed it. Then Astrid had vomited behind a rock and refused to speak of it, and Tam—who had seen everything and said nothing, which was its own confession—had looked at Sela with eyes like a man watching a fuse burn.

So. Sela crouched at the fire and did arithmetic.

If Astrid was what Sela suspected, then the free cities' commission was worth more than the council dreamed. A living swordspeaker of Ondrys's line was not a guide and not a symbol. It was a strategic asset beyond price—a deterrent that could stand on Vaelport's walls and make even Veyric hesitate. Or, failing deterrence, a bargaining chip of the first magnitude: *surrender the vessel, spare the city.* The council had sent her to retrieve a legend. She might instead be delivering them a god.

Her commission was explicit. Alive, unharmed, delivered to Vaelport. What happened after delivery was the council's business, not hers. She had signed that distinction herself, in ink, and had told herself it meant she was a courier, not a butcher.

Except.

Except that Astrid had buried the dead of Lowmark in frozen ground with their own hands, digging until their knuckles split, because Yara couldn't and Tam wouldn't and Brannic's hands were too big for the small graves. Except that Astrid had given Tam their own bedroll the second night, claiming they preferred the cold, and Sela had seen the lie in it and said nothing. Except that Astrid had looked at Yara when the girl cried in her sleep and had moved, wordlessly, to sit between the child and the door.

People were easy to use precisely in proportion to how much you respected them. Sela had learned that lesson young and paid for it twice. She was not going to pretend she didn't know what she was doing.

The fire crackled. Across the room, Astrid finally spoke, without turning their head.

"You're calculating. I can hear it from here. Like coins in a purse."

Sela smiled thinly. "Occupational habit."

"What's the sum?"

"That depends on a question I haven't asked yet." She turned, deliberately casual, and met Astrid's eyes across the embers. "The shrine. You've been here before. You knew exactly where it was, and the storm came up convenient, and the path bent east when the map says west. I've been in these mountains four days and I can already tell the trails don't behave normally around you." She kept her voice level, curious, harmless. "The land favors you, Astrid. Old land does that for old blood. So my question is simple, and you may lie to me if you like, though I'd take it as an answer either way."

Astrid's face did not change. Thirty years of smuggling had taught them a magnificent stillness. But their hand had drifted, without their noticing, to rest flat against their own sternum—over the heart, over whatever slept there—and that small involuntary gesture told Sela more than any answer would have.

"Ask your question," Astrid said.

Sela held the moment, weighing it. Ask now, in the grey cold with the others waking, and get a lie or a blade. Wait, and let the thing in the dark of this temple answer for her—for surely, whatever watched this place, it was not watching *her*.

She chose patience. It was the harder discipline and the better trade.

"Later," she said, rising, brushing ash from her knees. "When we're out of this valley. Some questions keep better than others."

Astrid watched her a moment too long. "You're not the first person to look at me like that," they said quietly. "My mentor did. Near the end. Like he was deciding whether to tell me something that would ruin my life."

"And did he?"

"He died first." Astrid stood, rolled their bedroll with sharp economical motions, and slung it over one shoulder. "Get the others up. We move in an hour, ahead of the light. There's a witch-finder supply column on the north road by today, and I want a river between us and it."

They walked to the doorway and stood there, silhouetted against the pale snow-light, looking out at the standing stones. And Sela, watching, saw Astrid's shoulders stiffen—just slightly, just for a heartbeat—as though someone had whispered something in their ear.

Then Astrid stepped out into the morning, and the mountain mists parted before them down the eastern trail, opening a road as neat and inviting as a carpet unrolled.

Sela stood alone in the hollow shrine a moment longer, looking up at the mosaic wolf and its black glass eyes, and felt the temperature of the stone floor through her boots—warm, faintly, impossibly warm, like the flank of a living animal.

"Well," she murmured to the empty temple, to the god or the ghost or the hunger in the walls. "I suppose we're all traveling together, aren't we."

The warmth did not answer. But as she turned to wake the others, she could have sworn the black glass eyes had shifted—half an inch, no more—to follow Astrid out the door.
