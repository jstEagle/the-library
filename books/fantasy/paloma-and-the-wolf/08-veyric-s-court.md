## 8. Veyric's Court

The king of Mereth did not sleep. He had not slept properly in nine years—not since the night the sky over Callowmere turned the color of a healing wound and his brother Maerin had stood at the window of their childhood house with light coming out of his mouth.

Veyric remembered it the way other men remembered their wedding days: completely, uselessly, in every detail. The smell of tallow and scorched wool. The sound Maerin made—not screaming, though there was screaming later—singing. A low, sweet, inhuman singing that came up through the floorboards and out of his brother's chest as if Maerin's ribs were the strings of some vast instrument being played by hands no one could see. Their mother on her knees in the hallway, praying to the old gods to stop, to take it back, to give her son back. And the light in Maerin's eyes going out even as the light in his mouth grew brighter, until what knelt on the floor wearing his face was only a husk, a lamp burned down to nothing, and something else looked out from behind his teeth.

They had burned him at dawn. That was the law then, before Veyric rewrote it—the old law, the merciful one, which said a vessel was already dead and must be given to the fire clean. Veyric had carried the torch himself because no one else would meet his brother's eyes, and because Maerin, in whatever fragment of himself still remained beneath the god's possession, had asked him to with one trembling hand pressed flat against the woodpile.

Veyric was twenty-two years old. He had held the torch steady. He had not wept until the fire caught, and then only once, briefly, and he had never wept since.

That was the truth of him that no historian would ever write: that King Veyric the Purifier, the Usurper, the God-Breaker, had begun as a boy holding a torch for his brother, and had simply never put it down.

* * *

Now he sat in the high council chamber of Castle Mereth with the morning fog pressing gray against the windows, and listened to his ministers explain why the world was ending again.

"The reports are consistent across four provinces," said Lord Chancellor Hesse, a dry stick of a man who had served Veyric's father and had learned, over thirty years, to deliver terrible news in the tone of a grain inventory. "Healings at roadside shrines. Statues found weeping—or bleeding, depending on the witness. In two villages, entire congregations reported hearing voices during the winter solstice rites. And in Lowmark—" Hesse paused, consulting his papers though he plainly knew the passage by heart, "a child dead of lungfever restored to life before forty witnesses. The shrine involved was cracked, your Majesty. Cracked and unsanctified for a generation. Whatever answered that woman's prayer did so through rubble."

"Through rubble," Veyric repeated softly.

The chamber was warm. The fires were banked high; they always were now, because the king could not abide cold rooms, could not abide any reminder of that dawn in Callowmere when the smoke had smelled of his brother's hair. But Veyric felt cold anyway, the particular cold that had lived under his sternum for nine years, the cold that no fire reached.

"Superstition," said Marshal Corvane from the far end of the table. He was a broad man with a soldier's blunt face and the king's fullest trust. "Fever dreams and mass hysteria. One dead child revived by a mother's refusal to accept the obvious. It happens. I've seen it."

"You haven't," Veyric said. Not unkindly. "You've seen children who were never truly dead. This is different." He rose, and the chamber rose with him, chairs scraping back in deference that he had stopped noticing years ago. He walked to the window, where the fog lay over the capital like a shroud someone had forgotten to remove. "My father believed the gods died at the Sundering. His father believed it. We built a kingdom on that belief, gentlemen—a kingdom where men answer to men, where no prayer goes higher than the roof it's prayed under. Nine years ago I learned how much of our peace rests on that belief remaining true." He turned back to face them. "It is no longer true."

Hesse's quill paused above the page. Corvane frowned, but did not argue; Corvane had been at Callowmere too. Corvane had held the torch with him.

"The witch-finders report a hundred and six confirmed manifestations since midwinter," Veyric continued. "Confirmed. Vetted. Cross-examined under oath. A hundred and six, against eleven in all the years before. Whatever slept is waking, and it is waking faster." He let that settle over the table like frost. "Gentlemen, I want you to understand me clearly. I am not a zealot. I take no pleasure in what must be done. Every shrine we salt, every vessel we burn, is a grief to me personally, and I will carry those griefs to my grave alongside the first one." His voice did not waver. It never wavered; he had trained it out of itself the way one trains out a limp. "But I have seen what happens when a god takes root in a living soul. I have watched it hollow out someone I loved while he sang. And I will burn this kingdom to ash before I watch it happen to another mother's son. Do you hear me? Ash."

Silence. Outside, the fog muffled the bells of the morning market, so that the city sounded underwater, drowned, distant.

"Your Majesty," Hesse said carefully, "the free cities will call it tyranny. They already do."

"The free cities can call it whatever they like from behind their walls." Veyric returned to his chair and drew toward him the map that dominated the center of the council table—a master map of the realm, inked and re-inked, its mountains rendered in careful crosshatching. His finger came down on Thornfell, the great spine of peaks along the western border, all passes and shadowed valleys and roads that no royal surveyor had ever fully charted. "Here. The manifestations cluster around the old god-roads like flies around a wound. Thornfell is riddled with shrines older than my dynasty—older than any dynasty. And it swarms with smugglers, hermits, and worse: descendants of the oath-lines. Swordspeaker blood, gentlemen, sleeping in shepherd families for three hundred years, waiting."

"Waiting for what?" Corvane asked.

"For exactly this," Veyric said. "For the gods to need hands again."

* * *

The order went out by noon, sealed in black wax with the sun-and-sword sigil Veyric had designed himself at nineteen, when he still believed a symbol could hold back the dark if you made it sharp enough.

By the terms of the Edict of Sealing: all passage through the Thornfell passes forbidden, effective immediately, save for licensed military traffic. All shrines, waystones, and standing circles within the range declared forfeit, to be desecrated, salted, or demolished by witch-finder companies operating under writ. All persons bearing the surnames of the recorded oath-lines—Hartwell foremost among them—to be registered, questioned, and detained for examination. Any person found to be a vessel, or to harbor one, to be remanded to the pyre without trial, per the amended statute of Callowmere.

Veyric read the final draft twice before signing. He read it standing at the window of his private study, where he kept the only object in the castle he had chosen for himself rather than inherited: a small, cheap clay figure of a smiling fat god, Ondrys-of-the-Hearth, the kind farmers once set on their doorposts for luck. Maerin had bought it at a fair when they were boys, laughing, saying it looked like their uncle. It sat on the sill now, facing outward, watching the road.

"I'm sorry," Veyric told it, as he sometimes did. He was not sure whether he apologized to the god or to his brother, and after nine years he had stopped trying to distinguish. "But you should have stayed dead."

A knock. Captain Dreven entered without waiting for leave—which was permitted, for Dreven, and for perhaps three other people alive.

Dreven was not a large man. He had the unremarkable face of a clerk and the eyes of a man who had looked into too many vessels' faces searching for the thing behind them, and had learned to see everywhere. He had joined the witch-finders at their founding, nine years ago, and he had never once flinched from the work, and Veyric trusted him precisely because Dreven frightened everyone else.

"Your Majesty." Dreven bowed exactly the correct depth and no deeper. "The Thornfell writ. You'll want to know what we've found before it's sealed."

"Speak."

"There is a Hartwell living in the range. Or passing through it. Our informants in Lowmark describe a smuggler—salt, tin, contraband letters—who has worked the mountain paths for decades without once being caught by weather, banditry, or the Crown's patrols. Decades, Majesty. No ordinary luck survives thirty winters in Thornfell." Dreven's voice was flat as slate. "And the surname checks against the second register. Hartwell. Direct line. One of only four Hartwell households left in the records, and the only one unaccounted for."

Veyric's hand had gone still on the edict. He became aware of it and forced it smooth again. "One smuggler is not an army."

"No. But the oath-bond doesn't need armies, sire. It needs one bloodline and a god looking for a door." Dreven stepped closer to the map, uninvited, and laid one pale finger on the western ranges. "There's more. The waystones. My surveyors report that stones desecrated in autumn have been found upright again by spring. Re-set. Cleaned. Some with fresh offerings—bread, milk, cut flowers. Someone in those mountains is tending them. Someone remembers the rites."

For a moment the room was very quiet, and Veyric was twenty-two years old again, and somewhere below him a woodpile was catching, and his brother was singing.

"Then we find them," he said. His voice came out level. He was grateful for that, at least; he had spent nine years building this voice, plank by plank, over the singing. "Every Hartwell. Every keeper. Every hand that has touched a waystone in the last year. I want names, Captain. I want bloodlines mapped like battle lines. And I want this smuggler found—not killed. If the gods are reaching through the old lines, then the old lines are the only map we have, and I will interrogate that map myself."

"And if the smuggler resists?"

"Then resist them," Veyric said, "but bring me the body whole. I have burned enough vessels to know the difference between a corpse and evidence." He signed the edict then, the black wax already softening by the fire, and pressed his seal into it, and watched the sun-and-sword bite deep into the cooling red.

Dreven took the scroll and bowed. At the door he paused—a habit of his, that half-turn, as if he understood that kings needed to be watched leaving as much as arriving. "Sire. Forgive the question. But the men talk. They say the king hates the gods. I tell them no. I tell them the king loves his brother." His colorless eyes held Veyric's. "Am I wrong?"

Veyric looked past him, at the little clay god on the sill, smiling its painted smile at a kingdom it no longer owned.

"You are wrong," he said quietly. "I love them both. That's why neither can be allowed to win."

Dreven inclined his head, satisfied, and was gone.

Veyric stood alone in the study for a long time. Below, the orders were already moving—riders mounting, writs copied, the machinery of the purge engaging gear by gear with the smooth inevitability he had spent a decade perfecting. Somewhere in the west, fog was lifting off the shoulders of Thornfell, and a smuggler with an old name was walking roads that shifted like a living thing to favor them.

Seal the passes, he thought. Find the blood. Burn what wakes.

It was not cruelty. It had never been cruelty. It was a man with a torch, standing guard over a world of sleeping brothers, sworn that this time—this time—he would reach the fire before the singing started.

He blew out the candle beside the clay god, out of old habit, so that no flame would touch it, and went down to sign the war orders.
