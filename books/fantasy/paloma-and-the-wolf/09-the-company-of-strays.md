## 9. The Company of Strays

The mill at Graywater had been abandoned for a decade, and it showed. Its wheel had rotted to a black lattice, its sluice gates hung open like slack jaws, and the grain chutes were furred with moss that glowed faintly green in the last light of evening. But the walls stood thick and dry, and the loft above the grinding floor had a hearth whose chimney still drew, and in Astrid's long experience those three things made a palace.

They arrived at dusk with Sela Voss walking half a pace behind them—close enough to be company, far enough to make clear she was no one's follower—and Astrid spent the first ten minutes doing what they always did: walking the perimeter, checking sightlines, noting which floorboards would announce a stranger's weight and which window shutters could be barred from inside. Old habits. The habits of a person who expected to die alone and wanted, at least, to choose the terms.

"You count exits the way other people count coins," Sela observed from the doorway. She had shed her envoy's coat for a plain woolen one, and without the city colors she looked younger, and harder around the eyes. "I've watched you do it at every stop since the pass."

"It keeps me alive."

"And what do you do," she asked, "on the nights when nothing needs keeping alive?"

Astrid didn't answer. They knelt at the hearth instead, laying the fire with the economy of long practice: birchbark, then splinters, then the good split wood, arranged so the flame would climb rather than sprawl. The flint struck true on the third try. Firelight filled the loft, and for a moment—just a moment—the ruined mill looked almost like a home.

They had picked up the others over four days of hard travel through the foothills, and each of them had come to Astrid the way strays come to anyone who feeds them: sideways, cautiously, with an air of having decided it themselves.

Tam had been first. They'd found him huddled under a waystone shrine two days out of Lowmark, in witch-finder gray with the insignia torn off his sleeve but not yet scrubbed from his skin, where the dye of the office stained the wrists a faded violet. He was maybe twenty, thin as a lath, with ink-stained fingers and the haunted look of a man who had read too many lists. When Astrid drew a blade on him he hadn't run. He'd held up both hands and said, "I know where Dreven's companies are quartered. I copied the orders myself." And then, quieter, as if the words cost him something: "I burned the shrine registries before I left. All of them. Every village that still prayed."

Sela had wanted to bind him. Astrid had looked into his exhausted face and seen a boy who had chosen to be hunted by the only institution he'd ever belonged to, and had said only, "He walks with us. If he betrays us, I'll deal with it." Tam had flinched at *deal with it* the way a dog flinches at a raised hand, and had said thank you anyway.

Yara they had found—or rather, Yara had found *them*. She'd appeared on the road at dawn, seventeen years old, barefoot despite the frost, carrying a bundle that clinked: her mother's shrine-bells, wrapped in cloth because she couldn't bear the sound of them anymore. Her father had kept the wayside shrine at Millbrook for thirty years, sweeping the stones, tending the little oil lamps, and when the witch-finders came he had stood in front of his shrine with a broom in his hands. They had branded him for it. He was dead now; Yara did not say so directly, but she wore mourning knots in her hair, three of them, and she touched them when she thought no one was watching.

"I hear them," she'd told Astrid on the first night, without preamble, the way children say alarming things. "The gods. Not all of them. Just—" she'd frowned, searching for the shape of it, "—like hearing voices through a wall. You can tell someone's home even if you can't make out the words. Except lately the wall's getting thinner." She'd looked at Astrid then with those unsettlingly direct eyes. "It's thinnest around you. Around you it's not a wall at all. It's more like... a door."

Astrid had said nothing. Inside their skull, in the place where Ondrys lived coiled in their blood, something had shifted, slow and pleased, like a great animal turning over in its sleep. Astrid had pressed it down and changed the subject.

Brannic had simply fallen in. A big man gone soft at the middle, with a soldier's rolling gait and a free-city tattoo half-scraped from his forearm—he'd done it himself, badly, with a knife and river sand, and the scar tissue still wept in cold weather. Twenty-two years in the Vaelport third cohort, and then one bad order, one massacre at a village called Hessel that he'd refused to carry out, and a dishonor that followed him like a smell. He'd been drinking himself to death in a border town when the purge reached it, and he'd killed two witch-finders with a wagon axle before he fully understood he was fighting, and after that there was nowhere left to go except wherever Astrid was going.

"Where are we going?" Brannic had asked, on the second night, around a mouthful of hardtack. "Not complaining. Just asking. A man likes to know what he's dying toward."

"North," Astrid had said. "Through Thornfell. Away from the roads."

"That's not a destination, that's a direction."

"It's the only one I have."

And Brannic had laughed—a real laugh, deep and unembarrassed, the first any of them had heard from him—and said, "Well. I've marched toward worse."

* * *

Now the fire crackled, and the company assembled itself around it the way iron filings assemble around a lodestone: Tam nearest the warmth, curled small, already half-asleep with his satchel of salvaged papers hugged to his chest like a child's doll; Yara cross-legged and upright, stringing one of her mother's bells onto a cord with fierce concentration; Brannic propped against the wall sharpening a short sword with long, meditative strokes; Sela sitting slightly apart, as she always did, mending a tear in her sleeve with tiny precise stitches, watching everyone.

And Astrid sat among them, and did not know what to do with their hands.

Thirty years alone teaches you a hundred small competencies and starves every other muscle. Astrid knew how to set snares, read weather, walk a ridge in fog, sleep in shifts, lie to soldiers, bleed quietly. They did not know how to sit in a circle of people who had, without discussion or ceremony, begun arranging themselves around them—as if Astrid were the fire itself, and not merely the one who'd lit it.

Yara finished her bell-cord and, without asking, leaned over and tied it to the strap of Astrid's pack. "So you can find your way back to camp," she said, "if the mountain tries to move the paths again."

"The mountain doesn't—"

"It does. I've felt it doing it. It's fond of you." She said it plainly, the way she said everything, and went back to her bells.

Brannic snorted. "Fond of them. The mountain's fond of them, the shrines light up for them, the witch-finders can't catch them. What aren't you telling us, Hartwell?"

"Brannic." Sela's voice, quiet and edged.

"No, I'm asking honest. We're all running from something, fine, that's the price of admission. But I've soldiered twenty years and I know when a camp has a center of gravity, and it's not me, and it's not the envoy, and it's not the girl with the bells." He pointed the whetstone at Astrid. "It's you. And a man wants to know what he's anchored to."

The silence stretched. In Astrid's blood, Ondrys stirred—not speaking, never quite speaking, just turning that vast patient attention toward the moment like a lens focusing. *Tell them,* the silence seemed to say. *Or don't. Either way, I am here.*

"I don't know what I am," Astrid said finally. It came out rougher than intended. "That's the truth. My mentor died with the answer in his mouth. Everything I've done for thirty years, I did to stay small enough that the question never mattered." They looked at the fire. "If that's not good enough for you, the north road is back the way we came."

Nobody moved. Then Brannic grunted, went back to his sword, and said, "Good enough. I once served under a captain who didn't know what he was either. Best officer I ever had." A pause. "Worst gambler. Lost his boots twice."

Tam opened one eye. "Did you steal them back?"

"We stole them back together. That's how you know a unit's real—when you'll commit crimes for each other's boots."

Yara giggled, a bright startled sound, and even Sela's mouth curved before she mastered it. And Astrid sat in the middle of it, feeling something in their chest loosen by a single degree—an angle so small no one else could have measured it, but Astrid had spent thirty years mapping their own defenses, and they knew a gate cracking open when they felt one.

* * *

Later, when the others slept, Astrid took the first watch by the broken window, and Sela came to stand beside them. Neither spoke for a while. Below, the black water turned the ruined wheel, slow and patient, grinding nothing.

"You almost belong here," Sela said at last. "With them. I hope you know that."

"Almost is a careful word."

"I'm a careful woman." She pulled her mended sleeve straight. "In Vaelport I sat across from men who could order a thousand deaths before breakfast, and I learned to hear what people mean underneath what they say. So let me say what I think you're afraid to say. You've spent thirty years being invisible, and it worked, and it cost you everything, and now these four strangers have known you nine days and they've already rearranged themselves around you like you're the hearth. And some part of you wants to burn down the world before you give that up, and another part of you is terrified that the world will burn down anyway, because of what you're carrying." She glanced at them. "Am I close?"

Astrid's jaw tightened. "You should have been a witch-finder. You have the knack for finding things people hide."

"I've been threatened by better." But she said it lightly, and her eyes stayed kind. "I'm not your enemy, Astrid Hartwell. Whatever my council sent me to do—and it isn't only what I told you—I have watched you for nine days now, and I would follow you off a cliff before I'd follow most of the men who sign my pay." She turned back to the window. "Get some sleep. I'll take second watch."

She walked away before Astrid could decide whether to be grateful or alarmed, which Astrid suspected was exactly the effect she intended.

Alone at the window, Astrid let their shoulders drop. The valley below lay silvered with frost-moon, and somewhere out in that darkness the old paths wound through Thornfell, shifting by inches each night, easing around obstacles, bending toward the travelers who walked them—toward Astrid, Yara had said, because the mountain was fond of them. Fond. As if the land remembered something Astrid had spent a lifetime forgetting.

*I could keep them safe,* whispered the thing in their blood, soft as snowfall. Not Ondrys's voice, not yet, not in words—but the shape of the thought, pressing gently against the inside of Astrid's mind like a palm against a door. *All of them. The scribe. The bell-girl. The broken soldier. The clever envoy. One word from you, and nothing in this world could touch them.*

Astrid closed their eyes. "No," they murmured, so quietly it barely stirred the air.

But the door did not close. It never closed anymore. And far to the south, where the roads ran straight and the bonfires of the purge burned nightly, a rider was already spurring north with sealed orders and a name written on them in fresh ink:

*Hartwell.*

Behind him, in the witch-finder archives, Captain Dreven stood over a scorched fragment of registry paper—the corner of a page Tam had failed to burn—and smiled at the single surviving word, and began to plan.
