## 10. Yara Hears Everything

The fire had burned down to a red heart by the time Yara came to sit beside Astrid, and the others had gone to their bedrolls—Tam curled tight as a fist, Brannic snoring with the untroubled rhythm of a man who had made his peace with bad decisions, Sela still awake somewhere in the dark, her breathing too even, too deliberate. Astrid knew that breathing. It was the breathing of someone listening.

Yara did not ask permission to sit. She simply folded herself down onto the cold stone beside Astrid's pack, knees drawn up, hands wrapped around a cup of tea she had let go cold hours ago. She was seventeen, maybe eighteen, with her mother's dark braids and her father's stubborn jaw, and she had the particular stillness of people who spent their lives being told things they hadn't asked to hear.

"You're not sleeping," Astrid said.

"Neither are you."

"I don't sleep much. Old habit." Astrid turned the whetstone along their knife's edge, slow and even, more ritual than necessity. The blade was already sharp. "You should try. Tomorrow we cross the scree fields, and they're cruel on tired feet."

"Astrid." Yara said their name carefully, the way you'd set down something fragile on uneven ground. "I need to tell you something, and I need you not to sharpen your knife while I say it."

Astrid looked at her. In the firelight her face was half gold and half shadow, and her eyes held that quality Astrid had been trying not to notice for days—the quality of someone looking *through* them at something standing just behind.

"The shrine-keeper's daughter," Astrid said lightly, "has an opinion about my sleeping habits."

"It's not about your sleeping habits." Yara set the cold cup down. Her hands were steady, but only just. "It's about what talks to you."

The whetstone stopped moving.

For three nights now, since the hollow shrine with its fallen god-face and its air like held breath, Astrid had been careful. Careful with their thoughts, careful with their silences, careful never to answer aloud when the whisper came—that vast, patient voice coiled somewhere behind their sternum, speaking in a language older than any kingdom, saying things like *soon* and *mine* and, once, terribly gently, *little sword*. They had told no one. They had barely admitted it to themselves. The whisper was a wound they kept covered, and wounds covered fester, and some part of Astrid had known that too.

"What do you mean," Astrid said slowly, "*talks* to me."

Yara took a breath. "I hear the gods. You know this. It's why Veyric's witch-finders burned our shrine, why my mother sent me east with strangers instead of letting me stay and die for it. I've heard them since I was six years old—the old ones, the quiet ones, the ones everyone says are dead. Most of what I hear is like wind through a broken house. Echoes. Grief. But—" She stopped. Started again. "Since we left the shrine, I've heard one of them very clearly. Constantly. And it isn't coming from the sky, Astrid. It isn't coming from the stones. It's coming from *you*."

The night went very quiet around them. Even the fire seemed to lean in.

"That's not possible," Astrid said.

"He speaks all the time," Yara went on, as if she hadn't heard—or as if she had heard this denial before, from inside Astrid's own head, and recognized its shape. "Not words, mostly. Not like people talk. More like... roots growing under snow. Pressure. Direction. He thinks in long slow thoughts, and every one of them passes through you like water through cloth. I can hear where he ends and you begin, because there isn't a clean line anymore. There used to be. When we met you, there was a line. Now it's blurring."

"Stop." Astrid's voice came out flat and hard, a smuggler's voice, a border voice, the voice they used on bandits and toll-keepers and anyone who got too close to the truth of them. "Whatever you think you're hearing, it isn't—"

"He calls you *little sword*."

The world tilted. Astrid felt it tilt—not the ground, not the mountain, but something interior, some floorboard of the self giving way. No one had heard that. No one could have heard that. Astrid had never spoken it aloud, had barely allowed it to exist as language even in the privacy of their own skull, because naming it would make it real, and it was already too real, it had been too real since Halvard died with the truth half-out of his mouth—

"How do you know that?" Astrid whispered.

"Because he says it constantly. He says it the way a farmer talks to his best field. Affectionate. Possessive." Yara finally turned to look at them fully, and her young face was full of something worse than fear: pity. "Astrid, I don't think he's haunting you. I don't think he's a ghost rattling around in your head. Ghosts fade. He's doing the opposite. He's *growing*. Your body is soil to him. Every day since the shrine, I can hear him a little louder, a little closer to the surface. He's using you to come back into the world. And I don't think you have much time before the question of who's wearing whom stops having an answer."

Astrid stood up. Their legs did it without consulting them, some old animal instinct demanding distance, motion, escape—and then they caught themselves, forced themselves still, because running was what they'd done for thirty years and it had never once saved them from anything that mattered.

"No," they said. "No. Listen to me. I am fifty-one years old. I have carried whatever this is since before you were born, and it has never once moved my hand or spoken through my mouth. Halvard taught me the disciplines. Containment. I have spent three decades being nothing but a woman with a knife and a mule and salt to sell, and if you think I'm going to stand here and be told I'm a *garden*—"

"I'm not telling you what you are," Yara said quietly. "I'm telling you what I hear. That's all I can do. My mother always said the hearing isn't a gift, it's a chore, and the chore is only to report truly. What people do with the report is theirs." She rose too, small and straight-backed in the firelight. "But there's one more thing. And I want you to promise me you'll test it instead of shouting at me about it."

"Test what?"

"Your reflection."

Astrid laughed—a short, ugly sound. "My reflection."

"In still water. Or polished steel. Look at yourself, and watch closely. Watch the moment between seeing and moving." Yara picked up her cold tea and hugged it to her chest like a talisman. "I noticed it two days ago at the stream. I didn't want to believe it either. But I've seen vessels before, Astrid. I grew up in a shrine. I've read the old accounts of what possession looks like in its early stages, and there's always a sign like this one. A lag. The body answering to two wills, and one of them slightly slower than the other."

"One of them slower," Astrid repeated. "Which one?"

"That's the question, isn't it." Yara's eyes were very dark and very sorry. "That's exactly the question."

She left then, curling into her blanket near Tam with the practiced economy of someone raised sleeping in sacred buildings that might be raided at any hour. Astrid remained standing by the dead fire, alone with the mountains and the stars and the thing behind their sternum, which said nothing at all—which had said nothing all evening, which had been silent as a held breath ever since Yara began to speak, and that silence was somehow the most frightening part. If Ondrys were innocent, wouldn't he protest? Wouldn't the great hungry god roar his denial?

Instead: silence. The silence of a farmer watching two visitors argue over the ownership of his field.

*Little sword,* Astrid thought, deliberately, testing. *Are you there?*

Nothing. Or rather—not nothing. Something *attended*. Something listened with the whole weight of an ocean pretending to be a puddle.

Astrid did not sleep. That was no surprise; sleep had been a sometime ally for decades. But tonight they sat awake with their back against a boulder and their knife across their knees, watching the stars wheel, and around the coldest hour before dawn they found themselves doing the thing they had sworn not to do.

There was a stream thirty paces below the camp. Astrid knew it well—they'd filled the waterskins there at dusk. It ran shallow over pale stone, and in the slack pools between the rocks the water went glass-still, mirror-perfect, holding the stars the way a cupped hand holds rain.

They went down to it.

The mountains at that hour were a country of grey and silver, frost on the scree, breath hanging in the air like small ghosts. Astrid knelt at the edge of the widest pool and made themselves look. This was the discipline, Halvard had always said: *look directly at the thing you fear, because fear feeds on corners.* Look directly. So Astrid looked.

A face looked back. Weathered, wind-burned, grey-streaked hair escaping its braid, the deep lines of a life spent squinting into mountain weather. Fifty-one years old. Smuggler. Liar. Last of the swordspeakers, though they'd have cut out their tongue before admitting it to anyone but the dark. An ordinary face. A tired face. *Their* face.

"See?" Astrid murmured to the water, to Yara's memory, to the silent god. "Just me."

And then they lifted their right hand—an experiment, a small foolish experiment, raising it slowly while watching the reflection do the same—and the reflection hesitated.

Half a second. Perhaps less. Perhaps more; time goes strange when terror takes the clock from the wall. The reflected hand hung motionless above the reflected water while Astrid's real hand rose, and in that gap—in that impossible sliver of desynchronization—Astrid saw their own face do something their face had not done. The mouth of the reflection curved. Not a smile, exactly. Not a sneer. Something older and more patient than either: the expression of a host watching guests arrive, of soil feeling the first warmth of spring, of a door opening onto a room long shut.

Then the reflection caught up. Hand, face, everything snapping back into accord so smoothly that Astrid might have called it imagination, might have called it exhaustion, might have knelt there until dawn constructing reasonable explanations—if the reflection's eyes, in the last instant before it became merely their own again, had not looked at them with recognition. With *pleasure*.

Astrid scrambled back from the pool. Their heel skidded on wet stone; they caught themselves hard on both palms, heart slamming, breath tearing in and out of the thin cold air. The water settled. The stars resumed their places. The face below was only a face again, frightened and ordinary and entirely synchronized, and somehow that was worse, because now Astrid knew what lived underneath the synchronization, waiting for its half-second of freedom.

*No,* they thought, pressing their palms against the frozen ground as if the mountain itself could anchor them. *I am Astrid Hartwell. I am the daughter of no one and the student of Halvard and I carry salt and lies and nothing else. I am fifty-one years old and I have survived Veyric's father and Veyric himself and thirty winters of Thornfell and I am MINE—*

*Ours,* said something vast and gentle at the base of their skull, so softly it might have been their own thought, so warmly it could not possibly be. *Little sword. Ours.*

Astrid stayed kneeling until the sky went grey, until the birds started up in the pines, until the camp above began to stir—Sela's deliberate breathing breaking off, Brannic's snores ceasing, the small sounds of a company waking. Then they rose, washed their face in the pool without looking at it, and climbed back up to the fire, where Yara was already awake, watching them with those dark, sorrowful, knowing eyes.

"Well?" she asked softly.

Astrid opened their mouth to deny it. To laugh. To build the wall back up, brick by brick, the way they'd built it for thirty years.

What came out instead was: "How long do I have?"

Yara's face crumpled with something that was grief and relief at once. "I don't know. Days, maybe. Weeks if you fight him. But Astrid—" she reached out and gripped their wrist, her small hand fierce around the old scars, "—there's something else. Something I heard at the edge of his thoughts, just before dawn. He wasn't thinking about you. He was thinking about *Veyric*. About armies. About a reckoning he's been planning for three hundred years." Her grip tightened. "He knows where we're going. He knows about the free cities. And I think—I think he means to arrive."

Behind them, unnoticed, Tam sat up slowly in his bedroll, his scribe's eyes wide in the pre-dawn grey, his hand pressed flat against his chest where the witch-finder's seal used to hang—and said nothing, absolutely nothing, as he reached for his boots and began, very quietly, to pack.
