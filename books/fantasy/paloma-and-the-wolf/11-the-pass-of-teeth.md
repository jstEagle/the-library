## 11. The Pass of Teeth

The pass had earned its name honestly.

Astrid remembered it from their smuggling years—a throat of gray stone where the Thornfell range pinched itself narrow enough that two loaded mules could barely pass abreast. The walls rose sheer on either side, streaked with old ice that never melted even in high summer, and the wind funneled through with a sound like a giant breathing through its teeth. Caravans paid extra for guides willing to take the Pass of Teeth. Most took the longer road around, through country that was merely dangerous instead of murderous.

Astrid had always liked it here. There was a certain honesty to a place that could kill you plainly, without pretense.

They didn't like it today.

"Too quiet," they said, and stopped walking.

Ahead, Yara turned, her breath pluming white. "It's a mountain pass. It's supposed to be quiet."

"Not this quiet." Astrid held up a hand and listened. No choughs wheeling above the crags. No pikas whistling among the scree. Even the wind seemed to be holding its breath, as if the mountain itself were listening for something. The hair on their forearms stood up beneath their sleeves—not from cold. From something older than cold.

Brannic shifted the pack on his shoulders and glanced back down the trail. "Dreven?"

"Dreven's a week behind us at least," Sela said. But her hand had already found the hilt of her sword, and she was scanning the ridgelines with the flat, hungry attention of a woman who had survived ambushes before and intended to survive another. "Unless he found a faster way."

"He found one," Tam said quietly.

Everyone looked at him. The scribe stood near the rear of their little column, his ink-stained fingers wrapped around the strap of his satchel, his face carefully blank in the way Astrid had learned—over weeks of hard travel and shared fires—to read as not-blank-at-all. Tam knew things. He had been a witch-finder's man; he knew how Dreven thought, how the Order moved, what resources they could call upon. And lately he had been saying less and less, which meant he was thinking more and more.

"How?" Astrid asked.

"The old courier roads." Tam nodded at the cliff wall to their left, where a seam of darker stone ran upward like a scar. "The Order kept them mapped. They were built for exactly this—for moving fast through country everyone else thought was impassable. If Dreven pushed his riders hard, he could have cut ahead of us by two days." He paused. "He'd want to take us in the narrows. It's doctrine. *Confine the quarry, collapse the flanks, close the mouth.*"

"You could have mentioned this an hour ago," Brannic said.

"I'm mentioning it now."

Yara had gone pale. She wasn't looking at the cliffs or the trail. She was looking at Astrid—or rather, slightly to the left of Astrid, at the empty air beside them, the way she sometimes did when she was listening to something no one else could hear. Her lips parted. Closed again.

"What," Astrid said. Not a question so much as a request for mercy.

"Nothing." Yara's voice came out thin. "It's nothing. We should keep moving. Fast."

"That's not nothing."

"Astrid—" She stopped herself, jaw tightening, and for once Astrid let it go, because the alternative was standing in a killing ground arguing about gods while witch-finders closed the trap, and thirty years of survival had taught them which arguments could wait.

"Move," Astrid said. "Single file. Brannic, you're our rearguard. Sela, point. Nobody stops for anything until we're through the narrows. Yara, stay at my shoulder."

They moved. The pass swallowed them.

* * *

The walls closed in, and the light went gray and strange, filtered through frost-ferns that grew in the crevices overhead. Their boots crunched on old scree. Somewhere ahead, water dripped in slow rhythm, each drop striking stone with a sound like a counting heart.

Astrid counted the drops. It was an old habit, a smuggler's habit—the drip became a metronome, and against the metronome they measured everything else: the scrape of Brannic's armor at the rear, the soft double-breathing of the mules, Sela's footsteps ahead, quick and economical.

Forty-one. Forty-two. Forty-three.

The dripping stopped.

Astrid froze mid-stride. Ahead, Sela had frozen too, her whole body gone rigid as a drawn bow. In the sudden silence Astrid heard it—the thing every animal in the mountains had already fled from. Leather. Steel settling against steel. The particular hush of many men trying very hard to be still.

"*Down!*" they shouted, and the world exploded.

Crossbow bolts hissed out of the rocks above like a flight of angry hornets. One buried itself in the pack of the lead mule; the animal screamed and went down, spilling salt across the stones. Another sparked off the cliff face inches from Yara's cheek. A third took Brannic high in the shoulder, spinning him half around with a grunt—but the disgraced soldier had been shot before, and he dropped behind the fallen mule with the practiced ease of a man returning to an old bad home.

"Flanks!" Sela roared, already moving, her sword clearing its sheath in a bright arc. "They're coming down both sides!"

She was right. Figures were rappelling down the cliff walls on knotted cords, witch-finders in their slate-gray cloaks, iron masks glinting dully in the gray light. More poured from the mouth of the pass behind them—Astrid twisted, saw the way they'd come sealed by a shield-wall of pikes, six men deep, and beyond them the banners of the Order snapping in the funnel-wind. And somewhere in that press of men, Captain Dreven, patient as a spider, watching his doctrine unfold exactly as written.

*Confine the quarry. Collapse the flanks. Close the mouth.*

They had walked into it with their eyes open. Astrid felt a flash of cold fury at themselves—at Tam, at the whole rotten arithmetic of being hunted—and then there was no time for feeling anything at all, because the first witch-finder hit the ground three paces away and came up swinging.

Astrid killed him.

That part was ordinary. Thirty years of mountain roads taught a person how to kill economically, without flourish, the blade going in under the arm where the mail gapped, a twist, a shove, the body used as a shield against the next attacker. What happened after was not ordinary.

Time changed.

It did not slow, exactly—that would have been too simple, too kind. Rather, Astrid found that they were suddenly living in the spaces between moments, in the gaps where decisions lived before bodies made them. They saw the second witch-finder's feint coming three heartbeats before his shoulders lied about it. They saw the crossbowman on the ledge above drawing down on Sela's unprotected back, and they were already moving, already turning, their sword sweeping up in a line that caught the bolt and snapped it and carried on into the man's thigh in a single unbroken gesture that no human wrist should have managed.

And underneath all of it, humming through their blood like a struck bell, was a voice that was not a voice:

*Yes.*

"No," Astrid gasped—but their feet kept moving, their blade kept singing, and the joy rising in their chest was not entirely their own and was not entirely containable either.

*This is what you were made for. This is what your line bled for, generation upon generation, the oath written into the marrow. You have spent thirty years pretending to be a merchant. Look at yourself. LOOK.*

A witch-finder came at them with a flail, screaming some psalm of purification through his iron mask. Astrid stepped inside the swing—inside it, impossibly inside it, into the space where the weapon could not live—and opened his throat, and laughed.

They heard themselves laugh. That was the worst of it, later. That they heard it, bright and savage and delighted, ringing off the walls of the Pass of Teeth, and that some distant chamber of their mind stood apart and observed with horror: *that is not my laugh.*

But the rest of them—the part holding the sword, the part dancing between blades—the rest of them was soaring.

* * *

Sela fought like a free-city duelist, all economy and angles, giving ground only when ground was cheaper than blood. Astrid fought like nothing she had ever seen. Later, when she told the story in Vaelport's taverns—and she would tell it, though she would leave out the parts that mattered most—she would say that Astrid Hartwell moved through the ambush like weather, like floodwater finding every gap in a dike, and that the witch-finders who had trained their whole lives to hunt creatures like this died confused, because their doctrine assumed the vessel would resist.

Astrid did not resist. That was the secret Astrid would carry out of the pass like a stone in the boot.

Because there was a moment—clear, crystalline, unforgivable—when a witch-finder's blade slipped past their guard and opened a burning line along their ribs, and instead of fear Astrid felt *gratitude*. Gratitude for the pain, because pain was proof, proof that they were alive in a way they had not been alive in thirty years, alive in the old way, the red way, the way their bloodline had been bred for across centuries of oaths and wars. The wound sang. Every kill sang. The pass filled with the music of it, and Astrid's body knew every note before it was played.

*Let me,* the voice murmured, tender as a lover adjusting a collar. *You are doing all the work, little sword, and I am so proud of you, but you are tired, I can feel it, let me carry some of it—*

"Don't," Astrid whispered, parrying, killing, laughing. "Don't you dare."

*I only want to help.*

"I know." And that was the horror under the horror—that the voice sounded kind, that it sounded like the mentor they'd lost, like the mother they barely remembered, like every warm thing that had ever offered to carry a burden. "I know you do. That's why I'll never say yes."

The battle broke the way storms break. The witch-finders had come expecting quarry, and quarry that fought back with the skill of a legend was not in the doctrine. When Astrid cut the last cord and sent a cliffside of balanced scree thundering down onto the eastern flank, the survivors on that side broke and ran. At the mouth of the pass, the shield-wall wavered—Brannic, bleeding but upright, had somehow gotten behind it and set fire to their supply cart, and burning pitch does terrible things to formation discipline—and then Sela's voice rang off the stone, promising death to the first ten men and mercy to the rest, and the rest decided they preferred the rest.

In four minutes—Astrid counted, because counting was the rope they clung to—it was over.

The pass was silent again. Truly silent, except for the moaning of wounded men and the crackle of the burning cart and the wind resuming its long breath through the teeth of the mountain.

Astrid stood in the middle of it, chest heaving, sword dripping, surrounded by eleven dead witch-finders, and realized they were smiling.

They wiped the smile off their face with the back of their hand, hard, like smearing away filth.

* * *

Afterward there was the grim arithmetic. The lead mule dead. Salt scattered—half their season's profit glittering uselessly on bloody stone. Brannic's shoulder bolted shut with needle and gut-thread while he cursed in three languages and refused the whiskey twice and accepted it the third time. Yara moving among the dying witch-finders with her healer's kit, because she was Yara, because grief and mercy lived in her side by side, and Astrid could not watch it and could not look away.

And Tam.

Tam stood at the edge of the carnage, satchel clutched to his chest, and he was looking at Astrid with an expression Astrid couldn't read—or could read too well. Not fear. Worse than fear. Recognition. The look of a man who had transcribed accounts of the swordspeakers in the Order's forbidden archives, who had copied out descriptions of what the oath-bound could do when the old blood woke, and who had told himself those accounts were embellishment, myth, the exaggerations of frightened priests.

He was not telling himself that anymore.

Their eyes met across the bodies. Tam's mouth opened slightly. Whatever he was going to say, he swallowed it. He looked down at the snow, at the churned red slush, and said nothing at all.

Astrid wanted to demand it—*say it, say what you saw, say what I am*—and wanted equally to never hear it spoken aloud, because words made things real, and there were still miles of mountain between them and anywhere safe.

They said nothing too.

* * *

They camped that night in a hollow below the pass's southern lip, a smuggler's cache Astrid had used a dozen times, ringed by stone walls tall enough to hide firelight. Sela doubled the watches. Nobody suggested otherwise.

Astrid sat apart, cleaning their sword with a steadiness they had to manufacture piece by piece, stitch by stitch, like darning a torn coat. The wound along their ribs throbbed. Around the wound, faintly, the skin had gone cold and numb in a pattern too regular to be natural—a shape like a feather, like a wing, pressed into their flesh from the inside.

Ondrys said nothing. That was almost worse than the whispering. The god had gotten what he wanted tonight—a taste, a demonstration, a seed of memory planted in Astrid's body that would grow toward wanting—and now he could afford patience. Astrid could feel him waiting, vast and courteous, the way a spider waits at the center of a web it has not yet needed to repair.

*Astrid.* Yara settled beside them, knees drawn up, her young face silvered by the small fire. She didn't look at them. "You enjoyed it."

The words landed like a knife between vertebrae. "No."

"Yes." Her voice was gentle, which made it unbearable. "I heard you laughing. I heard—" she hesitated, choosing, "—I heard *him* laughing too. But it wasn't only him, Astrid. That's what frightens me. He wasn't laughing alone."

Astrid stared into the fire. Across the camp, Tam sat awake on watch, and though he pretended to study the darkness, Astrid knew he was studying them, assembling his report in his head whether he ever delivered it or not. Sela stood at the hollow's rim, a silhouette against the stars, and Astrid wondered what she had seen tonight, and what she would do with it, and whose purposes it might serve.

Everyone had seen. Everyone would say nothing. And the nothing would pile up around Astrid like snowfall, soft and silent and burying.

"I used to think," Astrid said at last, very quietly, "that if I just stayed small enough, quiet enough, far enough from anything that mattered, none of it would ever find me. The oaths. The blood. Him." They flexed their hand and watched the firelight slide along the sword blade. "Tonight I laughed in a pass full of dead men, and it was the best I've felt in thirty years. So much for staying small."

Yara put her hand over theirs. She didn't say it would be all right. She was too honest for that, and Astrid loved her a little for it.

Far to the north, back down the black throat of the pass, a single lantern signal blinked three times from a ridgecrest—witch-finder code, steady and unhurried. Dreven, or what remained of his company, marking their position for whoever came next.

Astrid watched it blink, and understood, with the clarity that comes after blood, that running was finished. Every road from here led through what they were.

The lantern blinked again. And in their blood, patient as winter, Ondrys began—very softly—to hum.
