## 12. Sela's True Commission

The commission was sewn into the lining of Sela's left boot, where no one would think to look and where her own eyes could find it every morning when she laced them.

She had not read it in three weeks. She told herself this was practicality—she knew its contents by heart, word for word, in the council secretary's dry ink-smell of a voice—but the truth was simpler and uglier: reading it required unlacing her boot, and unlacing her boot required deciding, again, what she would do about it. Some decisions were easier to defer than others. This one she had been deferring since Vaelport.

Tonight, by the fire at the edge of a hollow that Astrid Hartwell had chosen for its two exits and its sightlines, Sela finally pulled the boot off.

* * *

The paper was soft as cloth now from handling. She did not need the firelight; she could have recited it into the dark like a prayer, and there were nights when it had felt exactly like one—the kind you say to keep something away.

*To Envoy Sela Voss, plenipotentiary of the Concord of Free Cities, from the Council of Nine, under seal of the inner session:*

*You will locate the individual known as Astrid Hartwell, last verified bearer of the swordspeaker line, and secure their person intact. Alive. This is not negotiable and is not to be communicated to the bearer under any circumstance.*

*The Council judges that a living swordspeaker, publicly bound to the Concord by rite and oath, constitutes the only credible deterrent against King Veyric's expansion should the old powers continue to wake. Should deterrence fail—or should Veyric offer terms—*

Here the secretary's hand had grown smaller, tighter, as if the pen itself had wanted to shrink from the page.

*—the bearer is authorized to negotiate the surrender of the swordspeaker to His Majesty's custody as consideration for a cessation of hostilities against the free cities. The Council notes with regret that a swordspeaker delivered alive may serve Veyric's purposes as readily as ours, but notes further that the Concord's survival takes precedence over any single life, including, if necessary, the envoy's own.*

*May the Nine keep you. May they also forgive us.*

Sela had laughed aloud the first time she read that last line, alone in her cabin on the *Kestrel's Debt*, so suddenly and so harshly that the sailor on watch outside had knocked to ask if she was well. She had said yes. She had been lying then too.

She folded the paper along its worn creases and fed it to the fire.

It changed nothing. She still knew every word. But watching the edges curl and blacken felt like a small rehearsal for a larger burning, and she needed rehearsals lately, because she had begun to suspect that when the moment came she would hesitate, and hesitation in her trade got people killed. Usually other people. Lately, she feared, it might be her.

Across the hollow, Astrid sat sharpening a knife that did not need sharpening.

* * *

Sela had been watching them for weeks now, and the watching had become a problem.

It had started as tradecraft. Of course it had. You assessed your assets: their strengths, their weaknesses, their tells. Astrid's strengths were considerable—a lifetime of Thornfell written into their body, an instinct for ground that seemed less like memory than like conversation, as though the mountain murmured suggestions and Astrid had the good sense to listen. Their weaknesses were equally legible: loneliness worn so long it had become architecture, a flinch at the word *oath*, and a habit of standing slightly apart from the fire's circle, close enough to feel its warmth, far enough to leave first.

But somewhere between Lowmark and the high passes, assessment had curdled into something else. Sela caught herself cataloguing things that had no tactical value. The way Astrid hummed—badly, tunelessly, three notes over and over—when they thought no one was awake. The way they always gave Yara the driest wood without comment. The way they looked at Brannic's terrible jokes with an expression of profound suffering that fooled absolutely no one, least of all Brannic.

The way they had stood over Tam's bedroll the night after the boy confessed his reports to Dreven, and said only, *You came back. That's the part I'm keeping,* and meant it, and Tam had wept like a child, and Astrid had pretended not to notice because pretending not to notice was, Sela was beginning to understand, the whole of Astrid Hartwell's kindness.

That was the problem. That was precisely the problem. The Council had sent her to retrieve a weapon, and instead she had spent six weeks traveling with a person—a tired, funny, guarded, quietly decent person who gave Yara the dry wood—and every mile of road made the paper in her boot heavier.

She watched Astrid across the flames and thought: *They already know.*

Not the specifics, perhaps. But Astrid had survived thirty years in a country that wanted people like them dead, and survivors of that particular school developed an ear for the false note. Sela had heard herself strike it twice in recent days—once when she'd asked, too casually, how Astrid would feel about going to Vaelport, and once when she'd lied about the council's interest being purely diplomatic. Astrid had said nothing either time. They had just looked at her a half-second too long, with those grey eyes that missed nothing, and then gone back to the knives or the map or the humming, and let her keep her lie the way you let a child keep a broken cup hidden behind its back.

That mercy was worse than accusation. Accusation she could answer.

* * *

"You're doing the thing," Yara said, dropping down beside her.

Sela blinked. "What thing?"

"The envoy thing." Yara hugged her knees, chin resting on them, firelight making her young face older than it had any right to be. "Where you go very still and polite and everyone can tell you're calculating. Brannic does it too, but he's bad at it. He breathes differently." She glanced toward Astrid. "Astrid does it best. Astrid can do it while asleep."

"Is that so."

"Mm." Yara studied her with those unsettling clear eyes, the ones that heard things no one else could hear. Sela had learned to be careful around that gaze. "The mountain's quiet tonight," the girl said, which was how she often began saying something else entirely. "Quieter than usual. Even Ondrys is quiet. Astrid says that's a mercy. I don't think it is. I think it's listening."

"To what?"

"To us. To choices." Yara shrugged. "Gods are mostly ears, my mother used to say. Everything else is decoration." She uncurled, patted Sela's shoulder once—briefly, matter-of-factly, the way you'd steady a fence post—and went back to her bedroll beside Tam, who was already snoring with the shameless abandon of the very young and very exhausted.

Sela looked at the ashes of her commission in the fire and thought about ears, and choices, and how many of hers had been made in rooms she'd never entered, by people who would never smell woodsmoke or hear Brannic laugh or see Astrid give away the dry wood.

* * *

She almost waited for morning. She was glad, later, that she didn't, though at the time it felt like walking off a cliff in the dark.

Astrid took the last watch—that hadn't changed in six weeks, some old smuggler's habit of trusting no one else with the hours before dawn—and Sela rose quietly, wrapped her cloak around herself, and crossed the hollow to sit on the cold stone beside them.

Astrid didn't startle. They never startled. They simply turned their head a few degrees, acknowledged her existence, and returned to the dark. The knife was still out. It was, Sela noticed, actually sharp now. There had been nothing left to do to it for twenty minutes.

"I couldn't sleep," Sela said.

"You slept fine until an hour ago. You snore."

"I do not snore."

"Brannic says you snore like a landslide arguing with itself."

"Brannic," Sela said, "is a disgraced soldier who sleeps with his boots on and snores in harmony with himself. His testimony is worthless." A pause. The fire had burned low, coals pulsing orange like slow breathing. "Astrid. We need to talk."

"We're talking."

"No. We need to *talk*." She heard her own voice go formal and hated it, that Vaelport drawing-room clip that crept in whenever she was frightened. "I have something to tell you, and I've been telling myself I'd tell you when the time was right, and I've realized the time is never right, there is no right time, there's just the time before you find out and the time after, and I'd rather choose the moment than have it chosen for me."

Astrid set the knife down. Slowly. In the ember-light their face was all planes and shadow, unreadable, and Sela thought: *there it is, the smuggler, the survivor, thirty years of practice at hearing bad news.* "Go on," Astrid said.

So Sela told her.

She told it plainly, because plainness was the only gift she had left to give. The commission. The words *alive, intact, non-negotiable*. The deterrent plan—the binding rites, the public oath, the swordspeaker paraded along the free cities' borders like a lit beacon so that Veyric would think twice. And then the second clause, the one beneath, the one she had carried against her skin for six weeks: *should deterrence fail, the bearer is authorized to negotiate the surrender of the swordspeaker.*

"A bargaining chip," Astrid said at last. The voice was level. Too level. "Or a sacrifice. Depending on the market."

"Yes."

"And the council sent *you*." Not an accusation. Worse—an observation, gentle as a blade laid flat against skin. "Because you're good. Because you get people to trust you. Because you sat across from me at that table in Lowmark and asked me to guide you through the mountains, and never once mentioned that you'd been sent to collect me like a debt."

"Yes," Sela said again. Her mouth had gone dry. "I told myself the mission mattered more than the method. I told myself I'd find a way to make it come out right, that I could deliver you to Vaelport and argue the council into something better, that I could control it." She laughed, short and bitter. "I'm an envoy. Controlling things is what I pretend to do."

The silence stretched. Somewhere up-slope an owl called, and the wind moved through the pines with a sound like the mountain shifting in its sleep. Astrid picked up the knife, turned it over once, and put it away.

"When," Astrid said. "When did you decide to tell me?"

"Just now."

"Why?"

And here was the question Sela had been turning over for days, the one she'd hoped to avoid by never answering it aloud. She looked at the coals. "Because Yara told me the mountain is quiet tonight. Because she thinks it's listening. Because—" She stopped. Started again, plainer. "Because I've spent six weeks telling myself you were a mission, and it stopped being true somewhere around the second week, and I kept waiting for it to become true again, and it wouldn't. You gave Tam the dry wood. You let him come back. You—" She gestured helplessly at the sleeping camp, at the whole improbable arrangement of strays Astrid had collected without meaning to. "I can't hand a person to Veyric's witch-finders. Not even for nine cities. Especially not for nine cities, because the cities would take them and thank me, and I'd have to live in the thanking."

Astrid was quiet for a long moment. When they spoke, their voice had lost none of its level quality, but something underneath it had shifted, tectonic and slow. "You know what the worst part is?"

"Tell me."

"I'm not surprised." A breath that might have been a laugh. "Thirty years, Sela. Everyone who ever found out what I am wanted something from me. My mentor hid the truth to protect me and died before he could explain it, and I loved him anyway. The gods want my blood. Veyric wants my head. The free cities want a banner to wave and a coin to spend. I'd started thinking maybe you were different, and then I remembered I'd thought that before, about other people, and here we are." They turned and looked at her fully, and their eyes in the coal-light were very old. "Here we are."

"Sela Voss," Astrid continued, before she could speak, "pleni-whatever-it-is of the Concord. Tell me one thing and tell me true. If the council ordered you, tomorrow morning, to bind me and ship me east—if they invoked whatever oaths you swore them—what would you do?"

Sela opened her mouth. Closed it. The honest answer sat in her chest like a stone she'd swallowed weeks ago.

"I don't know," she said. "That's the truth. I'd like to tell you I'd burn my commission and walk away, and maybe I would. But I've seen what Veyric's purge does. I walked through Lowmark's sister-town on my way south, two years ago, after the first shrine-burnings. I know what's coming for the free cities if he finishes with the mountains. And I've spent my whole life believing I was one of the people who holds the line, and I don't know, Astrid, I genuinely do not know, whether when the line and you are on opposite ends of the same rope, which end I'll pull." Her voice cracked on the last word and she let it. "That's my confession. Not treachery—worse. Uncertainty."

For a long moment Astrid said nothing. Then, unexpectedly, they huffed—not quite a laugh, but adjacent to one.

"Uncertainty," they repeated. "You realize that's the most honest thing anyone's ever offered me. Halvard lied to protect me. The council lies to use me. And you sit there and tell me you don't know what you'll do, and somehow that's—" They shook their head. "That's the first offer I've believed."

"It's a poor recommendation of my character."

"It's the only kind I trust anymore." Astrid drew a slow breath and let it out, and Sela watched thirty years of vigilance war visibly with something softer and lose, narrowly, by a margin of inches. "All right. Here's what happens now. Nothing. Tonight, nothing. Tomorrow we walk north, same as planned, because Dreven is still hunting us and Veyric is still burning shrines and neither of those cares what your council wants. You stay with us. You carry your commission or you burn it, that's yours to decide—I'm not going to make your conscience for you, I've had enough of people making mine." A pause. "But Sela. If the day comes when you have to choose—"

"I know."

"—choose fast," Astrid finished quietly. "Slow choices are how good people end up holding the wrong rope."

* * *

Sela did not sleep for the rest of that night. She sat by the dying fire with her boot off and her bare heel against the cold stone, and she turned the decision over and over like a stone in a streambed, looking for the smooth side.

Near dawn, Yara woke without seeming to wake—she simply opened her eyes in the grey light, looked across the embers at Sela, and said, drowsily and with terrible clarity:

"He's pleased, you know. Ondrys. He likes it when the people around Astrid fracture. Cracks are how the light gets in, he says. Or how he gets out. He's never been specific about which."

Then she rolled over and went back to sleep, and Sela sat alone with the ash of her commission and the weight of the morning coming, and far to the north, unseen, the first outriders of Veyric's sealed border began moving into the passes—with orders, though she could not yet know it, that would bring Captain Dreven down upon them within the week.
