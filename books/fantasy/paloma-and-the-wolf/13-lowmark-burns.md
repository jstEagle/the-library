## 13. Lowmark Burns

They smelled it before they saw it.

Astrid had been downwind of burning towns before—twice in the border wars, once when a granary caught in Dren's Hollow—and there was a particular sweetness to woodsmoke that meant homes, not hearths. This smoke was sweeter than any of those. It hung over the pass like a shroud, gray-brown and greasy, and it carried under it another smell that made Tam stop dead on the trail and press his knuckles to his mouth.

"Oil," he said. "Witch-finder oil. They use it for—" He didn't finish. He didn't have to. Everyone in the company knew what witch-finder oil was for.

"They're gone," Brannic said, but his voice was flat, the voice of a man saying a thing he did not believe so that no one else would have to say it first. "The town's not big enough to hold out. They'll have scattered. They'll be fine."

Yara said nothing at all. She had gone very still beside her mule, her hands folded around the little iron bell she wore at her throat—the bell her mother had given her, the shrine-keeper's daughter's inheritance—and Astrid watched her knuckles whiten around it until they looked like carved bone.

"We can go around," Sela said quietly. She was looking at Astrid when she said it, not at the smoke. "There's a track north through the birches. We don't need to see this."

"We need supplies," Astrid said. Their own voice sounded distant to them, level, reasonable, the voice of a smuggler calculating routes. "And we need to know if Dreven left anyone watching the roads."

That was true. It was also a lie, because Astrid knew perfectly well why they were going to walk into that smoke, and it had nothing to do with supplies. Lowmark was where they'd watched a dying child breathe again at a cracked roadside shrine. Lowmark was where an old woman named Maren had sold them dried plums every season for thirty years and never once asked what was in their packs. Lowmark was the closest thing to a home that a person who refused to have a home had ever allowed themselves.

*You've been running so long you forgot you were running,* Halvard had told them, in the last hour of his life, with blood on his lips. *Running feels like walking until you try to stop.*

Astrid turned their horse toward the smoke.

* * *

Lowmark had been a town of perhaps four hundred souls, strung along the river road like beads on a cord: the mill, the market square, the shrine district with its seven small temples where seven old gods had been honored in the careless, comfortable way that border folk honor things—a candle here, a first-fruits offering there, more habit than faith. Astrid had always liked that about Lowmark. The gods there had been neighbors, not masters.

Now the beads were cinders.

They came down the birch track single file, and the town opened up before them like a wound. The mill was gone—not burned but *unmade*, its stones pulled down and scattered, its wheel split into kindling. The market square was a field of black stubble and ash drifts, and the snow that had fallen two nights past lay over everything in a thin gray skin, dirty already, trampled by boots and hooves. Here and there a chimney stood alone above the ruin like a grave marker, and from some of them, impossibly, thin threads of smoke still rose, as if the houses below were trying to go on living without their walls.

The shrines were worst.

Veyric's soldiers had not simply destroyed them. They had *salted* them—every one of the seven, dragged stone by stone into the square and broken, and the broken pieces heaped together and drenched in white crystals that glittered wrong in the weak light. Salt cut with something else; Astrid could smell the bitterness of it from thirty paces, an alchemical spite designed to kill the ground itself, so that nothing green would grow where a god had been honored. Around each salt-heap, someone had painted the king's new sigil in tar: the closed eye, the barred circle. *Nothing watches. Nothing answers.*

"Yara." Sela's hand was on the girl's shoulder. "Don't look at—"

But Yara was already off her mule and walking, walking fast, toward the heap that had been her mother's shrine, the little temple of Vessia with its carved basin where the sick had come to wash their hands for three hundred years. She fell to her knees at the edge of the salt and reached out, and Astrid saw her fingers hover there, unable to touch, unable to withdraw.

"She kept the candles," Yara said. Her voice was very small. "Even after the edict. Even when everyone told her to stop. She said a candle isn't treason, a candle is just—" Her hands closed into fists against her thighs. "She said the gods don't ask much. That's all they ever asked. A candle. A cup of water. And he couldn't even—"

She broke then, all at once, the way young things break, completely and without armor, and the sound she made was one Astrid felt in their sternum like a blow. Brannic went to her and knelt, awkward as a felled tree, and put his arms around her, and she turned her face into his chest and sobbed with her whole body while the salt glittered and the chimneys smoked their ghost-smoke and the snow came down soft and indifferent as it has since the beginning of the world.

Tam stood apart, hat in his hands. He had been a witch-finder's scribe for six years. He knew exactly which unit had done this, Astrid realized. He probably knew the names of the officers. His face was the face of a man reading a report he himself had written.

"Survivors?" Sela asked, low, practical, because someone had to be.

"There." Astrid nodded toward the far side of the square, where a knot of figures huddled in the lee of the salted shrine-heaps: a dozen people, maybe fifteen, hollow-eyed, wrapped in whatever they'd grabbed when the soldiers came. And Astrid saw the marks then, and their stomach turned over slowly, like a boat taking water.

Every survivor wore a brand. On the cheek or the brow or the back of the hand—a seared circle with a bar across it, the same closed-eye sigil the soldiers had painted in tar, punched into living skin with heated iron. Some of the wounds were fresh, still weeping. One old man's brand had gone septic; the flesh around it was swollen and dark, and he swayed on his feet as if the ground moved under him.

An old woman detached herself from the group and came toward them, moving carefully, the way people move when they've decided not to trust their own legs. Astrid knew her. Of course they knew her. Maren, plum-seller, gossip, widow, the woman who had slipped Astrid an extra twist of honey-candy every midwinter for three decades and called it *for your sweetheart, whoever she is.*

Her left cheek bore the brand. The skin around it was cracked like drought-earth.

"Hartwell," she said. Her voice was rough but steady. "I thought you'd be dead by now. Or gone over the mountains."

"I'm hard to kill," Astrid said. "Maren. Who—how many did they take?"

"Took? None." Something moved behind her eyes, something worse than grief. "They didn't want prisoners. Prisoners talk back. They wanted examples." She gestured with her chin at the square, at the ash, at the places Astrid's eyes had been avoiding, the places where the snow lay thickest and darkest and most strangely shaped. "The ones who wouldn't spit on the shrines when the captain ordered it. There were forty-one who wouldn't."

Forty-one. Astrid made themselves look. Made themselves see the shapes under the snow, laid in rows, decently enough, because the survivors had done that much at least—had dug frozen ground with bare hands and iron scraps and laid their neighbors in rows. Forty-one mounds. At the head of each, someone had set a stone from the broken shrines, a fragment of carved god, salted and dead, but set upright anyway, facing east, facing morning.

"The child," Astrid heard themselves say. "The girl who was healed. Ilse. And her mother."

Maren's face did something complicated. "Gone south to her sister in Corvane before the soldiers came, thank every listening ear. The mother took the healing for a sign, and it was—it saved them." She looked at Astrid then, really looked, and her eyes were wet and fierce. "Some are saying the gods sent the sickness of fire to punish us for forgetting. Some are saying the gods are too busy being reborn to notice a town like ours. I say—" her voice cracked, steadied— "I say somebody has to make sure it wasn't for nothing."

* * *

They worked through the short afternoon.

It was Brannic who organized it, because Brannic was the kind of soldier who could not see an unburied dead and stand idle; he had the survivors show him where the tools were buried, and soon there were six of them digging, and the ring of the mattocks was the only sound in Lowmark besides the wind. Tam went house to house—or rather, house-remnant to house-remnant—with a ledger page and a pencil, writing down names, because he said records mattered, because he said someday someone would want to know these people had existed beyond a number in a purge report, and his hands shook the whole time and he did not stop. Sela walked the perimeter with her hood up and her sword loose, counting sight-lines, finding the tracks of the column that had done this—two days old, headed southeast, back toward the king's road. Yara sat by the salted shrine of her mother and would not move, and no one made her.

Astrid dug.

Their shoulders ached. Their palms blistered and bled inside their gloves. The frozen ground fought them like a living thing, and they fought it back, stroke after stroke, and somewhere in the middle of the twelfth grave—they were counting, they couldn't help counting—Halvard's voice spoke up from wherever dead mentors keep their opinions.

*This is what running buys,* the voice said. *Thirty years of careful silence, and this is the interest it collects.*

"It wasn't my silence that did this," Astrid muttered into the dirt. "It was Veyric's fear."

*His fear and your hiding are two halves of the same machine.* The voice was gentle, which was how Astrid knew it was their own memory speaking and not the other thing, the thing in their blood, which was never gentle. *He burns because he believes the gods will return through people like you. You hide because you believe the same. Between you, you've built a world where only the innocent burn.*

Astrid drove the mattock in hard enough to crack a stone.

Beneath them, faintly, so faintly it might have been the ache of their own blood, they felt Ondrys stir. Not words. Not yet. Just attention, the way a cat's weight shifts in a dark room when it hears its name. The god-fragment turning over in its sleep, tasting the grief in the air, the fury, the rich black soil of it.

*Not now,* Astrid thought at it, and for once the vastness seemed to listen, folding itself smaller, waiting. That patience frightened Astrid more than hunger would have. Hunger could be starved. Patience was already fed.

* * *

They buried the last of the forty-one as the light failed, and the survivors lit candles—one candle each, stolen back from the ashes of the shrine district, real tallow candles that Maren had hidden in a root cellar the soldiers never found. Forty-one flames in forty-one jars, set atop forty-one graves, and the wind tried to take them and mostly failed, and Yara walked down the rows lighting the ones that guttered, and where she passed, Astrid noticed, the flames stood straighter than they should have, burned brighter, leaned toward her like flowers toward sun.

The survivors watched her do it, and Astrid watched the survivors, and saw the moment when fear became something else in their faces. Not hope, exactly. Hope was too expensive a word for people who had lost everything in an afternoon. It was more like recognition. *The gods are real,* their faces said, *and they are small and kind and they live in a girl with a bell, and the king burned our town and could not touch that.*

Maren pressed a sack into Astrid's hands at the edge of the square: dried plums, of course, and hard bread, and a twist of honey-candy.

"For your sweetheart," she said, and her ruined branded face almost smiled. "Whoever they are."

"Maren." Astrid's throat was tight. "Come with us. South, to the free cities. There's room—"

"And leave them?" She nodded at the graves, the candles, the handful of neighbors. "Someone has to plant in spring. Someone has to prove the salt didn't win. You run north, Hartwell. Somebody has to stay." She squeezed their arm with a grip that had outlived a husband, two children, and now a town. "But if you're going to keep running—"

"I'm not."

The words came out of Astrid before they knew they were there. They stood in the falling dusk with the sack of plums in their bleeding hands and forty-one small fires burning behind them, and the words hung in the cold air, and Astrid found that they meant them—that beneath thirty years of careful silence, something had finally, irrevocably snapped.

Maren waited. She had sold goods to a smuggler for thirty years; she knew a bargain when she heard one forming.

Astrid turned to face the company. Sela, watchful by the perimeter. Brannic, mattock on shoulder, grave-dirt to the elbows. Tam, clutching his ledger of names like a talisman. Yara, coming up the row of candles with wax on her fingers and tear-tracks cutting through the ash on her cheeks. All of them looking at Astrid, because somehow, without anyone deciding it, they had become the company's center, its compass point, the direction everyone faced when the wind changed.

"I swore an oath once," Astrid said. Their voice came out low and rough. "When I was nineteen. My mentor made me swear I'd never speak the swordspeaker words, never draw the old power, never let them find me. I've kept that oath for thirty years, and it bought me nothing but distance—from him, from all of you, from—" they gestured at the graves, and could not finish.

Yara stopped. The candlelight moved on her face.

"So here's a new one." Astrid drew their knife—the plain smuggler's knife, not the oath-blade sleeping wrapped in oil at the bottom of their pack—and opened their palm, and pressed the edge to it, and let the blood well, and did not flinch. Somewhere in their blood, Ondrys went very, very still, listening. Let it listen. This oath wasn't for it.

"By the ground that holds them and the fire that remembers them"—their voice strengthened, and the old cadences rose in it unbidden, the rhythm of vows spoken at waystones before the world forgot how to listen—"by Maren's plums and Yara's bell and the forty-one who wouldn't kneel—I swear I am done running. Not vengeance. Not war. But I will not hide while he burns another Lowmark. If Veyric wants a swordspeaker to hunt—" they closed their fist, and the blood ran between their fingers, and the nearest candle-flames all leaned toward them at once, every one, like a held breath— "then let him find one standing in the open."

The silence afterward was enormous. Snow ticked softly down through it.

Then Brannic laughed—a short, startled bark—and said, "Well. About time." And Tam wrote something in his ledger, and Sela looked at Astrid with an expression Astrid couldn't read, something between triumph and sorrow, and Yara crossed the snow and took Astrid's bleeding hand in both of hers, and the cut closed—not healed, exactly, but quieted, the blood stilled—as if her touch and the god in Astrid's veins had reached some brief, unspoken truce.

"You should know," Yara said softly, so only Astrid could hear, "he heard that. All of it. And he's pleased."

"I know," Astrid said. "That's the price of oaths. Someone always profits."

They camped that night in the shell of the mill, under the one standing wall, with the candles of Lowmark burning behind them down the hill like a constellation brought low. And near midnight, Sela sat down across the fire from Astrid, uninvited, and turned her commission papers over and over in her fingers, and said, "There's something you need to know about why the council really sent me. Before we go any further north."

The fire cracked. Astrid looked up into her eyes, and saw that whatever it was had been weighing on her for weeks, and that she had finally decided the scales had tipped.

"Tell me," Astrid said.
