## 14. The God's Bargain

The fire had burned down to a red heart by the time Astrid finally admitted they could not sleep.

They sat apart from the others, at the edge of the camp's small circle of warmth, where the light gave out and the dark of the high valley began. Above them, Thornfell's peaks cut black teeth into a sky salted with stars—stars that seemed, tonight, to burn with an attention that felt almost personal. The wind came down off the icefields carrying the smell of stone and old snow and something else beneath it, something like incense burned in a room long shut.

*You are awake.*

The voice did not come through their ears. It never did. It arrived the way cold arrives, the way weight arrives—simply present, everywhere inside them at once, patient as erosion.

"I'm awake," Astrid murmured, too low to wake Yara or Tam in their bedrolls. "That doesn't mean I'm talking to you."

*And yet you answer.* A pause that might have been amusement, if gods could be amused. *Thirty years you have answered me, little sword. Every time you refused to pray, you were praying. Every time you turned your back on the shrines, you walked with your face toward me. You have spent your whole life defining yourself against my absence. Do you know what that is?*

"Exhausting," Astrid said.

*It is devotion,* Ondrys said gently. *It has always been devotion.*

Astrid stared into the coals until their eyes watered. Three days since Lowmark. Three days since the branded survivors and the salted earth and Yara kneeling in the ashes of her mother's shrine with her hands pressed flat against scorched stone, weeping without sound. Three days since Astrid had stood in the ruin of everything they'd spent three decades protecting and sworn an oath for the first time since they were young enough to believe oaths meant safety: *I will stop running.*

They had thought the oath was theirs. They were beginning to suspect the god had been waiting for it like a farmer waits for rain.

*You swore it beautifully,* Ondrys said now, as if reading the thought—which, Astrid reminded themselves grimly, he could. *I have watched you refuse me for thirty years with more faithfulness than most of my priests ever managed. That is why I offer this to you, and not to some desperate king or frightened girl. You have earned the right to hear it plainly.*

"Then say it plainly."

*Give me your body fully.*

The words landed in Astrid's chest like a dropped stone into a well. They heard the splash of it all the way down.

*Not your soul—I have no interest in souls; they are small and damp things. Not your will, even, though I confess your will is the finest thing about you. Your body. Your blood, which is already mine. Your hands, which remember what your mind has spent thirty years pretending to forget. In exchange—*

"In exchange," Astrid said, and their voice came out flat and cold, the smuggler's voice, the negotiator's voice, because that was the only way they could keep speaking at all, "you'll destroy Veyric's armies. You mentioned. In a night."

*In a night,* Ondrys agreed, with terrible mildness. *Perhaps less. His host is forty thousand strong, camped on the plain below Vaelport in tents that will burn like autumn grass. I do not need your permission to destroy them, little sword. I need only your consent to be fully present while I do it. There is a difference, and the difference matters. Without you, I must act through cracks and leaks—a waystone here, a guardian there, a frightened shrine-keeper's daughter hearing voices she cannot carry. Scattered. Slow. Cruel in the way floods are cruel, taking the guilty and innocent alike. With you—with a swordspeaker who opens every door at once—I can be precise. I can be a scalpel instead of a flood. I can spare the camp followers and the conscripted boys and the cooks, and take only the men who marched willingly under a king who burns children.*

Astrid's hands had gone still in their lap. That was the horror of it—not the threat but the reasonableness. The god was not snarling. He was reasoning. He sounded like Halvard had sounded, in the last weeks, laying out hard choices with soft regret.

"You killed people at the Pass of Teeth," Astrid said quietly. "Through me. While I was—watching from somewhere behind my own eyes."

*I saved your friends,* Ondrys corrected, without heat. *You enjoyed it. That is not an accusation. It is simply true, and you know it is true, and the shame you feel about it is the last wall between us. So let us speak of walls. What is yours made of, Astrid Hartwell? Fear of me? No. You have been afraid of me since you were nine years old and never once stopped functioning. Your wall is this: you believe that if you open the door all the way, there will be nothing left of you on the other side of it. And I am asking you to trust me with the one thing you have never trusted anyone with—not Halvard, not Sela, not the road itself.*

"And why would I?"

*Because the alternative is watching them die.* The gentleness did not waver; that was the worst part. *Veyric's witch-finders found Tam's family because we were slow. Dreven is dead, yes—but Dreven was one hound. The kennel remains. Sela's council will sell you the moment you become more useful sold than free. Brannic died holding a bridge so that you could run another day, and how many bridges remain? How many Brannics? You cannot guide everyone through Thornfell. You cannot outwalk an empire. But I can end one in a night, and then you can spend whatever years remain to you doing what you love—walking roads that remember you, drinking bad wine in Lowmark when it is rebuilt, teaching some sharp-eyed child to read the mountain weather. All I ask is the loan of your morning. One night of your life. And I will give you back the rest of it, unspent, whole.*

The fire cracked. Somewhere up-slope, a stone loosened and clicked down through scree, and Astrid flinched before they could stop themselves.

Because here was the thing they had told no one, not even Yara, especially not Yara: standing in the burning streets of Lowmark, they had wanted it. For one white-hot heartbeat they had stood among the ash and the brand-scars and wanted to open their chest like a gate and let something vast come through and unmake the men who had done it. The wanting had lasted no longer than a struck match. But it had happened. And Ondrys knew it had happened, and Ondrys had been building on that foundation ever since, patiently, brick by reasonable brick.

"If I say yes," Astrid said slowly, "what happens to me? During. After."

*During, you will see everything and feel everything, and it will be glorious—that is not flattery, it is physics; divinity experienced directly is glory, the way fire is hot. After, you will be tired. Wonderfully, humanly tired. You will sleep for two days and wake hungry, and Sela will make her sour jokes, and Tam will hide behind his ledger, and Yara will look at you the way she looks at you now—*

"No," Astrid said. "Don't use her."

*I am not using her. I am describing her. There is a difference, and the difference matters—you taught me that phrase, did you know? You have repeated it to yourself so many times that it has worn a groove in you, and I have simply walked along the groove.*

Astrid laughed. It came out wrong, cracked down the middle, half sob. They pressed the heels of their hands against their eyes.

"That's what frightens me," they said. "Not the power. The kindness. If you'd threatened me I'd have fought you till my heart gave out. But you sit in my skull and you make sense, and I can't tell anymore which thoughts are mine. When I decided to swear the oath at Lowmark—was that me?"

Silence. A long one. Long enough that Astrid lowered their hands.

*Yes,* Ondrys said at last. *That was yours. I would not lie to you about that. I want you to know that the choice is real, Astrid. That is the entire point. A coerced consent is worth nothing. A purchased one is worth little. But a chosen one—freely given, with full knowledge—*

"With full knowledge," Astrid echoed. "Except I don't have full knowledge, do I. I have your word. And your word comes from a god whose last reign ended—" They stopped. "How did it end, Ondrys? Nobody remembers. Halvard never taught it. The shrines don't say it. Thirty years I've walked past your broken temples and not one of them says how you fell. Why is that?"

The silence this time had a different texture. Not patience. Calculation.

*Ask the girl,* Ondrys said finally. *She hears what the stones remember. Or do not ask, and take my word, and save us both a night of old griefs. I am offering you a future, little sword. Do not spend it digging up my past.*

"There it is," Astrid whispered. "There's the price tag, tucked inside the gift."

*Everything has a price tag. You are a smuggler; you know this better than anyone. The question has never been whether there is a price. The question is whether the goods are worth it.*

Astrid looked up at the stars. Forty thousand men on a plain below Vaelport. Tam's sister in a witch-finder cell. Yara's burned shrine. Brannic under a cairn of river stones they'd piled with their own hands. And inside their own veins, warm and patient and reasonable, the oldest hunger in the world wearing the face of a fair bargain.

"Let me think," they said.

*Of course,* said Ondrys, and the satisfaction in his voice was soft as snowfall. *Take until dawn. I have waited three thousand years. I can wait four hours.*

---

Yara was sitting up in her blankets when Astrid crawled back toward the fire. She didn't ask why Astrid was awake. She never had to ask anymore.

"He talked to you tonight," she said. Not a question.

"How do you—"

"Because the fire leaned toward you." She said it simply, the way she said everything, rubbing sleep from her eyes with the heel of her hand. "Fire doesn't lean, Astrid. It leaned."

Astrid sat down across from her, close to the coals, and for a long moment neither spoke. Then Astrid heard themself say, in a voice they barely recognized as their own: "He offered me a bargain. My body, fully. In exchange, Veyric's army dies in a night. Everyone lives. The war ends before it starts."

Yara went very still.

"And you're going to say yes." Not an accusation. Worse—an observation, delivered with the flat certainty of someone who had grown up listening to gods and knew exactly how they sounded when they were winning.

"I haven't decided."

"You've decided. You're just not done grieving the version of you that wouldn't have." Yara pulled her blanket around her shoulders. Her face was very pale in the coal-light, and older than her seventeen years. "My mother used to say the old gods never threaten. They grieve. They show you the wound first, and then they offer to heal it, and by the time you notice the healing costs your name, you're already grateful. Astrid—he's not lying to you. That's what nobody understands. He means every word. The army dies in a night. Your friends live. And in ten years you won't remember your mother's face, because he'll have worn it smooth, the way water wears stone. Not cruelly. Just... completely."

"Then show me," Astrid said hoarsely. "If you hear what the stones remember—if you can carry what he won't tell me—then show me his last reign. Show me how it ended. Because he wouldn't. He asked me not to dig up his past, and every smuggler instinct I have says that's where the bodies are buried."

Yara looked at the fire for a long time. When she raised her eyes again, there was something in them that made Astrid's skin prickle—the same look she'd had kneeling in the ashes at Lowmark, listening to something vast through burnt stone.

"It will hurt," she said. "Visions always cost the vessel something. That's the law of it. Are you sure?"

No, Astrid thought. *No, I am not remotely sure, I am a tired smuggler who wants to go home to a cabin that smells of pine resin and pretend the world is simple.* But Lowmark was ash, and Brannic was stones, and forty thousand men were sleeping on a plain below a city that had given them shelter when no one else would.

"Yes," they said.

Yara took their hands. Hers were small and rough and warm. She closed her eyes, and Astrid felt the fire lean toward them both—

—and the world folded.

* * *

They stood on a green plain under a summer sky, and the plain was covered in people.

Hundreds of thousands of them. More. A nation on its knees, stretching to every horizon, wheat-gold and swaying in a wind that smelled of bread and honey—and every single face was turned upward, every mouth open, every throat pouring out the same prayer in the same breath, a sound like a ocean made of voices, and the prayer was not *help us* or *save us*. The prayer was *we are yours, we are yours, we are yours*, and it rose from the plain in waves, and Astrid understood with a lurching, sickening clarity that these people were happy.

That was the first horror. They were radiant with joy.

*Look closer,* said Yara's voice, thin and strained, coming from everywhere and nowhere. *Look at what the joy is standing on.*

Astrid looked down. The wheat was growing from soil the color of rust. And beneath the soil—beneath the roots, packed dense as gravel all the way down—were bones. Human bones. Generations of bones. The plain was a grave that had learned to bloom, and the people singing above it had forgotten there was anything underneath, because Ondrys had eaten the memory along with everything else.

*He called it the Harvest of Voices,* Yara whispered. *Every generation, he took a tithe. Not bodies—voices. Names. Each person who prayed himself empty gave him a piece of what they were, and he grew, and they smiled, because the giving felt like love. Whole villages knelt in the fields and poured themselves out until there was nothing left to pour, and they died smiling, and their neighbors sang louder to fill the silence. He didn't rule them, Astrid. He digested them. And the ones who kept their names—the ones who hid, who stayed silent, who clung to themselves—they were the heretics, the blighted, the ungrateful. Their neighbors reported them. Lovingly. Because a neighbor who keeps their name is a neighbor who isn't feeding the god.*

The vision shifted. Now a city—white-walled, beautiful, its towers hung with bells that rang themselves. In the great square, a woman stood on a scaffold, and the crowd below her was not angry. The crowd was heartbroken. *Say the prayer,* they begged her, weeping, holding up children so she could see their faces. *Just say it. One prayer and you can come down and be with us.* And the woman looked out over all those beloved, hollowed faces and said, in a voice that carried across three thousand years and into the skull of a smuggler crouched beside a dying fire:

"My name is Mereth. I keep it."

And the bells rang, and the crowd sang, and the woman died loved and unmourned, because to mourn her would have been to admit something was wrong, and admitting something was wrong meant having a name to be wrong *with*—

The vision lurched again, faster now, Yara gasping, the images coming like stones loosed down a mountainside: the war, when it finally came—not gods against mortals but mortals against the god they had fed, a rebellion of the named, burning the temples they had built, drowning the fields in salt, and Ondrys falling not in battle but starving, shrinking, his harvest cut off, his faithful slaughtered or freed, until all that remained of him was a fragment sealed in the bloodline of the only order that had served him faithfully to the end—the swordspeakers, the jailers, the oath-bound, carrying their prisoner in their veins for a hundred generations, guarding him not out of loyalty but out of guilt—

—and the last image, before the vision tore free: a field of smiling dead, and rising out of it, vast and gentle and sorrowful, a face Astrid knew from the inside, looking directly at them across three millennia, and saying, with infinite tenderness:

*Now you understand why I need a body that chooses. The last time, they gave me everything and it was ash. This time I want one door, opened willingly. That is all I have ever wanted. That is all I will ever ask.*

* * *

Astrid came back to themself on their hands and knees in the frost, gasping, Yara collapsed beside them with a nosebleed sheeting down her chin, Tam shouting, Sela's blade already drawn and scanning the dark for threats that weren't there.

But Astrid barely heard any of it. Because the terrible thing—the thing that would keep them awake until dawn and long after—was not the horror of the vision.

It was that even now, even knowing, even with the taste of that smiling graveyard plain in their mouth, some quiet chamber deep inside them was still whispering: *but he means it kindly. But the army would die in a night. But your friends would live. But—*

The god hadn't lost anything tonight. He had shown Astrid his own monstrousness, freely, honestly—and somehow that honesty had made the bargain stronger, because now Astrid could no longer pretend they hadn't known. Whatever they chose next, they would choose it with their eyes open. And Ondrys, patient as erosion, would wait for the exhaustion and the grief and the love to do their slow work.

Astrid got up. Wiped Yara's blood from her chin with their sleeve, gently, the way you'd touch something breakable. Met Sela's eyes across the dead fire and saw that she knew—had maybe always known—and had chosen, visibly, deliberately, to say nothing.

"Dawn's coming," Astrid said, and their voice was steady, and it was a lie, and everyone heard it. "We move in an hour. There's a road down to Vaelport that the maps have forgotten, and I'm the only one who remembers it."

*Take your time, little sword,* murmured the voice in their blood, warm as banked embers, kind as a knife wrapped in wool. *Whatever you choose, I will be right here. I have nowhere else to be—and neither, in the end, do you.*

Astrid shouldered their pack and started walking, and did not answer, and hated how much the silence felt like agreement.
