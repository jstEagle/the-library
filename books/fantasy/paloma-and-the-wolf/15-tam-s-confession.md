## 15. Tam's Confession

Tam had not slept in four days, and the ledger of his lies had grown so heavy he could feel it in his spine, a weight like wet wool packed between his shoulder blades. He sat apart from the others at the edge of the firelight, ostensibly mending a strap on his satchel with an awl and waxed thread, but his hands kept stilling in his lap, and his eyes kept going to Yara.

She was asleep with her back against Brannic's pack, her head tipped toward the flames, and even in sleep her lips moved faintly, as though she were listening to something no one else could hear. Tam knew what she was hearing. He had spent three years transcribing witch-finder interrogations; he knew what it looked like when a person carried a voice inside them that would not stop.

*If she listens closely enough,* he thought, *she will hear what I am.*

The thought had been circling him for days now, patient as a wolf. It had begun as a whisper—*they'll never know, you've been careful*—and had swollen into a certainty: *Yara hears everything. She will hear this too.*

He looked at Astrid across the fire. The smuggler was awake, as always, sitting with their sword across their knees and their hood down, watching the dark between the trees with the flat patience of someone who had made a profession out of waiting. The firelight moved on their face and did not seem to touch them. Thirty years of mountains had carved them into something like the stone they traveled through: hard, weathered, impossible to read unless you knew where to look.

Tam was learning where to look. That was the problem. Every day he learned more about these people—the way Sela checked the perimeter twice before sleeping and pretended she hadn't; the way Brannic hummed old marching songs when he thought no one could hear, always the sad ones; the way Astrid gave Yara the driest corner of any shelter without ever seeming to notice they'd done it. And every day he learned more, the lie in his chest grew heavier, because these were not marks. These were not targets. These were people who had saved his life at the Pass of Teeth without asking him anything, who had fed him from their own stores, who had let him walk beside them as if he belonged there.

And every night, when he scratched his report into cipher and sealed it into the hollow brass tube hidden in his satchel's frame, he told himself the same thing he had been telling himself since Dreven first put the knife to his throat three years ago: *It doesn't matter. I've been lying to them all along.*

That was true. That was the terrible, saving truth of it. Every location he had reported to Captain Dreven had been false—a day behind, a valley off, a pass already closed by snow. He had learned early that the only way to survive being a spy was to be a bad one, deliberately, in ways that could not be proven. His family lived because his reports were worthless. If Dreven ever discovered the pattern—and Dreven was not a stupid man, merely a tired one—then Maren and the children would die in a cell in Lowmark, or worse, in whatever place the witch-finders used now that Lowmark itself was ash.

Lowmark. The image rose in him unbidden: the town burned to its foundations, the shrine stones split and salted, the survivors stumbling along the road with brands seared into their cheeks. He had seen the smoke from three ridges away and had not been able to breathe until they'd confirmed the garrison had evacuated before the fires started. His family had been held in Lowmark. Were they still? Had Dreven moved them when the purge began, or—

He pressed the awl into his thumb, hard, and let the small bright pain cut through the spiral.

*Astrid swore an oath,* he reminded himself. *In the ruins of Lowmark, standing over that salted shrine, they swore to stop running.* An oath from a swordspeaker was not a small thing. Tam had transcribed enough of the old records to know what oaths meant to that bloodline—they were not words but bindings, threads tied into the world itself. Astrid had tied themselves to a purpose in front of witnesses.

Which meant that if Tam confessed—if he laid the whole rotten architecture of his betrayal on the ground between them and asked for help—there was a chance, a real chance, that they would say yes. That they would turn aside from whatever road they were walking and put themselves in Dreven's path to pull two children and a schoolteacher out of a witch-finder's cage.

And there was a chance, just as real, that Astrid would put a sword through him where he sat, and Sela would call it prudence, and Brannic would dig the grave.

He looked at the brass tube in his satchel. Tonight's report was already written. *Company of six, moving south along the Vaelwater, low on provisions, the swordspeaker wounded at the pass and favoring the left arm.* All of it true except the direction—he had shifted their route thirty miles east in the telling—and the wound, which was invented. Dreven would march his witch-finders east. Dreven always marched where Tam pointed. That was the arrangement: point, and your family breathes another week.

But the arrangement had a flaw, and Tam had finally named it to himself tonight, sitting alone in the cold while the found-family slept around their fire: *the arrangement requires me to keep choosing my family over theirs, forever, and one day the choice will kill them.*

Dreven was not stupid. Sooner or later he would test one of Tam's reports against the land itself, and find the company where Tam swore they could not be, and then the game would end—not with Tam's death, which he could almost bear, but with Maren's, with little Oskar's, with sweet, stubborn Ilse's, aged seven, who drew pictures of wolves and sent them to her father folded into eighths because paper was expensive.

Tam set down the awl. He picked up the brass tube. He turned it over in his hands, feeling its familiar weight, the weight of three years of cowardice dressed up as cleverness.

Then he stood up, walked around the fire, and knelt in the frost in front of Astrid Hartwell with the tube held out in both hands like an offering—or a confession, which amounted to the same thing.

Astrid's eyes opened. They had known he was coming, of course. They always knew.

"Before you speak," Astrid said quietly, "think very carefully. Some doors don't close again."

"I know." Tam's voice came out cracked. He cleared his throat. "I've been standing outside this door for three years. I'd like to come through it."

Sela was awake instantly—he heard the soft scrape of her knife leaving its sheath somewhere behind him—but Astrid raised one hand, and she stilled. Around the fire, Brannic stirred, and Yara sat up, blinking, her eyes finding Tam with that unnerving directness, as if she had been waiting for exactly this.

"My name isn't Tam Weller," he said. "Well—it is, but that's not what matters. What matters is that for three years I was a scribe in Captain Dreven's retinue, and for three years I have been reporting your movements to him." He set the brass tube down in the frost between himself and Astrid, and pushed it forward with one finger. "Ciphered reports. Locations. Strengths. Everything I could learn. There's one in there from tonight."

The silence that followed was so complete that Tam could hear the frost crackling as it formed on the log beside him.

"You're lying," Brannic said flatly. "You're lying to buy yourself mercy when we're taken."

"Brannic." Sela's voice was quiet. "Look at his hands."

Tam looked down at his own hands. They were shaking so badly the frost seemed to tremble with them.

"If he were inventing this," Sela went on, rising slowly to her feet, "he'd have invented a better story. A coerced spy confesses under pressure, not voluntarily, kneeling in the snow at midnight." Her eyes never left Tam's face, and there was something in them that was not quite anger—something colder and more careful. "How long?"

"Since the beginning. Since before the Pass of Teeth. Since the day you found me in the burned chapel at Harrow Ford." He swallowed. "I didn't lead Dreven to you there. I want to be clear about that—I never once led him anywhere. But I told him you existed. I told him a swordspeaker was moving through Thornfell, and that was the match that lit everything after."

"The ambush at the pass," Astrid said. Their voice was very level. "Was that your work?"

"No." Tam met their eyes and held them, because this was the one thing he could give them clean. "I told Dreven you'd gone east through the Wolfjaw. You went west instead—you changed route on a whim, remember, because Yara said the mountain felt wrong. Your whim saved us. But Dreven has scouts of his own, and he found you anyway, and I—" his voice broke, and he hated himself for it, "—I watched from the ridge and I did nothing, because doing anything would have cost me my cover, and my cover is the only thing keeping my family alive."

"Your family," Sela said.

Tam closed his eyes. When he opened them, he made himself look at each of them in turn—Sela with her knife loose in her grip, Brannic with his jaw working, Yara with her head tilted as though listening to some frequency beneath his words, and Astrid, still as stone, still deciding whether he was worth the breath it would take to kill him.

"My wife, Maren. My son, Oskar, who is eleven. My daughter, Ilse, who is seven." Each name came out steady, because he had rehearsed them for three years in the dark, and because they deserved steadiness. "Dreven took them the day I was conscripted into his retinue. Not arrested—taken. Held in a house in Lowmark with a guard on the door and food enough and no explanation, and a message delivered to me on my first night of service: *write well, and they live.*" He laughed, and it came out wrong, a dry broken sound. "So I wrote badly. Every report, I bent the truth just far enough to be plausible. Wrong valleys. Wrong days. Invented wounds. I have spent three years being the worst spy in Thornfell, and it worked, right up until Lowmark burned."

"And your family?" Yara asked softly.

"I don't know." The words cost him everything he had left. "The garrison evacuated before the fires. I've been telling myself Dreven moved them north, to the fort at Greywatch, because that's standard procedure. But I don't *know*, and every hour I sit here writing my little lies in my little cipher is an hour they're spending in a witch-finder's keeping, and I can't—" He stopped. Drew breath. "I can't do it anymore. That's why I'm here. Not for absolution. I'm not asking you to forgive me. I'm asking you to decide what to do with me, and I'll abide by it, because I'm done choosing between my family and strangers. I choose both. I choose both or nothing."

He knelt back on his heels in the frost and waited.

* * *

They argued for a long time. Tam sat apart again, where they'd told him to sit, and listened to his fate debated ten feet away as though he were dead already—which, in a sense, he was. The man who had knelt down with the brass tube no longer existed. Whatever stood up afterward would be someone new, and he did not yet know if that person would be walking free, walking in chains, or not walking at all.

"He should be bound," Brannic said, "and left for the next garrison. I'm sorry, but I buried friends because of men like him."

"He's not a man like that," Yara said. "You weren't listening."

"I was listening fine."

"No." Yara shook her head. "You were hearing. Listening is different." She turned toward the others, and the firelight caught her face, young and old at once. "When he spoke the names of his children, the gods went quiet. Do you understand what that means? Everything else in this world makes noise to them—every prayer, every curse, every secret. But when a man speaks the truest thing in him, there's a silence underneath it, like the silence under a bell's first strike. He wasn't performing. He was telling the truth, and the truth rang."

"That's poetry, not evidence," Sela said.

"I'm a shrine-keeper's daughter. Poetry is my evidence." But then she paused, and her voice dropped. "There's more. While he talked, I heard something else. Underneath. Something that follows him like a smell." She looked at Tam directly, and Tam felt his stomach drop through the frozen earth. "Fear. Old fear, worn smooth like a river stone. And under that—" she frowned, listening to something, "—a counting. He counts. Constantly. Days. Rations. Miles. Lies." Her eyes widened slightly. "He's counting how many times each of us has shown him kindness. He keeps a tally. He thinks he owes a debt he can't pay."

Tam looked down at his hands. They had stopped shaking. That was somehow worse.

"It's true," he said quietly. "All of it. I count everything. It's how I survived the retinue—make everything arithmetic and nothing hurts. Forty-one kindnesses from this company. I have them numbered."

Silence again. Then, unexpectedly, Brannic laughed—a short, harsh bark with no humor in it.

"Forty-one," he repeated. "Gods below. I once served under a quartermaster who counted like that. Worst man I ever knew, and the only one whose ledgers never lied." He rubbed his face with both hands. "Fine. Fine! I withdraw the grave-digging. But I want it understood that I think this is a mistake, and I reserve the right to say I told you so when he betrays us."

"Noted," Sela said dryly. Then she turned to Astrid, who had not spoken throughout, and the whole circle turned with her, because everyone understood that the decision had never really been anyone's but theirs.

Astrid had picked up the brass tube at some point during the argument. Now they turned it slowly in their fingers, and Tam realized with a lurch that they were reading the cipher—of course they were; a smuggler who survived thirty years in Thornfell read every cipher worth knowing—and that the slight deepening of the lines around their eyes meant they had reached tonight's report and found the falsified route.

"You moved us thirty miles east," Astrid said.

"Yes."

"And gave me a wound I don't have."

"A left arm. It buys you two days of slow travel in his estimation."

Astrid was quiet for a long moment. When they spoke again, their voice had something in it that Tam had not heard before—something almost gentle, worn like a smooth stone over something vast and bruised.

"Halvard lied to me for thirty years," they said. "Told me the gods were dead, told me my blood was ordinary, let me build a whole life on top of his silence. When I found out, I wanted to dig him up and kill him again." They looked at Tam across the fire. "I understand better now than I did then. Some lies are load-bearing. Take them out and the whole roof comes down on the people underneath." They tossed the brass tube back to him; he caught it against his chest like something hot. "Keep writing your reports. Keep them false. But from tonight, you write them for us—we tell you what Dreven learns, and when. And when we reach Greywatch, we take your family back together. All of us. That's not charity and it's not forgiveness. It's arithmetic. We need a scribe who lies beautifully, and you need soldiers. Forty-one kindnesses, forty-second: we go get your children."

Tam did not remember crossing the space between his seat and the fire. He only knew that suddenly he was on his knees again, but differently this time, and that Astrid's hand was on his shoulder, heavy and warm, and that he was making a sound he had not made since he was a child—some raw, unguarded noise that had nothing to do with dignity.

"There'll be conditions," Sela said, because someone had to.

"Of course there will," Astrid said.

"One: he surrenders the cipher key to me tonight. Two: every report gets read aloud to the group before it's sealed. Three—" Sela's mouth curved, thin and sharp, "—when we hit Greywatch, he carries the heaviest pack."

"Agreed," Tam said, laughing and crying at once, which was undignified and which he found he no longer cared about. "Agreed to all of it."

Brannic grumbled something that might have been *told you so* held in reserve, but he also threw Tam his spare blanket, which in Brannic's language was a full pardon. Yara simply smiled at him across the fire, and Tam had the sudden, disorienting sense that she had known for days—that the tally in his chest had been audible to her all along, ticking away like a second heart, and that she had waited for him to find his own way to the truth rather than dragging him to it.

"Why?" he asked her quietly, later, when the others had settled. "Why didn't you say anything?"

Yara poked the fire, sending up a fan of sparks.

"Because being exposed isn't the same as being free," she said. "Believe me. I hear things I never asked to hear. The difference between a prisoner and a person is whether they chose to open the door." She glanced at him sidelong. "You opened it. That was yours. Nobody could give it to you."

Tam looked at the brass tube in his hands—at the instrument of three years of cowardice, now repurposed, its lies now belonging to the company that had chosen to keep him. Tomorrow they would bend their road north, toward Greywatch and Dreven's fort and whatever cell held Maren and Oskar and Ilse. Tomorrow he would begin writing the most important false reports of his life, with five people reading over his shoulder, and for the first time in three years the arithmetic would not be his alone to carry.

He was still turning this over, warm despite the frost, when Yara went suddenly rigid beside him.

Her head snapped west, toward the black line of the mountains, and her breath came out white and fast.

"What is it?" Tam whispered. "What do you hear?"

Yara's eyes were wide and shining in the firelight.

"Singing," she said. "From Greywatch. Hundreds of voices, all in chains, singing the old songs to keep their courage up." Her hand found Tam's wrist and gripped it hard. "Tam—one of the voices knows your name. She's singing *your* name, over and over, like a prayer."

The fire crackled. Somewhere far to the north, beneath the walls of a witch-finder's fort, a woman was singing her husband home.

"Maren," Tam said, and stood up, and the whole company rose with him.
