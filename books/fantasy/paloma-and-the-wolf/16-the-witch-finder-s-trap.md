## 16. The Witch-Finder's Trap

The river at Grayford was a brown ribbon of ice-edged water cutting through the last folds of the Thornfells, and the bridge that crossed it was older than any kingdom Astrid had ever sworn against or served. They had known it as children—Halvard's voice in their ear, *mind the third plank, little wren, it lies*—and they knew it now, standing in the treeline with Tam's map trembling in their hands, feeling the way the morning sat wrong on their shoulders.

"It's too quiet," Yara said.

She had been saying it since before dawn, and every hour she said it, the quiet grew teeth. The village of Grayford should have been stirring by now—smoke from cookfires, dogs, the clatter of the mill wheel. Instead there was nothing but the river's voice and the creak of the old bridge swaying on its stone piers, and a thin gray mist that lay along the water like something breathing.

"Maybe they evacuated," Brannic offered. He stood with his arms crossed, his soldier's bulk making the young alders around him look like twigs. "Word travels. Witch-finders burn a town, folk learn to run."

"Folk don't run and leave their washing on the line." Tam's voice was flat. He hadn't looked up from the map in some time. His finger rested on the little inked square that marked the millhouse where his family had been held these three months, and Astrid watched him press down until the fingertip went white. "They don't leave the door open, either. Look."

Astrid looked. The millhouse door hung wide, black and welcoming as a mouth.

*He knew,* Ondrys murmured, from somewhere beneath Astrid's ribs, in that voice like deep water over stones. *The scribe's masters are not fools. This is a snare.*

"I know," Astrid said aloud, and then realized no one had asked them anything, and that Yara was looking at them with those clear shrine-keeper's eyes that saw far too much.

"You hear him," she said quietly.

"He's always talking. It doesn't mean he's right."

"He's usually right about the ugly things." Yara hugged her cloak around herself. "That's what makes him dangerous. A liar you can discount. It's the honest devil that gets you."

Brannic spat into the bracken. "So we turn back. We go around—there's a ford two days north—"

"And by the time we get there, if there's anything left to rescue, it'll be gone, or worse." Tam folded the map with hands that were not quite steady. "I've seen what Dreven does with hostages he decides are compromised. I transcribed the orders once. I burned the memory after, but it doesn't burn clean." He looked up, and his face had the terrible calm of a man who has already died a little. "I'm going across. You all can—"

"We're not leaving you," Astrid said.

It came out harder than they meant it to. Harder than they'd meant to let anything come out for years. But Yara's hand found their sleeve, and Brannic grunted—the particular grunt that in Brannic's language meant agreement—and Sela, who had been silent at the edge of the group all morning, watching the bridge with her envoy's stillness, only nodded once.

"The company votes," Sela said. "We cross together."

Astrid thought, not for the first time, how strange it was—that this word, *company*, which they had spent thirty years avoiding in every sense, now sat in their chest like a coal they were afraid to drop and more afraid to quench.

They crossed the bridge single file, boots soft on frost-slick planks, the third one lying just as Halvard had warned them it lied thirty years ago. The mist thickened near the water. Halfway across, Astrid stopped.

The waystone at the bridge's far end—an old marker, pre-kingdom, carved with spirals worn nearly smooth—was bleeding.

Not blood. Sap, maybe. Red resin seeping from a fresh crack in the stone, running down its flank and dripping onto the frost. Astrid had passed this stone a hundred times over thirty years of smuggling. It had never cracked.

*Astrid.* Ondrys's voice, and for once there was something in it that might have been urgency. *Turn back. Turn back, little sword.*

"You don't get to call me that," Astrid whispered, and kept walking.

* * *

The trap sprang forty yards past the bridge, in the yard of the millhouse, and it sprang beautifully, because Dreven had learned.

There were no shouts, no challenge, none of the witch-finders' usual theater of writs and pronouncements. There was simply the world folding shut: crossbowmen rising from the millrace culverts, soldiers stepping out of the mist behind them in perfect silence, and the great iron nets weighted with lead shot dropping from the millhouse roof to ring the yard like a bell. Astrid heard Yara scream a warning and heard the scream cut short—a hand over her mouth, not violence but capture—and then the yard was full of men and the sky was full of crossbows and Captain Dreven walked out of the millhouse door wiping his hands on a rag as though he'd been doing chores.

"Hartwell." His voice was mild. That was the worst thing about Dreven, Astrid had learned long ago; the burning came later, but first there was always this mildness, this bookkeeper's courtesy. "You came. I confess I gave your sentimentality perhaps too much credit. And yet here you are, walking into my parlor for a scribe who sold you out months ago."

Tam made a sound like a man being flayed slowly. "You said—if I brought them—my family—"

"Your family is alive, scribe. Inasmuch as they have been." Dreven didn't even look at him. His pale eyes stayed on Astrid, and there was hunger in them now, banked and patient, the hunger of a man who had chased one quarry across half a kingdom and finally, finally had it cornered. "Bind the others. The Hartwell comes with me unbound. His Majesty wants the vessel whole."

*Now,* Ondrys said, and it was not a whisper anymore, it was a tide coming in, heat flooding up Astrid's arms, the old words rising in their throat unbidden—*let me, let me, give me the hands and I will paint this yard—*

"No," Astrid said through their teeth.

*They will kill the girl. They will kill them all. You cannot stop this alone—*

"I know." Astrid's knees wanted to bend. Their vision had gone gold at the edges, the world sharpening into that terrible clarity where every crossbowman became a line of angles and openings, where killing was suddenly the easiest language they had ever spoken. They could feel Ondrys pressing up against the inside of their skin like a face against glass. "But I'm going to anyway."

Dreven tilted his head. "Talking to yourself, Hartwell? Or talking to *it?*" He raised one finger. "Loose on the girl if the vessel resists. Wound only. His Majesty was specific."

And that—that specific, bureaucratic mercy, the order to keep Yara alive as leverage—did what thirty years of caution could not. Something in Astrid went very cold and very simple.

They moved before the crossbows did.

Not with Ondrys—with themselves. Thirty years of mountain paths and border fights and the thousand small violences of staying alive when the world wanted you dead. They closed the distance to the nearest crossbowman in three strides, took the weapon's stock and turned it, felt the bolt tear past their shoulder instead of through Yara's throat, and drove their elbow into the man's jaw hard enough to fold him. Then the yard was chaos—Brannic roaring through a net like it was cobweb, Sela moving with a knife-hand speed that reminded Astrid uncomfortably that envoys survived by more than diplomacy, Tam pressed flat against the mill wall with his hands over his head.

Astrid fought toward Yara. That was the whole of their thought, the whole of their self: *reach her, reach her, reach her.* A soldier's blade opened their forearm and they barely felt it. A second man got an arm around their neck and they dropped their weight, twisted, broke his grip and his nose with the same motion. The god inside them screamed frustration, offering, *offering*, pouring images of what they could do—one gesture and every man in the yard would kneel with their hearts stopped—

"I said *no*," Astrid gasped, to the god, to the yard, to everything, and reached Yara, and cut her bonds with their own knife while a crossbow bolt buried itself in the dirt between their feet.

Then the yard cleared its throat.

It happened the way weather happens. One moment there was fighting; the next, every witch-finder in Grayford had frozen, and the reason stood at the yard's edge with his sword drawn and his shield planted, and he was alone.

Brannic had seen the second wave coming—twenty men, maybe more, circling from the riverbank to cut off retreat across the bridge—and he had done the arithmetic soldiers do, the arithmetic nobody thanks them for. Twenty against five, with two of the five bound a minute ago and one of them the most valuable prisoner in the kingdom. Someone had to hold the gap. Someone had to make twenty men walk around him instead of through it.

"Go!" Brannic bellowed, and his voice cracked across the yard like a whip. He caught the first blow on his shield and answered it with a stroke that put the man down, and planted himself on the narrow ground between the well and the fence where only two could come at him at once. "GO, you stubborn mountain goats! This is what I'm FOR!"

"Brannic—" Yara's voice, raw.

"Girl." He grinned at her over his shield rim, blood already sheeting down his forehead from a scalp wound, grinning like the disgraced soldier who'd drunk his way out of Vaelport and found something worth sobering up for. "Tell them at home I died better than I lived. It won't be a lie."

Sela grabbed Yara's arm. Tam grabbed Yara's other arm. And Astrid stood in the middle of the yard with their heart tearing down the middle, looking at the big man holding the gate of the world closed with his body, and Brannic met their eyes and shook his head, once, slow.

*Go,* he mouthed. *Be enough.*

Astrid went.

* * *

They ran for the bridge, and the mist took them, and the fighting behind them became sound—steel, shouting, the groan of timber—and then, horribly, became less sound. Astrid did not let themselves think about what less sound meant. They ran with Yara's wrist locked in their grip and Sela at their rear calling the turns, and they were ten steps onto the bridge when the mist ahead of them resolved into a figure, and the figure was Dreven.

Of course. Of course he would not trust the net to hold. He had hunted them across a kingdom; he would want the ending in his own hand.

"Captain," Astrid said. They set Yara behind them and heard Sela draw steel somewhere to their left. The bridge planks creaked under six feet of frost and old wood and everything that was about to happen on top of it. "Your men are busy."

"Some of them." Dreven drew his own blade unhurried. Behind him, at the bridge's far end, a dozen crossbows leveled—but he lifted a hand, and they held. "His Majesty's instructions regarding the vessel remain. Alive. Whole." His pale eyes flicked to Astrid's bleeding forearm, the blood dripping from their fingers onto the ancient planks. "Though I confess, Hartwell, I begin to hope you resist. I've read the accounts of what your blood does to waystones. What it called up in the high pass." He smiled, thin as a paper cut. "I would very much like to see it."

"You'll be disappointed."

"Everyone says that first."

He came in fast—faster than a man his age had any right to move, and Astrid remembered, dimly, that witch-finder captains earned their rank by surviving things that killed everyone else. The first exchange rang their arm to the shoulder. The second opened a line of fire along their ribs. Dreven fought like a scholar who had memorized every book of violence ever written, and worse, he fought like a man who had studied *them*—every feint Astrid favored, every habit of thirty years catalogued and countered. By the fifth pass Astrid was backing up the bridge, giving ground they could not afford, and Dreven knew it.

"There it is," he murmured. "The famous swordspeaker form. Rusty. Afraid." Another stroke; Astrid caught it a hair from their throat. "Where is your god, Hartwell? I can see him in you—he's *right there*, behind your eyes. Why won't you let him out?"

*Because,* Astrid thought wildly, parrying, slipping on frost, tasting blood, *because if I open that door I will never find myself again, because I have seen what waits behind my own face—*

*Let me,* Ondrys said, gentle now, so gentle, the kindest voice in the world. *You are losing. Let me save them. Let me save her. You need only step aside. You need only rest.*

And the horrible thing—the thing Astrid would carry for the rest of their life, however long that proved to be—was how good it sounded. How much like surrendering a burden. Like sleep.

Their boot heel found the gap where the third plank lay false, and the world tipped.

They went down onto one knee on the old bridge, above the brown ice-edged water, and Dreven's blade rose against the gray sky, and Astrid had one heartbeat in which to choose.

They chose.

Not the god. Not the vast warm power coiled in their blood, not the easy victory, not the erasure dressed as rescue. They chose the ache in their arms and the fear in their gut and the thirty years of roads and the four people behind them and the one man behind them dying on a riverbank so that they could stand here. They chose the small, stubborn, mortal weight of exactly what they were.

Astrid rolled inside the descending stroke—not away, *inside*, the way Halvard had taught them with a stick on a mountainside when they were nine: *the space a strong man leaves is bigger than the space he takes*—and drove their knife up under Dreven's arm, into the armpit where no captain's armor reaches, and held on with both hands as the man stiffened, shuddered, and sagged against them with a sound almost like surprise.

For a moment they knelt there together on the bridge, the witch-finder and the vessel, breath smoking in the cold.

"Disappointed," Astrid rasped.

Dreven's mouth moved. It might have been a laugh. Then his weight went heavy, and Astrid lowered him to the planks and took his sword, because the crossbows at the bridge's end were still leveled, and because their legs were shaking so badly they needed something to lean on.

What happened next, they never fully pieced together. The crossbows never loosed. Later, Tam would tell them the second wave had broken—had simply stopped, dropped weapons, fled into the trees—and Yara would say, quietly, that she had felt the old stones of the bridge humming under her feet like struck bells, and that the river had seemed, briefly, to be paying attention. Astrid only knew that the mist thinned, and the road north lay open, and there was nothing between them and the trees but cold air and the sound of the water.

Behind them, downstream, the fighting had ended entirely.

They found Brannic at the gap by the well, sitting up against the fence with his shield across his knees, and for one impossible moment Astrid believed—because he was upright, because his eyes were open, because the bodies around him numbered eight and the wounds on him looked survivable.

Then they saw the spear.

It had gone in under his arm, the same place Astrid's knife had found Dreven, and come out his back, pinning him to the fence rail, and he had broken the shaft off to keep fighting, and he had kept fighting for a while after that. Long enough. Long enough for all of them.

"Hey, wren." His voice was a ruin, but the grin was still there, fainter now, banked like a fire going down. "Told you. Better than I lived."

"Don't talk." Astrid was on their knees beside him, hands pressed uselessly around the wound, blood everywhere, everywhere, warm and terrible. "Yara—YARA—"

She was already there, already kneeling, her hands glowing faintly with the shrine-light she carried, and Astrid saw her feel the truth of it before her face admitted it—saw the light gutter and steady and gutter again, trying to close what could not be closed.

"S'fine." Brannic's hand found Astrid's wrist, grip still shockingly strong. "Listen. Listen to me. You beat him without it." His eyes were very clear. "Without *him*. You understand? All this time you thought you needed—" A cough, red at his lips. "You never needed anything but the stubbornness. Be enough. You were. You are."

"Brannic—"

"Company of strays." He smiled at all of them—at Sela standing rigid with her jaw tight, at Tam with tears freezing on his face, at Yara with her failing light cupped in her palms like a bird. "Best commission I ever had. Don't waste it arguing with a dying man. Bury me facing the mountains."

He died with his hand on Astrid's wrist, as the mist came off the river and the morning finally, properly began, and the old bridge creaked behind them, and somewhere in the deep places of the world, Astrid felt Ondrys withdraw—not defeated, not silenced, but *patient*, the way winter is patient—and understood that the god had learned something today too.

That mortals could refuse him and win.

That made Astrid, in the long arithmetic of gods, either useless or priceless.

They buried Brannic facing the mountains, as he'd asked, with his shield over his chest and Yara's light laid on the cairn like a lamp for the road. Nobody spoke much. There was nothing to say that he hadn't already said better, grinning through his own blood.

It was Sela who finally broke the silence, standing at the treeline with her commission scroll in her hand, her face unreadable, looking north toward Vaelport.

"Astrid," she said. "There's something you need to know. About why the council really sent me."

Her fingers tightened on the scroll until the vellum creased.

"And you need to hear it before we take another step toward the free cities."
