## 17. Council of Knives

Sela had sat in the Council Hall of Vaelport a hundred times before—petitioning for trade concessions, arguing tariff schedules, once memorably defending a harbor pilot accused of smuggling salted fish past the customs chain. She knew the room's rhythms: the way the nine chairs of the council curved like a half-closed hand around whoever stood on the mosaic floor below, the way the light through the high windows fell deliberately on the petitioner's face and left the councilors in shadow. It was architecture as interrogation. She had always thought it clever.

Standing on that floor now, with Astrid Hartwell at her shoulder and Tam, Yara, and Brannic's memory between them like a fourth presence, she understood it was also architecture as trap. The hand did not close to hold you. It closed to crush.

"Speaker Hartwell." The eldest councilor, Magister Corven, had a voice like dry paper and eyes like wet ink. "The free cities thank you for your service."

*Service.* Sela kept her face still. She had written that word herself, months ago, in the commission she now carried folded against her heart. *Service.* What a gentle word for what they intended.

"The free cities owe you a debt," Corven continued, "and debts must be honored. But honor cuts both ways. You carry something in you, Speaker. Something the world has not seen in thirty years. Something King Veyric has already burned three towns trying to find."

"We are aware," Astrid said. Their voice was level, but Sela heard the weariness beneath it, the way a rope frays invisibly until one day it parts. "We are also aware of what he burned them *for*. I'd hoped Vaelport might be different."

"Different," repeated Councilor Ashe, a sharp-faced woman whose family fortune rested on the armories. "Yes. Different is precisely the word. Different from Lowmark. Different from Thornfell. Different from every place that faced Veyric's witch-finders with nothing but courage and graves to show for it." She leaned forward into the light. "You are not nothing, Speaker. You are the only thing in thirty years that has made that man afraid. And fear, properly harnessed, is worth more than ten thousand spears."

"There it is," Yara whispered beside Sela. The girl's hands were clenched in her skirts. "There it is."

Corven raised a hand for quiet. "The council proposes—and by ancient rite, requires—that you submit yourself to the Binding of Vaelport. Rites of containment older than the city itself. You will be housed in the Undercroft, warded day and night, your power held in trust. In exchange, you will be fed, protected, honored—" his thin smile suggested he believed the word still worked on people, "—and when Veyric comes, as come he will, you will be *released*. Aimed. A sword does not choose its swing, Speaker. That is what makes it a sword and not a madman."

The silence after was total. Somewhere above, gulls cried against the harbor wind.

Sela watched Astrid's face. She had grown fluent in it over these weeks on the road—the set of the jaw that meant old grief, the flicker at the eye-corner that meant Ondrys was speaking, the terrible patience of someone who had spent thirty years being underestimated. Now she watched something new: not surprise. Confirmation. Astrid had known. Perhaps had known since the pass, since the bridge, since some look she herself had failed to hide.

"You want to put me in a box," Astrid said quietly, "and open the box when it's convenient."

"We want to keep the free cities *alive*," Ashe snapped.

"And when Veyric is dead? When the war is won?" Astrid turned, slow, letting the question reach every chair. "Do you open the box then too? Or does a sword stay a sword?"

"Speaker," Corven said, with the gentleness of a man handling something explosive, "the alternative is that you walk out of this hall unbound, carrying a god in your blood, answerable to no law but your own conscience. Forgive us if that prospect does not comfort nine old people who have seen what gods do to the world."

"I have seen it too," Astrid said. "Closer than you ever will. That's rather my point."

It was then that Sela moved—not toward Astrid but forward, alone, into the center of the light where all nine faces could see her clearly. Her heart was hammering. She had rehearsed nothing. There was nothing to rehearse; she had only understood, in the last hour, in the last minute, standing in that beautiful treacherous room, which side of the closing hand she stood on.

"Magister Corven. Councilors." Her voice carried better than she expected. "I am Sela Voss, envoy of the Concord of Free Cities, and I hold commission under seal to deliver the swordspeaker to this council." She drew the folded document from inside her coat. "I should tell you what else that commission says. Article Seven. In the event the Speaker proves... uncooperative, the envoy is authorized to negotiate the Speaker's transfer to King Veyric's agents—as a bargaining piece. As a *sacrifice*, if it came to it. Alive, if possible. The council judged a living swordspeaker in Veyric's hands preferable to a free one in anyone's."

Murmurs ran the curve of chairs. Corven's expression did not change, which told her everything: he had known. He had signed it.

"You would sell them to the man burning shrines," Yara said, loud, furious, her voice cracking across the marble. "You'd hand them to the witch-finders—"

"To save forty thousand lives in this city, girl, yes," Ashe said coldly. "That is what governance *is*."

"No," Sela said. "That is what surrender is, wearing governance's clothes." She held the commission up. "I have walked every mile of Thornfell with this person. I watched them refuse a god's bargain that would have won the war in a night. I watched them kill Dreven with their own two hands, mortal and bleeding, because they would not take the easy power. I watched Brannic die holding a bridge so strangers could escape, and I watched Astrid carry his body four miles because no one else would." Her voice steadied, hardened. "This person has more honor in their little finger than this entire council has in its vaults. And you propose to lock them in a cellar and call it statecraft."

"Envoy Voss," Corven said warningly, "you are out of order."

"Yes," she agreed. "I believe I am."

She struck flint from her belt kit—a smuggler's habit, fire always ready—and touched it to the corner of the commission. The parchment caught with a soft breath, orange light climbing the seal of the Concord, curling the elegant script of Article Seven into black lace. She held it until the flame kissed her fingers, then let the last of it fall and grind it under her boot heel on the mosaic floor, ash and ember smeared across the painted waves of Vaelport's founding.

"I resign my commission," she said. "Effective now. I am no longer the council's envoy. I am Astrid Hartwell's friend, and I find the second position considerably more demanding and considerably less shameful."

For a moment, no one breathed.

Then Corven sighed, an old, tired sound, and Sela saw the man beneath the magister—not cruel, just frightened, just small, just a steward trying to keep a lamp lit in a hurricane with whatever fuel lay to hand. "Then you make yourself an exile, Envoy. The gates will not open for you again."

"They didn't open for Brannic either," Sela said. "Or for Lowmark. Gates open for the people who need them least. I've noticed that about gates."

Astrid's hand found her shoulder—light, brief, real. Neither of them looked at the other. Looking would have undone them both.

"Speaker Hartwell," Corven said at last. "The offer stands. The Undercroft is warm, and the wards are kind, as such things go. Think on it. Veyric's armies are not a debate. They do not require your agreement to arrive."

"They never do," Astrid said. "That's how you know them."

They walked out together—Sela, Astrid, Tam with his scribe's stoop and his haunted eyes, Yara with her chin up and tears running freely down her face and no attempt whatsoever to hide them—past the guards who would not meet their gaze, down the great stairs, out into the salt wind and the noise of the harbor, where the city went on mending nets and haggling over fish as though the world were not tilting beneath it.

Only at the bottom of the steps did Astrid speak.

"You burned your life back there."

"It was already on fire," Sela said. "I just made it official."

And Astrid Hartwell, last of the swordspeakers, vessel of a broken god, laughed—a short, cracked, astonished sound, the first true laugh Sela had ever heard from them—and pulled her into an embrace that smelled of road dust and woodsmoke and, faintly, underneath everything, of lightning before a storm.

* * *

Astrid stood on the seawall past midnight, unable to sleep, watching the dark water and the lights of the fishing fleet and feeling Ondrys stir beneath their ribs like a sleeper turning over.

*They offered you a cage,* the god murmured. Not hungry tonight. Almost conversational. Thirty years of silence, and now it was as if the god had always been there, a lodger who had simply been very polite about unpacking. *And your friend burned her freedom rather than see you in it. Strange creatures, mortals. They build boxes for everything they love.*

"They build boxes for everything they're afraid of," Astrid said aloud. "Loving is incidental."

*Is that what you believe about Halvard?*

"Don't." The word came out flat and hard. "Don't use him."

*I say only what is true. He hid you in a box too. A mountain instead of a cellar. Silence instead of wards. And you lived in it thirty years and called it freedom because no one had ever shown you the difference.* The god's presence shifted, vast and patient, like weather deciding. *Veyric builds boxes. Your council builds boxes. Your dead mentor built boxes. Even your friend, bless her burning heart—she would build you a box of loyalty, if you let her. Everyone wants to keep you somewhere, little speaker. I am the only one who offers you the whole sky.*

"That's because the sky is where you live," Astrid said. "Not me."

They flexed their hands against the seawall stone, feeling the grain of it, the salt-crusted roughness, the honest dumb solidity of a thing that was only itself. Below, a fishing boat came in late, lanterns swinging, and a woman's voice carried across the water cursing her brother's laziness in terms that would have made a shrine-keeper blush. Ordinary. Human. Unboxed.

Yara found them there—or perhaps had been sent, or perhaps simply could not sleep either; with her, hearing everything, sleep was probably a rumor. She climbed onto the wall beside them, small and stubborn against the wind.

"They're going to come here," she said. Not a question. "Veyric. I've been listening to the city pray. Half of them are praying for safety and half are praying for profit and none of them are praying for what's actually coming. But the gods hear it anyway, and the gods are..." She hesitated, choosing. "Restless. Like dogs before weather. He'll march within the month. Maybe sooner."

"Then we have a month."

"A month to do what?" She looked up at them, and in the lantern-light from the quay her face was very young and very old at once. "Run again? Hide again? Because I love you, Astrid, but I've watched you pack your bag twice since we got here. Old habit. You keep one foot out the door even when the door's the safest place in the world."

Astrid opened their mouth to deny it and found, honestly, nothing to say. The bag *was* half-packed. It had been half-packed since Lowmark burned.

"Maybe," they said at last, "we stop running. Maybe we stand on the wall when they come. Not as a god. Not as a weapon. Just as people who live here now."

Yara smiled, wiped her nose on her sleeve, and leaned her head briefly against their shoulder. "The council's going to hate that."

"The council can get in line behind the god," Astrid said. "Everyone wants to tell me what I am. I'm starting to think I'd like to find out for myself."

Far out on the black water, lightning flickered without thunder, once, twice—like something far away opening its eyes. Or like something much closer, counting.

* * *

King Veyric did not sleep either. Sleep had left him years ago, the way a tenant leaves a house slowly, room by room, until one night the windows are dark and the locks changed.

He stood in the map room of his fortress at Cael Duren, alone except for the candles and the dead. The dead were everywhere in this room, though invisible: his father, who had taught him that order was the only wall between men and wolves; his mother, who had died praying to a god that never answered and never cared; and most of all Aldric, his brother, twenty years gone, who had screamed for six hours in the fire pit while Veyric stood at the edge and made himself watch, because a king who could not watch could not rule, and because some part of him had needed to know—had needed *proof*—that the thing wearing his brother's face was not his brother anymore.

It had spoken to him, near the end. In Aldric's voice. *Veyric. Brother. Why?*

Thirty years, and he could still hear it.

Now the reports lay spread across the table like a hand of losing cards. Thornfell sealed. The passes held, barely. Dreven dead—Dreven, his best hound, killed on a bridge by a single swordspeaker, and the witch-finder companies that survived the pass telling stories no sane officer should permit them to tell. Stories of a guardian in the stones. Of a warrior who fought like the old ballads said the speakers fought, impossible and beautiful and wrong. Of a child who heard gods. Of shrines waking from the Marches to the sea.

And now this: a rider in from his agents in Vaelport, mud-stiff and half-dead from the ride, reporting that the free cities had a swordspeaker in their walls and had, in their merchant wisdom, done nothing sensible with them at all.

"They'll bind her eventually," muttered Lord-Castellan Ossory behind him, gray and careful. "The free cities talk big, Majesty, but when the knives are at the purse-strings they always choose survival. Give them time."

"Time is precisely what the gods are giving *us*," Veyric said, "and I do not think it is a gift."

He traced the map with one finger. Vaelport sat on its promontory like a fat coin on a table's edge, rich, proud, undefended by anything but money and distance. Distance was failing. Money would follow.

His counselors wanted caution. Siege trains took seasons; the treasury groaned; the eastern lords grumbled at levies. All true. All irrelevant. Because Veyric had seen what happened when the old powers woke. He had stood at a fire pit and listened to his brother's voice come out of the smoke, and he had learned the one lesson that governed his life: *what the gods touch, they consume, and they begin with the ones you love.* Every shrine that woke was a door. Every believer was a handle. Every miracle was a hook baited with wonder, and wonder was how it started, wonder was how Aldric had started, a boy who prayed too earnestly at a cracked stone until something answered and moved in and wore him like a coat.

He would burn every door. Break every handle. Salt every stone. If the cost was towns, then towns. If the cost was his soul—well. His soul had been in the fire pit too, in a sense. It had screamed along with Aldric, and what crawled out afterward was sufficient for ruling, if not for sleeping.

"Majesty?" Ossory again.

"We march on Vaelport," Veyric said. "All of it. Everything we have. Within the month."

Ossory paled. "The siege train alone—"

"Will move faster if you stop explaining why it cannot." Veyric straightened, and in the candlelight his shadow climbed the map-room wall, long and thin and crowned. "Understand me, Castellan. This is not conquest. I take no pleasure in it and expect no glory from it. Vaelport is not a prize. It is an infection site. A swordspeaker loose in the richest city on the coast, with a population primed for miracles like a field primed for fire." His finger came down on the painted harbor, pressing hard enough to whiten the knuckle. "If the old gods rise again, they rise first there. So that is where we go. We do not besiege Vaelport. We *excise* it."

"And the swordspeaker?"

"Alive, if the gods are merciful." His mouth twisted. "Which they are not. Dead, then, and burned, and the ashes scattered at sea where nothing can grow. And if any of my officers hesitates—if any of them looks at that creature and sees anything but an abomination wearing a person—they may remember that I burned my own brother, and that I would burn them too, and that I slept well afterward."

He had not slept well afterward. He had not slept at all. But Ossory nodded, gray-faced, and went to give the orders, and the fortress began to wake around Veyric like a body around a wound: trumpets, hooves, the groan of the great gates, torches blooming along the walls.

Alone in the map room, the king gathered the candles close and looked for a long time at the little painted mountains of Thornfell, where it had all begun, where a child had been hidden in a box by a coward who loved them.

"Aldric," he said quietly, to the empty room, in the voice of a boy of nineteen. "I'm still watching. I haven't stopped watching. Whatever comes out of the dark this time—I'll see it coming. I swear it."

The candle flames bowed, all together, all in the same direction, as though something in the room had leaned close to listen.

Then they righted themselves, and the king snuffed them one by one, and outside, under a moon like a scythe, the armies of Veyric began to move west.
