## 18. The Siege Begins

The horns began at dawn.

Astrid stood on the seaward wall of Vaelport with the salt wind pulling at their coat, and watched the horizon fill. Not all at once—Veyric's host came like a tide deciding, banners unrolling across the hills in ranks of white and iron-grey, the sun glinting off spearpoints until the whole eastern sky seemed to be made of broken glass. They had seen armies before, from a distance, in thirty years of keeping to the edges of other people's wars. They had never seen one arrive for them.

"Counting?" Sela asked beside her, arms crossed against the wind.

"No point."

"Good. I stopped at eleven thousand." Her voice was flat as slate, but Astrid heard what moved underneath it—the envoy's careful arithmetic failing her for the first time since they'd met. "We have four hundred militia, two hundred harbor hands with whatever they could swing, and Brannic's old company, or what's left of it. Thirty-one."

Astrid said nothing. There was nothing to say that the numbers didn't say better.

Below them, the city was waking into fear. Smoke rose from a thousand hearths—cooking fires, because people still had to eat even at the end of the world—and between the smoke columns Astrid could see the streets moving with bodies: families hauling goods toward the granaries, carpenter crews nailing shutters over windows, children being herded inland toward the temple district where the walls were thickest. Somewhere a dog was barking itself hoarse.

And in the square below the wall, Yara was standing on the dry fountain's rim, speaking to a crowd of perhaps two hundred, and Astrid found their eyes going to her the way a compass finds north.

She wasn't shouting. That was the thing about Yara—they'd learned this crossing half of Thornfell—that she never shouted. She stood small and straight in her dead mother's shrine-keeper's shawl, and she spoke at the volume of someone explaining something important to a friend, and somehow two hundred frightened people went quiet enough to hear her.

"They want you praying *scared*," she was saying as Astrid and Sela came down the wall stairs. "That's what burning shrines is. It's not hate—it's farming. You burn a god out of a people by making prayer feel like drowning. So don't do it scared."

An older woman near the front, a fishwife with forearms like dock rope, called up: "Then what, girl? You want us to pray *happy*? With that—" she jerked her chin eastward at the host on the hills— "*that* coming?"

"I want you to pray like it's yours," Yara said. "Not his. Not theirs. Not some god's. Yours. Tonight, at sundown, everyone who wants to—light a candle in your window. Any window. Say whatever you want to say, to whoever you think might be listening, or to nobody. Don't kneel. Don't beg. Just speak." She paused, and something passed over her face that Astrid recognized—the flicker of a girl who heard far too much, choosing which of it to share. "I think it matters. I can't tell you why yet. But I think it matters more than the walls."

* * *

They spent the day preparing, and the city taught Astrid things about courage they hadn't known.

In the armory under the east gate, they watched a one-eyed smith named Corla fit militia pikes with boat-hooks and billhooks because there weren't enough proper heads to go around, humming an old work-song the whole time, steady as a loom. In the granary yard, harbor hands practiced passing water buckets down a chain because the cisterns were shallow and fire was coming—one way or another, fire was coming—and they joked while they drilled, terrible jokes, the kind men make when laughing is cheaper than screaming. A boy no older than fourteen followed Tam around the map room asking questions about siege engines with the hungry precision of someone who intended to live through this if it killed him.

Tam himself had gone grey around the mouth. Astrid knew why. Every report Tam had ever fed Dreven, every false location, every carefully poisoned line of intelligence—it had bought time, but time was spending itself now, and the debt was coming due in the shape of eleven thousand soldiers. He worked anyway, hunched over the city plans, marking sewer grates and cistern mouths in ink that smudged his fingers black. When Astrid set a cup of broth beside him without a word, he stared at it like he'd forgotten food existed.

"The main sump runs under the east field," he said finally, tapping the plan. "Where they'll stage the engines. The Vaelport engineers built it big, back when they thought the city would grow. It doesn't grow anymore, but nobody filled it in."

"You've been down there?"

"This morning. Before dawn." His jaw worked. "It smells like the bottom of the world, but it's dry enough to walk, and it comes up within forty yards of where any sane commander would put his trebuchets. There are three grate mouths. Iron, old, but the bars are thin. We could cut them."

"We," Astrid repeated.

Tam wouldn't look up. "Brannic held a bridge so we could run. I ran. I'm done running behind other people." Now he did look up, and his eyes were wet and furious and absolutely certain. "You're going. I know you're going. Let me come."

Astrid looked at him a long moment—the scribe who had betrayed them and been forgiven, whose family was still somewhere in Veyric's baggage train, whose hands shook and didn't stop working anyway.

"Bring your ink," they said. "You're our map. And bring a knife you've actually used."

* * *

Sundown came, and with it, the candles.

Astrid stood on the wall again to watch it happen, and felt something move through their chest that they had no clean name for. One candle first—in a fisherman's shack by the harbor—then three, then twenty, then streets of them, gold light kindling window by window through the darkening city like a slow contagion of hope. No chanting. No kneeling. From the wall Astrid couldn't hear the words, only the murmur, hundreds of voices speaking low and private into the dusk, each one different, none of them begging.

Yara stood at the center of it, in the square, and Astrid saw her lift her face and go utterly still—the stillness of someone listening to a sound no one else can hear.

Later, in the war-room that had been a merchant's parlor, she found Astrid alone over the maps.

"It's not Ondrys," she said, before Astrid could ask. Her voice was careful, the way it always was when she carried something heavy. "What's gathering tonight. It's not him. It's not any of the old ones, either—it's smaller and it's... wider? Like a hundred little flames instead of one bonfire." She pressed her hand flat against her sternum. "It feels like the mountain paths. Do you remember? How they shifted for you. Like that. But it's shifting for *everyone*, tonight."

Astrid's throat was tight. "Yara—if Ondrys hears that—"

"He already knows." She met their eyes steadily. "He's furious. Or frightened—I can't always tell those apart with him. Something about free prayer offends him down to the root. Which tells you everything, doesn't it?" A ghost of her mother's smile. "Go cut your irons, Astrid. We'll keep the lights burning."

* * *

They went down into the dark at moonrise: Astrid, Sela, Tam, and twelve volunteers from Brannic's old company, each carrying a satchel of lamp-oil thickened with pitch, and slow-matches coiled inside oilskin. Corla the smith came too, one-eyed and humming, lugging a pry-bar nearly her own height.

The sewer swallowed them by degrees—first the smell, then the light, then the sounds of the city, until there was only the slop of their own boots and the drip of water counting seconds in the black. Tam led by memory and by the chalk marks he'd made at dawn, calling turns in a whisper: "Left at the junction. Mind the drop, it's blind." Once, something living rustled away from their lantern-light, and a young soldier made a sound like a laugh strangled at birth, and after that nobody made any sound at all.

Two hours they walked beneath the earth of a city about to die, and Astrid thought about how strange it was that the worst night of a place's life could also hold moments like this—twelve strangers trusting one another's footing in total darkness because a nervous scribe had told them to turn left.

Then Tam stopped and raised his fist.

Ahead, faint but unmistakable: daylight. Grey, cold, filtered through iron bars, and beyond the bars—the enemy.

They crept forward single file. The grate opened onto a drainage culvert hidden in a fold of ground, screened by gorse, looking out across the east field. And there it was: the staging ground. Trebuchets in rows like sleeping insects, their long arms folded against the stars; wains of stone shot; tents pitched among them; picket fires dotting the dark. Maybe six hundred men within a bowshot, most of them asleep, none of them dreaming that the city above them had teeth underneath its streets.

"Three engines closest to us," Sela breathed, reading the field like she read everything, fast and cold. "Burn those and they'll rebuild in days—but days matter now. Fire spreads to the shot-wains. Stone doesn't burn, but the frames do, and the ropes, and the tents." She looked at Astrid. "Your call. You're the guide."

For one heartbeat—one long, cold heartbeat—Astrid felt the other presence stir, deep in their blood, patient and vast and *interested*. *Let me,* it whispered, without words exactly, more like heat rising. *I have burned greater things than wood.*

*No,* Astrid answered, and the word was a door slammed and barred. *Tonight we do it the human way. Slowly. Badly. Together.*

They cut the bars with files and cold-chisels, taking an hour they could barely afford, muffling every stroke with cloth. Then they went out into the enemy's night like ghosts with satchels.

It went wrong almost immediately, because it always does.

A sentry, walking his rounds, stopped ten feet from where Astrid lay flattened in the shadow of a trebuchet's wheel, and stood there, and listened. Astrid could hear him breathing. Could smell woodsmoke on his cloak. Behind them, Sela had her knife out and ready, and Astrid shook their head once—*no, not unless we must*—and the sentry stood, and stood, and then yawned enormously, muttered something about the cold, and walked on.

They worked then, fast and silent: oil soaked into axles and rope-lines and the great timber spars, slow-match touched to oilskin, and the first trebuchet caught with a sound like a drawn breath. By the time the alarm went up—a bell-clanging, shouting chaos of men pouring from tents—the second engine was well alight and the third was burning from the inside, and the fire had found the shot-wain's ropes exactly as Sela promised, and the east field was turning orange.

They ran. Back through the culvert, down the sump, twelve people and their smith stumbling laughing and gasping through the dark while the world above turned to glare, and when they finally climbed out into the city's streets, the whole population seemed to be on the walls watching the enemy's engines burn, and the cheering started somewhere near the harbor and rolled uphill like weather.

Astrid stood in the crowd, filthy to the knees, smelling of sewer and pitch, and let themselves be pounded on the back by strangers. Beside them, Tam was crying and pretending he wasn't. Sela wore an expression Astrid had never seen on her—something young, quickly hidden.

But high on the wall, alone, Astrid looked east, past the burning field, to where the king's pavilion stood untouched amid the chaos, its banner limp in the windless dark. And they felt Ondrys watching through their own eyes, tasting the fire, and saying nothing at all—which was worse than anything the god had ever said.

And then, from the direction of the royal camp, a single horn blew—not the short notes of alarm, but one long note, sustained and rising, a sound with intent in it, and along the entire eastern ridge a thousand watch-fires began to go out at once, all together, deliberate as a held breath.

The siege was not beginning.

It was changing shape.
