## 19. Where Astrid Ends

The wall had held for six days, and on the seventh it began to die.

Astrid felt it the way a rider feels a horse going lame beneath them—a wrongness in the rhythm of things. The stones of Vaelport's eastern rampart were not crumbling so much as *grieving*, mortar dust sifting down like grey snow, and through the smoke-haze they could see Veyric's siege towers crawling closer across the burnt fields, patient as beetles.

They had been in the breach-fighting since dawn. Somewhere behind them Yara's voice rose and fell with the crowd prayer—hundreds of frightened people speaking together, no god named, no rite demanded, just words given freely into the dark—and Astrid clung to that sound like a rope thrown into floodwater. It was the only thing that kept Ondrys quiet.

Mostly.

*You are bleeding,* the god observed, from the warm dark behind their ribs. *You have been bleeding since the second hour.*

"Shut up," Astrid muttered, and drove their sword through the visor slit of a witch-finder who came over the rubble too fast to stop.

The man folded. Another took his place. The breach was a throat and they were all being swallowed down it, defenders and attackers alike, and Astrid fought the way they had learned to fight over thirty years of mountain roads: economically, viciously, without glory. A parry that broke a wrist. A pommel to the temple. Back up the rampart steps, shoulder screaming where a crossbow bolt had gone through the mail links and torn the muscle beneath.

The bolt was still in them. They could feel its barbs every time they raised their arm.

*Let me,* Ondrys said. Not a command. Worse than a command—an offer, gentle as a hand extended across a table. *One hour. I will ask nothing after. You know this is true; you have always known when I lie, and I am not lying now.*

"I said shut up."

*And you will keep saying it,* the god replied, with something like sorrow, *until you cannot say anything at all.*

* * *

It happened at noon, or what would have been noon if the smoke had let any sun through.

The second breach opened at the salt-gate, and the fighting there collapsed all at once, the way a roof collapses—one beam goes and then everything goes. Astrid heard Brannic's name called by men who no longer had the right to call it, heard Tam shouting orders in his old witch-finder cadence, heard Sela's voice cracked raw from command. And they saw, past the press of bodies, the first of Veyric's soldiers reaching the inner street where the civilians had been herded—the old ones, the children, Yara standing in front of them with her arms spread and her shrine-keeper's pendant raised like it could stop steel.

Astrid did not decide. That was the thing they would remember afterward, the thing that shamed them most: there was no decision. There was only the bolt grinding in their shoulder, and the child's face beyond the soldier's arm, and the vast patient presence inside them turning toward the light like a flower toward sun—and Astrid *opened*.

Not a door. A dam.

*Yes,* Ondrys said, and the world went white.

* * *

Later—much later, though "later" is a word that belongs to creatures who live in time—Astrid would try to describe what it was like inside the god, and they never managed it. Language was made by small things for small things. But they remembered fragments, the way a drowning person remembers fragments of the river.

They remembered size.

Ondrys was not large the way a mountain is large. Mountains have edges. The god had no edges anywhere. Astrid fell—or rose, or unfolded—into a space where thought itself had room to stretch, where a single one of Ondrys's memories was longer than the whole history of mortals, and Astrid's own self, which had always felt too big for their body, suddenly felt like a coin dropped into an ocean. Small. Bright for a moment. Sinking.

*I have missed this,* the god said, and the saying of it moved through Astrid like weather. *To be carried again. To look out through eyes instead of remembering eyes. Do you know how long I have been soil, little sword? How long I have been seed?*

Astrid tried to answer and discovered they had no mouth here. They were a passenger in a body that still stood on the wall of Vaelport—they could see it, dimly, the way you see a lit window from far away—but the hands holding the sword were not deciding anything anymore. The hands were simply moving, and the movement was beautiful, and that was the horror of it.

The soldiers at the breach died quickly. Astrid watched from very far away as the body they had lived in for forty years turned Ondrys's ancient skill loose among them—not the desperate economy of mountain fighting but something else, something that had ended wars, something that had once been worshipped with rivers of blood. Every motion perfect. Every kill clean. And underneath the watching, underneath the coin sinking in the ocean, a terrible warmth began to spread, because some part of Astrid—the part that was still Astrid—felt the perfection and *wanted* it.

*There,* Ondrys murmured, noticing. The god noticed everything; Astrid understood now that being noticed by a god was not like being seen. It was like being *held*. *You feel it. This is what your mentor feared and what he prayed for, both at once. This is what oaths are for—to hold a shape around a thing like me. Your ancestors were not my servants, Astrid Hartwell. They were my borders.*

*Show me,* Astrid did not say, but the god heard it anyway, because inside Ondrys there was no difference between thinking and asking.

The god showed them.

* * *

The war came back.

Not as story—as *weather*. Astrid stood inside a memory ten thousand years deep and felt the sky itself at war: gods walking on the young world like giants wading a stream, and mortals below, numberless, brief, burning like mayflies in the grass. He had thought—he had always assumed—that the old war was gods against mortals. That was the version in the songs, the version Halvard had half-told, the version Veyric burned shrines over.

It was worse. It was gods against gods, and mortals were the ground it was fought on.

He felt Ondrys raise a hand—felt it from inside the hand, from inside the intention—and a city that had worshipped a rival god simply ceased. Not destroyed. *Ceased*, unmade backward, its whole history folding up like a fan, every person who had ever lived in it becoming a moment that had never happened. And Ondrys had felt—Astrid felt it now, the god's own memory of the act—Ondrys had felt *nothing*. No more than a man feels sweeping a floor. The mortals had been so small. So brief. So many.

*We loved you,* Astrid thought at him, uselessly, furiously, a coin screaming at the ocean. *We built the shrines. We sang the songs. We loved you and we were nothing to you.*

*You were everything,* Ondrys answered, and the terrible thing was that he meant it, that the love was real, vast and real and utterly without weight, the way a storm loves the field it flattens. *Every prayer was a jewel. I kept them all. I am keeping yours still—all thirty years of them, the ones you didn't say, the ones you swallowed. Little sword, I have been starving in the dark of your blood, and even your refusals have fed me.*

And Astrid understood, then, with a clarity that felt like dying: this was what surrender meant. Not death. Death was small and mortal and theirs. Surrender meant becoming a jewel in a hoard. It meant their love and their grief and their stubborn thirty years of survival all polished and shelved inside something that would cherish them forever and never once see them. It meant Yara's face and Sela's crooked smile and Tam's shaking hands and the taste of snowmelt on the high passes—all of it gathered up, adored, erased.

The body on the wall was still fighting. Below, the breach was clearing; soldiers fled from something they could not name. And Ondrys, feeling Astrid's horror, misread it beautifully, generously, as awe.

*Rest now,* the god said. *I will finish what you could not. I will break the usurper's host before dusk, and the free cities will sing of this day, and you need do nothing ever again. Nothing. Isn't that what you wanted, all these lonely years? To finally put it down?*

And oh, it was. That was the knife of it. Thirty years of carrying everything alone, and here was a voice offering to carry it all, forever, kindly.

Astrid felt their grip on themselves thinning. The coin was almost at the bottom of the ocean. Their name—it was getting hard to remember their name—

"Astrid."

Small. Far away. Coming from the lit window.

"*Astrid.*"

Yara's voice, cracked with smoke, ragged with fear, calling not the way prayers were called—not begging, not worshipping—calling the way you call someone home for supper. Calling their *name*. Not swordspeaker. Not vessel. Not god-fragment. Just Astrid, who liked their tea bitter and hummed off-key on the road and had once spent a whole evening teaching Tam to skip stones because he'd never done it as a boy.

*Astrid Eriksen Hartwell, you come back here.*

The coin stopped sinking.

It was not strength that brought them back. It was smaller than strength. It was the sheer stubborn particularity of being known—being known *wrongly*, even, known in all the ordinary human dimensions a god could never reach—and Astrid seized that smallness like a handhold on a cliff face and pulled.

*Stay,* Ondrys said, and for the first time in ten thousand years, the god sounded afraid. *Little sword. Stay. You will die down there. You are already dying. I can save—*

"That," Astrid said aloud, with a mouth, with blood in it, on a wall full of corpses and smoke, "*is exactly why I'm leaving.*"

They closed the dam.

* * *

The pain arrived all at once, honestly, wonderfully mortal. The bolt in their shoulder. The gash along their ribs. Knees hitting stone. Hands that shook. Breath that tore. Astrid knelt on the rampart of Vaelport among the dead and laughed until it turned into something else, and Yara was there—when had she crossed the breach, someone should never have let her cross the breach—holding their face in both small hands, looking into their eyes with the fierce unblinking attention of someone checking a lamp flame.

"You're you," she said. "Say something only you'd say."

"The tea here is terrible," Astrid rasped. "Even the good tea. Especially the good tea."

Yara burst into tears and pressed her forehead against theirs.

Around them the assault was breaking apart—not routed, not yet, but faltering, the attack losing its teeth the way attacks do when the impossible has happened at the wall. Sela was shouting reorganization in a voice like a whip. Tam was dragging wounded behind the merlons. Somewhere below, the crowd-prayer had stopped, and in its silence Astrid could hear the ordinary sounds of a living city: hammering, weeping, a dog barking, someone singing to calm a child.

All of it small. All of it brief. All of it worth more than every jewel in the god's hoard.

*You will come back to me,* Ondrys said from behind the dam, quiet now, patient again, patient as stone. *When the pain is enough. When the loss is enough. I can wait, little sword. Waiting is the one thing I do better than loving.*

Astrid looked down at their own trembling, bloodied hand and closed it into a fist.

"No," they said softly. "Waiting is the one thing I do better than you."

But the god's laughter followed them down off the wall, low and fond and terribly sure, and Astrid knew—with the knowledge of someone who had seen the bottom of the ocean—that the next time the dam cracked, there might be no voice small enough, close enough, human enough to call them back.

Unless they ended it first.

Unless they found the rite Halvard had died hiding, and used it before the walls fell—or before they did.
