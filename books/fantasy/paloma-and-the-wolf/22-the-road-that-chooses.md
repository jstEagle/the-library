## 22. The Road That Chooses

Spring came late to Thornfell, the way it always did—creeping up from the valleys in slow green increments while the high passes still wore their winter like a stubborn old man refusing to take off his coat. Astrid stood at the mouth of the Wolf's Throat with the morning sun on their face and did not flinch from it.

That was new. A year ago—a lifetime ago—they would have kept to the shadow side of the trail, hood up, eyes down, one more gray figure dissolving into gray stone. Now they stood in the open, hands loose at their sides, and let the light find them.

The path had shifted again in the night.

Astrid noticed it the way an old sailor notices a change in wind: not by looking for it but by feeling its absence. The rockfall that had blocked the lower switchback since before the war was gone—not cleared, not moved, simply *gone*, as if the mountain had swallowed it whole and offered instead a smooth stretch of trail wide enough for six travelers abreast. The scree slope that had cost them two days and Brannic's memory last autumn now bent gently eastward, curving around the worst of the climb like a hand cupped to shelter a candle flame.

"Doing it again," Tam said behind them. He had his ledger out—he always had his ledger out—and was making notes with the quick, guilty pleasure of a scribe who no longer had to hide what he wrote. "Third time this week. The pass near Hollow Shrine rerouted itself around the meltwater. The ford at Coldbrook's shallower than it has any right to be in spring runoff. And this." He gestured at the vanished rockfall with his pencil. "Mountains don't do this."

"Mountains don't pray either," Astrid said. "And yet here we are."

They heard Yara laughing before they saw her. She came up the trail with her boots muddy to the knee and her braid full of pine needles, carrying an armload of early wildflowers she'd no doubt pulled from some shrine niche where they'd seeded themselves. Behind her, Sela walked with the measured stride of a woman who had learned to read terrain the hard way, and behind *her* trailed the three travelers they were guiding this season—a wool merchant, a young couple bound for Vaelport, and a nervous scholar who kept asking questions about the ruins and writing down everything Astrid said as though it might be scripture.

Astrid had stopped correcting him weeks ago. It was easier to let people believe what comforted them.

"The ford's safe," Yara announced, dumping flowers into Astrid's arms without asking. "Coldbrook's running clear. I asked it nicely."

"You *asked* it nicely," the wool merchant repeated faintly. He was still adjusting to the fact that the girl who shared his campfire could hear water speaking.

"She did," Astrid confirmed gravely. "It's politer than most people."

Sela reached them and stood shoulder to shoulder with Astrid, looking out over the valley below—the green spill of Thornfell's foothills, the thread of the trade road, the distant smoke of Lowmark rising clean and straight into a blue sky. Rebuilt, half of it. The other half still charred, because some things should not be forgotten just because they've been forgiven.

"You're smiling," Sela observed.

"I'm not."

"You are. It's terrible. Do it more often."

Astrid snorted and handed the flowers back to Yara, who was watching them both with the particular smugness of a nineteen-year-old who had spent a year listening to gods and grown-ups and had concluded there wasn't much difference.

* * *

They made camp that night in a hollow beneath the old waystone—the same one, Astrid realized with a strange quiet feeling, where their blood had woken the guardian all those months ago. The stone stood weathered and patient, its carvings worn nearly smooth, and nothing about it felt hungry anymore. It felt, if anything, *content*. Like a dog sleeping in the sun.

Tam built the fire. Yara laid a sprig of rowan against the waystone's base—"not worship," she insisted when the scholar raised his eyebrows, "just manners"—and Sela dealt out dried meat and hardtack with the efficiency of a woman who had once provisioned a diplomatic mission and never quite lost the habit.

The travelers slept. The four of them stayed up, as they always did, as long as they could, because these hours belonged to them alone.

"Do you ever miss it?" Yara asked suddenly. She was lying on her back, studying the stars through the pine branches. "The god. Even a little."

Astrid considered the question honestly, because Yara deserved honesty and had earned it a hundred times over.

"No," they said at last. "I miss *certainty*. There was a version of me that could have burned Veyric's armies in a night and known, absolutely, that it was right. I don't miss the certainty. Certainty is how you get men like Dreven. And kings like Veyric." They paused. "But sometimes, in fights, my body remembers what it knew. And for about half a second, I understand why people give themselves away."

"And then?"

"And then I remember my reflection moving late." Astrid turned their hands over in the firelight—scarred, ordinary, entirely their own. "And I'm glad to be slow."

Sela said nothing for a long moment. When she spoke, her voice was quieter than usual. "The council sent word again. Through Vaelport channels. They want you to come south. Formally. There's talk of a new order—swordspeakers reborn, oaths renewed, the whole romantic apparatus. They want you at the head of it."

"What did you tell them?"

"That you were guiding a wool merchant through the mountains and couldn't be disturbed." Sela's mouth curved. "I may have used slightly stronger language."

"They'll keep asking."

"They will. And you'll keep saying no, and eventually they'll stop asking and start telling stories about you instead, which is much safer for everyone." She tossed a twig into the fire. "Let them make you a legend, Astrid. Legends can't be commanded."

Astrid looked at her—at this woman who had been sent to capture or trade them and had chosen, publicly, at the cost of everything she'd built, to burn her commission instead. Who had rebuilt herself from the ashes of that fire into something freer and stranger: a guide, a friend, a voice on the council's margins that argued for the small mercies.

"Why did you stay?" Astrid asked. It was a question they'd never dared ask directly. "After Vaelport. You could have gone anywhere. Rebuilt your career somewhere they'd never heard of Hartwells."

Sela was quiet for a while. The fire popped. Somewhere above them, snowmelt ticked down the rocks.

"Because I watched you shatter a god," she said finally, "to keep it out of anyone's hands—including your own. And I thought: *that* is the kind of person worth standing next to when the world ends." She shrugged, deflecting with a small, dry smile. "Also, the free cities' wine is terrible and the company here is better."

Yara made a gagging noise. Tam wrote something in his ledger that made him snort.

Astrid laughed—really laughed, the sound surprising them with its own ease—and for a moment the hollow under the waystone felt less like a campsite and more like the thing it had quietly become.

Home. Not granted. Earned, one hard mile and one honest night at a time.

* * *

Two days later, at the top of the Wolf's Throat, the mountain gave them a gift.

The final ascent had always been brutal—a chimney of bare granite that required ropes and patience and a willingness to trust narrow ledges. But when they rounded the last bend, the chimney was... different. Not easy—that would have been wrong somehow, suspicious—but *kind*. Handholds where handholds should be. Ledges widened by inches, not feet. As if the stone had listened to every traveler who ever cursed it and decided, in its slow mineral way, to be gentler.

The scholar was ecstatic. The merchant was terrified. The young couple climbed it hand in hand, laughing.

Astrid went last, as always, and paused at the top with their palm flat against sun-warmed granite.

"Thank you," they said quietly. Not a prayer. Not an oath. Just manners—Yara's word, Yara's teaching. "You didn't have to."

The stone held no voice. Nothing stirred in their blood; the silence Ondrys had left behind was total and clean, a wound healed to smooth scar. But the wind came up the pass warm from the south, carrying the smell of pine and distant rain, and it moved through Astrid's hair like something brushing past in affection.

The land remembered. Not the god—the land. The old paths, the old stones, the patient bones of Thornfell itself. What Astrid had shattered was only the fragment; what remained was older and smaller and kinder. The world's quiet attention. The sense of being *seen* by something that wanted nothing except, perhaps, to watch them walk home safely.

Yara appeared beside them, breathing hard from the climb, cheeks pink.

"It's not him," she said softly. She'd known what Astrid was going to ask before they asked it. She usually did. "What's in the mountain—it was never part of him. It's just... the world, paying attention. It always was. We just forgot how to notice."

"Is that what the gods were? Before?" Astrid asked. "Just the world, paying attention?"

Yara considered this with the gravity she reserved for real questions.

"I think maybe we were," she said. "Maybe that's all a god ever was. People paying attention together, so hard the attention became someone." She smiled, crooked and bright. "Which means we broke him right, Astrid. We didn't kill anything. We just... stopped paying attention to the wrong thing. Together."

Below them, the southern valleys opened in waves of green. Above, the peaks still wore white crowns. Between, the trail wound down and onward, unmarked on any map, known only to those who walked it.

Sela crested the rise behind them, then Tam, then their three charges, all of them blinking in the vast bright air. Nobody spoke for a moment. There was no need.

Then Tam cleared his throat, consulted his ledger with great ceremony, and said, "According to my calculations, if we make good time, we reach the Coldbrook ford by nightfall, Vaelport by month's end, and I finish this ledger exactly on the last page, which I consider a personal triumph."

"We're not going to Vaelport," Astrid said.

Three faces turned toward them.

"We're going home first. My cabin needs a new roof. Yara promised the shrine at Lowmark she'd plant the hillside with rowan. And Sela owes me a rematch at knucklebones, which she will lose, because the mountain likes me better." Astrid shouldered their pack and started down the trail, walking openly in the sunlight, unhurried, unhidden. "Vaelport can wait. The road's not going anywhere."

Behind them, Sela laughed. Tam made a note. Yara skipped to catch up, already chattering to the wind.

And the path ahead—old, patient, remembering—bent gently toward home, as if the land itself had chosen them back.
