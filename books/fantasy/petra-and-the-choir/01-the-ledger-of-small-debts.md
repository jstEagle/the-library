## 1. The Ledger of Small Debts

The candle had burned down to its last finger's width when Lior found the error.

Not an error, precisely. Errors were common enough in the Choir's ledgers—transposed figures, ink blots mistaken for punctuation, the occasional apprentice who wrote *paid* where they meant *payable* and set off three days of reconciliation. Lior had spent six years in the Counting House learning to catch such things, and they were good at it, which was a strange kind of distinction: being excellent at work no one noticed you doing.

They sat at the long table nearest the archive door, the one reserved for scribes of the third rank and below, with their inherited debt ledger open before them like a wound that had learned to keep time. Entry 4471-C. *Vane, Lior. Balance carried forward.* The number beneath it was written in the old red ink that never quite dried, so that if you dragged your thumb across it—and Lior had, once, years ago, unable to stop themselves—the figure smudged and seemed to grow.

It always grew. That was the nature of the debt. Interest compounded nightly, as it did for every name in the book, because everything in the guild was measured against something else, and nothing stood still. The lamp oil Lior burned tonight was debited against tomorrow's labor. The bread they'd eaten at dusk was entered against next week's copying. Even the hours they spent checking other people's accounts were themselves an account, logged by Magister Ovett's clerks in a separate volume Lior had never been permitted to read.

Lior blew on the page to dry the night's entries and told themselves, as they did every night, that the balance was survivable. That their grandmother's borrowing—what had she even bought? No record remained; the original loan had been made in a ledger older than anyone living—could be worked off in another twenty years, thirty, and then Lior would be free, or their children would, if Lior ever earned enough to have any.

Everyone in the Counting House had a version of this arithmetic. It was how they endured the tedium: by pretending the numbers were going somewhere.

"Vane." Ovett's voice came from the magister's dais at the far end of the hall, soft as dust settling. "You're behind."

Lior looked up. Ovett was a narrow man, gray as ash, with the habit of appearing exactly where he was least wanted. He wasn't even looking at them; he was reading his own volume, turning pages with fingers stained silver from decades of handling the special inks.

"The Merrow Street accounts require cross-verification," Lior said. "There are discrepancies in the toll receipts."

"There are discrepancies everywhere. That is what the world is made of." Ovett turned a page. "Verify faster."

Lior bent back to the work. Around them, the Counting House breathed its nighttime breath: forty scribes at forty desks, quills whispering, the great lamps hissing, and above it all—so constant that no one heard it anymore—the hum. The wards sang through the walls of the guildhall, a note below hearing that lived in the teeth and the breastbone. The Choir maintained the borders with song, everyone knew that; the guild maintained the Choir with accounting. Every verse the choristers raised at the border stones had to be paid for, and every payment passed across a desk like this one.

Lior had asked, once, as an apprentice, what happened if the payments stopped. The senior scribe who answered hadn't even looked up. *Then the singing stops,* she'd said. *And then you'll wish you'd counted faster.*

Lior checked the Merrow Street tolls, found the discrepancy—a ferryman double-charged for a crossing he'd sworn under seal he never made—and corrected it, and initialed it, and moved down the stack. This was the shape of a life. They had stopped minding, mostly.

It was well past midnight when they reached the bottom of the verification pile and found the star-glyph account.

It shouldn't have been there. That was the first wrong thing. The nightly ledgers ran in strict order—guild debts, ward payments, chorister stipends, the small debts of small people, all of it sorted by district and rank—and this entry sat at the very end, after the final district, after even the sealed annex items that were listed only as *see vault*. It had no account holder. No debtor's name. No creditor. Just a single glyph drawn in the margin in ink so old it had gone brown-black, the color of dried blood or old wine:

✶

And beneath it, columns. Two hundred years of columns.

Lior knew it was two hundred years because the entry was continuous—it began in the middle of the oldest working ledger and had been copied forward, hand to hand, generation to generation, every single night without interruption. Debit. Debit. Debit. The amounts varied slightly, rising and falling in a pattern Lior's eye traced down the page before they understood they were tracing it: a rhythm. A slow, vast rhythm, like breathing, like tide. Like a verse.

Every account in the guild could be closed. That was doctrine, taught in the first week of apprenticeship: *a debt is a song unfinished, and every song ends.* Debts were forgiven, discharged, settled in coin or labor or—there were rumors about the third option, whispered only, and Lior had learned early not to ask—settled otherwise. But every account closed eventually. Scribes retired. Debtors died and their balances passed to kin or dissolved into the general fund. Pages filled and were archived.

This one had been open for two hundred years, and it was not owed *by* anyone. It was not owed *to* anyone either, as far as Lior could tell. Something was simply being paid out, every night, to no recorded destination, and had been since before Lior's grandmother's grandmother drew breath.

Lior looked up at the clock over the archive door. The hour hand stood at the mark for the deep watch, when the border choirs changed shifts and the wards' hum shifted key—Lior could feel it now, faintly, the way you feel weather coming in an old scar. In an hour the night scribes would close their books, and the star-glyph entry would receive its debit, and no one would think anything of it, because no one ever thought anything of it. Lior had seen the entry a hundred times before. It was furniture. It was wallpaper. It was the hum itself.

But tonight—for no reason they could ever afterward explain, unless it was the candle burning low, or the way the hum had caught strangely in their chest at moonrise, a half-beat off, like a singer losing the conductor's eye—tonight Lior did the thing scribes were forbidden to do with closed matters.

They read backward.

The current ledger gave them fifty years. The archive gave them the rest. Lior rose quietly, crossed to the cage of the old stacks, signed the access roll with their own name—which felt suddenly like signing away something they didn't know they owned—and carried four volumes back to their desk, the spines cold and furred with dust.

Two hundred years ago, the entry began. Not gradually. There was no period of negotiation, no opening balance, no terms recorded. On one night in autumn, the account simply existed, already running, already debiting, as though it had been going on forever and the ledger had only just gotten around to noticing. The first line was written in a hand Lior recognized from the founding documents displayed behind glass in the guild museum: the Conductor's own.

*Paid out: one measure, per night, in perpetuity.*

One measure. A unit of song. Lior stared at it. The guild dealt in coin, labor, grain, years of service, and—in the sealed annex, in whispers—in other things. But *measures* appeared nowhere else in any ledger Lior had ever touched. What did it mean to pay out a measure of song? Song wasn't substance. You couldn't hold it. You couldn't owe it.

Except the wards were made of it. Except the stars—

Lior stopped. Their quill hovered over the margin, and they became aware, with a cold sliding feeling, of two things at once.

The first: the amounts debited each night matched, almost exactly, the nightly cost of maintaining the border wards. Not approximately. To the fraction. Lior had verified those costs for six years. They knew them the way you know your own pulse.

The second: the star glyph was not decoration. It was a designation. Somewhere in the guild's taxonomy of accounts—debtors, creditors, sureties, collateral—there was a category Lior had never been taught, and its symbol was a star, and whatever bore that symbol was paying for the sky.

The hum in the walls caught again, that same half-beat stumble, and this time Lior heard it clearly, and so did everyone else. Quills paused around the hall. Forty faces lifted. In two hundred years of recorded nights, the wards had never missed a beat.

Then the note resumed, smooth and seamless, and the quills went back to work, and the Counting House decided it had imagined things, because the Counting House was very good at deciding that.

Lior did not go back to work. They sat with their hand flat on the open ledger, over the star glyph, feeling the faint warmth of the page as though the entry were alive underneath their palm. Below hearing, below seeing, something vast was being spent, night after night, to keep the dark stitched with light—and the spending had no name attached, no end, no one to ask why.

*A debt is a song unfinished,* the doctrine said. *And every song ends.*

So whose song was this? And what, after two hundred years of payments, still remained unpaid?

The candle guttered. Lior looked at the entry a moment longer—at the little brown-black star, patient as a wound—and then, moving before the caution that had kept them invisible for six years could catch up, they turned to a fresh sheet of practice vellum and began, in a hand deliberately unlike their own, to copy.

* * *

They finished as the bells rang the closing of the deep watch. Lior folded the copy into the false spine of their own debt ledger—Entry 4471-C, the one place no auditor would ever look closely, because looking closely at Lior Coldharbour was something no one had done in years—and banked the lamp, and joined the quiet procession of scribes shuffling toward the dormitory stairs.

Behind them, the Counting House settled into darkness. And in the last row of the last ledger, in ink two centuries old, the star waited for its nightly debit—while somewhere above the guildhall, high past the smoke and the rooftops, the first star of the morning flickered.

Just once. Just slightly.

Like a singer missing a cue.
