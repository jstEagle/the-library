## 2. What the Wards Were For

The wards failed at moonrise, and Lior knew it first not by any sound or shudder in the world but by the ledger itself.

They were three hours into the night's accounting, ink to the second knuckle, working through the column of small debts—the cobbler on Vane Street who owed a fortnight of song for a resoled boot, the widow Ammas who paid her late husband's tithe in whispered lullabies. Routine work. The kind of work that made the hours go soft and shapeless, like wool worn thin. Lior had long ago learned to love that softness. In the guildhall there was no hunger, no cold, no one who knew their name well enough to hate them. There was only the scratch of the pen and the great slow breathing of the books.

Then the star-glyph account refused its debit.

Lior paused, pen lifted. Every night at moonrise, without fail, the account marked only with a small seven-pointed star was debited—some vast, uncountable sum flowing out into the dark, and every night the entry closed itself with a neat little flourish, as if satisfied. Two hundred years of nightly payments, if the marginalia was to be believed. Lior had noticed it months ago, during an inventory of the deep shelves, and had told no one, because noticing things was dangerous and mentioning them was worse.

Tonight the flourish did not come.

The pen hovered. The sum sat in the ledger like water refusing to drain, pooling, spreading. And beneath Lior's feet—beneath the floorboards, the vaults, the foundations of the guildhall itself—something went quiet.

Not silent. Quiet. The difference mattered. The guildhall was never silent; it hummed, always, a note so low and constant that most people lived their whole lives without hearing it, the way a fish might live without knowing water. Lior heard it now because it stopped.

They set down the pen very carefully, as one does when the floor might be listening.

* * *

By the time Lior reached the eastern gallery window, the whole city had reached its own windows too.

Moonrise had come strange—late, or early, or somehow sideways, the light arriving thin and wrong, like milk cut with water. Along the border hills, far off where the land met the sky, the ward-towers stood in their ancient line, and Lior could see even from here that they had gone dark. Not unlit. Dark the way a throat goes dark when it stops singing. The towers had never been beautiful exactly—they were squat, salt-weathered things—but they had always *hummed*, and travelers swore you could feel the song in your teeth a mile out, a sound like being welcome.

Now nothing. The hills were just hills. The border was just a line someone had drawn.

And then, an hour later, the stars began to go out.

It was not dramatic. That was the horror of it. They didn't fall or flare or weep fire across the sky. They simply stopped, one by one, in no order Lior could find—a star over the harbor gone, then two in the Hunter's Belt, then a scatter of them near the northern peaks, each winking out with the polite finality of a candle pinched between wet fingers. The gaps they left behind were not black. They were *nothing*, a color the eye refused to name, and looking at them made Lior's teeth ache in the place where the guildhall's hum used to live.

Below, Copperlane erupted. Doors banged open. Someone began ringing the temple bell out of rhythm, which was its own kind of blasphemy, and soon every bell in the city was ringing wrong, a chaos of bronze where there should have been harmony. People poured into the streets in nightclothes, faces tilted up toward the holes in the sky. A woman screamed something about the sea. A man knelt in the middle of Tallowmarket and prayed loudly to a god whose name Lior had never learned, because gods, like everything else in this country, had been folded into the Choir's arrangements long ago.

Inside the guildhall: nothing.

That was the thing Lior would remember all their life, the thing they would turn over at 3 a.m. in later years like a stone with something underneath it. The city tore itself open with fear, and the guild sealed its doors, lit its lamps, and *kept counting*. Somewhere below, the night-shift scribes went on entering tithes and transfers in their careful hands. Somewhere above, the magisters convened behind the green door, and through it came the sound of voices—not panicked, not raised, just *procedural*, men and women discussing the end of the sky in the tone of people discussing a supply shortfall.

Lior stood at the gallery window with a stack of ledgers under one arm that suddenly felt obscene. Small debts. Fortnights of song. Widow Ammas's lullabies. All of it still humming along in the books while the actual sky came apart overhead.

An apprentice named Pell skidded up beside them, breathless, eyes huge. "Did you see—did you *see*—"

"I saw."

"They say the conductor's dead." Pell's voice dropped to a whisper, though there was no one to hear. "Magister Ovett sent for the body an hour ago. They say he was found in the counting house with his—" The apprentice swallowed. "With his voice taken out of him. Just gone. Like a page cut clean."

"Go back to your desk," Lior said, and hated how steady it sounded.

"But the stars—"

"The stars are not in your column. Go."

Pell went. Lior stayed at the window a moment longer, watching a third of the Hunter's Belt wink out, and felt the first cold thread of something that was not yet dread but was learning to be. Because there was a thought forming, unwelcome, insistent, and it was this: the star-glyph account had missed its payment tonight. The wards had failed at moonrise. The stars had begun dying an hour later.

Coincidence was a luxury the ledger did not permit. Everything in the books was connected; that was the first law of accounting, before double-entry, before ink. Nothing in a ledger stands alone.

Which meant the account Lior had found—the one nobody spoke of, marked only with a star—was not a debt the Choir collected.

It was a debt the Choir *paid*.

* * *

Ovett summoned them at the fourth bell.

Magister Ovett occupied the corner office on the third tier, a room with one window that faced inward, toward the guildhall's central shaft, so that even his view was of the institution itself. He was a narrow man, dry as a ledger's spine, with the particular pallor of someone who had spent forty years trading daylight for margins. He did not look up when Lior entered. He was writing, in that beautiful terrible hand of his, and for a long moment the only sound was the pen.

"You were on the deep shelves last month," he said finally. "Inventory of the pre-Concord accounts."

"Yes, Magister."

"What did you find there?"

Lior's mouth had already gone dry. "Dust, Magister. Mislabeled folios. The usual."

"Mm." Ovett finished his line, blotted it, and looked up. His eyes were the pale gray of old paper. "The star-glyph account. Account Seven-Unbroken. Did you find that?"

There was no correct answer, and both incorrect answers were the same answer. "I saw it," Lior said. "I didn't touch it."

"No one touches it. That is rather the point of it." Ovett rose and moved to the inward-facing window, hands clasped behind him, watching the lamplight climb the central shaft. Below them, faintly, the night-shift pens scratched on. "You will have noticed it failed tonight."

"The entry wouldn't close."

"The entry wouldn't close," Ovett repeated softly, as if tasting the words. "Two hundred years, Coldharbour. Every night since the Concord, that account has paid out, and every night it has closed, and tonight—for the first time in the living memory of this institution—it would not. Do you understand what that means?"

"It means the wards are down," Lior said. "And the stars are going out."

"It means," Ovett said, turning, "that the account has been compromised. Or its payer has. Either way, the book is bleeding from a wound we cannot see, and when a book bleeds, you do not stand admiring it. You close the wound." He returned to his desk and drew a single sheet of heavy cream paper toward himself. An order form. Lior knew the format intimately; they had transcribed hundreds of them. *By order of the Magistry, the following account is to be struck, expunged, and rendered void, its pages burned and its ash sealed—*

"You're destroying it," Lior said.

"I am protecting it. There is a difference, though I don't expect a scribe of your seniority to appreciate it." Ovett wrote the account number in the blank, and his pen did not hesitate, and that—that more than anything—was what curdled something in Lior's chest. No hesitation. Forty years of institutional calm applied to the amputation of whatever kept the sky lit. "The copy will be made by my own hand, for the archive. The original burns at dawn. You will witness the burning, as procedure requires, and you will speak of it to no one, including yourself, including in your own head, which I know is a habit of yours."

He knew. Of course he knew. Lior had spent nine years perfecting invisibility, and Ovett had spent forty years seeing through it.

"Witnessing requires reading," Lior said carefully. "Procedure."

"Procedure," Ovett agreed, with the ghost of a smile that held no warmth whatsoever. "Read it, then. Witness it. Understand what you are looking at, so that when you burn it, you burn it *knowingly*. That is the whole of our profession, Coldharbour. Knowing what we burn." He slid the order across the desk. "Dawn. The brazier in the Vault of Records. Don't be late."

* * *

Lior did not go to the brazier.

Or rather—Lior went to the brazier, eventually, at dawn, with the account under their arm and ash on their conscience. But between the summons and the burning there were four hours, and four hours is a long time when you are invisible, because nobody watches an invisible thing.

They took the account to the copying room on the second tier, the one with the bad lamp that nobody repaired because nobody was assigned there after midnight. They locked the door. They sharpened a fresh nib. And they copied Account Seven-Unbroken, line by line, glyph by glyph, two hundred years of nightly debits, and the deeper they went the less any of it made sense and the more it mattered.

The sums were impossible. Not large—*impossible*. Figures that used notation Lior had only seen in the founding folios, numbers that seemed to describe not money but *mass*, not coin but substance. The payer column was blank. It had always been blank. But the payee column, night after night, year after year, contained a single word in a script older than the guild:

*Sky.*

Every night for two hundred years, something had paid the sky. And tonight, the payment had failed.

Outside the copying room's one grimy window, the darkness where the Hunter's Belt had been seemed to press against the glass like a face. Down in the city, the bells were still ringing wrong. Somewhere below, the magisters were convening again, and their voices through the floors had lost even the pretense of procedure—Lior could feel the panic coming up through the stone now, the way you feel weather in a joint.

Lior finished the last line, blotted the page, and folded the copy into the false binding of a hymnal they'd hollowed out years ago for exactly this kind of contingency, though until tonight they'd never imagined needing it. Then they carried the original down to the Vault of Records, fed it to the brazier page by page while the dawn came up gray and starless, and watched two hundred years of payments curl into black lace and crumble.

Complicity, they thought, watching the ash settle. It smells like nothing at all.

Ovett arrived to witness the final page burn, as procedure required. He watched the last of it go with his dry gray eyes and said, "Well done," and meant it, and that was the worst part.

But Ovett did not know about the hymnal. Nobody ever watched an invisible thing.

And Lior had read enough, in those four stolen hours, to know one more thing—something they had not put in the copy, because some knowledge is too dangerous to duplicate. At the very bottom of the oldest folio, in the founder's hand, beneath two centuries of payments to the sky, there was a single annotation. One line, faded almost to nothing:

*When this account fails, the debt falls to the collateral.*

Lior had spent their whole life believing they had no collateral. They were a scribe. They owned a pen, a knife, three shirts, and a hymnal with a secret in it.

But everyone in the guild was in the ledger. That was what the guild *was*.

And Lior Coldharbour's own entry—thin, neglected, gathering dust on a low shelf—had been opened before they were born, and had never once been closed.

They needed to talk to Sefa Quill.
