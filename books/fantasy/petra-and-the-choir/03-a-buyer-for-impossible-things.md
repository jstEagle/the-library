## 3. A Buyer for Impossible Things

The city had not slept since the stars went out, and neither had I. By the time I reached the Saltmarket, the panic had settled into something worse than screaming: a low, grinding murmur, like a millstone turning over bone. People stood in doorways looking up at a sky the color of wet slate. A woman was selling candles at triple price and no one begrudged her. Somewhere a bell rang without rhythm, because whoever was ringing it had forgotten why bells were rung.

I kept my hood up and my satchel close against my ribs. Inside it, wrapped in oilcloth beneath three layers of worthless inventory ledgers, lay the copy of the star-glyph account.

I had told myself all night that copying it was instinct. That any good scribe, ordered to destroy an entry, would preserve it first—that destruction without record was simply bad bookkeeping. It was a comfortable lie. The truth was that when Magister Ovett had said *burn it*, his voice had done something I had never heard it do in eleven years of service. It had shaken.

Magisters did not shake. Magisters were the fixed points around which the rest of us orbited. If Ovett was afraid of an account, then the account was worth more than my life, and possibly more than his.

Sefa Quill's shop sat where the Saltmarket narrowed into the tanner's warren, wedged between a chandler that smelled of tallow and regret and a shuttered pawnhouse whose windows had been painted black so long ago the paint had begun to flake like dead skin. There was no sign. There never is, on shops that sell impossible things. Instead there was a small brass plate, unpolished, bearing a single engraved line:

*Appraisals. Honest ones.*

In this city, honesty was the most forbidden trade of all.

I knocked the way Ovett had once described to me, years ago, in one of his rare expansive moods: three slow, two fast, then wait as long as your patience lasts. He'd said it with contempt, describing how to find a fence for stolen goods, and I had filed it away the way I filed everything—because a debt-bound scribe learns early that knowledge is the only currency that can't be repossessed.

The door opened on the fourth knock. Sefa Quill was smaller than I expected, gray-streaked hair cut short and practical, hands stained with ink of at least four colors. Her eyes went from my face to my hood to the way I held my satchel, and something in them sharpened like a blade being tested against a thumb.

"You're guild," she said. Not a question. She could probably smell the ledger-oil on me, the particular blend of gall and ash we used to keep entries legible for centuries.

"I'm nobody," I said. "That's rather the point of me."

"Nobody pays double." She stepped back from the doorway. "Come in before someone sees you standing there."

* * *

Her shop was narrower inside than out, which should have been impossible and probably was. Shelves climbed past the reach of candlelight into darkness, and every shelf held objects under glass or cloth: a compass that pointed at whatever you'd lost; a jar of preserved echoes; a wedding ring still warm after forty years. On the counter, alone and uncovered, sat a small brass scale with empty pans.

"Rules first," Sefa said, lighting a second lamp. "You don't tell me where you got what you're selling. You don't tell me who sent you. And if what you show me is guild property, you tell me now, because the price changes and so does my answer."

"It's a copy," I said. "Made by my own hand, on my own paper, with my own ink. The original is about to be destroyed by order of Magister Ovett himself."

She went very still. "Ovett. He signs the star accounts." Her voice had dropped half a register. "Which account?"

"The one marked only with a glyph. No name, no debtor, no creditor. Debited every night for two hundred years, give or take. Never closed. Never *closable*—the rule won't let anyone write a final line under it."

For a long moment she just looked at me. Then she pulled a stool from behind the counter and set it down with a small, deliberate click, the way one does when one's knees have opinions.

"Sit," she said. "And unwrap it slowly."

I did. The oilcloth fell away and there it was: my copy, forty lines of careful script, the star glyph crowning the page like a small cold sun. I had reproduced it exactly—the debits, the notation in the margins that wasn't musical notation but wasn't anything else either, the column of figures that didn't add up to anything because they weren't numbers, not really. They were amounts. Of something else.

Sefa read it the way I had never seen anyone read anything. She didn't move her lips. She didn't trace the lines. She read it whole, top to bottom, the way you'd look at a painting, and as she read, the color left her face in stages—first the warmth, then the color, then something underneath both, until she looked like paper herself.

"Where did you learn to copy like this?" she asked quietly. "Your hand is exact. Frighteningly exact."

"Eleven years of writing other people's debts," I said. "You get good at disappearing into the letters."

"No," she said. "You get good at becoming the letters." She turned the page toward me and tapped the margin notation. "Do you know what this is?"

"Marginalia. Verification marks. Every account has them."

"Every account has *audit* marks. This isn't audit. Look at the spacing." She laid her own hand flat beside the glyphs. "These intervals are consistent. Deliberate. They're a *line*. A melody, written sideways, in the margins of a ledger. Lior—" she had read my name off the watermark of my paper, of course she had, "—do you know what the Choir actually is? Not the story they tell. The actual thing."

"A guild," I said. "A guild of impossible trades. They keep the border wards singing. Everyone knows that."

"Everyone knows the story. Here's the truth." She leaned across the counter, and her voice came down to almost nothing, though there was no one to hear it but me and the jar of echoes. "The wards aren't walls. Walls keep things out. The wards are a *song*, and songs need singers, and the country has been singing itself to itself for six hundred years. The borders are drawn in notation. The rivers follow the time signature. The mountains are rests. And the sky—" her finger rose, trembling slightly, to the ceiling, to the dark beyond it where stars should have been, "—the sky is the sustained note. The long chord. Held, without pause, since the founding."

"That's a fable," I said. My voice sounded far away. "That's a children's fable."

"It's an *account*," she said. "Look at your copy again. You've been reading it as a debt owed *to* the Choir. A payment coming in. But read the direction of the entries. Read which side the figures fall."

I looked. Eleven years of training made it obvious the moment she said it, and I hated myself for not seeing it, and I hated the world for making it true. The debits fell on the Choir's side. Night after night, for two hundred years, the Choir had been the one paying.

"Paying whom?" I whispered.

"Nothing. Paying *out*. From its own principal." Sefa's hand closed around the edge of the counter hard enough to whiten her knuckles. "This isn't a debtor's account, Lior. This is the Choir's soul, mortgaged nightly to keep the sky lit. Every evening, at moonrise, the Choir pays a portion of itself into the song to hold the long chord through the dark hours. Two hundred years of installments. And last night—" she looked up at me, and her eyes were terrible with understanding, "—last night the payments stopped. Because whoever was supposed to authorize them is dead. The conductor. That's why the stars went out. The Choir ran out of will, or permission, or *soul*, and the chord broke."

The shop seemed to tilt. I gripped the stool's legs. The jar of echoes murmured faintly, as if it too were trying to make sense of it.

"But that means—" I started, and stopped, and started again. "If the Choir has been mortgaging its own soul, then the Choir isn't the power here. The Choir is *collateral*. Something holds the mortgage. Something above the Choir."

"Or outside it," Sefa said softly. "Something the founders signed with. Something the ledger serves, not the other way around." She straightened, and for the first time since reading the entry, she looked away from me, toward the painted-black window of the pawnhouse next door, as if she could see through it to something farther. "You came to sell me a copied page. Do you understand what you're holding? This is the most dangerous document in the country. Not because of what it says. Because of what it proves: that everything—the wards, the borders, the stars, the *sky*—is a debt instrument. We are living inside someone's collateral. All of us. The whole country is a lien."

"And if the payments stop," I said, "the lien gets called."

"The lien gets called," she agreed. "And the holder collects."

Outside, the bell that had lost its reason rang again, arrhythmic, desperate. Sefa blew out the second lamp, leaving us in the small circle of the first, and when she spoke again her voice had changed—flatter, harder, the voice of a woman deciding something.

"There are people who know about this account. Have known for longer than you've been alive. They're called the Hollowed, and if they learn a guild scribe walked out of the vaults with a copy of it, they'll come for you—and not gently, because they want that ledger burned down to its covers, and everyone written in it with it." She met my eyes. "Including you, Lior Coldharbour. Your name is in that book. Isn't it."

It wasn't a question. Debt-bound scribes always think their names are the smallest thing about them. I nodded anyway.

"Then listen carefully," Sefa Quill said, and reached beneath the counter for something wrapped in black cloth, "because you're going to want to be gone from here before moonrise. And Lior—"

Three knocks sounded on her door. Slow, slow, slow. No fast ones after. Just silence, and then, faintly, a sound like many people breathing in a single shared rhythm, as if one throat served them all.

Sefa's face went white for the second time that night.

"They're already here," she breathed. "They followed you."
