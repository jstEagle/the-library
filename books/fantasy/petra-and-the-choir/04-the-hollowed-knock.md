## 4. The Hollowed Knock

Sefa Quill had spent eleven years learning to hear the lies in objects. A forged ring sang a half-tone flat when you held it to the lamp. A stolen deed carried the sourness of another man's hand. Her shop above the tannery row smelled of vinegar and old vellum, and every item on her shelves had been appraised honestly, which was to say: illegally, beautifully, and at considerable risk.

The copy of Lior's ledger entry lay open on her counter, and it was lying to her, and she could not stop looking at it.

*Account of the Star. Debited nightly. Two hundred years. No creditor named. No term of repayment. No closing entry possible.*

She had gone pale reading it — she remembered going pale, remembered her hands shaking — but that had been hours ago, before dark, before she'd sent Lior Coldharbour away with instructions to keep their head down and their copy hidden. Now the lamps were lit, the shutters drawn, and Sefa sat alone with the worst appraisal of her life and no buyer brave enough or fool enough to take it.

The Choir's own soul, mortgaged out night by night to keep the sky burning. And someone had killed the conductor who kept the payments flowing.

She poured herself plum wine she couldn't taste. That was new. The wine had tasted fine yesterday.

* * *

It began with the candle.

Not guttering — *narrowing*. The flame drew itself thin as wire, then thinner, until it was less light than suggestion, and the shadow it threw off Sefa's own hands did not match them. Her shadow's fingers were too long. Its head was bowed, though hers was not.

Then the knock.

Three raps at her street door, spaced like the beats of a slow hymn. Sefa knew every kind of knock this city produced: the guild's officious triple-bang, the debt collectors' polite horror, the drunkards' chaos. This was none of these. This knock sounded like it came from inside the door.

She did not answer it. Answering felt like an admission.

"Appraiser," said a voice through the wood, and it was a voice made of many voices, the way a chord is one sound made of several notes. "We know you're awake. We heard you thinking."

Sefa reached beneath the counter for the iron stylus she kept there — guild-issue, weighted, good for breaking teeth or scratching wards into doorframes. She scratched the ward first. It took the ink like water takes rain, drinking it without resistance, and went dark.

"Oh, don't bother," said the chord-voice, almost kindly. "Wards are entries in the book. We were entries in the book. We know how they're written. We know how they're *unwritten*."

The lock turned itself.

The door swung open onto the fog of Tanners' Row, and standing in it were people.

That was the horror of them, Sefa thought afterward, and thought now: they were *people*, arranged correctly, proportioned correctly, dressed in clothes of twenty years' fashion gone soft as wet paper. But they stood wrong, all of them slightly, as if the world had forgotten to finish deciding where they ended. The lamplight passed through the nearest woman's shoulder and came out the other side carrying a smear of her, like light through dirty glass.

They filled her shop without entering it, the way cold fills a room.

"We want you to look at us," said the chord-voice, and Sefa found its source: a tall figure at the front, half-man and half-absence, his left side rendered in careful detail and his right side dissolving into something like static, like snow on a dead scrying-glass. His remaining eye was brown and patient and terribly alive. "Properly look. You're the only one left who does."

"I'm closed," Sefa said. Her voice came out steadier than her hands. "Come back never."

"We've been *never*," said a small man near the back, and his voice frayed at the edges like rope. "Eleven years of never. My wife remarried. There's a child in my house calling another man *Da*. I signed nothing. I owed nothing. Someone erased me to balance a line." He lifted a hand toward her, pleading or accusing; she couldn't tell; possibly he couldn't either. "You deal in what things are worth. Tell me what I'm worth. Tell me what I *was*."

"Vess," warned the tall one, and Sefa understood: the chord-voice had a name.

"Vess," she said. "I know that name."

"You shouldn't," Vess said. "That's rather the point. But yes. Once I conducted second voice for the western wards. Once I had a name that appeared in ledgers, and letters, and the memories of people who loved me. Then I found out what the Choir does with voices that ask questions, and I was subtracted. Cleanly. Professionally." His good eye did not blink. It occurred to Sefa that maybe it couldn't anymore. "You're wondering why we haven't hurt you. Good. Keep wondering. It means you're paying attention."

* * *

They moved through her shop while they talked, and that was worse than anything they said. They touched things the way starving people touch bread behind glass. The erased woman ran her fingers along Sefa's appraisal shelf and her fingers left no disturbance at all — no dust shifted, no object rocked. She was touching the world and the world was declining to notice.

"You appraise forbidden things," Vess said. "So appraise this."

He set something on her counter, and it landed with weight, real weight, the first fully real thing any of them had produced: a conductor's baton of white bone-dark wood, snapped clean in half.

The shop went very quiet. Even the Hollowed stilled, the way a congregation stills.

Sefa looked at the baton for a long time. She didn't need to hold it. She could read it from here, the way she could read most things, because everything true about an object is written somewhere on its surface if you've spent eleven years learning the alphabet.

Magister-Conductor Elandrine Vay. First Voice of the Choir. The only person in the entire guild structure with authority over the nightly debits from the Account of the Star. Found three nights ago at the bottom of the choir stairs, neck broken, and the guild had announced it as an accident, and the city had believed it because believing was easier than the alternative, and the wards had held for exactly two more days and then failed at moonrise, and the stars had gone out an hour after that, and somewhere in a sealed guildhall the magisters were still counting.

"You," Sefa said. "You killed her."

"We unmade her," Vess said. "There's a difference, and the difference matters. We didn't break her neck. We broke the *entry*. She was written into the ledger same as everyone — do you understand? Everything in this country is written in that book. Every soul, every stone, every star it props up. And an entry, once you've been one, can be *edited*." His half-face tightened. "She begged. I'll tell you that plainly, because you'll hear it plainer nowhere else. She begged, and she offered us restoration, full restoration, our names back in ink, if we let her keep the payments flowing. And we refused, because her restoration offer was a lie — the ledger doesn't restore, it only *re-lends*. She'd have owned us twice over by morning."

"So you murdered the one person keeping the sky lit."

"The sky was never lit *for us*," said the small man, sharp and sudden. "We don't get a sky. We don't get anything. The stars burn on our subtraction — did you know that? Did your precious copy show you where the debits come *from*?" He was shaking, or the idea of him was. "Two hundred years of payments, appraiser. Paid out of *us*. Out of everyone. The whole country is a purse the Choir keeps dipping into, and when a name runs dry, they erase it and call it accounting."

Sefa's mouth was dry. She thought of Lior Coldharbour, sitting in the guildhall tonight, keeping count, invisible, obedient, copying forbidden entries by lamplight. She thought of how Lior had looked at the star glyph. Not greedy. Not ambitious. Just *attentive*, the way she herself was attentive, the way the dangerous always are.

"And now," Vess said, "the offer."

* * *

He drew closer, and up close the static of his unfinished side whispered, faintly, like a choir heard through a wall. Sefa forced herself not to step back.

"You have something of ours," Vess said. "Or nearly. The scribe who visited you today — Coldharbour — copied an entry they should not have copied. You read it. You know what it is. And the guild will be hunting that copy within the day, because it is proof, the only surviving proof, of what the Account of the Star really is."

"They'll destroy it," Sefa said. "Ovett already ordered—"

She stopped. Too late. Vess's eye brightened with terrible satisfaction.

"Yes. Ovett ordered it destroyed. Which means the scribe has it still, or had it, which means the scribe is already guilty of theft in the eyes of the guild, which means the scribe will soon be desperate, and desperate people find doors." He gestured, and the Hollowed drew in around him, a constellation of almost-persons. "Here is the offer, Quill, and I'll make it only once. Bring us the scribe and the copy. Help us reach the founding ledgers below the guildhall — you know they exist, everyone in your trade knows, and none of you speak of it. Help us open the Door Out. And when the book burns, we will write your name back into the world ourselves. Full restoration. Not the ledger's counterfeit version — ours. You would be *remembered*, Sefa Quill. Truly remembered. Is that not what a dealer in honest appraisals secretly wants? To be worth something to somebody?"

The wine sat untouched. The candle burned thin and strange. And Sefa stood in her shop between two appetites: the Hollowed's hunger to be remembered, and her own, which she had never once said aloud, which lived in her chest like a swallowed coal.

*Restoration.* Eleven years disgraced, expelled, unlicensed, selling honesty out of a room above a tannery while the guild that ruined her counted souls upstairs like coin.

She could have it. All she had to do was hand over a nervous scribe who had trusted her with the only dangerous thing they'd ever done.

"No," Sefa said.

The word came out small. She made herself say it again, larger. "No."

Vess tilted his head. "Barely a refusal. I heard the pause."

"You heard a pause. You'll die of old age waiting to hear more." She planted both hands on the counter, over the broken baton, and met his single living eye. "I won't sell them. Not because they're special — they're not, they're a clerk, a frightened little clerk with inherited debts and ink-stained cuffs. I won't sell them because *selling people is the guild's trade*, and I got out of the guild, whatever else I lost getting out. You want me to become them to spite them. I'd rather stay poor."

Something moved across Vess's half-made face — not anger. Recognition, maybe. Grief's cousin.

"Barely," he repeated softly. "'I refuse, barely.' Do you know what barely means, appraiser? It means the door is still open a crack. It means part of you is already counting what restoration would cost and finding the price almost bearable." He straightened, and the static along his right side flared, hissing faintly, and every Hollowed in the room turned toward Sefa at once with the same hungry patience. "So we won't ask again. We'll simply tell you what happens next. Tomorrow the guild audits its own. They'll find the copy, or the memory of it, and they'll pull your visitor apart to learn who helped. And when the scribe comes running to you — and they will run, Quill, the guilty always run to the last person who was kind — you'll have to choose again, with less time and worse options."

He turned toward the door. Paused.

"One kindness, since you gave us a kindness. Ask your scribe about their grandmother. Ask them what was sold, and when, and by whom. The ledger remembers what people beg it to forget." His voice dropped to nearly human, nearly gentle. "And when you see what's written under their name — remember that we told you first. Remember *someone* told you."

The door closed. The candle stood back up to its full height, flame round and ordinary, and the shadows of Sefa's hands matched her hands again.

She stood alone for a long moment. Then she snuffed the lamp, pulled on her coat, and went down into the fog toward the guildhall, because there was one thing worse than being caught visiting a disgraced appraiser, and that was being caught alone.

The stars were still out. All of them. Burning on borrowed light, paid for nightly, and Sefa walked beneath them feeling, for the first time in her life, that the sky was watching her spend.
