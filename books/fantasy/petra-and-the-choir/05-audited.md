## 5. Audited

They came for Lior at the fourth bell of morning, when the counting floor smelled of ink and cold tallow and everyone was too bleary to make a scene.

Two auditors in grey. Not the ordinary grey of guild wool—*audit* grey, dyed with ash and something that made the eye slide off them, so that clerks looked up, frowned, forgot what they'd seen, and bent again over their columns. They stood at either end of Lior's desk like bookends made of consequence.

"Lior Coldharbour," said the one on the left. Their voice was pleasant, which was worse than shouting. "You'll come with us."

Lior set down their pen with exaggerated care, as if the pen were the important thing, as if the last twelve hours had been an ordinary night of accounting. Around them, three hundred scribes kept writing. Three hundred pens scratched on, because that was the first rule of the guild: *the count does not stop.* But Lior felt their eyes anyway, dozens of them, flicking up and away like startled birds.

They rose. Their legs worked. That seemed worth noting.

The audit chamber was on the top floor of the guildhall, a room Lior had cleaned twice a year for eleven years without ever being invited into. It was round, which no room in the guild should be—the Choir thought in straight lines, ledgers and staves—and its walls were shelved to the ceiling with ledgers so old their spines had gone soft as bread. In the center stood a single table and two chairs. Magister Ovett sat in one.

Ovett was a small man built entirely of angles, with the kind of face that looked like it had been folded and refolded so many times the original expression was unrecoverable. He had been Lior's superior for nine years. He had never once learned Lior's name without consulting a roster; Lior had checked, once, spitefully, and been proved right. There was a comfort in that. There had been a comfort in it.

"Sit," Ovett said.

Lior sat. The second chair was warm, which meant someone else had recently been interrogated here, or perhaps unmade, and the chair had not distinguished between the two.

"You copied an entry," Ovett said. "Account marked with the star glyph. Night of the collapse. You took a fair-copy sheet from the restricted rack, transcribed the account in full, and removed the copy from this building." He said it all flatly, like reading a column of figures, because that was what it was. "Do you dispute any part of that?"

"No."

"Good. Honesty is efficient." Ovett opened a ledger on the table—Lior's own service record, they realized, every hour of their eleven years reduced to entries. "I want you to understand your position before we proceed. The wards fell. The stars went out. The city is rioting in the lantern districts. The conductor is dead. And into that catastrophe, a debt-bound scribe with no family, no faction, and no future stole a page of the most sensitive account this guild has kept in two centuries." He looked up. His eyes were the color of pond ice. "So the question is not *whether* you did it. The question is *for whom.*"

"For myself," Lior said. "I wanted to know what it was."

"That is not an answer the guild recognizes."

"It's the only true one I have."

Ovett turned a page. The sound it made was very loud in the round room.

"Do you know why I ordered that account destroyed?" he asked.

"Because it failed?"

"Because it *succeeded,* for two hundred years, and then it failed, and a thing that fails after two hundred years draws attention. Attention is a debt, Coldharbour. Every eye that looks at the guild's books is a debit against our solvency. The star account was debited nightly, silently, by generations of clerks who were told only: *post the figure, ask nothing.* You were told the same. For eleven years you posted figures and asked nothing. Last night you stopped." He closed her record—closed *their* record—with a small decisive snap. "Why?"

Lior thought about lying. It lasted about a heartbeat, and then they remembered that auditors could smell a false entry the way sharks smelled blood, that the whole guild was built on the principle that a lie in the books would eventually surface somewhere, in some account, at some ruinous interest rate.

"The night the wards fell," Lior said slowly, "I was posting the nightly debit to the star account, same as always. And for the first time it occurred to me that I didn't know what we were paying *to.* Two hundred years of payments. A debt that never closes. And I realized—" they stopped, hearing their own voice as if it belonged to someone braver, "—that everything I've ever been told about this place assumes the debts are honest. That someone out there lent us something real. And I don't think anyone has checked in two hundred years whether there's anything on the other side of the page."

Silence. Ovett regarded them for a long moment, and then he did something Lior had never seen him do: he leaned back in his chair.

"You have a scribe's mind," he said. "Wasted, but present." He drew a slim folio from his coat and laid it on the table between them. It was bound in grey leather, unmarked, unnumbered. Lior knew instantly what it was, the way you know a door in your own house even in the dark. "This is your account, Coldharbour. Your personal entry in the Ledger of Small Debts. Do you know what's in it?"

"I've read the summary. Debts of inheritance. My mother's mother's obligations, passed down."

"Your grandmother's," Ovett agreed. "Vessa Coldharbour, choirwoman, third voice, drowned in the flood year. Her debts were substantial and her estate consisted of one daughter. So the debt rolled forward. And when your mother also died insolvent—" he paused, delicately, "—it rolled forward again."

"To me. I know. I've paid on it my whole life."

"You've posted *interest* your whole life. Principal untouched. Standard practice." Ovett slid the folio across the table. "But you've never read the actual terms. No debtor is permitted to. Read them now. Consider it a professional courtesy, extended because in approximately ten minutes you will no longer be in a position to be harmed by knowledge."

Lior's hands were steady until they touched the folio, and then they weren't. They opened it.

The handwriting was old-fashioned, flourished, confident—the hand of someone who wrote for beauty and never doubted the world would pay. The entry began with a date Lior did not recognize and a name: VESSA COLDHARBOUR.

And beneath it, in the same beautiful hand:

*Received of Vessa Coldharbour, in full satisfaction of outstanding principal: one unborn child, sex undetermined, to be entered upon birth as collateral against the Choir's general obligation. The child shall serve in the counting house for the term of its natural life. Its labor shall be applied to interest only. The child may not close this account. The child may not withdraw from the Choir. The child may not die outside the guild's jurisdiction.*

Lior read it twice. Three times. The words did not change, which felt like a personal insult.

"She sold me," Lior said. Their voice came out level, which surprised them. "Before I was born. Before she even knew what I'd be."

"She was drowning," Ovett said, without inflection. "Debtors are permitted collateral substitutions under the founding clauses. She substituted you."

"She didn't *have* me to substitute. I wasn't anything yet. I was a possibility. She bet a possibility against her own debts and the guild *accepted the wager.*"

"The guild accepts all wagers. That is what makes it the guild." Ovett folded his hands. "Now you understand your position. You were never invisible, Coldharbour. Invisible people are not useful. You were *collateral.* Collateral is kept safe, kept working, kept ignorant of its own valuation. Every kindness this guild has shown you—your desk, your lodging, the fact that no magistrate ever dragged you off for vagrancy—was not kindness. It was maintenance. We maintain our assets."

Something in Lior's chest went very quiet and very cold, the way water goes just before it freezes. Eleven years of small mercies rearranged themselves in memory, one by one, and became line items. The extra coal in winter: maintenance. The surgeon's fee when they broke their wrist falling from the scaffolds: maintenance. The way the older clerks had always been vaguely, unfailingly decent to them, in the way one is decent to a good horse: maintenance.

"I used to think," Lior heard themselves say, "that if I just kept my head down and counted honestly, someday the arithmetic would work out. That the debt would close. That I'd walk out the front door a free person."

"The debt cannot close. That is written into the terms you are currently holding. You may not close it, and no payment of yours can satisfy it, because you are not the debtor. You are the *security.* Security doesn't settle accounts. Security gets redeemed or forfeited." Ovett's voice softened by a degree, which in him was roughly equivalent to another man's tears. "I am telling you this because you are going to hear it from someone less careful otherwise. The audit has already begun, Coldharbour. When the wards fell, every asset in this guild came up for review. You are being reviewed. Do you understand what happens to collateral when the institution holding it becomes insolvent?"

Lior understood. They had spent eleven years learning what happened to debts when debtors died. The estate was seized. The assets were liquidated.

"They'll sing us," Lior said quietly. "Like the rite. Like the conductor tried to do. Fuel."

"The magisters are considering all options." Ovett rose, gathering his folios, suddenly brisk, business concluded. "Here is what remains of your choices. Confess to theft of guild property, accept forfeiture, and go to the vaults as a cooperative asset—which is survivable, for a while. Or continue this absurd pretense of independence, be recorded as *uncooperative collateral,* and be transferred to the rite-chamber as fuel. The Choir must stand again, Coldharbour. The sky is dark. Someone must pay to light it." At the door he paused, and did not turn around. "Your grandmother's handwriting was beautiful, incidentally. You hold the pen the way she did. I've watched you for eleven years. It's the only reason you're getting this conversation instead of the grey room."

He left. The auditors did not—they took up posts at either side of the door, ash-grey and patient as furniture.

Lior sat alone in the round room, surrounded by two centuries of soft-spined books, and let the betrayal finish moving through them like a swallowed stone. It did not shatter anything. That was the strange part. They had assumed that learning the truth about yourself would break you, and instead it was more like the moment a fog lifts off a harbor: everything is exactly where you thought it was, only now you can see how far the drop is.

Their grandmother's signature curled at the bottom of the folio, lovely and damning. *Vessa Coldharbour.* And below it, in the guild's dry hand, the acceptance clause, the seal, the whole machine closing over a person who did not yet exist.

They had spent their whole life trying to be too small to notice. And all along, the noticing had been done. Their price had been set before they drew breath. Every quiet year, every humble ledger, every night of posting figures and asking nothing—it hadn't been safety. It had been storage.

Cold clarity arrived the way dawn arrives in winter: gradually, and then all at once, and without warmth.

They could confess. They could go to the vaults as a cooperative asset and survive, for a while, in the dark, among the oldest accounts—the ones Ovett guarded, the ones that held whatever the star glyph really was.

Or.

Lior looked down at their hands. A scribe's hands. Ink-stained, precise, trained for eleven years in the one discipline the guild trusted absolutely: the faithful copying of numbers.

They had made one copy already. It was hidden where no auditor would look, because no auditor believed a collateral asset had anywhere to hide anything.

"Cooperative," Lior said aloud, to the empty round room, and was surprised at how easily the word came. "I confess. I'll cooperate fully."

It was even true. Cooperation, after all, was simply the fastest road down.

The auditors opened the door for them, and Lior walked between them toward the vaults, toward the oldest accounts, with the guild's own grey escort clearing the way—straight down through every locked door between them and the truth.

* * *

The stairwell to the sealed levels had not been lit in living memory. The auditors carried lamps whose flames burned without warmth, and the deeper they went, the more Lior noticed the strangeness of the architecture: each landing's door bore not a lock but a *rest*, like the rest before a note, and the doors grew progressively larger, as if the building itself swelled to hold what waited below.

At the fifth landing, the lead auditor stopped.

"Cooperative assets proceed to processing," she said. "You'll be assigned a workstation. You'll post figures. You will ask nothing."

"Yes," Lior said agreeably.

The processing room was small and warm, almost comfortable—a deliberate cruelty, Lior suspected, a room shaped like forgiveness. A clerk with kind eyes took their confession, stamped their status, and issued them a desk lamp and a stack of fair-copy sheets.

The stack was the star-glyph account. Of course it was. Cooperative assets posted the figures nobody else would touch.

Lior dipped their pen. Above them, muffled through five floors of stone, they could hear the guild beginning to move—messengers running, bells ringing in a pattern they had never heard before, a summons pattern, an emergency convocation. The magisters were gathering. Whatever Ovett had warned of, whatever the review of assets meant, it was happening now, tonight.

Lior looked at the star glyph at the head of the column, two hundred years of nightly debits marching down beneath it, and thought: *you're in the vaults now, Coldharbour. You're inside the oldest rooms in the building. Everything Sefa guessed, everything the Hollowed died refusing to forget—it's within walking distance of this desk.*

They posted the figure. Then, with the unhurried motion of eleven years of habit, they began, in the margin, in a cipher of their own devising, to count something else entirely: doors, guards, intervals, the rhythm of the lamps' refilling, the notation of a possible escape.

The count does not stop.

Let it count something worth counting.
