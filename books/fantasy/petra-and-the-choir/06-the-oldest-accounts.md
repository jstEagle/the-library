## 6. The Oldest Accounts

The vault door had no lock. That was the first thing Lior noticed, and the last thing they wanted to see.

Locks could be picked. Locks implied a thief might succeed. What the vault had instead was a seal of notation—a bar of music pressed into the iron in raised silver, the same clef that appeared on every ledger's spine—and beneath it, an inscription so old the letters had gone soft as river stones:

*What is counted cannot be uncounted.*

"You're hesitating," Sefa said behind them.

"I'm reading."

"Reading won't help. I've read everything there is to read about this place, and none of it mentions what happens to people who go down." She shifted the satchel on her shoulder. Inside it lay the copied star-glyph account, wrapped in oilcloth like something that might still be breathing. "Ovett will have found your copy missing by now."

"He'll have found *a* copy missing," Lior said. "I made three. One in my hand, one in a fair hand, one deliberately sloppy. He'll spend an hour deciding which forgery to be angry about."

Sefa almost smiled. It didn't reach her eyes. Nothing had reached her eyes since she'd read the entry and said, in a voice Lior would hear for the rest of their life, *"Lior. This isn't a debt the Choir holds. This is a debt the Choir owes."*

Now they stood at the bottom of the Counting House, past the counting floors and the ink stores and the dormitory where the junior scribes slept in rows like entries waiting to be filled in, at a corridor that appeared on no floor plan Lior had ever catalogued—which was its own kind of confession, because Lior had catalogued all of them. The corridor ended in the sealed door, and the door was warm.

"Why is it warm?"

"Because something down there is still being sung," Sefa said. "Two hundred years of nightly debits, remember? Whatever keeps the sky lit doesn't keep itself."

Lior pressed their palm flat against the iron. Through the metal, faint as a pulse through a wrist, they felt it: a sustained note, low, patient, older than any voice they'd ever heard. The wards had failed at moonrise three nights ago. The stars had gone out an hour later. But this note went on, indifferent, as if the collapse were a missed payment rather than a death.

"The conductor tried to stop it," Lior said quietly. "Whoever killed him. He must have learned what we're about to learn."

"Then let's learn it faster than his killers can catch us." Sefa reached past and laid her own hand beside Lior's. Her fingers were ink-stained to the second knuckle—she'd been a choirsister once, before whatever disgrace, and her hands still moved like a musician's even when they only held a pen. "On three. And if the note gets louder, we run."

The note did not get louder. The door simply stopped being closed.

There was no swing, no hinge, no grinding of ancient stone. One moment iron; the next, a stairway descending into dark, exhaling air that smelled of vellum and cold candle smoke and, underneath, something mineral and vast, like the inside of a mountain asked to hold its breath.

Lior went first. They told themselves it was because they knew the guild's ways better. They knew it was because if something down there took a payment, they had less to lose than anyone.

Their whole life, that had felt like a wound. Tonight, descending, it felt—for the first time—like a tool.

* * *

Sefa counted the steps. It was a habit from her choir days—you counted rests or you drowned in them—and by the two hundredth step she understood why no floor plan showed this place.

The stairs weren't in the guildhall. The guildhall was in the stairs.

She couldn't have proven it. But the walls had changed somewhere around the hundredth step, plaster giving way to dressed stone, stone to bedrock, and the bedrock bore marks: grooves cut in long parallel lines, evenly spaced, curving off into the dark above and below like the staves of a score written for an instrument the size of a country.

Because that was what it was. She'd seen borders drawn as lines on maps her whole life—the Vell with the Marchlands, the Marchlands with the sea. But maps were copies. Maps were for people who hadn't been let downstairs.

These were the originals. The border wasn't a line on a map. It was a line of music, and someone had carved it into the world's bones, and everything inside it—every field, every debt, every scribe who ever thought they were merely tired—was a note held in place by the singing.

"Lior," she said. Her voice came back wrong, flattened, as though the stairwell resented interruptions. "When we get down there, don't touch anything until I've looked at it."

"You're the appraiser."

"I'm the appraiser, yes. Which means I know that the most dangerous objects in any collection are the ones that are still owned." She kept her hand on the wall as she walked, feeling the grooves hum against her fingertips, a sensation like touching a throat mid-note. "Everything up there is owned by the Choir. Everything down here is what the Choir owns things *with*. There's a difference, and the difference will bite."

The stairs ended.

The chamber opened around them like the inside of a bell—not round, but resonant, shaped to return whatever sound entered it. Thousands of ledgers lined the walls, floor to unseen ceiling, each spine stamped with the clef, each shelf a line of a staff, so that the room itself was written. In the center, on a plinth of black stone, lay a single book, open, its pages blank.

And above it, hanging in the air without support, burned a small cold light. Not firelight. Starlight. A single star, caged, the last one left working.

"Oh," Sefa said. All her cleverness drained out through the floor. "Oh, you poor thing."

"Sefa—"

"It's the first one." She was moving toward it without deciding to, the way water moves downhill. "The original collateral. When they incorporated the country—when they *filed* it—they needed something to pledge. You can't float a nation on nothing; even then, even in the founding, the accounts had to balance. So they pledged a star. And every night since, the Choir has debited the star-glyph account to buy back one more hour of its light." She laughed, and it came out broken. "We've been paying ransom to ourselves for two centuries. The sky isn't failing because the wards broke. The sky is failing because the Choir is running out of soul to mortgage."

Lior stood very still by the plinth. "Then what's the blank book?"

"That's worse," Sefa said. "That's the founding ledger. That's the *first* one. Look at the cover."

Lior looked. Embossed in the leather, worn nearly smooth, was a title. Not *Ledger of Small Debts*. Not any of the working books upstairs.

*The Country, An Account.*

* * *

Lior read for a long time. Reading was the one thing they had never been afraid of.

The founding pages were written in a hand so controlled it barely seemed human—every letter identical, every numeral placed as if set by instrument rather than finger. The opening entry was simple, and simple was the worst thing about it:

*Item: one (1) country, comprising lands, waters, peoples, customs, and obligations entire.*
*Consideration received: continuity.*
*Term: perpetual, renewable by performance.*
*Collateral: see attached.*

Attached was everything. Every ledger Lior had ever balanced was a sub-entry here, a footnote to a footnote. The wars were reconciliations. The famines were write-downs. The Choir's songs—the ones sung at coronations and funerals and the small gray ceremonies in between—were not worship and not art. They were *servicing the debt*. Every hymn was a payment. Every payment kept the note sounding. Every hour the note sounded, the borders held, and the stars—the stars, plural, once—stayed lit on borrowed light.

And there, in the middle of the founding page, set apart in a box ruled in red:

*Clause of Withdrawal. Any debtor may exit the Account by signature at the Door Out, provided every living debtor countersigns. No partial withdrawals. No exceptions. The Account closes whole or not at all.*

Lior read it four times. Each time, the trap drew itself more completely around them.

Every living debtor. Every person born inside the borders was a debtor—had been since incorporation, since the founding hand had entered the entire population as *peoples, obligations entire*. Millions of names. Millions of signatures. And the Hollowed didn't count; erased names couldn't sign. So the Door Out required the consent of everyone the ledger still held, gathered in one place, holding one pen.

It was not a door. It was a joke told by accountants.

"There isn't a way out," Lior heard themselves say. Their voice sounded like someone else's entry. "There's a clause that exists so the founders could say one existed. 'You may leave whenever everyone leaves.' It's—it's a locked room with the key nailed to the inside of every mind in the country."

"Or," Sefa said slowly, "it's exactly what it looks like, and the founders never imagined a world where anyone would try."

"Nobody can try. You can't get signatures from the dead, and you can't get them from people who don't know they're signing."

"No." Sefa turned from the caged star. In its cold light her face looked younger and much harder. "But you could get them from people who *learn*. If every soul in the country understood what they were—if the debt were read aloud, publicly, in terms plain enough that no magister could obscure it—then consent stops being impossible and starts being a question. A terrible question. Asked of everyone at once." She touched the open founding ledger, not its pages but its edge, respectful as touching a sleeping animal. "The clause doesn't need obedience, Lior. It needs agreement. Those aren't the same thing, and the Choir has spent two hundred years making sure no one knows the difference."

Above them, muffled by two hundred steps of stone, a bell began to ring. Not the hour-bell. The audit-bell—three strokes, pause, three strokes. Lior knew its grammar the way they knew their own heartbeat. Three and three meant: *an account has been opened under suspicion.* Four and four would mean: *the holder is summoned.* Five and five—

Five and five had never been rung in Lior's lifetime.

The bell struck a fourth time. Paused. Struck again.

"They've found the copy," Lior said. "They've found all three copies, and they've decided which forgery to be angry about, and it's mine."

Sefa was already wrapping the star-glyph entry back into its oilcloth. "Then we take the founding ledger's location and we go up and face it."

"We can't take the book?"

"Look at it again." She nodded at the blank pages. "It's not blank because nothing's written. It's blank because it's *current*. The country is the open entry. You don't steal an open entry, Lior. You amend it—or it amends you."

The bell struck five. Paused. Struck five.

From the stairwell above, faint but unmistakable, came footsteps—many, unhurried, keeping perfect time, like a congregation coming down to sing.

Lior closed their eyes and did the only accounting that mattered: one scribe, one disgraced choirsister, one stolen page, one clause requiring millions of signatures, and between those numbers and the daylight, the full weight of the Choir, arriving at a measured pace.

"Back door?" Sefa asked.

"There's no back door."

"Of course there isn't." She slung the satchel across her body and squared her shoulders toward the stairs. "There's a clause. We just have to survive long enough to become a question the whole country answers."
