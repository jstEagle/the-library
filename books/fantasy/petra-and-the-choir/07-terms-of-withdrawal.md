## 7. Terms of Withdrawal

We came at the hour when the guild's candles were changed, because that was the only hour the Hollowed still understood. Once, we had all been people of schedules—matins ledgers, vespers tallies, the small liturgies of ink and arithmetic. Erasure had taken our names and our faces and most of our memories, but it had left us our habits, the way a burned house leaves its doorframe standing. So we besieged the Choir's guildhall at candle-changing, when the doors opened for the wax-carts, and we poured through the gap like smoke with intentions.

I will not pretend we were gentle.

The first wardens who tried to bar the counting hall found their torches guttering blue, because fear is a debt too, and we had learned to collect it. They saw us the way the ledger had made us: outlines of people, faces like wet paper, voices that arrived a half-second after our mouths moved. One young warden dropped his pike and began to pray to the stars, which was a cruel joke, since the stars had been out for three days now and his prayers fell on nothing but cold arithmetic.

"Stand aside," I told him, and my voice scraped like a quill running dry. "You're not in the book. You're not what we want."

He stood aside. Most of them did. The Hollowed are terrible, but we are not indiscriminate; we know exactly what it is to be collateral, and we do not make more of it.

* * *

The guildhall at night was a body with its heart cut out. Since the conductor's murder, no one had sung the wards back up, and the building knew it. The stones hummed wrong. The lamps burned with a nervous flicker, as if they suspected they might be next on the audit list. We moved through corridors lined with portraits of magisters whose names I almost remembered—I had been one of them once, or something adjacent to one—and every portrait's eyes seemed to track us with the particular horror of the recorded watching the unrecorded.

Sefa Quill found me before I found Lior Coldharbour.

"You shouldn't be here," she said. She had a knife out, which was honest of her, and her hand was steady, which was braver than honest. "Vess. Whatever you think you're owed—"

"I'm not owed anything." I let my outline settle as close to solid as it ever got these days, so she could see the ruin of me clearly. "That's rather the point. The ledger doesn't owe debts to things it has deleted. It owes nothing to nothing. That's the mathematics of it."

"The conductor—"

"Yes," I said. "We killed him."

She flinched. The knife wavered and steadied again. Behind her, at the far end of the corridor, a door opened on lamplight and the smell of cold tallow, and Lior Coldharbour stepped into it—small, ink-stained, wearing the exhausted face of someone who had stopped sleeping and started merely pausing between horrors.

"Ah," Lior said. "The Hollowed knock."

"We knocked," I said. "Then we entered. It's what erasure does to manners."

* * *

We spoke in the scriptorium, because Lior insisted on neutral ground, and because Sefa refused to leave them alone with me, and because I refused to speak anywhere near the vaults until I understood what Lior intended. Three people around a scarred oak table, with a dead fire and a hundred empty desks watching us like an audience that had already read the ending.

"Tell me what you want," Lior said.

"What I want?" I laughed, and the laugh came out of me late, thin, wrong-pitched, the way all my sounds did. "I want what you want, scribe. I've watched you for three nights through the guild's windows. You copy accounts you're forbidden to touch. You ask questions your superiors punish you for asking. You want out. The Door Out. The clause in the founding ledgers that lets a debtor withdraw from the Choir entire."

Lior's hands went flat on the table. Not surprise—calculation. I had forgotten how much I missed being able to calculate.

"And you don't want out," Lior said. "You want the book burned."

"I want the book *ended*. There's a difference, and it matters." I leaned forward, and the lamplight passed through my shoulder and pooled on the wood behind me, and I watched Lior watch that happen without flinching. Good. A flincher would have been useless to me. "Do you know what the ledger does when it closes an account? It doesn't kill you. Killing is a transaction—it moves something from one state to another, and transactions require entries. No. Closing an account *unwrites* you. Your mother forgets your face. Your debts transfer to strangers. Your grave, if you have one, reads *here lies the settlement of a minor obligation.* I have walked this city for eleven years, scribe. Shopkeepers sell me bread and cannot say afterward why their loaf count is short. Children see me in alleys and remember only a draft. I am the most patient ghost in the world, because I am not even a ghost. Ghosts are remembered."

Sefa's voice was rough. "Vess—"

"Eleven years," I repeated. "And there are hundreds of us. Thousands, if you count the ones erased so long ago they've stopped walking and just stand in places they used to live. And here is what I learned in those eleven years, Lior Coldharbour, the thing I killed the conductor to learn: the Door Out is a lie of scale. One signature saves one debtor. The ledger simply rebalances. Every coin withdrawn is minted back out of someone else's life. You sign yourself free, and some child in the lowtown wards wakes up owing two more years of soul-rent, and the sky stays lit, and the country keeps singing its one long lie, and nothing ends."

"That's not—" Lior started.

"It is. You've read the founding ledgers. You've seen the notation the borders are drawn in. Tell me I'm wrong."

Silence. The dead fire ticked as it cooled further into itself. Lior looked down at their own hands—those careful, ruined-nail, ink-stained hands—and I saw the exact moment the justice of my argument landed, and the exact moment the horror of it landed right behind, because they arrive together, always, those two. Justice and horror are a matched set. The Choir taught us that. We just kept forgetting.

"Burning the ledger," Lior said slowly, "unmakes everyone in it. Not just the Choir. Everyone. The whole country is collateral—the borders, the stars, the wards, the people. You'd be freeing the erased by annihilating the recorded. That's not liberation, Vess. That's just deciding which half of the wound gets to close."

"Yes," I said. "And?"

"And I can't—" Lior pressed the heels of their hands against their eyes. "Do you know what I am? Did you read my entry, before you came here to recruit me?"

"I read everything," I said. "Collateral since before birth. Your grandmother's signature. Two hundred years of nightly debits against the star account, and you attached to it like a barnacle to a hull. You were never a person to the Choir, scribe. You were a fixture. An anchor-point. Which is precisely why you're useful."

"Useful for burning everything."

"Useful for *choosing*," I said, and I put as much of my lost voice into it as I could, which wasn't much, but enough. "That's all any of us are asking for, in the end. The Choir chose for us. It chose for your grandmother, and for the conductor, and for every star it lights with borrowed souls. I am offering you the one thing this country has never permitted anyone: a real choice, made with open eyes, about whether the lie continues. Burn it, and everyone ends honestly. Sign, and you end comfortably while the machine grinds on. Those are the terms. I didn't write them. I just refuse to pretend there's a third option."

For a long moment, nobody spoke. Somewhere above us, the guild's remaining magisters were arguing in a room with a sealed door, and their voices came through the floorboards as pure vibration, wordless, like the building itself muttering.

Then Lior lowered their hands, and their eyes were dry and terribly clear.

"There's always a third option," they said. "That's the whole disease of this place—that it trained us to believe in binary ledgers. Debit or credit. Pay or be paid. Burn or sign. But a ledger is just a language, Vess, and languages can say anything. You want restoration. Your names back. To be remembered. That's not destruction—that's an *amendment*. A correction entry. And corrections require a pen authorized to make them."

I felt something move through my half-real chest that I had no name for anymore. Hope, maybe. Hope is the cruelest residue; erasure takes everything except the capacity to want.

"The founding pen," Sefa breathed. "The one that wrote the country."

"If it wrote the borders in notation," Lior said, "it can write a rest. A whole page of rests. A general discharge—not withdrawal, not annihilation. Closure. Every account settled fairly, every name returned to its owner, the Choir dissolved not as a murder but as a *completion*. The song ends because the song is finished. Not because someone tore the sheet music."

"And the cost?" I asked, because there is always a cost; I had been one, once. "What does the ledger charge for closing every account in the book?"

Lior was quiet for a moment. When they answered, they didn't look at me, and I understood that they already knew, and that knowing had happened sometime in the last three sleepless nights, alone, among the forbidden accounts.

"I'll pay it," they said. "Whatever it is. But I need you to get me to the Door alive, because Ovett knows what I am now, and the surviving magisters would sooner sing the city down than let a piece of collateral pick up the founding pen. So. Terms of withdrawal, Vess: you and yours clear the way to the vaults and hold off whatever the guild sends. In exchange, I swear on the oldest account in the book—on the star glyph itself—that I will restore every name the ledger erased. All of you. Written back. Remembered."

It was a bad bargain. I knew it was a bad bargain. Debtor's oaths are the only currency the Hollowed can still spend, and Lior Coldharbour was offering to spend themselves on our behalf, and every instinct the erasure had left in me screamed to take the deal before they came to their senses.

But I had been a magister once. I had audited souls. I knew what an oath on the star glyph weighed.

"Swear it properly," I said. "In ink. Tonight. Before we move."

Lior nodded once, already reaching for the satchel of pens and pots they carried like other people carry weapons.

Outside, far off across the dark and starless city, a bell began to ring—not the guild's bells, but the lowtown chapel, tolling the alarm for a breach in the border wards. The sound rolled over the guildhall like a wave over a seawall, and beneath my feet I felt the vaults answering, deep and resonant, the founding ledgers humming in their seals.

Something down there had heard us talking.

And something down there, I thought, watching Lior's face go pale in the lamplight, had started counting.
