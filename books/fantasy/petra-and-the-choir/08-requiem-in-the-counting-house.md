## 8. Requiem in the Counting House

They gathered us in the Counting House at dusk, all four hundred of us—scribes, clerks, binders, the ink-mixers and the ledger-walkers—and they told us it was a muster for safety. They told us the wards would be restored by morning and we should stand quietly while the magisters worked.

They did not tell us what the work was made of.

I knew. I had known since I copied the star-glyph account, since Sefa read it aloud in her shop with her voice shaking like a struck string: *the Choir's own soul, mortgaged nightly to keep the sky lit.* The wards had never run on ink or prayer or the conductor's genius. They ran on voices. On names entered into the ledger and debited until nothing remained but the singing itself. The guild staff were not employees. We were inventory.

Magister Ovett stood on the counting dais beneath the great brass scales that had weighed nothing honest in two hundred years. She had changed into her ceremonial vestments—the black-and-gold of the First Verse—and behind her the surviving magisters arranged themselves in crescent formation, eleven of them where there had once been thirty. The Hollowed's siege had thinned their ranks. So had the truth, which is a weapon slower than a knife but just as fatal to men who live by books.

"Citizens of the Choir," Ovett said, and her voice carried the way trained voices carry, pitched to fill a hall without effort because effort was for people who could be spent. "The city panics. The stars are dark. The borders bleed. But the Choir has kept this country singing through plague, famine, and war, and it will keep singing now. Stand in your rows. When the verse begins, do not be afraid."

*Do not be afraid.* It was almost funny. Fear was the one thing they'd never needed to order from us; we came with it built in, like the debt.

I stood in the third row, between a binder named Corrin who hummed when he worked and an old ink-mixer whose hands were stained permanently blue. Around us, four hundred people shuffled into the chalked lines on the floor—lines I now recognized as notation. We weren't standing in rows. We were standing in a score.

The magisters began to tune.

It started as a sound below hearing, a pressure in the teeth, in the breastbone. The great chandelier of the Counting House—a wheel of silver tines that had hung silent my whole life—began to shiver. Light crawled along its arms like frost forming in reverse. And I felt it then, felt it in the place behind my ribs where my name lived: a hook going in.

Not mine. Not yet. The rite began at the bottom of the ledger, with the smallest debts, the oldest entries, the voices least able to object. I saw it happen before I understood it. In the second row, a young clerk named Tam went rigid, his mouth opening, and out of him came a note—pure, silver, terrible—and with the note came *him*, the color draining out of his face, his hair going grey at the temples in the space of three seconds, his eyes fixing on nothing as something essential was drawn up through his throat and into the chandelier, which blazed brighter.

The first tine lit. The first voice was spent.

A woman near him screamed. The magisters sang louder. That was the whole of the guild's answer to screaming: more song.

"They're burning them," Corrin whispered beside me. His hand found my sleeve. "Lior, they're *burning* them, that's not—that's not how the rites go, is it? That's not how it's supposed to—" He stopped, because he had been here twenty years and he knew exactly how it was supposed to go. It was supposed to go like this. It had always gone like this. We simply hadn't been shown the page.

The second tine lit. A binder I'd shared lunches with for a decade folded like wet paper, and her voice rose out of her, gorgeous and unwilling, and joined the growing chord. The chandelier turned. Slowly. Like a thing waking.

Above us, faintly, through the vaulted ceiling, I could feel the wards reaching down for the fuel. The border-song, broken since moonrise, hungry since the stars went out. Ovett had promised the sky a meal and the sky intended to collect.

My grandmother sold me into this book before I drew breath. I had learned that in a cold room with Ovett's pen scratching against the silence, learning that my invisibility had been purchased, that my whole careful life of being overlooked was a line item. I had walked out of that interrogation with cold clarity and no plan, only a direction: *down*, toward the vaults, toward the Door Out, toward the clause that required every living debtor's signature.

But every living debtor was here, standing in a score, being spent one tine at a time to patch a lie over the sky.

You cannot save people who have been rendered into fuel. You can only stop the fire.

I looked at the chandelier—at the mechanism of it, the silver tines, each one keyed, I realized with a sick lurch, to a specific account. Each tine a name. Each name a voice. And the master control, the conductor's podium, empty since the murder, stood at the center of the dais with its open score and its baton rest shaped like a closed fist.

Between me and it: three rows of the doomed, two magisters, and Magister Ovett herself.

I had spent my whole life being invisible. Being overlooked. Being the entry no one read twice. Every skill I had, every survival I'd carved out of the guild's indifference, had been training for exactly one act: getting somewhere I wasn't supposed to be, unnoticed.

I slipped out of the row during the crescendo, when the chord swelled and every eye that still belonged to its owner was fixed on the light.

* * *

Moving through the Counting House during the rite was like moving through deep water. The song had weight. It pressed on my shoulders, tried to turn my feet, tried to fold me back into my place in the score—I felt the notation of the floor tugging at my ankles like a current, the music wanting its notes in order. Three times I staggered. Three times I thought of Tam's grey face and kept moving.

The first magister I passed didn't see me. Of course she didn't. No one had ever seen me; that was the point of me. She stood with her arms raised, pouring her trained voice into the chord, sweat running down into her collar, spending herself as gladly as she'd spend any of us.

The second one saw me. An old man, Magister Halle, his eyes flicking sideways mid-verse, widening. His mouth formed my name—or tried to. The rite held his voice; he could not shout, could not break the chord without breaking himself. He reached for me instead, fingers grasping, and I did the only thing I could think of: I showed him the ledger page I'd torn from my copy, the star-glyph account, two hundred years of nightly debits, and I watched him read it even as he grabbed for me. I watched him understand. His grip loosened. His eyes went to the chandelier, to the blazing tines, to the row where Tam stood hollow-eyed and grey.

Halle let go of me. Then, deliberately, he turned his face back to the front and stopped singing.

He didn't fall like the others. He fell like a man setting down a weight he'd carried too long. The chord faltered where his part should have been, and Ovett's head snapped around, and in that half-second of her divided attention, I reached the podium.

The conductor's score lay open. The Requiem for the Wards, full orchestration, the guild staff marked throughout as *continuo*—the sustaining part, the part that plays underneath everything and never stops. Continuo. Fuel. We had always been the continuo. The word had been sitting in plain sight on every program, every ceremony, my entire life, and I had read it as an honor.

The baton rested in its fist-shaped cradle. I took it. It was lighter than I expected, bone-white, warm, as if it remembered hands.

Ovett saw me then.

She didn't stop singing—she couldn't, not without collapsing the half-built ward and killing everyone the rite had already touched—but her eyes found mine across the dais and I watched her calculate, in real time, the cost of me versus the cost of stopping. I watched her decide I was affordable. She redirected the chord, bent the whole rite toward me, and the music *turned*, a wall of sound with intent behind it, and I felt my own name begin to pull loose from my body, felt the hook set deep—

And I slammed the baton down across the podium's edge and snapped it in half.

* * *

The silence afterward was the loudest thing I have ever heard.

Every bound voice came home at once. Four hundred stolen notes reversed themselves in a single instant, and the Counting House filled with the sound of people falling—not dying, most of them, though some did not rise, some had been drawn too thin—but *returning*, gasping, clutching their own throats, weeping at the unfamiliar weight of their names back inside them. Tam collapsed into the arms of the woman behind him, young again, sobbing. The chandelier guttered, its tines darkening one by one like a city at curfew, and the half-built ward above us unraveled with a sound like a sigh of relief.

The magisters' chord died in their throats. Eleven trained voices, and not one of them knew how to make a sound that wasn't commanded.

Ovett stood alone on the dais amid the wreckage of her requiem, and for a long moment she said nothing at all. Then: "Do you know what you've done?"

"Yes," I said. My voice cracked on it, and I found I didn't care. "I've done the arithmetic. For once, I've done it correctly." I held up the torn ledger page, the star-glyph account, so the whole room could see it. "This is what your wards cost. This is what your country costs. Every night, for two hundred years. And tonight you were going to pay with *us*."

"The alternative is the dark," Ovett said. "The borders will fail. The Hollowed will pour through. Everything ends."

"Then let it end honestly," I said, and heard Vess's words come out of my mouth, and did not take them back.

Something shifted in the room. Not the crowd—though the crowd was shifting too, rising off the floor, stepping out of the chalked notation, four hundred people discovering simultaneously that they could. What shifted was deeper. Beneath us, beneath the guildhall, beneath the founding stone, something old heard a clause break and responded the way any locked thing responds when its key turns.

The floor of the Counting House split along a seam no one had ever noticed, a seam drawn—of course—in the shape of a clef. Stone ground against stone. Dust breathed up from the dark. And the vaults beneath the guildhall, sealed for two centuries, opened themselves from the inside.

Down in that darkness, I could hear paper. Thousands of pages, stirring like wings.

Ovett lunged for me. Halle's hand caught her arm, and then Corrin's caught her other one, and then there were simply too many hands, four hundred of them, belonging to people who were done being counted.

I went down the stairs into the oldest accounts, and the dark closed over me like water—and somewhere below, faint but unmistakable, someone was already waiting.
