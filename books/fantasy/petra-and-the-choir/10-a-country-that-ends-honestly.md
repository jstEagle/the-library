## 10. A Country That Ends Honestly

The stars came back on a Tuesday.

Sefa Quill knew it was a Tuesday because the fishmonger's boy told her so, shouting it up and down Saltrow with the same breathless urgency he'd once used for the price of herring. *Stars! Stars tonight!* As if the sky were a shipment everyone had been waiting on.

She was sweeping the floor of her new shop when the first one lit—a thin white point above the eastern hills, where the border had been, where the notation had been, where nothing in particular was now except land that belonged to no one. She leaned the broom against the doorframe and watched until her neck ached. By full dark there were hundreds of them, then thousands, scattered across the black like salt across a tablecloth, and no two of them arranged in any pattern at all. That was the part that made her cry, quietly, into her apron: not their return but their disorder. For as long as anyone could remember, the stars had sung in parts—had been *made* to sing in parts, scored by hands in a guildhall vault—and now they were just lights. Burning for nobody. Owed to nobody.

Free things, she thought, were always slightly ugly at first. Then you learned to love them honestly.

* * *

The shop had been a chandler's before the collapse, and before that a counting house of some minor trade guild that had dissolved itself the moment its members understood what dissolution now meant—that they could simply *stop*, walk out, be people instead of entries. The room still smelled faintly of tallow and old ink. Sefa had spent three weeks scrubbing the walls where someone had chalked balances in columns taller than she was. The ghost-arithmetic wouldn't quite come off. She'd decided to let it stay. A country that ends honestly should keep some record of what it used to be.

She called the shop what it was: **Honest Appraisals**. No forbidden valuations. No reading of souls against collateral. If you brought her a ring, she told you what the ring was worth—the metal, the stone, the maker's hand—and nothing else. If you brought her a deed, she told you whether the paper was sound. If you brought her a question about who you owed and how much, she told you the only answer that mattered anymore:

"Nothing. You owe nothing. Nobody's keeping the books."

They didn't always believe her. The first month, half the city came through her door just to hear it said aloud, the way you visit a well in a drought. An old ferryman wept at her counter. A woman brought her daughter, six years old, born after the fall, and asked Sefa to explain to her what a debt had been, because the word survived in songs and the meaning hadn't. Sefa explained as gently as she could: once, everything you were was written down somewhere, and what was written down could be collected.

"And now?" the girl asked.

"Now," Sefa said, "you're just yours."

The girl considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. "That sounds lonely."

Sefa laughed until she had to wipe her eyes, and gave her a dried apple from the jar she kept behind the counter, and did not say: *yes, child, sometimes it is.*

* * *

Because here was the thing Sefa could not appraise, could not price, could not enter in any honest column: she remembered him.

Everyone else had lost Lior Coldharbour the way you lose a word on the tip of your tongue. They walked past the guildhall ruins—half fallen, half simply *emptied*, its vaults open to the weather like the ribs of a whale—and felt a vague sorrow without knowing its name. They found scribes' cells with beds neatly made and no memory of whose bed it was. Ovett's body had been buried in an unmarked grave paid for by public subscription; no one recalled why they'd felt they ought to. The ledger itself was gone—not burned, not hidden, but *unwritten*, every page blank, every binding empty, as if the book had finally become what it had always pretended to be: paper.

But Sefa remembered.

She remembered because she had never been in the book. Erased people don't forget each other—that was the Hollowed's whole bitter truth, the wound Vess had carried across decades of being half-real. And whatever Lior had done on the final page, whatever general discharge had swept through every account in creation like a tide going out, it had not erased Sefa. It had only tried. She felt it some mornings as a softness at the edge of him, his face going slightly blurred in her mind like ink touched by rain—and every morning she pulled him back, deliberately, the way you'd pull a drowning stranger to shore. *Lior Coldharbour. Scribe. Inherited debt. Quiet dread beneath tedium. Chose the third path with open eyes.* She said it like a litany. She said it like a debt she was paying on purpose, the only kind left in the world.

Vess remembered too. The Hollowed—all of them, restored or not, and Sefa still wasn't sure which they were; they flickered at dusk like candles in a draft—came by the shop on the anniversary of the fall. Vess stood in the doorway a long time, examining her with those eyes that had seen the inside of erasure.

"You kept him," Vess said.

"I keep what I'm given."

"He isn't given. He's *gone*. He paid himself out of existence to unmake the thing that owned us. There's no residue. There shouldn't be a residue." Vess's voice cracked on the last word, and Sefa saw, for the first time, that under all that hunger for annihilation Vess had wanted exactly what everyone wanted—to matter to somebody, somewhere, unpaid.

"There's a residue," Sefa said. "I'm the residue."

Vess looked at her for a long moment. Then, awkwardly, like someone relearning an old gesture, they bowed—the small bow of one debtor to another, from a country that no longer existed—and left a sprig of starflower on her counter, and went out into the evening, flickering, real enough.

* * *

The blank book arrived in winter.

No courier claimed it. It was simply there one morning among the ordinary stock—a parcel of good paper, a box of pens, a bolt of ribbon—sitting square in the middle of her counter as though it had grown there overnight. Plain boards, undyed cloth spine, no title. When she opened it, every page was empty, and the emptiness had a quality she recognized from her disgraced years: not absence but *attention*. Pages that were waiting.

She knew better than to write in it. Some books are doors, and doors swing both ways.

So she set it on the counter where the light fell, and she sold her honest appraisals around it, and life went on in the manner of life everywhere now—ungoverned, unscored, occasionally chaotic, frequently beautiful. The border towns argued over wells and married across old lines that meant nothing. Children grew up who had never heard a ward-song and could not have hummed one if they tried. Once a quarter, someone proposed rebuilding the Choir, and once a quarter the proposal died of laughter.

And sometimes—she could not predict it, could not summon it, could only notice afterward, the way you notice you've been happy—there came a faint stirring from the counter. Not wind. The shop was tight as a drum. But the pages would turn, softly, one after another, a slow deliberate riffling, as if a hand were moving through them. Reading. Or remembering. Or perhaps—this was the thought she allowed herself on the good nights, the nights when the grief and the hope braided together so tightly she couldn't tell which strand was which—perhaps writing. Perhaps somewhere on the far side of the unwritten page, a scribe who had given away his name was keeping the only ledger left in the world: a private accounting of gratitude, entered line by line, for the one person who refused to forget him.

She never spoke to it. Speaking felt like asking for change back from a gift.

But she developed habits. She left the lamp burning late. She turned the book to face the window on clear nights, so it could see the stars—his stars now, unowned, singing to no conductor at all. And on the first anniversary of the Tuesday the sky came back, she stood behind the counter with a cup of tea going cold in her hands and said, quietly, to the empty shop and the turning pages and the country ending honestly outside her door:

"The accounts are closed, Lior. All of them. Even mine."

The pages stilled.

Then, very faintly, from nowhere—from the space between one page and the next—came something she had not heard since the night she'd read the star-glyph entry and gone pale over her own table. A single note. Clear, warm, unhurried. Not a ward. Not a payment. Just a voice, saying hello.

Sefa set down her tea, opened the blank book to its first empty page, and picked up a pen.

"Well then," she said, smiling through it. "Let's start a new one."

And the hand on the other side of the page began, at last, to write.
