## 1. The Ledger of Small Debts

The Vellum Guild kept its ledgers the way other institutions kept their dead: carefully, privately, and with a great deal of money changing hands over the question of who got to look at them.

Mara Winterbourne had spent eleven years among those books, and in all that time he had never once appeared in one.

He worked the night shift by design. The counting room on the third floor of the Guildhall was a long chamber of black oak and green-shaded lamps, and at night it belonged to him alone—him, the ledgers, and the soft tick of the memory-clocks that measured not hours but decay, each clock wound to a different client's account, each hand falling a little further behind as the memories it stood for faded from their sellers' heads. A good auditor learned to read the clocks before he read the books. The books could lie. The clocks only told you how much time a man had left to regret what he'd sold.

Tonight there were forty-one entries to audit. Mara took off his coat, hung it on the peg behind the door—the peg was his, unofficially; the door was always locked when he worked—and sat down at the sloped desk nearest the window, where the fog off the harbor came up the glass like something trying to read over his shoulder.

He opened the first ledger.

*Ledger of Small Debts, Volume IX.* The guild's economy ran on three tiers, and this was the bottom one: griefs, fears, small joys, the ordinary coin of ordinary lives. A fisherman's terror of deep water, purchased from him after a storm had nearly drowned him and sold onward to a playwright who needed to write drowning convincingly. A baker's memory of her mother's hands kneading dough, bought when she could no longer afford flour and needed the money more than the past. Small debts. Small sums. The guild called it the kindness trade, because what they took was usually something people were relieved to be rid of, and what they gave back was rent, or bread, or a coffin for a child.

Mara did not think of it as kindness. He thought of it as arithmetic, and arithmetic was safe. Numbers did not weep at night. Numbers did not stand outside your window in the rain wondering whose childhood they were wearing.

He checked the first entry against its clock. *One grief, widow's grade, seller: Maren Ostrey, dockside. Purchased: 14th of Frostmonth. Decay rate: slow. Current value: unchanged.*

The grief belonged, as of tonight, to no one. It sat in its vial in the vault below like a jarred eel, waiting for a buyer. Mara made his notation, blotted it, moved on.

* * *

The second entry was the reason he'd asked for the night shift in the first place.

A trade, scheduled for midnight: the Widow Ostrey's grief for a merchant's courage. The merchant was one Aldous Vey, who owned four ships and was afraid of exactly one thing, which was the sea, and who had discovered that a man afraid of the sea cannot sign contracts without his hand shaking, and that his partners had begun to notice. He wanted the fear out. The guild had a fear in stock—a widow's grief was not a fear, but grief and fear traded close enough in weight that the ledger-men could balance them with a small adjustment fee—and so at midnight the two parties would come to the Salt Door, the guild's public entrance, and walk out again lighter and heavier respectively, and neither would remember precisely what they had lost, only that they could now breathe.

Mara's job was to make sure the weights balanced. If the guild gave too little, the client felt cheated in some way he couldn't name, and cheated men talked. If the guild gave too much, the margin died, and margins were the only scripture the Master of Ledgers truly believed in.

At half past eleven, Mara went down.

The Salt Door opened onto a street that smelled of tar and cold iron. The widow arrived first. She was younger than the term *widow* suggested—young enough that the grief she carried had been a husband of two years, not fifty—and she walked like a woman carrying water, careful and level, as though any jolt might spill it. That was how grief behaved in the days before sale. Afterward, sellers always described the same strange lightness, the same terrible floating feeling, like a tooth finally pulled.

"You're the ledger-man," she said. Not a question. "They said someone would explain the paper."

"I'll explain the paper." Mara set the contract on the table between them. "You sell one grief. Yours. It goes into the vault, and it stays there until someone buys it, and then it's theirs, and you won't feel it anymore. You'll know you had a husband. You'll know he died. You'll know everything except—" he searched for the honest phrasing, the one the guild preferred and the one he'd given so many times it had worn smooth as a river stone, "—except the weight of it."

"And if I want it back someday?"

"Buy-back is possible for seven years. After that, decay sets in, and what's in the vial is no longer quite yours. The guild will still sell it to you." He said it flatly, because it was true, and because lying about buy-backs was the kind of lie that came back through the door with a knife in it. "But it will feel like remembering someone else's dream."

She looked at the contract a long moment. Then she signed, and her hand did not shake, and Mara thought—not for the first time—that the steadiest hands in the city all belonged to people about to amputate something.

Vey arrived as she left, wrapped in a coat worth more than her tenancy, sweating despite the cold. He barely glanced at the contract. Men buying courage never read; men selling it always did. Mara noted that too, and filed it under the general law of the trade: *what a man will not read tells you what he believes he deserves.*

The exchange itself happened upstairs, handled by a broker, sealed with wax and a drop of blood apiece. Mara watched the clocks afterward. The widow's clock stopped. Vey's began to run backward, which was how the guild's instruments showed a fear leaving a man—as time returned to him, minute by reclaimed minute.

By one in the morning it was done, and Aldous Vey walked out into the fog standing straighter, and somewhere across the city a woman would wake tomorrow knowing her husband had died but unable to find the place inside herself where that fact used to live. She would grieve, the brokers said, in a cleaner way. Grief without weight. Grief as information.

Mara climbed back to the counting room and wrote the entry into Volume IX in his small, exact hand:

*Traded: one grief (Ostrey) against one fear (Vey), adjustment fee 4%. Balanced.*

Then he did the thing he did every night, the thing that was less a task than a ritual: he audited himself.

* * *

There was no page with his name on it. There had never been a page with his name on it. This was not an accident.

Most people believed the guild recorded everything, and the guild encouraged the belief, because a city that thinks you see all will confess anything. But the truth was that the ledgers recorded only what passed through the guild's hands—every memory bought, sold, traded, pawned, stolen, or destroyed—and a man could live alongside that river his whole life without ever touching the water. Mara had decided at fourteen, though he could not have told you why with any certainty, that he would be such a man.

No debts. That was the first rule, and it meant more than money. He owed no favors, held no grudges on record, had never taken credit against a future self. No debts owed, either: no one alive had a claim on him, no broker could say *Winterbourne, you'll remember this*, no client could point to a vial and say *that piece of him came from me*. And no name in any book but his own—he kept a private notebook, cheap paper, unnumbered pages, locked in his rooms, in which he wrote down nothing but the weather and the occasional price of candles. Even that book was more habit than need. Habit was the closest thing to a self he permitted himself.

It had cost him. He knew that. There were evenings—rare, and getting rarer—when he caught himself trying to remember his mother's face and finding, instead of a face, a smooth professional blankness, the blankness of a ledger line awaiting entry. He assumed he had simply never bothered to store the memory well. A man who keeps nothing keeps nothing, even of himself. That was the price of safety, and he paid it gladly, the way you pay rent: without love, without complaint, on time.

Because he had seen what recording cost. He saw it every night. Every name in these books was a hook, and somewhere above every hook hung a man or woman who could be lifted by it—blackmailed, hunted, repossessed. The guild rarely repossessed. But it could. That was the point. In Vellum City, the difference between freedom and slavery was a signature you'd forgotten giving, and Mara Winterbourne had never given one.

He finished the self-audit, as he did every night, by running his finger down the master index—the great alphabetical roll of everyone the guild had ever touched—and confirming that the W's contained no Winterbourne. There was a Waxman, a Wagstaffe, a family of Weatherspoons who had sold their entire family history in a single lot during the famine year. No Winterbourne. No Mara.

"Clean," he said aloud, to the empty room, and the word tasted the way clean linen smells.

* * *

He worked until the fourth bell, closing entries. Near the end he came to a loose folio tucked into the back of Volume IX—an interoffice memo, the kind the Master circulated when he wanted something done without appearing to have ordered it. This one concerned the deep archive: a request that the sub-basement collections be inventoried before quarter's end, *with particular attention to lots acquired prior to the current administration.* Prior to the current administration meant older than twenty years. Twenty years was a date the guild did not discuss. Twenty years was the Cartographic Erasure, when the empire had paid a fortune—not in coin, the story went, but in something better—to have a country removed from every map, chart, atlas, and sailor's tongue, and the guild had been the broker. What exactly the empire had paid, and what exactly the guild had sold, was known to perhaps three living men, and the Master of Ledgers was one of them.

Mara read the memo twice, filed it, and thought no more about it. Old lots were old lots. Dust was dust. He had a creed, and the creed had served him for thirty-one years, and the creed said: *the past is inventory, and inventory is not yours.*

Outside, the fog pressed at the windows. Somewhere below, in the vault, forty thousand vials sat in their dark racks, each one a life reduced to a stoppered glass, each one labeled in a hand like his own—small, exact, anonymous. He blew out the lamps one by one, locked the ledgers, locked the desk, locked the door behind him, and stood a moment in the corridor listening to the building settle around its secrets.

He was walking home through the salt-stink streets at the fifth bell, collar up, footsteps making the only sound in the world, when he saw the smear of blood on the steps of his own hidden stair.

Not much of it. A handprint, dragged, going up.

Mara stood very still in the fog. His heart performed the small cold arithmetic it always performed: *no debts, no claims, no name.* Whoever was bleeding in his rooms had made a mistake, and mistakes could be corrected, and correction was simply another kind of audit.

He told himself that all the way up the stairs. It was the last night he ever fully believed it.
