## 11. The Audit

The snow had started an hour before dawn, and by the time Corvo reached the guild hall it was falling with the particular patience of a winter that intended to stay. He stood a moment in the courtyard, letting the flakes settle on his shoulders without brushing them off. There was a discipline to arriving at the Master's door already marked by weather—it said he had come from somewhere, that he had been out in the world doing the work the world required, and not merely sleeping in one of the warm cells beneath the archive like some of the senior auditors who had forgotten what their ledgers cost.

The Vellum Guild's hall did not look like a place where memories were bought and sold. It looked like what it pretended to be: a counting house, respectable and grey, its windows tall and narrow, its stone the color of old milk. Merchants came here to settle accounts of coin and grain. Only the second floor—the floor with no windows—held the other kind of ledger, the vellum pages that drank a life and gave back silence.

Corvo climbed the stairs to the Master's study, and the climb hurt more than it should have. Three days of hard riding from the coast, two of them following a trail that went cold in a way trails should not go cold—Mara Winterbourne's tracks ending at a shrine in the hills as neatly as if a hand had lifted him off the page of the world. Corvo knew how Mara moved. He had taught him half of it himself, years ago, when they were both young men learning the guild's quiet trades: how to walk so that doors didn't notice you, how to stand in a room until the room forgot you were there. Corvo had never quite mastered it. That was why he'd been made an enforcer instead of an auditor. You could not enforce what you could not see.

But Mara had vanished like smoke going up a chimney, and Sera Ilvaren with him, and now Corvo stood dripping in front of the Master's door with a report that would decide whether his oldest friend lived or died.

He knocked.

"Enter," said the Master, though Corvo had the sudden irrational certainty that the man had known he was standing outside for some time. The Master of Ledgers always knew. It was said—he had said it himself, often enough, in the tone of a joke—that the Master remembered everything that entered his building, including things that had not yet happened.

* * *

The study was small, which always surprised people. A man with the Master's power might be expected to keep a hall. Instead he kept a cell: one desk, one lamp, one chair for visitors, and walls lined not with books but with ledgers, thousands of them, spines outward, each one a life or a fragment of one. The air smelled of tallow and cold iron and, faintly, of something sweeter underneath—the smell of the vials themselves, the memory-glass, which Corvo had never been able to describe except as the smell of someone else's summer.

"Sit," the Master said. He was a narrow man, older than the building seemed, with hands like folded paper and eyes the pale grey of a page left blank too long. "You've ridden hard. Report."

Corvo sat. He had rehearsed this on the road, and rehearsed it again in the courtyard, and now found that all his rehearsals had been built on a decision he had not yet made.

"Mara Winterbourne left the coast three nights ago," he said. "Heading into the high passes. The lighthouse keeper confirmed it."

"The keeper." The Master's voice was mild. "And what did the keeper confirm, exactly? That he saw them? Or that he remembers seeing them?"

"There's a difference?"

"There is always a difference, Corvo. That is the first lesson of our trade, and you learned it thirty years ago." The Master turned a page on his desk—a page Corvo realized, with a small cold shock, was the coastal audit, the record of the keeper's own purchased memories. "A man who has sold his grief can still describe his wife's funeral. Whether what describes is what remains—that is the question we exist to answer."

"He saw them," Corvo said. "Both of them. And he let them pass."

Something moved behind the Master's blank eyes—not anger; Corvo had never seen the Master angry—but the stillness deepened, the way water deepens before ice.

"Let them pass," the Master repeated. "Through fog that our own maps call open sea. Past a light that does not appear on any chart the empire recognizes." He closed the audit gently, as one closes a book whose author has disappointed. "Do you know what I paid for that coastline, Corvo? Not in coin. In patience. Twenty years of patience, keeping every memory of those waters locked and catalogued, waiting for the day the sale matured. And one mad old keeper with a hoarded recollection of a horizon undoes it in an evening."

"He's half-mad," Corvo said. "They all are, the ones who kept pieces. That's what hoarding does."

"Yes." The Master regarded him. "And yet the country holds together anyway, doesn't it? Inside their heads. Stubbornly. Like a fire banked under ash." He folded his hands. "Which brings us to your report, and to the part of it you are deciding whether to give me. You've been composing it since the courtyard. Your face does that when you're arithmetic-ing, Corvo. It always has."

Corvo felt heat rise in his neck and hated it. Thirty years in the guild and the old man could still strip him to the studs with a sentence.

"You know about the map," he said.

"I know it is absent from my books," the Master said. "That is worse than knowing about it. An object recorded can be tracked, priced, recovered. An absence spreads. Absences are how countries die, Corvo—you above all people should appreciate that, you wrote half the entries." He leaned forward slightly. "Tell me about the shrine."

So Corvo told him. The trail ending at the hillside shrine where the confiscated caches were stored—the memories seized over the years from smugglers and hoarders, bottled and shelved in the dark behind the altar stone. The trail ending there because Mara had gone inside, and what came out had gone somewhere no track could follow. And—one detail Corvo had turned over and over on the ride, like a coin he suspected was clipped—one vial missing from the shelf, its label scraped clean, the wax seal broken on the ground.

"A vial," the Master said softly. "Labeled with what?"

"I don't know. The label was scraped."

"But you suspect."

Corvo looked at the wall of ledgers. Somewhere on those shelves was the audit of a boy's childhood, sold twenty-two years ago by a father with debts and ambitions, a sale Corvo had helped process without ever reading past the lot number. He had been young. He had told himself then, as he told himself now, that the guild only kept accounts; it did not make them.

"I suspect," Corvo said, "that Mara Winterbourne just got back a piece of himself that your vault has held since before either of us understood what we were holding."

The silence that followed was long enough for the lamp to gutter once. When the Master spoke again, his voice had changed—not warmer, but quieter, the way a man speaks when he stops performing for a subordinate and starts thinking aloud in front of one. Corvo had heard that voice perhaps four times in thirty years, and every time, afterward, he had wished he hadn't.

"Do you know what the Cartographic Erasure actually was?" the Master asked. "Not what the histories say—we wrote the histories, so you may discount them. Do you know what was done?"

"An empire wanted a country gone. We brokered it. Every atlas amended, every record burned or rewritten, every memory of Ilvaren bought or confiscated until the borders had nothing left to stand on. The country didn't fall. It was unrecorded." Corvo had given this summary a hundred times, to apprentices, to nervous merchants. It sounded almost clinical. Almost clean.

"That is the transaction," the Master agreed. "Now tell me what was purchased."

Corvo frowned. "The erasure itself. The empire paid for—"

"No. Think like an auditor, not a clerk. When a man sells his grief to us, what do we hold afterward?"

"The grief."

"When a country sells its memory—" the Master spread his paper hands, "—what do we hold?"

The cold that went through Corvo then had nothing to do with his wet coat.

"The whole of it," he said slowly. "Everything. Every song, every name, every grave. All of it, in one collection."

"The master-copy," the Master said. "Yes. The empire believed it was buying a deletion. What it was actually funding was a consolidation. Every memory we stripped from Ilvaren's people—every one we bought from the desperate, confiscated from the stubborn, pried out of dying mouths—was brought here. Catalogued. Bound. And locked below this floor, in the Vault of First Entries, in a single continuous binding twenty thousand pages thick." He smiled, and it was the worst thing Corvo had ever seen him do, because it was gentle. "Ilvaren exists, Corvo. It exists in a box in my basement, complete, entire, and appraised. It has existed there for twenty years, appreciating."

"And the empire—"

"Has finally completed its payment schedule. Their agents arrive within the month. They will take delivery of the master-copy whole, and with it they will hold the only complete proof that Ilvaren was ever anything at all—and the means to make certain it stays nothing. A country cannot be resurrected from a memory its owners control. They will burn it, of course, eventually. But first they will read it. Empires always read what they intend to kill. It's how they learn what else to fear."

Corvo sat very still. Outside the small windowless study, he could hear the snow against the roof, patient, accumulating.

"They'll pay," he said. "For the master-copy. How much?"

"More than the Erasure earned. That is the elegance of it. The empire pays twice for the same crime—once to commit it, once to bury the evidence. We are not brokers, Corvo. We never were. We are the only bank in the world whose deposits are souls, and we have held this one through a twenty-year term, and the interest—" he touched the closed audit on his desk, lightly, almost fondly, "—the interest is magnificent."

* * *

Afterward Corvo walked the long corridor on the second floor, the one with no windows, because his legs wanted motion and his mind wanted darkness and the corridor supplied both. Lamps burned at intervals, low and steady. On either side the ledgers stood in their thousands, spines outward, and he found himself reading them as he passed—not the titles, which were lot numbers, but the wear patterns. Which volumes had been pulled down often. Which had gathered dust. A ledger that was consulted was a wound being reopened; a ledger left alone was a wound cauterized. He had known that for thirty years. Tonight it felt less like knowledge and more like an indictment.

He stopped at a shelf he knew too well. Lot 4471. The spine was thin. Childhoods ran short—they were cheap, that was the horror of it, a boy's first ten years cost less than a merchant's single season of courage, because children had not yet compounded their lives with interest. He had carried this volume to the binding room himself. He remembered the weight of it. Less than a loaf of bread.

His father sold me, Mara had told him on the coast road, three days ago, in the dark, with the sea making its indifferent noise below. Not the guild. Him. He signed the transfer with his own hand, and then he took the money and bought a house in a city that doesn't remember why it's rich.

Corvo had stood there holding his lantern and had said the correct thing: *I'm sorry.* And Mara had laughed—not bitterly, which would have been easier—and said, *You sound like an audit finding. 'Irregularity noted.'*

Now, standing in the corridor of everything the guild had ever taken, Corvo understood what the laugh had cost, and what it had been for. Mara had forgiven him in advance, the way you forgive a man who is about to discover the floor of his house is a lid.

Because that was the arithmetic, wasn't it. That was the sum Corvo had been carrying since the courtyard, turning it over and over like the clipped coin. The guild had fed him, trained him, trusted him, made him what he was. Thirty years of belonging. Against that: a thin spine on a shelf, and a country in a box, and an empire coming to buy the burial of a murder it had already committed, and the Master's gentle smile as he priced it all.

Loyalty was a ledger too. Corvo had spent his life believing the guild's books balanced. Now he ran the column and found the entries did not agree, and the discrepancy was not in the numbers. It was in what the numbers were *of*.

He thought of Mara at nine years old, before any of it—no. He couldn't think of that. There was no record of that. That was rather the point.

He thought instead of the night Mara had taught him to walk unnoticed, both of them fifteen, drunk on stolen wine, laughing in the guild's rafters while old Aldery tried to catch them and failed because they had already learned the lesson better than their teacher. *The trick isn't hiding,* Mara had said, solemn with wine. *It's giving them nothing to see. You can't arrest a shadow, Corvo. You can't even dislike it.*

And Corvo had said, *One day they'll promote you for that.*

And Mara had said, *One day I'll disappear so completely even I won't find me.*

He had meant it as a joke. Corvo had laughed. Neither of them had known it was a prophecy, or a price, or a plan.

The lamps hissed. Snow ticked against a roof that had no windows to catch it. Somewhere below Corvo's feet, behind three locks and a door that answered to one pair of paper hands, twenty thousand pages of a country waited in the dark for an empire's caravan.

He put his palm flat against the shelf, against the thin spine of Lot 4471, and stood there a long time, doing the only arithmetic that had ever mattered and discovering, at last, which column he belonged in.

Then he went down to the stables, saddled a fresh horse, and rode north into the snow—toward the high passes, toward the shrine where a trail ended, toward whatever door his friend had walked through—because there was one more audit to finish, and Corvo intended, this once, to file the findings himself.
