## 1. The Valley That Magic Forgot

The dead of Emberfall are terrible gossips, and I am the only person in the valley who has to listen.

I want to be clear about that from the start, because people—living people—always assume it must be a gift. A comfort. *Oh, you hear your loved ones still? How precious.* And yes, sometimes it is. But mostly it's old Marta Corren, dead eleven years now, complaining through the bone of her own grave-token that her son planted the wrong flowers. Again. Every morning. The same three words, worn smooth as river stones: *Not the blue ones.*

Marta, I said this morning, setting my chisel against a fresh disc of riverbone. Your son planted the blue ones because your daughter-in-law likes them. Your son married the daughter-in-law. This is how marriage works. I have explained this to you four hundred times.

*Not the blue ones,* said Marta Corren, with the serenity of a woman who will never have to be right about anything ever again.

That is the shape of my days. I am Gideon Brightwater, bone-carver of Emberfall, and I carve grave-tokens—small discs of bone and horn and river-stone, worked with a name and a sigil and whatever small emblem the family asks for. A lark for the singer. A sheaf of barley for the farmer. A door, slightly ajar, for old Tam Weller, who swore to his last breath that death was just a room he hadn't entered yet and wanted to be remembered as an optimist.

And when I carve, I hear them. Not the whole soul, mind you. Not a conversation. Just—echoes. The loudest regrets, pressed into the token like a thumbprint in wax. The dead leave their small unfinished business in the bone, and I am the valley's listening post, its complaint department, its priest of the almost-forgotten.

It is not magic. I want to be very clear about that too, because in Emberfall, magic is the one word we do not say, the way other towns don't say *plague* or *tax collector*. What I have is a knack. A craft. The wards that ring our valley keep out the other thing—the court, the cold, the hungry dark—and whatever leaks through the wards into a dead woman's grave-token is just the residue of a life, the way a kettle holds the shape of the water after it's poured out.

That is what I tell myself, anyway. I have had seven years of practice telling myself things.

* * *

The morning I am going to tell you about began, as all my mornings begin, with Marta Corren and a cup of barley tea gone cold because I forgot about it mid-listen. My workshop sits at the edge of the churchyard, which is convenient in the way that living above a bakery is convenient—everything I need, right outside, in quantities that would ruin me if I indulged.

Through the window I could see the terraced fields going gold up the valley walls, the orchards heavy with early apples, the smoke rising straight and lazy from forty chimneys. Emberfall in autumn looks like something painted by a man who has never known grief. The valley is a cup, and the mountains are its rim, and we sit inside it like tea left to steep, warm and protected and mostly unaware of what's holding the cup.

The wards, the elders call the mountain-rim, when they call it anything at all. Which is rarely. In Emberfall we do not discuss the wards the way we do not discuss the shape of our own skulls. They are simply the condition of being ourselves. No one remembers when they were raised. No one remembers who raised them. There is a founding story, of course—the Seven Founders, the Long Night, the bargain—but the founding story is told to children at hearth-side in the same tone as tales of talking foxes, and by the time we're old enough to ask what the bargain actually *was*, we've learned better than to ask. Questions about the wards are like questions about your parents' marriage. You sense that the answers exist, and you sense, with equal clarity, that you do not want them.

So we don't ask. We carve our tokens, plant our barley, and let the mountains do their silent work.

I had three commissions that morning. A token for the Hollis baby, who lived four days and whose parents had asked for something with a wren on it—*a wren, if you can manage it, Master Brightwater, something small and brave*—and I had to sit with that request for a long moment before I could pick up my knife.

You should understand: wrens are not uncommon requests. It's a common bird. People like it because it's small and loud and refuses to be impressed by larger birds. I have carved perhaps thirty wrens in my career.

I carve them anyway. I always carve them anyway. My sister has been dead seven years, and I have carved every wren anyone has ever asked for, and I have never once refused, because refusing would mean explaining, and explaining would mean saying her name in a voice that didn't hold, and I am a professional.

The baby Hollis's token went well. Riverbone takes a fine edge, and the wren came out with her tail cocked at exactly the correct insolent angle. And because the bone was fresh and the grief on it was raw, I heard something—not words, babies that young don't have regrets to press into the bone, only warmth, only the ghost-echo of being held. It's the rarest thing I hear, and the hardest. Regret I can carve around. Warmth just sits in my chest all day like a swallowed coal.

The second commission was Tam Weller's widow, come to have the door on his token re-cut. *Slightly ajar,* she said, *not wide open, Gideon, he'd haunt me proper if I let them think he'd gone through it willingly.* Tam himself, from the token in my hand, offered no comment, which from Tam was a kind of applause.

And the third commission—

The third was the one that unmade my ordinary morning, though I didn't know it yet. A slip of paper delivered by the miller's boy: *Old Nan Sorrel has gone. Family wants her token by the dark of the moon. She left instructions.*

Nan Sorrel had left instructions the way a general leaves battle plans. Three pages. She had specified the bone—elk, from the trophy on her own wall, she'd shot it herself in '61 and trusted no other elk since. She had specified the sigil. She had specified the border-work. And at the bottom of the third page, in handwriting that had gone shaky but determined, she had written: *And tell Gideon I know what he hears, and it's all right, and he should stop apologizing to Marta Corren, the blue flowers were the right choice.*

I sat down.

Now, here is the thing about my knack: nobody in Emberfall knows about it. Not officially. My mother knows, because mothers know. The old priest knew, before he died, because he'd caught me arguing with a grave-token when I was nine and had the good sense—rare in priests—to say nothing. But the valley at large? No. To the valley, I am a decent carver with a melancholy disposition, which they attribute to my sister's death and my father's before that, and they are not wrong, they're just not *right*, either.

And Nan Sorrel, dead three days in the ground, had just told me, from beyond the veil of everything we pretend, that she knew.

*It's all right,* she'd written. *He should stop apologizing to Marta Corren.*

I looked out the window toward the churchyard, where Nan's fresh mound of earth was still bare of grass. "You couldn't have told me this while you were alive?" I asked the mound. "One conversation? You lived eighty-one years. You had *time*."

The mound said nothing, because mounds say nothing; it was the token that would speak, once I carved it, and Nan Sorrel's last regret was going to be, I could already tell, the fact that she hadn't said it sooner.

* * *

I carved Nan's token that afternoon, and she was exactly as I expected: unrepentant, specific, and fond. *Should have told the boy years ago,* she murmured through the elk bone as my knife worked. *Should have told him the wards have a sound, if you stand close enough. Should have—* and then, softer, the regret under all the regrets, the one that made my knife go still: *Should have told Wren's brother that Wren's name isn't in the ground.*

I set the knife down very carefully, the way you do when the alternative is throwing it.

Because that is the other thing you should know about me. Seven years, and I have heard every dead soul in Emberfall who ever left an echo in a token—hundreds of them, thousands, the whole murmuring congregation of the valley's departed. I heard my father, dead nineteen years, still fretting about a fence he never mended. I heard the priest, dead four, still composing sermons. I heard a girl who drowned before I was born, still practicing her wedding dance in the bone of her token, over and over, all her unfinished joy pressed into elk horn.

I have heard everyone.

I have never heard Wren.

Not once. Not a syllable. Not a sigh. When my sister died—fell from the high pasture path in a wet October, they said, quick and clean, they said—I carved her token myself, from her own cradle-bone, the way our family has always done, and I sat with that bone for three days and three nights, knife in hand, listening so hard my ears rang.

Nothing. Silence. The only silence in a valley where I never get silence.

The elders said it was because she died whole. No regrets, they said. A soul at peace leaves no echo. And I was twenty-two and grieving and I *wanted* to believe it, so I believed it, the way you believe the kind thing because the alternative is a shape you can't sleep beside.

But I have carved tokens for the peacefully dead since. Old men who died smiling in their beds, surrounded by grandchildren. And even they leave *something*. A warmth. A hum. The residue of a life, the way a kettle holds the shape of the water.

Wren's token holds nothing. It is the only piece of bone in my workshop that is just—bone.

Nan Sorrel's voice, thin as smoke through the elk horn: *Wren's name isn't in the ground.*

"Nan," I said aloud, to my empty workshop, to the token in my hands, "what does that mean?"

But Nan had said all she was going to say. The dead are like that. They spend their one coin and go quiet, and you're left standing in the shop with your heart going like a hammer and your tea cold again, and outside, the valley going gold and peaceful and utterly, perfectly silent about itself.

* * *

I did what I always do when the valley cracks a little and shows me the seam. I went home to my mother's house for supper.

Mother lives in the old Brightwater house below the terraces, and she keeps it the way she has kept it for seven years: meticulously, warmly, and with one chair at the table that no one sits in. Wren's chair. It's oak, older than either of us, and it sits at the table's end with its back to the window, and every evening Mother sets it with a plate and a cup as though the meal were for three. She has done this every single evening for seven years. I have never once said a word about it. This is the treaty we live under, my mother and I: she sets the chair, I don't comment, and neither of us says the true thing, which is that we are both still setting a place for a girl who is never coming down the stairs.

"Supper," Mother said, when I came in. "Wash your hands. You've bone dust in your hair."

"I always have bone dust in my hair."

"Yes, and I always say so. It's how we love each other."

She was in good spirits, my mother. She'd been preserving pears, and the kitchen smelled of sugar and clove, and she talked while she ladled—about the Hollis family, about the apple crop, about the miller's boy growing so fast he'd need a new coat by midwinter. Ordinary talk. Valley talk. The talk of people who live inside a cup and trust the cup.

I almost didn't ask. I want you to know that. I carried Nan Sorrel's sentence home in my chest like a swallowed hook, and I sat through the pears and the apple talk and the coat talk, and I *almost* let the evening close over it, the way evenings do, the way seven years of evenings have done.

But I have spent my whole life listening to the dead say the thing they didn't say while they could. I could not, in good conscience, become one more person in this valley leaving the true thing unsaid.

"Mother," I said. "Nan Sorrel's token spoke today."

Mother's ladle paused, mid-pour, over Wren's cup. Just for a breath. Then it finished pouring, steady as ever.

"Did it," she said. "Lovely old woman. What did she have to say for herself?"

"That Wren's name isn't in the ground."

The kitchen was very quiet. Out the window, the valley was turning blue with dusk, the first stars pricking out above the mountain rim—sharp and bright and close, the way they always are in Emberfall, as if the wards that kept everything else out kept the stars near.

Mother set down the ladle. She did not look at me. She looked at Wren's chair, at the plate, at the cup she had just filled, and her face did the thing that faces do when a door opens inside them that they've kept painted shut for years.

"Gideon," she said, and her voice was calm, which is how I knew it was bad, because my mother is calm the way deep water is calm. "Eat your supper. And tomorrow, when a stranger comes to the door asking questions—" she finally looked at me, and her eyes were the exact grey of Wren's, and they were frightened, "—you let *me* answer."

"Mother, what stranger? What questions?"

She picked up her knife and fork. She began to eat, with perfect composure, the meal she had set for three.

"The debt of the valley," she said, to her plate, to the chair, to the seven years. "Someone's come to call it due. Eat, love. We've one night left of not knowing."

Outside, above the mountain rim, the stars were going out—one by one, like candles after a wake.

And the dead of Emberfall, for the first time in my life, fell silent mid-sentence.
