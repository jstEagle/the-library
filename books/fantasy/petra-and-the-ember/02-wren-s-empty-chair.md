## 2. Wren's Empty Chair

The dead of Emberfall are terrible gossips.

I mean that with affection. Mostly. Old Marta Tull, seven years gone, spent her entire afterlife apologizing to a fence post she'd once backed a cart into. The miller's father, Harl, narrated his own funeral from somewhere above his own coffin, offering unsolicited critiques of the eulogy—"he pronounced my name wrong, boy, *HARL*, like a man clearing his throat"—for three days straight until his widow buried a second stone over him out of spite. They murmur through my workshop the way weather comes through a badly fitted window. Small regrets. Unpaid debts of the heart. A hundred little *I should have said*s drifting through the shavings like dust.

I carve their grave-tokens, and I listen, and mostly it is fine. It has been fine for years. That's what I'd have told you, if you'd asked me on the morning of the seventh anniversary of my sister's death. It was fine. Everything was fine. Emberfall was a valley that magic forgot and I was a man who had made his peace with being invisible, and I had a commission—a token for the Fenner child, drowned last spring—and honey still in the pot, and no reason at all to expect that this particular day would be any different from the six that came before it.

The dead, of course, knew better. Dead things usually do. But they were keeping it to themselves, which should have been my first warning. The dead are never discreet when there's gossip to be had.

* * *

I woke before dawn, as I always do on this day, though I'd have paid good coin to sleep through it. There is no honest way to describe the anniversary of your sister's death except as a day you survive. You get through it the way you get through a fever: one hour at a time, drinking water, keeping the windows shut.

Wren died seven years ago in the month of Falling Leaf, on a morning so ordinary I can barely forgive it. She went up the shepherd's path to check the boundary stones—ward-keeper's work, though she wasn't a ward-keeper, just helping old Sef for an afternoon because he'd wrenched his back—and she didn't come down. They found her at the third stone, lying neatly on her side, hands folded, no wound, no mark, her face wearing an expression I have spent seven years trying not to think about. Not fear. Not peace either. Something between. The expression of someone interrupted mid-sentence by something enormous.

Here is the thing about my gift—the thing I don't say aloud in Emberfall, though I suspect half the valley knows anyway: I hear the dead. Every soul that dies within the ward-line leaves a residue, an echo, a voice caught in bone and stone and the memory of wood. I can't raise them, can't question them properly. They just talk. Around me, through the materials I carve. And Wren—

Wren left nothing.

No echo. No murmur. Seven years of listening to every departed soul in the valley grumble about fence posts and mispronounced names, and my own sister's voice is the one I have never found. The dead go quiet when I ask about her, which is its own kind of answer, though I've never decided what question it answers. Grief does strange arithmetic. I stopped trying to solve it around year four.

So: dawn. I lit the lamp over my bench, took up the Fenner token—riverstone, because the child had loved the river—and began to carve.

"You want the reeds," said the voice in the riverstone. The Fenner child's echo was faint, new, still more water than words. "She liked the reeds. Put the reeds."

"I'll put the reeds," I told it.

"And a fish. A fat one. She always said she'd catch a fat one someday."

"She did catch one," I said gently. "Summer before last. Mother Fenner talks about it."

A pause in the stone, the way a pause happens in a room when someone is deciding whether to cry. Then, small: "Oh. Good."

I carved the reeds. I carved the fish, fat and improbable, leaping. This is the whole of my trade and most of my life: giving the dead the small mercies they missed, one knife-stroke at a time. It isn't heroism. It's joinery. You fit grief to stone until it holds.

By mid-morning the workshop was warm, the shavings were piling sweet-smelling at my feet, and the dead were murmuring their usual inventory of regrets. I remember thinking—not thinking, really, more the way your bones think—that today of all days, the quiet would have been welcome. I remember noticing that the voices seemed thinner than usual. Farther off. Like guests at a gathering who keep checking the door.

I remember thinking: *they know something.*

Then I remembered it was the anniversary, and that my bones thought dramatic things every year on this date, and I got back to the fish.

* * *

Mother set three places at table.

We had agreed—years ago, in the fierce unspoken treaty of the grieving—that we would not do this. No place settings for the absent. No empty chairs dressed up like shrines. Mother had sworn it herself, hand flat on the table the way she swore oaths, which in our house was binding as law. *One plate each,* she'd said. *Your sister eats with us in other ways.*

And every year, on this one day, she broke the treaty.

I came in at noon with bread and the last of the autumn apples and stopped in the doorway like a man walking into glass. Three plates. Three cups. And at the head-adjacent seat—the seat that had been Wren's since we were children, because she'd claimed it at age six on the grounds that it had the best view of the door and she liked to see who was coming—her chair, drawn out from the table. Waiting. Not pushed in neat the way an unused chair sits, but angled, casual, the way a chair looks when someone has just gotten up to fetch something and will be back any moment.

"Mother."

"It's airing," she said, without turning from the stove.

"Chairs don't need airing."

"This one does." Her back was straight. Her hands were very busy with a pot that did not need her. "Sit down, Gideon. Dinner won't serve itself."

"It's noon."

"Then lunch won't serve itself." She turned then, and gave me the look—the one she'd worn for seven years, the one that dared me to make this harder than it already was. My mother is not a large woman but she has the particular gravity of people who have survived something enormous and refuse to apologize for the wreckage. "One day a year. Let me have the one day."

I sat. I ate. Across from me, Wren's chair held nothing but lamplight, and I did the thing I do every year: I looked at it exactly once, properly, and then arranged my face into something survivable and talked about the Fenner token and the price of honey and whether the roof would take another winter.

Mother answered in monosyllables and kept glancing at the window.

That was the second warning, and this time I noticed it. She kept looking at the door too—but not toward Wren's chair-view of it, fondly, the way she usually did. Anxiously. Like a woman waiting for someone she dreaded.

Like a woman expecting company.

* * *

The stranger came at dusk.

I was banking the forge when I heard the footsteps on the lane—wrong footsteps, if you understand me. Everyone in Emberfall has a walk I know; I've carved half their grave-tokens and listened to the other half complain about their knees. These steps were measured, deliberate, and entirely unfamiliar, and they stopped at my gate.

The dead, I realized, had gone quiet again. All of them. Not thin, not far-off—gone. The workshop behind me felt suddenly like a held breath.

He stood at the gate as if gates were a formality he tolerated. Tall, gray-cloaked, hooded against the evening, and beneath the hood a face that was pleasant the way a well-made lock is pleasant: smooth, precise, with nothing behind it you were meant to see. He looked at my sign—GIDEON BRIGHTWATER, BONE & STONE—and smiled slightly, as if the sign had said something amusing in a language I didn't speak.

"Carver," he said. Not a greeting. An identification.

"That's me." I wiped my hands on my apron, which was a habit, not a need; my hands were already clean. "If you're wanting a token, I'm afraid commissions run two weeks out."

"I'm not wanting a token." His voice was soft and unhurried, a voice used to being listened to. "I'm wanting information. About the debt of the valley."

The words went through me strangely—not like cold, like *recognition*. Somewhere in the marrow-deep part of me that hears the dead, something leaned forward.

"I don't know what you mean," I said.

"No," he agreed pleasantly. "But you know that you know." He rested one gloved hand on my gatepost, and the post—the old ash post my father planted, the post whose grain I know better than my own palm—went silent under his touch. Not quiet. Silent. As if the wood had been emptied. "Seven years ago, a bargain was struck at the third boundary stone. A protection was purchased. Protections, carver, are never free. Someone pays. I am here to ask who, and to ask what is owed, and to ask"—and here the pleasantness thinned, just slightly, the way ice thins over dark water—"whether anyone in this valley intends to honor it."

My heart was doing something undignified in my chest. And here is the moment I will be turning over for the rest of my life, however long the Undercourt or God or the mountain permits it to be:

I lied.

"I think you've taken a wrong turn, friend," I said, easy as breathing, easy as anything, my voice level and my face doing whatever faces do when they lie well. "Emberfall's got no debts but honest ones. Owe the baker, owe the cooper, owe your neighbor a fence mended. Nothing owed to strangers at gates. Good evening to you."

Silence. The hood tilted. Behind those smooth features, something considered me—weighed me, the way I weigh stone—and found me, apparently, adequate.

"So the valley keeps its secrets even from itself," he said softly. "How very like them. How very *useful*." He released the gatepost. The ash wood stayed silent. "Thank you for your honesty, carver."

It was such an obscene word, coming from him, about that, that I nearly laughed—which would have been worse, so instead I nodded like a man with nothing in him but tiredness, and watched him turn, and watched the gray cloak move down my lane toward the village, unhurried as dusk itself.

I stood at my gate a long time. The dead did not come back. The valley around me felt scoured, hollowed, like a bell with the clapper stolen. And my hands, which had lied smoothly and easily for the first time in twenty years, were shaking so hard I had to grip the gatepost to stand.

*I think you've taken a wrong turn, friend.* Where had it come from, that lie? Not from me—I don't lie, haven't lied since I was a boy, couldn't afford to with a hundred graves' worth of witnesses murmuring at my shoulder. And yet it had come out whole, polished, ready. Almost as if—

Almost as if something in me had been *waiting* to say it. Had been rehearsed. Had known, the way the dead had known all day, the way my mother's eyes kept going to the door.

*Someone's come to call it due.*

I hadn't heard those words yet. Wouldn't hear them for another hour yet, over a table set for three. But some part of me—the part that lives in the bone, the part that listens—had already understood what the stranger was, and what he wanted, and had done the only thing it knew how to do.

It had protected the valley the way everything in this valley had been protected for seven generations.

With a lie, and a price, and a soul.

* * *

I walked home the long way, past the boundary road, and stood a while at the rise where you can see the third ward-stone—a gray tooth in the dark hillside, older than the village, older than the name Brightwater. Wren died beside it. I have never once, in seven years, been able to walk past without stopping, and tonight I stood there in the blue dark and put my hand on the cold grass and said, out loud, to nobody, to the one silence that had ever mattered:

"Wren. If you're anywhere. If there's any part of you anywhere—"

Nothing. Of course nothing. There was always nothing.

But as I turned to go, I caught it—one thread of sound at the very edge of hearing, so faint it might have been wind in the stones, might have been blood in my own ears. A voice. Hers. Just a fragment, torn at both ends, the middle of a sentence whose beginning and end I would never have:

*"—didn't tell them I'd say yes—"*

Then gone.

I stood in the dark with my heart hammering and told myself it was wind. I told myself I was a grieving man on a hard night hearing what grief wants him to hear. I was very thorough about it. I almost believed it.

Then I looked up, out of habit, to find the Shepherd's Star—the first star over the mountain rim, the one Wren and I used to race home by, the one constant of every evening of my life—

—and it flickered.

Not twinkled. Flickered. The way a candle gutters when someone, somewhere in the house, has opened a door.

Down in the valley, my mother's kitchen window glowed yellow, and three places waited at the table, and somewhere between my gate and her door a stranger's words were walking ahead of me like a lantern: *someone pays. I am here to ask who.*

The star guttered again.

I started running.
