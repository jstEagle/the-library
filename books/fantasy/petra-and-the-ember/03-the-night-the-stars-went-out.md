## 3. The Night the Stars Went Out

The dead talk in their sleep.

That's not a metaphor, though I've used it as one at funerals when the family wanted something poetic and I was too tired to invent anything better. I mean it plainly: the voices I hear have a rhythm to them, an ebb and flow, like breathing. During the day they murmur—regrets mostly, small ones, the size of coins. *I never fixed the fence. I should have told her about the money. I meant to learn the fiddle.* At night they quiet down, the way living people do, and what's left is a kind of hum, like a valley full of bees thinking gentle thoughts.

I had lived forty-one years inside that hum. I want that understood before anyone judges what I did next, because there is no version of this story where I come out looking entirely sensible.

That night I was working late on a grave-token for old Marta Hedley, who had died the week before at eighty-three with the satisfied expression of a woman who had outlived everyone she'd ever disliked. Her voice was the loudest in my workshop, which was unusual—most of the newly dead are confused for a while, all echoes and panic—but Marta had always been direct in life and saw no reason to change.

"The blue bowl," she kept saying. "Tell Tamsin the blue bowl was hers. Not the green. The green goes to nobody, the green was ugly, I only kept it because Aldous's mother gave it to us and I didn't want to hurt her feelings, and she's been dead thirty years so it can go in the fire."

"You could have told her," I said, sanding the rim of the token. Bone takes a fine edge if you're patient. "You had thirty years."

"And ruin the one interesting thing about my death? Boy, I waited my whole life to be mysterious."

I liked Marta. I'd liked most of them, over the years, which is either a testament to Emberfall or an indictment of it—I've never decided. The dead of our valley are a mild lot. They grumble about weather and inheritance and the price of wool, and once in a great while one of them will go quiet in a way that makes me put down my knife and sit very still until the feeling passes. But mostly they are company. Forty-one years of company, since the day I was born hearing them, or close enough—the earliest memory I have is of my mother rocking me and a dead shepherd two fields over complaining about his boots, and me unable to tell which voice was which until I learned their textures. The living sound like water. The dead sound like wind through a keyhole.

Wren had sounded like neither. Wren, when she was alive, sounded like a door opening.

I set down my knife. It was past midnight, the candle guttering, and outside the autumn air had gone strange—not cold exactly, but *thin*, the way air feels before a storm rearranges it. The hum of the dead was quieter than usual. I noticed it the way you notice a clock stopping: not the silence itself but the shape it left behind.

"Marta," I said. "Do you feel that?"

"Feel what? I'm dead, boy, I don't feel things anymore. That was rather the point of the whole arrangement." A pause. Then, softer, in the tone the dead use when they remember they still have edges: "Although. There's something pulling. Like a hook behind my ribs. Like someone reeling in a line."

I stood up. My hands were steady, which surprised me; I'd expected them to shake. Outside, the night was absolutely black—no moon that I remembered rising, no lantern-glow from the far farms—and I thought at first that cloud had rolled in off the mountains. Then I stepped into the yard and looked up.

The stars were going out.

Not fading. Not being covered. Going out—one, then three, then a whole constellation at once, like candles pinched by a hand moving faster than any hand I could imagine. And underneath it, faint but unmistakable, coming from every direction at once, from the border stones miles away in every direction, came a sound I will hear for the rest of my life however long or short that turns out to be:

Harp strings snapping. Hundreds of them. Thousands. A music breaking everywhere at once.

The wards. Every ward on every border of Emberfall, singing themselves silent.

I did what any sensible man does when the sky starts dying. I went back inside and shut the door.

* * *

Let me be honest about those first minutes, because honesty is the whole point of what comes later, and I might as well start practicing now.

My first feeling was not terror. My first feeling was relief—a hot, shameful wash of it, like brandy on an empty stomach. Because the wards had been humming too. All my life, underneath the voices of the dead, there had been the wards' low note, the sound of the valley's protection, and I had never known how much it grated on me until it stopped. For perhaps ten heartbeats I stood in my workshop in the dark and felt *lighter*. Unwatched. Unguarded. Free in a way I hadn't the vocabulary for.

Then the dead began to scream.

Not all of them. Not at first. It started at the edges of my hearing—the far voices, the ones from the farms near the ward-stones—and it moved inward like frost across a window. Old Halric the miller, dead sixty years, whose entire afterlife had consisted of complaining about his daughter-in-law's bread, suddenly made a sound I cannot describe except that it was the sound of a man being *unmade*, of a name being erased from a ledger while the owner watches. Then another voice. Then five. Then dozens.

And the rest went silent. Mid-sentence. Mid-*word*.

Marta got as far as "The blue bowl was—" and stopped. Not faded. Stopped, the way a sentence stops when the mouth speaking it is closed by force. In forty-one years the dead had never once stopped talking. They murmured through storms and feasts and funerals, through my sister's funeral most of all, a hundred patient ghosts narrating my grief back at me because they had nothing else to do and neither did I. Silence was the one thing they had never given me. Silence was the thing I had begged the dark for at fourteen, at twenty, at thirty, face pressed into a pillow, *please, just one hour, just one minute of quiet.*

I got my wish at half past midnight on the third of Harvestmonth, and I would give it back piece by piece if the world would let me.

Because here is what I understood, standing in the dark with my ears ringing with nothing: the voices weren't a curse that had finally lifted. They were a *connection*, and the connection ran through the wards, and the wards had just been cut. Whatever had held the dead of Emberfall in its gentle orbit—whatever let them linger instead of passing on, whatever kept them mild and near and mine—was gone. And the ones who screamed as it went were the lucky ones. They at least got to make a sound.

The others simply weren't there anymore.

I lit every lamp in the house. It was stupid and I knew it was stupid and I did it anyway, because the stars were gone and the lamps were something I could control. Then I did something less stupid: I took down the bone whistle Wren had carved me when we were children—it was a bad whistle, it had never made a proper note in its life, she'd carved it from a shinbone before she knew the first thing about hollowing—and I held it, and I listened.

Nothing. Wind. My own heart, going like a drum in a parade.

Seven years I had searched the hum for her. Seven years of listening under every voice, behind every whisper, hoping against all sense that somewhere in that vast gray chorus was one voice shaped like a door opening. She had died at nineteen, drowned in the millrace, and I had never heard her afterward—not once, not a fragment, and I had told myself a hundred comforting lies about why: that she'd passed on cleanly, that she'd gone wherever the good ones go, that my gift simply didn't reach that far. Standing there in the lamplight with the wards broken and the sky blind, a colder lie offered itself: that she had never been in the chorus at all. That whatever the wards had done with the dead of Emberfall, they had done something else with Wren.

I didn't know yet. Gods forgive me, I didn't know yet what the valley had bought its safety with. That knowledge was still a day away, wearing a stranger's boots and bleeding through her sleeve. That night all I knew was that the sky had gone out, the dead had been cut off mid-word, and my sister's absence had just acquired a new and terrible texture.

I saddled the mule. Of course I saddled the mule. My mother would say I inherited the impulse from her, the need to *go toward* the bad thing, and she would say it in the particular voice she used when she was really talking about herself.

* * *

Mother was already in the yard when I led the mule around the house, standing in her shawl with her arms crossed, looking up at the starless sky the way other people look at a debt collector's letter.

"You felt it too," I said.

"I felt it seven years ago," she said. "Tonight I just recognized it."

That should have told me everything. It nearly did. But the sky chose that moment to finish its work—the last few stars, the stubborn ones near the horizon, went out all together, and the darkness that followed wasn't ordinary darkness. It was *thick*. It had weight. It lay on the valley like a lid on a pot, and somewhere beneath us, beneath the mountain, beneath even the deep roads the old stories whispered about, I felt—or imagined I felt, I will never be sure—a vast attention turn toward us. Toward Emberfall. Patient. Hungry in the way of things that have waited a long time for an appointment.

"Gideon." Mother's hand found my arm in the dark. Her grip was stronger than a woman of sixty has any right to. "Listen to me. Whatever comes to our gate in the next days—whatever asks you questions, whatever shows you papers, whatever *weeps*—you remember that this valley survived because your father and I kept one secret, and keeping it broke us both, and I would break again tomorrow. Do you understand me?"

"No," I said. "I understand that you're telling me to lie."

"I'm telling you to protect what's left of this family."

"Wren is what's left of this family," I said, "and she's dead, and you won't even say her name on the anniversary, and now the sky is out and you're talking about secrets like they're heirlooms." I heard my own voice climb and couldn't stop it. "What did the valley buy, Mother? What did we pay?"

For a moment she said nothing, and in that silence—for the first time in my life, true silence, no dead murmuring under her words, no wards humming under hers—I heard something I had never heard before: my mother breathing. Just breathing. Just a frightened old woman in a shawl, alone inside her own skull same as me.

"Go to bed, Gideon," she said.

I didn't. I sat up with the lamp and the bad whistle and Marta's unfinished token, and I watched the window, and I waited for the dead to come back the way you wait for a fever to break. They didn't. Hour after hour, nothing. The valley lay under its lid of dark, and the only sounds were living sounds—wind, a dog, my own breath—and I discovered that terror dressed itself remarkably well as relief. Anyone seeing me would have thought I was calm. A man sitting quietly by a lamp. Inside, I was screaming along with the voices that had gotten out in time, and grieving the ones that hadn't, and hating myself for ever having wished them quiet, and missing Wren with a freshness that seven years should have worn smooth but somehow hadn't.

Near dawn, I heard hoofbeats on the north road.

I want to say I was brave about it. What I actually did was blow out the lamp and stand behind the door with a carving knife, which is the posture of a man who has decided to die defending a workshop full of grave-tokens, and I held that heroic position while the hoofbeats slowed, and stopped, and were replaced by the sound of footsteps—uneven, dragging, the steps of someone running on a ruined leg—and then a weight falling against my gate with a thud and a small, bitten-off cry.

Through the gap in the boards I could see a shape crumpled against the ward-stone that stood at the edge of my property, one of the old boundary markers, and here is the detail I will carry to my grave: the stone was *cracked*. Split clean down the middle, the way the harp strings had snapped, the way the stars had gone out. And the woman leaning against it had blood on her sleeve and a satchel clutched to her chest like a child, and she was saying something over and over, barely above a whisper, in a voice cracked raw from miles of road:

"He has to know. He has to know what they sold. He has to know it was *her*."

I opened the gate.

Later—much later, in places I couldn't have imagined that morning—I would understand that this was the moment everything turned, the hinge of the whole story, the second the valley's long lie walked up my path and collapsed against my fence. But standing there in the last dark Emberfall would know for a year, with a knife in my fist and my mother's warning in my ears, all I knew was this: a stranger had said *her*, and the word had landed in my chest like a struck bell, and no power under the mountain or above it was going to stop me from answering.

I opened the gate, and the age of silence ended.
