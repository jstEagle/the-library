## 4. Petra at the Gate

I had been walking for eleven days when I finally understood why ward-keepers are taught never to look back at the wards they've broken.

It's not superstition. It's not some old wives' lesson about tempting fate. It's because if you look back and see what's following you—and something *is* always following you—you'll stop running. And a keeper who stops running dies tired instead of dying fast, which is the only real difference between the two.

So I didn't look back. I kept my eyes on the hills ahead of me, on the grey-green shoulder of the valley called Emberfall rising out of the morning mist like the back of a sleeping animal, and I told myself the ache in my side was just a stitch, and the wetness soaking through my coat was just sweat, and the reason the world kept tilting sideways was just that I'd forgotten how to eat properly on the road.

Lying to yourself is a skill. I'd had a great deal of practice.

The valley of Emberfall does not appear on any map I ever studied in the ward-house at Vael Corren, which should have been my first clue, years ago, that my order was lying to me about something. Whole valleys don't just fall off maps. Maps are drawn by people who want other people to find things—or, more rarely, by people who very much want them not to. Emberfall was the second kind of place. A valley warded so thoroughly that magic itself slid off it like rain off oiled leather. A hole in the world's ledger.

And I had come to kick a hole in the hole.

By midday I could see the border stones—tall grey monoliths sunk into the earth at intervals along the valley's rim, each one carved with the old signs of keeping, worn nearly smooth by centuries of weather and deliberate neglect. From a distance they looked like nothing. Teeth in an old jaw. But I could feel them from half a mile out, the way you feel a fire in a closed room before you open the door: a pressure against the skin, a wrongness in the air, the particular hush of a place where something enormous has been told *no* for a very long time.

I had spent six years learning to build that hush. I had spent one terrible night learning what it cost.

My legs gave out about two hundred paces from the nearest stone. Not dramatically—I would like to report that I went down in a heroic sprawl, all flying cloak and tragic poetry—but in the deeply undignified way of a body that has simply finished negotiating. One moment I was walking; the next I was on my knees in the bracken with my palms full of cold dirt, and the stitch in my side announced itself properly at last as what it had been all along: a knife wound, courtesy of a ward-hunter named Serel who had caught up with me on the fourth night and whom I had, through considerable luck and no small amount of dishonesty, left face-down in a river.

He'd be awake by now. He'd be reporting. Which meant I had perhaps a day, maybe less, before more hunters came, and I could not afford to die in the bracken two hundred paces from the only door that mattered.

"Up," I told myself, in the brisk tone Master Ellery used to use on recruits who fainted during their first warding. "You are a keeper of Vael Corren. You do not lie in ditches."

Except I wasn't a keeper anymore. I'd burned that bridge with rather more literal fire than the phrase usually implies. And the ditch was starting to look genuinely inviting.

What got me up was not courage. I want to be honest about that, since honesty is supposed to be the whole point of what I was doing. What got me up was guilt, which is a far better fuel than courage and considerably cheaper. Guilt has a specific weight. It sits behind the sternum like a swallowed stone, and every step you take away from the thing you did makes it heavier, and there is exactly one direction in which it gets lighter.

I walked toward the stones.

* * *

The last hundred paces took me the better part of an hour. I remember it in fragments: the bracken scratching at my hands, the taste of blood in my mouth where I'd bitten my tongue somewhere back along the road, the strange quality of the light under the ward-line—flatter than it should have been, as though the whole valley existed behind a pane of very old glass. Magic couldn't cross into Emberfall. Apparently neither, quite, could afternoon.

When I reached the stone, I put my hand flat against it.

Nothing happened. That was the horror of it, really—the nothing. At Vael Corren, touching a living ward-stone was like putting your hand into a river. You felt the current of it, the deep slow turning of the working, the intention of everyone who had ever fed it. This stone felt like touching a wall in an abandoned house. The signs were carved there, the structure was intact, but whatever had once moved through it had gone somewhere else. Or been taken somewhere else. Or been *spent*.

Wards don't run on nothing. Every apprentice learns this in their first year, usually from a teacher who says it lightly, the way you say anything terrible to someone too young to understand how terrible it is. Wards are a bargain. Something gives so that something else is kept. The question—the question I had asked for six years and been punished for asking—was always: *gives what?*

I knew now. Gods help me, I knew now, and the knowing was the stone behind my sternum, and the stone was the reason I had crossed three hundred miles of hard country with a knife-hole in my side to stand here pressing my palm against a dead thing and whispering to it like a mourner at a grave.

"I'm sorry," I said. To the stone, or the valley, or whoever might be listening. "I'm sorry. I came to fix it."

The stone said nothing, being a stone. But the hush shifted—just slightly, just enough that I noticed—and somewhere down the line, faint as a bell heard through fog, another stone answered. Not power. Not magic. Just... attention. The valley noticing that something had knocked.

Then I heard footsteps, and made the tactical error of trying to stand at attention, and the world tilted sideways, and the bracken came up to meet me after all.

* * *

I woke to a fire, a blanket that smelled of woodsmoke and tallow, and a man's voice saying, with the weary patience of someone talking to a stubborn animal, "No, you don't. Lie still or you'll open it again."

I opened my eyes anyway, because I have never in my life done what I'm told, and found myself looking up at a broad-shouldered man kneeling beside me with a needle in one hand and a look on his face that suggested he'd stitched worse and enjoyed it less. He was maybe thirty, dark-haired, with the ink-stained fingers of a craftsman and the careful hands of someone accustomed to delicate work. Behind him the fire crackled in a low room with a single window, and outside the window the light was going amber and long. Evening already. I'd lost most of the day.

"You're not a hunter," I said. My voice came out scraped raw.

"No."

"You're not a soldier, either. Your hands are wrong for it." His knuckles were nicked and stained; carver's hands. "Who are you?"

"Gideon Brightwater." He threaded the needle without looking at it, which told me he'd done this often enough to frighten me a little. "Bone-carver. Grave-tokens, mostly. You're on my workbench, incidentally, which I'll thank you not to bleed on further—it's oak, and oak holds a grudge."

"A bone-carver," I repeated. Of all the people in Emberfall who might have found me at the stones, the odds had delivered me a bone-carver, and I felt the laugh climb up my throat before I could stop it—a thin, cracked, half-hysterical sound that I converted, mostly successfully, into a cough. "Of course. Of course you are."

"Yes," he said dryly. "Fate has a sense of humor. Hold still."

The needle went in and I hissed and gripped the edge of the bench, and he worked in silence for a while—competent, unhurried, humming almost under his breath, a habit I recognized and which made my chest hurt, because keepers hummed like that too, when we were listening to a ward. Listening to something underneath the silence.

"You were humming," I said, when I could speak again. "Just now. While you stitched."

"Was I?" He didn't look up. "Habit."

"What were you listening to?"

His hands paused—one heartbeat, no more—and then resumed. "Nothing," he said. "Old habit. Now. You're going to tell me who cut you, and why you walked three hundred miles to bleed on my workbench, or I'm going to finish this stitching and carry you back to the stones and let the valley decide what to do with you. Those are your options. I'm told I'm a reasonable man, but I'm also told I'm a bad liar, so believe both parts."

I looked at him. He looked back, steady and unimpressed, and I thought: *here it is, then. The moment. The whole reason you crawled across a country on your hands and knees, and it comes down to whether you can trust a stranger with needle and thread.*

Here is what nobody tells you about betrayal: it doesn't feel like falling. It feels like arithmetic. You sit in the dark and you add up what you know, and what you owe, and what you can bear, and then you arrive at a number, and the number is a person's name, and you say it out loud, and there is no taking it back.

"The order I deserted," I said, "keeps the wards of the high places. Mountain passes, old roads, borders like yours. We're taught the wards are gifts. Old workings, fed by consent, maintained by devotion. Pretty words." I swallowed. "Three nights ago I read the founding terms of this valley's protection. The actual contract, in the archive under Vael Corren, in a cipher I wasn't cleared for and read anyway, because I ask questions, and asking questions is apparently a firing offense."

Gideon's needle had stopped moving entirely now. He was very still, the way animals go still.

"Wards are a bargain," I said. "Something gives so that something is kept. Do you know what Emberfall gives?"

"No," he said. His voice had changed. Quieter. Careful. "Tell me."

"One soul," I said. "Every generation. Sold to the Undercourt beneath the mountain, to buy the valley's invisibility. The founders signed it four hundred years ago and the order has renewed it ever since, quietly, generation after generation, and the families whose children were taken were told their sons and daughters died of fever." I made myself hold his eyes. I owed him that much, at least—the discomfort of it, the ugliness. "The current payment was collected seven years ago. Her name was Wren."

I will spend the rest of my life remembering what happened to his face in that moment. Nothing dramatic. No cry, no denial. Just a kind of settling, like a house shifting on its foundations after a tremor—everything still standing, everything subtly, permanently wrong.

"That's not funny," he said at last, very evenly.

"No," I agreed. "It isn't."

"People don't sell souls. Souls aren't *currency*. And my sister died of fever. I sat with her. I held her hand. I—" He stopped. His jaw worked. "I would have known."

"Would you?" I asked gently. "Did anyone in this valley ever actually see her buried?"

The silence that followed lasted long enough for the fire to pop twice. When he spoke again, his voice was the flattest I'd ever heard from a living mouth. "Show me."

"It's in my coat. Left pocket. Don't—"

He was already digging it out: a folded sheaf of papers, oilskin-wrapped, travel-worn, and beneath them the thing I had risked everything to carry—the original page itself, cut from the founders' ledger with a hot knife and a colder heart. He unfolded it with hands that were not quite steady, and I watched him read by firelight, watched his lips move around the words, watched him arrive at the line I had arrived at three nights ago in a locked archive with a stolen lamp.

*Payment rendered: one (1) soul, freely given, of the Brightwater line. Collected in full. Account settled until the stars return.*

"Freely given," Gideon read aloud. His voice cracked on the word *freely*, split it clean down the middle. "She volunteered. Is that what this says? She *volunteered*?"

"Or was persuaded to. Or was lied to. The ledger doesn't record coercion, only consent, and consent extracted from a frightened nineteen-year-old by people she trusted counts as consent in the Undercourt's law." I pushed myself up on one elbow, ignoring the pull of the stitches, because some things you cannot say lying down. "Gideon. Listen to me. There's more, and it's worse, and you need to hear all of it before you decide what to do with me. The account isn't closed. 'Until the stars return'—that's a deferral clause. The court doesn't consider the debt paid. It considers it *held*. And three days ago the order's high wardens voted to renew the bargain rather than let the protection lapse, which means—"

"Which means they're going to take someone else." He was on his feet now, pacing the little room, papers crushed in his fist. "Another child. Another family told a comfortable lie. And the stars—Petra, the stars over this valley haven't—"

"I know what the stars do here. I read the survey reports." I swung my legs off the bench, gasping, refusing to stop. "The stars went dark the night Wren died. They've stayed dark for seven years because the court *holds* the account open, and the deferral clause means they can call it due whenever they choose—and Gideon, they're choosing. The renewal vote wasn't precautionary. The ward-hunters were dispatched the same night. I was meant to lead them."

That stopped him. He turned and looked at me—at the wound, the mud, the three hundred miles written all over me—and asked the question I had been dreading, the one I'd rehearsed answers to across every mile and never found one good enough.

"Why? Why turn on your own order? Why bleed for strangers in a valley you've never seen?" His eyes narrowed. "Unless this isn't the first time you've been part of it."

There it was. Arithmetic again. Add the columns. Say the number.

"Because I helped forge the terms," I said. "Six years ago. I was a junior scribe in the contracts office, and I was proud of my cipher-work, and I drafted the deferral clause myself. I thought I was making the bargain *kinder*. Deferral instead of collection. Breathing room instead of a knife at the throat. I told myself I'd softened it." My voice failed and I dragged it back. "I built the cage and called it mercy. And seven years ago a girl named Wren Brightwater went into that cage because of paperwork with my handwriting on it, and I didn't even know her name until three nights ago, and I am not—"

I stopped. Breathed. The stone behind my sternum had gotten lighter, just barely, the way it does.

"I am not going to be the kind of person who builds cages and calls them kindness. Not anymore. So. That's who cut me, and why I'm here, and what I've done. Ward-hunters are coming—Serel escaped me, and Serel reports to the high wardens, and the high wardens do not leave witnesses. You can give me to them. Honestly, given what I just told you, I'd understand."

Gideon stood very still for a long time. Outside, the evening had gone fully dark, and through the window I could see the sky above the valley—black, starless, holding its breath. Then he looked down at the crumpled page in his fist, at his sister's name in ember-red ink, and when he raised his head his eyes were wet and furious and more alive than anything I had seen in years.

"They're coming *here*," he said. "The hunters. How soon?"

"A day. Two, if Serel's slower waking up than I think he is."

"Then you're not staying in this room." He was already moving—throwing a canvas satchel onto the bench, pulling a wrapped bundle of carving tools from a chest, moving with the sudden terrible efficiency of a man who has just discovered his whole life was a waiting room. "You're wounded, you can barely walk, and you're going to help me steal my sister's soul back from under a mountain. So drink this—" he shoved a cup of something bitter into my hand, "—and start telling me everything about the Undercourt, because I have a feeling, Petra Vane, that you're the only person alive who knows the way in."

I drank. It tasted like pine tar and regret.

Somewhere beyond the window, above the black valley, the starless sky waited—and beneath us, far beneath, I felt for the first time in six years the faint, patient tug of something old taking notice of its debt.

It knew we were coming.
