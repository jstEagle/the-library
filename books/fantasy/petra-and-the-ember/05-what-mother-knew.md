## 5. What Mother Knew

My mother had always been a woman who answered questions before they were asked. It was a habit of hers, like salting water before it boiled or humming when she was worried — she'd hear the shape of a thing forming in your mouth and hand you the answer whole, whether you wanted it or not.

So when I walked into her kitchen at dawn, still dressed in yesterday's clothes, with bone dust under my nails and Petra Vane asleep in my workshop behind three locked doors, and said nothing at all — she knew.

She was kneading bread. Of course she was kneading bread. My mother's answer to every crisis from flood to funeral was to make dough and punch it as though the dough were personally responsible. Her hands were white to the wrist. She didn't look up.

"Sit down, Gideon."

"I'll stand."

"You'll sit down because if you stand there looming, I'll drop this loaf, and then we'll have two tragedies in one morning instead of one." She did look up then, and whatever she saw in my face made her flinch — a small motion, quickly buried, but I've spent thirty years reading faces in bone, and bone doesn't lie any better than mothers do. "You found something."

"I found someone."

The dough stopped moving. For a moment the only sound in the kitchen was the tick of the stove iron cooling, and beneath that — nothing. The nothing that had lived in my head for two days now. The dead of Emberfall were silent, all of them, gone mid-sentence like a choir struck dumb, and I had learned in forty years of listening that silence has a texture too. This one felt like a held breath.

"Someone," my mother repeated.

"A woman. She came through the gate last night with a wound in her side and a ward-keeper's brand burned off her own arm." I pulled the chair out and sat, because my legs had decided the matter without consulting me. "She knew about the debt, Mother. The debt of the valley. And when I asked her what it was, she looked at me like I was the only person in Emberfall who didn't know."

My mother set the bowl down. She wiped her hands on her apron, once, twice, three times, though they were already clean, and I understood that she wasn't cleaning them. She was deciding how long she could keep being a woman making bread instead of a woman answering me.

"Gideon—"

"Don't." My voice came out harder than I meant it to. Harder than I'd meant anything to come out in years. "Don't do the thing where you give me half an answer wrapped in a warm loaf. Wren died seven years ago tonight. Seven years ago tonight, Mother, and I have spent every one of those seven years listening for her voice among the dead, and I never found it, and I told myself it was because the wards muffled things, or because grief plays tricks, or because some souls go quiet on purpose. And now a stranger falls out of the dark telling me our valley was bought with a soul, and the stars are gone, and every dead voice I ever heard has stopped talking at once — and you're going to tell me you don't know what she means by *debt*?"

"I'm going to tell you to make tea," my mother said. "Because what I have to say is going to take longer than tea takes to steep, and you'll want something to do with your hands."

I made the tea. It seemed important, suddenly, to let her have her rituals. People hold onto small ceremonies the way drowning men hold onto driftwood, and my mother was gripping hers hard enough to splinter.

* * *

She talked while the tea steeped, and then while it cooled, and then long after it had gone cold, and I learned that the history of Emberfall is not the history we carve on the founding stones.

The stones say: *In the year of the Long Frost, the founders came to this valley fleeing the courts of magic, and raised wards so strong that no power of the Undercourt could cross them.* That much is true. What the stones leave out is the price tag.

"There were nine founders," my mother said. She'd finally sat down across from me, and she'd taken off her apron, which frightened me more than anything else she'd done. My mother without her apron was like a house without a roof. "Nine families. And when they reached the valley, starving, hunted, their children dying of cold — they were met at the border by a thing wearing a beautiful face. An envoy of the court beneath the mountain. And it offered them exactly what they needed. Sanctuary. Wards no court-magic could breach. A place where their children could grow up ordinary and safe."

"And in exchange?"

"One soul." She said it steadily, the way you'd recite a recipe. I think she'd rehearsed. I think she'd been rehearsing for seven years, waiting for this morning the way you wait for a tooth to finish rotting. "One soul, given freely, each generation. Not taken — that was the condition. Taken souls break the bargain; the wards would fall. It had to be given. Volunteered. And the family who gave it would be protected above all others, honored, remembered." Her eyes came up to mine. "Ours was the first family. Brightwater blood bought the first ward-stone."

The kitchen tilted slightly. I gripped my cup and watched the tea shiver against the rim.

"That's not possible," I said. "Grandmother Hesta died in her bed. Great-grandfather Aldous died in his bed. I've carved tokens for every Brightwater grave in the east row, and none of them are empty—"

"The bargain doesn't take bodies, Gideon. It takes souls. The body lives out its natural span, hale and whole — that's part of the sweetness of it, that's why nobody ever refused. You live your whole life, you die old and honored, and only *then* does the court collect. Only then do you go down beneath the mountain instead of wherever souls are supposed to go." She folded her hands on the table. They were shaking. I had never seen my mother's hands shake. "Every generation, one Brightwater. Your great-aunt Maren. Your grandfather's brother Tam. And then—"

"No." I heard myself say it from very far away. "No. Wren drowned. Wren drowned in the millrace, I carried her body home myself, I carved her token with my own—"

"Wren didn't drown."

The words went through me like a chisel through green wood — clean, splitting, irreversible.

"She drowned," my mother said, and her voice cracked at last, seven years of rehearsal failing her at the crucial line. "But she didn't die. Do you understand the difference? There is a difference. The river took her breath, and the bargain took the rest. We laid out a body that breathed but wasn't — oh, Gideon, she was *there*, she opened her eyes, she smiled at us, but it was like looking through a window into an empty room. The healers called it a fever of the mind. The priest called it God's mystery. But your father and I knew. We'd always known what the family knew. We signed the ledger when she was born, the way every Brightwater signs it, the way *you* were signed—"

"I wasn't—" I stood up so fast the chair went over backwards. It cracked against the flagstones and neither of us moved to pick it up. "I'm alive. I'm sitting right here. If I'd been given, the court would have collected me when—"

"When the stars return," my mother whispered. "Collection comes when the stars return. That's the rhythm of it. One soul per generation, gathered at the turning. Wren's term is due *now*, Gideon. Now, with the wards fallen and the sky gone black. Whatever that woman told you — whatever she showed you — the timing isn't a coincidence. The court has come to collect its due, and the valley's protection died with the last payment."

I stood there with my heart slamming and my head full of silence, and I thought: *the dead fell quiet because the wards fell, and the wards fell because the debt came due, and the debt came due because—*

"Who chose her?" My voice sounded like someone else's. Like one of the dead voices, come back wrong. "Wren was nineteen. Who decided?"

My mother looked at the table. At the flour dust. At anywhere but me.

"She volunteered."

"*Volunteered?*"

"It's true." Her chin came up, and there it was — the stubborn set I'd inherited, the same jaw, the same refusal to be moved. "It's true, and I know how it sounds, but it's true. She found the ledger. She was nineteen and curious and she'd found the loose stone in the pantry the way you found everything, and she read the whole account, every entry back to the Long Frost, and she came to us crying and furious and she said — she said, *why does it always have to be someone who doesn't get asked?*" My mother's composure finally broke altogether, and she pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes. "She said the founders' children never got a choice, and that was the real crime, worse than the bargain itself. She said if someone had to go down beneath the mountain, then let it be someone who walked there with open eyes. She said she'd rather choose the cage than be put in it sleeping."

"So she killed herself," I said, "to protect a ledger."

"She *sacrificed herself* to protect a valley. Four thousand people, Gideon. Every child in this village who's never once been afraid of the dark, because the dark here was warded clean. That's what she bought. That's what she—" My mother stopped. Took a breath. When she spoke again her voice had gone very quiet, which in my mother was the register reserved for the most terrible truths. "That's what she thought she was buying. Because there's a second part, the part even most of the council doesn't know. The soul given to the court isn't just payment. It's fuel. The Ember beneath the mountain burns souls, and the ward-stones of this valley are fed from that fire. Wren isn't resting somewhere below. Wren is *burning*. She has been burning for seven years to keep four thousand people safe, and she knew that too, and she went anyway."

I don't remember crossing the room. I remember the door slamming, and the cold outside, and the walk up through the wet grass toward the workshop where a wounded stranger slept behind my locks, and somewhere in the middle of that walk I realized I was crying, which was its own kind of obscenity, because I hadn't cried at her funeral — hadn't been able to, standing there with half the village watching the bone-carver who hears the dead, waiting to see if he'd flinch when his sister's body went into the ground.

I hadn't heard her voice at the graveside. I'd told myself the wards muffled it.

They hadn't muffled anything. There had been nothing to hear. She wasn't dead. She was *fuel*.

* * *

Petra was awake when I got there. Sitting up on the cot with her back against the wall, gray-faced, one hand pressed to the bandage at her side, watching the door with the flat patience of someone who has spent her life expecting it to be kicked in.

"You talked to her," she said. "Your mother."

"How could you possibly—"

"Because you look like a man who's been told the floor was never there." She shifted, winced. "Sit down, carver. You're swaying."

I sat. On the floor, actually, because the chair seemed like a betrayal of chairs just then, and I put my back against the door and my knees up and looked at this stranger who had bled her way into my life carrying the end of the world in a satchel.

"Tell me about the ledger," I said.

Something moved behind her eyes — guilt, maybe, or its close cousin, fear. "You've heard enough for one morning."

"I've heard that my sister has been burning alive for seven years to heat the wards of a valley that thanked her with a gravestone and a lie. I've heard that my family has sold a soul every generation since the Long Frost and called it honor. I have *not* heard how a ward-keeper — one of the people sworn to maintain that arrangement — ends up bleeding in a bone-carver's workshop with her oath burned off her arm." I leaned forward. "And I have not heard why the stars went out, or what happens next, or what a man is supposed to do now that he knows his sister's soul is sitting in a vault under a mountain instead of at rest under a yew tree where I put a stone with her name on it."

Petra was quiet for a long time. Outside, the valley went on being beautiful — mist off the fields, smoke from the chimneys, the ordinary blessed machinery of a morning — and it looked obscene to me now, all of it, a garden planted over a furnace.

"The stars went out," she said finally, "because the Ember is nearly starved. The court has been burning souls faster than the tithes come in — there's a war in the deep places, or a hunger, or both, I never got the whole of it. The Ember dims, the wards fail, and the regent calls in every debt at once. Every valley. Every bargain. Every soul whose name is inked red in a ledger." She looked at me straight on, and I saw that underneath the exhaustion and the guilt there was a spine of something very hard, and that she'd built her whole self around it. "Your sister's name is in that ledger, Gideon. Inked ember-red. Due for collection. And I helped write the terms."

The words landed like stones in still water. I waited for the ripples.

"You *what*?"

"I was a tithe-clerk. Twelve years old, clever with figures, useful. I copied ledgers for the ward-houses — the true ones, not the pretty versions your founders show their children. Terms of collection, schedules of burning, the conversion rates of a soul into ward-light." Her voice didn't waver, but her hand had found the bandage again and pressed, as if holding herself together at the seam. "I wrote the clause that specifies when a volunteer's term begins. I wrote the clause that says a given soul cannot be reclaimed except by equal exchange. I wrote the arithmetic of your sister's damnation, and I was seventeen years old and I told myself it was just ink, ink isn't blood, until the day I watched them collect a farmer's daughter from a village two valleys over and I understood that every line I'd ever written ended in a mouth."

"Why are you telling me this?"

"Because you deserve to hate me accurately." She almost smiled. It was a terrible smile, thin as a knife's edge. "And because there's a clause I wrote that I've never told anyone about. A flaw. Deliberate. In case I ever grew a conscience, I wanted to leave myself a door." She reached for the satchel at the foot of the cot, wincing as the motion pulled her wound, and drew out a roll of vellum sealed with black wax. "The exchange clause. *A given soul cannot be reclaimed except by equal exchange.* Everyone reads that as: another soul must take her place. That's the intended reading. That's the trap." She held the vellum out to me. "*Equal* doesn't mean equivalent. It means equal in the court's own accounting. And the court values one thing above all other souls in creation. One thing it has been trying to replace ever since I ran."

"What?"

"A voice," Petra said. "The court lost its bone-carver when I fled — the one who kept the Ember's flame-souls calm, who listened to them burn. The vault is going mad without him. The regent would trade a hundred tithed souls for a man who can hear what the dead are saying while they burn." Her eyes held mine, steady and merciless and, beneath it, genuinely sorry. "Gideon. You're the only bone-carver in living memory born outside the mountain who can hear the dead at all. I didn't come to your valley by accident. I came because of *you*."

I took the vellum. My hands were steady, which surprised me. Somewhere past the roaring in my ears, past the image of Wren at nineteen walking into the dark with open eyes, past seven years of a lie carved in stone with my own chisel — somewhere past all of it, a thought assembled itself with terrible clarity:

There was a door beneath the mountain. And I was the key.

"Rest," I said, standing. "Eat something. Heal." I tucked the vellum inside my coat, over my heart, where the silence lived. "Tonight's the anniversary. Tonight I'm going to sit at my sister's empty chair and tell my mother what I intend to do. And tomorrow, Petra Vane, you and I are going to find out exactly what a soul costs — and start saving up."

Behind me, as I closed the door, I heard her say, very quietly, to no one:

"I was afraid you'd say that."
