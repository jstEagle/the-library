## 6. The Ledger of Souls

The ward-house had been beautiful once. I knew because I'd studied its blueprints as a girl, back when the Order still trusted me with paper that mattered. Vaulted ceilings, ward-stones set in a ring like a crown of teeth, and above it all a skylight of smoked glass so the keepers could watch the stars they were sworn to serve.

Now the roof was open to a sky with no stars in it at all, which struck me as either very funny or very blasphemous, depending on how much sleep you'd had. I'd had none.

"Mind the glass," I said, picking my way over the rubble. "It bites."

Gideon stepped where I stepped, which was either trust or stupidity, and with Gideon Brightwater it was usually both. He hadn't spoken much since his mother's kitchen. Some men shout when their world cracks. He went quiet the way deep water goes quiet—still on top, and something enormous moving underneath.

"You've been here before," he said. Not a question. His voice had that grave-dust rasp it got when he was working something out.

"I trained here." I kicked a shard of smoked glass off a fallen beam. "Four years. They don't tell you at induction that the building can be unmade. They tell you the wards are eternal. They tell you a lot of things."

"And now it's—" he gestured at the ruin, at the blackened stones, at the smell of burnt salt that still hung in the air three days after the collapse, "—this."

"Now it's this." I found what I was looking for: the archway to the record vault, half-collapsed but passable if you didn't mind stooping. Which I didn't. Humility comes easy when you're short. "And somewhere in there is the reason your sister's name is in my mouth instead of in the ground where it belongs."

He stopped walking.

I felt him stop, the way you feel a door close in a cold room. "You said," he began, carefully, the way a man walks on ice, "that Emberfall's protection was bought with a soul."

"I did."

"You didn't say whose."

"No," I agreed. "I didn't." I ducked under the lintel. "Coming?"

* * *

The record vault was the only part of the ward-house the collapse had spared, which told me something about priorities—the Order would let the roof fall before it let the paperwork burn. Keepers are like that. We'll sacrifice anything except our own accounting.

Inside, the dark smelled of vellum and old tallow. My hands remembered the shelves without my eyes' help, which is a strange thing, being guided by your own past self. Third row. Second shelf from the floor. The ledgers were bound in grey hide—not cow, not goat, nothing anyone could name—and each one covered a generation of the valley.

I pulled down the fourth ledger and blew the dust off it, and the dust went up my nose and I sneezed, loudly, in the dark, which rather ruined the gravity of the moment.

"Bless you," said Gideon dryly, from the doorway.

"It's not a blessing I need." I carried the book to the doorway where the last grey light came through, and set it on a fallen slab of marble like an altar, which is more or less what it was. "It's courage. And I'm about thirty years short."

He stood across from me, arms folded, waiting. He was good at waiting. A man who spends his life listening to the dead learns patience the way other men learn drinking.

"The founders built the wards," I said. "You know that much. Everyone in the valley knows that much. What nobody knows is what the wards cost to build, and what they cost to keep." I opened the cover. The first page was a contract, written in a hand so fine it looked embroidered. "Magic doesn't hold for free, Gideon. Nothing holds for free. The valley wanted a wall between itself and everything beneath the mountain, and the mountain's court agreed to sell one—for a price paid once a generation."

"What price?"

I turned pages. Generation by generation, the same fine hand, the same terms renewed, and each renewal signed by a keeper of the Order and witnessed by a founder's descendant. My own signature was four renewals back. I'd been nineteen and proud of my penmanship.

"A soul," I said. "One soul per generation. Volunteered, or chosen, or volunteered-so-hard-they-had-no-choice—I never learned which, at first. The court takes them below and keeps them. Feeds them into the Ember, eventually. The Ember burns souls the way your hearth burns wood, and the heat of it runs the wards." I looked up at him. "Your valley has been warm for two hundred years because someone's daughter has been burning underneath it."

His face did something terrible and small. "Wren."

"Wren," I confirmed, and turned to the last written page.

There it was. Seven years old, the ink still impossibly red—not the brown-red of old blood but the living red of embers, the red of something still burning. The entry read:

*Received into keeping: WREN BRIGHTWATER, of the line of the founders. Term: until the stars return. Disposition: the Ember.*

Below it, in the margin, in a different hand—a hurried, guilty hand I recognized because it was mine—someone had written: *She sang when she went down. God forgive us all.*

Gideon read it for a long time. Long enough that the light changed. When he finally spoke, his voice was almost gentle, which frightened me far more than shouting would have.

"'Until the stars return,'" he repeated. "The stars went out three days ago."

"Yes."

"So the term is—"

"Due. Yes. The moment the stars come back—or the moment the court decides they're not coming back—Wren goes into the Ember. If she hasn't already." I closed the ledger. "That's why I ran. That's why I broke my oath and walked through a collapsing ward-line to find you. Because there might still be time. There might be almost no time. But there might be some."

He was quiet. Then, in that same gentle voice: "You wrote that note in the margin."

My stomach dropped through the floor and kept going. "Yes."

"You were there."

"I was there."

"When she was taken."

"When she was taken," I said. "I was twenty-two years old and I held the pen while the terms were sealed, and I watched a girl your sister's age walk down into the dark singing, because the alternative was letting the wards fail and every soul in Emberfall go unprotected, and I was too young and too obedient and too much of a coward to ask whether those were really the only two choices." My voice cracked on the last word, which I hated. I hate crying in front of people. It feels like handing someone a knife handle-first. "So yes, Gideon. I helped forge the terms. I helped write her into that book. And then I spent seven years telling myself it was necessary, right up until the night I read the renewal schedule and realized the court intended to collect early, and I couldn't tell myself anything anymore."

The silence stretched. Somewhere in the ruined roof above us, wind moved through broken glass and made a sound like a very sad orchestra tuning up.

"You lied to me," he said at last. "At the gate. You said you came to warn me. You didn't say you were the warning."

"I know."

"My mother confessed her part. You made me drag yours out of a book."

"I know."

"Why?"

"Because if I told you at the gate, standing in the road, bleeding, you'd have looked at me the way you're looking at me now, and I needed you to walk with me first." I made myself hold his eyes. "Because I'm a coward, Gideon, and I've been one my whole life, and the only brave thing I have ever done is come here. Judge me after we save her. Please. Judge me after."

He looked at me for a long moment. I want to say I saw forgiveness in his face, but that would be a lie, and I've decided—at twenty-nine, late, but firmly—to stop lying. What I saw was arithmetic. A man weighing what I was against what I knew, what I'd done against what I could undo.

"You could have stayed," he said finally. "Run anywhere. The whole world just opened up. Instead you walked toward the court that raised you and the crime you committed. Why should I believe that?"

"Because I heard her sing."

He blinked. "What?"

"That night. In the margin, I wrote that she sang. That's not poetry, Gideon—that's memory. She sang a walking song, the kind children sing going down into cellars to make themselves brave. I've heard it every night for seven years. Every time I close my eyes." I pressed my hand flat against the ledger's grey cover. "I don't know if that's guilt or haunting or both. But I do know I can't make it stop, and the only way I've ever found to make a thing stop is to finish it. So. Finish it we will."

Another silence. Then Gideon did something I didn't expect. He reached out and turned the ledger back to Wren's page, and laid his palm flat beside mine, over the ember-red ink.

"She was warm," he said quietly. "When she was born. The midwife said she came into the world warm as a coal, and my mother took it for a blessing." His jaw tightened. "They put a coal in the ground and called it a blessing."

"Gideon—"

"I'm not angry at you." He said it like a man reporting the weather, testing whether it was true. "Or—I am. But she's due for collection, Petra. Due. Like a debt. And I have spent my whole life settling debts for the dead." He lifted his hand from the page. "Yours can wait in line."

It wasn't absolution. It wasn't even trust, exactly. But it was a door left open, and I have learned to take doors however they come.

"There's one more thing," I said, and winced at my own timing. "In the vault. Before we go."

He gave me a look that said, *of course there is,* but he followed me deeper into the records, past the ledgers, to a locked iron cabinet whose lock I broke with a pry-bar I'd stolen from the ward-house toolroom on my way out three nights ago. Inside were the seal-matrices—the stamps the keepers used to authorize renewals—and a bundle of correspondence tied in black cord.

Letters. From the court. To the Order. I untied the cord with fingers that shook, and spread the most recent one flat on the cabinet's shelf.

It was dated the night the stars went out.

"They wrote ahead?" Gideon asked.

"The court doesn't wait for events. It schedules them." I traced the fine, horrible script. "Look at the date. Look at the phrasing. 'In anticipation of the conclusion of the current term, the Court requests the presence of the debtor's kin for the ceremony of collection.'" I looked up. "They planned the collapse, Gideon. The wards didn't fail. They were called in. And they want you there when it happens."

"Why me?"

"Because you're the founder's blood. Because you can hear the dead." I swallowed. "And because, according to this letter, the regent believes a bone-carver who hears souls might be useful in the vault. As a tool. Or as a second deposit."

For a long moment neither of us moved. Above us, through the broken skylight, the starless sky sat over the valley like a lid.

Then Gideon picked up the letter, folded it with great care, and tucked it inside his coat, over his heart, the way a man carries a warrant or a grudge.

"Well," he said. "Then we'd best not keep the regent waiting."

* * *

We camped that night in the ward-house yard, because the road down from the valley passed the gate and neither of us wanted to be caught in the open. Gideon slept badly—I could tell by the way he arranged himself, curled around some invisible wound—and I sat up keeping watch over a fire the size of a cupped hand, turning the ledger's facts over and over like stones in a riverbed.

Near midnight, he stirred, and I thought he was dreaming, until he said, clearly, into the dark:

"Petra. Her voice is gone."

I went cold. "What do you mean, gone? You said you couldn't ever find her—"

"I could never find her among the dead. But she was there. Somewhere below. I always knew—like knowing there's a room in a house you've never opened." He sat up. His face in the firelight was grey. "Tonight the house is empty. Either she's gone somewhere I can't reach—" he met my eyes, "—or she's already in the Ember."

I looked at the ledger, lying shut beside me, its grey hide catching the firelight.

And beneath us—far beneath us, under miles of stone and older dark—I swear on every oath I ever broke that I felt it: a faint warmth rising through the ground, like a hearth lit in a cellar, like something beginning to burn.

"We leave at dawn," I said. "No. Now. We leave now."
