## 7. Voices in the Bone

The dead are terrible gossips. I want that on the record before anything else happens.

For three days after Petra Vane collapsed at our gate with a ledger full of my sister's name written in ember-red ink, the dead of Emberfall would not shut up about it. Old Marta Corren, dead forty years and still offended about her funeral turnips, kept muttering through the floorboards: *A soul sold, a soul sold, we always knew the founders were thieves.* The drowned boy from the millpond—Tomas, sweet Tomas, who has been ten years old for sixty years now—sang his little song about the water closing over him and changed the words to *the mountain ate her, the mountain ate her.* And my mother's father, who in life had been a stern man and in death was somehow sterner, stood at the foot of my bed every night saying nothing at all, which from him was the loudest thing anyone said.

I did not sleep. I sat up with the ledger open on my knees like a man reading his own death warrant, which is more or less what it was, except the name on the warrant belonged to someone else.

*Wren Brightwater. Sold to the Undercourt. Due for collection when the stars return.*

And beneath it, in smaller script, in handwriting I recognized because I'd spent three days staring at it until the letters swam: *Terms forged under seal of the Wardens' Order, by the hand of P. Vane.*

She'd told me herself, kneeling in the ruin of the ward-house with blood still crusted in her hair. "I helped write it," she'd said, in the flat voice people use when they've already sentenced themselves and are just waiting for the axe to catch up. "I was nineteen. They told me it was arithmetic. One soul for seven thousand lives. I checked the sums myself." Her hands had been shaking. "I checked them twice."

I should have hated her. I want to be honest about that too, since apparently honesty is going to be the theme of whatever comes next. I tried to hate her. I sat across the kitchen table from her while my mother bandaged her hands, and I looked at this woman who had signed my sister into hell with neat, careful penmanship, and I reached for hatred the way you reach for a tool you know is in the drawer.

What I found instead was something worse. Recognition.

Because she had done what everyone in this valley does. She had done the arithmetic. She had weighed one against seven thousand and found the one wanting, and she had gone home and slept, or hadn't slept, and life went on, and the valley stayed safe, and nobody ever said Wren Brightwater's name out loud again except once a year over a cold cup of tea.

That's not evil. That's just Emberfall. That's the whole valley, distilled down to ink and a signature.

"You're staring at me like I'm a grave-marker," Petra said that first night, watching me watch her.

"Old habit," I said. "I carve those."

"I know," she said. "I read your file."

"My *file?*"

"The Order keeps records on every warded settlement. Notable citizens. You were listed under 'anomalies.'" Something moved across her face—not quite a smile. "'Bone-carver, hears the dead. Harmless. Do not approach.'"

"Harmless," I repeated.

"Do not approach," she confirmed. "So naturally, here I am. Approaching."

My mother snorted from the stove. It was the closest thing to laughter our house had produced in three days.

* * *

Here is what I could not stop thinking about, in the small hours, while the dead muttered their commentary and the stars stayed gone and the silence where the sky used to be pressed down on the valley like a thumb:

Sold is not consumed.

Petra had said it herself, in the ward-house, tracing the ledger's columns with a finger that trembled only slightly less than mine. "There's a difference between sold and spent. Look—" and she turned the book toward me, and there it was, generations of names in ember-red, and beside most of them, in black, a second notation: *Consumed.* Dates. Little tidy dates, like harvest records.

But Wren's line had no black notation. Just red. Just *due.*

"They keep them," Petra had whispered. "Until they need them. A soul in the vault is a lamp. A soul in the Ember is fuel. As long as she's a lamp, Gideon—as long as she burns instead of being burned—she's still *her.*"

Still her. Still screaming, maybe. But still her.

I am a bone-carver. This sounds like a modest trade and mostly is. Grave-tokens, memory-beads, the little carved figures families press into a dead hand so the dead have something to hold onto in the dark. What the valley doesn't talk about—the thing the ward-keepers filed me under 'do not approach' for—is that bone remembers. Bone holds the shape of a life the way wax holds a seal. And I don't just carve the bone. I hear it.

Every token I've ever made has spoken to me. The dead murmur through the material of their own memorials—small things, mostly. Regrets. Recipes. The location of money hidden in walls. Old Marta's turnip grievance. It's not grand. It's not prophecy. It's a man sitting alone in a workshop all day with a chorus of quiet voices for company, and I had made my peace with it, I truly had, right up until the night my sister died and I realized that of all the voices in Emberfall, hers was the one I would never hear.

Seven years I thought that meant she was gone past reaching. Gone whole. Gone somewhere even bone couldn't follow.

Now I knew better. Now I knew she wasn't beyond hearing. She was *held*. There was a difference, and the difference had a vault, and the vault had a door, and doors—

Doors can be opened. I'm a carver. Doors are just wood and stone that haven't met the right knife yet.

* * *

On the fourth morning I went out to the barn before dawn, before my mother was up, before Petra woke on the pallet by the hearth, and I took down the box from the loft.

You have to understand what was in the box.

When Wren was born—two years before me, though you'd never guess it from how much she acted the elder—our grandmother did what the old families of Emberfall still did, back when the old ways weren't yet embarrassing. She took the afterbirth bone, the small pale nub of cradle-bone that every mammal carries into the world and leaves behind, and she dried it, and she wrapped it in linen, and she put it in a cedar box because cradle-bone is the first bone you ever owned. It knew you before your name did. It held the shape of you before you had a shape.

Most families burn it at the child's coming-of-age. Ours didn't. Our grandmother said Wren's should be kept, and no one asked why, because our grandmother had a way of saying things that closed the subject behind them like a door.

Wren died at twenty-three. The box went into the loft. I never opened it. Opening it felt like a kind of theft, or a kind of prayer, and I've never been sure which sin I was more afraid of committing.

Now I sat on a hay bale in the grey light with the box on my knees and the lid off, and there it was. Small as a walnut. Pale as a fingernail moon. Seven years of dust on the linen and underneath the dust, impossibly, it still smelled faintly of her—of rosemary oil and woodsmoke and the particular iron tang of her blood, which ran hot even for a Brightwater.

"Well," I said aloud, to no one, to the box, to her. "Either this works, or I'm the madman the ward-keepers always suspected I was."

The barn offered no opinion. The dead, oddly, had gone quiet—all of them, everywhere, as if they'd gathered close to listen and were holding their breath. Even Marta Corren. Even Tomas. The silence of several hundred attentive ghosts is its own strange pressure, like weather.

I got out my knives.

I won't pretend my hands were steady. I'm a craftsman; steadiness is my trade; and my hands shook like a drunkard's for the first hour anyway. Because this was different from every carving I'd ever done. Every other piece of bone I'd worked had belonged to someone finished—a life completed, sealed, murmuring its last small regrets. This bone belonged to someone unfinished. Someone whose story was still being written somewhere below the mountain, in fire, in a language I didn't speak but was about to learn.

I carved the way I'd never let myself carve before: listening with everything I had. Not shaping the bone into a symbol, the way you shape a grave-token into a bird or a star. Shaping it into *her*. The set of her jaw when she was being stubborn, which was always. The crooked half-smile that meant she'd already thought of three ways out of any trouble and was choosing the funniest. The way she hummed when she worked, off-key, defiantly, as if melody were a rule she'd personally decided to break.

Dawn came up gold through the slats of the barn. My fingers bled at two knuckles. The little figure grew in my palm—no bigger than my thumb, a woman mid-stride, head up, laughing at something I couldn't see.

And then—

Not a voice. Not at first. A *pull*, like a hook behind my sternum, like the moment on a riverbank when a fish takes the line and the whole world narrows to the tension in it. The bone warmed in my hand. Warmed past body-heat. Warmed toward something older than heat.

Then:

***GIDEON.***

I dropped it. I'll admit that freely. Seven years of composing dignified little elegies for other people's dead, and when my own sister's voice hit me like a thrown stone, I dropped her on the barn floor like a hot coal.

The voice didn't stop. It came up out of the bone where it lay in the straw, thin and ragged and *burning*, and it was her—it was her, it was her exactly, the way she shouted across the millstream when we were children, the way she called my name through market crowds—but wrong, stretched, frayed at the edges like cloth caught in machinery.

***Gideon, Gideon, can you hear me, oh gods, oh gods, you can HEAR me—***

"Wren." I picked her up. I held the little carved figure in both hands like a candle flame in wind. "Wren, I'm here. I'm here."

***It's dark. It's so dark and it BURNS, it burns all the time, they feed us to the light and the light eats slow, it eats slow on purpose, they LIKE it slow—*** A sound that might have been laughter, if laughter could bleed. ***No. No, listen. Listen to me. I don't have long, the lamp draws on me when they come to look, I can feel it drinking—***

"What do I do?" I heard my own voice crack down the middle like green wood. "Wren, tell me what to do."

***The ledger. Petra—did Petra come? Did she get out?***

"She's here. She's in the house. She brought the ledger."

***Good. Good girl. Stubborn, stupid, brave girl.*** Affection, fierce and undimmed, seven years and a mountain between us and still arriving intact. ***Gideon. Listen. The terms. Read the terms. The bargain isn't what they told the founders it was. It was never one soul for protection. Read the fine script. The Undercourt doesn't buy souls, it LENDS protection against them, and the interest compounds, and when the wards fell it wasn't an accident, it was FORECLOSURE—***

The valley. Seven thousand people. Generations of tidy annual payments, one soul each generation, and the whole time the debt growing underneath like rot under paint. The wards hadn't failed. They'd been *called in.*

"They're coming to collect," I said. "All of it."

***Everything. Everyone. The whole valley is collateral and none of them know, they never knew, Mother didn't even—*** She stopped. When she spoke again her voice had gone very quiet, and quiet, from Wren, was worse than screaming. ***Mother knew about me. Ask her what else she knew. Ask her what Father signed.***

"Father's been dead twelve years."

***Ask her anyway.***

The bone flickered in my grip like a dying lamp. I felt her start to slip—not away, but *downward*, pulled back toward that burning dark, and everything in me clawed at the edges of the connection like a man grabbing at a rope.

"Wren—wait—how do I reach you? How do I find you?"

***The Ember vault. Below the third gate, past the tollhouse of faces, where the court keeps its light.*** Her voice was thinning, stretching, a thread drawn through a needle's eye. ***Gideon. Brother. Don't come. Whatever you're thinking—don't. They'll take you too and then it will have been for nothing, all of it, and I will burn knowing I cost the valley TWO Brightwaters, and I swear I will haunt you so thoroughly you'll beg Marta Corren's ghost for peace—***

Despite everything—despite the terror and the grief and the shaking—I laughed. Wet, ugly, helpless laughter. "You always were generous with your threats."

***Someone has to be.*** A pause. The pull downward gentled, or she fought it to a standstill, or both. ***Listen. The dead here remember you. The ones the court has ground down—they pass your name around like bread. 'The listener,' they call you. 'The one who hears.' Gideon, they've been waiting for someone who hears for longer than this valley has existed.***

"Why?"

***Because a voice can be stolen.*** The words came slowly now, each one costing her something I didn't want to calculate. ***But a listener... a listener is a door. And doors, little brother—***

"Doors can be opened," I finished. "I know. I've been telling myself that all week."

***Then stop telling yourself.*** And there she was suddenly, blazing up through the frayed edges of her voice, all of her at once, my sister entire: the stubbornness and the iron and the ridiculous unkillable warmth of her. ***Come down. Come badly, come unprepared, come terrified—come ANYWAY. Bring the keeper. Burn the ledger. And Gideon—***

"Yes."

***Tell Mother I'm not angry. Tell her I understand about the arithmetic.*** A breath that was not a breath. ***But tell her the sums were wrong. Tell her someone taught her to count wrong, and I want it FIXED.***

The bone went cool in my hands.

Not silent—never quite silent, now. A hum lived in it, deep as a struck bell heard through a wall. She was still there. Still burning. Still *her*. But the door had closed, and on the other side of it my sister was alone in the dark with a debt the size of a valley, and I was standing in a barn covered in bone-dust and my own blood, holding the smallest, heaviest thing I had ever carved.

* * *

They were both in the kitchen when I came in—my mother at the table with her hands around a cold cup of tea she hadn't drunk, Petra by the window with the ledger open on her lap, both of them wearing the same expression, the look of people who have been awake all night doing arithmetic they hate.

They saw my face and stopped talking.

"It worked," I said. My voice came out scraped raw. I put the little figure on the table between the teacup and the ledger, and the hum in it seemed to fill the room, and both women leaned toward it without seeming to decide to. "I heard her. She's alive—or whatever they call it, down there. She's a lamp, Petra, just like you said. They haven't spent her. But the wards falling wasn't an accident, it was foreclosure, the whole valley is collateral, and the debt's been compounding since before any of us were born."

My mother made a sound I'd never heard her make. Small. Like a stitch tearing.

"And she left a message." I looked at my mother, and I made myself say it plainly, because Wren had earned plainness. "She says she's not angry. She understands about the arithmetic. But she says the sums were wrong—that somebody taught you to count wrong—and she wants it fixed."

My mother's hands came off the teacup slowly, the way you move when a snake is nearby. "Gideon—"

"There's more. She said ask you what Father signed."

The silence that followed had weight and texture. Outside, the starless sky pressed down. Somewhere in the walls, faintly, the dead of Emberfall had begun to murmur again—and for the first time in my life, they sounded afraid.

My mother looked at the little bone figure of her daughter, and then at me, and something in her face finally, finally broke open.

"Sit down," she said. "Both of you. And Gideon—fetch the strongbox under the floorboards. It's time you read what your father bought with his grave."
