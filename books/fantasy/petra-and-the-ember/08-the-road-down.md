## 8. The Road Down

### Gideon

I had lived forty-one years inside a bowl of hills and never once wondered what was over the rim, the way a fish does not wonder about the shape of water. This is not a compliment to my curiosity. It is simply what wards do: they don't lock you in so much as convince you there is nothing worth locking.

Petra Vane disabused me of that in about four minutes.

"The border's not a wall," she said, shouldering her pack at the edge of my mother's yard while the sky behind us still held the memory of stars going out like snuffed candles. "It's a promise. And promises, once broken, have edges. We're standing on one."

"Charming," I said. "Do you say things like that often, or am I special?"

"You're special," she said, without looking at me, which somehow made it worse.

We left before dawn because Petra insisted hunters travel at night and sleep at first light, and because my mother stood in the doorway holding Wren's old shawl and would not let me leave with an argument. She pressed the shawl into my hands and said, "Bring her home or bring yourself home, Gideon. Either will do. Only do it honestly." Then she kissed my forehead like I was nine years old, and I let her, because some debts you pay gladly.

The valley in early morning is the only country I know how to love—mist pooled in the low fields, the bone-shop shutters rattling, the dead murmuring their small regrets along the lanes the way other towns have stray dogs. Except the dead had gone silent when the stars went out, all of them at once, mid-sentence, and the silence had followed me for three days now like a dog that had finally caught its tail and found nothing there.

You'd think silence would be a mercy. Everyone always assumed I'd celebrate it—a quiet man at last, no more whispers of grandmothers fretting over unwatered roses, no more soldiers asking me to tell their mothers they'd meant to write more. But a man who has heard voices his whole life learns their rhythms the way you learn your own heartbeat, and when they stopped, it wasn't peace. It was a room where the fire had gone out. Cold in a way that felt personal.

"They'll come back," Petra said. She'd caught me listening to nothing again. I was getting easy to read; I hated it.

"You don't know that."

"I know what took them." She adjusted the strap across her chest where the ward-keeper's sigil used to sit; she'd burned it off her own skin two nights ago in my mother's hearth, and the smell of it still lived in my nose. "And I know what it wants. Silence isn't the punishment, Brightwater. It's the packaging."

"Wonderful. Do you practice being comforting, or does it come naturally?"

"It comes naturally," she said, and for the first time since she'd collapsed bleeding at the ward-stones, she almost smiled. It was a bad smile. A crooked, guilty, stubborn thing. I found myself absurdly glad to see it anyway.

We walked up through the terraces, past the shrine where the founders' names were carved so deep into the stone that generations of lichen had given up on them, and then the land tilted, and the trees changed, and I understood we were leaving.

Here is what nobody tells you about the border of a warded valley: crossing it feels like walking through a curtain of cold water that isn't there. One step I was Gideon Brightwater, bone-carver of Emberfall, a man whose whole world fit in a day's walk. The next step the air tasted different—thinner, wilder, carrying smells I had no names for. Pine resin and something metallic, something like rain on hot iron. My ears popped. Somewhere behind us, the mountain hummed a note I felt in my teeth rather than heard.

I stopped. I couldn't help it. The world simply *kept going*.

Hills beyond our hills, folding away into blue distance. A river down in the next valley catching light like spilled coin. Clouds moving with purpose, as if they had somewhere to be and had always had somewhere to be. I had spent my life carving small memorials for small lives, and here was a horizon that made the word *small* feel like an insult to arithmetic.

"Oh," I said. It was not my most eloquent work.

"Yeah," Petra said quietly. She was watching me watch the world, and there was something raw in her face—not pity, exactly. Recognition. "That's how it was for me too. First time out. I sat down on a rock and cried for an hour, if you want the truth."

"I absolutely do not want the truth," I said. "It makes me want to sit on a rock."

"We've got rocks," she offered. "We've got time. Well—" she glanced back the way we'd come, and the smile died, "—less time than I'd like."

Because we were pursued. Let's be honest about that part, since honesty is apparently the theme of my life now. Ward-hunters: six of them, maybe seven, sent down from the order Petra deserted, with orders to erase witnesses. *Witnesses* meaning Petra, who carried proof that Emberfall's protection had been bought with my sister's soul, and me, who had committed the crime of being related to the merchandise. They moved fast, they moved quiet, and according to Petra they did not send polite letters ahead.

"How far behind us are they?" I asked.

"If they're good? Half a day." She started walking. "If they're very good, they already know where we're sleeping tonight, and they're just deciding whether to kill us before or after breakfast."

"That's reassuring."

"I'm not paid to reassure you."

"You're not paid at all."

"No," she agreed. "Which makes the reassurance a luxury good."

We walked. The road down from the valley was old—older than the wards, older than the founders' bargain, worn into the hillside by feet that predated every name in our graveyard. That thought kept snagging in me like a burr. People had come this way before. People had left. Which meant leaving was possible, which meant everything the valley had ever told itself about safety and necessity was a story, and stories can be rewritten. I was a carver. I knew what could be done to stone with enough patience and a sharp enough edge.

By midday we reached the river—the same one I'd seen catching light—and Petra taught me to cross it at the shallows where the stones made a broken bridge. I slipped twice. She laughed at me both times, a short surprised sound, like laughter was a language she was relearning after years abroad.

"You carve bone but you can't cross a river?"

"Bone doesn't move."

"Bone moves," she said. "Believe me. I've seen what this court does with bone." Then she looked sorry she'd said it, because Wren was bone now, somewhere under the mountain, sold and shelved, and neither of us needed the reminder carved quite that deep.

We didn't speak for a while after that. The river talked instead, cheerful and indifferent, and I realized with a start that I could hear it clearly—no dead murmuring underneath it, no layered whispering, just water being water. For the first time in my life I was hearing the world alone. Just the world. Just me.

It should have been a relief. Instead I kept turning my head toward silences, waiting for someone to fill them.

### Petra

Let me tell you what I knew about Gideon Brightwater before we left the valley: nothing useful. He was the bone-carver who heard the dead, the brother of the tithe, the reason my order's dirtiest secret had a face. I'd read his file. Files are how cowards know people.

What I learned on the road was better and worse. Better: he was funny in a dry, self-deprecating way that he deployed like armor, and underneath the armor he was kind in a way that had cost him—he'd spent thirty years listening to strangers' regrets and never once, as far as I could tell, charged anyone for it. Worse: he trusted me. Already. After four days. He watched me make camp, watched me check our backtrail, watched me count our food twice, and he did it with the open, unguarded attention of a man who had decided I was on his side and saw no reason to revisit the decision.

I had betrayed an order, forged the terms of a soul-bargain, and lied to him about half of it so far. He trusted me anyway. It was like being handed a live coal and told to keep it warm.

On the second evening we made camp in the ruins of something—I never learned what, an old tollhouse or watchpost, walls hip-high, a chimney standing like a lone tooth. Gideon built the fire while I scouted, and when I came back he was sitting with his knife and a piece of river-driftwood, carving without looking at it, the way he must have done ten thousand times.

"What are you making?" I asked.

"A token." He turned it over. A bird, half-born, wings suggested rather than finished. "Habit. When I can't hear the dead, my hands get loud."

"Do they always ask to be listened to?" I nodded at the wood. "The dead."

"Not always. Mostly they want small things remembered. A name spelled right. Where they buried the good spoon." He smiled faintly. "People are mostly small, Petra. Even their ghosts. Especially their ghosts."

"And the big things?"

"The big things they take to their graves." His knife paused. "Or sell to courts beneath mountains, apparently."

There it was. I'd been waiting for it since the ward-house, since the ledger with Wren's name inked in ember-red, since I confessed—badly, in a voice I didn't recognize as mine—that I had helped draft those terms. That I had been nineteen years old and ambitious and told myself the math was clean: one soul per generation, weighed against thousands of lives, and surely the court would choose fairly, and surely fairness was a thing the Undercourt practiced.

Gideon hadn't forgiven me. I hadn't asked him to. But he hadn't left me on the road either, and every hour he didn't leave was an hour I owed interest on.

"Hunters," I said, because guilt is easier to survive when you're busy. "We need to talk about tomorrow."

"Talk about tonight first," he said. "Tonight, I'd like to eat something that isn't jerky."

"Tomorrow we cross the moor. Open ground, no cover, moonless. If they catch us there, we die tired."

"And the alternative?"

"There's a hollow under the old quarry ridge. Natural caves. Cold, wet, miserable."

"You're selling it beautifully."

"I'm not selling it. I'm telling you the truth, which is a new experience for both of us, so bear with me while I find the rhythm."

He laughed—really laughed, brief and rusty, like a door opening on a room long shut—and something in my chest did a thing I refused to examine. Found family, the old keeper texts called it, usually with contempt: bonds formed outside contract, outside oath, unpredictable and therefore dangerous. The texts were right. It was dangerous. I could feel it already, the way it made the idea of running alone seem suddenly unthinkable, and unthinkable things get people killed.

We ate. He carved. I took first watch, because I didn't trust him to know what hunter-sign looked like, and because watching the dark gave my hands something honest to do.

They came at the third hour, or nearly did.

I heard them before I saw them—the particular wrongness of a forest going quiet in a pattern, birds stopping in sequence rather than all at once, which means movement, deliberate movement, coming through. Two scouts, flanking wide. A main party holding back. Textbook. I'd been trained by the people doing it, which is the one advantage of betrayal: you know exactly what you're capable of.

I woke Gideon with a hand over his mouth, which he took remarkably well for a man who'd slept his whole life in a place where nothing had ever crept up on him. His eyes went wide, then narrow, then calm. He pointed at himself, then the fire, then raised an eyebrow. I nodded. He understood: the fire dies, we move, we don't fight.

We killed the coals and went out through the back of the ruin, low and quick, into bracken deep enough to swallow us. Behind us I heard the hunters reach our cold camp, and a voice—Vess, I was almost sure, Vess who had shared my bunk for six years—say softly, "Warm ash. Minutes. *Move.*"

We ran. Not far—they'd hear us—but far enough, then down into a streambed where our scent and sound would tangle, then along it in freezing ankle-deep water until my legs shook and Gideon's breath came in ragged hooks. We climbed out under an overhang of roots and lay there in the dark, side by side, soaked, listening to the hunters cast about above us like hounds cheated of a line.

For a long time neither of us spoke. Then Gideon said, barely a breath: "Is this what the world outside is like? All the time?"

"No," I whispered. "Sometimes it's worse."

A pause. Then, absurdly, I felt him shaking next to me and realized he was laughing—silent, helpless, terrified laughter, the kind that lives right next to crying.

"I spent my whole life," he breathed, "thinking the valley was the safe place and everywhere else was the story mothers invent to keep children from wandering. And it turns out I'm the story. I'm the cautionary tale. Some mother out there is telling her child about the fool who walked out of Emberfall."

"Every story needs a fool," I whispered back. "Otherwise nobody learns anything."

"Careful, Vane. That was almost warm."

"Don't get used to it."

But in the dark, in the cold, with death quartering the hillside above us, I reached over and gripped his forearm—just briefly, just to confirm he was solid, real, mine in whatever uncontracted way a person can be yours—and he gripped back, and neither of us mentioned it afterward, and it mattered more than anything either of us said all week.

### Gideon

Dawn found us in the quarry hollow, which was exactly as advertised: cold, wet, miserable, and alive. I counted that as a win. I was beginning to understand that outside the valley, winning was measured differently—smaller, meaner, more frequent. You didn't win the war. You won the morning. You won dry socks. You won another sunrise with both your throats uncut.

Petra slept first this time, curled against the cave wall with her knife in her hand even unconscious, and I sat watch and carved the driftwood bird finished and thought about my sister.

Seven years I'd searched the valley's dead for Wren's voice and never found it, and now I knew why: she wasn't in the valley's dead. She was under the mountain, a flame in a vault, a lamp burning in the dark for people who wore borrowed faces. Every mile we walked down was a mile closer to her, and every mile closer made the silence in my head louder, if silence can do such a thing. It could. Mine had learned.

Near midmorning Petra woke, drank from the seep at the cave's mouth, and studied me with that look she got when she was deciding how much truth I could carry today.

"There's a town," she said. "Three days south, where the river bends. Ashmarket."

"Inviting name."

"It's a place where souls are pawned. Literally. Heirlooms of the self, bought and sold and resold. If we want to get under the mountain, we need someone who knows the doors, and there's a man there—an ex-underpriest, disgraced, drunk, expensive—who does."

"So we're going to a town that sells souls to hire a priest who lost his, to break into a court that owns my sister's." I turned the carved bird over in my fingers. "When you put it in a list, it sounds insane."

"When you put anything in a list it sounds insane. Lists are honest. That's their whole problem."

I looked at her—this woman who had burned her own skin free of her order, who had lied to protect me and fought with me about it and not apologized because she wasn't sorry, who had gripped my arm in the dark last night like I was something worth keeping—and I thought about the fish and the water. I had spent my whole life in a valley built on a lie so old it had become landscape. She had spent hers in an order built on the same lie from the other end. Neither of us had chosen our prisons. But here we both were, walking out of them, together, toward the thing that had taken my sister and made her.

"Petra," I said. "When this is done—if it gets done—what happens to you? To us? There's no ledger line for this. No contract."

She was quiet a moment. Outside the cave mouth, the moor stretched away gold and endless under a sky that had, overnight, grown back a single star—faint, wrong-positioned, but burning. We'd both seen it at dawn and neither of us had spoken of it, because speaking of hope out loud is a way of daring the world to test it.

"I don't know," she said finally. "Nobody's ever asked me that question without wanting something."

"Well," I said, and held out the little driftwood bird, wings finished now, ready for whatever sky it got. "Consider it asked for free."

She took it. She looked at it a long time. And then, far off across the moor, a horn sounded—one clear note, then another, answering from the south, from the direction of Ashmarket.

Not hunters. Hunters didn't announce themselves.

Something else had begun moving, and it was moving faster than we were.
