## 9. The Tollhouse of Small Mercies

I have been robbed before. I have been the robber, too, and I can tell you which side of that transaction teaches you more about people. When you steal, you learn what a person guards. When you're stolen from, you learn what they never thought to guard at all.

The tollhouse sat where the Vell River narrowed to a throat of grey stone, a squat building with a roof of river-slate and a sign that read, in cheerful red paint, *Small Mercies — All Debts Considered*. The ferryman was a broad woman with forearms like mooring rope and a smile that had been sanded down to something functional. She did not ask for coin.

"Memories," she said, as if discussing the weather. "One each. Nothing dramatic. We don't want your wedding day or your mother's face. Just something small. A taste. The river's not greedy, only hungry."

Gideon went pale in the particular way he did when his bone-carver's hands couldn't solve a problem. "You want to *eat*—"

"Nibble," she corrected. "There's a difference. Ask anyone who's crossed."

"I'd rather not."

Behind her, on the far bank, the road climbed into hills I had never seen except in the ward-house maps, where they were drawn as flat brown smudges labeled *beyond concern*. Beyond Emberfall. Beyond everything I'd known. The hills were green. Nobody had mentioned they would be so green.

"We could swim," Gideon said.

"You can't swim."

"I could learn very quickly."

"The Vell doesn't freeze, but it doesn't forgive either." I said it lightly, because I'd already done the arithmetic, and because I had decided something in the last mile of road that I hadn't yet told him. "We pay. It's one memory. You have thousands."

He looked at me then, and I watched him do his own arithmetic, and I knew we'd arrived at different sums.

* * *

Here is what you should understand about me, since apparently I'm telling this part: I was raised by the Undercourt, and the Undercourt does not take memories. It takes names. A name is worse. A memory is a thing that happened to you; a name is the person it happened to. They took mine when I was six and gave it back when I was sixteen, worn thin at the edges like a coin passed through too many hands, and I spent ten years learning which parts of Petra Vane were original manufacture and which were court-issue replacements.

So I know the price of selfhood down to the copper. And I know that Gideon Brightwater, who heard the dead every day of his life until the stars went out, was walking around with an open wound where his sister's voice used to be, and that wound was stuffed full of guilt, and guilt is the most nutritious memory there is. The river would find it. Rivers always find the soft spot.

"One memory each," the ferrywoman repeated. She'd produced a ledger, naturally. Everything in this world runs on ledgers; I should know, I helped forge one. "Step up to the window, think of the thing, and let it go. Most folk describe it as a sigh."

"And if we refuse?" Gideon asked.

"Then you walk upstream eleven days to the bridge at Karst, which is guarded by something I won't describe on an empty stomach." She smiled her functional smile. "Everyone pays, carver. That's the mercy. It's small, but it's honest."

Honest. I nearly laughed.

* * *

He paid first. I watched him grip the windowsill, close his eyes, and exhale—and I saw the moment it left him, because his shoulders dropped and his face went briefly, terribly blank, like a page turned before you finished reading it. Then he blinked and said, "Huh," and couldn't say what he'd lost, because that's the trick of it. You can't miss what you no longer remember missing.

"What was it?" I asked.

"No idea." He flexed his hands. "That's the worst part. There's a shape where it used to be. Like a tooth gone wrong."

Then it was my turn, and here is where I tell you what I decided, and let history judge me for it, because I've judged myself plenty and I've grown bored of the company.

I stepped up to the window, and instead of giving the river one of my own memories, I gave it one of his.

I know how it worked because I'd seen the mechanism in the ward-house archives—the toll-river takes from whoever stands at the window, yes, but memory is portable. It lives in the body. And I had spent three days walking beside Gideon Brightwater listening to him talk about Wren, and I knew exactly which memory the river would want from him if given the choice: the night she died. The sound of the house. His mother's face. The specific quality of the silence after.

So I stood at the window and I held that memory in my mind—not mine, never mine, borrowed grief—and I let the river drink.

It took the anniversary. Not the death itself—I couldn't give what I didn't own—but the seventh marking of it. The empty chair. The candle his mother lit. The way the whole house had arranged itself around an absence for seven years running. He woke the next morning and it would be just another day, and some part of me howled at the cruelty of that, and another part, the part the Undercourt raised, said quietly: *he'll grieve better without the ritual. Rituals are hooks. Hooks are how courts reel you in.*

Both parts of me were lying. One of them was just better dressed.

"There," the ferrywoman said, stamping her ledger. "Painless."

Nothing is painless. It's only painless for whoever isn't paying.

* * *

We crossed. The ferry cost nothing further, which should have been my first warning that I'd overpaid. The far bank was all birch and bracken, and the road bent north toward Ashmarket, and for two hours I let myself feel clever. I had protected him. I had spent myself cheaply so he wouldn't spend what he couldn't afford. If that wasn't loyalty, what was?

Then, at the second milestone, he stopped walking.

"You didn't pay," he said.

"I paid."

"You paid *something*. But you came off that boat lighter than you went on, and I came off heavier, and I've been turning it over for two miles like a stone in the shoe." He turned. His eyes had that flat, patient look I'd learned meant trouble—the look of a man who works with dead things and has stopped being hurried by them. "What did you give them, Petra?"

"A memory. As instructed."

"Whose?"

And there it was. The question I'd hoped he'd never ask, because I'd built my whole plan on the assumption that Gideon Brightwater was too decent to suspect other people of arithmetic. I had forgotten that decency and intelligence often share a house.

"Mine," I said.

"Say it again, slower, and mean it."

I looked at the birches. I looked at the road. I looked anywhere but at him, which was answer enough, and we both knew it.

"You gave them one of *mine*." His voice didn't rise. That was somehow worse. "You stood at that window and you sold a piece of my life without asking, and then you lied to my face about it while the ink was still wet on their ledger."

"It was the anniversary," I said. "Of Wren. The river would have taken it anyway—it goes for grief like a magpie goes for shine. I just chose *which* piece. I chose the one that was eating you alive. Seven years of lighting candles for a dead girl, Gideon, seven years of arranging your whole life around a chair—that's not mourning, that's a hook, and anything with a hook in it can be *reeled*, and I have been reeled before, and I will cut the line out of my own hands before I watch it happen to you—"

"You don't get to decide what my grief costs me!"

He shouted it. I actually flinched, which I haven't done since I was nine years old and the regent's steward taught me what flinching was for. Somewhere in the birches a bird went up in a clatter of wings.

"You don't," he said again, quieter now, which was worst of all. "Do you understand what you took? That morning was the last morning I had her. Not her death—I still have that, gods help me—but the *morning*. Mother setting her chair. The candle. The whole stupid liturgy of it. It was the last thing I did as her brother on purpose, every year, and now—" He pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes. "Now there's just a hole shaped like it, and I can't even mourn the loss of it, because I don't remember it well enough to miss."

"I was protecting you."

"From what? From feeling things? Petra, I carved grave-tokens for a living. I *sat with the dead*. Feeling things was the entire job." He dropped his hands. His eyes were wet and furious. "You know what the difference between us is? I spent my whole life being trusted with other people's worst moments. Every family in Emberfall handed me their grief and said, hold this, make something of it. And you—you were raised by thieves of souls, and when you love someone, your instinct is to pickpocket them for their own good."

"That's not fair."

"No," he agreed. "It isn't. Fairness would be asking. Fairness would be standing next to me at that window and saying, 'the river wants your worst memory, and I think we should fight it together.' Instead you did what the Undercourt does. You decided for me. You made me smaller and called it shelter."

The word landed like a nail driven true. *Shelter.* Because that was exactly what I'd called it, in the privacy of my own skull. Shelter. Protection. The same words the court used, right before they explained why the tithe-children weren't allowed outside walls.

"I'm sorry," I said. And I meant it, which I could tell surprised him, because he'd braced for something else—an argument, a justification, the smooth court-trained deflection I keep holstered for exactly these occasions. "I'm sorry. I did the math and I put you on the wrong side of the ledger, and I've done it before, and I'll probably do it again, because it's the only language I was taught. But I'm sorry, and I'm telling you the truth now, which is more than they ever did."

He was quiet a long time. The river muttered behind us, digesting.

"I believe you're sorry," he said finally. "I don't yet believe you won't do it again. Those are different problems, and I need you to work on both before Ashmarket, because in Ashmarket everyone will be offering to protect us, and most of them will mean it exactly the way you meant it."

"That's fair."

"And Petra?" He picked up his pack. "Next time there's a price, we pay it together or not at all. Even if together costs more. *Especially* if together costs more. That's the whole point of not traveling alone."

* * *

We walked. The fight didn't end so much as bank itself, like a fire raked over for the night—still hot underneath, still glowing if you stirred it, but survivable to sit beside. I counted that as progress. In the Undercourt, disagreements were settled by whoever owned whose name, and nobody ever apologized, because apology implies the ledger can be amended, and the whole architecture of the court rests on the belief that it cannot.

By late afternoon the birches gave out and the land opened into long gold grass, and Gideon started pointing out things like a man seeing color for the first time—which, I suppose, he was. A hawk. A fence post carved with someone's initials, decades old, weathered to lace. A farmhouse where a woman was beating a rug and paused to watch two strangers pass, and waved, just waved, expecting nothing.

"People live out here," he said, wonderstruck. "Without wards. Without walls. Just—out here."

"That's most of the world, I'm told."

"Why does no one in Emberfall mention that?"

"Because mentioning it raises awkward questions about why you'd stay behind wards that run on a soul a generation." I said it before I could stop myself, and watched it land. He'd known the theory—we'd found the ledger, we'd read the terms—but theory is different when you're standing in the actual world those terms bought. The green hills. The waving woman. All of it purchased with Wren Brightwater, due for collection when the stars returned.

He was quiet for a mile. Then: "When we get her back—when the stars come back—do you think they'll rebuild the wards?"

"Do you want them to?"

"I don't know." He kicked a stone off the road with more force than the task required. "A month ago I'd have said yes without thinking. Now every ward-stone looks like a receipt."

Ahead, the road crested a low rise, and the land beyond it was dark in a way that had nothing to do with the hour. A town crouched in a fold of the hills, roofed in slate the color of old bruises, chimneys bleeding smoke that didn't rise so much as *hang*, as if even the smoke had debts it was avoiding. Lights burned in windows, but they burned the wrong colors—green, violet, a deep arterial orange—and music drifted up the hill, fiddle and drum and something underneath the music that my skin recognized before my mind did: the particular hum of soul-magic being spent casually, the way a rich man spends small coins.

Ashmarket.

"Rules," I said, stopping at the crest. "One: we're not from Emberfall. Two: we don't say the word 'ward' above a whisper. Three: whatever anyone offers you—a bargain, a kindness, a drink—assume there's a clause."

"And four?"

I hesitated. This was the moment to say it, and I knew it, and some traitor corner of me wanted to hoard the knowledge a little longer because knowledge was the only currency I'd never been robbed of.

But I'd promised. Together or not at all.

"Four," I said. "In Ashmarket, they can see what you've lost. The toll-river's payment leaves a mark—a gap, like a missing tooth in your shadow. Pawnbrokers read gaps the way you read bone. Someone will notice yours tonight, and they'll guess what kind of memory it was, and they may try to sell it back to you."

His head came around slowly. "They can do that?"

"They can do anything in Ashmarket. That's the town's whole religion." I started down the hill. "So decide now, before we're inside: if someone offers you back the morning of your sister's anniversary—at a price—what's your answer?"

Gideon walked beside me in silence for a hundred paces, the bruise-colored roofs rising to meet us, the wrong-colored light strengthening, and when he finally spoke, his voice was steady in a way that frightened me more than his shouting had.

"My answer is that I want to hear the price first," he said. "All of it. Before anyone decides anything. Including you."

Below us, the gates of Ashmarket opened onto the dark, and something in the dark opened its ledger.
