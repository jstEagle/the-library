## 10. Ashmarket

The first thing Ashmarket taught me was that I had been raised in a nursery.

I don't mean that as an insult to Emberfall. I mean it the way you'd mean it about a man who grew up thinking a candle was the sun, and then one evening walked out of his valley and found the actual sky waiting for him, enormous and indifferent and full of things that wanted to eat him. The wards had kept out more than magic. They'd kept out the knowledge that everywhere else, people had simply stopped pretending magic needed to be kept out at all. It had moved in. It paid rent. In some neighborhoods it owned the building.

Petra walked ahead of me through the gate with her hood up and her wound freshly dressed, moving like a woman returning to a house she'd sworn never to enter again. I followed, doing my best impression of someone who belonged in a lawless town, which mostly consisted of not looking up. Looking up in Ashmarket was a mistake. Above the market stalls, strung between crooked chimneys on lines of silver wire, hung lanterns. Each one glowed with a light that had no business being that color—that deep, patient amber of banked coals—and each one flickered in a rhythm too much like breathing.

"Soul-lanterns," Petra said, without turning around. "Don't stare."

"I'm staring."

"I know. Stop."

"How can I tell if they're—"

"You can't. That's rather the point." She adjusted her pack strap over the bandaged shoulder. "Some are empty. Sold back, redeemed, whatever. Some aren't. The ones that aren't flicker faster. Like they're trying to get your attention."

One of them, three stalls down, was flickering very fast indeed. I made myself look at the turnips instead. The turnips were honest. I have always appreciated honesty in root vegetables.

* * *

Let me explain what I knew about souls before that morning, so you understand the size of the education I received by nightfall.

In Emberfall, a soul is a private thing. You're born with it, you carry it around like a stone in your shoe—sometimes heavy, sometimes barely noticeable—and when you die it goes wherever souls go, and the dead who stay behind stay because they choose to, murmuring their small regrets into the ears of anyone unlucky enough to be born listening. That was my whole theology. Dead people hang around, they complain, occasionally they tell you where they hid the good spoons. It's not a grand system, but it's mine, or it was.

Ashmarket's theology fit on a sign nailed above the pawnbroker's row: **SOULS BOUGHT. SOULS SOLD. SOULS HELD IN TRUST. FAIR RATES. NO QUESTIONS.**

The fair rates were chalked on a slate beneath it, and I will confess that my first reaction, God forgive me, was arithmetic. Seven years of hearing the dead, and some part of my mind looked at those numbers and thought: *that would buy Mother a new roof.* The second reaction arrived half a heartbeat later and felt like being dunked in cold river water, because I understood what I'd just priced, and whose face I'd nearly put on the collateral.

"That look," Petra said quietly, "is why we don't stop here longer than we have to."

"I wasn't going to—"

"You were doing sums. Everyone does sums their first hour. Then they see the redemption counter." She nodded toward a smaller window off to the side, where a line of people stood clutching claim-tickets, waiting to buy back pieces of themselves. An old man at the front was arguing with the clerk, his voice cracking: *"But that was my wedding day, you can't sell my wedding day, she's still alive, I need to remember her—"*

"Memory, hope, grief, courage," Petra said. "It all pawns. A soul isn't one thing, Gideon. It's a thousand small flames, and this town has figured out how to sell them one at a time." Her jaw tightened. "The Undercourt invented the practice. Towns like this are just the retail end."

We were looking for one particular piece of retail: a disgraced underpriest named Brannic Odo, who according to Petra had once held keys to the mountain's doors and now held nothing but debts and a drinking problem, in that order of severity.

"Why do you know he exists?" I asked.

"Because I helped ruin him." She said it flatly, the way you'd report weather. "Back when I was still a keeper. He was ordered to seal a door we needed open. He refused. They stripped his office for it." A pause. "He never learned why I wanted the door open. He probably assumed the worst. He'll want payment anyway."

"Payment in what? We have almost nothing."

"We have something he wants more than money." She glanced sideways at me, and there was something apologetic in it. "You."

* * *

We found him—or he found us, which in Ashmarket amounts to the same thing—in the back room of a tavern called the Drowned Bell, a name that turned out to be literal, since the cellar flooded every spring and the owner had decided to lean into it. Brannic Odo sat at a table built from a wagon bed, a mountain of a man gone soft at the edges, with a beard that had clearly given up on ambition and eyes that hadn't. That was the first thing I noticed about him. Drunk men's eyes go vague. His went through you like an audit.

"The ward-keeper," he said, before Petra had finished sitting down. "Or the ex-ward-keeper. Or the traitor, depending on which ledger you read. And this must be the bone-carver." He looked at me the way I imagine a jeweler looks at a stone he suspects is worth more than the seller knows. "Sit down, carver. Let's talk about what you hear."

"I don't hear anything right now," I said, and it came out harder than I intended, because it was true and it still felt like a lie. Since the stars went dark, the dead of Emberfall had fallen silent—the wards' collapse had taken even them, or muted them, or done whatever it is that silence does to voices. Three days of quiet. The first true silence of my life. People assume silence would be a mercy to a man like me. Those people have never had their whole world go quiet at once, mid-sentence, like a room full of friends struck dumb.

"No," Brannic agreed. "But you did. And you will again, somewhere under the mountain, where the old dead are thick as fog. Which is why she brought you to me." He poured three cups from a jug that had seen better decades and pushed two across the table. "Ask me."

"Ask you what?"

"Whatever she told you to ask. There's always a question she coaches people to ask. Keeps her from having to say the hard part herself." He smiled, and it was not entirely an unkind smile, but it had teeth. "Go on. I'll wait. I'm very good at waiting. It's most of what disgrace consists of."

I looked at Petra. She was studying the grain of the table with tremendous focus.

"What do you know about the doors under the mountain?" I asked.

"Everything," Brannic said. "That's the short answer. The long answer is that I spent nineteen years as an underpriest of the Ember rite, and in nineteen years I opened the vault road twice, sealed it four times, and buried six people who went in without proper rites. I know which doors answer to prayer, which answer to blood, which answer to nothing at all, and which one—" he tapped the table, once, "—answers to a bone-carver's hand, though I'd wager everything in this jug that nobody up top remembers that anymore. Including her." A nod at Petra. "She knows the court. She doesn't know the craft. You want to steal a flame out of the Ember vault, carver, you need both."

The word *steal* sat in the air between us. Nobody had said it aloud until then. Planned it, yes—three nights of planning around small fires, Petra drawing the deep roads from memory while I tried not to think about what was drawn at the end of them. But saying *steal* out loud, in a public tavern, to a stranger, made it real in a way the planning never had. My sister's soul, sold to buy my valley's safety, burning in a lamp-vault beneath the world. We were going to steal her back.

"Yes," I said. "That."

Brannic drank. Set the cup down. Looked at me for a long moment with those auditing eyes.

"Here's my price," he said. "Not coin. I've got creditors who'd take coin off your corpse, so coin's no use to either of us. My price is this: you carve me a door-token. Bone, cut with the old rite, the kind that answers the third door—the one that answers to nothing else anymore. They took my office. They didn't take my hands." He held them up, big and scarred and steady. "I want back in, carver. Not into their priesthood. Into the mountain. On my own terms, through my own door, and I want the tool that opens it made by someone whose gift is real. Do that, and I walk you to the vault road myself. Refuse, and enjoy the mountain without me. It's very large, and it eats people who guess."

"A door-token," I repeated. "You want me to make you a key."

"I want you to make me a *door*. Keys get confiscated. Doors just stand there looking innocent." He leaned back. "Well?"

I should have haggled. Petra was giving me a look that said *haggle, you soft-hearted fool, he expects you to haggle, it's how these people show affection.* But I was tired, and my shoulder ached from sleeping on stones, and somewhere behind me a soul-lantern was flickering fast, fast, fast, like something trying to remember its own name.

"One condition," I said. "When we're under the mountain—if we pass any of the dead, the bound ones, the ones they use for lamps and locks and worse—you help me listen to them. Whatever's left of them. Even if it slows us down."

Something moved behind Brannic's eyes. It might have been surprise. It might have been the expression of a man realizing he'd been hoping to be asked exactly that and hadn't dared hope it.

"Done," he said, and stuck out a hand like a ham hock, and that was that. Found family, chapter three. Though I didn't know yet that I was reading it that way. At the time it just felt like a deal with a large drunk man in a flooding tavern, which is honestly how most families start.

* * *

We spent the rest of the day in Ashmarket, because Brannic refused to travel at night—"the hunters work nights, and so do worse things"—and because there were supplies to buy, and because Petra had one errand of her own that she wouldn't explain.

So I saw the town properly. I wish I could unsee some of it.

I saw the memory-brokers' row, where you could sell a happy afternoon and get enough to eat for a month, and where the brokers wore little brass scales on chains around their necks and weighed recollections like fish. I saw a woman buy back her mother's voice—one minute of it, that was all she could afford—and stand in the street afterward with her eyes closed, playing it over and over on a small bone whistle, crying without any noise at all. I stood near her for a while. Old habit. Waiting to hear if anything lingered around grief like that. Nothing did. Grief that gets bought and sold leaves no ghost. That might be the cruelest thing I learned in Ashmarket: the trade is so thorough it even robs the mourning of its echoes.

I saw children playing a game with knucklebones where the loser had to give the winner "a secret," and I heard one boy solemnly inform another, "My da sold his brave, so if the whistlers come at night, don't count on him," and the other boy nodding like this was ordinary weather-talk. To them it was. That's the part that got under my skin deepest—not the cruelty, but the *domesticity* of it. Evil that announces itself you can fight. Evil that keeps regular market hours and offers fair rates, that you just live inside, the way a fish lives inside water.

And I saw the tithe-house. I didn't mean to. It was at the end of the pawnbrokers' row, a low black building with no windows and a single door, and outside it stood a rack of small lanterns, child-sized, each one dark. Unlit. Empty. Waiting.

"Gideon." Petra's hand on my arm. "Walk."

"What happens in there?"

"Nothing you need to see."

"Petra."

She was quiet long enough that I thought she wouldn't answer. When she did, her voice had gone somewhere flat and far away, the voice she used for the worst facts.

"It's where the court collects. Souls owed to it come due, and the tithe-house holds them until collection. Children born under debt-contracts. Families that gambled and lost. Whole bloodlines, sometimes, sold generation by generation." Her fingers tightened on my arm. "I was collected from a house like this one. Different town. Same door. Same smell of tallow and iron." She let go of me and straightened her shoulders. "Now you've seen it. Now we walk, because if you keep standing there looking at it like that, you're going to do something noble and stupid, and I've only known you two weeks and I already can't afford it."

I walked. But I looked back once, at the dark little lanterns on their rack, and I made a promise to them that I had no right to make and no intention of breaking: *somebody is coming for all of you, eventually. Maybe not me. But somebody.*

It's a strange thing, discovering you're an idealist at thirty-four years old. I'd always thought of myself as a practical man. The valley had a way of teaching practicality. But standing in that street, I understood that I had crossed some line I couldn't uncross—I was no longer a man trying to save his sister. I was a man who had seen the whole shape of the machine, and you cannot unsee a machine. It follows you home.

* * *

Evening found us back at the Drowned Bell, supplies stacked by the door, Brannic marking our route on the tabletop in spilled ale. He drew the mountain as a smear of charcoal, the deep roads as veins running down into it, and at the center a circle he tapped twice without naming.

"The vault road enters through the Weeping Stair," he said. "Three days' travel from here, less if the weather's kind, which it won't be. From the stair down, we move by rite and riddle, and carver—" he pointed the ale-stick at me, "—your gift comes back the moment we cross under the roots. The old dead down there don't care about valley wards. They never did. You'll hear more than you've ever heard in your life, and none of it will be friendly, and you will *not* answer every voice that calls your name. That's the first rule. Say it back."

"Don't answer every voice that calls my name."

"Second rule: whatever you hear about me down there, it's all true, and I'm not proud of any of it, and we can discuss it after we're alive."

"Noted."

"Third rule—" He stopped. His eyes had gone past my shoulder to the tavern door, and everything in his big soft face closed up like a shop at curfew.

I turned.

Two figures stood in the doorway, dressed in traveling grey, faces unremarkable in the way that only trained faces are unremarkable. Ward-hunters. I knew it the way you know a storm by the smell of it—Petra had described them often enough, and besides, the whole room had gone quiet around them, the way rooms do around certain kinds of authority. One of them scanned the benches slowly. The other spoke to the barkeep, low, and the barkeep's eyes flicked—just for a heartbeat—toward our corner.

Toward Brannic.

"They're not here for us," Petra breathed. "They can't know we—"

"They're here for names," Brannic said quietly. "Hunters shake towns like this one for names. And everyone in this room knows a disgraced underpriest drinks at the Drowned Bell, and everyone knows the underpriest had visitors today, and one of those visitors is the most famous traitor the keeper order has produced in a century." He drained his cup and set it down with terrible calm. "Question is whether they already have my name on their list, or whether I'm about to earn a place on it."

The hunter by the bar finished speaking and began to walk. Toward the back tables. Toward us.

Brannic rose, unhurried, a mountain deciding to relocate. "Back cellar," he murmured. "There's a drain. It's disgusting. Go." And then, as Petra grabbed my sleeve and pulled, he picked up the ale-jug in one enormous hand, turned to face the walking hunter, and raised it in cheerful salute like a man greeting an old friend.

"Brother Vess!" he boomed, loud enough for the whole tavern. "By the Ember, it *has* been years! Come, sit, drink—let's talk about old times!"

It was magnificent. It was also, I realized as Petra shoved me through the cellar door into the dark and the stink, a man spending the last of his anonymity to buy us a head start—which meant that whatever happened next, we were leaving Ashmarket tonight, through a drain, with a drunk priest's fate hanging on how long he could hold a conversation.

Behind me, above the cellar stairs, I heard the hunter's voice, pleasant as a knife: "Old times, Brother Odo? Let's start with tonight. Who were your guests?"

And I heard Brannic laugh, easy and huge, and begin to pour.
