## 11. The Hunter's Kiss

I have been hunted before, so I want to be clear about one thing: being hunted is not the worst part. The worst part is the waiting, the long gray hours when you know they're behind you but not how far, and your mind does their work for them, painting shadows into ward-hunters and wind into footsteps. Fear is a diligent employee. It never stops working, never asks for leave, and it always, always finds something to do.

We had three days of that between Ashmarket and the mountain's root.

Brannic said we'd made good time. Brannic said a lot of things in the tone of a man who has learned that confidence is cheaper than competence and twice as convincing. He walked ahead with his stolen rites wrapped in oilcloth like a swaddled infant, muttering door-names under his breath, and Gideon walked beside me with his lantern and his terrible, transparent face, and I spent those three days listening to the country behind us the way you listen to a sleeping house for the sound of a floorboard.

On the third night, the floorboard creaked.

* * *

It happened at a place Brannic called the Hollow Ford, though there was no ford and precious little hollow—just a dry riverbed cutting through hills of black pine, the kind of landscape that eats sound and gives nothing back. We were making cold camp because Gideon insisted fires drew eyes, and I let him believe it was his idea to skip it because he needed the small victories.

He was carving. Of course he was carving. The man carved the way other people breathed; give him a scrap of bone and ten minutes of silence and he'd produce something small and perfect and haunted. That night it was a token for a dead woman whose voice he'd caught at the last crossroads—a miller, by the sound of her, complaining eternally about damp grain. He held the little bone up to the firelight-less dark, turning it, and I watched him listen to nothing, and I thought: *this is what I betrayed my order for. A man who talks to ghosts and can't lie to save his life.*

Then the pines went quiet.

Not silent—quiet. There's a difference. Silence is empty. Quiet is full, holding its breath, and every animal in that riverbed was doing it at once.

"Brannic," I said, very softly.

"I hear it." He was already moving, oilcloth bundle clutched to his chest. "Downstream side. Two, maybe three."

"Four," I said. "Maybe five." Because I knew the sound they made, or didn't make. Ward-hunters don't crunch gravel. They walk the way regret walks—everywhere at once and nowhere you can point to.

Gideon set down his knife. "How many can you handle?"

"Handle?" I almost laughed. "Gideon, sweet valley-boy, you don't *handle* ward-hunters. You survive them or you don't. Handling implies a conversation."

"We could talk to them," he said, and God help me, he meant it. "I could explain. About Wren. About why we're—"

"They're not here to discuss theology." I stood, drawing him up with me, and put my back to his, which is an old habit from a life I'll get to later, and which felt, in that moment, like coming home to a house that had burned down. "They're here to erase witnesses. That's you, me, and our charming guide. In that order, probably, because Brannic's name is on more lists than mine."

"It is not," Brannic said, wounded.

"It absolutely is."

The first hunter came out of the trees on the downstream side, exactly where I'd guessed, and I will grant them this much: they are beautiful, in the way a drawn blade is beautiful. Tall, gray-cloaked, faces masked in white porcelain worked with ward-script—the same script I used to cut into standing stones, repurposed as a blindfold. They carried hooked staves that hummed at a frequency I felt in my teeth. Ward-breakers. Oath-seekers. The order's hounds, and once, in another life, I'd have called them brothers.

"There are four," I told the others. "One circling wide to cut off the north trail. Leave them to me."

"Petra—" Gideon started.

"No." I turned just enough to look at him. His face in the starless dark, all jaw and worry. "You carry the flame-lore now. You carry the map in your head that Brannic gave you. If I fall, you go north and you do not stop, and you do not come back, and you especially do not do anything noble, because you are catastrophically bad at it."

"That's not fair."

"It's completely fair. You traded three years of your gift for a mask you're not even wearing anymore. That's not nobility, that's a shopping problem."

He almost smiled. Under the circumstances, I count that as my finest work.

Then the hunters moved, and there was no more time for wit.

* * *

Fighting ward-hunters is like fighting the memory of a fight. They don't commit. They flow. Their staves hum and wherever the hum touches you, your own protections turn traitor—wards unravel, oaths loosen, old promises you made years ago come unstitched and flutter away. I felt mine going within the first minute: the little defensive knots I keep tied around myself, the ones that say *no* to magic in all its pushy forms. Unraveling like cheap knitting.

So I did what I do best. I got dishonest.

I feinted left toward the riverbed, took a glancing hook-staff blow across the shoulder that lit every nerve I owned, rolled, came up inside the second hunter's reach—which no one ever expects, because everyone fights ward-hunters at staff distance—and put my knife through the gap beneath the porcelain mask. Not deep. Just enough. The hunter folded with a sound like a sigh, and I was already moving, because the third one's staff was singing toward Gideon.

Here is the thing about Gideon Brightwater in a crisis: he doesn't freeze, and he doesn't flee. He *positions himself between the danger and other people*, like some kind of self-propelled memorial stone. I saw him step in front of Brannic—I saw the fourth hunter, the one I'd sent to the north trail in my head, materialize out of the pines behind him—and I saw the hook of that humming staff arc down toward his spine.

I have done the arithmetic of my life many times since, lying awake in various ditches, and the answer never changes. There was time to shout. There was time to throw the knife. There was even, possibly, time to reach him. What there was not time for was all three, and my body chose before my mind finished the sum.

I reached him. I turned us both, so that my back was where his spine had been.

The staff's hook caught me below the left shoulder blade and the hum went *through* me, and I learned something interesting in that half-second: ward-breaking hurts less than you'd think at the point of contact and infinitely more everywhere else. It wasn't a wound so much as an unmaking. Something in me that had been warded since birth—I didn't even know what, some knot deeper than the ones I tie deliberately—came loose, and the pain arrived a heartbeat later like a debt collector who'd finally found the right address.

We went down together. Gideon caught me. Of course he did.

The rest happened fast and I remember it in shards: Brannic screaming a door-name at the darkness and the darkness, astonishingly, opening. Gideon hauling me upright with an arm under my ribs, apologizing—I swear to every god I don't believe in, he *apologized*—for hurting me by carrying me. The remaining hunters regrouping with that terrible patience. And then the ground itself opening along the line Brannic had named, a seam of older dark in the dark, and us falling through it into the mountain's skin, and the seam closing behind us like a mouth.

The last thing I saw above ground was the lead hunter's porcelain mask, tilted, watching. Not angry. Patient.

They always know where you're going. That's the thing nobody tells you about hunters. They don't chase. They *escort*.

* * *

The place Brannic's door-name had opened was a waystation cut into the mountain's outer root—an old keeper's post, pre-Empire, its walls still bearing oath-marks from men and women three hundred years dead. Brannic lit a single candle, which felt obscene after days of dark, and Gideon laid me down on a stone shelf that some long-gone keeper had cushioned with moss, and someone—him, it must have been him—cut my cloak away from the wound.

I want to describe the fever honestly, because everything important I said in the next two days, I said inside it, and I'd rather you have the context than think I'm simply a woman who overshares.

Ward-break wounds don't bleed the way normal wounds bleed. They leak. What comes out isn't just blood; it's *structure*. Everything I'd built myself out of—discipline, silence, the careful architecture of a girl who escaped a court that owned her name—all of it had been held in place by wards I'd woven so young I'd forgotten they were mine. The hook-staff had found the oldest one. The first one. The one I'd tied at age six, kneeling on cold marble, while a regent with a borrowed face told me I belonged to the Ember now.

And so the fever pulled the thread, and everything came out.

I surfaced once, briefly, to Gideon pressing a cup of something bitter to my lips. "Drink," he said. "Brannic says it fights infection. He also says it's made from bat lichen, so manage your expectations."

"Worse things," I managed, "have been in my mouth," and passed out again to the sound of him choking on a laugh, which I counted as a victory, because a man laughing is a man not panicking, and I needed him not panicking. One of us had to hold the shape of the world while the other one dissolved.

* * *

The second day—or maybe the third; fever flattens time like a thumb on wet clay—I surfaced properly and found him sitting beside me, whittling. He'd filled the whole shelf-edge with little carvings: birds, mostly, and one lopsided cat. My hands, I noticed distantly, were clean. Someone had washed the blood off. Someone had been very gentle about it.

"You're awake," he said, with the enormous casualness of a man who has been terrified for forty hours.

"Barely." My voice sounded like gravel arguing with sandpaper. "Report."

"Brannic's scouting the next junction. Says the deep roads fork ahead—one branch to the vault approaches, one to the Court proper. He wants to argue about which one tomorrow." He set down the knife. "Petra. The wound's bad. Brannic says ward-breaks fester if the broken ward isn't rewoven, and neither of us can weave."

"So I'm dying in a hole in a mountain. There's a symmetry there I refuse to examine."

"Petra."

"Don't say my name like that. Like it's a prayer. I'm not a prayer, I'm a tactical liability with a hole in her."

But he kept looking at me with those grave-carver's hands folded and that terrible honest face, and I was feverish, and feverish people should not be trusted near the truth. So I told it.

"You want to know why the hunters follow me specifically," I said. "Why the regent wants me back alive when any sensible tyrant would want me dead."

"You don't have to—"

"When I was six years old, the Undercourt bought me." I said it flatly, the way you read a ledger entry, because if I gave it any feeling at all it would drown me. "Not metaphorically. There was a contract. My mother signed it for a cure she couldn't afford and a winter she couldn't survive. Tithe-children, they're called. Every generation the Court takes a handful—children with the knack for ward-work, because wards are just promises made solid, and children make the best promises. We're flexible. We haven't learned yet which truths are load-bearing."

Gideon had gone very still. Carvers' stillness. The stillness of a man who listens to the dead and knows how to hold space for a hard story.

"They raised us under the mountain. There were nine of us in my cohort. We slept in alcoves off the Ember vault—not near it, understand, the Ember burns souls and children are too valuable to burn—but close enough that we grew up hearing it. Like a hearth that breathes. They taught us ward-craft the way you'd teach a lockpick to pick locks: thoroughly, lovingly, and exclusively for their benefit. I was good. I was the best of my cohort, and I know how that sounds, but it matters, because it's why they gave me a name."

"A name?"

"The Court keeps its property unnamed. Names are claims, and claims invite rivals. But a senior keeper needs a name to sign oaths with. So when I turned sixteen they *gave* me one. Petra Vane. Invented it out of court-script and old grievances. Do you understand? Even my name is a ward they built. I've been living inside their architecture my entire adult life." I laughed, and it hurt, and I did it anyway. "When I ran, I didn't escape. I defected. There's a difference. Escapes end. Defections get hunted."

"And Wren," he said quietly. "That's how you knew. How to read the ledger. Why you came to warn us."

"I helped forge the terms." I'd confessed this before, in the ward-house, but saying it again in the fever-light felt different—less like confession, more like amputation. "The soul-tithe clause. The collection schedule. I inked the ember-red myself, Gideon. Your sister's name went through my hands and I told myself it was just ink, just law, just the way things were done, because the alternative was admitting I was building the cage I lived in, one clause at a time. Seven years ago I finally couldn't stand it anymore and I ran. And the first useful thing I did with my freedom was find the valley the tithe was buying and knock on a bone-carver's door."

"Why?" he asked. "Really. Not duty. Not guilt. Why come to me?"

I looked at the ceiling of that ancient keeper's post, at the oath-marks of the dead, and I answered him, because fever had burned away everything except the answer.

"Because you carve tokens for the dead so their regrets aren't lost. And I needed—God, I needed someone in the world who believed that *anything* lost could be gotten back. I came to you because I wanted to be the kind of person you already assumed I was."

The candle guttered. Somewhere in the deep roads, water dripped with the patience of mountains.

"Gideon," I said, and my voice cracked down the middle like old ice. "I'm scared. I don't say that. I have literally never said that to anyone. The Court raised me to be a tool and tools don't get scared, they get *recalibrated*. But I'm lying here leaking my own wards into the moss and I can feel the place where they broke, this—hole, this absence, and underneath it there's a six-year-old girl on cold marble who never stopped kneeling, and I don't know how to stand up without the wards, I never learned, that wasn't part of the curriculum—"

His hand found mine. Carver's hand, scarred across the knuckles, warm as a hearthstone.

"Then I'll hold you up until you learn," he said. "That's not a bargain. No terms. No collection date. It's just true."

And I—Petra Vane, tithe-child, deserter, liar, the best ward-forger the Undercourt ever produced—wept like the six-year-old I'd been carrying for twenty years, while a man who talked to ghosts sat with me in the dark and didn't try to fix anything, because he understood, the way only listeners understand, that being heard *is* the fixing.

* * *

I woke clear-headed on what Brannic informed me was the fifth day, weak as a wet banner but fever-broken, and the first thing I saw was Gideon asleep sitting up beside my shelf, chin on chest, a half-finished bird still in his hand. On the stone beside my hip lay a new carving: a small figure of a woman, arms crossed, expression unmistakably mine, rendered with insulting accuracy.

At my feet, where the wound had leaked into the moss, something impossible had happened. The moss had grown back in the shape of a ward-knot. Not mine. Older. Wilder. As if the mountain itself had looked at the hole in me and, unprompted, offered a stitch.

"Brannic says the deep roads here remember the old keepers," Gideon said when he woke, watching my face as I touched the green-woven knot. "Says the mountain's been waiting a long time for someone worth warding."

I looked at that small wild knot of living stone holding my broken promise together, and then down the dark throat of the passage where the road forked—one way to the vault, one way to the Court—and I felt the hunters' patience out there in the dark, escorting us onward.

"Well," I said. "Then let's not keep it waiting."

But as we packed to move, I marked what neither of the men had: fresh ward-script scratched at the waystation's threshold, new-cut, porcelain-white.

Someone had already found our door.
