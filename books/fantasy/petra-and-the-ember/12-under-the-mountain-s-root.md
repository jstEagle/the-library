## 12. Under the Mountain's Root

The door Brannic knew was not a door.

That should have been my first warning. Everything about the man had a way of presenting itself as one thing while being quietly another—a priest who wasn't a priest, a guide who'd sold them out once already in small ways I hadn't caught yet, a door that was, on inspection, a crack in the world with ambitions.

We found it two days past Ashmarket, where the foothills of the mountain folded into themselves like a hand closing. There was no archway, no carved lintel, none of the things doors are supposed to have. There was only a shelf of black stone jutting from the hillside, and beneath it a gap barely wide enough for Petra to squeeze through if she exhaled her opinions first.

"This is it?" I said.

Brannic spread his hands, which were ink-stained to the second knuckle. He'd been copying rites onto his forearms since we left Ashmarket, muttering under his breath like a man rehearsing an apology he'd never deliver. "This is it. The Underdoor. Or one of nine of them. The others are watched."

"By whom?"

"By the court." He said it the way you'd say *by weather*. "And by worse."

Petra crouched at the gap and put her palm flat against the stone. I watched her face do something complicated—recognition, revulsion, homesickness all fighting for the same chair. She'd been raised down there. Tithe-child, she'd told us, in the fever-dark after the hunters' knives. A child given to the Undercourt as payment, raised among candlelight and borrowed faces until she ran. Every step toward the mountain was a step back toward the nursery that had eaten her.

"We could still go around," I said, though we both knew there was no around. The mountain's roots went deeper than its peak went high. You didn't climb over the Undercourt. You went through it or you didn't go at all.

"No," she said. "Wren's been down there seven years, Gideon. We're not taking the scenic route."

So we went in.

* * *

I want to describe the deep roads properly, because everything I'd imagined about the Undercourt—and I had imagined plenty, in the sleepless arithmetic of the last weeks—was wrong in ways that mattered.

I'd imagined darkness. What we got instead was light without any source: a faint amber glow that lived in the stone itself, as if the mountain had swallowed a sunset centuries ago and never finished digesting it. I'd imagined cold. It was warm, instead—the close, breathing warmth of a place with a pulse. And I'd imagined silence, the dead quiet of deep earth.

What we got was noise.

Not the dead. Not yet. Just the mountain itself: water moving somewhere below us in slow veins, stone settling with sighs like old men in chairs, and underneath it all a hum so low I felt it in my teeth before I heard it in my ears. Brannic said it was the Ember. He said it the way believers say holy words and con men say sales pitches, which for Brannic was the same register entirely.

"The roads run like this for three days," he told us, walking backward so he could face us while he talked, which struck me as either confidence or a death wish. "Then they fork. The pilgrim way goes up toward the Court proper. We want the root-ways—down and sideways, through the old galleries. They're not used anymore."

"Why not?" Petra asked.

"Because the things that used them stopped needing roads."

Nobody asked him to elaborate. That was the general policy with Brannic: don't ask questions whose answers might cost extra later.

He led by rite. Literally—he'd copied the passages onto strips of vellum tucked into his sleeves, and at each junction he'd consult them, trace a sign in the air with two fingers, and choose a direction with the gravity of a man defusing something. Once, when I asked what the sign did, he said, "Tells the road we're expected," and then wouldn't meet my eyes for an hour.

I've spent my whole life listening to the dead, and I'll tell you what nobody believes about that work: it's mostly patience. The dead talk slowly. They repeat themselves. They hold grudges the size of a spilled cup of ale and griefs the size of a lifetime, and they narrate both at the speed of dripping wax. You learn to wait inside their voices the way you'd wait out rain.

The deep roads tested every year of that patience in the first mile.

Because the dead here were different.

* * *

They started as pressure. That's how I'd explain it to someone who couldn't hear them—a change in the weight of the air, the feeling of standing in a room just after an argument has emptied it. By the second hour it was more than pressure. By the third, it was voices, and I stopped in the middle of the road so hard that Petra walked into my back.

"Gideon?"

"They're loud," I said.

"Dead people are usually quiet," Brannic offered helpfully. "That's sort of their brand."

"Not these." I pressed my palms against my ears, which has never once worked and didn't now. "Up in Emberfall, the dead are... small. Small regrets. Where they left the good scissors. Words they meant to say. Here—" I tried to find the shape of what I was hearing and couldn't, not cleanly. "Here they're enormous. Old. Angry in a way that doesn't have a person attached anymore. Like the anger outlived whoever was angry and kept going on its own."

Petra had gone very still. "How old?"

"I don't know. Older than the valley. Older than wards. Some of these voices were old when the founders made their bargain." I looked at her. "Petra, some of them remember the mountain being empty."

She swallowed. "The Court came from somewhere, Gideon. Nobody likes to say where."

"And the dead who were here first?"

"Got moved," she said quietly. "Or got kept."

That was the phrase that lodged in me like a splinter: *got kept.* Because I knew about keeping. I'd grown up in a valley that existed to keep things out, built on a bargain that kept one soul in reserve per generation, and I'd spent seven years failing to keep track of my sister's voice. Keeping was the family business of everyone I'd ever trusted.

We walked on. The voices grew.

* * *

I'll be honest about what happened next, because Astrid Oyelaran never taught me to flinch from it, and neither will I: the dead of the deep roads frightened me in a way the dead of Emberfall never had.

In the valley, hearing the dead was a chore and a comfort in roughly equal measure. Mrs. Halloway wanting her grave-token recarved because the lettering had weathered. A farmer's grandfather complaining about drainage. Children, sometimes, asking after parents in voices like blown-out candles. My gift was a village well—shallow, familiar, drawn from daily.

Down there, the well went down forever, and what came up had teeth.

They didn't speak in words, most of them. They spoke in *insistence*. A pressure behind my eyes like a headache learning language. Fragments surfaced: *the first door was ours. they took the warm dark. we were the mountain's marrow before they lit their little fire.* And threaded through all of it, a single recurring demand, repeated by voice after voice after voice until I could have carved it in my sleep:

*listen. listen. LISTEN.*

No one had listened to them in a very long time. I understood that the way you understand a wound is infected—not from evidence but from smell.

On the second day underground, one of them got through clearly enough to use my name.

It shouldn't have been possible. They didn't know my name. But the dead trade in whatever's loose, and my name had been rattling around inside my own skull for thirty years like a coin in a jar. A voice older than the valley's founding said *Gideon Brightwater, bone-carver,* with the satisfaction of a merchant confirming a delivery, and I sat down on the road right there between one step and the next.

Petra was beside me instantly. "What is it? What did you hear?"

"It knows me."

"Nothing down here should know you."

"Well," I said, "it does. And it wants something." I rubbed my face with both hands. "It wants me to listen. Properly. Not the way I listen up top—half an ear, polite, keeping the volume turned down. It wants—" I struggled. "You know how a lock wants a key? Not desperately. Just... structurally. Like listening to them is what I'm *for*, and everything else I've ever done with the gift has been using a chisel to stir soup."

Brannic had stopped consulting his sleeve-rites and was watching me with an expression I didn't care for. It was the expression of a man watching an asset appreciate.

"The old rites," he said slowly, "the ones the Court suppressed when it took the mountain—they were built around listeners. Bone-carvers, grave-speakers, the ones who could hear the first dead. The Court doesn't have one anymore. Hasn't since—" He glanced at Petra and had the grace to look embarrassed. "Since a certain tithe-child burned her oath-mark and walked out with the regent's favorite voice in her head."

"That was different," Petra snapped.

"It was theft," Brannic agreed pleasantly. "Very effective theft. The Court's been deaf ever since. Deaf courts get paranoid. Paranoid courts get cruel. You've seen the candlelit horror firsthand; you tell me I'm wrong."

"You're not wrong," she said bitterly. "You're never wrong in a way that helps."

But I'd stopped listening to both of them, because the dead had surged closer—not physically, nothing so crude, but the way a crowd leans in when a rumor starts—and the oldest voice, the one that remembered the mountain empty, was saying something new. Not *listen* this time.

*Carve,* it said. *Little carver. Carve us a door.*

And beneath the words, so faint I almost missed it, almost mistook it for the mountain's hum: a second voice. Thin. Frayed. Familiar in the way your own reflection in a dark window is familiar—you know it, and it frightens you anyway.

A girl's voice. Screaming, still, after seven years. Sold but not yet consumed.

Wren.

* * *

I didn't tell them right away. I want that on the record, since everyone in this story keeps confessing things out of order. I heard my sister's soul calling from somewhere in the roots of the mountain, and I stood up, and I said nothing, and we walked another half-mile before Petra looked at my face and said, "Gideon. What happened back there?"

Because she'd learned my silences. Seven days on the road together and she could read them like ward-script. There's a betrayal in that, and a mercy, and I've never fully decided which one it is.

"She's close," I said. "Wren. The fragment I heard through the cradle-bone—it's stronger here. Closer. And the old dead..." I hesitated. Telling the truth had gotten expensive lately; every truth I'd spoken since the stars went out had cost somebody something. "The old dead want to help me. They want a door carved. And I think—I think the reason they want *me* is that my gift isn't a village trick. Down here it's a key. Maybe *the* key. The Court lost its listener when Petra ran. The old dead have been locked out of their own mountain for longer than anyone remembers. And I'm standing in their hallway holding the only key that fits."

Brannic made a low whistle. "That's either wonderful or catastrophic."

"In my experience," Petra said, "with Gideon it's both, sequentially."

"Thank you."

"Don't mention it."

But she was watching me with an expression I hadn't earned yet and would spend the rest of this story trying to deserve—the look of someone deciding to trust a plan that frightens them, because the person making it frightens them less than the alternative.

"So we carve them a door," she said. "And what do they give us?"

"A way in that the Court doesn't watch. A road to the vaults that isn't on Brannic's stolen maps, because it predates the maps, the Court, and possibly stone itself." I flexed my hands. They ached. Carver's ache, but deeper, like the bones remembered something. "And maybe—maybe—an army. Everyone the Court ever silenced, still down here, still waiting for someone to listen."

"That's not an army," Brannic murmured. "That's a debt collection."

"Wren's flame is in the Ember vault," I said. "Behind every lock the Court owns. We can't pick those locks. We can't buy them, bribe them, or burn them—not the three of us, not with ward-hunters behind us and a regent ahead who collects souls the way my mother collected teacups." I looked at the amber glow living in the walls, at the dark road bending down into the mountain's oldest dark, and felt the dread in my chest do something I didn't expect.

It turned over. It changed shape. It became, of all things, purpose.

"But the dead can open doors," I said. "If someone finally listens long enough to ask them properly."

* * *

We camped that night in a side gallery Brannic swore was safe, which in Brannic's vocabulary meant *safe-ish, probably, statistically.* Petra took first watch. I lay on bare stone with my pack for a pillow and let myself listen—really listen, the way the old dead wanted, dropping the volume-dampening habit of a lifetime like shrugging off a coat.

It was like opening a door onto a storm.

Thousands of voices. Tens of thousands. The kept dead, the moved dead, the dead who remembered the warm dark before the Ember was lit. They didn't stop talking when they noticed me paying attention. They got louder, the way lonely things do, and I lay there in the roar of them and picked through it for hours, hunting one thread.

Near midnight—or near what I decided was midnight, since time underground is a rumor—I found her again.

*Gideon.* Faint as a lamp through fog. *Gideon, is that you? Don't come. It's a trap, the whole thing, the ledger, the terms, they wrote me into the walls—*

"Wren," I whispered, and my eyes were wet, and I didn't care. "I'm coming. Do you hear me? I'm coming, and this time I'm not leaving without you."

*You never leave without me,* her voice said, frayed and furious and achingly hers. *That's the whole problem.*

And then, beneath her, beneath everything, the oldest voice in the mountain spoke one final time before I slept—patient as stone, hungry as spring:

*Tomorrow, little carver. Bring your knife. We have waited so long for a door.*

Above us, through a mile of rock, the Court's candles were burning. Below us, the first dead were waking.

I closed my eyes, and for the first time in my life, the silence I feared was the silence of everyone finally talking at once.
