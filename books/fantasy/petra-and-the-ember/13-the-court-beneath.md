## 13. The Court Beneath

I had been raised in the Undercourt for eleven years, and I had spent every one of them learning how to look at things without seeing them.

It's a skill, like any other. You start small—a servant's bruised wrist, a tithe-child's hollowed eyes—and you practice until you can walk past anything with your chin level and your hands folded. By the time I escaped, I could look at a noble wearing a dead man's face and think only *lovely weather we're having*. I thought I'd never lose that skill. I thought it was welded into me like my oath-mark.

Then Gideon Brightwater walked into the Court Beneath beside me, looked at the first borrowed face he saw, and said—out loud, in a carrying whisper—"That woman's face is wrong. It doesn't fit her skull."

And every candle in the hall flickered toward him like a thousand eyes opening.

* * *

Let me back up, because the descent deserves its own accounting, and because if I don't narrate it now I'll spend the rest of my life pretending it was shorter than it was.

The deep roads took us three days. Brannic led us through them with his stolen rites—chalk marks on stone, salt poured in lines that the dark respected, little prayers in a dialect so old the words had more teeth than meaning. He'd been an underpriest once, before whatever disgrace unmade him, and watching him work was like watching a man handle snakes he used to keep as pets: competent, reverent, and quietly furious about it.

Gideon changed down there. I don't mean his mood. I mean something in the architecture of him shifted, the way a building settles when the ground beneath it remembers it's alive. The dead of Emberfall had murmured to him all his life—small regrets, small griefs, the domestic static of the recently departed. The dead under the mountain were not that. The dead under the mountain were a tide.

He told me about it on the second day, when we stopped to sleep in a niche that Brannic swore was safe, which is to say merely haunted rather than hungry.

"It's not voices anymore," he said. His hands were shaking, and he kept them busy with his carving knife and a scrap of bone, working without looking at it. "It's weather. Pressure. Like standing in a river and trying to hear one conversation."

"Can you?" I asked. "Hear one?"

"I can hear all of them." He turned the bone over. "They know I'm here. They've been waiting for someone who listens."

I should have said something wise then. I was, after all, the one with the plan, the maps in my head, the eleven years of court-craft. Instead I said, "Try not to make friends with anything that wants to eat us," and rolled over to sleep, and hated myself for it in the dark, quietly, the way I do most things.

The third day, the road began to change. The stone went from grey to black to something that wasn't quite either, and warmth rose up through our boots—not fire-warmth, but body-warmth, the heat of a thing vast and sleeping. And then the road opened, and there it was.

The Court Beneath.

You cannot describe it honestly without sounding mad, so I'll describe it dishonestly, the way the court would want: it was beautiful. Candles beyond counting, thousands upon thousands, floating or fixed or carried by servants whose faces I refused to examine. A hall carved from the mountain's living root, vaulted so high the ceiling vanished into darkness that was less absence than presence—darkness that was *watching*. Music played somewhere, though I never saw musicians. Tables of black glass held food that steamed but never cooled. And everywhere, everywhere, the nobility of the Undercourt, moving through their eternal evening in silks the color of bruises and embers.

And every face on them was borrowed.

That's the horror I trained myself not to see, and Gideon saw it in four seconds flat. The nobles wear faces taken from the dead—or from the living, in the worst cases, in the cases the regent found interesting. A face is a trophy here. A face is currency. A face is a joke you tell at parties for a century. The woman nearest the door wore the features of a farmwife dead two hundred years, stretched slightly wrong across a skull built for something else, smiling with teeth that didn't match the smile.

"That woman's face is wrong," Gideon said. "It doesn't fit her skull."

The candles flickered. The music stumbled—one note, dropped like a glass.

Brannic made a sound like a man swallowing his own tongue. "Say nothing else," he breathed. "Nothing. They'll take that too, boy. They'll take your face off your head and wear it to dinner."

Gideon, to his credit, closed his mouth. To his discredit, he did it slowly, with the expression of a man filing something away for later, and I realized with a lurch that I was following a bone-carver from a valley that magic forgot into the seat of everything magic, and that his weapon was not a sword but an unbearable habit of noticing things.

We were expected. Of course we were expected. The Undercourt has never once in its long rotten history been surprised by anything; surprise requires not knowing, and knowing is the only thing they farm down here.

A herald stood at the foot of the dais steps—a tall figure in grey whose face was smooth as an egg, featureless, waiting to be filled. When it spoke, its voice came from somewhere behind its own throat.

"The ward-keeper Petra Vane," it announced, "returned. And guests."

*Returned.* As if I'd stepped out for air. As if eleven years of servitude and six of running had been a stroll around the garden.

I bowed. That's the part I need you to understand, the part that still sits in my chest like swallowed stone: I bowed. Not because I wanted to. Because the body I escaped with remembered what the mind had tried to forget, and my spine bent on its own, smooth and practiced, eleven years of choreography firing in sequence. Knee, waist, neck. Eyes down. Hands open.

Old chains don't rust, down here. They just wait for you to come home.

* * *

The regent received us in the Hall of Small Hours, which is what the court calls the space between midnight and the hour that doesn't exist—the hour they keep down here the way surface folk keep saints' days. I remembered it from childhood: the long table of black glass, the candles arranged in constellations no sky ever made, the regent at the far end, small and enormous at once.

She looks like nothing. That's the trick of her. The regent wears no borrowed face—she wears a plain one, middle-aged, forgettable, the kind of face you'd see across a market stall and never think of again. She dresses in undyed wool. She might be somebody's aunt. She might be anybody's aunt. That is precisely why everything with a heartbeat in this hall fears her more than it fears the dark itself, because the dark at least announces itself.

"Petra." My name in her mouth sounded like a coin being bitten. "You've brought guests to my table. How generous. I remember when you couldn't give anything away."

"Regent." My voice came out level. I'll be proud of that until I die. "I've brought you a problem, actually. Your ledger has an error."

Murmurs along the table. The nobles leaned in like plants toward light. One of them—a lord wearing the face of a young man drowned in some river I'd bet money I could name—actually smiled at me, delighted, the way people are delighted by executions.

"An error," the regent repeated. She turned a candle idly, and its flame bent toward her finger like a dog leaning into a hand. "The Ledger of Souls does not err. It is the only honest thing in creation. Everything written in ember-red comes due exactly as written. You helped write it, if memory serves."

"I helped forge the terms," I said. "Which is different. Forging means making something false look true. I was good at it. I was fourteen." I heard Gideon shift beside me—heard, because I'd asked him before we entered to let me speak, and to my lasting astonishment he was letting me. "Wren Brightwater's name is in your book. Her soul was sold by the founders of Emberfall to buy wards against this court. But the terms were forged, Regent. The collection clause was mine. And forged terms are void terms. Even here. Even for you."

Silence. The particular silence of a room full of immortals recalculating.

The regent studied me for a long moment with those forgettable eyes, and I felt eleven years old again, standing in this same hall, holding the oath-mark fresh on my arm, being told that my name belonged to the court now and that belonging was a gift.

"You always were clever," she said at last, softly. "It's why we chose you. Clever children make excellent tools. Dull ones break." She gestured, gracious as a hostess refilling wine. "But you mistake the situation, Petra Vane. You believe you have come to negotiate. Look around you. What do you imagine you are negotiating *with*?"

And that's when I saw Wren's face.

Not Wren. Never Wren—I have to hold on to that, I will hold on to that—but a noble three seats down the table, a slender figure in silver-grey, turned toward me wearing Wren Brightwater's face the way you'd wear a coat you found. Her cheekbones. Her crooked left eyebrow. The small scar on her chin she got falling out of the pear tree at nine years old, the scar Gideon had described to me on the road with his voice cracking on the word *pear tree*, and there it was, rendered perfectly in someone else's skin, smiling at me with Wren's mouth while something ancient and amused looked out through Wren's eyes.

Gideon made a sound. A small one. A sound like a harp string snapping.

I put my hand on his arm and gripped hard enough to leave marks, and I felt him trembling, and I felt him choosing—visibly, effortfully, the way a man chooses not to scream—not to lunge across the table at a creature wearing his sister.

"There she is," the regent said gently, watching us both. "There's the debt, walking on its own legs. You see? No error at all. The valley sold us a soul. We kept it. We are keeping it still. And when the stars return—as return they shall, all things here circle—we will collect what remains of her, and light the Ember with it, and the wards of Emberfall will fall forever, and your clever forgery, Petra, will be a footnote in a book that does not err."

"The flame isn't lit yet," Gideon said.

Every head turned. The music stopped entirely this time.

He stepped forward, past my grip, past sense, into the open floor of the Hall of Small Hours, and he looked down that long black table at the regent of the Undercourt the way he looked at badly carved grave-tokens: with pity, and with the certainty that he could do better.

"Wren's soul is still whole," he said. "Still hers. You haven't burned it yet, because burning it ends the bargain, and ending the bargain ends your claim on the valley. So she's not fuel. She's collateral. Which means"—and here he smiled, and it was Wren's smile, the family smile, the one I'd only seen described, suddenly alive in front of me—"which means this is a negotiation after all. You just don't like the terms yet."

The candles guttered. Somewhere in the dark above us, something vast turned over in its sleep.

The regent regarded Gideon Brightwater for a long, long moment. Then she laughed—a real laugh, warm and awful, the laugh of somebody's aunt—and clapped her hands once.

"Oh, I have missed the surface," she said. "You bring such *interesting* things down with you." She rose, plain wool and forgettable face, and the entire court rose with her. "Very well, bone-carver. Negotiate. You have until the Hour That Isn't. After that, the ledger closes, the flame lights, and your sister becomes a star that never was."

She swept toward the doors, then paused, and looked back at me, and her voice dropped into something almost kind, which from her was the cruelest register she owned.

"And Petra. Do enjoy the hospitality. Your old rooms are ready. I kept them exactly as you left them."

The doors closed behind her. The court sat back down. The music resumed, mid-phrase, as if it had never stopped, and the noble wearing Wren's face raised a glass to me across the table and drank with my friend's sister's throat.

Gideon's hand found mine under the black glass. His fingers were ice.

"The Hour That Isn't," he whispered. "How long do we have?"

"Nobody knows," I said. "That's rather the point of it."

Above us, in the watching dark, the first candle of the new constellation lit itself—and I swear to you, on the oath-mark I was about to burn, that it flared the exact color of Wren Brightwater's soul.
