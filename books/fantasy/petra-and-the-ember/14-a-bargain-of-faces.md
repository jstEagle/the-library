## 14. A Bargain of Faces

The mask-maker's shop sat in a crooked lane where the candles burned without wicks and the wax pooled upward instead of down. I want you to understand that by this point I had stopped being surprised by anything under the mountain. Wax flowing toward the ceiling? Fine. A shopkeeper whose shadow bowed to customers before she did? Of course. I had eaten soup that whispered. My standards for wonder had collapsed entirely.

The mask-maker was a thin woman with silver thread stitched through her lips—not through her skin, thank whatever gods still bothered with this place, but through the lips of every mask hanging on the walls behind her, dozens of them, all facing outward like an audience waiting for a play to begin.

"You're the bone-carver," she said. Her voice had a doubled quality, as if someone stood just behind her throat repeating her words a half-second late.

"I'm told that's my qualification."

"It's your *liability*." She gestured to a stool. "Sit. The regent's steward sent word you'd come. You want passage into the Ember vault."

Petra shifted beside me. She'd been quiet since we left the court proper—quiet in the way a drawn bow is quiet—and now she said, "We want to *see* the vault. The regent promised access to the archives. The vault is—"

"The vault is what everyone wants and no one sees," the mask-maker said pleasantly. "Unless they pay the toll of faces." She turned to me. "You've heard the dead since you were a child. Seven-and-twenty years of voices, yes? Every grave-token you ever carved, every murmur, every confession, every small regret whispered into your hands. That is worth more than gold here. Gold we print ourselves."

I kept my face still. Under the mountain, I'd learned, stillness was currency too.

"What are you offering?" I asked.

"A courtier's mask. Woven from your own bone—I'll need a rib; you won't miss it—with your hearing braided into its weave. Wear it, and the nobles will read you as one of them. Doors open. Guards look through you. And in exchange—" she spread her hands, "—three years. Three years of silence. No voices. None at all. Starting the moment I finish the stitching."

Three years. I did the arithmetic the way you do when you're trying not to feel anything: three years out of a lifetime. I'd been listening since before I could write. Silence wasn't emptiness to me. It was a room I'd never entered, and I had no idea what was kept inside it.

"Gideon." Petra's hand found my sleeve. "Don't. There has to be another way. Brannic knows the old servant passages—he said—"

"Brannic also sold us," I said quietly. "His knowledge comes with a price tag attached to everything, including his loyalty. This woman's price is at least honest."

"Honest," Petra repeated, and something flickered across her face—the tollhouse memory, the river, the memory she'd paid on my behalf without asking. We'd fought about it for two days. We'd made something like peace about it last night, sitting back to back in a rented room while ward-hunters patrolled the streets outside, and the peace had gone like this: *I would do it again,* she'd said, and *I know,* I'd answered, and that was the whole treaty.

"Three years," I said to the mask-maker. "And then it returns? Fully?"

"Fully. Though most who trade it find they've grown fond of the quiet." Her doubled voice softened, oddly. "Some find they can't go back. The dead sound different after you've lived without them. Smaller. You may find you stop needing them."

"They don't need me to need them," I said. "That's rather the point of the work."

Something moved behind her eyes—approval, maybe, or grief wearing approval's coat. She took out a knife of black glass and set it on the table between us alongside a spool of silver thread and a mask blank, pale and unfinished, carved from some bone I didn't recognize and didn't ask about.

"Take off your shirt," she said. "And think of someone. The weave takes better if the thread has somewhere to anchor. Most people think of their mothers. Some think of enemies. The clever ones think of no one, and those masks always crack within a month."

I thought about lying. About thinking of Mother, or of nothing.

Instead I thought of Wren.

Not dead-Wren, not the screaming fragment I'd heard through the cradle-bone token, but Wren at fourteen, standing in the shallows of the Millbrook with her skirts knotted up around her knees, daring me to catch a fish with my bare hands, laughing so hard at my failure that she fell in herself and came up soaked and triumphant anyway. That laugh. That specific laugh, which I had spent seven years trying to hear again through forty layers of grave-dirt and other people's dying regrets.

"Good," the mask-maker murmured. "Hold it there."

The knife went in under my ribs and I learned something interesting about black glass: it doesn't hurt going in. It hurts coming *out*, because it takes a little of whatever it touches with it, polishing as it cuts. When she lifted the sliver of rib free, it gleamed like a chip of moonlight, and for one vertiginous moment the whole shop went silent—not the ordinary silence of rooms, but *my* silence, the silence of a man who had never once in his life been alone inside his own skull—

—and then the voices rushed back, all of them, the whole drowned choir, and I gripped the edge of the table and breathed until the world stopped tilting.

"That was a taste," the mask-maker said, not unkindly. "So you'd know what you're buying. Now hold still. This part takes a while, and it tickles."

She began to stitch. Silver thread through the mask-blank, through the sliver of my rib, and—this was the part I felt, a cold tugging deep behind my sternum—through something in me. With every pass of the needle I felt a voice go quiet. Not muffled. *Removed.* An old man who'd spent two hundred years apologizing to his wife—gone. A child humming a lullaby in a dialect nobody spoke anymore—gone. They didn't fade. They were unpicked, stitch by stitch, and wound into the mask like thread onto a bobbin.

Petra watched with her arms crossed tight, jaw set, and I knew she was cataloguing every voice that left, filing them away to be angry about later. She grieved loudly on other people's behalf. It was one of the things I'd decided not to hold against her.

"How does it work?" she demanded finally. "The disguise."

"The mask reads intention," the mask-maker said, needle flashing. "Anyone who looks at him will see what he expects them to see—a minor courtier, unremarkable, entitled to be wherever he stands looking bored. But the weave is anchored in his wanting." Her needle paused. "It will take him exactly where he wants to go. Once. Masks like this don't steer twice. So he should want very carefully."

*Once.* One shot at the vault. I looked at Petra, and she looked back at me, and we had the conversation we'd gotten good at having without words: *you could refuse, I could carry this alone, neither of us will.*

"Finish it," I said.

* * *

The mask went on like cold water.

I felt it settle over my face—weightless, seamless, drinking my features the way sand drinks rain—and then I caught sight of myself in the mask-maker's tall mirror and had to press a hand flat against the wall. Not because the face was wrong. Because it was *right*, and it wasn't mine. A courtier looked back at me: handsome in a forgettable way, mouth already curved in mild disdain, eyes holding the particular boredom of someone who has seen everything the court can offer and found it adequate.

"Well," Petra said faintly. "You look insufferable."

"That's the idea. Insufferable people get left alone."

"And the rest of it? Do you feel different?"

I listened. That was the thing—I couldn't help listening, it was breathing to me—and the room was silent. Not empty-silent. *Vacant*-silent. Like walking through a house where everyone had stepped out and left the doors open. For twenty-seven years I had never once been the only person in my own head, and now I was, and the silence didn't feel like relief. It felt like a held breath.

"It's very quiet," I said.

"Do you regret it yet?"

"Ask me in the vault."

* * *

The Ember vault lay beneath the Court Beneath, which lay beneath everything else, and getting to it required crossing the Long Gallery—an avenue of candlelit marble where the nobles promenaded at all hours, because sleep, I gathered, was considered vulgar down here.

Walking the Gallery in the mask was the strangest hour of my life, and I include the day the stars went out.

They let me pass. That was the horror of it—they *let me pass*, nodded even, one ancient noblewoman in a gown of moth-wing grey inclining her head as though we were acquainted, and I inclined mine back and smiled my borrowed smile and understood suddenly how easy it must have been, all these centuries, for the Undercourt to take what it took. Not through force. Through *belonging*. You simply wore the right face and the doors opened and no one asked what was underneath, because asking was gauche.

Petra walked ten paces behind me, hooded, playing a servant, and I could feel her anger radiating off her like heat off a forge. Every noble we passed, she flinched slightly. Somewhere in this crowd was the regent who'd owned her name, and I watched her keep her chin level through sheer stubborn muscle.

The gallery ended at a door of black iron worked with a tree in flame. Two guards in ember-lit livery stood beside it, and as I approached, the mask pulled gently at my attention, steering, and the door—

The door opened before I reached it.

Not the guards. The mask itself, humming against my skin, the silver weave warming, my stolen hearing resonating in the lock like a key finding its own song. The guards looked straight through me, exactly as advertised, and I walked through the doorway with my heart hammering so hard I was sure the whole Gallery could hear it.

Inside was stairs. Down, and down, spiraling into warmth, the air growing thick and amber-smelling, and at the bottom—

At the bottom was the Ember.

I'd expected fire. What I found was a garden.

A cavern the size of a cathedral, ceiling lost in darkness, and rising from its floor thousands upon thousands of small flames, each burning in its own niche of black stone, row upon row upon row, receding farther than light should carry. Each flame burned a different color. Each flame burned *differently*—some steady, some guttering, some flickering in rhythms almost like speech.

Souls. The souls of the valley. Seven generations of them, bought and banked and shelved, burning patiently in the dark while the valley above forgot they existed.

I stood at the entrance with my borrowed face and my hollowed-out ears and felt the silence press against me like deep water. All those lives. All those voices. And for the first time in my life, I couldn't hear a single one of them. They were right there. Thousands of them, inches away, and I was deaf to every flame.

Except.

Except one.

I don't know how to explain it except that the mask was woven from my rib and anchored in my wanting, and my wanting had a shape I'd carried for seven years, and the mask *knew*. It warmed against my cheek and turned my head, gently, the way you turn a child's face toward something wonderful, and down the endless rows, past flame after flame after flame—

There.

A lamp-flame, small, in a niche at chest height, burning a color I knew in my bones before my eyes finished confirming it. Not orange. Not gold. *Ember-red*, the exact red of the ink in the ledger, the exact red of the ribbon she'd tied in her hair the summer she died, the exact red I'd been mixing into every carving since, without ever once knowing why.

"Wren," I said. Out loud. To a flame.

It flickered. Just once. In a rhythm I recognized, because I'd heard it a thousand times across a kitchen table: the quick double-flicker she did when she was trying not to laugh.

Seven years. Seven years of searching graves and tokens and silences, and she'd been here, thirty feet tall in flame-form and six inches wide, burning in the dark while our mother set her chair and I carved her name into other people's memorials.

I pressed my palm flat against the stone beside her niche. The stone was warm. Everything here was warm—it was the warmth of the valley, I realized, the inexplicable coziness of Emberfall, the reason crops grew kindly and winters forgave us—we were being heated, gently, by the banked souls of our own dead. Our refuge was a hearth and we were the wood.

"I'm getting you out," I whispered. "I don't know how yet. But I'm getting you out."

The flame leaned toward my hand. Just barely. A flower toward sun.

And then, behind me, soft as a closing book, a voice said:

"She always leans like that. Toward anyone who promises."

I turned. Standing between the rows of flames was the regent, unannounced, unhurried, dressed in a simplicity so expensive it circled back around to ostentation. The mask hummed against my face, trying to make me boring, trying to make me furniture.

The regent smiled at the mask, and then—worse—smiled *past* it, directly at me, at whatever was underneath.

"Bone-carver," the regent said. "Did you honestly believe we wouldn't recognize our own thread?"
