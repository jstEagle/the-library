## 15. The Regent's Offer

### Gideon

The regent received us in a room that had no right to exist.

I don't mean that in the ordinary way courtiers mean it—oh, the ceilings were high, the candles numbered in their thousands, the floor a single slab of black glass that showed you your own reflection walking on air. I mean it in the way a bone-carver means things: structurally. The chamber was carved from femurs. Femurs the size of cathedral pillars, polished to a warm ivory shine, arranged in a ring so vast I couldn't see where the walls began and the architecture gave up pretending. Whoever had built this place had built it out of somebody.

"You're staring," Petra murmured at my elbow.

"I'm appraising," I said. "It's what I do."

"Then appraise quietly."

The regent sat at the far end of the bone hall on a throne that was, mercifully, plain wood—the one honest object in the room, or possibly the most dishonest, because everything else was trying so hard to be beautiful that plainness could only be a disguise. The regent itself—I never did learn whether it preferred he, she, or neither—wore a face like a half-finished carving. Not blank. Worse than blank. It had features, but they kept *suggesting* themselves: a jaw that might have been a man's, eyes that flickered between grey and brown as though the face behind them hadn't decided who it was today.

When we'd entered, it had been wearing Wren's face.

It had worn it for exactly four steps of our walk across the black glass—long enough for my heart to stop, long enough for Petra's hand to close hard around my wrist—and then it had set my sister's features aside like a mask hung back on its peg, and I understood that this was the greeting. This was the court saying: *we know what you came for, little carver, and we keep it within arm's reach.*

Now the regent folded its not-quite-hands and said, in a voice assembled from several voices, none of them quite its own: "Gideon Brightwater. Bone-carver of Emberfall. The man who hears the dead and answers them."

"That's me," I said. "Though mostly they hear me grumbling about grain direction."

Petra made a small sound that in any other person would have been a laugh and in her was a warning.

"The dead are noisy here," the regent said. "You've noticed."

I had. Even through the mask I still wore—a courtier's face, traded for three years of my hearing, a bargain I'd made in a side chamber with a broker whose smile had too many teeth in reserve—the dead pressed against me like weather. Not voices anymore; the mask had taken those. But pressure. A tide against a door. Thousands of souls under the mountain, all speaking at once, and me standing deaf in the middle of the flood, feeling every word as weight.

"They're louder than anywhere I've known," I said carefully.

"Because nowhere else do they wait so long." The regent rose from its wooden throne and descended the three steps, and its reflection on the black glass rose to meet it, and for a moment there were two regents, and I honestly couldn't tell you which one was which. "Emberfall sends us a soul each generation. One soul, gently given—or chosen, the distinction matters less than you'd hope—and in exchange, your valley sleeps safe behind its wards. Your founders called it protection. We called it rent." It stopped an arm's length away. Up close, its borrowed face smelled faintly of candle smoke. "And for seven generations, the rent has been paid, and the wards have held, and no one in your pretty valley asked what happened to the soul at the bottom of the ledger."

"Wren didn't volunteer," I said. "My mother told me she did. But the ledger says otherwise. The ink says otherwise."

"The ink is honest," the regent agreed. "Your mother was kind. Kindness and honesty are rarely carved from the same piece." It tilted its head, and the half-finished features shifted, and for one horrible heartbeat I saw my own jawline looking back at me. "But you didn't come all this way to litigate the past. You came for the flame."

"I came for my sister."

"A distinction without a difference, to you." The regent turned, robes whispering, and gestured toward the far end of the hall, where a pair of doors stood open onto a corridor of red light. "So let us speak plainly, carver, because I am told you are a plain man, and I confess I find that restful. My court has a problem. Seventeen years ago, a tithe-child fled these halls. She took with her a voice the court had spent her whole childhood training—a voice that could speak to the dead *for* the dead, translate their grievances, settle their disputes, keep ten thousand souls from tearing each other apart in the dark. Do you know what happens underground when no one listens?"

I thought of the tide against the door. The pressure. The anger, old and patient and rising.

"They stop waiting their turn," I said quietly.

"They stop waiting their turn," the regent repeated, with something that might have been satisfaction, if satisfaction could be worn by a face that belonged to no one. "We have brokers. We have masks. We have priests of the deep roads chanting the old rites into the dark. But none of them *listen*. Listening cannot be taught to someone who wasn't born with the ear. And you—" it turned back to me, and now the face was Wren's again, just for a moment, just long enough, "—you were born with the ear, Gideon Brightwater. Seven years in your valley, hearing the small regrets of the small dead, and you answered every one. We know. We watched. We always watch."

Petra's hand found my wrist again. "Whatever it's offering—"

"It hasn't offered anything yet," the regent said mildly. "That's the trouble with deserters. They assume every conversation is a trap." Its gaze slid to her, and something passed across the unfinished face that I couldn't read—old recognition, old ownership, the look of a man checking a fence he once built. "Very well. Plainly, then. Here is my offer."

The candles dimmed. All of them, thousands, at once, as though the room itself had leaned in to listen.

"Gideon Brightwater. Stay. Take the tithe-child's empty place—not as a prisoner, not as a tithe, but as our carver. Our listener. Court position, full honors, workshops of your own, bone beyond your imagining to work. In exchange, Wren Brightwater's flame walks free. Tonight. Unconsumed, unspent, released to the sky above your valley with our blessing written after it." The regent spread its borrowed hands. "Her freedom for your service. Her star for your silence. That is the shape of it."

The hall went very quiet. Somewhere in the bone pillars, I swear I heard the dead hold their breath.

"And if I refuse?" I asked.

The regent smiled with Wren's mouth, and it was the cruelest thing I had ever seen done with a kind face.

"Then the flame stays where it is. And flames, carver, are fuel. The Ember eats eventually. It has eaten slower souls than hers—but it does eat." It clasped its hands. "There is no third door. There is only the offer, and the hour, and the dark getting hungry."

* * *

They gave us a guest cell while we deliberated, which told you everything about how the court viewed deliberation: as a formality, a ritual, a bow performed before the inevitable yes. The cell was comfortable. That was the trap inside the trap. Warm stone, soft furs, a window of thick glass looking out onto a cavern where soul-lamps drifted like slow golden fish. They wanted me rested when I signed.

Petra paced. She'd been pacing since the doors closed, five circuits, six, her boots wearing a groove into stone that had probably hosted a thousand identical grooves.

"Say it," I said.

"You know what I'm going to say."

"Say it anyway. I like the sound of your voice better than mine right now."

She stopped. Turned. In the lamp-light her keeper's oath-mark—the brand on her forearm she'd burned half to ruin opening the forbidden way—looked like a wound that had decided to become a map. "You can't take it."

"Why not? It's a good bargain. Better than good. One life spent listening—that's what I've done my whole existence anyway, Petra. I sat in a valley carving grave-tokens and answering the dead because nobody else would. At least down here the dead are interesting."

"Don't." Her voice cracked on it. "Don't make it small. Don't stand there making jokes while you sign yourself into the same cage I crawled out of."

"It's not the same cage."

"It's *exactly* the same cage. Different bars, same lock. They'll be kind for a year. Two. They'll give you workshops and honors and bones to carve until your hands forget any other shape. And then one day you'll ask for something—for a kindness, for a concession, for Wren's name struck from some other ledger somewhere—and you'll find out what 'full honors' meant. It meant you were valuable. Valuable isn't free, Gideon. Valuable is owned."

I looked at my hands. Carver's hands, scarred, steady. Hands that had held a lantern with my sister's soul inside it and felt it warm against my ribs like a second heart.

"If I refuse, she burns."

"If you accept, you burn slower, and the court gets a carver forever, and nothing changes. The next valley pays its rent. The next tithe-child is raised in a cell learning to listen. You'll be the instrument they tune her with." Petra crossed to me and took my face in both hands, and her eyes were furious and wet and closer than they'd ever been. "I know what duty looks like. I wore its uniform for seventeen years. Duty is the story they tell you so you'll hold the door shut from the inside."

"And love?" I asked. "What's love's story?"

"Love doesn't trade people." Her thumb traced my cheekbone. "Love carries them out."

I closed my eyes. Behind them: Wren at nine, teaching me to whistle through a blade of grass. Wren at sixteen, laughing at my terrible first attempts at a memorial token. Wren's voice, seven years silent, screaming through a scrap of cradle-bone—*sold, sold, sold but not consumed*—and the fragment of her that had come through, the part that had recognized me, that had said my name like a hand reaching up out of deep water.

Duty said accept. Duty said: one man's service against a sister's soul, clear arithmetic, pay the toll. Every founder of Emberfall would have called it honorable. My mother would have wept and called it right.

But love—love remembered that Wren had never once, in all her life, let me carry her pack. *Walk beside me,* she used to say. *Not for me.*

"She wouldn't want it," I said aloud. The words came out rough. "If I stayed—if I traded myself for her—she'd spend eternity knowing it. That's not freeing her, Petra. That's just moving her cage next to mine and calling the view a gift."

Petra exhaled, long and shaking, and pressed her forehead to mine. "There you are," she whispered. "There's the stubborn Brightwater spine."

"We're going to need a third option."

"There is no third door. He said."

"He also wears other people's faces and sits on a throne of thigh-bones. I'm inclined to doubt his architectural claims." I pulled back and looked at her, and felt the plan begin to assemble itself in the place where despair had been—ugly, improvised, alive. "The vault route Brannic described. The one through the old tithe-tunnels. How much of your oath-mark is left to burn?"

Petra went still. Then, slowly, terribly, she smiled—the first true smile I'd seen from her since the mountain's root, and it was not a comforting sight.

"Enough," she said. "Barely enough. Once."

"Then we say yes to his face," I said, "and no to his back."

* * *

### Petra

The regent knew.

I want that written down somewhere, in ink that outlasts me: the regent knew. I saw it in the last minute of the audience, when Gideon bowed his head and said the words—*"I accept"*—in a voice pitched perfectly between grief and surrender, and the half-finished face across the throne went smooth and pleased, and the candles brightened, and every courtier in the bone hall murmured like a hive fed sugar.

But beneath the pleasure, in the space of a blink, the borrowed eyes flicked to me. To my sleeve. To the arm underneath it.

It knew we were lying. It had probably known before we did. And it let us say yes anyway—which meant the acceptance was worth more to it than the truth, which meant either arrogance or a trap, and in the Undercourt those were usually the same animal wearing different fur.

So I did the arithmetic in the corridor afterward, walking beside Gideon while an honor guard of masked courtiers escorted us toward the "workshops" where our new carver would supposedly be installed, and I reached the answer I should have reached days ago.

Brannic.

He'd been quiet since the Hunter's Kiss. Quiet since the fever broke. Too quiet—he was a man whose silences usually had invoices attached. He knew the vault route. He knew the timing of tonight's transfer ceremony, when Wren's flame would be moved to the consumption-chamber for the equinox rite. He knew everything, and he had a debt of his own to buy back, and disgraced underpriests do not stay disgraced out of loyalty.

"Gideon," I said low. "Where's Brannic now?"

"Chambers. Resting. Said the equinox rites wear on him." Gideon glanced at me, and I watched him arrive at the same sum. His jaw tightened. "How long do we have?"

"If he's already sold us—an hour. If he's drafting the sale—two. If he's hesitating over the price of his own soul—" I shook my head. "Assume an hour."

We stopped walking. The honor guard stopped with us, patient as furniture. Ahead, the corridor branched: left toward the gilded lie of the workshops, right toward the service galleries where the tithe-tunnels began, where my burned oath-mark was the only key in the world that fit.

Gideon took my hand. His palm was steady. Mine wasn't, and I hated that, and loved him a little for not mentioning it.

"For Wren," he said.

"For Wren," I said. "And for every tithe-child after her."

And that was when the bells began—deep, wrong, ringing backward through the mountain's bones—and every candle in the Undercourt guttered at once, and somewhere below us, in the dark of the old tunnels, something started screaming in a voice I recognized.

Brannic's price had just been paid. And the court had come to collect *us*.
