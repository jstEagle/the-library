## 16. Brannic's Betrayal

I have been betrayed before. Twice by the Undercourt, once by my own knees on the night I ran from the mountain, and once—memorably—by a goat in Ashmarket who ate my last coin and then looked at me with what I can only describe as smugness. So you would think I'd know the signs by now.

The signs were all there. Brannic had started sleeping facing the door. He'd stopped complaining about the food, which for Brannic was like a lark going quiet before a storm. And three nights ago, when Gideon showed him the chalk sketch of the vault route—the one we'd worked out over cold mutton and cheaper wine—Brannic had asked a question he had no business asking.

"How many guards between the Ember vault and the reliquary stair?"

We hadn't mentioned the reliquary stair to him. Not once. It was a detail I'd pulled out of my own memory of the mountain's guts, back when I was small enough to be property and curious enough to be dangerous.

Gideon hadn't noticed. Gideon noticed everything about bone and nothing about people, which was either his tragedy or his charm depending on the hour. But I noticed. I filed it away in the same drawer where I kept all my other suspicions, and I told myself that men like Brannic always asked too many questions, that it meant nothing, that paranoia was just fear wearing a smarter coat.

That is how betrayal gets you, in the end. Not through the front gate with torches and horns, but through the side door while you're busy telling yourself stories.

* * *

It was raining in Ashmarket, because it was always raining in Ashmarket, a fine greasy rain that smelled of tallow smoke and something the town insisted was fish but I suspected was regret. We were lodging above a pawnshop run by a woman with six fingers on each hand who charged us double and threw in unsolicited life advice for free.

"Your priest friend," she said that morning, counting out our change with all twelve fingers, "left an hour ago. Wearing his good collar."

"He doesn't have a good collar," I said.

"He does now."

I stood very still in the doorway of the pawnshop while the rain came down and the advice settled over me like ash. Then I went upstairs two steps at a time, which for me is ambitious, and found Gideon at the little table where he did his carving, because Gideon carved everywhere, always, as if his hands feared stillness more than his heart feared anything else.

"Get your things," I said.

He looked up. He had shavings in his hair. He always had shavings in his hair; I think they grew there. "We're leaving?"

"We might be. Or we might be about to have a very educational conversation." I was already pulling our maps from under the floorboard. "Brannic's gone out in his good collar."

"So?"

"Brannic hasn't worn that collar since the day we met him. He keeps it folded at the bottom of his pack like a love letter from someone who broke his heart. Men don't dress up for nothing, Gideon. Men dress up when they're going to sell something."

"To whom? There's no one here to—" He stopped. I watched him arrive at it, watched the thought land on him the way grief lands, heavy and sideways. "The ward-hunters have a post in Ashmarket."

"The ward-hunters have a *bought* post in Ashmarket. There's a difference, and the difference costs money." I shoved the maps into my coat. "And do you know who else pays money? Institutions. Institutions with vestments. Gideon, if he's selling us, he's not selling us to hunters. He's selling us to the church. To buy his way back in."

Gideon set down his knife. That was how I knew it was bad. Gideon's knife went down maybe twice a year, like a comet.

"He wouldn't," he said.

"He absolutely would."

"He prayed with me. In the deep roads, when the dead got loud—he put his hand on my shoulder and he *prayed*, Petra. You don't pray with a man and then sell him."

"You grew up in a valley where everyone tells the truth," I said, and heard how bitter it came out, and didn't care. "I grew up in a court where they teach you prayers specifically so you'll trust the people saying them. Brannic didn't betray us despite his faith. He's doing it *because* of it. They'll take him back if he brings them something worth forgiving. And what's worth more to them than the man who stole a soul out of their vault?"

He was quiet for a long moment. Rain ticked against the window. Somewhere below us, the six-fingered woman was arguing with a customer about the resale value of a haunted mirror.

"What do we do?" he asked finally.

It was, I want it noted, the first time he'd asked me that and simply waited for the answer. No arguing, no carving his way toward a decision with his knife talking for him. Just trust, handed over like something breakable.

What I wanted to say was: *we run*. We leave now, tonight, go around the mountain instead of into it, find some other way or make one, live long stupid lives in some damp country where nobody knows our names. Every survival instinct I owned—and I owned excellent ones, I'd kept them polished since I was seven years old—was screaming *run*.

But running meant the route closed forever. Running meant Wren Brightwater stayed a lamp-flame in a vault until the Ember ate her down to nothing. Running meant Gideon spent the rest of his life listening to every dead soul in the world except the one he'd come all this way to save.

"We go tonight anyway," I said. "Before they can move against the vault. If Brannic sold the plan, the court will seal the reliquary stair within a day. Maybe less. Which means we don't use the reliquary stair."

"There's another way in?"

"No," I said. "There's a worse way in. There's a way in that's forbidden even by Undercourt standards, which is genuinely impressive, because the Undercourt's standards include ritual cannibalism and tax law." I took a breath. "It requires an oath-mark. A keeper's mark. Mine."

Gideon frowned. "You said burning it would—"

"I know what I said."

When I'd fled the mountain at fourteen, I'd burned the edges of my oath-mark just enough to blur it, enough that the court's hounds lost my scent. The full mark sat over my left ribs like a brand: the interlocked circles of the keepers' order, inked in something that wasn't ink and hurt in a way that wasn't entirely physical. Burning it completely would open the forbidden ways—the old doors, the ones cut before the court learned manners—but it would also sever the last thread tying me to the order. Not the protections. Those I'd lost years ago. The *identity*. The part of me that was still, in whatever grudging bureaucratic sense, Petra Vane, Ward-Keeper, rather than Petra Vane, Runaway, At Large.

I'd been telling myself for eleven years that the mark meant nothing. That it was just scar tissue with ambitions.

Turns out I lied better than I thought.

* * *

We spent the afternoon preparing, and I will spare you most of it because preparation is boring even when death is the alternative. Gideon carved. Of course he carved. He made a token from a splinter of the cradle-bone he carried against his chest, a small thing no bigger than a thumbnail, and when he finished it he held it flat on his palm and listened to whatever he listens to, and his face did something complicated and then went simple.

"She says hurry," he reported.

"Your dead sister has excellent tactical instincts."

"She says hurry *and* she's sorry about the goat."

"That was not her fault and I resent the implication."

He almost smiled. In the weeks since Ashmarket, since Brannic, since the tollhouse and the fight we hadn't quite finished having, I'd learned the geography of Gideon's face the way I'd once learned the mountain's corridors: slowly, wrongly, then all at once. The smile lived in the same neighborhood as the grief. It always had. I suspected it always would.

Brannic came back at dusk.

I was watching from the window when he turned onto our street, and I saw it immediately: the collar, clean and white, and walking beside him a man in grey with the smooth, unhurried gait of someone whose violence is scheduled rather than spontaneous. A tithe-collector. I'd grown up dodging them. Some things you never forget, like a mother's voice and the walk of a man coming to collect what you owe.

They stopped beneath our window. Brannic pointed up at it. His hand only shook a little.

"Gideon," I said quietly. "Don't come to the window."

He came to the window. Of course he did. I grabbed his collar—his ordinary, unbetraying collar—and pulled him back, and we stood there in the dim room breathing at each other.

"They're coming up," he whispered.

"Yes."

"Should we—"

"Back stair. The pawnshop woman showed me the first day. She charges extra for it, but I've decided to consider it a donation." I slung my pack on. "Gideon. Listen to me. When this is done—if this is done—you're allowed to hate him. You're allowed to hate him *now*, actually, I give you permission, I know how you are about permission."

"I'm not angry at him," Gideon said, and the terrible thing was that he sounded like he meant it. "I'm sad for him. He wanted to go home, Petra. Everyone wants to go home. I just wish—" He stopped. "I wish home hadn't cost him us."

And that, right there, is why loving Gideon Brightwater was going to be the death of me. Not the ward-hunters. Not the regent. Him, and his enormous stupid forgiving heart, standing there mourning the soul of the man selling ours.

"Move," I said, because my voice was doing something unreliable, and pushed him toward the back stairs.

* * *

We heard the pounding start as we slipped out through the pawnshop's cellar, past barrels of pickled things I chose not to examine, into an alley that smelled like the inside of the rain itself. Behind us, boots on boards, the six-fingered woman's voice raised in theatrical outrage—she was charging them for the door, I'd have bet my life on it, and possibly did.

Then we ran.

Ashmarket at night in the rain is a maze designed by a drunk god, and thank every merciful power for drunk gods, because it swallowed us whole. We went down through the fish quarter, past the shuttered toll bridge, along the black lip of the river where the mountain rose up ahead of us like a held breath. Its roots went down farther than any map showed. Farther than the court admitted. And somewhere in those roots, in the oldest dark, were the doors the keepers had sealed when the order was young and still ashamed of what it had agreed to.

There was a hollow beneath the third boundary stone of the old river road. I'd marked it myself, years ago, on my way out—a child's insurance policy, scratched into stone with a stolen stylus. Inside it, wrapped in oilcloth that had somehow survived eleven years of weather, was the one thing I'd taken from the ward-house the night I ran: a keeper's fire-striker, forged from star-iron, the only metal that could burn an oath-mark clean.

"You've known where this was the whole time?" Gideon crouched beside me as I dug, rain streaming off both of us. "Since before we met?"

"I told you I deserted. I never said I planned poorly." My fingers found the oilcloth. My heart was doing something embarrassing. "Gideon. Once I light this, there's no version of me that goes back. The mark is the last thing the order recognizes. Burn it, and I'm not a deserter anymore. I'm nothing. An unregistered person. The wards won't know me, the court won't claim me, and the old doors will open because the old doors answer to exactly one thing: a keeper's ending."

"Burning it hurts," he said. Not a question. He'd seen what the blurred edges had done to me, that fevered night after the hunters, when I'd babbled about tithe-children and borrowed faces and he'd sat up with me till dawn holding a cool cloth and asking nothing.

"It hurts," I confirmed.

"Then let me—"

"You can't carve your way out of this one, Gideon. It's not bone. It's me." I struck the first spark and watched it die in the wet. Struck again. "And I need you to understand why I'm doing it, because I've decided I'm done keeping secrets from you, even the flattering kind. I could run. Right now, tonight, we could run, and we'd probably live. I've thought about it all day. I've thought about almost nothing else since I saw that collar."

The second spark caught. Star-iron burns green, did you know that? Green as spring, green as envy, green as the light under a door you should never open. I pressed it to the edge of my mark, over my ribs, and the pain arrived like a choir.

"But you'd hear her," I said through my teeth. "If we go in tonight, you'd hear her. Whole. Free. Going home. And I have discovered—I am *furious* to discover—that I cannot run from that. Not anymore. Somewhere between the tollhouse and the vault sketch, protecting you stopped being my job and became my—" I searched for the word and hated every candidate. "*Choice.* Do you understand the difference? A duty you can lay down. A choice lays down with you."

The mark burned. I burned. The rain hissed off the star-iron and the smell was ozone and something older, and underneath the pain I felt the order itself letting go of me—not violently, not like chains breaking, but like a hand opening. Eleven years of running, ended not by capture but by surrender. It should have felt like defeat.

It felt like taking off boots I'd forgotten I was wearing.

Gideon knelt in the rain beside me and put his hand over mine on the striker, not to guide it, just to be there, the way he'd been there in the deep roads, the way he was always there, steady as grain in wood.

"Petra Vane," he said formally, and I realized he was making it a vow, the bone-carver's kind, the kind spoken over grave-tokens. "Whatever the doors show us. Whatever's down there. I'm with you. Not because of the debt. Because of the choice."

"Oh, stop," I said, crying, laughing, burning. "You'll ruin my reputation."

The mark came away in a curl of green smoke, and down in the mountain's root, in the dark where forbidden things wait for forgotten keys, something ancient heard the sound of a keeper's ending—

and opened its eye.

* * *

The door was exactly where I remembered it being, which frightened me more than if it had moved. Old doors shouldn't wait patiently. Old doors should rot, collapse, become geology. This one stood in the cliff-face beneath the third boundary stone, thin as a coin and black as the space between stars, and across its surface moved faint patterns like frost forming in reverse.

As the last of the green smoke faded from my ribs, the patterns stilled. Then, with a sound like a long-held breath released, the door thinned to nothing, and beyond it lay a corridor of raw stone, unlit, unwalled by anything but intention, sloping down into the mountain's oldest dark.

Down toward the vault. Down toward Wren.

Down toward whatever the court had already begun to do with the knowledge Brannic sold them.

I stepped through first. That was the deal I'd made with myself: my choice, my lead. Gideon followed, his lantern held high, the cradle-bone token glinting at his throat, and behind us the door sealed itself shut with the finality of a page turning.

Somewhere far above, stars that had been dark for weeks began—very faintly, very few—to flicker.

The court had noticed us coming.

Good, I thought, flexing my hands against the ache where my mark used to be. Let it watch.

Let it learn what a keeper does when she stops keeping.
