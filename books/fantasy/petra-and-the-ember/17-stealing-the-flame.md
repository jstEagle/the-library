## 17. Stealing the Flame

There is a particular kind of quiet that comes just before you do something unforgivably stupid. I had heard it before—heard it in the pause before a chisel slips, in the breath my mother took before telling me what Wren had volunteered for—and I heard it now, standing in the dark beneath the Ember vault, holding a lantern carved from my own ribs.

Yes. My own ribs.

I want it noted, for posterity and for whatever historian eventually writes about this disaster, that I did not volunteer for the rib part. Brannic had simply looked at me over the stolen rites, at the mask of courtier's bone still clinging to my face like a second skull, and said, "The vault only opens to court-bone. You've got three years of your hearing stored in that mask. Bone remembers what it was."

"And?"

"And," Petra said, in the flat voice she used when she'd already decided and was merely informing me, "we need a vessel that can carry a soul-flame without burning. Court-bone holds fire. Living bone holds love. We need both."

So Brannic had taken his knife—clean, this time, with a prayer to no one—to the space between my seventh and eighth ribs, where the mask's magic had made the bone soft as green wood, and cut me two slivers of myself. They hadn't even hurt much. That was the worst of it. The Undercourt had already remade me enough that my own body gave up its pieces like a tree dropping ripe fruit.

He shaped them into a small cage-lantern while I watched, which I would not recommend to anyone, watching a disgraced underpriest whittle your own skeleton into luggage. But his hands were steady, and there was something almost tender in it, the way he worked. When he finished, he turned the little lantern in the candlelight and said, "It'll hold her. It'll hold anything that wants to be held."

"That's either very beautiful or very ominous," I said.

"Down here, boy, those are usually the same thing."

* * *

The vault lay at the root of everything—the literal root, if the old stories were true, the place where the mountain's heartwood met the Undercourt's foundations. To reach it we went down through corridors that Brannic navigated by rites he'd memorized as a child and stolen as an adult, touching stone in patterns that made doors admit us like honored guests rather than thieves.

Which we absolutely were. Let's be clear about that. I had spent forty-one years being an honest man in a valley built on a lie, and now I was creeping through a court of the dead with a forged face and a bone lantern, planning to steal a soul out of a lamp. My mother would have fainted. Wren, I suspected, would have laughed herself sick.

Petra moved ahead of me, and I watched the way she moved—not like a deserter, not anymore, but like someone coming home to a house she'd burned down on purpose. Every corridor made her jaw tighten. Every candlelit niche made her flinch somewhere behind the eyes. She had been raised in these halls. She had been *property* in these halls. And still she walked point, because she trusted me to carry the flame and trusted herself to get us to it.

"You're staring," she murmured.

"I'm admiring. There's a difference."

"There isn't, down here." But her mouth did the thing it did—half a smile, quickly confiscated—and we kept moving.

The dead were everywhere, and they were loud. Louder than any dead I'd known above ground. These were old dead, angry dead, dead who had been worn as masks and burned as lamps and spent as currency until almost nothing of themselves remained except the anger, which apparently keeps better than anything. They pressed against the edges of my hearing like moths against glass. Even through the mask, even muffled by the three years of silence I'd traded away, they found me.

*Bone-carver,* they whispered. *Listener. Listener.*

"I'm not listening tonight," I told them under my breath. "I'm working."

They laughed. It was not a comforting sound.

* * *

The Ember vault opened onto a round chamber, and I will do my best to describe it, though description feels inadequate to the offense of it.

Imagine every soul the Undercourt has ever collected, kept as flame. Imagine shelves upon shelves of lamps—bone lamps, glass lamps, lamps of woven shadow—each one holding a person reduced to light. The room was warm. That was the horror of it. It was *warm*, cozy as a kitchen, and the air tasted faintly of every life burning in it: bread baking, rain falling, someone's childhood summer, someone's last words. Thousands of people, rendered into ambience.

"There," Petra breathed.

Wren's lamp sat on a shelf at chest height, third row, marked with a small brass plate: *BRIGHTWATER, WREN — TITHE, GENERATION 19.* Seven years of my sister, burning quietly behind a pane of smoked glass, a flame the color of autumn leaves.

I crossed the room before I remembered to be careful. I should have been careful. But my hands were already on the lamp, already lifting it, already feeling the warmth of it soak through my palms like a hand squeezing back—

"Gideon." Petra's voice, sharp. "The glass. Don't break the glass until the lantern's ready. A soul uncontained down here doesn't fly free. It gets *claimed*. First thing that touches it owns it."

My hands shook. I set the lamp down on the shelf, and Brannic stepped up beside me with the little rib-cage lantern, and together—we actually worked well together, which I intend never to forgive him for—we eased the smoked pane open and coaxed the flame across.

It came willingly. That's what undid me. The flame leaned toward the lantern like a cat toward an open door, like a tired traveler toward home, and when it settled into the cage of my own bone it flared once, twice, and then burned steady and bright and *warm*, warm against my chest where I hung the lantern by its cord, right over my heart, because where else would I put my sister?

"Wren," I said. Just her name. Just to say it.

The flame flickered. And through the mask, through the muffle of my traded years, I felt—not heard, *felt*—something that might have been my sister saying mine back.

Then every alarm in the Undercourt went off at once.

* * *

I don't know what I expected the waking of a court of the dead to sound like. Bells, maybe. Screaming. What it actually sounded like was a great indrawn breath—a single vast inhalation from ten thousand throats, all around us, above and below—and then the lights began to come on.

Not electric lights. Candlelight, racing along the corridors like fire along a fuse. Somewhere a gong began to beat, slow and patient, the heartbeat of a thing waking up.

"The shelf wards," Brannic snarled, already moving. "Taking a lamp off its shelf tripped them. Of course it did. Why wouldn't it. Move, move, move—"

We ran.

I have never been a runner. I am a sitting-down sort of man, a workshop man, a man whose greatest daily exertion is deciding between walnut and yew. But terror does wonders for the cardiovasculature, and I ran through the candlelit corridors of the Undercourt with my sister's soul knocking gently against my sternum and two other people's lives in my hands, and I thought, distantly, absurdly: *Wren would find this hilarious.*

The first guards found us at the Gallery of Borrowed Faces—a long hall lined with masks, and the masks were not empty. Courtiers poured out of side passages wearing faces they had bought or stolen or inherited, and their eyes had the flat shine of things that had stopped being people a very long time ago.

Brannic threw up his hands and spoke a word of rite, and a door slammed itself between us and half of them. The other half came on.

And that is when the dead decided to remember me.

I had spent my whole life listening. Forty-one years of grave-tokens and deathbeds and small regrets murmured into a patient ear. I had never once charged for it. I had never once turned away. In Emberfall, where the dead are quiet and polite, that had seemed like a small kindness, hardly worth noticing.

But the dead of the Undercourt had noticed. Word travels, even—or especially—among the dead. The listener who never charged. The carver who gave every ghost a name in bone. And now, as the guards closed in, the old dead of the deep roads rose up around us like a tide remembering the shore.

They didn't fight. Dead things rarely fight; they *insist*. They crowded into the corridor between us and the guards, and they showed the guards what they were—what the guards themselves had been, once, before the court wore them down and wore them out. Faces surfaced in the crowd that made the guards stagger. Mothers. Sons. The people they had been before their names were spent.

"*Listener,*" the dead said, all together, a sound like wind through a canyon of bones. "*Go. We will hold the door.*"

"Hold it how?" Petra shouted, dragging me forward.

The dead laughed again, and this time it sounded almost kind. One of them—an old woman, older than the court, older maybe than the mountain—pressed something into my free hand as I passed. A tooth. Just a tooth, yellowed and cracked.

"For the road," she said. "Debts go both ways, bone-carver. You listened to the small dead all your life. Tonight the small dead listen back."

Behind us, the corridor filled. Not with violence—with *presence*, with the sheer unbearable weight of the forgotten standing up and refusing to be furniture. Guards screamed. Candles guttered. The gong lost its rhythm.

We ran.

* * *

We nearly made it. I want that recorded too. We nearly made it clean.

The last stretch was the Long Stair, a spiral of black stone climbing toward the mountain's root-gates, and we took it two steps at a time with the alarms fading below and hope rising like water in a dry throat. Brannic ahead, counting gate-rites under his breath. Petra behind me, her hand pressed between my shoulder blades as if she could push me faster. The lantern warm against my chest. Wren warm against my chest.

At the final gate, Brannic laid his palm on the stone and spoke the opening rite, and the gate began to grind open, and cold night air spilled through the gap like mercy—

and the flame in the lantern dipped.

Just once. Just a flicker, like a candle in a draft. But I felt it go through me like a hook behind the breastbone, and I stumbled, and Petra caught my arm.

"What? What is it?"

"She's—" I pressed my hand flat over the lantern. The flame was smaller than it had been. Dimmer. "The vault fed her. Seven years, the vault fed her, and now she's out of it and she's—" My voice cracked. "She's starving. Souls burn, Petra. Out in the open air, away from the Ember, she'll burn through what's left of her before we ever see the sky."

Brannic turned back from the gate, and for once—for the first time since Ashmarket—there was no calculation in his face at all. Only a tired, honest grief.

"Then we run faster," he said. "It's all we've got. Run."

So we ran. Up through the root-gates, out into the deep roads, the dead of the mountain streaming past us in the dark—some fleeing the court's wrath, some following us, some simply watching the strange procession of a bone-carver carrying his sister's soul in a cage of his own ribs. The flame dimmed with every step. I talked to it as we ran. I told it about the valley. About mother's chair. About the grave-tokens I'd carved and the ones I'd never gotten to carve for her, and how I'd finally made one, seven years late, from her cradle-bone, and how she'd screamed through it, and how sorry I was, how sorry, how sorry.

The flame flickered at each word like a pulse. Holding on. Stubborn. Ours.

We broke the surface at the mountain's mouth as the false dawn was graying the east, gasping, filthy, alive. Behind us the mountain seethed with pursuit. Ahead lay the long road home to Emberfall, and stars that were still missing from the sky, and a valley full of people who had no idea what was coming home to them.

Petra bent double beside me, breathing hard. Brannic leaned against the gatepost, laughing the ragged laugh of a man who cannot believe he is not yet dead.

And the flame in the lantern guttered low, low, lower—

and then, at the edge of the road, where the gray light met the dark of the mountain's shadow, someone was waiting for us.

Not a guard. Not the regent's soldiers.

Someone in a long coat, leaning on a staff, with a face I knew from a lamp-shelf and a ledger written in ember-red, smiling at us with my sister's borrowed mouth.

"Well," said the regent of the Undercourt, wearing Wren's face like a coat it had no right to, "you've made a remarkable mess of my vault. Shall we discuss payment?"
