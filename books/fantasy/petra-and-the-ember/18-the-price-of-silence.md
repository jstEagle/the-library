## 18. The Price of Silence

I have run from the Undercourt twice in my life. The first time, I was seventeen and I did not look back. The second time, I was running toward something instead of away, and I will tell you what no one tells you about that: it is much harder. Running away, you only have to be faster than your fear. Running toward, you have to be faster than your love, and love is quick, and it does not tire, and it keeps whispering *faster, faster* the whole way up the mountain's throat.

We came up out of the deep roads with the vault alarms still shrieking behind us like a kettle left too long on the fire, and Brannic's stolen rites still burning a sour smell into the air, and Wren Brightwater's soul riding against Gideon's chest in a lantern carved from his own rib—because that is the kind of man I had thrown my lot in with, the kind who hollows out his own body to make room for someone else's light.

The mountain's mouth opened ahead of us. Real night beyond it. Real stars—or the place where stars should be, which for seven years had been nothing at all, and which we were about to change or die trying.

And standing between us and that night, patient as a debt, stood the regent of the Undercourt.

* * *

I want to say I was surprised. I am not going to lie to you; lying is the thing I am trying very hard to stop doing. But I wasn't surprised. I had known him since before I could write my own name. He had held me on his knee when I was small enough to be charming instead of useful. He had taught me the names of the candles. He had been the one voice in all that candlelit horror that had ever sounded almost kind, and I had spent eleven years telling myself that the almost didn't matter.

He looked exactly as he always looked: a tall man in grey, with a face like a door someone had painted to look like a face. His borrowed features never quite settled. If you stared long enough—and I had learned not to stare—you could see them shifting, the way water shifts over stones.

"Keeper Vane," he said. My title, not my name. He had never once used my name. "You are carrying stolen property."

"I'm carrying a person," I said.

"A distinction without a difference, down here." He turned his painted eyes to Gideon, and I saw Gideon square his shoulders the way men do when they are about to do something brave and stupid, which in my experience are the same thing wearing different coats. "Bone-carver. You have caused my court considerable embarrassment."

"Good," Gideon said.

The regent smiled. It did not reach anywhere near his eyes, because his eyes were not really his. "The flame cannot leave," he said simply. "You know this. The mountain does not give. The mountain only trades. Someone stays. That is the price of silence, carver—the same price you have been paying all your life, only now it comes due in full."

Behind us, down the dark throat of the passage, I heard the court waking. Bells. Feet. The particular rustle of nobles putting on other people's faces.

Gideon set the lantern down on the stone between them, gently, the way you'd set down a sleeping child. And then he said, in the flat, reasonable voice of a man who has already decided and is only waiting for everyone else to catch up:

"Then I stay."

"No," I said.

"Petra—"

"*No.*" My voice cracked off the walls. "You don't get to do this. You don't get to trade yourself after everything—" I stopped, because the truth was climbing up my throat and I had promised myself I would stop swallowing it. "After I paid a toll with one of your memories without asking. After I burned my oath-mark open. After I—" My hands were shaking. "I did not walk out of that court at seventeen so I could watch it swallow someone else I—"

Someone else I what? I hadn't finished the sentence. I hadn't let myself finish it. But Gideon looked at me then, really looked, with those tired bone-carver's eyes, and I think he finished it for me, silently, in the space between us where honest things were supposed to live now.

"The dead gave their years for you," he said quietly. "In the vault. They slowed the guards. They bought us the road out. They wouldn't have done that for a man they expected to keep." He smiled, small and crooked. "Besides. I've spent my whole life listening to the dead, Petra. Staying with them isn't a punishment. It's just more of the same work."

"That's a lie," I said. "That's the loneliest lie I've ever heard."

And that is when the dead arrived.

* * *

They came up the passage behind us—not the guards' dead, not the court's dead, but *his*. The ones whose grave-tokens he had carved over thirty years in Emberfall. The murmurers of small regrets. The ones whose voices had gone silent when the wards fell, and who had found him again in the vault, and who had risen for him there because gratitude is the one coin even death respects.

I felt them before I saw them. A cold that was not unkind. A pressure like a crowd leaning close in a small room. And then the dark of the passage filled with shapes—not solid, not quite, but *present*, the way a memory is present, the way grief is present. Old farmers. A drowned girl. A woman who had died waiting for a letter. Hundreds of them, maybe thousands, all the quiet dead of a valley that magic forgot, and every one of them looking at Gideon Brightwater with something I can only call tenderness.

"He listened," said the drowned girl, and her voice was water and wonder. "All those years. Nobody listens."

"He carved our names true," said the woman with the letter. "Not the pretty versions. The true ones."

The regent took a step back. I had never seen him take a step back. In all my childhood beneath the mountain, through every ritual and every reckoning, the regent did not move unless he chose to move, and here he was, moving, his painted face flickering like a candle in wind.

"This changes nothing," he said. "The debt stands. The mountain does not give."

"You're right," said the drowned girl. "It doesn't."

She reached out her cold hand, and the others did the same, hundreds of hands, thousands, and I understood—one heartbeat before it happened—what they intended, and I did not stop them, and I have never once regretted that.

"It doesn't give," she said. "*But it owes.*"

They took him. There is no gentler word for it and no crueler one either. The dead of Emberfall closed around the regent of the Undercourt like water closing over a stone, and he screamed—a sound with no face attached, a sound older than his borrowed shape—and the faces began to come off him. All of them. Every stolen countenance, peeling away like bark, like paint, like lies finally called due, until what was left underneath was small, and grey, and old, and *hungry*, and terribly, ordinaryly afraid.

The dead did not destroy him. I think destruction would have been a mercy, and these were not merciful people; they were *just* people, which is worse. They claimed him. They folded him into their number the way you fold a debtor into a ledger, and they carried him down into the deep roads, and the last thing I heard was his voice—his real voice, thin and reedy and human, which was somehow the most horrifying thing of all—asking them to wait, asking them to listen, asking for the one thing he had never given anyone.

They did not wait. But I think—I choose to believe—they listened anyway. Because that was the whole of Gideon's teaching, passed hand to hand among the dead: you listen. Even to the ones who deserve it least. *Especially* then.

* * *

The passage went quiet. The bells below went quiet. Even the mountain itself seemed to exhale.

Gideon stood staring at the empty dark where the regent had been, and his face was doing several things at once, none of them simple. Relief and horror and grief and something that might have been grace.

"They'll keep him," he said at last. "Not cruelly. But they'll keep him."

"Yes," I said.

"Is that justice?"

I thought about the tithe-children. About the ledger with its ember-red ink. About a girl named Wren Brightwater sold by her own valley to buy its sleep. "No," I said. "But it's a start. Justice is a thing you build, Gideon. Like wards. Like homes. You don't find it lying around."

He picked up the lantern. The flame inside it—Wren, or what was left of Wren, sold but never consumed, seven years of screaming pressed into a thumb-height of light—leaned toward him. Toward the opening. Toward the night.

"Come on," he said, and his voice broke on it, and he did not try to hide that, because we had agreed somewhere on the road down that we were done hiding things. "Let's take your sister home."

We walked out of the mountain together, under the black and starless sky, and behind us the Undercourt sat leaderless for the first time in a thousand years, and ahead of us—

Ahead of us, the first star of the night was already kindling. Small. White. Stubborn as a grave-token carved in honest bone.

And I swear to you, before it even finished lighting, I heard it hum.
