## 19. Stars Returning

The mountain spat us out at dawn, which felt like a personal insult.

I had spent—by my rough count—three years of hearing the dead, one courtier's mask, most of a ribcage's worth of dignity, and an entire lifetime's assumption that I would die warm and boring in Emberfall. And the mountain, having taken all of that, could not be bothered to open onto anything better than a scree slope and a sky the color of unwashed wool.

"Charming," Petra said. She was leaning on me more than she'd admit and less than she needed. The wound in her side had stopped bleeding somewhere around the fourth mile of the deep roads, which Brannic had assured us was good, and which I had privately decided to interpret as *not immediately fatal* rather than *good*. "You'd think they'd at least give you a view."

"They gave me a sister," I said. "I'm not greedy."

The lantern swung between us. That was the thing about it—the thing I kept having to relearn every time my eyes found it. It was a small lantern. Bone-carved, because of course it was; I had made it from my own rib in the dark of the vault while the court slept around us like a lake of candles, and I had done the carving badly, hurriedly, with hands that would not stop shaking. It was ugly work. My apprentices would have laughed at it. My *master* would have wept.

And inside it burned a flame no bigger than a thumbnail, gold-white, steady as a held breath.

Wren.

Seven years of silence, seven years of reaching through the murmur of the dead for one voice that never came—and there she was, riding in a lantern against my hip, light enough to read by, small enough to lose.

"Don't," Petra said quietly.

"I'm not doing anything."

"You're doing the thing where your face does arithmetic." She touched the lantern's frame with two fingers, careful, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal. "It doesn't add up yet. It will. But not here, and not while you're counting."

Brannic came up the scree behind us, wheezing like a bellows with opinions. He had lost his hat in the vault, his composure somewhere around the moment three hundred grateful dead had risen out of the floor to slow the guards, and apparently nothing else, because he was still talking. "The mouth of the valley road is half a day if we push. The wards are down, so nothing stops us crossing—but nothing stops *anything else* either, which is worth remembering. Also, I want it noted for posterity that I said the vault route was a bad idea, and then it worked, and I have decided this makes me a prophet."

"Noted," Petra said.

"For posterity," Brannic insisted.

"Posterity is busy," I said. "Posterity has a lantern to carry."

We pushed. Of course we pushed. You do not walk out of hell at a stroll.

* * *

The strange thing about carrying a soul is how ordinary the walking is.

I had expected—I don't know what I'd expected. Weight. A hum. Some sign from Wren that she knew where she was, that she was aware in there, that the screaming fragment I'd heard through the cradle-bone token had quieted into something bearable. But the flame gave nothing back. It burned. It did not flicker when I spoke to it, though I spoke to it often, mostly complaints, because that was our native language: *you're drifting left, the strap's cutting my shoulder, if you burn my coat I'm telling Mother it was your fault.*

Petra caught me at it once, on the second night, when we'd made camp in the lee of a fallen waystone with the stars still missing overhead—a sky like a swept floor, like a room after a funeral.

"You talk to her like she can hear you," Petra said.

"She sold her voice to buy this valley. Seems rude of her not to listen now that I've come all this way."

"That's not—" Petra started, and stopped, and I watched her decide not to say whatever it was. She was getting better at that. Or worse. With Petra it was always hard to tell whether restraint was growth or exhaustion. "Gideon. When we let her go. At the top of the pass, under the open sky—that's when she'll hear you. All of it. Everything you've said since the vault. The dead don't hear inside the flame. They keep it. Like letters unopened."

I looked at the lantern a long time.

"So everything I've been saying—"

"The complaining. Yes."

"Good," I said. "She should know what she put me through."

Petra laughed—short, cracked, real—and then pressed the heel of her hand to her eyes, and I understood that the laugh had been holding something up, and that the something had come down anyway. I sat beside her. I didn't touch her. There is a kind of grief that needs a witness more than it needs comfort, and I am, whatever else has been taken from me, a very good witness.

"I was four," Petra said, eventually, to the fire. "When they took my name. You don't get a name, in the Undercourt. You get a function. Mine was *keeper-of-the-tenth-door*, which sounds grand until you learn the tenth door is where they store the things even the court is ashamed of." Her jaw worked. "I told myself for twenty years that escaping was enough. That surviving was the debt paid. And then I stood in that ward-house and read a ledger with a girl's name in ember-red ink, and the girl had volunteered, and the volunteering had been *arranged*, and I had helped arrange it, and surviving turned out to be worth exactly nothing."

"It bought her seven more years," I said. "Of being alive. Of being *Wren*."

"It bought the valley seven years of lying to itself."

"Yes," I said. "Both things are true. That's the part nobody warns you about. Truth isn't a coin you spend once. It's a stone you carry, and it cuts whichever hand holds it, and you just—keep choosing which cut you can live with."

Petra looked at me sideways. "You've gotten philosophical since you gave away three years of your hearing."

"I've gotten *tired*. Tired sounds wise if you say it slowly."

Above us, the swept-floor sky stayed empty. Below us, in the lantern, my sister kept her own counsel, keeping mine.

* * *

We crested the pass above Emberfall on the third evening, and the whole valley opened below us like a cupped hand.

Even dark—even wardless, starless, its watch-fires banked low—it was the most beautiful thing I had ever seen, and I include in that assessment several cathedrals' worth of candlelit horror, so I feel qualified. The river caught what little light remained and ran silver. The terraces climbed the hillsides in their patient rows. And there, near the center, the lamplit scatter of the town, and among those lamps, one window I knew the shape of by heart: our kitchen, where our mother was, right now, sitting beside an empty chair.

"We should go down," Brannic said. "Tonight. Before anything else comes out of that mountain after us."

"In a moment," I said.

"Gideon—"

"*In a moment.*"

Because here is the thing I had known since the vault, since the moment I lifted the lantern off its hook between two sleeping courtiers and felt the flame lean toward me like a child toward a door: I could not carry her home. Not all the way. A soul is not a rescued cat. You do not bring it back to the kitchen and set it by the stove and feed it soup. Wren had been sold into the Ember, and the Ember had kept her, and what burns in the Undercourt's vaults does not simply step back into a life. There was one door left to her, and it was the oldest door there is, and it opened upward.

The dead had told me so. The ones who rose in the vault—the ones my three purchased years had bought free along with everything else—they had pressed close in the dark as we ran, and their voices had been the last voices I would ever hear, and they had spent them well. *Carry her high,* they said. *Carry her where the sky can reach. What was stolen downward must be given back upward. That is the old arithmetic. That is the only mercy the mountain never learned.*

So we did not go down into the valley. We went up. There was a shelf of granite above the pass—an old watch-place, the kind the founders had used when the wards were young and honest—and it stood clear of the slopes, open to the whole bowl of the sky, and it was as close to the top of the world as Emberfall had ever bothered to build.

I set the lantern on the flat stone at its center.

Petra knelt across from me. Brannic hung back at the edge of the shelf, and for once in his life said nothing, which I choose to believe was reverence and not windedness.

"Now what?" Petra asked.

"Now I talk," I said. "Apparently she's been saving my mail."

My hands were shaking. I noticed them shaking with a kind of distant irritation, the way you notice weather. Seven years. Seven years of carving grave-tokens for other people's dead, of listening to their small regrets—the unpaid debts, the unsaid apologies, the man who wished he'd told his brother the joke was funny, the woman who wished she'd planted the plum tree—and through all of it, one absence shaped exactly like my sister, one silence with her laugh's exact outline. I had built a whole quiet life around that hole. I had called it peace. It had not been peace. It had been a well-maintained wound, and here at the end of the world, on a shelf of rock under a starless sky, I finally set it down.

"Wren," I said.

The flame did not move. The flame was a flame. And then—

The sky moved instead.

It began at the zenith, or where the zenith should have been: a single point of light, small and white and utterly ordinary, the kind of star you'd never notice on any other night. Except there had been no nights with stars for eleven days, and every soul in the valley below had learned to look up and see nothing, so when that one point kindled, I felt the whole valley feel it. I swear I felt it through the stone. A thousand people stopping mid-motion. A thousand faces tilting back.

Then a second star. Then ten. Then the sky tore open like a seam giving way, and the stars came pouring back—not gradually, not politely, but in floods, constellations reassembling themselves like a household lighting every lamp at once after a long illness, and the Milky Way spilled across the dark in a river of crushed pearl, and I sat down hard on the granite because my legs had resigned without consulting me.

"Oh," said Brannic, from the edge, in a very small voice for a very large man. "Oh, that's—yes. All right. Yes."

And in the lantern, the flame rose.

Not flickering. *Rising.* It lifted off its wick of bone-ash like a moth leaving a chrysalis, stretching tall and thin, and its color changed—gold going to white going to something past white, a color I have no name for except that it looked the way the first morning after a fever feels. The lantern grew too heavy to hold its passenger. The little flame pressed against the bone frame, testing it, the way Wren used to press her palm against windows in winter, checking whether the world outside was worth the cold.

"Wren," I said again, and my voice broke in the middle like a boy's. "I've got—there's a lot of messages. I've been leaving them the whole way. You probably got the ones about the coat."

The flame pulsed. Once. Twice. And then the air itself remembered how to carry her, and I heard my sister's voice for the first time in seven years, and it was not the screaming fragment from the cradle-bone, and it was not the ghost-echo I'd invented a hundred times in a hundred lonely workshops.

It was *her*. Whole. Unhurried. Amused, already, at whatever my face was doing.

*"Gideon Brightwater,"* she said, from everywhere, from the light itself, *"you absolute disaster of a man. Did you carve that lantern out of your own RIB?"*

"It was expedient."

*"It was deranged. Mother is going to kill you. Well—Mother is going to hug you until your eyes pop out, and THEN kill you."* And then the amusement gentled, the way hers always did, quick as weather, and her voice came closer, though she had no nearer to be. *"I heard everything. Every word since the vault. The complaining, yes. And the rest of it. Gideon—you came DOWN THERE. For me. Nobody comes down there. I watched the ledger for seven years. I watched the terms. I knew what the collection meant. I had made my peace with being fuel."*

"You weren't fuel."

*"No,"* she said softly. *"Turns out I was a debt. And debts can be paid. Fuel just burns."* A pause, and when she spoke again there was something underneath it that I had never heard from her in life, because in life she had never needed it: *"I forgive you. I want you to actually hear that, so I'll say it properly: I forgive you. Whatever you've been carrying—whatever you think you failed to do, the night I died, all the years after—you're forgiven. You were forgiven before you finished climbing the first hill. Forgiveness was never the thing standing between us. Only the distance was."*

I did not manage words. I managed something that was technically breathing.

*"Tell Mother—" * The flame wavered, gathered. *"Tell her the chair can be filled however she likes now. Tell her I didn't volunteer because I was brave. I volunteered because I was angry, and I thought anger was the same as courage, and it took me seven years in the dark to learn the difference. Tell her I'm sorry for the row we had. Tell her the plum tree—plant the plum tree, Gideon, the south corner, where the light pools. I always wanted the plum tree."*

"I'll plant three."

*"Greedy."*

"Family trait."

The flame was thinning now, stretching upward, going the way of smoke and morning mist, and I understood with a clarity that felt like cold water that this was the leaving, that the sky full of returned stars was not a backdrop but a door, and that my sister was walking through it in front of me, unhurried, looking back.

*"One more thing,"* she said. *"The silence they took from you. The voices. I know what you traded. I know what it cost, and I know you're telling yourself it was cheap."*

"It was cheap," I said. "Three years against a life."

*"Mm. And what will you do, bone-carver, when the valley starts asking you to listen again? When the new wards need a man who hears? When some frightened kid turns up with a gift like yours and everyone looks at YOU?"*

"I'll figure it out."

*"That's the answer,"* she said, satisfied. *"That's exactly the right answer. Don't plan it. Just—figure it out. You always were better at mending than at planning."*

The flame stood at its full height above the lantern, taller than my hand, brighter than any star it was about to join, and the light of it lay over the three of us on the stone shelf like a blessing with weight to it. Petra had her head bowed. Brannic had removed his nonexistent hat. And I looked up at my sister's soul, whole and forgiven and going home, and I gave her the only thing I had left that was worth anything.

"Go on, then," I said. "We'll be fine. Go home."

*"Home,"* she repeated, tasting the word. *"Yes. Oh—Gideon. It's so much BIGGER than I thought. All this time I thought going home meant the kitchen."*

And she laughed—her laugh, the real one, the one that snorted at the end when she forgot to be dignified—and the flame leapt.

It went up like a spark off a bonfire, straight and swift and joyous, and the sky received it, and for one impossible moment there was a new star above Emberfall that had not been there before, burning gold-white, burning *hers*, brighter than every ancient sun around it. Then it settled. Then it steadied. Then it was simply one star among hundreds, one light in a sky gone riotously, extravagantly full, and if you hadn't seen it born you'd never have picked it out.

But I'll pick it out every night for the rest of my life. That I promise the dark.

* * *

Nobody spoke for a while. The stars did the talking. They're better at it than we are; they've had longer to practice.

Eventually Brannic cleared his throat with the delicacy of a landslide. "Right," he said. "Emotionally devastated, spiritually transformed, and it's freezing. Can we go down the mountain now?"

"We can go down the mountain," Petra said. She was still kneeling by the dead lantern—empty now, just bone and ash and my bad carving—and she closed its little door gently, the way you close a book you loved. Then she looked up at me, and her face was wet, and she didn't wipe it, and something in my chest turned over and refused to explain itself.

"Gideon," she said. "What she said. About the valley asking you to listen again."

"Mm."

"There's something you should know before we're down there. Something I saw in the regent's hall, right before the dead took him." She stood, slowly, favoring her side, and the starlight fell across her face and made her look both younger and older than any age I could name. "When the court woke and the guards came, the regent wasn't shouting about the flame. He was shouting about the *ledger*. About the other names in it. Gideon—the bargain wasn't one soul per generation. That's what the founders wrote down. It's what they believed. But I saw the vault shelves. I counted the hooks."

The wind came up the pass, smelling of pine and snow and the clean cold nothing of a sky with its lights back on.

"How many?" I asked.

Petra looked at me, and then down the mountain, toward the valley, toward the lamplit kitchen and the empty chair and the thousand neighbors who had just looked up and seen miracles and would want, by morning, to know what the miracles cost.

"I stopped counting at forty," she said. "And the newest name was inked six days ago. While we were on the road. Someone down there is still paying."
