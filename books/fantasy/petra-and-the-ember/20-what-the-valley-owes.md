## 20. What the Valley Owes

The road home smelled of rain and old ash, and I hated how much it smelled like childhood.

Petra Vane does not get sentimental. Petra Vane gets *practical*. That's the woman I built, brick by brick, in a court where sentiment was a currency you paid with your throat. But there's no practical way to describe walking back into Emberfall after everything—after the tollhouse, after Ashmarket, after Brannic's face going grey when he sold us out, after Gideon carrying his sister's soul in a lantern made from his own ribs' worth of bone, after the regent's candlelit smile and the dead rising like a tide to swallow him whole.

So I won't be practical about this one thing: when the valley's rim came into view and the first star—the new star, Wren's star—hung low over the mountain's shoulder, I stopped walking and cried like a tithe-child who'd just learned her name was never hers at all.

Gideon pretended not to notice. That's why I keep him.

"You're leaking," he said.

"Rivers do that."

"Rivers don't glare while they do it."

We came down through the western pastures, past the ward-stones that had stood silent since the night the stars went out. They looked smaller than I remembered. Everything did. The Undercourt had seemed infinite down there in the dark, all borrowed faces and candle smoke, but up here under a sky stitching itself back together with light, the mountain was just a mountain, and the court beneath it was just a wound that had finally been lanced.

The valley knew we were coming before we reached the first farmhouse. Word travels fast in places where nothing else does. By the time we passed the mill, people were standing in their doorways—not cheering, not running, just *watching*, the way you watch weather you can't decide whether to trust.

An old man by the well made the ward-sign against evil as we passed. Not at me. At Gideon. At the lantern he still carried, empty now, its carved ribs catching the starlight.

"They're afraid of you," I said quietly.

"I'm afraid of me," he said. "Seems fair."

* * *

Emberfall's council met in the ward-house, which felt like a joke someone had forgotten to finish. The building had been Petra Vane's second home for eleven years—I'd slept in its loft, memorized its ledgers, forged its terms with my own steady keeper's hands—and now I walked into it as a stranger, a deserter, a witness nobody had asked for.

They'd set up chairs in the great hall where the ledger shelves used to stand. Someone had burned the ledgers. All of them. I could smell it in the walls, that sweet char of vellum and binding-ink, and something in my chest went cold and stayed cold.

Keeper Aldous Vane sat at the head of the table. My father. Of course he was. When I'd fled eleven years ago, he'd been third chair; grief and ambition had promoted him in my absence, or perhaps he'd simply outlived everyone more honest.

He didn't look surprised to see me. That was the worst part. He looked like a man watching a bill come due.

"Gideon Brightwater," he said, "and—" a pause, delicate as a knife laid flat, "—the keeper who abandoned her post."

"The keeper who found out what her post was *for*," I said.

"Sit down, Petra."

"I'll stand. Standing's honest."

There were nine on the council. I counted them the way I'd been trained to count exits. Three were farmers—Hale, Maren Ostrow, and young Tam Brightwater, Gideon's cousin, who wouldn't meet my eyes. Two were craft-masters. One was the miller's widow, who'd buried two husbands and trusted no one, which made her the most dangerous person in the room besides my father. And three were keepers, robed in undyed wool, sitting with the particular stillness of people who have rehearsed their innocence.

Aldous folded his hands. "The valley owes you gratitude," he said. "Whatever happened beneath the mountain, the stars are returning, and the wards—"

"The wards are *dead*, Father." I let the word land. Half the hall flinched at it; none of them had known, apparently, that the deserter was the regent's own—no. Later. One horror at a time. "You're sitting in a burned building asking me to pretend the fire was weather."

"The wards served us for two hundred years."

"The wards served *you* for two hundred years. Everyone else got protection priced in souls."

Silence. The kind that has weight. Tam Brightwater shifted, and his chair creaked like a confession.

Maren Ostrow spoke first, because she always would. "What proof? Words from a runaway and a grave-carver who hears voices. Forgive me if I want more than a story."

I'd expected this. I'd rehearsed it on the road, lying awake while Gideon slept, composing the speech that would make them understand without making them burn me first. And every version I'd composed had one flaw: it softened things. It arranged the truth like flowers instead of dumping it out like a drawer.

So I dumped the drawer.

"There's no proof," I said. "Proof is a court word. Proof is what the Undercourt demands before it takes your name. What I have is testimony, and I'll give all of it, and then you can decide what kind of valley you live in. Here is the testimony."

I told them about the founders' bargain. Every generation, one soul, sold to the Undercourt to buy the wards that kept magic out—and kept the court's attention off Emberfall. I named the founder's terms, clause by clause, because I'd read the original. I'd *copied* the original, in my own hand, at nineteen, believing I was doing sacred work.

I named the keepers who had maintained the ledger. Three generations of them. I named the families whose children had been taken—families told their daughters had died of fever, their sons lost on the mountain, because the bargain required the valley's grief to look natural.

I watched Hale's face go white as I said his grandmother's name. I watched Maren's hands close around each other like she was holding a bird that might fly apart.

And then I said the part I'd sworn on the road I'd say last, because it was mine.

"My name is Petra Vane. Eleven years ago I was given to the Undercourt as a tithe-child—the court's half of an older arrangement, a courtesy exchange between the regent and this valley's keepers. I escaped. In doing so, I cost the court its bone-carver, and the court has spent eleven years arranging to collect a replacement. The replacement was Wren Brightwater. Her soul was sold seven years ago to buy the wards another generation of silence. I helped forge those terms. I wrote clauses I knew were traps. I told myself it was mercy—that a clean contract hurt less than a messy one. It wasn't mercy. It was cowardice wearing a keeper's robe."

I turned to my father. "Say something, Aldous. You signed the tithe order. Your own daughter. Say it wasn't for the good of the valley."

For one moment—one long, suspended moment—he looked at me, and I saw something move behind his face that might have been the man who'd taught me to read star-charts on his knee. Then it closed, the way doors close in the Undercourt, smooth and total.

"The tithe was necessary," he said. "Every founding requires sacrifice. You were chosen because you were strong."

"*Chosen.*" I laughed, and it came out wrong, jagged. "I was *surplus*. I was the daughter who asked questions. Wren Brightwater was chosen because she volunteered, and she volunteered because your keepers told her the valley would fall without her, and she was nineteen and kind and believed you." My voice cracked and I let it. Let them hear it. "She believed you the way I believed you. That's the inheritance of this office, Father. We break the ones who trust us, and call it stewardship."

Gideon spoke then, from beside me, quiet as carving. "My mother signed nothing. She was told Wren died of river-fever. Seven years she's left a chair empty for a lie you fed her."

Tam Brightwater stood up. Young Tam, who hadn't met my eyes once, planted his palms on the table and said, "I move we hear the rest before anyone decides anything. All of it. Even the parts that shame us."

"It shames *her*," Aldous said, nodding at me. "Not the valley."

"No," Maren Ostrow said slowly, and her voice had changed, gone flat and hard as threshing-floor stone. "It shames all of us. We ate the harvest. We raised our children behind those wards. We let ourselves not ask." She looked at me, and her eyes were wet and furious. "How many?"

"How many what?"

"Families. How many families gave someone and were lied to?"

"Fourteen," I said. "In two hundred years. Fourteen names in ember-red ink. I can recite them. Do you want me to recite them?"

Nobody answered. So I did.

I recited fourteen names, slow, letting each one sit in the air like a struck bell. Some of the council had known some of them. None had known all. By the seventh name, the miller's widow was crying without any change in her expression, tears just falling down a face carved from refusal. By the fourteenth, Hale had his head in his hands.

When I finished, the silence was different. Heavier. Alive.

Aldous rose. "This changes nothing," he said, and his voice was steady, and I hated how much I envied that steadiness. "The valley needs wards. Magic will find us now that the court knows our roads. Without protection, we are naked. Rebuild the stones, restore the terms, and we survive. Or tear it all open and watch the valley die of honesty."

"And pay for the next wards with the fifteenth name?" Gideon asked. "Whose child this time? Do you draw lots, or do you keep choosing the ones who volunteer because they're too kind to refuse?"

"We choose the strong," Aldous said. "As we have always—"

"As you have always chosen the *disposable*," I said. "That's the word, Father. Say it. Disposable. Tithe-children. Surplus daughters. Kind girls who volunteer. You've had two hundred years to dress it up and you still can't make 'sacrifice' mean anything except 'someone else.'"

He sat down. Slowly, like a man whose legs had finally gotten the news his pride refused to send.

It was Maren Ostrow who called the vote. "Two matters before this council," she said. "First: do we rebuild the old wards on the old terms? Second: does this testimony enter the valley record entire, names and all, including the keeper's confession?" She paused. "Including Keeper Vane's part in forging the terms that took Wren Brightwater."

Oh, she meant it as a blade. I could see it—she thought exposing my guilt would discredit everything, that the valley would rather shoot the messenger than bury the message.

She was right about the valley. She was wrong about me.

"Yes," I said, before anyone else could speak. "Enter it all. Enter my confession first, if it helps. Write that Petra Vane forged the clauses, wrote the traps, handed Wren Brightwater's name to the court with her own steady keeper's hands. Write it in ink that doesn't fade. And then write the rest, and let the valley weigh a coward against a crime and decide what either of us is owed."

Aldous stared at me. For the first time in the whole terrible evening, he had nothing rehearsed.

The vote on the wards failed. Nine seated, five against rebuilding on the old terms, three abstaining, one—my father—not voting at all, just looking at his hands like they belonged to a stranger.

The vote on the record passed. Eight to one.

As the hall emptied, the miller's widow stopped beside my chair. Up close her face was ordinary, seamed, human—the face of a woman who had buried two husbands and trusted no one and had just discovered that her distrust had been the sanest thing in the valley for two centuries.

"You'll be tried," she said. "Some of them will want it. Deserter, collaborator—it's all true, isn't it?"

"All of it."

"Then don't flinch when it comes." She patted my shoulder once, hard, like closing a gate. "Truth's only worth what it costs the teller. Cheap truth is just gossip."

She left. Gideon touched my elbow. "Come on," he said. "Mother's put water on. She does that when the world ends."

"She's done it twice this month?"

"Three times. You weren't here for the first."

* * *

His mother's kitchen was warm and yellow-lit and full of the smell of bread, and I stood in the doorway like a ghost checking whether it still had permission to haunt.

Sela Brightwater looked up from the table, where she'd set three bowls instead of two. She looked at me for a long moment—at the ward-hunter scar on my jaw, at the keeper's oath-mark burned off my wrist, at all the evidence of everything I'd confessed in front of her neighbors an hour ago.

"You're the one who wrote the paper," she said.

"Yes."

"And you're the one who brought my daughter home."

"Also yes."

She nodded slowly, and pushed the third bowl an inch toward me. "Then sit down and eat, girl. We'll sort out what you owe after you're fed. That's how we do things in this valley—or it's how we're going to do them, starting tonight."

I sat. The bread was still warm. Outside, Wren's star climbed over the mountain, and somewhere below us, in the dark under the root of the world, a door stood open that no one had yet decided whether to seal or guard or walk through.

I meant to sleep. I truly did.

But near midnight, a knock came at the Brightwaters' door—three soft raps, spaced like a ritual—and when Sela opened it, there on the step stood a keeper in undyed wool, hood back, rain-wet, holding out a scrap of parchment sealed with wax the color of dried blood.

"For Petra Vane," the keeper said. "From the council. Sealed tonight."

I broke the seal with thumbs that had stopped shaking years ago and started again only recently.

It was not a summons to trial.

It was a map.
