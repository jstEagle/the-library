## 21. The Carving of New Names

The morning of the vote, I woke to a silence so complete it felt like weather.

Seven months since the mountain's mouth. Seven months since the dead went out of my head like candles pinched one by one, and I had learned to live in the quiet the way you learn to live with a limp—first resenting it, then forgetting it, then catching yourself leaning on it without thinking. But that morning the silence had a texture to it. A held-breath quality. The whole valley was holding its breath, and for once I could feel it without hearing anyone's ghost tell me so.

I lay in bed and listened to nothing, which is a strange thing to write down but true. No murmuring from the churchyard across the lane. No old Aldous Fenwick complaining through my floorboards about the damp in his grave—I'd heard that complaint every rainy season for twenty years, and I confess there are mornings now when I miss it, the way you miss a dog that barked too much and kept the wolves honest.

I got up. I put the kettle on. Outside my window, Emberfall sat under a sky that had been slowly remembering its stars, three or four new ones kindling each week like lamps being lit in a house where someone had finally come home.

The vote was at noon, in the meeting hall by the millpond. My mother did not ask if I was going. She simply set out my good coat—the one with the bone buttons I'd carved her the winter Wren died—and said, "Wear this. Whatever they decide, they should see what a Brightwater looks like when he's not hiding."

Which was her way of saying she was proud of me and also still sorry, and both of those things could be true at once. That was the year I learned they could.

* * *

The hall was full by half past eleven. Full past full—people standing along the walls, sitting on windowsills, old Tam Hobb perched on the woodpile like a crow who'd decided the meeting concerned him personally, which knowing Tam, it did.

Petra stood at the front beside the council table, and I watched the valley look at her. They'd had seven months to decide how to feel about Petra Vane: deserter, ward-keeper, tithe-child, the woman who burned her own oath-mark to open a forbidden road and carried my sister's name out of the dark in her two hands. Some of them had decided to forgive her. Some had decided never to. Most, I think, were still deciding, and she stood there and let them look, because that was the bargain she'd made with herself—that she would never again stand in a room and let people believe something comfortable instead of something true.

Maren Cobb spoke first, because Maren always speaks first; it saves everyone else the trouble of pretending they might. She wanted the wards rebuilt exactly as they'd been. Old wards, old stone, old terms. "It worked," she said, thumping the table hard enough to rattle the inkwell. "Two hundred years, it worked. My grandmother slept safe. My mother slept safe."

"Your grandmother slept safe," said Petra quietly, "because a girl your grandmother's age died screaming under the mountain. Every generation. You just never knew their names."

"We know one now," someone called from the back, and the hall went very still, because everyone knew whose name he meant, and everyone knew he was right, and nobody liked the arithmetic of it.

My mother rose. She is not tall and has never needed to be. "I set Wren's chair for seven years," she said, in the voice she used for telling children the truth about death, gently and without flinching. "I set it because I thought she was gone, and grief deserves its rituals. Then I learned she wasn't gone. She was *paid*. We spent her like coin and called it peace." She looked down the length of the tables. "I will not vote for peace bought that way again. Not with anyone's daughter. Not even if the daughter is willing. Especially then."

Old Hollis the miller asked the question I'd been waiting for, the practical one, because Hollis trusts nothing he can't grind between two stones: "And if we refuse the bargain? What stops the court from simply taking?"

"They tried taking," Petra said. "In the spring. Ask them how it went." And a few people laughed—not many, but enough—remembering the regent's hunters who had come up the valley road in April and found the ward-stones singing with the consent of four hundred living voices, and had turned around and gone home like dogs called off by a whistle they couldn't argue with.

That was the thing Brannic had taught us, before he left—before his betrayal and his partial, grudging, human redemption, in whatever order those actually happened; I've stopped trying to make him into a tidy story, because he wouldn't thank me and wouldn't recognize himself in one anyway. The old wards ran on theft. One soul, taken. The new ones could run on gift. Four hundred small yeses, renewed each year at the spring equinox, spoken aloud over honest stone. Less elegant. More work. Like tending a fire instead of buying one with somebody else's wood.

They voted at noon. I have been present at births and deaths and one memorable barn fire, and I will tell you the vote felt like all three. Two hundred and six votes for the new wards. Forty-one against. Nine abstentions, including, I noticed, Tam Hobb, who afterward explained that he abstained on principle because "a man shouldn't choose a future until he's seen how the bread turns out," which is either wisdom or cowardice depending on how much you like Tam.

Forty-one against. I looked at those faces as the count was read. Maren Cobb's jaw set like a knot in oak. But an old woman near the door—Widow Prill, who had lost a son the same year I lost Wren—was crying, and when I caught her eye she didn't look away. "Forty-one," she said to me, almost apologetically. "But forty-one honest ones, lad. That's more than we ever had before. Before, we were all lying together."

I have thought about that sentence a great deal since. I think it may be the truest thing anyone said to me that year.

* * *

The cutting of the stones took eleven days.

We quarried from the seam above the old shrine—the pale granite that rings true when struck, no iron in it, no history. Brannic, before he went back down to whatever arrangement he'd negotiated with the court, had shown us the rites for testing stone: hold it to your ear, speak your name into it, wait. If the stone keeps your name, it's listening. If it gives it back, it's only echoing, and echo is all the old magic ever was—a stolen voice bounced back at you until you mistook it for protection.

I carved the first token myself. Not because I was the best carver in the valley anymore—we had apprentices now, three of them, and young Sef with her quick hands could out-cut me on a good day—but because the council asked, and because some things you do yourself or not at all.

The tokens were different from anything I'd made before. Grave-tokens are for the dead; these were for the living. Each household would carve their own names into their own stone, freely, and set it at the border, and the ward would be four hundred small declarations rather than one large secret. Consent, cut in granite. It sounded simple when Petra described it. It took eleven days and most of the skin off my knuckles, and on the fourth day Sef caught me re-carving the same curve for the third time and said, "Master Gideon, you're stalling."

"I'm being careful."

"You're stalling," she said, with the fearless honesty of the fifteen-year-old, "because the last token you carved for the border was Wren's, and you're afraid this one won't matter as much."

I put down my chisel. I looked at this girl I'd known since she was small enough to fit in a grain sack, who had come to me last autumn asking to learn bone-work and stayed asking harder questions than any apprentice I'd ever had.

"When did you get wise?" I asked.

"From watching you talk to empty chairs," she said. "You're kinder to ghosts than to yourself. Somebody ought to balance the books."

* * *

On the ninth day, I went up to the border alone, in the grey hour before dawn, carrying two stones.

One was the new ward-token, finished, with the valley's covenant already ringing faintly inside it like a bell that hadn't been struck yet. The other was a piece of cradle-bone—no. That's not true, and I promised myself I'd stop lying in this account, even the small comfortable ones. It was a piece of applewood from the tree over Wren's grave, seasoned seven years, and I had been carrying it unfinished in my coat pocket since the winter, taking it out sometimes at night, holding it, putting it back.

Because here is what I had not told anyone, not even Petra, not even my mother: I had not yet carved Wren's memorial token. Seven years a bone-carver, thousands of tokens, every soul in the churchyard sent off with something shaped by my hands—and my own sister's memorial sat uncarved in a pocket like a stone I couldn't swallow.

Before, I would have told you why easily enough: I couldn't hear her. Every other soul in that valley spoke to me, and she was silence, and carving her token meant making something permanent out of that silence, and I wasn't ready.

Now I know better. Now I know I was waiting to hear her *whole*. The fragment I pulled out of the cradle-bone that terrible night—her screaming, sold but unconsumed—that fragment haunted me worse than silence ever had. I couldn't carve a memorial to a scream.

And then the flame came home, and the star relit, and I heard her one last time on the ridge above the valley: whole, forgiven, going wherever souls go when they're done being bargains. And after that—nothing. The price of my own trade, the years I gave the court for a mask, stacking on top of the silence I'd chosen at the mountain's mouth. Mostly deaf now. A murmur here and there on certain nights, like a radio catching a station two counties over, fading before I can make out the words.

So the question was: could I carve her token without hearing her say it was right?

I sat down on the cold grass at the border with the applewood in my lap and the dawn coming up behind the eastern ridge, and I thought about what Widow Prill had said. Forty-one honest ones. We were all lying together, before. Maybe I had been lying longest of all—lying to myself that the silence was a wound, when maybe it was also a room. A quiet room where, for the first time in my life, I got to decide what filled it.

I began to carve.

I won't describe every stroke; a carver's work is mostly decisions, and decisions are duller to read than they are to make. But I will tell you what I made, because it surprised me. I had expected to make her a bird—she loved birds, Wren, obviously, the name was practically prophecy—or a star, or one of the old protective knots. Instead my hands made a chair. A small wooden chair, carved from the applewood, with the seat left rough and unfinished, and around the backrest I cut the words we'd found in the ledger, the ones Petra had forged and then unmade with her confession: *Not paid. Loved.*

When I finished, the sun was clearing the ridge, and I set the little chair on the border-stone shelf beside the new ward-token, and I waited for—what? A sign. A whisper. Some last grace note from the other side, one word of my sister's voice to bless the work.

Nothing came. The birds sang. The wind moved the apple trees below. Somewhere down the valley my mother was putting the kettle on, and Sef was probably already at my workbench, carving badly and asking better questions, and the new ward hummed its low patient hum built out of four hundred yeses.

And I found—to my genuine surprise, sitting there in the wet grass with my sister's memorial finished at last—that the silence wasn't empty. It was full. It was full of everything I'd chosen: the vote, the stones, the girl waiting at my bench, the chair my mother still set at the table though now it got filled by whoever needed filling. The dead had been my company for fifty years, and I loved them, and I let them go, and what remained was not absence.

What remained was morning.

I picked up the little chair and carried it home.

* * *

We set it on the mantel that evening, in place of the seventh setting. My mother looked at it a long time—longer than was comfortable, longer than she lets herself look at most things—and then she did something I had never seen her do. She touched it. Just one finger, on the rough unfinished seat, the way you'd touch a sleeping child to confirm the breathing.

"She'd have hated it," my mother said. "She'd have said a chair wants cushions, and you've left her sitting on bare wood for eternity."

"She'd have said worse than that," I said. "She'd have said I wasted good applewood."

And my mother laughed—the short, cracked laugh that means the feeling underneath is too big for the container—and Petra, who had come for supper and was pretending very hard not to witness anything, reached over and squeezed my hand under the table, once, and let go.

Later, walking her back toward the keeper's house, we passed the churchyard, and I did what I always do now, which is nothing. We walked through the ground where fifty years of voices used to live, and I heard none of them, and Petra glanced at me sideways—she still checks, sometimes, the way you check a scarred knee before rain.

"Any of them tonight?" she asked.

"No," I said. And then, because I promised myself I'd stop lying: "Maybe. Far off. Like someone humming in another room. But it doesn't pull anymore. It's just... weather."

She nodded. We walked on. At her gate she stopped, and looked back up the valley at the border-ridge, where the new stones had begun to catch the starlight—real stars now, dozens of them, the sky healing overhead like skin.

"Brannic sent word," she said. "Down from the mountain. He says the court's regent is gone—truly gone, replaced, and the new one is..." She hesitated. "He says the new regent is asking questions about the valley. About the vote. About *consent*, Gideon. He says she wants to send an envoy. An honest one, he thinks. He thinks."

I stood at her gate in the dark, with my knuckles still raw from nine days of honest stone, and felt the silence inside me shift—not painfully, not like the old silences, but the way a house shifts when someone opens a door you'd painted shut.

"An envoy," I repeated.

"Due at the equinox," Petra said. "Spring. When we renew the wards." She smiled, and it was her complicated smile, the one with the old chains still faintly clinking somewhere behind it. "Apparently, Gideon Brightwater, the Undercourt would like to learn how a valley says yes."

I looked up at the returning stars, and at the dark seam beneath the mountain where a door stood open for the first time in two hundred years, and I thought of the little chair on our mantel with its unfinished seat, waiting to be filled.

"Well," I said. "Then we'd better teach them properly."
