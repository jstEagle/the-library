## 22. Ember and Morning

### Petra

One year is not enough time to rebuild a valley's soul, but it is apparently enough time for the valley to decide it wants a school.

I stand in the old ward-house—the same one where I found the ledger with Wren's name burning red off the page—and watch eleven children argue about consent.

"You can't just *take* a ward-oath," says Tam, who is nine and already has the moral rigidity of a magistrate. "That's the whole point. That's what Petra keeps saying."

"I'm not taking it," sulks Merrin, who is twelve and has more raw talent than I had at twenty and knows it. "I'm *offering* it. There's a difference."

"Is there?"

"Yes," I say, before the argument curdles into a fistfight. "There is, and it's the difference between everything that went wrong here and everything we're trying to make right." I hold out my hand. "Show me."

Merrin places her palm over mine. Her oath-mark is still unformed—a smudge of potential under the skin, like a bruise deciding what shape to be. In my day, in the order that trained me, we didn't ask. We measured children at seven, marked them at ten, bound them by sixteen, and called it an honor. I bore my oath-mark like a brand for fifteen years before I burned it off against a forbidden door beneath the mountain, and I would do it again, but I'd rather no one else ever have to.

"So," I tell her. "You offer your gift to the valley's wards. The valley offers its protection back. If either side breaks faith, the ward goes dark—honestly, visibly, so everyone knows. No one gets sold. No one gets spent. Understand?"

"What happens if I change my mind when I'm older?"

"Then you walk away, and we thank you for your service, and nobody hunts you across three kingdoms."

Merrin frowns. "That sounds made up."

"It is made up. I made it up last spring. That's how new things work." I let her go and turn to the rest of them, eleven faces ranging from solemn to skeptical, and I feel the old familiar vertigo—the one that hits every time I remember that the Undercourt trained me from childhood to be exactly this: a keeper of wards, a keeper of secrets, a keeper of other people's souls. They built me to serve their bargain. And now here I am, using everything they taught me to teach children they will never be allowed to touch.

The regent would find it funny, if the dead hadn't dragged him down into his own Ember. I choose to believe he finds it funny anyway. It improves my mornings.

"Again," I say. "From the beginning. And someone wake up Tam, he fell asleep standing up again."

"He does that," Merrin says. "It's his gift."

"It is *not* his gift—"

But Tam opens one eye and grins, and outside the window the morning comes up gold over Emberfall, and the stars are all still there, every one of them, including the small fierce one just north of the mountain's shoulder that the valley has already named. The children call it Wren's Star. Gideon pretends this doesn't undo him every time he hears it. He is very bad at pretending.

After lessons, I climb the path behind the ward-house to where the new stones stand. Honest stone, cut from the quarry above the falls, each one carved with the name of the person who consented to feed it—not their blood, not their soul, just their name, freely given, renewable every year at the autumn fair like a promise you get to keep making. The wards hum when I pass. Not the old hum, the one that felt like a held breath. This one sounds almost like contentment.

Almost like a harp string that hasn't snapped yet.

I put my hand on the nearest stone and say the thing I say every morning, quietly, because some habits of honesty deserve to be kept even when no one listens:

"My name is Petra Vane. I was a tithe-child. I helped forge the terms of the old bargain. I'm sorry. I'm staying to fix it."

The stone hums a little warmer. Or maybe that's just the sun. With wards, as with people, you can never entirely prove the warmth was meant for you. You just have to decide to believe it.

### Gideon

My workshop smells of cedar dust and beeswax and, faintly, of soup, because Brannic has decided that apprentices learn better fed and has taken over my stove without asking, which is very much what a disgraced underpriest does instead of apologizing for betraying you. He stirs. He hums hymns he's no longer allowed to sing. He has never once mentioned Ashmarket or the price of his priesthood, and I have never once asked, and between us this silence has somehow become a form of forgiveness. Some debts you retire rather than collect.

Six apprentices sit at my benches. A year ago, a bone-carver with my gift worked alone, behind a closed door, and told no one what he heard. Now the door stands open and the room is full of children pressing their palms to grave-tokens and going quiet with wonder.

"They're talking," whispers Odile, who is eight and hears the way I used to hear—everything, all at once, a river with no banks. "The lady in the token. She's saying she's sorry about the fence."

"The fence?" asks Joren, whose gift is gentler—he feels echoes rather than words, weather rather than rain.

"She never fixed her neighbor's fence after the flood. It bothered her for forty years." Odile looks up at me, stricken. "That's such a small thing to carry that long."

"That's most things," I tell her. "Most regrets are fences." I guide her hand to the carving knife. "So we listen, and we carve what they wanted said, and we give it to whoever's left. That's the whole trade. Small mercies, done carefully."

The dead murmur around us the way they always have—softly now, softer than before, since I gave most of my hearing to the Ember to buy Wren back. What remains is like hearing through a wall: voices without edges, kindnesses without names. I know what I lost. I also know what I bought. On the bad nights, when the silence presses close, I take out the last token I ever heard clearly and hold it until the missing voices feel less like theft and more like a payment honored.

On the good nights—which are most of them now—I don't need the tokens at all.

Because there is one voice I kept. One voice the Ember couldn't collect, because it was never sold in the first place; it was only borrowed, and borrowed things come home.

"Gideon." Wren's voice, from the doorway, amused. "You're teaching them to talk to fences again."

"Fences are people too," I say, not turning around, because if I turn around too fast she'll flicker, and I've learned to let her arrive at her own pace. My sister's ghost leans against my doorframe the way she leaned against everything—like the world was furniture she was fond of. She stayed a year past her release. Nobody knows why, least of all her. Ghosts don't get explanations. But she stayed, and the valley adjusted, and my mother set two chairs at the table again and stopped crying when she looked at either of them.

"Brannic's making soup," Wren reports. "He's making too much soup. He always makes too much soup."

"He's feeding six apprentices."

"He's feeding six apprentices and feeling guilty about something. Watch. He'll add dumplings."

He added dumplings. I have learned not to bet against the dead on matters of domestic intelligence.

### Petra

Evening finds us all at the Brightwater house, because evening always finds us all at the Brightwater house now; it stopped being an invitation somewhere around midwinter and became simply a fact of the valley, like weather.

Mother Brightwater—she insists I call her Sela, and I insist on forgetting, and this has become our longest-running joke—has set the table long. Wren's chair stands at the head, as it has every night for seven years. But tonight it isn't empty, and it isn't singular. Sela has pulled four more chairs up alongside it, because the table has outgrown the house the way the truth outgrew the valley: messily, and for the better.

Gideon sits at Wren's right. Brannic sits where he can reach the dumplings. Odile and Joren and Tam and Merrin crowd the bench, invited because Sela declared that apprentices who work all day eat free all evening, and because half of them have nowhere better to be, and because found family, I have learned, is mostly just a table with room made on purpose.

And Wren sits in her chair, translucent in the lamplight, telling the story of the time she convinced the entire harvest festival that the mayor's wig was haunted. It wasn't haunted. She had simply bribed the mayor's cat. The children are losing their minds. Even Gideon is laughing—that low, rusty laugh he seems to have relearned this year, syllable by syllable, like a language recovered from a burned book.

"Petra," Wren says, catching my eye across the table. "Tell them what the ward-keepers did to the festival bonfires. Tell them about the *blessing tongs*."

"There were no blessing tongs," I say.

"There were absolutely blessing tongs. You wore special gloves."

"We wore *work gloves*, because fire—"

"Ha! So there were tongs!"

The table roars. Under the noise, Wren's grin softens into something just for me, and I understand, finally, why she stayed. Not unfinished business. Not a debt. She stayed the way you linger at a window on a morning too beautiful to leave—because watching the people you love figure out how to be happy is its own kind of home, and ghosts, whatever else they are, are allowed to be homesick for the living.

Later, when the children have been sent home and the dishes are done and Sela has gone up to bed, Gideon and I walk out past the last house to the rise where you can see the whole valley laid out below—lamplit, warded, honest. Above the mountain, the stars burn in their hundreds. Wren's Star holds steady just north of the peak, small and stubborn, like its owner.

"Do you ever think about going back down?" I ask him. I don't mean it as temptation. I mean it the way you test a healed bone—gently, once a year, to make sure it set true.

He considers it honestly, which is the thing I love best about him. "Sometimes. When the silence gets loud." He turns the thought over. "But the door's not shut, Petra. That's the difference. Before, the mountain was a locked mouth. Now it's just... a door. Doors wait."

Below us, the new ward-stones catch the starlight and hold it, name by name, promise by promise. Somewhere in the dark, a child is probably practicing her oath-mark without supervision. Somewhere in my workshop, the ledger of the old bargain lies open to its final page, where I wrote, in ordinary black ink, the only entry that matters anymore:

*Debt paid. No further obligations. All souls accounted for.*

All but the ones who chose to stay. And those were never debts at all.

"Come on," Gideon says, and offers me his arm like the valley's least graceful gentleman. "Sela saves us the ends of the bread if we're quick, and Wren will tell the cat story wrong if we're not there to correct her."

"She tells it wrong on purpose."

"Of course she does. She's dead, not honest."

We walk down toward the lamplight together, and behind us the mountain stands quiet, its deep roads dark and patient, its door neither open nor closed—just waiting, the way doors do, for whoever next has a reason to knock.

And far overhead, Wren's Star flickers—once, twice—almost, I'd swear, like laughter.
