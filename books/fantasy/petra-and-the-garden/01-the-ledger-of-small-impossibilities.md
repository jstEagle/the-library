## 1. The Ledger of Small Impossibilities

The cellar of the Guildhall smelled of tallow, cold stone, and the particular sweetness of things that should not exist. Edmund Whitlock had worked beneath the Guild of Small Impossibilities for six years now, and he had learned that the smell was the first thing visitors noticed and the last thing they ever forgot—the scent of a jar of last year's sunlight, perhaps, or a bottle of borrowed rain, or the small clay pot on the third shelf in which someone's grandmother's luck had been kept warm through a hard winter.

He sat at his desk with its single candle, its inkwell capped against the damp, and he balanced the books.

This was the work. Not the trading itself—that belonged to the brokers upstairs with their velvet voices and their measured smiles—but the accounting. Every impossibility the guild bought, sold, lent, or held in trust passed across Edmund's desk in ink. A jar of last year's sunlight, traded by a widow in the Marsh Quarter for one hour of her husband's voice. A grief, borrowed from a mason who could not carry it another day, entered at fair weight and stored in the west cabinet until someone came willing to feel it for him. The guild's motto, stitched above the cellar door in thread gone silver with age: *Everything may be given. Nothing is free.*

Edmund believed in the ledger the way other men believed in scripture. Numbers did not lie. Ink did not flatter. When a trade was written down honestly, it stayed honest forever, and there was a comfort in that which he had never found anywhere else—not in his mother's house before the fever took her, not in the streets where a boy with quick fingers learned quickly how little honesty was worth. Here, everything balanced. Here, if you were careful, nothing vanished.

He dipped his pen and began with the morning's entries.

*Received, the fourth of Frostmonth: one jar, sealed wax, containing sunlight of the previous summer. Condition: good. Slight fading at the corners of the light, as is natural. Taken in trade from Widow Ansel of the Marsh Quarter against one hour of her late husband's voice, to be drawn from stores.*

His pen moved steadily. He wrote the condition of the jar, the seal's impression, the faint warmth still rising off the glass when the broker had carried it down. He wrote the price paid and the margin held. He wrote the small marginal note—*widow wept; charge no handling fee*—that Master Corvus pretended not to see and never once reprimanded him for.

Then he turned the page to check the running balance against the master inventory, and his pen stopped.

There was an entry below the widow's. Dated the same day. Written in a hand he knew better than his own face, because it *was* his own face, rendered in ink: the slight forward slant, the looped descenders, the way he crossed his *t*s with a single impatient stroke.

*Paid out, the fourth of Frostmonth: one memory, minor, of a green door. To an unnamed party. Terms undisclosed.*

Edmund read it three times.

He had made no such entry. He had been at his desk all morning—he had been at his desk since the bell rang Prime—and the page had been blank below the widow's line when he turned to it not ten minutes past. He was certain. Certainty was his profession.

He looked at the entry again. The ink was dry. It had been dry for some time, if the way it had settled into the paper's grain meant anything, and yet the date was today's.

"Master Corvus," he called up the stair, and heard the old man's footsteps come slow and deliberate, each one placed like a coin.

Corvus filled the doorway the way he always did, stooped under the low lintel, his grey beard trimmed close, his eyes the color of watered wine. Forty years a ledger-keeper before Edmund; sixty years in the guild before that. He looked at the open book, then at Edmund's face, and sighed the sigh of a man who has answered this question many times in many decades.

"You've found one," he said.

"Found one?"

"A stray entry." Corvus came down the two steps and stood beside him, smelling of pipe smoke and dried lavender, which he tucked into his sleeves against the damp. "It happens. Old trades surface. The books remember things the traders forget." He reached out and turned the page back, studying the phantom line without touching it. "A memory of a green door. Small thing. Someone will come asking after it eventually, and when they do, we'll know where it went."

"But I didn't write it."

"No," Corvus agreed mildly. "You didn't."

"Then who—"

"The guild writes sometimes, boy. It's an old building full of old bargains. Paper remembers." He tapped the margin with one knuckle. "You'll find others, over the years. Entries in hands long dead. Trades nobody living recalls making. You learn to let them be. A ledger-keeper who chases every ghost in the ink goes mad by midwinter, and I say that with feeling."

He said it lightly, but Edmund caught the thing underneath it—the careful flatness, the way the old man's eyes did not quite meet his own. In six years, Corvus had taught him everything: how to weigh a sorrow, how to price a dream so it neither bankrupted the seller nor cheapened the goods, how to hold his hand steady when a client wept across the desk. He had never lied to Edmund. He had never needed to.

Until, possibly, now.

"I'm not tired," Edmund said.

"I didn't say you were."

"You were about to."

Corvus almost smiled. "I was about to tell you to eat something. You skip meals when the month-end comes, and hungry men see ghosts in their own handwriting." He straightened with a small crack of his spine. "Balance the rest. Lock the west cabinet when you go. And Edmund—" he paused at the stair, half-turned, the candlelight catching the deep lines of his face, "—if you find more of them, bring them to me before you bring them to anyone else. Before you even bring them to yourself, if you can manage it. Some pages are best read slowly."

Then he was gone, his footsteps climbing away into the sounds of the guildhall waking above: brokers' voices, the creak of the great door, the smell of bread from the kitchen drifting down the stairwell like an apology.

Edmund sat alone with the books.

He finished the morning's balancing because that was who he was—if the world tilted, you wrote it down until it steadied—and then, because he was also honest with himself in the way that mattered most, he pulled the older volumes from their shelf and began to search.

He told himself it was diligence. Month-end reconciliation required checking prior volumes for carried balances, and that was true, and he used the truth the way a man uses a lantern: to give himself permission to look where he wanted to look.

The ledgers went back a long way. The guild was old—older than the current king's grandfather, older than the city walls in their present form—and the earliest volumes were bound in leather gone dark and soft as soil. Edmund worked backward through them, decade by decade, the candle burning lower, the cellar's quiet pressing in around him until he could hear his own heartbeat and the distant tick of the building settling overhead.

In the volume from ninety years past, he found a note in the margin of a routine trade—a jar of courage, sold cheap—that read simply: *the ink moved again tonight.* No signature. The handwriting shook.

In the volume from a hundred and forty years past, whole pages of entries appeared in no identifiable hand at all, the letters tall and narrow, the dates impossible: trades recorded on days that had not yet arrived when the ink dried. He checked three times. The dates were future dates. One of them, he realized with a coldness spreading through his chest, was a day next spring.

And in the oldest volume of all—the founding ledger, kept behind a chain and lock that Corvus had never given him the key to, but whose shelf he dusted every week and whose chain he had long ago learned to lift without unfastening—he found what he would spend the rest of his life trying to forget.

Entries in a dead man's hand. He knew the name from the flyleaf: Aldous Vane, First Keeper, dead two hundred years and more. The handwriting was beautiful and terrible, each letter placed with the precision of a man writing under some vast and patient pressure. And the entries were dated forward. Days ahead. Months. The final entry in Vane's hand sat alone at the bottom of the last leaf, dated—for Edmund counted twice, and then a third time, his lips moving silently—in the winter to come.

*The guildhall burns. The ledger survives.*

Below it, in fresher ink, in handwriting Edmund knew intimately, in his own slanting, impatient, careful hand:

*And the boy keeps it.*

The candle guttered. Somewhere above, the guildhall went on with its ordinary evening—bread, conversation, the soft commerce of impossible things—and Edmund sat in the cellar with the founding book open before him, listening to the sound of his own breathing, understanding for the first time in his orderly, balanced life that the numbers had been lying to him all along.

Or else, said a quieter and far worse thought, they had been telling the truth, and the truth was simply this: something in the guild knew his name, and had known it for two hundred years.

He closed the book. He lifted the chain back into place. His hands, he noticed distantly, were perfectly steady—which was either the discipline of his profession or the beginning of something he did not yet have a word for.

Outside, snow had begun to fall on the city, the first of the season, and in the orchard above the cellar—Petra's orchard, the impossible one, where trees fruited out of season and the gardener walked at midnight talking softly to branches—one of the trees, the old ones said, grew leaves of paper.

Edmund had never thought much about that tree.

He thought about it now.
