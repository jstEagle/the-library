## 2. What the Crown Proclaims

The herald came on the first morning of true frost, when the orchard wore its silver like a borrowed coat.

Petra felt him before she heard him—felt the wrongness of iron shoes on cobbles that had only ever known gardeners' boots and children's feet. She was in the high rows, tying back the winter pear branches with strips of soft linen, because a tree that bears fruit out of season must be treated gently, like an old woman's hands. The pears themselves were warm. They always were. Each one held a small July inside it, and when you cupped them they hummed against your palms like sleeping birds.

She heard the herald's voice before she saw his banner: a voice trained to crack stone, pitched to carry over market crowds and across parade grounds, and it did not belong in this quarter where voices were kept low out of courtesy to the growing things.

"Hear ye! Hear ye! By decree of His Majesty, sovereign of all the realm—"

The words rolled down the lane and up the walls of the guildhall, and Petra stood among her warm pears and listened to the kingdom change shape around her.

"—a general inquiry into persons practicing arts unnatural, being sorcery, hedge-craft, gift-work, or any commerce with powers not sanctioned by crown and church. Such persons are commanded to present themselves for examination—"

*Examination.* She turned the word over. It had teeth in it somewhere; she could feel them.

"—under penalty of fire."

A murmur went through the market street below, the particular murmur of a crowd doing arithmetic. Petra knew that sound. She had heard it in three other centuries, in two other kingdoms, under other banners with other beasts stitched upon them. It was the sound of neighbors counting neighbors. Of a woman glancing at the widow who never seemed to age. Of a man remembering that the miller's bread rose too well, that the midwife's hands were always warm, that the boy down the lane could find lost things a little too easily.

Fire, said the herald, as though he were announcing a tax on wool.

Petra set down her linen strips. She pressed one palm flat against the pear tree's trunk, feeling the slow pulse beneath the bark—the guildhall's oldest resident, older than the hall itself, older than most of the elders who would presently be voting on how best to hide from a king.

"They've come at last," she told the tree. "Well. We knew they would."

The tree said nothing, but a pear dropped into her hand, warm as a held secret, and she took it as agreement.

* * *

The council chamber smelled of tallow and old arguments.

Petra sat at the far end of the long table, in the chair nobody remembered assigning to her, which was how she preferred it. Around her sat the eleven elders of the Guild of Small Impossibilities, wrapped in wool against the cold that the hearth-fire seemed unable to fully address—as though the room itself had already begun holding its breath.

Edmund Whitlock sat near the middle, ledger open before him, quill moving in small precise strokes. He was the youngest keeper, and he took minutes the way some men took vows: completely. Petra had watched him for years now, this careful boy with ink-stained fingers and a memory like a locked drawer, and she had come to think of him as the guild's conscience in training—not yet called upon, but growing.

"Master Corvel will speak for the motion," said Elder Hesse, and Corvel rose, a broad man gone soft at the edges, his voice the voice of reasonableness itself.

"We scatter," Corvel said. "That is the whole of it. We have done it before. The guild has survived every hunt by becoming no one. We close the trades. We seal the cellar. We send our members into the towns with new names and quiet hands, and in ten years, twenty, we return and reopen. The ledgers wait. The gifts keep. Invisibility has preserved us for two hundred years—"

"Three hundred," Petra said quietly.

Corvel paused. "I beg your pardon?"

"The guild is three hundred years old. You say two hundred because the founding records begin with Aldous Vane, and Vane burned what came before him." She folded her hands. "But go on. Scatter. Hide. Become no one."

"It has worked," Corvel said, with the patience of a man addressing a beloved aunt who has begun saying strange things.

"It has worked *so far*." Petra rose. Her knees complained; she let them. "Master Corvel, I have watched this guild hide four times. In my lifetime. Do you understand what I am telling you? Not in the guild's lifetime. In *mine*. I was here when we hid from Queen Maren's inquisitors. I was here when we hid from the Purification of the eastern provinces. Each time we scattered, each time we sealed the doors and waited, and each time we came back smaller. Fewer members. Fewer gifts. Fewer willing to swear the oaths. Hiding does not preserve us, Master Corvel. Hiding *erodes* us. We are a candle burning at both ends and calling the shortening prudence."

"The alternative?" Elder Hesse again, dry as August. "You propose we *march*? Present ourselves for examination?"

"I propose we stop pretending the problem is attention." Petra walked the length of the table slowly, and the elders' eyes followed her the way birds watch a snake—not in fear exactly, but in the unwilling fascination of things that sense an old pattern waking. "Ask yourselves why the crown hunts us now. After three hundred years of leaving us be. Ask what changed."

Silence. Somewhere above them, faint through the floorboards, the impossible orchard creaked in the wind.

"The king changed," Edmund said.

It was barely a whisper, but the chamber heard it. He looked up from his ledger as if surprised to find his own mouth open. "Forgive me. But the entries—I've been reviewing the accounts, and the tribute schedules, the payments the guild makes quarterly, they were... amended. This summer. Without any elder's seal. The amounts doubled, and the delivery point moved from the border shrine to—" He stopped. Swallowed. "To the capital. To the palace itself."

"And you didn't think to mention this?" Hesse demanded.

"I mentioned it to Master Ovid. He said the amendment was routine." Edmund's jaw tightened. "It is not routine. Nothing about those numbers is routine. And now the herald comes, and the hunt begins, and we vote to *hide*—from what? From a king who suddenly wants more than we've ever paid, and sends witch-finders to collect?"

"He's right," Petra said. "And he is younger than every person in this room, which means he is the only one still capable of being astonished."

Corvel spread his hands. "Even granting all this—even if the crown's hunger has grown—what would you have us do? Fight?"

"No. Fighting is just hiding with louder instruments." Petra reached the window and looked out at the grey sky, at the smoke of the city rising straight in the cold air. "I would have us *go*. To the capital. To the source. Something wears the crown, my friends, and it has worn it long enough that it has forgotten it is wearing anything at all. I have known since before most of you were born that our power is not ours. Every jar of sunlight we sell, every grief we carry, every small impossibility—we are not makers. We are *middlemen*. And the debt is coming due, and the collector has arrived."

"You speak treason," Hesse said, but softly, the way one speaks of weather.

"I speak arithmetic," Petra answered. "The same arithmetic young Whitlock keeps. Read your own ledgers, Elders. Follow the tributes backward. I promise you they do not end at any shrine. They end at a throne."

The silence that followed was different from the earlier silences. It had weight. Several elders looked down at their hands. One or two looked, briefly and guiltily, at Edmund—and Edmund, catching it, bent lower over his ledger as though the pages might shelter him.

"The motion stands," Corvel said at last, heavily. "We scatter. We hide. Those in favor?"

Nine hands rose. Hesse's. Corvel's. Others that trembled slightly and rose anyway.

Petra did not raise hers. She stood at the window with her back to the vote, watching the frost climb the glass in fern-patterns, and she thought: *nine hands, nine candles pinched out, and the dark calls it wisdom.*

"The motion carries," Corvel said. "We disperse within the fortnight. The guildhall will be sealed—"

"I will not sign," Petra said, without turning.

"Pardon?"

"My name comes off no register. My door stays open. Seal your cellars and burn your correspondence if it makes you feel safe, but I am an old woman who talks to trees, Elders, and I have been invisible for three hundred years." She turned then, and let them see her face—all of it, every year of it, the way she almost never allowed. "Invisibility is not a strategy. It is a habit. And habits, like gardens, must eventually be broken ground, or nothing new grows."

No one spoke. The tallow flame guttered in the draft from the window.

It was Edmund who broke it—Edmund, who should have kept his eyes on his minutes, who instead closed his ledger with a small decisive snap and said, in a voice that shook only slightly:

"I'll help her."

Corvel stared at him. "Whitlock. You're a child. You balance books."

"Yes," Edmund said. "And someone in this guild ought to know exactly what we've been balancing, and where the payments go, and what happens when the account comes due." He looked at Petra, and there was something in his face that made her chest ache—an old thing, a familiar thing, the look of the young deciding to be brave before they understand the price. "The ledgers don't lie, Elder. People do. Ledgers just wait."

Petra smiled at him then, small and sad and proud.

*There*, she thought. *There is the fracture. Nine hands vote for the dark, and one boy puts down his pen.*

* * *

That evening she walked the orchard rows alone, as the light died blue behind the western roofs.

Frost had come fully now. It sheathed every branch, every trellis, every impossible vine, so that the whole garden glittered like a thing built of glass and patience. Here a bush that flowered only in the presence of lies, its blooms shut tight tonight—for the garden, at least, told no falsehoods. There the well whose water tasted of whatever the drinker had lost. The beds where they grew forgiveness, slow and difficult, one leaf per season.

At the orchard's heart stood the oldest tree, the one no member but the founders was permitted to approach, and Petra stopped before it as she always stopped: with reverence, and with dread.

The tree bore pages. It always had. White leaves, thin as onion skin, and on them, in ink that deepened or faded with the truth's weight, grew the record of the founding—the bargain struck beneath the mountain, the thing that had been bound, the words that held it.

She did not read them tonight. She had read them enough for one lifetime, and then some.

Instead she laid her palm against the frozen bark, and beneath the ice she felt the pulse—slow, patient, older than the guild, older than the kingdom. The same pulse she had felt in a throne room once, three hundred years ago, when she had helped do a terrible necessary thing to a friend, and had spent every year since telling herself it was mercy.

"They're scattering," she told the tree. "They think hiding is living."

The wind moved through the high branches, and a single page detached itself, spiraling down through the frost-light like a feather. Petra caught it.

She read it once. Then she read it again, standing very still while the cold climbed her sleeves and the first stars came out over the guildhall roof.

Then she folded the page carefully, put it inside her coat, next to her heart, and began walking—not toward her rooms, but toward the cellar stairs, toward the ledgers, toward the one young man in the guild who still knew how to be astonished.

Because the page had given her a date.

And the date was soon.
