## 3. Entries in a Dead Man's Hand

Edmund did not sleep that night. He told himself it was the cold, the cellar draft finding the gap beneath his door, but he knew the shape of the lie as well as he knew the shape of his own signature—and it was the signature that kept him awake.

He rose before the bells, dressed in the dark by touch, and carried a candle down the spiral stair to the cellar where the ledgers lived. The Guild of Small Impossibilities kept its accounts in a vaulted room older than any charter anyone could produce: stone walls sweating salt, shelves of black oak cut from trees no one remembered felling, and on those shelves, two hundred years of trades recorded in ink that never faded and never smudged, no matter how carelessly a tired clerk handled the page.

The ledger-keeper's oath was simple. *What is traded must be written. What is written must be true.* Edmund had sworn it at fourteen, kneeling on cold flagstones with Master Corvin's hand heavy on his shoulder, and in nine years he had never once broken it. The books balanced. They always balanced. That was the whole comfort of his life—the arithmetic of wonder, tidy as bread and twice as reliable.

Except for the entries.

He set his candle on the reading desk and opened the current ledger to the place where he had stopped yesterday, heart hammering in a way that felt absurd for a man alone with paperwork. There they were, three lines down from his last honest entry:

*Traded: one memory of the sea, held forty years, to a fisherman's widow who wished to forget drowning. Received: her talent for mending nets, unbroken.*

The handwriting was his own. Not similar—*his own*, every loop and ligature, the small hesitation-stroke he made before the word *Received* because his master had beaten the habit into him and then, years later, confessed he'd invented it himself out of superstition. No forger alive could have copied it. No forger would have thought to.

And the date was wrong. The entry was dated the fourteenth of Thawmonth—four months from now.

Edmund sat down slowly on the clerk's stool, its leather seat worn to the shape of every keeper who had sat there before him, and looked at the impossible line until the candle guttered. Then, because he was Edmund Whitlock, youngest ledger-keeper of the guild, and because fear had never once in his life been permitted to interrupt procedure, he did what a ledger-keeper does.

He began to audit.

* * *

He worked through the morning while the guildhall woke above him. He heard Petra's boots on the cellar stairs at dawn—she came down sometimes to ask the orchard's roots for cuttings, though the orchard grew three floors up and its roots went wherever roots pleased—and heard her pause at the bottom, sensing him among the shelves like a fox sensing another fox.

"You're here early," she said. Her voice was dry leaves and river stones; everything about Petra sounded like something weathered. "Trouble sleeping?"

"Ledger trouble," he said, not looking up.

"Ah." A pause. "The worst kind. The kind that's also soul trouble." She did not press. She never pressed. She left a sprig of something green and impossible on the corner of his desk—he didn't see what, only smelled it later, rain on hot stone—and climbed back into the dark.

By midmorning he had found eleven phantom entries. All in his hand. All dated in the future, scattered across the coming year like seeds. And all of them, when he checked the guild's historical registers against them, corresponded to trades made long ago—trades recorded in the founding ledgers, in a hand that was not his but which his own handwriting resembled the way a son resembles a father.

Aldous Vane. First Keeper of the Guild of Small Impossibilities. Dead two hundred years, if the portrait in the great hall could be believed—a narrow-faced man with ink-stained fingers and eyes that seemed less painted than *waiting*. Edmund had walked past that portrait every day for nine years. He had never noticed before how much his own reflection had begun to look like it.

He pulled the founding ledger down with both hands—it was heavier than any book had a right to be, as though the paper itself were dense with what it held—and laid it open beside the current one. Vane's entries marched down the page in that old brown ink: trades of sunlight and sorrow, of first words and last breaths, of things that had no business being owned by anyone. And beside them, in fresh black ink, in Edmund's hand, the same trades again—but with dates that hadn't happened yet.

It wasn't forgery. It wasn't haunting either, or not the kind the stories told about. It was more like an echo running backward. Or a debt being called forward.

His candle had burned to a stub. He replaced it without noticing he'd done so. Somewhere above, the elders were arguing again about the herald's proclamation—he could hear the muffled rise and fall of voices through the floorboards, fear dressed up as procedure—and below, in the salt-sweat silence of the archive, Edmund turned page after page and felt the floor tilt gently under his certainty.

Then he found the last entry.

It sat alone on a otherwise blank page, near the end of the ledger, in his handwriting but larger than usual, written with the deliberate care of a man carving rather than writing:

*The guildhall burns. The ledger survives.*

No trade. No received. No price listed. Just that, and beneath it a date: the ninth of Frostmonth. Nine days away.

* * *

He did not remember climbing the stairs. He found himself standing in the great hall with the founding ledger still under his arm, staring up at Aldous Vane's portrait while winter light fell through the high windows in pale slanted bars. Dust hung in the light like something suspended between decisions.

"Master Corvin," he said, when the old man shuffled through, late for whatever the elders were failing to decide upstairs. "I need to show you something."

Corvin was seventy, bent like a question mark, and had taught Edmund everything worth knowing about ink. He squinted at the ledger, then at the current book Edmund thrust at him, and his face did something complicated—went through surprise, arrived somewhere past it, and settled on a weariness so old it looked geological.

"Hm," said Corvin.

"*Hm?* Master, these are my entries. Trades I never made. Dated in the future. Dated—" His voice cracked and he hated it. "Dated nine days from now. It says the hall burns."

Corvin lowered himself onto the bench beneath the portrait with the groan of a man whose knees had opinions. For a long moment he said nothing, just turned the pages with a scholar's delicacy, and Edmund watched him and understood, with a slow cold settling, that his mentor was not surprised either.

"You've seen this before," Edmund said.

"I've seen *versions* of this before." Corvin tapped the phantom entries with one yellowed nail. "Every keeper finds them eventually. Usually later than you. Usually after they've started making trades they shouldn't." He sighed. "The guild's power isn't ours, boy. You know we say it's borrowed. We say it lightly, the way you'd say borrowed about a cup of sugar. But a debt is a debt, and debts keep their own calendar. Sometimes the ledger keeps it too."

"What does that mean?"

"It means," Corvin said, closing the book with a soft terrible finality, "that the founding bargain is not finished with us. That the thing we trade from remembers what it gave, and expects return, and writes its expectations down in whatever hand it can reach." He looked up, and his eyes in the winter light were the color of watered ink. "Yours, apparently. It likes yours. I never asked why. I was afraid of the answer."

"The hall burns," Edmund repeated. "In nine days."

"So it says."

"And you're going to tell me it might be wrong. Fatigue. A trick of the light. Too much time in the cellar."

"I was going to tell you," Corvin said, "that prophecy is just accounting done badly, and that the wise response to a frightening number is to check the sum yourself." He stood, joints crackling like green wood in a fire—a comparison Edmund would think about later, much later, and flinch. "But I'm old, Edmund, and old men check sums slowly. You're young, and you have good eyes, and you've already read further into the founding ledgers than I ever dared." He pressed the book back into Edmund's arms. "So check. And when you find what Vane hid—and you will find it, because it wants finding—come find me before you tell anyone else. Not the elders. *Me.*"

"Why not the elders?"

Corvin glanced up at the portrait, and for a moment the two old men regarded each other across two hundred years, and Edmund saw that Corvin was afraid of it. Actually afraid. Of paint and varnish and a dead man's waiting eyes.

"Because," Corvin said quietly, "the elders will vote on it. And votes are how frightened people hide from the truth. Whereas you—" he patted Edmund's cheek with a hand that trembled slightly, "—you are the kind of fool who balances books at four in the morning. The truth will be safe with you."

* * *

He went back down. Of course he went back down. The cellar at midday was dimmer than at dawn, the candle-flames doubling themselves in the polished spines of two centuries of ledgers, and Edmund worked through the afternoon with the focused calm of a man dismantling a trap he is already standing in.

Vane had hidden things well—but Vane had hidden them two hundred years ago, and paper is patient, and patience is a ledger-keeper's whole religion. In the binding of the third founding volume, behind a false spine glued with something that still smelled faintly of honey, Edmund found loose pages. Dozens of them. Vane's hand throughout, but different now—faster, wilder, the writing of a man who has stopped pretending to himself.

They were letters. Unsent. Addressed variously to *the reader*, to *whoever inherits this*, and once, in a line that made Edmund's neck prickle, to *the boy with my hands*.

Most of it was rambling—apologies, invocations, fragments of a bargain half-recorded, terms struck with something Vane referred to only as *the Patient One beneath the crown*, a phrase Edmund read five times before its meaning landed and knocked the breath out of him. Beneath the crown. Under the king. Inside him, maybe. Wearing him.

But near the bottom of the stack, one page was different. Calm. Final. Written in the large deliberate hand that matched the last ledger entry exactly, as though Vane had known that someday a frightened young clerk would need to hear a dead man speak plainly:

*I have seen how it ends, because endings are the only thing the Patient One lends freely. The hall will burn—I chose to write it down so that someone would believe it in time. Believe it, boy. Do not waste your nine days on disbelief. The fire is not the danger. The fire is only the crown's way of closing an account. What matters is what walks out of the smoke carrying the truth of the bargain. Save the ledgers. Save the gardener. She remembers the words, and the words are the only key ever cut for that lock.*

*You will want to save your gift instead. Everyone does. But listen: the gift is the chain. It was always the chain.*

*Come to the capital before midwinter. Unbind him. And when you stand in the throne room deciding what you are willing to lose—remember that I am the proof of what keeping it costs.*

Below the signature, in smaller script, almost an afterthought, almost gentle:

*P.S. Check the orchard. The tree keeps the rest.*

Edmund sat in the dark among the ledgers for a long time, the page trembling very slightly in his hands, listening to the muffled argument of the elders overhead and the wind rising off the snowfields beyond the city walls. Nine days. The ninth of Frostmonth.

Above him, faint through the stone, he heard Petra's boots cross the cellar stairs, pausing—as she always paused—at the top step, as if listening for something growing in the dark below.

He took the candle and the letters and went up after her.
