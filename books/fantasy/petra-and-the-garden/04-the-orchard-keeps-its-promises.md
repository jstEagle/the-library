## 4. The Orchard Keeps Its Promises

The bells began at dawn, and Petra knew before she opened her eyes that they were not the market bells.

She had lived long enough to hear a city change its voice. Cities rang for weddings, for fires, for the death of kings, and each of those had its own music. This was none of them. This was the sound of a city holding its breath — the great bell of Saint Verrin struck three times and then left to die away, unanswered, as though even the bell-ringer had decided silence was safer than song.

Witch-hunters at the walls.

Petra rose in the dark of her room above the orchard, dressed without light, and stood a while at the window with her hands flat against the cold glass. Below her, the guildhall's impossible orchard lay silvered with frost, every tree bearing what it bore: the plum tree that fruited only in other people's dreams, the rowan hung with small brass bells instead of berries, the apple whose blossoms opened backward, petal by petal folding themselves into bud again as though the tree had thought better of spring. Two hundred years she had tended them. Two hundred years, and the frost lay on them now like any ordinary frost, indifferent and white, and she found herself grieving that already, though nothing had yet been lost.

They would come with fire. They always came with fire. Fire was the one honest thing about the crown's new piety.

She went down through the sleeping hall, past the elders' chamber where the vote of two nights ago still hung in the air like smoke — *scatter, hide, become no one* — and out into the orchard where the cold took her breath and gave it back to her as a small ghost.

Edmund was already there.

Of course he was. The boy had not slept properly since the phantom entries; she had watched him at supper pushing his food around his plate the way he pushed numbers around a ledger page, looking for the line that didn't balance. He stood among the trees in his too-thin coat, ledger clutched under one arm like a shield, and when he saw her he did not pretend to be doing anything else.

"The bells," he said.

"I heard them."

"They're at the walls. The gate-warden's boy told me they rode in under the king's banner at first light. Inquisitor's men. He said—" Edmund stopped, swallowed. "He said they're asking after the gifted house by house."

"Then we have perhaps a day," Petra said. "Perhaps less." She looked at him a long moment — this youngest of the ledger-keepers, nineteen years old and carrying the whole arithmetic of the guild's impossible trades in his careful head, and carrying something else besides, though he did not yet know it. She had promised herself she would not be the one to tell him. But promises, she had learned across two centuries, are debts wearing kinder clothes, and the orchard had never once let her keep a secret from it.

"Come," she said. "There is something you must see. Something you should have seen the day you joined us."

* * *

The heart of the orchard was not marked on any plan of the guildhall. It sat behind the espaliered pears, down a path of stepping-stones that appeared only for those who already knew where they were going — an old courtesy of the founder's design, so that the innocent would simply find themselves walking elsewhere and never know they had turned aside.

At the path's end stood the oldest tree in the garden.

It did not look old. That was the first strangeness, and Petra watched Edmund feel it, the way one feels a wrong note in a familiar song. Every other tree in the orchard wore its age openly — gnarled, lichen-crusted, bent like old women into the wind. This tree stood straight and pale-barked and young, its leaves a green so deep it seemed lit from within, and around its roots the snow did not lie at all, though the frost had taken everything else to the doorstep.

"What is it?" Edmund whispered.

"A paper-mulberry," Petra said. "Or it was, once. Before." She laid her palm against the trunk, and the bark was warm, faintly, the way a sleeping animal is warm. "The founder planted it the year the guild was founded. Or rather — the founder did not plant it. It grew where the bargain was struck. Trees remember where things happen, if the happening is heavy enough. Ask any gallows hill why nothing grows there."

Edmund came closer. And then he saw what grew on it, and Petra saw the color leave his face by degrees, like water draining from a basin.

Pages.

Not leaves. The branches carried leaves, yes, ordinary green leaves along their lower lengths — but higher up, where the morning light came through the canopy, the tree bore paper. White pages, hundreds of them, fluttering gently in a wind that touched nothing else in the garden, each one attached by a stem of pale fiber, each one covered edge to edge in writing.

"The ledgers record trades," Edmund said. His voice had gone thin. "I've read every volume in the cellar. There's no—"

"No, there is no volume for this one," Petra said. "This tree keeps its own accounts. It grows a page whenever the truth of us grows heavier than we can carry. Come. Read the lowest branch. The oldest fruit ripens lowest, like any fruit."

He reached up with both hands, and the page came free of its stem with a sound like a held breath released, and he held it in the grey morning light and read aloud, slowly, because the hand was old and strange:

*We were seven. We were desperate. The winter of the Long Frost had killed one child in four, and the granaries were ash, and the king — our king, our friend — came to us weeping and said, I have a gift inside me that can feed them all, but I cannot carry it alone.*

*So we helped him carry it. That is the founding truth of the Guild of Small Impossibilities: we did not steal power. We shared a burden. Seven pairs of hands beneath one unbearable weight.*

*And then the weight taught us how sweet it was.*

* * *

Petra let him read. She walked the rows of the older pages while he worked downward through the tree's long memory, and the frost breathed off the warm ground around the roots, and somewhere beyond the walls the bells began again, three strikes, silence, three strikes, a city counting itself.

She remembered the winter those first pages described. Of course she did. She had been there. That was the thing the elders voted to scatter rather than say aloud, the thing she had kept folded up inside herself for two hundred years like a letter never sent: that the gardener who tended the guildhall's orchard had planted it, and had been young once, and had loved a king.

*We bound him,* the pages said, in the handwriting of the second year. *Do not call it anything gentler. We bound him. The gift was eating him alive — it would have eaten the world — and binding was the only mercy we knew that preserved both the man and the city. But a binding wants an anchor, and the only anchor strong enough was the debt itself. So we made the guild the keeper of the debt. Every gift we trade, every jar of sunlight, every borrowed grief, every small impossibility — each one is a payment made on what we owe him. The interest on our mercy. We tell ourselves it is charity. It is installment.*

*And the crown sits empty above him, and the kingdom thinks its king is merely quiet, and we grow rich on the overflow of a chained god, and every year the tree grows another page because the truth gets heavier, and we get better at not reading it.*

Edmund had gone very still among the roots. When he finally spoke, his voice was steady in the way of a man standing on ice and listening to it speak.

"The entries in my handwriting. In the cellar. Dated in the future."

"Yes."

"That's the debt, isn't it. Writing itself forward. The guild doesn't just pay the debt — the debt keeps books of its own." He looked up at her, and his eyes were wet and furious and terribly awake. "The final entry. 'The guildhall burns. The ledger survives.' You've read it. You've known."

"I have known since the night the herald proclaimed the hunt," Petra said. "I have known longer than that, if I am honest, which I am trying, at my age, to learn to be. The tree showed me a month ago. I told no one, because telling would make it true, and because—" She stopped. Breathed. The cold air tasted of snow coming. "Because I am the last of the seven, Edmund, and I have spent two hundred years being the keeper of a chain, and some part of me hoped that if I said nothing, the fire might pass over us the way storms pass over houses that have never once been struck."

"And now?"

"Now the hunters are at the walls, and hoping has failed, as it always fails, and all that is left is what we do next." She took his arm — the boy was shivering, though whether from cold or revelation she could not say — and drew him to the highest branch, where the newest page hung, still pale, still soft, its ink barely dried. "Read this one. Not the old truth. The new one."

He reached, hesitated, reached again. The page came free. He read it silently first — she watched his lips move — and then, when she waited, aloud, and his voice cracked in the middle like green wood in a fire:

*"The debt can be ended. What seven bound together, one may loose — but only in the presence of the bound, and only by the hand that holds the binding words, and only before midwinter, while the crown-thing sleeps deepest in the frozen dark. The gardener remembers the words. The ledger-keeper knows the price. Between them stands everything the guild ever was, and it will burn rather than be unmade. Choose quickly. The bells are ringing."*

Silence, except the wind in the paper-leaves, which sounded almost like applause, or like rain on a roof, or like a great many people turning pages all at once.

"The binding words," Edmund said hoarsely. "You have them. In your memory."

"I have had them in my memory for two hundred years," Petra said. "Every planting season, every pruning, every harvest, I have carried them the way one carries a stone in the shoe — small, and always there, and shaping every step. Your phantom entries, Edmund. Your handwriting that isn't yours. Did you never wonder why the debt chose *you* to write through? Why the youngest, the most ordinary, the least gifted of us?"

He shook his head.

"Because inheritance skips the mighty," she said. "It always has. The gift goes where it will be noticed least and needed most. The tree chose you the day you signed your name in the guild book. I watched it drop a leaf. I told myself it meant nothing." She smiled, and it hurt, the way old smiles do. "Trees keep their promises, Edmund. All of them. Even the ones we wish they'd break."

Beyond the orchard wall, far off toward the eastern gate, a thin thread of black smoke stood up against the white sky. Then another. Then, closer, much closer, a shout — human voices, many of them, and the smell of torch-pitch riding the wind over the frost.

Edmund looked at the smoke. Then at the tree. Then at her.

"Petra," he said. "How long do we have?"

She counted the voices, the way she had once counted seasons.

"Less time than the tree says," she said. "Run."
