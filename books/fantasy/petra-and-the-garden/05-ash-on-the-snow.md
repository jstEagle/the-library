## 5. Ash on the Snow

*Edmund*

The snow came down all night, soft as ash, and in the morning it was ash.

Edmund knew it first by the smell — a sweetness under the cold, like honey left too long on a hot stove. He was in the cellar again, third night running, cross-checking the founder's entries against the guild's oldest trade-book by lamplight. The final entry still sat where he had found it, in that dead man's careful hand: *The guildhall burns. The ledger survives.* Below it, nothing. The page after it was blank, and had been blank for two hundred years, and Edmund had begun to think of that blankness the way a man thinks of an open grave.

He heard the bells before he heard the shouting.

Not the chapel bells — those rang the hours, patient and dull. These were the watch-bells from the Gate of Thorns, and they were ringing wrong, fast and ragged, the way they rang for fire or plague. Then boots on the street above, many boots, and a voice amplified past any human throat — one of the elders, no doubt, spending voice like coin because coin no longer mattered.

*"They're at the walls! The hunters are inside the walls!"*

Edmund sat very still. The lamp flame leaned though there was no draft. Somewhere overhead, a shelf of preserved sounds — birdsong bottled in spring, a child's laughter traded by a mother who could no longer afford it — began to rattle in its case.

He should run. He knew he should run. But his hands went instead to the ledgers, all of them, the whole long history of small impossibilities bound in calfskin and string, and he began to stack them into his arms the way other men might gather children from a burning house.

The door at the top of the cellar stairs burst open and light spilled down — torchlight, orange and hungry, and against it the silhouette of Master Corvane, his mentor, breathing hard, soot already on his face though nothing yet burned.

"Boy." Corvane never called him Edmund; boy was the word, and it had always stung, and now it terrified him. "You've heard."

"I've read," Edmund said. His voice came out strange and flat. "Two hundred years ago. It's all written down. Tonight, or soon. The hall burns, and the ledger survives, and I don't know which side of that sentence we're meant to be on."

Corvane came down the stairs slowly, an old man's descent, each step deliberate. Up close Edmund could see how grey he'd gone since the herald's proclamation, how the gift that kept him hale had begun to look less like vitality and more like something wearing him from inside.

"There are votes being taken upstairs," Corvane said. "Elders arguing about whether to scatter to the four winds or stand. Fools, both camps. You can't hide from what's written in your own books." He looked at the ledgers in Edmund's arms, and something moved across his face — not quite greed, not quite grief. "You believe the entries. The dead man's hand."

"I balance them every day. They balance."

"Then listen to me once, boy, as your mentor and not your master." He gripped Edmund's shoulder, and his hand shook. "There is a back way through the coal cellar, out under the wall of the orchard. Take the ledgers. Take them north. Don't stop for anyone, don't trade anything, don't use whatever scrap of gift you carry, because they smell it, boy, they *smell* it like wolves smell blood—"

Above them, the first window broke.

It did not shatter like glass breaks. It shattered like glass *refuses*, all at once, inward, and through the gap came the smell of pitch and oil, and then the light. Not torchlight. Fire given intent, thrown by hands that had learned their burning from scripture. Edmund heard the roar go up through the floors of the guildhall, heard the bottled birdsong burst in its cases and release two hundred springs' worth of larks into a house full of smoke, singing while they died.

"The orchard," Edmund said. "Petra's in the orchard."

Corvane's grip tightened. "She knows what she's doing."

"She's *old*, Master. She's older than this hall. She can't outrun—" 

"She isn't going to run." And there it was in the old man's face at last, plain as a ledger line: he had known, and he had let her choose, and he was ashamed, and he was going anyway. "Go down through the coals, boy. Go north. If you love this guild — if you love what it was *supposed* to be — take the truth out of the fire and don't let them burn it twice."

Then he turned and went up into the noise, and Edmund stood alone in the cellar with an armful of dead men's handwriting, and made the only choice the sentence allowed him.

* * *

*Petra*

Petra had been planting when the fire came, which she supposed was fitting. Her whole life had been a long argument that things should be put in the ground rather than taken out of it, and here at the end she knelt in the frozen soil of her orchard with bulbs in her cupped hands — ordinary tulip bulbs, bought with ordinary coin, because even a garden of wonders deserved its share of the slow world — when the windows of the guildhall bloomed orange behind her.

She did not turn around. There would be time enough for turning. Instead she pressed the bulbs into the earth, six of them, in a little crescent beneath the bare boughs of the plum tree, and patted the frozen ground over them as gently as if tucking in children.

"They'll come up in April," she told the tree. "Someone will see them. That will have to be enough."

The hunters came through the orchard gate four abreast, torches high, white mantles gone grey with smoke-fall. Petra counted them the way she counted everything, patiently, without hurry: eight. Young, mostly. Afraid, mostly. The oldest one carried the iron censer of the Inquisition, and the smoke that poured from it was not incense but something that made the snow hiss where it fell.

"You're the gardener," the old one said. "The one they call the last founder."

"I'm the gardener," Petra agreed. She rose, knees cracking, and brushed the soil from her skirts. Behind her the guildhall roared, and she heard — she would hear it until she died, however long that turned out to be — the great beam over the entry hall split and fall, the beam her friend Aldous had cut and raised with his own hands before any of them had thought to bind a king. "You're standing in my garden. I'd ask you to wipe your boots, but I see you've been wiping them on the whole city."

"We're ordered to take you alive," the hunter said. "The Inquisitor-General has questions about the binding."

"He would." Petra smiled at them, and it was an old woman's smile, tired and fond and utterly without fear, and she saw it unsettle them more than any curse would have. "I have questions about it too. We never did get the wording quite right."

The censer swung forward. The hissing smoke rolled toward her across the snow, and where it touched the ground the frost blackened, and Petra felt it reach for the gift in her the way a hook reaches for a fish — patient, practiced, certain.

"Well," she said. "Let's not make it easy."

She had saved this a long time. Every gardener keeps something back, a seed bank against the worst winter, and Petra's seed bank was the orchard itself — two hundred years of promises the trees had made her and she had made them, stored in root and ring and heartwood. She put her bare hand flat against the plum tree's trunk, felt the sap moving under the bark like a sleeper's pulse, and gave the tree back everything it had ever lent her.

The orchard woke.

Snow exploded upward in a white geyser as the roots heaved. The hedgerow along the south wall — yew, clipped and civilized for a century — stood up off its foundations and became, in the space of three heartbeats, a wall of grasping green. Brambles unbraided themselves from the compost heap and ran across the paths like spilled ink, quick and black and thorned. The apple trees bent their branches down and shook, and the frozen fruit came off in hailstones that struck the hunters' shields with the crack of sling-stones. One young man screamed and ran. Another swung his torch at a bramble and the bramble took the torch out of the air and swallowed it, and went on growing, glowing faintly now from within, lit by borrowed fire.

Petra held on. It cost her what she had known it would cost — she could feel the years leaving her, feel herself becoming exactly as old as she looked, the gift draining out through her palm into the desperate green. Her hair whitened where it wasn't white already. Her hands, on the trunk, grew thin as paper. She had bargained with powers older than kingdoms, and this was the smallest trade of her life and the dearest: her strength for their delay, minute by minute, thorn by thorn.

Through the smoke and the shaking branches she saw the coal-cellar door open far across the garden, saw a slight figure stumble out into the snow with an armload of books, saw him look back at the burning hall and then — bless him, bless his careful balancing heart — look for her.

"Don't," she said, though he couldn't hear her. "Don't you dare stop. Go north, Edmund. Go and finish the arithmetic."

The hunters were regrouping behind the censer-smoke. The old one was shouting words of office, and the brambles were beginning to wilt where the smoke touched them, and Petra knew she had perhaps a hundred breaths left in this garden, in this body, in this shape of herself.

Enough. It would have to be enough. It had always, in the end, been enough.

She drew a knife — a pruning knife, curved and honest — and cut her palm, and pressed the blood to the bark, and spoke to the plum tree in the old language, the founding language, the one she had sworn never to use for harm and was using now for shelter, which was nearly the same thing.

"Grow," she said.

The tree grew. Two hundred years of slow patience spent in ten wild seconds, trunk thickening, branches thundering outward, until the plum tree stood over the whole orchard like a green cathedral and its lowest bough swept down and closed around Petra like a hand closing around something precious, and lifted her, and held her, while below her the hunters hacked and burned and shouted at a tree that had finally, after two centuries, decided to be a forest.

From up there, cradled in the dark arms, Petra watched the guildhall fall.

* * *

*Edmund*

The road north was white and empty and Edmund ran along it until his lungs tore, the ledgers strapped to his back under his coat, heavy as a sleeping child. At the top of the ridge outside the city he stopped — he hated himself for stopping — and turned.

The Guildhall of Small Impossibilities burned like a lantern set on the snow. He could see the roof go, and the tower where the hour-bells hung, and he could see, impossibly, absurdly, the orchard — a single vast tree risen out of the smoke like a fist, holding, holding, while around its base the little flames of the hunters' torches swarmed and failed and swarmed again.

He could not see Petra. He could not see Master Corvane. He could not see anyone at all, only the fire writing its bright terrible entry across the winter sky, and he understood suddenly that this was what the dead founder had seen, or almost, and why the entry ended where it ended. *The guildhall burns. The ledger survives.* Nothing about who survived. The ledger did not care. The ledger had never cared; that was what he had loved about it, and what he loved about it now, kneeling in the snow with his face wet and freezing, because it was the only honest thing left in the world and it was on his back.

Behind him, the city gates opened. Small figures came out onto the north road — refugees, gifted folk fleeing ahead of the hunt, a woman carrying a birdcage with nothing in it, a man leading a horse that wept. They passed him without speaking. No one stopped anymore; stopping was how they caught you.

Edmund got up. He wiped his face with a sleeve that smelled of smoke and old paper, and he tightened the straps of the ledgers, and he looked north, where the road ran white between black fields toward the capital and the thing that wore the crown.

One figure remained at the ridge's edge, apart from the others. An old woman, snow in her hair, leaning on a staff of living wood that budded as he watched — and walking, slowly, stubbornly, following the road north, as if she had simply decided that if the world insisted on burning down, she would plant something in the ashes on the way.

Edmund waited for her. It was the first trade he ever made that balanced perfectly: her steps for his, the rest of the road shared between them.

They walked north together, into the falling dark, while behind them the glow of the guildhall faded like a coal going out — and ahead, far off, past the frozen fields, the towers of the capital showed their lights, waiting, patient as a debt.
