## 6. The Road of Borrowed Names

We went north the way smoke goes—thinly, and against our nature.

The road out of the city was a gray seam stitched through white country, and we followed it with our collars up and our names folded away like letters we could not afford to post. Petra had shed her gardener's smock for a widow's black, and I had buried my guild badge in a frozen ditch outside Harrowgate, though my fingers had trembled doing it, as if I were interring a finger of my own hand.

"Names," Petra said on the second morning, her breath pluming like a kettle's steam. "You'll want to choose them carefully now. A borrowed name should fit the way borrowed boots fit—loose enough to forget you're wearing them."

"What did you go by, before?" I asked. "Before the guild."

She was quiet so long that I thought she hadn't heard. The fields around us were stubbled with dead corn, and crows walked the furrows like small clerks inspecting accounts.

"Maren," she said at last. "I was called Maren. But that was—" She stopped herself with the particular care of someone closing a door on a room they do not wish to re-enter. "That was a long time ago, Edmund. Longer than you can reckon."

I had stopped trying to reckon it. There were things about Petra that sat in my mind like stones in a shoe: the way she moved through snow without sinking quite as deep as I did; the way the horses in the passing drovers' trains turned their heads to watch her go; the way she sometimes spoke of the founding of the guild as one speaks of a quarrel with a sibling—intimate, unfinished, still bleeding.

We learned quickly what the winter country had become. At a toll bridge over the river Vell, a boy no older than twelve stood beside the toll-keeper's booth holding a slate, and on the slate was chalked: *Have you seen wonders? Report them.* At an inn in the village of Cold Ash, the landlord asked each guest, pleasantly, whether they'd noticed anything *unusual* on the road—crops out of season, illnesses cured too fast, luck too good. And in the market square at Dunmore we watched a woman point at her own neighbor, a cooper whose barrels had never once leaked in thirty years, and we watched two men in crown-gray take him away while his wife screamed into the muffling snow. No one else looked. That was the worst of it—not the cruelty but the practiced blindness of everyone else, eyes down, hands busy, the whole country performing innocence in unison.

"They inform because they are afraid," Petra said that night, in the hayloft where we'd paid our last copper to sleep. "And they are afraid because they remember, somewhere under everything, that the world used to be more than it is—and they cannot bear the difference."

"The guild made that world," I said. "Every jar of sunlight we sold. Every grief we lifted. We taught them to expect miracles."

"No," she said softly. "We taught them to expect *us*. It's not the same thing, and the difference is going to be the death of a great many people." She pulled the blanket higher. "Sleep, ledger-keeper. Tomorrow we cross into the hill country, and there's a woman there I need to see."

* * *

Her name was Ilse Vane, and she lived alone at the end of a lane that the snow had all but erased, in a house with a crooked chimney and a fence built from old cart wheels. Petra knew the way without asking directions, which should have told me something even then.

She opened the door before Petra knocked. She was perhaps fifty, broad-shouldered, with wind-chapped cheeks and eyes the color of weak tea—and when she saw us she went very still, the way animals go still when they smell the thing that has been hunting them.

"You," she said. "After all this time. You come to my door."

"Ilse," Petra said gently. "May we come in? It's cold, and we have something of yours."

"I have nothing of yours," Ilse said, but she stepped back from the doorway, and her eyes went to me—to my hands, my ink-stained fingers, the satchel of ledgers I carried—and something in her face cracked open like river ice. "Unless you mean—no. No, you wouldn't dare. You people don't give anything back. That's the whole of it, isn't it? That's the trade."

Inside, the house smelled of bread and lamp oil and, beneath both, a thin sourness I couldn't name until later. Ilse fed us barley soup without being asked, because hospitality runs deeper than grievance in the hill country, and while we ate she interrogated us with the flat efficiency of a woman who had rehearsed this conversation for years in an empty kitchen.

"I sold it in the spring of the year the king's son died," she said. "My sorrow. You know which sorrow. Everyone in three parishes knew which sorrow." Her hands were steady around her bowl; only her voice wavered. "A man came with a ledger book and a little glass jar, and he said the guild would carry it for me. He said grief was a weight, and weights could be borne by those strong enough to bear them. He gave me a paper with a seal on it. He said I could buy it back whenever I wished."

"That was Aldous Marsh," Petra murmured. "He meant kindly. They all meant kindly."

"Kindly." Ilse laughed, and the laugh had rust in it. "Do you know what it is, mistress, to lose your child and feel *nothing*? To stand at the graveside and watch yourself weep from a great distance, as though it were happening to a woman you'd once met at a fair? I ate. I slept. I planted my garden. My neighbors said how strong I was, how admirably I bore it. And inside—" she pressed her fist against her breastbone— "inside there was just this smooth empty place, like a stone worn round in a stream. Twenty years. Twenty years I've been a house with the hearth swept out."

I set down my spoon. "You want it back."

"I want *me* back," she said. "All of me. Even the ugly parts. Especially the ugly parts." She looked at me then, directly, and I understood that she had known since the door opened what I was—that the ledgers in my satchel were as legible to her as a brand. "It's illegal now, isn't it? Returning a trade. The new proclamations say all guild-work is witchcraft. They burned your hall in the city—I heard it from a tinker, they burned it with everyone inside."

"Not everyone," Petra said.

"No," Ilse said, looking at me. "Not everyone. So. Ledger-keeper. Can you do it?"

* * *

Here is what I had never told anyone, not even in the privacy of my own balancing columns: I loved the gift. Not the miracles—the *work*. The moment when a trade balanced, when the jar sealed and the weight transferred and some burdened soul walked out of the guildhall lighter by exactly one sorrow. I loved the arithmetic of mercy. For eleven years I had believed the ledgers were a kind of prayer, each entry a small absolution written in my own careful hand.

Now I sat in a stranger's kitchen and understood that every entry was also a theft, and that I was the thief's accountant, and that the whole beautiful machine ran on a debt older than any of us—a debt I had seen the edges of in the orchard, in pages grown from a tree, in words about a bargain struck with the thing beneath the crown.

"It can be done," I said. "But not here. Trades need to be witnessed and recorded, or the balance frays. And—" I hesitated. "There's a cost. To return a grief after twenty years, the bearer must take it back all at once. It won't come gently. It comes like a flood through a broken dam."

Ilse looked at me with those weak-tea eyes, and I saw that she had already grieved this decision twenty times over, and that what remained in her was only the terrible clean readiness of a woman who had chosen.

"Floods pass," she said. "Stones don't."

* * *

We did it at midnight, by lamplight, in her kitchen, with the snow ticking against the window like a patient finger.

I opened the ledger to her entry—spring, the year of the king's son, Aldous Marsh's neat script—and beneath it I wrote the counter-entry in mine. *Returned in full. Witnessed. Willingly taken back.* My pen shook. Not from fear. From something worse: from *wanting*.

Because here is the truth I have carried ever since, and will carry to my grave: when I broke the seal on that little glass jar—when the sorrow rose out of it like breath from a mouth, gray and silver and shaped like nothing and like everything—I felt it pass near me on its way home. And for one heartbeat, one traitorous heartbeat, I wanted it. I wanted the weight. I wanted to feel something that large move through me, wanted the gift to open in my chest like a door in a dark house, wanted to be the vessel, the bearer, the strong one. The guild's power hummed in my blood like a struck bell, whispering what it had always whispered to every member since the founding: *you were made for this, you are nothing without this, take more, hold more, be more.*

That is the pull. That is what the crown's hunters could never understand, hunting us like wolves—they thought we hoarded power out of pride. But pride is only the coat temptation wears outdoors. Underneath, it is loneliness. It is the fear of being ordinary, of being a man with ink on his fingers and no wonders left in him.

I wrote the entry. I closed the ledger. And Ilse Vane sat down hard on her kitchen floor and began, at last, after twenty years, to weep for her child—great ragged sobs that shook the crooked house, while Petra knelt and held her and murmured words in a language so old I did not know it, though I somehow understood every one.

I stood at the window with my back to them and pressed my hand flat against the cold glass, and I made myself watch the snow fall, ordinary snow, falling the slow way, and I did not let myself turn around until the wanting had drained out of me like heat from a stone.

When it was over, near dawn, Ilse rose from the floor looking ten years older and, somehow, ten years more alive. She wrapped bread and cheese and a slab of salt pork in cloth and pushed them into my arms, refusing thanks.

"There are soldiers on the north road," she said quietly. "Crown-gray, six of them, asking after travelers. Asking especially after a young man with books and an old woman who doesn't leave footprints." She glanced at Petra, who had gone very still by the banked fire. "The tinker who told me about the burning—he told me other things too. He told me the chief inquisitor himself rode out of the capital last week. Heading north. Heading *this* way." Her tea-colored eyes found mine. "They say he knows the guild's roads better than the guild does. They say he was one of you, once."

Outside, far off across the white hills, I heard—or thought I heard—a bell. One clear note, hung in the frozen air, neither coming nor going.

Petra's face, in the lamplight, was the face of a woman hearing a name she had spent two hundred years trying to forget.
