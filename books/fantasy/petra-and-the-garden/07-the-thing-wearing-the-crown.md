## 7. The Thing Wearing the Crown

The capital announced itself three days before we saw it.

We smelled it first—woodsmoke and tallow and ten thousand bodies pressed close against the cold—and then we heard it: a low continuous hum, like a hive the size of the horizon. Edmund climbed the last hill with his collar up and his breath streaming behind him like a scarf, and when he crested it he stopped so suddenly I nearly walked into his back.

"That's it," he said. "That's Aurelian."

I had not looked at that city in two hundred years. I found I did not want to look at it now. But the road bent toward it the way all roads in this country bend toward it eventually, the way water bends toward a drain, and so I looked.

Aurelian sat in its winter valley like a coal in ash. The palace rose from its center, white stone gone gray with centuries of smoke, and above the palace the storm-light gathered and would not disperse—a bruise-colored cloud that had hung over the crown for as long as anyone could remember. The common folk called it the King's Weather and said it meant the realm was watched over. The guild's ledgers called it something else. I had written some of those entries myself, long ago, in ink I mixed from things better left unnamed.

"We'll go around," I said. "Come to the walls from the river side. The fish gates are unwatched in winter."

Edmund glanced at me. He had learned, on the road, to read my silences the way he read ledgers—for what was entered between the lines.

"You've been here before," he said.

"Everyone has been here before. It's the capital."

"You know what I mean."

I pulled my cart—the poor borrowed mule, the poor borrowed name we traveled under—off the crest of the hill and onto the downward track, and I said nothing, and he let me say nothing, which was one mercy among the few the winter still allowed us.

* * *

We made the fish gates at dusk, slipping through with the ice-cutters' carts, and found a room above a chandler's shop in the tanners' quarter, where the smell was foul enough that no one asked questions of anyone. The city under midwinter was a held breath. Proclamations papered every wall: the hunt for the gifted, rewards printed in bold black numerals, woodcuts of hooded inquisitors with their iron censers. In the tavern below, men argued cheerfully about whether the baker's boy who could make bread rise overnight was a danger or merely useful, and whether reporting him counted as civic duty or greed. Both, most agreed. Both, and no shame in either.

Edmund sat by the candle with the ledgers open across his knees, though he was not reading them. He was watching me mend a tear in my sleeve with thread I'd bought from a woman whose hands shook too badly to sell it without spilling half her basket.

"Petra." He said my false name first—Maren, we were Maren and her nephew, come down from the wool country—and then, quietly, my true one, which he had not spoken aloud since the burning. "Petra. Tomorrow we go into the palace, or we die in the attempt, and you still haven't told me how you know the binding words. Not where you found them. How you *know* them. You said them to the orchard tree like a woman reciting her own prayers."

The needle went through the cloth. Through again. I have done needlework for a very long time; my hands know their business even when the rest of me is somewhere else entirely.

"I told you I was old," I said.

"You told me you were older than the guild. That isn't the same as an answer."

"No," I agreed. "It isn't."

I bit the thread and set the sleeve across my knee and looked at him—this young man with soot still faintly in the creases of his ears from a fire two hundred miles south, holding the debt of an entire guild in a satchel on the floor beside his boot—and I understood that the hour had come when the truth would cost less than its continued concealment. Truth is like that. It ripens. Leave it too long and it rots into something that poisons whoever finally eats it.

"Put the ledgers away," I said. "This is not in them. This is the thing they were written to hide."

* * *

He put them away. He banked the candle low, because he thought I was about to commit treason, and in a sense I was, though not against any crown that deserved the word.

"There was a king once," I said, "before the king there is now. A real man, flesh and appetite and temper, named Alaric. He was crowned young and he was not cruel—not at first. No king is, at first. Cruelty is a craft and it takes apprenticeship."

Edmund said nothing. Outside, the wind came off the river and worried the shutters, and somewhere below a dog complained about the cold in the flat voice dogs use when they have given up expecting anything to be done about it.

"Alaric had a gift," I went on. "You understand that gifts were rarer then, and stranger. His was memory. He could take a memory out of a person the way you'd draw a splinter—cleanly, whole, with the feeling still in it. And he could give them back. Or give them to someone else. Or keep them."

I heard Edmund's breath change.

"The court loved him for it, at the start. Grieving lords paid him to lift their griefs. Soldiers gave him the memories of battles they couldn't bear to carry home. Mothers—" My voice caught on that one, and I let it catch; I wanted him to hear it catch. "Mothers who had lost children gave him those memories, and walked out lighter, and everyone said the king was a blessing on the realm.

"But a man can only hold so much. Did you never wonder, Edmund, why the guild's trades work the way they do? Why a jar of sunlight spoils if kept past the year? Why a borrowed grief must be returned or it turns on the borrower? We wrote those rules over centuries, thinking we were being careful. We were only copying the shape of the original disaster.

"Alaric kept everything. Every grief, every horror, every dying soldier's last sight of the sky—he kept them all, because he was kind, and because he believed someone should hold them, and because each time he took one more he felt more necessary and less alone. And they changed him. You cannot be a vessel for a nation's suffering and remain a man. He began to give memories away too—to move them between people like coin, to rearrange marriages and loyalties and loves, always telling himself it was for the realm's good. Always necessary. Do you see? Necessity is the sweetest poison there is. It tastes exactly like virtue going down."

"And you?" Edmund said softly. "Where were you?"

"I was his gardener." I smiled, and it was an old wound smiling. "The palace gardens were mine. Roses that bloomed at midwinter, because even then I could not leave well enough alone. He used to walk with me among the beds and tell me things he told no one else—that he was tired, that the memories whispered at night, that he sometimes could not remember which sorrows were his own. I loved him. Not the way the ballads mean. The way you love a friend who is drowning and will not put down the stones in his pockets because each one belongs to somebody.

"So six of us—six gardeners and physicians and one very frightened priestess—went to the deep archives beneath the throne room, where the old kings were buried with their names, and we found the binding. A working older than the dynasty. It could seal a gift inside a body like sealing a letter inside lead. It required words of great specificity and greater cost, and it required"—I looked at my hands, which had planted ten thousand springs since—"it required that someone who loved him speak them. Because only love could find the seam between the man and the gift. Only love knows where a person ends.

"I spoke them. On the solstice, two hundred years ago, in the throne room, while he begged. He begged, Edmund. He understood what we could not—that the binding would not kill the gift, only cage it, and that a caged thing so vast would press outward forever, leaking, needing, trading. That it would reach out through whoever came near and offer them wonders in exchange for keeping it fed. He told us our grandchildren would build a church around his prison and call it a guild. We bound him anyway. We decided the world's safety outweighed one man's freedom, and we were wrong, and the wrongness has been compounding interest ever since."

The candle flame stood perfectly still, the way flames do when the air in a room has stopped moving for reasons that have nothing to do with drafts.

"The guild," Edmund said. His voice was very careful, the voice of a ledger-keeper checking a column that refuses to balance. "Every trade. Every gift we sold. That was—"

"Him. Leaking through the binding, and the founders learning to channel the leak rather than end it. They told themselves they were containing it. Then managing it. Then profiting by it. Each generation a little further from the crime and a little closer to the comfort of it. That is how corruption works, Edmund—it doesn't arrive, it's inherited. Like a house. Like a name. You live in it before you ever understand what it's built on."

"And the king now? The body on the throne?"

"Is Alaric's body. Kept alive by the thing inside it the way a wick is kept by the flame. There is no king anymore. There is only the caged gift, wearing its own prison, dreaming of being fed." I folded my ruined sleeve and set it down. "And tomorrow we walk into its dream and wake it, and it will hate us, and it will be right to hate us, because I am the one who locked the door."

For a long moment Edmund was silent. Then he said, not unkindly, which was somehow worse: "You could have told me on the road. Any of the roads. Why the shadow of the palace? Why now?"

"Because here," I said, "you can still turn back. And I wanted to watch you decide not to, knowing everything. If you forgive me with the whole truth in your hands, then perhaps—" I stopped. Two hundred years is a long time to carry a sentence you cannot finish.

But I never got to finish it, because that was the moment the bells began.

Not the midnight bells. The alarm bells—every tower in Aurelian at once, a sound like the sky cracking, and beneath them the deeper note of the inquisitors' censers being swung, a sound I knew, a sound I had hoped never to hear again. Boots in the street below. Torches blooming orange against the shutter slats.

"They're searching house to house," Edmund breathed, already moving for the ledgers. "How—"

"The chandler's daughter," I said, sick with it. "She looked at me too long when we took the room. She saw what I am. Or she guessed, and chose the reward." I stood, and my old knees ached, and I thought: *two hundred years, and it ends in a tanner's garret because a girl wanted thirty silver crowns.*

Or perhaps it does not end here. Perhaps it only arrives where it was always going.

The stairs groaned under iron-shod boots, and Edmund looked at me across the candlelight with the ledgers clutched to his chest, and I said the only true thing left to say:

"When they ask, I'm the one they want. Remember the counter-ledger. Whatever happens—remember the words."

The door came down like judgment.
