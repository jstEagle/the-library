## 8. The Long Winter Finishes Falling

The snow came down like the sky had finally decided to bury its mistakes.

Edmund had learned to read weather from ledgers—grain prices rising before a hard frost, candle wax climbing before a long dark—but nothing in any book had prepared him for a storm that felt *aimed*. The wind came off the northern plain in long white shrieks, and every flake that struck his face felt less like winter than like fingers, searching.

They had made it within sight of the capital's walls. That was the cruelty of it. From the crest of the last hill, through a tear in the cloud, Edmund could see the city: black rooftops under white, the palace rising at its heart like a splinter of night, and around the whole of it a ring of watchfires burning in the storm, each one a small orange eye that did not flicker in the wind.

"Bonfires," Petra said beside him. She stood very straight against the gale, her grey braid whipping loose from its coil. "Fed with something that doesn't mind the wet. That's not wood keeping those fires alive."

"No," Edmund said. "It isn't."

He had carried the ledgers for eleven days now, strapped to his chest beneath his coat, three volumes wrapped in oilcloth, their weight against his ribs like a second heartbeat. He had begun, somewhere on the road, to think of them that way—as an organ he'd been given, or grown, something that would kill him if it were cut out. He knew this was exactly the kind of thinking the guild had always encouraged. He knew it was the trap. Knowing changed nothing. The books were warm against his chest even in this cold, and warmth, in a world of witch-hunters, was a confession.

"We go around," Petra said. "The river gate. There's a ferryman who owed Aldous a debt—"

The lights came out of the storm before she finished speaking.

Not lanterns. Lanterns swung; these came steady, six points of pale gold moving through the whiteout with the unhurried certainty of things that had never once been lost. Edmund heard Petra's breath catch, and then he heard the other thing—the sound he had been dreading since the guildhall burned, since Ashfall, since the road of borrowed names began: hoofbeats. Many. Closing.

"Run," Petra said, and grabbed his wrist, and they ran, but the snow took their legs and gave the horses everything, and it was over in less time than it takes to tell. The riders formed a ring around them so smoothly it seemed rehearsed, which of course it was, and the golden lights resolved into lanterns held by men in grey cloaks with the crown's sun stamped in ash-white on their shoulders.

And at their center, on a black horse too calm for this weather, sat the Chief Inquisitor.

Edmund had seen him only in broadsheets, where he was rendered as a tall shadow with kind eyes, *Father Maren, Shepherd of the Strayed*. The broadsheets had been almost honest. Maren was tall, and his eyes were kind the way a hearth is kind—it drew you in, and it burned what it chose. He was perhaps fifty, silver at the temples, with a face like a man who had spent decades listening to confessions and found most of them tedious.

But it was none of those things that made Edmund's stomach drop. It was the *warmth* coming off the man. In a storm that was freezing the tears on Edmund's own cheeks, the air around Father Maren's horse steamed gently, like a kettle left on a banked fire. The snow did not land on him. It turned aside, politely, as if it had been asked.

*Gifted.* The chief hunter of the gifted, wearing his power like a lining inside his coat.

Maren looked down at them for a long moment. Then he smiled, and it was the smile of a man greeting guests he had been expecting for years.

"The ledger-keeper," he said. His voice carried easily through the wind, pitched as if the storm itself were leaning in to listen. "And the gardener. Do you know, I have dreamed about this meeting? Not metaphorically. I mean I have *dreamed* it. The thing under the crown shows me things, when it pleases. It has shown me your faces every night for a month." He dismounted, unhurried, and the snow where he stepped did not crunch so much as sigh. "It is very much looking forward to seeing you both."

* * *

They were taken not to a cell but to a chapel—a hunting lodge's chapel, abandoned, its saint's niches empty, its windows boarded so that the only light came from a fire that never needed feeding. Edmund understood the choice immediately. Cells were for prisoners. Chapels were for confessions. Maren wanted them comfortable enough to talk.

Petra sat with her hands folded, ancient and unreadable, and Edmund sat beside her with the ledgers still strapped to his chest because no one had ordered him to remove them, and the not-ordering frightened him more than a search would have.

Maren dismissed his men. Then he poured three cups of wine from a flask, set two of them on the floor between himself and his prisoners, and drank his own slowly, watching them over the rim.

"You've come to unbind him," he said at last. Not a question.

"Yes," Petra said.

"And you believe that will end the hunt."

"I believe the hunt exists to protect the binding. Remove the binding—" 

"—and the hunt has no reason to be." Maren nodded, agreeable, terrible. "That is precisely what the guild believed, two hundred years ago, when they made the bargain. Did you know that? Your founders were not conquerors. They were *tired*. There was a war on, a real one, the kind that unmakes countries, and the king—whatever he was, whatever he is—could have ended it in an afternoon, and nearly ended everything else doing it. So they bound him. And the world got small again, and safe, and slow." He spread his hands. "I am the continuation of their work, Mistress Petra. Nothing more. Someone must hold the lock while everyone else sleeps."

"You feed on him," Edmund said. The words came out before he could weigh them. "The gift. You're sustained by the same debt. If we unbind him, you starve with all the rest of us."

Something moved behind Maren's kind eyes—not anger. Recognition. The look of a man hearing his own secret spoken aloud by a stranger.

"Yes," he said simply. "I do. Twenty-two years now. I was dying of a wasting fever when the crown-thing found me—young, devout, furious at God for the waste of me. It offered me a trade. My service for my life. I have served." He looked into his wine. "I will not pretend I love it. But I have watched the sun rise ten thousand times I should never have seen, and I find I have developed opinions about continuing."

Then he set down his cup, leaned forward, and did the thing Edmund had been dreading since the lodge door closed behind them.

He offered the trade.

"I know what you carry," Maren said. "Three ledgers. The counter-book. Every scrap of stored power the guild ever banked—every traded grief, every jarred summer, every borrowed year. Give them to me, freely, and I give you something back." He paused, letting the silence do its work. "Your mentor. Aldous Vane."

The name went through Edmund like a blade finding the gap in armor.

Aldous, who had waved off the phantom entries with tired eyes and a tired joke. Aldous, who had stayed behind at the guildhall when the hunters came, standing on the cellar stairs with a poker in his hands, shouting for Edmund to *run, boy, run*. They had never found a body. Edmund had told himself, for eleven days, that this meant nothing—that the hall had burned too hot, that ash kept its secrets—and every night he had told himself it, and every morning he had woken having failed to believe it.

"He lives," Maren said softly. "In the palace cells. The crown-thing keeps him the way it keeps me—on credit. A stolen life, held in escrow. He is old, ledger-keeper, and the interest is being paid out of him hour by hour. But he lives. He talks, sometimes. About his student. About the boy who balanced the impossible books." Maren smiled his hearth-smile. "Give me the ledgers, and tomorrow morning you can sit with him. Feed him soup. Hear him complain about your penmanship. You could have years. Real ones. Ordinary ones."

The fire crackled. Somewhere outside, the storm screamed against the boards.

And Edmund felt it—the pull, the awful gravitational honesty of the offer. Not temptation dressed in silk; temptation dressed in wool, in a mentor's cardigan, smelling of ink and tea. He saw it so clearly: a bookshop, maybe, the two of them, arguing over stock. Spring. Soup. Years.

His hands had gone to the straps without his permission. He made himself look down at them, at his own traitor fingers resting on the oilcloth, and he thought about the entry in the dead founder's hand: *The guildhall burns. The ledger survives.* The ledgers had survived everything because people had refused, at the last inch, to let go. That was the whole inheritance. Not the power. The refusing.

"If I give them to you," Edmund said, and his voice cracked, and he hated it, and he kept talking anyway, "you don't save Aldous. You feed him to it slower. You keep the lock on the king another two hundred years, and there's another hunt, and another boy running through snow with these books against his chest, and someone offers *him* his dead mentor, because by then Aldous will be a story too." He lifted his head. "You're not offering me a trade. You're offering me a place in the ledger. An entry in your hand instead of mine."

Maren regarded him for a long time. The kindness in his face did not leave; that was the worst part. It settled deeper, the way frost settles into stone.

"They always think refusing will feel noble," he said quietly. "It doesn't. I remember. It feels like closing a door on a warm room." He rose, and the fire bowed toward him. "Keep your books, then. Carry them to the throne room on your own feet, since you insist on walking. The storm will bring you there either way—the crown-thing has been pulling on you both for days, haven't you felt it? Like a hook behind the sternum." He moved to the chapel door and paused with his hand on the latch. "For what it is worth, ledger-keeper: I hope you succeed. I have opinions about continuing, but I have begun, lately, to have opinions about *how*."

* * *

They marched at dawn, or what the storm allowed to be dawn—a bruised grey suggestion of light, the wind gone from shriek to a low continuous roar like the sea. The capital's walls rose out of the white, and the gates stood open, and Edmund understood with a lurch that no gate in the world would have stopped them now. The crown-thing wanted them inside. It had wanted them inside since midwinter began gathering itself.

The streets were empty. Doors shuttered, bells silent, the whole city holding its breath beneath the snow. Only the watchfires lined their way, those wrong orange flames that bent toward the prisoners as they passed, straining like dogs on leads.

At the palace doors, guards took the ledgers' weight from Edmund's chest—not taking them away, merely *touching*, confirming, the way a customs man confirms cargo—and then pressed them back. They walked the long corridor to the throne room through cold that deepened with every step, until their breath fell as frost and the torches guttered blue.

And ahead, at the end of the corridor, the great doors of the throne room stood open onto darkness, and out of that darkness came a voice. Not spoken aloud. Felt, in the teeth, in the marrow, in the strap-marks on Edmund's shoulders where the ledgers had rested for eleven days:

*Bring me my bookkeeper.*

Behind them, far to the south, muffled by distance and storm, the first bell of the solstice began to ring.

Petra reached over and took Edmund's hand. Her grip was astonishingly strong for a woman of her years, and cold as riverbed stone, and steady.

"Whatever it offers you in there," she murmured, "remember the warm room is a lie. The only real warmth left is the kind we make ourselves. Slowly. The ordinary way."

The doors opened wider, groaning on hinges that had not moved in two hundred years, and the darkness inside breathed out across them like a held exhale finally released.

Edmund stepped through, carrying the light.
