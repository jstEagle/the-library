## 9. Unbinding

**Edmund**

The throne room had not been warmed in a hundred years, and it showed. Frost climbed the pillars like pale ivy, and the great windows—once stained with the deeds of kings—had gone grey with ice, so that the light that came through them was the color of old milk. Our breath hung in the air and did not fall.

At the far end of the room, on a throne of black basalt furred with frost, sat the king.

He did not move. He had not moved, Petra said, in living memory—not since the last founder died. The courtiers who attended him were few and silent, and they stood at a distance, as one stands from a fire that has gone strange. His crown was iron, unadorned, and beneath it his face was handsome in the way of a portrait: perfect, still, and belonging to no hour in particular.

Petra knelt before him with her bare hands pressed to the frozen floor, and I stood behind her with the ledgers stacked against my chest like a shield, my heart going so hard I could hear it in my ears.

"The counter-ledger," she said, without turning. "Open it to the first page. When I begin, you will read aloud every entry, exactly as written, and for each one you will give up what it holds. Do you understand what that means?"

I understood. Every trade the guild had ever made was stored somewhere, and the counter-ledger held the keys. A jar of last year's sunlight. A borrowed grief. A stolen life—my mentor's life, which the inquisitor Vane wore like a coat he had no right to. To spend them all was to empty the guild of everything it was.

"It means we lose," I said. "All of it."

"It means we pay," Petra said. "There is a difference. Begin when I do."

She began to speak, and her voice was not the voice of an old gardener anymore. It was older than that, older than the guildhall, older than the snow on the windows. It was the voice of someone reciting words she had helped to write two hundred years ago, and the frost on the pillars began to weep.

I opened the ledger and read the first entry: *One jar of last year's sunlight, traded fair.* And as I spoke the words, something left me—a warmth behind the ribs, a small bright hoarded thing—and went out into the room like a coin flung onto ice.

The king's fingers twitched.

* * *

**Petra**

I had spoken these words once before, kneeling in this same room with three friends beside me and terror in all our throats, and I had sworn then that I would never speak them again. Words are promises, and promises are debts, and I had carried this debt through two centuries of springs the way a tree carries a scar.

*By root and by ransom, by seed and by stone,* I said, and the binding woke.

It woke angry.

The thing beneath the crown had been patient for two hundred years because patience cost it nothing, but now it felt the walls of its prison thinning, and it came up through the king's body like floodwater through a cellar door. The king's eyes opened. They were not his eyes. They were the color of deep water where nothing lives, and they found me across the frozen floor, and the cold in the room turned personal.

*Little gardener,* it said, in a voice that came from everywhere—the pillars, the ice, the marrow of my own bones. *You grew old. How kind of you to come back and die.*

"Edmund," I said, and kept my hands flat on the stone, and kept speaking. *By the word that was given and the word that was kept—*

He read. I heard his voice shake and steady, shake and steady, and each entry he spent struck the thing like a hammer on a bell. The sunlight jar burst somewhere unseen—I felt it go, a season's warmth scattering into the dark. A borrowed grief returned to the air with all its weight. The guild's whole treasury of small impossibilities poured out around us, and the throne room filled with the ghosts of everything we had ever traded: the smell of cut grass in midwinter, a snatch of a dead woman's laughter, rain falling upward.

The thing screamed without a mouth, and the windows cracked from top to bottom.

*You would make them ordinary,* it said. *You would make them small.*

"They were never yours to make large," I said.

* * *

**Edmund**

Vane came for us at the turning of the hour.

I saw him through the cracked doors, coming down the long gallery with snow whirling at his back and his gift burning off him like heat off a forge—he was spending the stolen life recklessly now, spending my mentor's years to move faster than any man should move. Behind him the solstice storm broke over the capital, and the sound of it was like the end of the world being rehearsed.

"Petra—" I said.

"I know. Keep reading."

He crossed the threshold of the throne room and stopped, because the floor between us and him had gone soft with growing things. Moss, green and impossible, spreading fast as spilled water; then ferns unfurling from the frost; then a hedge rising out of the stones itself, thorned and dense, a garden grown in three heartbeats where no garden could grow. Petra knelt inside it with her hands bleeding on the ice, and the hedge stood between her and Vane like an old dog standing over a child.

"You're killing yourself," I said. My voice cracked on it. Every entry I read took something else from her too—I could see it now, see her shrinking, her hair going white at the roots, her shoulders bowing under a weight she had carried alone for two hundred years and was finally setting down.

"I have been dying since the founding," she said. "This is just the honest version. Read."

Vane struck the hedge with a hand full of borrowed fire, and the hedge burned and regrew, burned and regrew, and I read faster. *One winter's silence, traded fair. One name, forgotten on purpose, returned to its owner. One life, taken unjustly—*

That entry. My mentor's life. I hesitated over it, pen hovering, and felt the pull of it like a hook behind my sternum: I could keep it. I could carry Aldous's years home and pour them back into him, or into myself, or hold them against some future need. One line. One refusal. The guild's last coin, saved.

The king's head turned toward me, and the deep-water eyes fixed on mine, and the thing wearing him said, gently, almost kindly: *Keeper of ledgers. You above all know the value of what you hold. Would you throw it away?*

And I thought of the phantom entries in my own handwriting. Of the final page: *The guildhall burns. The ledger survives.* The founder had known, two hundred years ago, that someone would stand exactly here, holding exactly this, and be asked exactly this question. He had written the answer in advance so that I would only have to borrow his courage instead of finding my own.

"One life," I read, "taken unjustly—returned."

Aldous's years tore out of me like a bandage off a wound, and I cried out, and the thing in the king screamed louder, because that entry had been its favorite collateral, its surety, its leash on the inquisitor who hunted us. Across the room, Vane staggered as if struck. The fire went out of him. He looked suddenly, terribly ordinary—an aging man in a wet coat, standing in a ruined hall, holding nothing.

He fell to his knees. He did not get up again. Whether from grief or exhaustion or the simple weight of being only himself at last, I never learned. The storm swallowed the sound of him.

* * *

**Petra**

The last words of the binding were the first words of it, reversed, and I had kept them in my mouth for two hundred years like a stone I could not spit out.

The treasury was empty. Edmund stood swaying behind me, hollowed out, a boy again in every way that mattered—no gift, no guild, nothing but the ledger in his arms and the stubbornness in his face that I had loved in his founder, once, in another life. Everything we had was spent. What remained was the oldest payment there is, the one no ledger records because no one survives the transaction to enter it.

I looked at the king. Not the thing—at the king. Beneath the deep-water eyes, beneath two centuries of occupation, there was a man, and I had known him when his beard was brown and his laugh was loud and his gift was kindness so enormous it frightened everyone who felt it. We had bound him to save the world from what his kindness might become in the wrong hands, and in doing so we had put it in the worst hands of all: our own.

"Aldric," I said.

The thing recoiled. It had been master of that body for two hundred years and no one had dared the name.

"Aldric of the Long Meadow. You gave me apples from your orchard the autumn before we bound you. You gave them freely, though you knew—we told you we were afraid of you, and you gave us apples anyway, and said, *be careful with the world, it is younger than you think.*"

The deep-water eyes shuddered. Beneath them, like a lamp relit in a drowned house, something warm surfaced.

*I remember,* said a voice that was not the thing's voice. It was rusty and human and broken with two hundred years of disuse, and it was the most beautiful sound I had ever heard. *Petra. Your garden. Is it—*

"Gone," I said. "Burned. All of it. I'm going to plant another."

The thing made one last rush for the surface, one last surge of cold and hunger and ancient entitled rage—and Edmund, God bless him, threw the empty ledger open and shouted the only entry left, the one written in a dead man's hand, dated for this day:

*"The king wakes. The debt is paid."*

And the binding broke.

* * *

**Edmund**

It did not break loudly. That was the strangest part. After the storm of it—the screaming windows, the burning hedge, the storm howling at the shattered glass—it ended the way a fever ends: all at once, quietly, leaving only the shaking aftermath.

The iron crown slid from the king's brow and rang on the frozen floor, and rolled, and stopped.

The man in the throne drew a breath like a man surfacing from deep water, and coughed, and wept without knowing why, and looked at his own hands as though greeting old friends. He was old—two hundred years old, though his body had been held at the age of its binding—but he was *his*, entirely his, and when he spoke his own name again, "Aldric," simply, testing it, the frost on the pillars began to melt.

Everywhere in the room, everywhere in the city, perhaps everywhere in the world, the small impossible things went out. I felt the guild die in my chest—not painfully, not like a wound, but like a hand opening after being clenched for so long the fingers had forgotten how. Somewhere, a woman got her sorrow back. Somewhere, a hundred quiet trades unraveled into honesty. The age of small impossibilities ended in a throne room that smelled, faintly, absurdly, of cut grass and turned earth, the last ghost of Petra's hedge dissolving into the thawing air.

Petra collapsed. I caught her before she struck the stone, and she was lighter than a bundle of dried stalks, and she was laughing.

"Help me up," she said. "Carefully. I've never been mortal before. I don't know how it's done."

The king rose from his throne on unsteady legs, the first time in two centuries, and stood among us—not above us, among us—in his plain robes, an old man blinking in the milk-grey light. Outside, the storm was passing. Through the cracked windows came a smell none of us had smelled in the capital in living memory.

Snowmelt. Wet stone. The green, blind, hopeful beginning of a thaw.

Petra gripped my arm with fingers that trembled like any old woman's, and pointed at the window, and said, in a voice worn down to wonder:

"Edmund. Find me some seeds."
