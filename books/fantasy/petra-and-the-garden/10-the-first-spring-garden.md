## 10. The First Spring Garden

Petra had planted gardens for four hundred years, but never one like this.

The ground was stubborn. That was the first thing she noticed, kneeling in the thawing mud behind the bookshop with her skirts dark to the knee and her hands already filthy past the wrist. The soil here was ordinary—no orchard magic humming beneath it, no borrowed warmth seeping up from some deep and impossible root. It was just earth: cold, clodded, full of stones that bit her fingers when she pulled them free. She held each stone a moment before setting it aside, as if it might confess to something.

The slow way, she thought, and laughed at herself, alone in the empty lot, an old woman cackling at dirt.

She had forgotten how long it took. That was the truth of it—the truth she'd spent two centuries avoiding. Magic had made her a liar about time. When a guild gardener pressed her palms to a seedbed and whispered the trade-words, spring arrived in an afternoon. Roses bloomed in November. The impossible orchard had carried fruit in every season, heavy and sweet, because the orchard had never once been asked to wait. Everything the guild grew had been grown on credit, drawn from a debt that sat wearing a crown in a frozen city far to the north.

Now there was no credit. There was only March, thin and gray and grudging, and a paper packet of seeds she'd bought from a market woman for two copper bits, and the whole of the waiting stretched out ahead of her like an honest road.

Petra pressed a pea seed into the dark. One. Then another. Her knuckles ached—not with the deep immortal ache of a body held together by someone else's power, but with the small mortal ache of a woman of indeterminate years doing honest work. She found she could not tell which hurt more, or rather, she found the question no longer mattered. The unbinding had taken everything: the gift, the long life, the green whisper under her skin that had answered her since she was nineteen years old and desperate. What it left was this—a body that would end, hands that would fail, and a garden that would come up in its own good time or not at all.

She had helped bind Aldric. She reminded herself of it every morning now, the way other people said their prayers. *I helped bind him. I did it to save the world. I let him wear the chains so that we could wear our gifts.* For two hundred years the logic had held her like a wall holds a roof. But walls, she had learned in the throne room, can become tombs, and the thing they had buried in the king had grown fat and patient on all those small impossibilities, all those jars of sunlight and borrowed griefs, all those payments on a bargain no one living had struck.

Edmund had ended it with a ledger and a refusal. He had traded away his own gift—his beautiful, useless gift of finding lost things—and he had done it with the same steady hand he'd used to balance the books, as if even his sacrifice were an entry to be recorded properly. *Paid in full,* he had written in the counter-ledger, and then the guild had simply... stopped. Like a clock winding down. Like breath released.

She set the last pea and covered it, patted the earth flat with both palms, and sat back on her heels.

Above her, the town went about its morning. A cart rattled past on the lane. Someone was arguing cheerfully about the price of wool. In the house behind her, through the open kitchen window, Edmund's kettle began its low whistle. Ordinary sounds. She had heard them all her long life from the edges—from the gardens where she worked while the gifted came and went—but she had never lived inside them before. They fit strangely, like new shoes. They fit.

A child stopped at the fence, a girl of perhaps seven with mud on her chin, watching Petra work with frank curiosity.

"Are you planting?" the girl asked.

"I am."

"It's too cold."

"It is," Petra agreed gravely. "That's the trick of it. You plant anyway. You plant before it seems possible, and you trust the seed to know things you don't." She held out her hand, palm up, showing the girl the last few peas. "Would you like to help? It will take weeks before anything shows. Months before you eat anything. Most people find that unbearably dull."

The girl considered this with the seriousness of a magistrate. "My nan says nothing grows without magic anymore. Since the guild broke."

"Your nan," Petra said, "is going to be very surprised in June."

* * *

The guild dissolved the way snow dissolves: not in catastrophe but in a long, quiet giving-up of shape.

There had been no formal decree. There didn't need to be. After midwinter, after the throne room, the members simply drifted home to lives they had set aside—one to a bakery in the river towns, one to a fishing crew that would never again call up fair winds, one, poor Maren, to her father's farm, weeping over the gift of mended bones she'd traded away and calling it, through her tears, the cheapest price she'd ever paid for anything. The witch-hunters, leaderless once their chief inquisitor starved of the crown-thing's sustenance, became merely men again, and men who had burned a guildhall do not linger where they are known. Some fled north. Some, it was said, took up farming, which Petra thought was either penance or poetry, and possibly both.

No one hunted the gifted now. There were none left to hunt—or none who admitted it. And that was the strange mercy of it: the ones who kept their powers hidden, the hedge-witches and weather-readers who had never joined the guild, found themselves suddenly safe, because the world had watched the mighty fall and decided that small magics were not worth a pyre. Fear, Edmund observed, was also a kind of ledger, and the crown's accounts had finally balanced against it.

Petra grew old the normal way, or what passed for it in a body that had already lived four centuries. Her hair whitened at the temples within the first year, silver spreading like frost across her dark braids. Her knees complained. She caught colds—actual colds, with sneezing and misery, and she treasured every one of them the way a freed prisoner treasures his chains' weight gone. Mortality sat on her shoulders like a well-worn cloak. She kept expecting it to chafe. It never did.

In the second summer, the peas came up. Of course they did. That was the whole point of peas.

* * *

The bookshop occupied the ground floor of the narrow house on Tanner's Lane, below the rooms where Edmund slept, and it smelled the way he had always imagined heaven might: dust and paper and glue and the faint sweetness of old leather. He had named it Whitlock's, which required no imagination, and painted the sign himself, which accounted for the slightly crooked W that customers found charming and that Edmund found, every single morning, quietly infuriating.

The ledgers lived behind the counter, in a glass case that had once held a jeweler's watches. He opened it rarely. When he did—usually for a scholar, twice for the king's new archivists, once, memorably, for a young woman with hungry eyes who asked too many questions about the jar-of-sunlight entries—he handled the books like relics, which is what they were. Not spellbooks anymore. The trades written there could no longer be made; the counter-party to every contract was ash and memory and a man named Aldric who had walked out of the winter palace blinking in the light, asking after a garden and a friend. But history lived in those pages, and history, Edmund had come to believe, was the only inheritance worth keeping.

He wrote in the margins now. That had started the first spring and never stopped. Beside the entry for a borrowed grief, returned to a widow on Tanner's Lane: *She wept for three days and then planted roses. She says the grief was hers to carry, and carrying it made her strong. I am beginning to understand that we stole strength from people when we thought we were lending comfort.* Beside the founder's final entry—*The guildhall burns. The ledger survives*—he wrote simply: *It did. We did.*

His gift was gone. He still reached for it sometimes, out of habit—reaching for that warm interior compass that had always pointed toward lost things, keys and rings and, once, heartbreakingly, a runaway brother. The reach closed on emptiness now, the way a tongue finds the gap where a tooth was. But here was the discovery he made slowly, and then all at once: things could be found the slow way too. You looked. You asked. You posted notices and searched hedgerows and waited by doors. And when you found what was lost—when the miller's daughter came home, when the wedding ring turned up in the flour barrel—you had earned it, and earned things stayed found.

On a bright morning in the third spring, Petra came down the outside stairs with dirt under her nails and a basket of the season's first greens, and found Edmund wrestling with a delivery of books, dropping them, swearing mildly, reordering them alphabetically by a system no one else could fathom.

"You know," she said, setting the basket on the counter, "in the old days I could have ripened that entire shipment overnight. Every page soft and settled, the ink dried, the glue cured. An afternoon's work."

"And now?"

"Now I'm going to sit in your terrible chair and read one of them, slowly, the way it was meant to be read, and it's going to take me a week, and I have never been happier about anything in four hundred years."

Edmund laughed. Outside the window, the garden she had coaxed from stubborn ground was green to the fence line—peas and beans and roses, all of it grown on patience alone, all of it hers, all of it real. Somewhere in the capital, an old man who had been a king tended a garden of his own, and wrote letters that arrived irregularly and were always signed with his own name, the one he'd spoken again at last in the frozen throne room.

Edmund opened the ledger to a fresh page—the plain one, the ordinary one, bound in calf leather with no sigil—and wrote at the top, in his careful keeper's hand:

*Year three. No debts outstanding.*

Then he closed the book, and went to make the tea.
