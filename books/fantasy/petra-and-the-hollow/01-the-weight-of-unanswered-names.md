## 1. The Weight of Unanswered Names

The ledger room of the Guild of Impossible Things smelled of tallow, dust, and the particular sourness of ink that had been asked to do too much. Paloma Eriksen liked it that way. A ledger room should smell like work, not like wonder. Wonder was what happened *after* the accounting was done, and only if you were lucky.

They sat at the long oak table beneath the third window—theirs by twenty years of seniority and one small act of intimidation involving a paperknife—and worked through the morning's entries with the steady rhythm of someone counting their own heartbeat.

*One bottle of grief, vintage unspecified, traded for six months of good weather in a vineyard that had never known a bad year.* Paloma weighed the bottle in their hand before recording it. It was heavier than grief ought to be. Grief, properly bottled, should weigh almost nothing—a thing carried so long it forgot its own mass. This one clinked when they tilted it.

"Bad bottling," they murmured, and made a note in the margin. *Seller advised: decant and rebottle. Do not accept payment until quality assured.*

Across the table, young Oskit was pretending to copy out receipts while actually watching Paloma with the open, hungry curiosity of an apprentice who had not yet learned that curiosity about impossible things was how apprentices became cautionary tales.

"You're frowning at the grief," Oskit said.

"I'm frowning at the *cork*. It's wax-sealed over blood. That's either very traditional or very stupid, and tradition is usually just stupidity that survived."

Oskit wrote that down, which was either flattering or alarming.

Paloma returned to the page. The morning's audit was ordinary by guild standards, which is to say that any magistrate in the free cities would have wept, prayed, or called for soldiers. *Borrowed luck, three days' worth, drawn from a man who owed us and repaid in fortune rather than coin—he'll regret it around the third day, they always do.* *A year of someone else's childhood, purchased by a woman who had none of her own; she wanted to know what it felt like to be forgiven for something small.* Paloma had held that entry longer than the others. There was no rule against selling childhoods. There was simply a feeling against it, and feelings were not enforceable, and so the trade stood recorded in fair, unblinking script:

*Item: twelve months of childhood, secondhand, gently worn. Seller: unnamed per contract. Buyer: M— of the Tanner's Row. Price: her name on our rolls, redeemable at need.*

Everyone who traded with the guild paid eventually in something they could not spare. That was not cruelty. That was arithmetic.

* * *

By midday the light through the third window had gone honey-colored, and Paloma's hand ached in the pleasant way of a hand that had done honest work. They stretched, cracked two knuckles, and reached for the next folio—the prayer ledger.

Every guild kept its books, but theirs kept one book more. The prayer ledger recorded petitions: requests made to the guild that could not be fulfilled by trade because they required something the guild did not stock. Miracles, essentially. People came to the door on Salt Lane with their hands full of everything they owned and asked for their son back from the war, or for rain shaped exactly right, or for the courage to leave a husband. The guild took the petition, weighed it, wrote it down, and then—this was the important part, the part Ivetta insisted upon—did nothing.

"We are not gods," Ivetta said, whenever anyone suggested otherwise. "We are clerks. Gods answer prayers. We merely *file* them."

And Vess—the god to whom the guild's founding oath was sworn, the silent god, the still one—had filed nothing for twenty years. Not one whisper. The theologians said Vess was dead. The merchants said Vess was resting. Paloma, who had sworn their oath at nineteen with salt water in their eyes and had heard nothing since but their own pulse, believed neither. Paloma believed Vess was *waiting*, though waiting for what, they could not have said under torture, and had in fact once been asked, in a manner of speaking, and had said nothing then either.

So the prayer ledger grew fat and unanswered, year upon year, a book of names with nothing written opposite them. Paloma knew it by touch the way a physician knows a body. Entry four thousand and eleven: *a fisherman of the Weir district asks that the sea spare his neighbor's boat, as his own is insured and his neighbor's is not.* Entry four thousand and twelve: *a child asks for her mother, deceased these two winters, returned.* And so on, down through the whole soft, hopeless catalog of human wanting.

Paloma dipped the pen. Began the new entry. *A widow of Corvane asks—*

And stopped.

Because the ink moved.

Not smeared. Not blotted. *Moved*—the way a sleeping man moves, a slow settling, the letters of the widow's request shifting on the page as though rearranging themselves to be more comfortable. Paloma watched the words settle into place, watched the space opposite them—the empty space where an answer would go, the space that had stayed blank across four thousand entries and twenty silent years—

—and the space filled.

Not with ink. With something older than ink, something that looked like ink the way a portrait looks like a face. A single line appeared opposite the widow's prayer, in a hand Paloma knew better than their own, because it was the hand from the guild's founding charter, the hand on the oath-page, the hand they had pressed their thumb against at nineteen while salt water ran into their mouth:

*Granted.*

Paloma set down the pen very carefully, the way one sets down a sleeping animal.

"Oskit," they said. Their voice came out level, which they counted as the finest professional achievement of their career. "Fetch me the master. Tell her the prayer ledger has developed opinions."

Oskit went pale in the specific gradations Paloma had catalogued over the years—first white as receipt-paper, then gray as a bad debt. "Is that—should I run?"

"No. Walk briskly. Running suggests panic, and panic devalues the guild's shares." A pause. "Actually, run."

* * *

Ivetta came herself, which she almost never did, descending into the ledger room with her cane striking the stairs in a rhythm like a patient creditor. She was seventy if she was a day, built like a dock crane, with eyes the color of money left out in the rain. She read the entry without touching it. One did not touch a miracle; one appraised it.

"Twenty years," she said at last.

"Twenty years, four months, eleven days," said Paloma, who had been counting.

"Don't be precise at me, Eriksen, it's unsettling." But her voice had gone soft, and Paloma knew that softness. It was the voice Ivetta used for debts she intended to collect personally. "Who is the widow?"

"Maren Ostrow, Weaver's Lane. Her petition—" Paloma checked the page, and felt the floor tilt slightly under their chair. "Her petition was for rain. Just rain. For her garden."

"And the ledger says granted."

"The ledger says granted."

Ivetta lowered herself into the chair across the table, which she did only for matters of state or matters of gods, and these days those were increasingly the same meeting. "In twenty years," she said, "Vess has been silent. Dead, most say. And now a prayer gets answered in the middle of your audit, in the middle of the morning, without ceremony, without omen, without so much as a thunderclap to advertise." She tapped the page once, lightly, with one crooked finger. "Gods don't wake up quietly, Eriksen. When a thing that has been still for twenty years moves, it isn't waking. It's *reaching*."

"For what?"

"For whatever's closest." Ivetta looked up, and her rain-colored eyes were, for just a moment, afraid—an expression so foreign on her face that Paloma felt it land somewhere behind their ribs. "You swore to it. You're closest."

* * *

That evening Paloma walked home the long way, along the canal, because the short way passed the shrine niches and they could not bear them tonight. Corvane in autumn was all smoke and gulls and the smell of frying onions from the night-stalls, and ordinarily Paloma loved it with the fierce, unsentimental love of a person who had chosen their city the way other people chose spouses. Tonight every bell in every tower sounded like a question.

The widow's garden would have rain tomorrow. That was the arithmetic of it: one prayer, one answer, one small green mercy. Any priest in the city would call it grace. Paloma kept hearing Ivetta's voice instead. *Reaching.*

At their rooms above the chandler's shop, Paloma lit one lamp, sat at the small desk where they kept their private accounts—debts owed to them, debts they owed, the twin columns of a life—and found, without surprise, that they could not write. The pen hovered. The columns waited. Somewhere in the dark between them, a god that had been silent for twenty years had spoken a single word, and the word had been *granted*, and there was no column anywhere in any ledger for what that might cost.

Paloma blew out the lamp and sat in the dark a long time, listening to the gulls argue over the canal, and did not sleep.

* * *

Morning brought the second prayer.

It came in through the front door this time, in person, attached to a fisherman from the Weir district who arrived soaked to the knees and radiant, babbling about how he'd asked—not seriously, mind, just a joke between him and the water—that the sea look after his boat, and how he'd woken to find his nets mended, his hull re-tarred, and a strange calm on the water that the other fishermen swore hadn't been there. "Someone's looking out for me," he told Oskit, grinning through his beard. "I don't know who, but I mean to keep asking."

Paloma pulled the prayer ledger and found the entry. Four thousand and eleven. *A fisherman of the Weir district asks that the sea spare his boat.* Opposite it, in that old, patient hand: *Granted.*

Two in two days. Twenty years of silence, and now the ledger answered like a well-trained clerk.

Paloma sat with the book open for a long moment, feeling the unease move through them like weather. Because here was the thing they had not said aloud, not even to Ivetta, not even to themselves in the dark above the chandler's shop: they knew that hand. Had known it since nineteen, since the drowned chapel, since salt water and an oath and a god's presence like a hand resting on the back of their neck—warm, heavy, *approving*. And the hand on the page was writing the way that presence had felt. Unhurried. Generous. Certain that whatever it gave was wanted.

But twenty years was a long time to hold your breath, and things that hold their breath that long come up changed.

"Oskit," Paloma said. "The drowned chapel on the Salt Road. Is it still standing?"

"The one where—" Oskit stopped himself, which showed learning. "Yes. Fishermen use it for storage now. Why?"

"Because I have a delivery to make," Paloma said, "and a suspicion that deliveries are about to become very interesting."

They closed the prayer ledger, and their hand lingered on the cover a heartbeat longer than habit allowed—as it does when you say goodbye to a thing, or hello—and outside the window, far off toward the sea, the first drops of the widow's rain began to fall on Corvane, soft and even, like fingers counting.
