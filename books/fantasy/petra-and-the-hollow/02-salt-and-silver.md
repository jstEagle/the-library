## 2. Salt and Silver

The payment for the drowned chapel was, as always, a bowl of silver and a bowl of salt.

Paloma had asked Ivetta once, early in their service, why both. Why not one or the other. Ivetta had looked at them over her spectacles the way she looked at everyone who asked questions before their third year — which was to say, the way she looked at furniture that had developed opinions.

"Salt because Vess is of the sea," she'd said. "Silver because Vess is not. If you ever work out what that means, tell me. It was explained to me by a woman who's been dead forty years, and I've been pretending to understand it ever since."

So Paloma carried the two bowls down through Corvane's lower terraces on the morning of the tide-turn, when the city smelled of kelp and hot bread and the particular sourness of fishermen who had argued all night about the weather and lost. The bowls were old pewter, dented in matching places, and they clinked against each other inside their cloth wrapping like two old friends gossiping.

"You could just send me," said Brannic, who had insisted on coming along, because Brannic insisted on coming along everywhere, on the theory that any errand he didn't attend might turn out to be interesting without him.

"You're guild quartermaster," Paloma said. "You have inventory."

"I have *finished* inventory. Do you know how rare that is? The ledgers balance. There is nothing left to count. This is a crisis, Paloma. A man could die of it."

"A man could die of falling down the chapel stairs, which is what you'll do if you keep walking backwards while talking."

Brannic walked backwards while talking. He was a large man built along the lines of a well-fed door, with a beard he oiled for festivals and a heart he pretended he didn't have. He had been the first person at the guild to be kind to Paloma, twelve years ago, and had never once acknowledged it since, which was its own kind of courtesy.

"The thing I don't understand," he said, "is why you get the chapel runs. You're the ledger-keeper. You're the most important person in that building after Ivetta, and arguably above her, because she can't read her own handwriting—"

"She can read her own handwriting."

"—and yet every quarter, off you go, down to the wet end of the world, carrying soup bowls to a god nobody's heard from since before you were born. It's favoritism. I want favoritism."

"It's not favoritism." Paloma adjusted the bowls under their arm. "It's seniority. I swore my oath there. The chapel knows me."

"Chapels don't know people. Chapels are buildings that lost an argument with the ocean."

They had reached the stairs down to the drowned chapel by then, and Brannic stopped arguing, because even he went quiet here.

* * *

The chapel of Vess had been built on a headland, back when headlands were allowed to be dry. Then the sea had come up — slowly, politely, over a century or more, the way debts come due — and now the chapel stood waist-deep in the tide pool at the base of the cliffs, its bell tower leaning like a drunk uncle at a wedding, its doors permanently ajar beneath the waterline. Twice a day the tide withdrew far enough to expose the threshold, and twice a day it came back, and the chapel tolerated this arrangement with the patience of stone.

Inside, the water never rose past the third pew. Nobody knew why. The sea came right to the doors, licked the threshold, and stopped, as if it too were waiting to be invited.

Paloma waded in, boots and all. This was traditional. You did not enter Vess's house dry; dryness implied you expected to leave unchanged, and Vess had opinions about people who expected things.

The interior smelled of brine and cold candle smoke. The altar was a slab of black basalt furred with barnacles, and upon it — Paloma set the bowls down carefully — the salt would stay white until the next tide took it, and the silver would stay bright, though there was no one to spend it and nowhere down here to spend anything at all.

Payment rendered. Twenty years of payments rendered. Paloma knelt on the submerged flagstones, water soaking through to the knee, and performed the office: hands open, palms up, eyes lowered, mouth shut.

That last part was the whole religion, really. Vess was the silent god. No prayers were spoken *to* Vess — speaking was for gods who answered, and Vess had not answered in living memory. Instead you held your need in your cupped hands like water and offered it up silently, and the god, if the god existed, if the god was listening, if the god was anything at all — kept its silence, and you kept yours, and between the two silences something passed that no one had ever satisfactorily described.

Paloma had sworn their oath here at nineteen, kneeling on these same stones, with Ivetta's hand on their shoulder and the tide in their boots. They remembered the words. They remembered everything about that day except, maddeningly, the face of the priest who'd administered it — a gap in their memory shaped exactly like a person, smooth-edged, painless, wrong.

They were halfway through the office when they noticed the candles.

There were no candles. That was the tradition. The chapel was lit by whatever light fell through the water, green and wavering, and worshippers brought nothing that burned, because fire was a language and Vess spoke none.

But on the ledge beside the altar, someone had wedged three stubs of tallow candle into the barnacles, and lit them. Underwater. They burned anyway, small flames trembling inside little bubbles of air, and Paloma stared at them for a long moment with the specific discomfort of a bookkeeper who has found money in a drawer where the ledger says there should be none.

Then they saw the rest.

A child's shoe, placed neatly on the second pew. A knot of red thread. A clay whistle, chipped at the mouthpiece. A strip of linen with writing on it, rolled tight and tied with hair. And on the flat top of the altar itself, arranged around the bowls of salt and silver with a care that made Paloma's chest hurt: a handful of small smooth stones, each one painted with a single closed eye.

Offerings. Fresh ones. Days old at most — the tallow hadn't slumped, the linen hadn't bloomed with mold. Someone had been here recently. More than one someone, probably; the offerings were in different hands, different styles, the shoe belonging to a child of perhaps six, the stones painted by someone with a steady brush and a patient heart.

Strangers were praying to Vess.

Not the guild. Not Paloma, keeping the old forms out of duty and habit and something they refused to name in front of themselves. Strangers. People who had grown up in a Corvane where Vess was a footnote, a dead god, a name the usurper's histories mentioned only to explain why the drowned chapel was permitted to stand — *a monument to a faith extinguished by the Conquest, preserved for its historical interest.* Schoolchildren came and threw pebbles at the bell tower. Paloma knew this because Brannic had done it, as a boy, and confessed it once in his cups with genuine remorse.

And now someone was leaving a child's shoe on the pew, and lighting candles underwater, and painting eyes on stones.

Paloma picked up one of the stones. It sat warm in their palm, which was impossible, everything down here being cold as a tax collector's conscience. The painted eye was closed, lashes fine as spider silk. It was the kind of thing a mother might make. Or a daughter. Or anyone who had wished very hard for something and been told, all their life, that wishing was a superstition for the conquered.

*Please,* said the stone, in the wordless way stones say things. *Please. Please.*

Paloma put it back exactly where it had been, aligned with the others, and found their hands were not entirely steady.

They finished the office. Hands open, palms up, mouth shut. But for the first time in twenty years, the silence felt less like ritual and more like eavesdropping — as if Paloma were standing in a room where someone else's conversation had just gone quiet, and everyone present knew something they didn't.

On the way out, thigh-deep in green light, they paused at the threshold and looked back. The candles burned in their bubbles. The salt lay white. The silver gleamed for no one.

"Someone's been spending your money," Paloma told the chapel, quietly. "I'd look into that, if I were you."

The chapel, being a building that had lost an argument with the ocean, said nothing.

But the tide, which had been due to return, hesitated. Just for a moment. Just long enough for Paloma to climb the stairs dry-shod.

* * *

Ivetta received the report in her office, which occupied the guildhouse's only room with a view, on the theory that the guildmaster should always be able to see trouble coming. The view currently consisted of rooftops, laundry, and the distant glitter of the harbor, none of which appeared to be plotting anything.

She listened without interrupting, which was worse than interrupting. Ivetta was a small woman assembled mostly from angles and disapproval, with grey hair cut short for practicality and hands that had strangled better arguments than yours. She had run the Guild of Impossible Things for thirty years by the simple method of being the least impressed person in any room.

"Candles," she said, when Paloma finished.

"Burning underwater."

"That's not possible."

"No."

"And yet."

"And yet."

Ivetta took off her spectacles, polished them on her sleeve, and put them back on, which was her version of pacing the floor and tearing her hair. "The shoe?"

"A child's. Left sole. Well-worn."

"The stones with the eyes?"

"Seven of them. Fresh paint."

Ivetta leaned back in her chair until it creaked in protest. Through the window behind her, a gull hung motionless on the wind, watching the office with what Paloma had always felt was unprofessional interest.

"Do you know," Ivetta said, "when I was a girl, my grandmother told me the sea keeps everything it's given. Every coin thrown for luck, every body, every promise. She said the sea doesn't spend, it only holds. And then she'd make me throw a coin off the pier, and I'd watch it go down, and I'd think: there goes my luck, into the vault." She tapped the desk. "Twenty years we've paid that chapel, Paloma. Salt and silver, four times a year, since before you were sworn. It's the oldest account in our books. Older than the guild charter. And in twenty years, no one has touched those offerings but the tide."

"I know."

"So either the tide has learned piety—" she spread her hands, "—or someone has decided the account is active again. Someone who lights candles underwater and paints eyes on stones and leaves a child's shoe in a god's house. And I want to know who."

"Why?" Paloma asked. It was a dangerous question to ask Ivetta, but the morning had made them reckless. "With respect. Worship is free. It's the one trade we don't hold a license for. Some fisherfolk remember the old rites, some grandmother teaches her granddaughter the eye-stones — what does it cost us?"

Ivetta looked at them for a long moment. When she spoke again, her voice had shifted into the register she used for contracts and funerals.

"Because worship isn't free, Paloma. Worship is a *debt*. It's the first debt there ever was. Somebody gives you rain, you owe harvest. Somebody gives you victory, you owe blood. Somebody gives you silence for twenty years—" her eyes narrowed, "—you start wondering what the silence was *for*. Whether it was death, or whether it was something quieter. And whether whoever's kneeling in that chapel knows something the rest of us don't."

She opened the ledger on her desk — the master ledger, chained to the furniture, older than the charter — and turned to a page near the front. Paloma glimpsed the entry before she covered it with her hand: the drowned chapel account, written in ink so old it had gone brown, in a hand that was not Ivetta's and not the dead woman's and not anyone's Paloma recognized.

*Payments current,* the page said. *Debt outstanding.*

"Find them," Ivetta said. "Whoever's praying. Gently — I don't want frightened faithful, I want *informed* faithful. Ask what they're asking for. Ask what they think they'll receive. And ask—" she paused, and for a moment the angles of her softened into something almost human, "—ask whether they've gotten an answer."

"They won't have," Paloma said. "Vess doesn't answer. Vess hasn't answered in—"

"In twenty years. Yes. I know the doctrine; I helped write half of it." Ivetta put her spectacles away. "Humor me. Ask anyway."

* * *

Brannic was waiting outside the office, ostensibly reorganizing a shelf of invoices that did not need reorganizing.

"Well?" he said.

"I'm to find the worshippers."

"Ah." He nodded sagely. "And how does one find people who pray in secret at a drowned chapel at odd hours? Asking for a friend. The friend is me. I'm nosy."

"Patience," Paloma said. "I'll watch the stairs. Whoever leaves offerings will come back for the shoe, or the stones, or to check the candles. Devotion is a habit, and habits keep schedules."

"Mm. And if they don't come back?"

"Then I'll ask around the lower terraces. Someone taught those people the rites. Rites have teachers. Teachers have neighbors, and neighbors have tongues."

Brannic considered this. "You know, when you say it like that, accounting sounds genuinely sinister. 'Rites have teachers. Teachers have neighbors.' You should say that at parties."

"I'm not invited to parties."

"That's because you say things like that at parties."

He clapped them on the shoulder — Brannic's shoulder-claps had been known to realign spines — and wandered off toward the inventory rooms, humming. Paloma watched him go, then stood a moment in the corridor, doing the thing they did instead of thinking: counting. Stairs from the office to the street: fourteen. Streets to the headland path: six. Hours until the next low tide: five and a half.

Somewhere in that arithmetic, uninvited, slid the memory of the candle flames burning in their bubbles of air, steady as hearts. Fire was a language, and Vess spoke none. So whose language was that?

And underneath it, quieter, the other thing. The thing from the chapel they had not reported, because there was nothing to report, because it had been nothing: the way the tide had hesitated. The way the whole morning had felt, afterward, like a room where a conversation had stopped just as Paloma walked in.

The past knocking politely, they thought. And the trouble with polite knocks is that they always come back louder.

Five hours later, soaked to the ribs behind the headland rocks, Paloma got their answer sooner than expected — though not the one they'd ordered. Because the first stranger to come down the chapel stairs that evening, moving with the unhurried certainty of someone who has traveled a very long way and expects to be received, wore the grey traveling coat of the Conquest's Inquisition, and carried a truce banner folded under one arm like a letter she intended to deliver personally.

Paloma knew the walk before they saw the face. They had spent twelve years trying to forget it and had evidently failed the examination.

Sera Halvard came down to the water's edge, saw them standing among the rocks, and stopped.

"Paloma," she said.

"Sera," said Paloma, and discovered that twenty years of silent prayer had prepared them for absolutely nothing at all.
