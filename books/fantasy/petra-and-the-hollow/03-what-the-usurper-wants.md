## 3. What the Usurper Wants

The truce banner arrived on a Tuesday, which Paloma felt was typical of the usurper's people. They had no sense of occasion. If you were going to march the vanguard of a conquering army into a free city under a flag of peace, you ought at least to do it on a day that meant something—a feast day, an anniversary, the birthday of someone's least favorite aunt. Instead it was a Tuesday, gray and drizzling, and the banner itself was cheap dye that bled pink in the rain so that the golden sun of the Concordat looked less like a symbol of divine order and more like a wound in a wet shirt.

Paloma watched from the window of the guildhouse's counting room, where they had been reconciling a shipment of bottled grief that had come in three jars short.

"Three short," they muttered, tapping the manifest. "Somebody in Harrowgate is skimming sorrow."

"You could file a complaint," said Doss, the junior clerk, who was seventeen and believed that institutions worked.

"I could," Paloma agreed, "and then somebody in Harrowgate would file a complaint about my complaint, and by midwinter we'd have a ledger of complaints about complaints, and no one would have their grief." They set down the pen. "That's how empires start, Doss. Not with armies. With paperwork nobody resolves."

It was at this point that the bell above the counting room door rang, and a boy from the street floor came up breathless to say that there were soldiers in the square, and a banner, and—this was the part that made him swallow—"a lady in gray asking for the Guild of Impossible Things by name."

"By name?" Paloma said.

"She said she wanted the keepers of ledgers. She said—" the boy screwed up his face, remembering "—she said 'the ones who trade in what can't be owned.'"

Doss went pale. Paloma did not go pale, because they had spent twenty years learning not to, but something behind their ribs went very quiet and very still, the way a house goes still when you hear a footstep upstairs in a home you thought empty.

"Show her up," Paloma said. "No. Wait." They looked out the window again. The rain. The bleeding banner. The woman in gray standing apart from her escort, hood back, not bothering with it, as if to say *I am not hiding, I have never once hidden from you.*

Paloma knew that stance. Paloma had loved that stance.

"Clear the counting room," they said. "And Doss—if I shout, run for Ivetta. If I laugh, run for Ivetta anyway. It'll mean something worse has happened."

* * *

Sera Halvard had not changed, or had changed only in the ways that mattered least and hurt most. Her hair was shorter, cropped for the gray traveling cloak of the Inquisition. There was a new scar through one eyebrow, thin and white, a signature someone else had left on her face. She stood in the middle of the counting room with rain beading on her shoulders and looked around at the shelves of impossible inventory—the jar of last words, the crate marked *SPRING (ASSORTED)*, the locked cabinet where the guild kept its single sample of a dead man's laughter—and something in her expression suggested she found it all exactly as ridiculous as she had always found Paloma's profession.

"Inquisitor Halvard," Paloma said, from behind their desk, because a desk was a kind of armor too.

"Keeper Eriksen." Sera's voice was flat and official and did not tremble even slightly, which Paloma respected and hated in equal measure. "I'm here under truce. The Concordat recognizes Corvane's independence—for now—and I've come on legitimate scholarly business."

"Scholarly business." Paloma folded their hands. "With six soldiers."

"Four soldiers and two archivists. The archivists are the dangerous ones; you know how scholars get."

Despite everything—despite twenty years, despite the conquest, despite the scar and the banner and the wet shirt with its wounded sun—Paloma felt the corner of their mouth twitch. That was the cruelty of it. Sera had always been funny. Nobody warned you that the person sent to destroy your life might still be the person who made you laugh.

"What does the Concordat want with scholarship, Sera? You people burn books. You don't read them."

"We're reading this time." Sera unslung a leather case from her shoulder and set it on the desk—not opening it, just letting it sit there like a bet placed. "Records of pre-conquest worship. Specifically"—and here her voice did change, dropped half a tone, went careful—"worship of Vess."

The silence that followed had texture. Paloma heard the rain against the glass, heard Doss's quill stop scratching somewhere below, heard the faint slosh of the jar of last words settling on its shelf, which it sometimes did, though the guild had never determined whether this was physics or opinion.

"Vess is dead," Paloma said. "Twenty years dead. Your own histories say so. Your usurper built his throne on the grave."

"And yet." Sera opened the case. Inside were papers, and Paloma's professional eye catalogued them before their heart could intervene: copies of parish rolls, tithe records, hymn fragments, a rubbing taken from a temple stone. All of it old. All of it Vess. "And yet people are praying again, Keeper. In villages your free cities think are loyal to me. At shrines you people forgot you had. And the prayers are being answered."

"That's superstition."

"The fisherman at Saltmere wished for a full net and got one. His neighbors drowned in it." Sera said it without drama, which somehow made it worse. "A child in Dunmark asked for her mother back. Something wearing her mother's face tucked her into bed for three nights and then asked the girl, quite politely, to return the favor of her memories. The girl hasn't spoken since. These are not rumors, Paloma. These are reports I wrote myself, in my own hand, and signed."

Paloma looked at the papers and did not touch them. Twenty years of keeping ledgers had taught them that paper never lied but paper could be made to say anything, and the difference between those two facts was the entire discipline of their life.

"Why bring this to us?"

"Because your guild traded in Vess-tithes before the conquest. Because your ledgers hold names, dates, offerings—the only complete record of how Vess was worshipped when Vess was alive enough to answer." Sera closed the case. "The Concordat wants to understand what's waking up. My lord the Arch-Inquisitor wants to cage it. I want—" she stopped. For the first time the official voice cracked, just a hairline fracture, just enough to see the old warmth underneath like light through a shuttered window. "I want to know if it's really him answering. Or something wearing him. Like the thing wearing that child's mother."

*Him.* Paloma caught the pronoun and held it carefully, the way you'd catch a glass someone knocked toward a table edge. Sera had always used *him* for Vess. Paloma had always used *it*. Small theologies. They had argued about it once, in another life, in a bed in a border town, laughing, naked, certain the world would let them keep arguing forever.

"You knew I served Vess," Paloma said quietly.

"I knew you *loved* Vess. There's a difference, and I was hoping—" Sera exhaled. "I was hoping the difference still held."

"It holds."

"Then help me. Under truce. Records only. No oaths, no rites, nothing your guildmaster can call treason. Paper for paper. I'll give you everything the Concordat knows about the answered prayers—all of it, every report—and you give me the worship records. Fair trade." She almost smiled. "You of all people should appreciate a fair trade."

"Oh, I appreciate it," Paloma said. "That's what frightens me. A bad offer I could refuse on principle. This one's good, and good offers are how you get people to hand over the knife handle-first."

They looked at each other across the desk, across twenty years, across the width of a conquered country. Rain ran down the window behind Sera and made the light shiver.

"You look well," Sera said, and the officialdom fell away entirely for one sentence, one bare human sentence, and Paloma felt it land somewhere beneath the ribs where they kept the things they didn't count. "Older. But well."

"You look like someone gave you a scar."

"A man in Vell gave me the scar. He was wrong about several things and I corrected him." She shrugged. "It happens."

"Sera." Paloma stood, finally, because sitting behind the desk had begun to feel like hiding. "Why are you really here? Not the Arch-Inquisitor's errand. Yours."

She held their gaze. She had never been able to lie to them—that had been the joke between them, once, that an inquisitor-in-training who couldn't lie was like a locksmith afraid of doors—and now she didn't try.

"Because I've started hearing him too," she said. "In my prayers. Which I shouldn't have, because I stopped believing in anything but the law eleven years ago. And because every report I write ends up on the Arch-Inquisitor's desk, and every report makes him hungrier, and I am starting to suspect that the man who wants to cage the returning gods built his throne on top of the first one's grave for a reason." She picked up her case. "Think about the trade, Keeper. I'm at the Gilded Anchor until the week's end. Truce holds seven days. After that, I do what I was ordered to do, and neither of us will enjoy finding out what that is."

At the door she paused, hand on the frame, back to Paloma.

"The jar on your third shelf," she said. "The one that keeps sloshing. It's been doing that since I walked in, hasn't it."

Paloma said nothing.

"It did that in your rooms in Vell, too. Before the conquest. When we—" She stopped herself. Straightened. Became the inquisitor again, all gray wool and official spine. "Seven days, Paloma."

Then she was gone, and the stairs took her footsteps away one by one, and Paloma stood alone in the counting room among the impossible inventory, listening to the rain, listening to the jar of last words sloshing softly in its shelf like something impatient to be said.

They sat down. They pulled the current ledger toward them. And there, at the top of the fresh page, in ink they had not written, in handwriting that was almost—but not quite—their own, a new entry had appeared:

*Received: one visit, unpaid. Owed: everything.*

Below it, smaller, in letters that seemed to press up from underneath the page rather than sink into it:

*Ask her about Merrow's Bend.*

Paloma's hands went cold. They had never spoken that village's name aloud in twenty years. It existed nowhere in any ledger, in any memory they permitted themselves, in any prayer.

Except, apparently, in the handwriting of a god everyone believed was dead—who had just told them that the woman they had loved, the woman sent to destroy them, already knew.
