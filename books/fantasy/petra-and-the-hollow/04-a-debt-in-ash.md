## 4. A Debt in Ash

The ledgers of the Guild of Impossible Things were kept in a cellar beneath Corvane's counting-house, in a room that smelled of tallow, dust, and old decisions. There were forty-one of them, bound in leather gone soft as earlobes, and each one was chained to its shelf—not to prevent theft, but because a few of the older volumes had been known to wander. Paloma had personally witnessed Ledger Nine attempt to leave through a wall in 1189, and had never entirely forgiven it.

"Good morning," Paloma said to the room, as they did every morning, because it was bad luck to enter a room full of debts without greeting them politely.

The room, as always, declined to answer. This was, until recently, one of the few reliable things in Paloma's life.

They lit the lamps, shrugged off their coat, and settled at the long table with Ledger Thirty-Seven open before them—the current volume, the one that held everything since the conquest. The morning's work was reconciliation: matching entries against receipts, confirming that what the guild had sold was what the guild had actually delivered. Bottled grief, three vials, sold to a widow in the Salt Quarter who wanted to feel her husband's death properly instead of around its edges—delivered, receipt signed, grief confirmed authentic by taste. Borrowed luck, one year's worth, leased to a card-sharp named Osk who had, against all actuarial expectation, survived the year and returned it slightly used. A childhood, eleven years of it, traded by a man who had decided he'd rather not have been a boy in that house, thank you very much; the guild had warehoused it carefully and had no intention of ever selling it back to him, though they'd told him the option remained open if he changed his mind. He hadn't. He sent flowers every spring.

Paloma worked down the columns with a dry quill and a drier heart, and for an hour or two the world was arithmetic, which was the closest thing to peace the world offered them.

Then the prayer came back.

It arrived the way weather arrives—not announced, just suddenly *there*, pressing against the inside of their skull like a thumb testing fruit. It was the same prayer from two nights ago, the one that had slipped through their fingers mid-entry like a fish made of candle smoke: *let her sleep, let her sleep, please, let her sleep.* Someone in Corvane was praying to Vess. Someone who didn't know better, or knew exactly well enough. And this time it didn't slip away. This time it sat down in Paloma's chest like a guest who intended to stay for supper.

The lamp flames leaned east, all six of them, in a room with no draft.

"Well," Paloma said to the room. "That's new."

The room still didn't answer, but it felt, for the first time in twenty years, like it was *considering* it.

* * *

Paloma did what any sensible person does when a dead god starts taking messages: they finished the column first. Grief reconciled, luck reconciled, childhood accounted for. Then they put down the quill, pressed both palms flat on the table, and let themselves think about it, which took considerably longer than the arithmetic had.

Twenty years ago, Vess had gone silent. Not quietly—there had been a night when every worshipper in every shrine across nine cities had woken at the same instant with the sensation of a door closing somewhere very far below them, and after that, nothing. No answers. No warmth. No presence. The god of thresholds and small mercies had simply stopped being home, and the world had done what the world always does with absent gods: it had written them off, burned their shrines where convenient, and gotten on with things.

Everyone except the guild. And everyone except Paloma, who had kept swearing the oath anyway, alone, in a drowned chapel, out of a stubbornness they'd never fully examined because examining it would have required admitting it might be love.

And now prayers were being answered. Which meant either Vess was back—or something was wearing Vess's silence like a coat.

There was really only one way to find out, and it was downstairs, in the deep stacks, where the guild kept the old volumes. The pre-conquest ones. The ones with Vess's name in them.

Paloma took a lamp and went down.

* * *

Ledger Twelve was the god's volume. Every guild had one—a volume for accounts involving powers too large to invoice normally—and Ledger Twelve was Vess's: every offering received, every mercy granted, every threshold opened, recorded in a hand so fine it looked like frost forming. Paloma had not opened it since the silence began. Opening it felt like visiting a grave, and Paloma had never been good at graves. They stood outside them and talked too loudly.

But the deep stacks were cold, and the lamp was steady, and twenty years is long enough to make even a coward curious.

The chain on Ledger Twelve's shelf had rusted through. That was the first wrong thing. The guild replaced those chains every decade; Ivetta was fanatical about it. This one had rusted as if a hundred years had passed instead of ten, and when Paloma lifted the book free, it was warm.

Books are not warm. Cellars are not warm. Paloma stood there holding a warm book in a cold room and thought, with the particular clarity of someone standing on ice and hearing it speak: *ah.*

"I'm opening you," they told it sternly, "because it's my job. Not because you asked."

The book, being a book, said nothing, but the warmth pulsed once against their palms, and Paloma chose—with a professionalism they intended to be proud of later—to interpret that as professional acknowledgment.

They carried it up to the long table, set it beside Ledger Thirty-Seven, and opened it to the last written page.

The last entry was dated twenty years and two days ago. Of course it was. Two days *before* the silence. The entry read, in that frost-fine hand:

*Received: one prayer, unanswered, from P.E., sworn servant. Held in trust.*

Paloma stared at it. They remembered the prayer. They'd been thirty-one, kneeling in the drowned chapel with seawater in their boots, asking Vess for something small and human—they couldn't even remember what now, some plea about Sera, about the war coming, about wanting to keep one thing—and the god had not answered. And the guild's own ledger had recorded the non-answer as a *holding*. As if the prayer hadn't failed. As if it had been *filed*.

Held in trust. For twenty years. In a vault of silence.

"You absolute bureaucratic horror," Paloma whispered, with something between fury and awe. "You've had my mail this whole time."

The warmth in the pages shifted. Almost sheepish. Almost pleased.

Paloma closed the book gently, the way you close a door on a sleeping animal, and then—because they were thorough, because thoroughness was the only virtue they'd ever fully managed—they flipped back through the earlier pages to see what else the god had been holding in trust.

That was how they found Merrow's Bend.

* * *

It was four hundred pages back, in a section of Ledger Twelve that shouldn't have existed, because Paloma had audited this volume every year of their service and the section had never been there before. Between "Thresholds Opened, Wartime" and "Offerings Refused" sat eight pages labeled, in handwriting that was almost—but not quite—Paloma's own:

*Debts Outstanding: Merrow's Bend.*

Paloma did not turn the pages so much as the pages turned themselves under their fingers, eager, like a dog bringing a stick.

Merrow's Bend had been a fishing village on the estuary, ninety-one souls, famous for eels and for a bell that rang itself when storms were coming. In the fourth year of the silence, in the worst of the conquest, the usurper's provosts had come to the Bend looking for Vess-worshippers, and the village had hidden them—hid them badly, hid them bravely—and word had reached the guild that the provosts would return with fire unless the village was emptied of its faith by dawn.

And Vess was silent. And Paloma was Vess's hand in the world, oath-bound, and the logic of that time had seemed clean as a blade: a god who cannot act needs hands, and a hand that will not act is only decoration. So Paloma had gone to Merrow's Bend in the dark before the provosts came, and they had done what the oath demanded. They had taken the village's faith from it. All of it. By force, by fear, by means Paloma's memory kept in a locked room at the bottom of themselves and fed only after midnight.

Ninety-one people had woken in the morning unable to remember why they'd ever knelt. The provosts had found a village of bland unbelievers and moved on. The village had lived. And Paloma had walked home through the reeds with their hands shaking and told themselves, for nineteen years, that it had been mercy. That it had been the lesser horror. That the ledger would show it as a debt *paid*.

The ledger showed no such thing.

*Debt incurred: the faith of ninety-one souls, taken without consent, in the name of Vess.*
*Status: UNPAID.*
*Creditor: the people of Merrow's Bend.*
*Note:* —and here the handwriting changed, became finer, colder, became unmistakably not-Paloma's— *a debt owed to the faithful may be collected in kind. Faith taken may be returned. Memory taken may be returned. When the account comes due, all of it comes due at once.*

"All of it," Paloma repeated. Their voice sounded fine. Their hands did not. "All of it at once. Ninety-one people remembering everything I took, everything they suffered, everything—" They stopped. Because here was the thing they had spent nineteen years not thinking: the villagers' faith hadn't been the only thing in the room that night. Memories cling to faith the way damp clings to salt. When Paloma had stripped the Bend bare, they had stripped *everything*—weddings, funerals, the smell of the eel-smoke, mothers' faces. Ninety-one people had woken up hollow and called it peace, because they had nothing left to know the difference with.

And Paloma had carried all of it out of the village in their own head, because the oath required the collector to hold what was collected. Twenty years of ninety-one lives, warehoused in the locked room at the bottom of them, feeding only after midnight.

The ink moved.

Paloma watched it happen. Watched the words *UNPAID* shiver, and darken, and begin—slowly, wetly, like something surfacing through pond-water—to crawl toward the edge of the page. Toward the margin. Toward the space where a collector's mark went. Toward the space where *P.E.* was written in the register of sworn servants.

"No," Paloma said.

The ink paused. Considered. Continued.

"No." They slammed the ledger shut, and the sound rang through the cellar like a struck bell, and every lamp in the room guttered at once, and in the half-dark Paloma heard—faintly, from inside the closed covers, from inside the locked room at the bottom of themselves—the sound of a village bell ringing before a storm.

They sat there a long while. Long enough for the lamps to recover. Long enough for their hands to stop shaking, mostly.

Then they did what a keeper of ledgers does. They got a fresh sheet, and they wrote, in their own careful hand:

*Entry: Ledger Twelve, anomalous section, pages unnumbered. Debt of Merrow's Bend discovered active. Ink mobile. Creditor status: waking. Collector (myself) notified by the account itself. Recommend immediate consultation with Guildmaster Ivetta. Recommend immediate consultation with anyone. Recommend, privately, and off the record: that I find out who is praying in this city, and ask them what in the name of every silent god they think they're doing.*

They blotted it. They squared the sheet. And then, because the alternative was sitting in the dark listening to bells, they picked up Ledger Twelve again—it was warmer now, almost feverish, and it leaned into their hands like a cat—and carried it up the stairs to find Ivetta, praying with genuine and unaccustomed fervor to a god who had ignored them for twenty years that the old woman would already be awake.

She was. She was standing at the top of the stairs with a lamp of her own, and she was looking at the book in Paloma's arms the way you look at a dog that has come home with blood on its mouth.

"Ivetta," Paloma said. "We have a problem with the accounts."

"I know," said Ivetta. "I dreamed about eels. Put it on the table, child, and fetch me the strong tea. It's going to be one of those mornings."

Behind them, faint and patient, from the depths of the cellar they had just climbed out of, the bell of Merrow's Bend rang twice more.
