## 5. The Guild of Impossible Things

The guildhouse in Corvane had once been a counting-house for salt merchants, and it still smelled faintly of the sea and of money that had changed hands honestly, which made it unique among buildings Paloma had known. Now its vaults held stranger stock: bottled grief arranged by vintage, luck borrowed against future misfortune, a single jar containing one year of a shepherd's childhood, purchased from him when he was sixty and homesick for being nine.

Paloma climbed the stairs to the council room with the ledgers under their arm and the taste of ash still in their mouth. Three days since they'd found the page for Merrow's Bend, three days since the ink had begun to move like something swimming upstream. They had not told Ivetta about the ink. They had told her only that an audit was needed, and she had summoned the partners, which was either exactly what Paloma wanted or exactly what would get them all killed, depending on how the evening went.

The council room was round, which Ivetta insisted upon because "a table with corners lets someone sit at the head, and no one at this guild sits at anyone's head." Candles burned in dishes of salt. The map of the free cities hung on the wall, annotated in Ivetta's crabbed hand with toll roads, safehouses, and one small drawing of a dog labeled *Briar, do not trust*.

Ivetta herself sat with her boots on a second chair, a woman built like a dock crane and dressed like a widow who had won her mourning clothes in a card game. She was fifty-three, had buried two husbands and one guildmaster before her, and greeted catastrophe the way other people greeted rain — with a sigh, a shrug, and a search for something waterproof.

"Sit," she said. "Everyone's here except the priest, who is late, because he's always late, because he says punctuality is a form of pride and he's too proud to be humble about it."

"I heard that," said a voice from the doorway.

Father Ambrose entered the way weather enters — gradually, apologetically, taking up more room than expected. He was a broad, soft man in robes that had been dyed by three different faiths and never quite matched, and around his neck he wore three amulets: a sun-wheel, a river-stone, and a small brass door. All three gods were dead. He had outlived them the way a man outlives dogs he loved, and he spoke of them constantly, fondly, and without any expectation of reply.

"You're late," said Ivetta.

"Punctuality is a form of—"

"Yes, yes. Sit down."

The third partner was already present, though Paloma hadn't seen them arrive, which was normal. Wren sat in the corner shadow as if the shadow had grown a person. Small, quick-eyed, wrapped in gray layers, they were the guild's thief — but not of purses. Wren stole names. A name, properly taken, was worth more than a purse; a man whose name was stolen could not collect his own debts, sign his own confessions, or be remembered accurately by his own mother. Wren kept theirs in a locked box and introduced themselves to no one twice.

"Ledger-keeper," Wren said, by way of greeting. Their voice was dry as old paper. "You look terrible."

"They've been auditing," said Ivetta.

"That would do it."

The fourth partner came last, because the fourth partner was always last, having farther to travel than anyone. Master Ondine Quill entered through the window.

She did this every time. She was seventy years old, tiny, fierce, and had spent forty years mapping places that no longer existed — drowned islands, burned cities, a forest the treaty-makers had unmade on paper until the trees forgot how to stand. She climbed through windows on principle. "Doors," she had once explained to Paloma, "are for people who accept the architecture."

She landed lightly on the floorboards, brushed off her coat, and looked around the circle with bright, unsettling eyes.

"Well," she said. "You've all felt it too, then."

* * *

"Felt what?" Paloma asked, because nobody else seemed inclined to say it aloud.

Ondine settled into her chair like a bird onto a branch. "The world has a seam in it, child. Always has. Most people walk over seams all their lives and never notice. But a seam that's been sewn shut for twenty years—" she held up one knotted finger "—doesn't start pulling at its stitches unless something on the other side is tugging."

"The prayers," said Father Ambrose quietly.

Everyone looked at him. He spread his hands. "I keep candles lit for my three. Dead gods don't answer, but they listen — or so I choose to believe, and belief is the one trade I never charge for. Last week, for the first time in twenty years, I got the distinct impression that someone on the other end of the candle had picked up."

"You said nothing," Ivetta said.

"I'm saying it now." He smiled, gentle and sad. "Also, I wanted to see if saying it out loud made me feel foolish. It did, slightly. But less foolish than silence."

Paloma set the ledgers on the table. The sound they made was heavier than paper should make. "Then I'll say mine. On Tuesday, mid-entry, a prayer came through my hands. Not to me. Through me. The ledger recorded it before I understood what I was writing. An answered prayer. First one in twenty years, and it didn't come to a temple or a priest. It came to a bookkeeper."

Wren leaned forward, and even the shadow around them seemed to lean. "Whose prayer?"

"A fisherman in Saltrow. He asked for a full net." Paloma kept their voice level. "He got one. So full it sank his boat, and the wake drowned two of his neighbors' children."

Silence. The candles crackled.

"And there's more," Paloma said. "The answers aren't coming from nowhere. They're coming from somewhere, and whatever's answering is using material it shouldn't have. Stolen memories. Borrowed faces. I've seen the entries. The god isn't creating miracles. It's spending something."

"Which brings us to inventory," said Ivetta, and swung her boots off the chair, and for the first time since Paloma had known her, she looked tired. "Ondine. Show them."

* * *

Ondine rose and went to the wall map. She did not touch the map of the free cities. Instead she pulled a cord beside it, and the whole map swung away from the wall like a cabinet door, revealing a second map behind it — one Paloma had never seen, though they had kept this guild's books for eleven years.

It was a map of nothing.

That was the first impression: blank vellum, empty space where a country should have been, between the mountains and the sea, roughly the size of a kingdom. But looking longer, Paloma saw it wasn't empty. There were lines there, ghost-faint, drawn in something silver that shifted when you weren't watching directly. Rivers. Roads. The suggestion of cities, sketched the way you might sketch a face from memory after decades.

"This," Ondine said, "is Vess's country."

"There is no such country," said Paloma automatically, because everyone knew there was no such country. Schoolchildren learned it as a lesson in cartography: here the mountains, here the sea, and here — nothing, a gap, an absence so complete that maps printed the words *Terra Nullius* across it in letters of legal certainty.

"No," Ondine agreed. "There isn't. That's rather the point. Twenty-two years ago, the treaty of Erastus rewrote the borders, and the rewriting took. Every map, every deed, every birth record, every grave marker — struck. And it wasn't just ink, child. It was the place itself. The treaty didn't redraw the borders around Vess's nation. It erased the nation from under them. The rivers forgot their courses. The cities forgot their names. The people—" she paused, and her bright eyes dimmed for just a moment "—the people forgot they had ever been."

"Witnesses," said Wren softly. "That's why there were no witnesses. Not that they were killed. That they couldn't remember."

"The usurper's founding miracle," said Father Ambrose. He touched the brass door at his throat. "My gods died around the same season. I used to think it was grief that killed them. Grief, or neglect. Now I wonder if they died of eviction."

Paloma stared at the ghost-lines. Something cold was assembling itself in their chest, piece by piece, like a debt accruing interest. "And Vess?"

"Vess was inside it," Ondine said. "Gods are anchored to their ground, child. Everyone knows that. Take away the ground — truly take it, not conquer it but *unwrite* it — and everything standing on it goes with it. The usurper didn't kill Vess. He locked the god outside reality, along with its whole country, and threw away the key, and the key was the memory that the country had ever existed."

"But the prayers are getting through now."

"Now they are." Ondine tapped the seam of the hidden map, right at its center, where the silver lines converged into a knot of light. "Because erasure is expensive. Even treaties run out of credit. The unwriting is wearing thin, and something on the other side has noticed, and it is pushing."

"The Hollow," said Ivetta.

"The Hollow," Ondine agreed.

* * *

Paloma had heard the word before, the way you hear a word in another room — often enough to recognize it, never enough to know it. Partners said it in low voices. Contracts referenced it obliquely, clause forty-one, *in the event of Hollow conditions*. Paloma had assumed it was a place. A valley, perhaps, or a smuggler's route.

"It's a door," Ondine said. "The only one. A thin place where the erasure never quite closed — a valley that appears on no map because it belongs to neither side of the seam. On one side, our world. On the other side—" she gestured at the ghost-country "—everything that was taken. The land itself. The dead. The names. And, if the prayers are any indication, one very large, very patient, very angry god."

"Open it," said Wren, "and we don't have to guess what's on the other side anymore. It gets to come visit us."

"Why would we ever open it?" Paloma asked.

Ivetta answered, and her voice had none of its usual dryness. "Because in six months, maybe eight, the usurper's armies finish eating the borderlands and come for the free cities. You've read the same reports I have. And when they come, we will need somewhere to put several hundred thousand people who cannot fight. A door out of the world, ledger-keeper. Think about the logistics."

Paloma thought about the logistics. It was, professionally speaking, a beautiful problem. It was also insane.

"How do we open it?"

Ondine and Ivetta exchanged a look — the particular look of partners who have agreed in advance which of them will deliver bad news.

"Debts," said Ondine.

"Come again?"

"The Hollow isn't locked with a key, child. It's locked with an account. The erasure was a transaction — the largest one ever conducted. A whole country, sold for nothing, its debts unpaid, its obligations voided, its god cheated of worship and its people cheated of existence. The universe keeps books too, whatever the usurper believes. And the Hollow will open for exactly one reason." She held up one finger. "When every debt written against that erasure has been settled. Paid, forgiven, transferred — the how doesn't matter. The balance does. Zero, and the door opens. Anything outstanding, and it stays shut."

Paloma looked down at the ledgers on the table. Eleven years of entries. Bottled grief, borrowed luck, a year of childhood. And deeper in, older in, the pages the guild rarely spoke of — the pre-conquest accounts, inherited from the old guildmaster, including—

"Including Merrow's Bend," Paloma said.

The name landed in the room like a dropped knife.

"You know that entry," Ivetta said. It wasn't a question.

"I audited it Tuesday night." Paloma's mouth was dry. "A village in the borderlands, destroyed in Vess's silent years. Recorded as a debt owed — to whom, the entry doesn't say. Collected: nothing. And the ink is moving, Ivetta. The ink is *moving*, and I didn't tell you because telling you sounded like madness, and now I'm telling you anyway, because if the door opens on settled debts, then someone has to settle Merrow's Bend, and I think—" their voice caught, steadied "—I think the ledger already knows who owes it."

Nobody spoke. Father Ambrose's hand had found his river-stone amulet and stayed there. Wren had gone very still, the stillness of a person calculating exits.

"Who does owe it?" Ivetta asked finally, gently, in the voice she used for hard entries.

Paloma looked at the table. At the ledgers. At twenty years of service to a silent god who had apparently never been silent by choice, and at one page that had waited, patient as interest, for the world to start remembering.

"Me," they said. "By the hand, if not the heart. Vess ordered it. I carried it out. And the ledger recorded the debt under my true name, which means the door out of this world will not open one inch until what I did at Merrow's Bend has been paid for."

* * *

The silence afterward lasted long enough for two candles to drown in their own wax.

It was broken, unexpectedly, by Wren, who laughed — a short, sharp sound, quickly smothered, but unmistakably a laugh.

"Forgive me," they said, not sounding very sorry. "It's just — the guild of impossible things. Bottled grief. Borrowed luck. And now the price of salvation turns out to be one bookkeeper's sin, payable in full." They shook their head. "Of course it is. The universe has Ivetta's sense of humor."

"The universe has better handwriting," said Ivetta. Then she stood, and planted both fists on the table, and looked around the circle at each of them in turn — the priest of dead gods, the thief of names, the cartographer of vanished places, and the keeper of ledgers who had just confessed to a massacre.

"Here's where we are," she said. "The armies are coming. The door exists. The door needs a zero balance. Our ledger-keeper owes the single largest entry in the book, and the god we all assumed was dead is awake on the wrong side of the world and answering prayers like a creditor who's found our address." She exhaled. "So. Do we settle the accounts? All of them? Knowing what settling might cost?"

"Knowing," said Father Ambrose, "that some debts can't be paid with money."

"Money was always the easy currency," said Ondine.

Wren turned a coin over their knuckles, caught it, and pocketed it out of pure habit. "I want it noted," they said, "that I think this is a terrible idea, and that I'm in."

"Noted," said Ivetta. "Anyone else?"

Paloma looked at the ghost-map, at the faint silver rivers of a country that no one remembered, at the knot of light where a door waited behind the world. Somewhere out there, an old woman in a border market was selling charms and could not remember her own village. Somewhere out there, Sera Halvard was reading reports about answered prayers, and sooner or later her reports would lead her here.

"We settle them," Paloma said. "All of them. Starting with mine."

Ivetta nodded slowly. "Then we'd best find out what a massacre costs these days." She glanced at Ondine. "And you'd best tell them the rest."

Paloma went cold. "There's a rest?"

Ondine smiled the smile of a woman about to ruin someone's month. "There's a rest, child. You see, the debts have to be settled by the debtor — in person, at the site of the owing. And Merrow's Bend, as it happens, lies directly along the road the usurper's vanguard will be marching." She tapped the map, gently, almost kindly. "You'll want to leave soon."
