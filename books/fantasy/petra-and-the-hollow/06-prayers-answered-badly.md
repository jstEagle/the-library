## 6. Prayers Answered Badly

The fisherman's name was Oskar Vell, and what he wanted was simple enough to fit in a thimble: a full net.

Paloma found the prayer three days after it had been answered, which was two days after anyone in the free city of Corvane had thought to ask why the harbor had gone so quiet. The prayer itself arrived the way they all did now — a faint pressure behind the eyes, a taste like coins and candle smoke, and then a line of ink surfacing on whatever page happened to be nearest, as though the ledger had been waiting under the surface of the world all along and had only just decided to stop holding its breath.

*Oskar Vell, mender of nets, asks for a catch that will feed his street through winter.*

And beneath it, in ink that was still wet:

*Granted.*

Paloma sat in the back room of the guildhouse with the page flat on the table before them and their hands very still on either side of it, the way one sits with a sleeping animal that has recently begun to dream of biting.

"Granted," they said aloud, to the empty room, because saying things aloud made them less true sometimes, or at least less comfortable.

The harbor had gone quiet because there was nothing left in it to make noise. Oskar Vell had asked for a catch that would feed his street through winter, and Vess — silent Vess, dead Vess, the god whose silence Paloma had served for twenty years like a second spine — had looked out over the gray water of Corvane and taken the request literally, the way a child takes a promise literally, the way something takes a promise literally when it has been locked outside the world for two decades and has forgotten that the world is a soft place full of exceptions.

Every fish in the harbor. Every eel, every crab, every silver-sided thing that had ever turned beneath the pilings. Drawn up in a single night into nets that split under the weight, into boats that listed and sank at their moorings, into an ocean floor that Paloma's informants described, in hushed voices, as *swept*. And the fishermen who had gone out in the dark to haul those miraculous nets — nine of them, because the sea does not care what a god intends — had gone down with their boats when the weight shifted.

Oskar Vell had survived. He had been on the dock, not the water. He stood there at dawn surrounded by more fish than his street could eat in ten winters, and he wept, and the fish rotted faster than anyone could salt them, and by the third day the whole harbor smelled like a lesson nobody wanted to learn twice.

Paloma wrote the entry. That was the job. That had always been the job: *one miracle, nine dead, entered fair and entered square.* The guild traded in impossible things, but the ledgers only worked if everything went in them, especially the parts that made your hands shake.

They were blotting the ink when the second prayer surfaced, uninvited, on the facing page.

*

Two hundred miles south, in the free city of Ashkarin, where the canals ran green and the bells rang slightly out of tune because no two founders could agree on a pitch, a girl named Mirei knelt in a shrine that smelled of wet stone and old tallow.

She was seven years old. She had been told by her grandmother that the old gods were dead, and she had decided, with the magnificent stubbornness of seven-year-olds everywhere, that this was a rumor spread by people who had never actually checked.

Her mother had died in the spring fever. Her mother had been warm and loud and had sung off-key while kneading bread, and the house had been wrong-shaped and cold-quiet for four months, and so Mirei had done the thing that children have always done in shrines: she had put her hands together, closed her eyes, and made a demand of the universe with the full force of her small unreasonable heart.

*Give her back.*

Something heard her.

It is worth noting, here, that the grandmother did not believe at first either. When the knock came at the door on the evening of the third day, and the woman on the step said *Mirei, open the door, it's Mama*, the grandmother had stood in the kitchen with a ladle in her hand and felt joy arrive in her chest like a bird flying into a window — sudden, stunning, and followed by a slow slide down the glass.

Because the voice was right. The voice was exactly right. But the mother had been tall, broad-shouldered, with hands like shovels, and the woman on the step was slight, narrow, stooped in a way her mother had never been, and her face —

Her face was her mother's face. Rendered perfectly. From memory.

From *someone's* memory.

She sang off-key while kneading bread. She knew the words to every song. She knew the lullaby, the work songs, the rude one about the miller's wife that she'd only ever sung when she thought Mirei was asleep. She knew everything a memory could hold, and nothing a memory couldn't: she did not know how much flour remained in the bin, because the memory had been made on a day the bin was full; she flinched from the stove's heat until she learned it, because the memory had been made by a woman standing beside the stove, never touching it; and once, when Mirei hugged her, the girl's arms passed through her middle as though through cold smoke, and both of them pretended, with tremendous discipline, that this had not happened.

The grandmother watched. The grandmother counted. On the ninth night she caught the thing standing over Mirei's bed at three in the morning, perfectly still, its borrowed face tilted at an angle no neck should permit, watching the child breathe with an expression that was almost hunger and almost grief and entirely, unmistakably, *studying*.

The grandmother did what grandmothers do. She got the neighbors.

By dawn there were eleven of them with iron pokers and lanterns, and the thing wearing the mother's face stood in the doorway of the bedroom and said, in that beloved off-key voice, *"Please. I only want to finish learning her."* And then it came apart like breath on a window, and it was gone, and Mirei screamed for four days, and the grandmother sat by her bed and held her hand and did not say *I told you the gods were dead*, because the truth was worse than being wrong.

The truth was that the gods were alive, and this was what alive meant now.

*

In the border town of Kel Harrow, a farmer prayed for rain and received a flood that took his top field, his neighbor's barn, and the road to market, leaving him with a farm that was, technically, extremely watered.

In the port of Sable Quay, a widow prayed for her son's safe return from the whaling grounds, and her son returned — three years early, unchanged, unaware of any voyage past the one that had already ended, walking out of the fog onto the dock with a smile that stopped halfway and a story that repeated itself whenever anyone pressed it, like a coin worn smooth. His mother kept him anyway. What else was she going to do? He set the table. He remembered her birthday. He simply could not tell you anything that had happened after the moment her memory of him had last been refreshed, and when asked about the future he would go quiet in a way that made dogs bark three streets over.

In the hill country above Vane's Crossing, a shepherd prayed for his sick dog to live, and the dog lived, and also did not die, and also, when struck by a cart in the autumn, stood up without a mark and looked at the cart driver with eyes that had seen too many mornings, and the shepherd shot it himself and then walked into the hills and did not come back, and everyone agreed this was sad, and nobody agreed on why it had felt necessary.

The prayers were being answered. All of them. Everywhere. Sloppily, hungrily, at scale, by something that had been starved for twenty years and had come back to the table with no manners and no sense of portion.

And every answer, if you looked closely — and looking closely was Paloma Eriksen's entire profession, their curse, their craft — every answer was built out of the same material.

Memories. Stolen ones.

The thing in Ashkarin had been assembled from a mother's remembered self. The son at Sable Quay had been cut whole from a widow's recollection, seams and all. The flood at Kel Harrow had taken its shape from the farmer's own childhood memory of the great storm of his grandfather's day — the exact storm, wave for wave, because Vess had reached into the man's head for a template of *rain* and used the biggest one it found.

Vess was not creating. Vess was *withdrawing*. Reaching into the minds of the faithful and the faithless alike, pulling out what it needed to build its answers, and paying nothing back.

Paloma understood this on the fourth night, sitting in the guildhouse with eleven pages spread across the table and a pot of tea gone cold beside them, and the understanding arrived not as a revelation but as an accounting error finally reconciled — the click of numbers that had refused to balance suddenly, horribly, balancing.

Twenty years ago, the prayers had stopped being answered. Everyone assumed the god had died. Paloma had assumed it too; they had grieved it, in their dry ledger-keeper's way, and built a life on the grave.

But you cannot answer prayers with nothing. A god needs material. And a god that has been locked outside reality — a possibility Paloma had not yet let themselves think all the way through, though the thought stood at the edge of the lamplight like a patient creditor — a god locked outside reality has no fields to draw from except the ones it stocked before the door shut.

Vess was answering prayers out of its hoard. Out of everything it had ever gathered from its worshippers' minds, back when gathering was called blessing.

Out of everything it had devoured.

Paloma put down the pen. Their hand left a small smear of ink on the table, shaped vaguely like a bird, or a wound.

"Oh," they said quietly. "Oh, you *fool*."

They weren't sure whether they were talking about the god, the worshippers, or themselves. It seemed safest to assume all three.

* * *

Ivetta found them there at dawn, asleep sitting up with their cheek on the ledger, ink transferred to their face in a pattern that would take two days of scrubbing to fully renounce. The guildmaster was sixty, built like a doorframe, and possessed the particular gentleness of people who had spent their lives handling dangerous things carefully.

"You look," she said, "like a man who lost an argument with arithmetic."

"I won the argument," Paloma said, lifting their head. "That's the problem."

Ivetta poured herself tea from the cold pot without complaint, drank it cold, and waited. She was very good at waiting. It was half of what guildmasters were for.

"The prayers are real," Paloma said. "All across the free cities. And they're all wrong in the same way." They turned the pages toward her, one by one, the drowned fishermen, the false mother, the flood, the son cut from fog. "Look at the construction. Every granted wish is assembled from existing memories. Not new miracles. Recycled ones. Withdrawn from — somewhere."

Ivetta studied the pages for a long time. Outside, Corvane was waking up: cart wheels, gulls, someone arguing cheerfully with a fishmonger about the price of anything at all, given that the harbor was currently a graveyard.

"And the somewhere?" she said.

"That's the part I don't want to say out loud."

"Say it anyway. I'm old. I've survived worse than words."

Paloma looked at the window, at the light coming up gray over the rooftops, and said it.

"Vess isn't answering prayers out of divine power. It's answering them out of inventory. Everything it ever took from its worshippers — every memory it ever swallowed, every mind it ever emptied — it's spending it now, to buy its way back into the world. The god didn't die twenty years ago, Ivetta. The god went *quiet* because it ran out of credit. And now the prayers have started again, and it's spending down the vault."

The room was very quiet. Somewhere below, the cheerful argument about fish resolved itself, one party having apparently been argued into surrender.

"If that's true," Ivetta said slowly, "then every person praying to it right now is making a withdrawal against a debt they don't know exists."

"Yes."

"And when the vault runs empty?"

Paloma turned to the last page. The newest prayer, surfaced overnight, still wet. A young man in Corvane's tanner's quarter, asking for his father back — the father lost at Merrow's Bend, twenty years ago, in a village that officially had never existed, in a massacre that officially had never happened, carried out by a servant of the silent god whose name Paloma had not spoken aloud since.

The ink beneath the prayer was moving. Writing itself. Forming, letter by letter, with the unhurried confidence of a thing that had all the time in the world and intended to use other people's:

*Granted. She remembers him. He will come to her wearing the shape she kept.*

Paloma closed the book.

"Ivetta," they said, "we need to talk about Merrow's Bend."

The guildmaster went still in the way that stone goes still. "We agreed never to—"

"We agreed. It didn't." Paloma tapped the cover of the ledger once, softly, like knocking on a door they very much hoped nobody would answer. "The ledgers remember even when we don't. And I think—" their voice did something complicated and came out lower, "—I think the god is starting to read them too."

Above the guildhouse, the gray light strengthened. In the tanner's quarter, a young man was lighting a candle before a cracked shrine, and far away, beyond the edges of maps, something vast and loving and starving turned its attention toward Corvane, toward a ledger, toward the one servant who had kept accounts faithfully through all the silent years.

It knew Paloma's handwriting.

It was coming to collect.
