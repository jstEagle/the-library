## 7. Sera's Confession

Sera Halvard had interrogated forty-one heretics in her career, and she had a system for it. She learned their habits first. What they drank, when they slept, which gods they'd stopped believing in and which they lied about still believing in. By the third day in Corvane she could have written Paloma Eriksen's routine in her own hand: up before the gulls, tea at the chandler's on Tallow Street, ledgers until noon, ledgers after noon, and at some ungodly hour of the night, a walk along the seawall that no sane person took because the wind there could strip paint.

So she waited by the seawall, out of the worst of the wind, behind the old pilings where they used to sit when they were young and stupid and the world still had edges that held.

She told herself this was surveillance. The word *surveillance* was doing a great deal of work in her internal accounting, and she knew it, and she let it, because the alternative was admitting she'd chosen this spot for the same reason Paloma always chose it — because from here you could see the drowned chapel's spire breaking the harbor water like a rib, and something in both of them had always needed to keep an eye on it.

The moon was a thin nail-paring. The tide breathed. And then there were footsteps on the salt-crusted stone, unhurried, and a voice she had spent six years teaching herself to hear without flinching.

"You're standing in my thinking spot," Paloma said.

"Finders keepers."

"That's not how thinking spots work." They came around the piling, hands loose at their sides, ledger satchel slung across their body the way a soldier wears a sword. Their eyes went to her belt, to the truce badge pinned above her heart, to her face, cataloguing. Old habit. "You're off the processional route, Inquisitor. Should I be flattered or frightened?"

"Yes," Sera said.

Paloma laughed — short, surprised out of them, gone again. Six years and they still laughed at her worst jokes first and thought about them later. It was unbearable. She had missed it the way you miss a limb.

"I'm not here to arrest you," she said.

"I know. You'd have brought more than your charming self."

"I'm here to confess."

That landed. She watched it land — watched Paloma go very still, the way they did with entries that didn't balance. Somewhere below them the tide sucked at the chapel spire, and the sound it made was almost, almost like breathing.

* * *

Here is what Sera had learned in the Usurper's service, in the gray fortress at Karst, in the archive rooms where the candles were counted in and counted out: that an empire is built on the assumption that silence means absence. That a god who does not answer is a god who is dead, and a dead god needs no cage, only a paperwork trail proving it never mattered.

She had believed it. She had been good at believing it; belief was just discipline pointed inward, and she had always had excellent discipline.

Then, four months ago, in the third watch of the night, kneeling over a heretic's confiscated prayer-book, she had heard a voice say her name.

Not aloud. Not in her ear. In the place behind her sternum where she kept the things she didn't think about — her mother's lullabies, the smell of Paloma's hair, the specific weight of a promise made in a drowned chapel by two people too young to know what oaths cost. The voice came from *there*, and it said, *Sera. You kept the book.*

And she had done the thing any disciplined woman would do. She had reported herself to her superiors as possibly compromised, requested reassignment, undergone three weeks of examination by priests whose eyes were the color of pond ice, and lied through every single hour of it, because somewhere between the second day and the third she had realized that if she told them the truth, they wouldn't cure her. They'd use her. The Usurper did not free people from voices. He collected them.

"They're marching," she said now, to Paloma, to the sea, to the nail-paring moon. "You know that. Everyone knows that. But do you know why? Not the taxes, not the succession, not any of the reasons on the proclamations. He's marching on the free cities because the prayers have started again. Because twenty years of nothing, and then—" she snapped her fingers, and the sound cracked flat against the water— "*this*. Answered prayers, all down the coast. Fishermen hauling in catches that shouldn't exist. Sick children waking whole. And the Emperor — he was there, at the founding, whatever the histories say he was doing — he knows what answered prayers mean. He knows what comes back when they start."

"And what comes back?" Paloma asked quietly.

Sera looked at them. Salt wind. Gray coat. The little ink stain on their knuckle that had been there since they were nineteen and would presumably be there at their funeral.

"A god," she said. "A god who was supposed to stay gone."

* * *

Paloma sat down on the piling. Not dramatically — they just sort of folded, the way they did when an entry refused to reconcile, lowering themselves onto the stone like a person setting down something heavy for a moment to rub their arms.

"Say the rest," they said.

"He hears them too. The prayers. That's his gift, if you want to call it that — the reason the old order fell. He can hear a god moving under the world the way you can hear rats in a wall. Twenty years of quiet and he'd almost convinced himself it was permanent. Then Corvane starts answering back, and he—" She stopped. Started again, more honestly. "He's afraid. I've seen him afraid twice, and both times afterward, someone burned. He wants the free cities because that's where the worship is pooling. And he wants every record of it. Every ledger, every hymnal, every name anyone ever called Vess by. He's building a cage, Paloma. A big one. Big enough for a god and everyone who ever loved one."

The tide turned below them. Sera felt it rather than heard it, the way the whole harbor seemed to exhale and draw breath the other way.

"You've known this for how long?" Paloma asked.

"Since Karst."

"Four months." Their voice didn't rise. That was worse. "You've known for four months that an army is coming here, and you've been walking around our city with a truce banner counting our altars, and you're telling me this *now*?"

"I'm telling you now because I couldn't tell you before." She heard her own voice go rough and hated it. "Because before, I still thought I might find a way to fix it inside the law. Because every time I tried to write you a letter I got as far as *Dear Paloma* and then my hand shook so badly the ink blotted, and I ruined nine sheets of good vellum, and the quartermaster started giving me looks." She laughed, and it came out wrong. "Because I'm a coward, and I love you, and those two facts have been having a war in my chest since the day I put on the gray, and tonight the coward finally lost."

Paloma said nothing. The wind moved their hair. Below, the chapel spire dripped.

"The book," Sera said. "The prayer-book I was examining when the voice came. It was yours. Your handwriting in the margins. You wrote prayers into it during the silent years — I recognized the way you cross your *t*s, I'd know it anywhere, I used to fall asleep watching you write. And the voice said *you kept the book*, and Paloma, I need you to tell me — has it spoken to you? Has Vess come to you? Because if it has, then you are already on the Emperor's list, and I am the only person in his entire apparatus who is trying to keep you *off* it."

* * *

What Sera did not say, what she had carried up the seawall steps like a stone in her mouth:

*I hear it too. Every night now. It doesn't speak in words anymore, it speaks in memories — it gives things back. Last week it gave me the smell of bread from a bakery in Merrow's Bend, and I have never been to Merrow's Bend, and I woke up crying and didn't know why. It is filling me up with other people's lives like a jug under a tap, and I don't know what it wants, and I am so tired, and the only person alive who might understand is sitting three feet away from me looking at me like I'm a ledger entry that won't balance.*

She kept it in her mouth. Some confessions you ration.

Instead she said the practical thing, the thing she'd rehearsed eleven times against the wall of her rented room while the landlady's dog listened with its head tilted.

"There's a way out. For you. The Emperor's men will want the guild's records — all of them, everything the Guild of Impossible Things has ever traded in. If you bring them to me, voluntarily, before the army arrives — I can register you as a cooperative asset. Protected status. When the cities fall, you walk out under my seal. Alive. Free. Whole." She swallowed. "It's not nothing, Paloma. It's the only door I have."

"And the guild?" Paloma said. "Ivetta? The partners? The people whose debts we hold?"

"Collateral."

The word hung there. Sera watched Paloma take it, turn it over, set it down with terrible care, the way they handled anything fragile and expensive.

"Collateral," they repeated. "You learned that word in Karst."

"I learned a lot of words in Karst."

"Do you believe them? The ones you serve. Do you believe the cage is mercy? That caging a god keeps people safe?"

And here was the trap she'd built for herself, because the honest answer was *no*, and the loyal answer was *yes*, and she had never once in her life managed to give either answer cleanly. So she gave the true one instead, the one underneath both.

"I believe the Emperor is a man who once saw a god eat a country," she said, "and has been building walls against it ever since, and that fear makes him cruel, and cruelty dressed in armor still cuts. I believe Vess is awake and hungry and answering prayers, and I am *hearing it*, Paloma, in my sleep, in my bones, and whatever it wants, it isn't good. I believe both of them would burn this city to win their argument. And I believe you are standing in the middle holding a bag full of paper that both sides would kill for, and I cannot — I *cannot*—" Her voice cracked clean through. She pressed on anyway, because she was Sera Halvard, and finishing was the only prayer she'd ever been sure of. "I cannot watch them take you. Arrest you, burn you, use you, *anything*. So yes. Betrayal. Offered gently. Take it or don't."

* * *

For a long moment there was only the sea.

Then Paloma reached into their satchel, and Sera's hand went to her knife before her brain caught up — but what they drew out wasn't a page. It was a dried fig, slightly squashed, wrapped in wax paper. They held it out.

"You still do this," they said. "When you're terrified, you negotiate. You offered me a whole empire's worth of mercy and your voice only broke on the last sentence, which means the first part was rehearsed and the last part was you." They pushed the fig toward her. "Eat. Your blood sugar goes when you're scared, you get shrill. You're being shrill."

"I am being *reasonable*—"

"You're being shrill and reasonable, it's very intimidating." A ghost of the old crooked smile. "I'm not taking your deal, Sera. Not yet. But I'm not turning you in, either, which should tell you something about where my loyalties are rotting."

Sera took the fig. Her hands weren't quite steady. She ate it in the dark on a seawall in a city she'd been sent to dismantle, and it tasted like a market day twelve years ago, and she hated how much she loved it.

"The voice," Paloma said quietly, watching the chapel spire. "Mine started talking last month. Ink moves in my ledgers on its own. Debts waking up. Names I buried." They paused. "There's a page I haven't been able to look at directly. Merrow's Bend."

Something cold walked down Sera's spine. The bread smell. The crying. *Merrow's Bend.*

"Paloma," she said slowly, "what do you know about Merrow's Bend?"

"Nothing," Paloma said. "That's the problem. There's a debt in my ledger for a village I have no memory of collecting from. And tonight, for the first time, the ink on that page spelled out a word instead of numbers."

The tide pulled back, far back, further than the tide should go, baring the mudflats and the drowned chapel's whole black skeleton to the moon.

"It said *come*," Paloma said. "And Sera — I think it's inviting us both."
