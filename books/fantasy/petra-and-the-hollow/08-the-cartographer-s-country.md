## 8. The Cartographer's Country

The cartographer's shop did not exist on three days of the week.

Paloma had learned this the hard way, over years of guild business: you could stand in Saltcandle Lane on a Tuesday and find nothing but a blank stretch of wall between a chandler's and a shop that sold nothing but left shoes, and if you were foolish enough to knock, your knuckles would meet stone as old as the city itself. But on Thursdays—on Thursdays—the door was there, painted the green of deep water, with a brass plate bearing no name at all, because a name would have been a kind of map, and maps were the one thing Maren Ostrova refused to make of herself.

Today was Thursday. Paloma knocked anyway, out of habit, and heard Maren's voice call out, "If you're here about the invoice for the bottled grief, I paid it in full and I have the receipt tattooed somewhere I'm not showing you."

"It's Paloma."

A pause. Then, warmer: "Oh, thank every dead god. Come in before the lane changes its mind."

The interior of the shop was larger than the exterior, which was the least impossible thing about it. Every wall was papered floor to ceiling with maps—not framed, not filed, but *papered*, overlapping like scales on some enormous patient fish, so that walking through the room felt like wading through the world's skin. There were maps of Corvane with streets that hadn't been built yet, drawn in pencil so faint they might have been regrets. Maps of coastlines that had drowned before anyone thought to name them. A map of the moon, annotated in a furious hand: *WRONG, WRONG, WRONG, but closer.*

Maren Ostrova sat at a drafting table in the middle of it all, a small woman past seventy with ink-stained fingers and spectacles pushed up into a cloud of white hair. She looked up at Paloma over the top of her current work—a chart of a river that, judging by the annotations, had recently decided to flow uphill—and her expression did something complicated.

"You look terrible," she said.

"Thank you."

"No, I mean it. You look like a ledger that's been rained on." She set down her pen. "Ivetta sent word you'd come. She also sent word that I should give you tea and my full cooperation and absolutely no opinions, which is how I know she's frightened."

"Is she?" Paloma asked. "Frightened?"

Maren snorted. "Ivetta doesn't get frightened. She gets *precise*. And she's been precise all week, which is worse." She waved a hand toward a low chair upholstered in something that had once, possibly, been a church pew. "Sit. Tea first. Bad news keeps better when it's warm."

* * *

The tea was terrible, which meant Maren had made it herself rather than letting her apprentice do it, which meant this was going to be serious. Paloma held the cup and watched the cartographer clear a space on her drafting table with the ceremony of someone preparing an altar.

"I need to show you something," Maren said. "And before I do, I want you to understand what I am. People think a cartographer draws the world. We don't. The world draws itself, constantly, carelessly, the way a drunk draws his own face. What we do is *agree* with it. A map is a treaty between the land and everyone who looks at the land. That's all geography is. Agreement."

"And sometimes the agreement breaks?"

"Sometimes the agreement is *broken*." Maren's voice had gone quiet in a way Paloma had only ever heard from people describing funerals. "There are two ways a place stops existing. It can die—flood, fire, plague, the ordinary appetites. Or it can be unwritten. Do you know the difference?"

Paloma thought of the ledgers. Of entries struck through so thoroughly the paper thinned. "One leaves a body."

"One leaves a *wound*," Maren said. "A dead place becomes history. An unwritten place becomes a hole, and holes pull. Rivers bend toward them. Roads curve around them without knowing why. Sailors lose three days crossing water where there's nothing to lose days over. And people—" she paused, and for the first time since Paloma had entered, her hands were not steady, "—people who lived there wake up one morning with a shape missing from their hearts and spend the rest of their lives trying to fill it with drink or doctrine or children, and none of it fits, because what they lost wasn't a thing. It was a *where*."

She reached beneath the drafting table and drew out a long wooden case, banded in iron, locked with three separate keys that she wore on a cord around her neck like relics. When she opened it, Paloma expected parchment.

What lay inside was leather. Old, cracked, scarred with burn marks along one edge—as if someone had tried to destroy it and failed, or stopped halfway, or been interrupted by their own conscience.

"This is the oldest map I own," Maren said. "It's older than me. Older than Ivetta. Older than the guild, though don't tell her I said that, she gets territorial. It came to me from my teacher, who took it from her teacher, who—" she waved vaguely, "—stole it, probably. We're cartographers. Theft is just acquisition with better posture." She lifted it out with both hands, reverent as a priest. "It shows a country that does not exist."

* * *

Maren unrolled it across the drafting table and weighted the corners with four brass instruments, and Paloma leaned over it, and for a long moment could not breathe.

It was beautiful. That was the first thing—the obscene, unbearable beauty of it. Coastlines drawn in a sure hand, mountain ranges hatched like sleeping animals, rivers braiding toward a central plain where a city had been rendered in such loving detail that Paloma could see individual bridges, individual gardens, tiny trees drawn leaf by leaf. Around the borders ran text in a script Paloma half-recognized—the liturgical hand of Vess's old priesthood, the one they'd been taught as a child and forbidden to use as an adult.

And across the whole of it, stamped in wax the color of dried blood, the seal of the Concordat. The treaty that ended the Godswar. Beneath the seal, in the conqueror's official hand:

*NO SUCH PLACE.*

"They didn't burn it," Paloma said slowly. Their voice sounded strange in their own ears. "They *cancelled* it."

"They cancelled it," Maren agreed. "Vessmark. The heartland of the silent god. Two hundred leagues of coast and mountain and river, eleven cities, the whole shining apparatus of a nation—and at the signing of the Concordat, the victors didn't annex it, didn't garrison it, didn't even loot it properly. They wrote it out. Every treaty copy, every census, every deed and birth record and gravestone. Every map in every archive in every capital. They erased a country the way you'd erase a debt." Her mouth twisted. "Forgive the comparison. I know you deal in those."

"The people?"

"Scattered. Resettled. Told, gently, firmly, with soldiers standing behind the telling, that they had always been citizens of wherever they now stood. Their memories were—" Maren hesitated. "Adjusted. Not fully. Fully forgetting kills most minds; the priests of the Concordat were thorough but not cruel, in the way that a surgeon is not cruel. They left the shape of the loss. Just removed the content. Like scooping the flesh out of a fruit and leaving the skin to hold its own absence."

Paloma stared down at the map, and something cold moved through them, slow as tide. Because they knew this feeling. They had felt it their whole life without having a name for it—that hollow behind the sternum, that sense of grieving something they couldn't identify, that had driven them at seventeen into the arms of a silent god, because Vess's silence had felt *familiar*. Like recognizing a house you'd never lived in.

"How many people remember?" they asked.

"None, truly. Fragments. Dreams. A woman in Harrowgate who has spent forty years setting a third place at her table and cannot tell you why. Fishermen who avoid a stretch of open sea because it feels, they say, *crowded*." Maren tapped the map's western coast, where the blue of the ocean met the ink of the shore. "But here's what the Concordat never understood, and what I've spent my life confirming: you can erase a place from every map in the world, but the world itself keeps its own accounts. Geography has a memory longer than any treaty. The land remembers being Vessmark. It strains toward itself. And where reality has been torn, reality *thins*."

She looked up at Paloma over her spectacles.

"There is a valley," she said, "three days east of here, that appears on no map. Not because it's hidden. Because it's *load-bearing*. It sits exactly where the wound is deepest—at the center of the erasure, the point where the tearing began. The Hollow, the guild calls it. I've mapped everything that exists, child, and everything that doesn't, and I am telling you: that valley is a door. It opens onto this." She spread her hands over the beautiful, cancelled country. "Onto Vessmark. All of it. Preserved. Waiting. Locked outside the world like a letter in a drawer."

* * *

Paloma set down the tea. Their hands needed to be free for what came next, though they couldn't have said what.

"Say it plainly," they said. "I've had a week of people saying things near the point instead of at it. Ivetta. Sera. Even the thief, and he charges by the syllable. Say it plainly."

Maren took off her spectacles and polished them on her sleeve, which gave her time to decide how honest to be. Paloma waited. In the silence, the maps on the walls seemed to lean in—all those pencilled futures and drowned coasts, listening.

"Plainly, then," Maren said. "Your god is not dead. Your god is not even imprisoned, not the way the stories say, chained under a mountain or sleeping in a well. Your god is *exiled*. When the Concordat unwrote Vessmark, they unwrote it completely—which means they unwrote the god whose country it was. Vess didn't fall. Vess was filed away. Sealed outside reality with every temple, every prayer-book, every worshipper's memory of worship. Twenty years of silence, Paloma. Not because Vess chose silence." Her voice dropped. "Because there was nowhere in the world for Vess's voice to *be*."

The room tilted—or Paloma did. They gripped the edge of the drafting table and the carved wood bit into their palms and the tilt passed, mostly.

Twenty years. They had served twenty years. They had carried out orders in a dead god's name, had knelt in a drowned chapel and sworn oaths to a silence they'd believed was grief, had built their entire self around the architecture of mourning. And all that time—

"All that time," Paloma said, and their voice cracked in the middle of it, humiliatingly, "the prayers. Everyone said the prayers went nowhere. That Vess couldn't hear. That we were speaking into a grave."

"Were you?" Maren asked softly. "Or were you speaking through a wall? Because those are very different things, and only one of them means the listener isn't home." She tapped the map again, right at its heart, where the little city stood with its little bridges. "The Hollow opens onto this country. Which means it opens onto *him*. Whatever has been happening lately—the answered prayers, the offerings, the ink moving in your ledgers—it's coming through that door. The seal is thinning. Reality heals, given time, and as it heals, the edges fray. Things leak. Gods leak worst of all."

Paloma thought of the fresh offerings in the drowned chapel. Bread and salt and small silver coins, left by strangers. Thought of the prayer that had slipped through their fingers mid-entry, warm as a bird's heartbeat. Thought of the Merrow's Bend page, ink crawling like something waking up.

"He's waking up," they said. "Because the door is opening."

"Because the door is *fraying*. Opening it fully is another matter entirely." Maren began rolling the map back up, and her hands were careful, almost tender, the way you handle a thing that has already survived one attempt on its life. "Which brings us to the part Ivetta told me not to tell you, and which I'm going to tell you anyway, because you have the face of someone who deserves bad news delivered promptly."

"I've been told I have several faces."

"This one especially." She tied the case shut. "The Hollow doesn't open for armies or ambition. It opens for *settlement*. That valley is the seam of a treaty, and treaties respond to accounting. Every debt tied to the erased country—every vow made in Vess's name, every promise broken, every sin committed while the god slept—every single account must be balanced before the door will swing wide. The guild has been settling what it can. But there's one entry that dwarfs all the others. One debt so large it's less a line item than a load-bearing wall." She met Paloma's eyes. "You know the one I mean."

Merrow's Bend. The village. The smoke. The ledger page with its restless ink.

"Yes," Paloma said.

"Then you understand what I'm telling you. The door out of this war—the door that saves the free cities, that saves everyone Ivetta has sworn to protect—opens onto the country of a god who is waking hungry. And it will not open at all until the bloodiest page in your ledger is paid." Maren smiled, and it was a tired, kind, terrible smile. "Geography is a wound, child. And wounds don't close until what's inside them comes out."

* * *

Paloma walked back through Corvane as the light went amber, and the city felt different, the way a familiar room feels different after you learn what happened in it once, long ago.

Every street curved slightly wrong. They saw it now—saw how the great processional road bent a degree east of true for no reason any engineer would admit to, how the harbor pilots steered wide of a patch of empty water, how the whole city leaned, imperceptibly, like grass bending around a buried stone. Around the hole where a country used to be. Around the drawer where a god was folded up, waiting for the paper to wear through.

At the mouth of Saltcandle Lane, a beggar was singing—an old song, tuneless, half-remembered, about a river with a name Paloma almost caught and then didn't, the way you almost catch a word in a dream. The beggar didn't know what he was singing. He was just singing the shape of it.

Paloma pressed a silver coin into his cup and kept walking, faster now, toward the guildhall, toward the ledgers, toward the page with the moving ink.

Behind them, faint as a memory of a memory, the beggar sang on—and somewhere three days east, in a valley on no map, Paloma would have sworn the wind changed direction, drawing inward, like a breath taken before a name.
