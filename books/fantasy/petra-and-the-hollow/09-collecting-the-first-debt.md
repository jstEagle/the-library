## 9. Collecting the First Debt

The border market at Saltcross smelled of wet wool and frying onions, which Paloma had learned over the years was the smell of almost every place where two countries pretended not to touch. Stalls leaned against each other like drunk friends. A man sold pigeons that were, according to his sign, *definitely* pigeons. Somewhere behind the fishmonger's row, a fiddler played the same eight bars over and over, either from devotion or because he'd forgotten the rest.

Paloma walked through it all with the ledger wrapped in oilcloth against their chest, feeling like a person carrying a lit lantern through a powder store.

The page for Merrow's Bend rode in the inner pocket now, separate from the others. Ivetta had insisted on that after the ink began moving. "You don't keep a waking thing in a drawer with sleeping things," she'd said, in the tone she used for rules that were older than she was. So Paloma carried it alone, and twice on the road down from Corvane they had felt it shift against their ribs — not moving ink anymore but something more like breathing.

They had not opened it since. Cowardice, probably. Or wisdom. In Paloma's experience the two wore each other's coats so often it hardly mattered.

*Find the survivor,* the guild's plan said. Simple as a recipe. There had been one hundred and eleven souls in Merrow's Bend the night Paloma burned it in Vess's name, and one hundred and ten had died, and the ledgers recorded the survivor only as *the girl who ran.* No name. Vess had been silent then, but silence, Paloma knew now, was not absence. The god had taken even the accounting of its own atrocity and left it half-finished, like a prayer interrupted.

Finding a girl who ran, twenty years later, in a country that had agreed to forget her village existed, should have been impossible.

But the guild traded in impossible things, and impossible things, Paloma had learned, tended to gather at borders, where nobody's law quite reached.

It took them most of the morning. They asked after charm-sellers, after hedge-witches, after anyone who sold luck or love or protection against the evil eye, and they were pointed down three wrong alleys and one right one before they found her.

She had the best stall in the market, though it was also the smallest and the shabbiest, which told Paloma everything about how good she was. It stood at the end of the fishmonger's row under an awning patched with four different colors of canvas, and it was crowded — not with customers, though there were those too, but with goods. Bundles of dried herbs tied with red thread. River stones with holes through them, strung on leather cords. Little knotted figures of straw that were supposed to be houses, Paloma realized, looking closer. Charms *for the home.* Dozens of them. Rows and rows of little straw houses, every one different, every one made with the particular fussiness of someone who remembered exactly what a house ought to look like.

The woman herself was small and brown and sharp-faced, with hands that moved constantly, tying, sorting, threading, as if stillness might cost her money. She looked up when Paloma's shadow fell across her stall.

"Charms," she said. Not a question. Her eyes did a quick professional inventory — boots, coat, the sword worn like an afterthought, the face of someone who hadn't slept properly in a month. "You look like you need about six of them. Sit. No, don't sit, you'll break my stool. What do you need? Luck? Love?" She squinted. "Not love. You've got the look of someone love already happened to and they're still deciding how they feel about it."

"Protection," Paloma said. It was true enough. "Against — being found."

"Mm." The old woman selected a knot of herbs without looking, the way another person might pick up a spoon. "That one's dearer. Being found is usually a thing people do to themselves, you know. But I don't judge." She held it out, then paused, and her sharp eyes sharpened further. "You're not here for the herb."

"No."

"You're here because you've been looking at my houses." She nodded at the rows of little straw buildings. "Everyone looks at the stones and the threads. You looked at the houses. Nobody looks at the houses." She set the herb bundle down. "Who are you?"

"My name is Paloma Eriksen." They let the pause stretch just long enough. "I keep ledgers for the Guild of the Unwritten Contract."

Something moved behind the woman's face — not fear, exactly. Recognition, arriving late, like a guest who'd gotten lost on a road she used to know.

"I know that guild," she said slowly. "Or I knew — no." She pressed the heel of her hand against her eye, hard, the way a person does when their own head is misbehaving. "I know *of* it. Everyone knows of it. That's not what I mean. Why do I feel like I owe you money?"

"You don't," Paloma said quietly. "I think it may be the other way around."

* * *

Her name was Maren Ostrova, and she had been selling charms at Saltcross for nineteen years, and before that —

Before that was the wall. Paloma watched her hit it. They sat on two crates behind the stall while the market roared on around them, sharing a cup of cider the color of strong tea, and Maren talked about her life the way you'd read a book with pages torn out.

"— and before that I came up the coast road, and before that—" She stopped. Frowned. Her thumb worried the red thread on a finished bundle. "There's a hole. Right there. Isn't that strange. I've never noticed it's a hole before. I always thought, well, I arrived, everyone arrives somewhere, you come up the coast road and you start selling charms. But it's a hole, isn't it. It has edges."

"It has edges," Paloma said.

"And you know what's in it." It wasn't a question, and her voice had changed — gone flat and careful, the voice of someone standing at the edge of ice. "Guilds that trade in impossible things. Ledgers. You looked at my houses like you were counting them." She turned to face Paloma fully for the first time. "Say it. Whatever it is. I'm seventy-one years old and I've buried two husbands and outlived every debt I ever owed except this one, whatever it is, so say it plain."

Paloma had rehearsed this on the road. They had composed speeches. Elegant ones, gentle ones, ones that circled the truth like a bird over water. All of them died in their throat now, and what came out instead was the plainest sentence they had ever spoken:

"There was a village called Merrow's Bend. You were born there. It was burned, and everyone in it died except you, and afterward something erased it — from the maps, from the records, from every mind that remembered it. Including yours. Including mine, mostly. I was there. I helped burn it. And I have come to settle the debt."

The market noise went on. The fiddler played his eight bars. A pigeon that was definitely a pigeon escaped its seller and caused a small riot two stalls down.

Maren didn't scream. Paloma had braced for screaming, for grief, for the thrown cup. Instead she sat very still, and her hands — those restless, busy hands — went quiet for the first time since Paloma had seen her.

"Burned," she repeated. "All of them."

"All but you."

"How many?"

"One hundred and eleven."

"One hundred and eleven." She said it like she was testing the weight of a coin. Then her brow creased. "I can't — I hear the number but I can't see them. I can't see *anyone.* I try to picture my mother and there's just — a shape. A shape where a mother goes." Her breath caught, and now the tears came, sudden and furious, and she wiped them away with the back of her wrist like they were an inconvenience at market. "Twenty years I've been selling charms to homesick people. Straw houses. Do you know why I make the houses? I told myself it was because they sold well. They don't sell well. Nobody buys the houses. I make them anyway. Every market day, I sit and I build houses I cannot remember living in, and I tell myself it's trade sense." She laughed, wet and terrible. "It's not trade sense. It's a hole with edges."

Paloma reached into their coat and drew out the ledger page.

They had meant to be careful with it. They had meant to explain the erasure first, the god, the prison, the whole architecture of the wound. But the page seemed to feel the nearness of her — Paloma would swear this later, under oath if anyone had asked — because the ink was moving before it was fully out of the oilcloth, the letters swimming like fish rising to feed, and Maren saw it, and Maren went white.

"What is that," she whispered. "What is *that.*"

"The record of what was done. Kept by the guild. Owed by me." Paloma laid it on the crate between them, face up. "The guild's law is simple. Every entry must be settled. Debts are paid to the one owed. The village is owed. You are what remains of the village." They swallowed. "I came to pay you. However you name the price. Vengeance, if you want it. My life, if you want it. Anything in the ledger's power. Name it, and it's yours, and the account closes."

Maren stared at the page. The ink writhed. And then she did the last thing Paloma expected, the thing none of the rehearsed speeches had prepared for.

She reached out and touched the paper, gently, the way you'd touch a sleeping child, and said:

"Read it to me."

* * *

Paloma read.

They read the names — all one hundred and eleven, recovered from parish rolls the guild had bought decades ago from a priest of three dead gods, names that existed nowhere else in the world now. Aldous Wren. The sisters Tam and Ilse Broadfoot. Old Father Ellery, who had baptized half the village and married the other half. Children. So many children, listed with their ages because the guild's law demanded precision and precision was its own kind of cruelty.

Maren listened with her eyes closed. And as Paloma read, something extraordinary happened, something Paloma would spend the rest of their life trying to describe: the names stopped being ink. Paloma could see it happening — could see memory flowing backward along the words like water finding an old channel. With each name, Maren's face changed. A twitch of a smile at *Tam and Ilse.* A soft huff at *Old Father Ellery,* who had, apparently, once fallen asleep during his own sermon. When Paloma read *Mira Ostrova, aged 34,* Maren's eyes flew open.

"That's my mother," she said. "Mira. She — oh. Oh, she sang. She sang *badly,* she sang like a gate hinge, and she sang all the time, and I used to—" The tears again, but different now, and she let them run. "I remember her. I remember the kitchen. The blue door. We had a blue door, why do I remember a blue door—"

"Because it was real," Paloma said. Their voice cracked on it. "Real things leave marks. Even erased, they leave marks. The houses. You've been building your village for twenty years, Maren. Every house you've ever tied out of straw."

Maren looked at her stall — at the hundreds of little buildings, each one different, each one fussy and specific — and made a sound that was half laugh and half keening, and for a long moment she simply wept among her merchandise while the market flowed past, oblivious, buying luck.

When she could speak again, she said: "The rest of it. Read the rest. What was done. Who did it. All of it."

So Paloma told her. Not the ledger version — the true one. Their own part in it. The oath, the silent god, the order that had come through channels Paloma had obeyed for twenty years without asking why. They told her about the fire. They did not soften it, because softening it would have been another theft, and she had been robbed enough.

Maren listened to all of it without interrupting. At the end she was quiet for a while, turning her cup in her hands.

"I should want you dead," she said at last, conversationally. "Any decent person would want you dead. My mother would want you dead, and she'd want it done politely, with an apology first, because she was like that." She looked up. "But here's the thing, keeper of ledgers. You can't hang a man and get my mother back. I've thought about vengeance my whole life without knowing what it was for — I could feel the shape of it, like a tool missing from a kit. And now I know what the tool was for, and it turns out it doesn't fit anything. There's nothing to cut. The ones who did it are ash and a god and a bad memory, and killing you just makes a new widow somewhere, and widows are the one thing I sell charms against."

"So what do you name as the price?"

Maren set down the cup. She picked up a straw house — one of the oldest, softest ones, frayed from years of handling — and held it in her palm.

"You said the erasure took the village from every mind. Not just mine. Everyone's. The whole country agreed to forget, or was made to forget." Her eyes came up, and they were bright and hard and clear as winter water. "Then here's my price. You take this memory — all of it, the blue door and the singing and the burning and the names — and you give it back. Not to me. To *everyone.* Let the country remember what it agreed to forget. Let them remember what was done in that god's name, and by whose authority, and who profited." She leaned forward. "I don't want vengeance, girl. Vengeance is a debt paid to the dead, and the dead are paid. I want *witness.* Witness is a debt paid to the living. Can your ledger do that?"

Paloma sat very still. Because the ledger could — that was the terrible, wonderful thing. Memory was currency; the guild had always known it, had bottled grief and borrowed childhoods for generations. A restored memory, properly entered, properly witnessed, could spread. Truth was contagious. The cartographer had said so, in his careful way: *a place unwritten can be written again.*

And the usurper's throne, Sera had warned, was built on the founding crime. On erasures. On villages like Merrow's Bend, plural, uncounted.

One old woman's memory, loosed into the world, could burn down more than any army.

"Yes," Paloma said. "The ledger can do that. It will cost me — the guild's law says debts transfer, and a debt this size transfers to the debtor. Me. I'll carry it."

"Good," said Maren Ostrova, seventy-one years old, merciless in the way of the truly merciful. "Carry it well."

* * *

The settling took until dusk. Paloma wrote the entry by lantern light while Maren dictated corrections — *"not the north field, the *east* field, Father Ellery kept his bees in the north"* — and the ledger drank the memory like dry ground drinking rain. When it was done, the page for Merrow's Bend lay still at last, the ink fixed and calm, an account closed.

Maren pressed a charm into Paloma's hand at parting — the herb bundle, the one against being found, refused payment. "For the road. And keeper—" she caught their sleeve. "Whatever else you're carrying in that book, whatever other villages, whatever other fires — there are more, aren't there. I can see it in your face."

"There are more," Paloma said.

"Then you'd best hurry." She nodded east, toward the salt road, where the evening light was going the color of old blood. "Riders came through this morning. Usurper's vanguard, flying truce banners they weren't pretending to honor. They're making for the free cities, and they're not slowing for weather or prayer or anything else." She squeezed Paloma's arm with surprising strength. "Go be a witness quickly, girl. Before there's no one left to witness to."

Paloma walked out of Saltcross under the first stars, the closed ledger light against their chest and the open question heavier — *how many pages, how many fires, how much of me will it cost* — and behind them, faint on the wind, they could swear they heard the fiddler finally remember the ninth bar of the song.

By dawn, the smoke of army cookfires smudged the eastern horizon, and the ledger, in its inner pocket, had begun — very softly — to hum.
