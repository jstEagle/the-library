## 11. The Name Thief's Bargain

The rain in Corvane had a way of making the whole city smell like an apology — wet stone, wet wool, wet everything, all of it murmuring *sorry, sorry* under its breath. Paloma had lived here eleven years and had never once gotten used to it. In the salt country where they'd been born, rain came down like judgment, hard and honest. Here it just lingered, polite and useless, like a guest who wouldn't leave and kept refilling his own cup.

Paloma pulled their hood lower and followed the thief.

Or rather, followed the absence of the thief, which was harder. Nix Aulde — guild partner, keeper of the most dangerous page in the ledger house, and as of three days ago a traitor — had a gift that made tracking him less like following footprints and more like following the memory of footprints. People along the Salt Road remembered seeing him, but only vaguely, the way you remember a dream about someone you went to school with. The fishwife at the harbor gate recalled selling him eels and could not recall his face. The toll-keeper on the bridge swore no one had crossed, then frowned at his own ledger, where a crossing fee had been paid in exact change at dawn.

"He's wearing stolen names," Paloma muttered to themselves, ducking under the awning of a shuttered dye shop. "Of course he is. Why walk through a door when you can be the kind of person nobody remembers opening doors?"

The rain said nothing. It was very good at that. Paloma had recently begun to notice how much they preferred company that was silent, and had decided not to examine why.

They had one advantage over the city, over Sera Halvard's inquisitors, and over Nix himself: twenty years of keeping accounts for a guild that traded in impossible things had taught Paloma that everything leaves a residue. Grief left a residue. Luck left a residue. And a man walking around inside other people's identities left a residue too — a faint wrongness, like a coin clipped on one side. You couldn't see it. But if you'd spent two decades weighing intangibles on scales that existed nowhere in particular, you could feel it, the way a jeweler feels paste by temperature alone.

So Paloma walked the night markets of Low Corvane not looking for Nix, but looking for the places where the world's arithmetic didn't quite add up.

It took them until the third bell past midnight.

* * *

The residue pooled thickest around a noodle stall near the tannery canal, where an old woman named Besh served eel broth to dockworkers who were mostly asleep upright. Paloma sat down, ordered a bowl, and did what they always did when they needed information from someone who would never give it: they became boring.

"Besh," they said, after the appropriate amount of slurping. "You raised four sons alone after your husband drowned. Your middle boy, Tam, sends money from the pearl fleets even though he tells you he's a cook. You've been overcharging the tannery workers by two coppers a bowl for nine years because they've never once counted, and you put the difference in a jar you tell yourself is for your funeral but is actually for Tam's dowry problem, since his girl's family wants more than a cook earns."

Besh stared at her ladle. Then at Paloma. Then she laughed, a great bark of a laugh that scattered pigeons off the awning. "Ledger-keeper," she said. "Ivetta's shadow. What do you want?"

"Nix Aulde. He passed this stall tonight."

"Did he?" Besh refilled Paloma's bowl without being asked, which among certain people of Low Corvane constituted a formal treaty. "Funny. I remember a young man asking after the tannery stairs. Handsome. Or maybe not handsome. Maybe I'm thinking of my second husband." Her eyes sharpened, suddenly and completely awake. "He asked me what I'd take to forget my sons' faces. I told him there wasn't enough money in the world, and he looked sad about it, like I'd turned down a fair price."

"The tannery stairs," Paloma repeated.

"Top floor's empty since the fire. He paid me a silver to forget he'd asked." She shrugged. "I took the silver. I'm old, not stupid. But I'm telling you for free, ledger-keeper, because you knew about Tam." Her voice dropped. "That man doesn't steal things. He steals the *having* of things. There's a difference, and the difference is worse."

Paloma left coins on the counter — exact change, plus two coppers, out of respect for the craft — and climbed the tannery stairs into the dark.

* * *

The top floor of the burned tannery still smelled of char and sheep fat, and the rain came through the roof in six separate places, each drip finding its own rhythm so that the room sounded like a badly run clock shop. Nix Aulde sat in the middle of it on an overturned vat, feeding scraps of paper into a small flame, and he did not look up when Paloma's boots stopped at the top of the stairs.

"You're heavier than most people," he said. "Not in body. In consequence. Most folk, I hear them coming like weather. You, I hear coming like a debt collector." He smiled without warmth. "Which, to be fair."

"Nix."

"Paloma Eriksen." He pronounced it carefully, savoring it, and Paloma felt the syllables land somewhere behind their sternum like swallowed stones. Their true name. The one given at their oath to Vess, spoken aloud perhaps five times in twenty years, and never by anyone who understood what it weighed. Hearing it in a stranger's mouth felt like watching someone juggle knives near a sleeping child. "Sit. The vat's dry-ish. Can't offer tea. I burned the kettle's name last week and now it's just a pot with ambitions."

Paloma did not sit. "You took the Merrow's Bend page."

"I took several pages. That's the one you care about." Nix fed another scrap to the flame. In the light Paloma could see the pages were blank — every one of them. Names, dates, entries, all gone, eaten clean. "Do you know what happens when I burn a name, Paloma? Nothing dramatic. No scream. The person just gets slightly easier to be around. Less stubborn. Less *themselves*. It's the gentlest theft in the world, which is why no one's ever hanged me for it." He glanced up. His eyes were ordinary brown eyes, which somehow made it worse. "Except now something's been stealing back. From the other direction."

"What do you mean?"

"I mean three nights ago I tried to lift the name off a beggar in Tanner's Row — routine work, he owed the guild — and it wouldn't come. It was already *claimed*. Marked, like livestock. And the mark wasn't the usurper's church brand." Nix's voice lost its flippancy entirely. "It was older. It was *salt-country* old. And it led, if you followed it down, to a god everyone says is dead, sitting in a chapel full of fresh flowers, quietly repossessing the souls of everyone who ever promised Him anything." He tilted his head. "People like you, Paloma."

"Vess is imprisoned," Paloma said. "Locked outside the world. The cartographer proved it. The erased country—"

"Yes, yes, the Hollow, the unwritten nation, the geography-as-wound, Ivetta gave us all the same lecture with the same maps." Nix waved a hand. "And it's a lovely theory. Very tidy. God as prisoner. Prisoner as victim. Victim as someone we might rescue, which flatters us terribly." He leaned forward, and the firelight moved across his face like water. "But I've spent thirty years handling names, Paloma. I know what fear smells like on one. And I'm telling you what I found when I traced that mark on the beggar's soul all the way down to its root."

The rain filled the pause, six drips, seven.

"Vess isn't locked away," Nix said softly. "Vess is *hiding*. There's a difference, and the difference is worse." He echoed Besh without knowing it, and Paloma's skin prickled. "A prisoner wants out. A thing that's hiding wants to stay forgotten — unless forgetting stops being safe. And something has changed lately. Prayers are getting through. Doors are opening. So Vess is doing what any guilty creature does when the past comes knocking." He spread his hands over the little fire, over the blank pages. "He's eating the evidence."

Paloma thought of the ledger page in the vault beneath the guildhouse — the entry for Merrow's Bend, ink crawling like insects, the debt recorded and never collected. They thought of the prayers answered badly across the free cities: the fisherman's wish that drowned his neighbors, the child's mother returned wearing her face like a borrowed coat. Answered through stolen memories. Answered out of a pantry of devoured lives.

"If that's true," Paloma said slowly, "then opening the Hollow doesn't free a prisoner. It lets something out."

"Now you're catching up. Slowly. Like a rich man climbing stairs." Nix reached into his coat and drew out a folded page, and Paloma's whole body recognized it before their mind did — the weight of it, the way the candlelight bent toward it unwillingly. The Merrow's Bend entry. The massacre, itemized. Two hundred and six souls, taken in Vess's silence, entered in Paloma's own handwriting, in ink that had begun to move.

"Why did you defect?" Paloma asked. "Sera Halvard pays well?"

"Sera Halvard pays in absolution, which is worth less than nothing." Nix snorted. "No. I defected because I read this page. All of it. Every line." He held it up between two fingers. "And because I realized the guild's grand plan — settle every debt, open the door, save the free cities — requires settling *this* debt. And there are only two ways to settle a debt like this, Paloma. Someone pays it, or someone forgives it. And Vess—" he tapped the page, "—does not forgive. Vess collects. Which means either you find two hundred and six souls to pay with, or you repeat the transaction that created the debt in the first place. Another village. Another silence. Another ledger line in your handwriting."

The rain came down. Somewhere below, a dog barked twice and thought better of it.

"So I ran," Nix said simply. "First time in my life I've run from anything. Felt awful. Highly recommend it." He turned the page over in his fingers. "Unless, of course, you have a third option."

"That's why I'm here."

"Mm. I assumed. Nobody climbs tannery stairs at this hour for the ambiance." He studied Paloma with those ordinary eyes, and for a moment the flippancy fell away entirely and Paloma saw the craftsman underneath — the same look Ivetta got appraising a bottled grief, careful and a little hungry. "What are you offering?"

"My true name."

Nix went very still. Even the fire seemed to lean in.

"You understand what that means," he said quietly. "Your oath-name. The thread Vess holds. Cut it, and the god's claim on you snaps. No more answered prayers in your ear. No more being the instrument. You'd be free to burn the ledgers, refuse the massacre, walk away from all of it." His voice dropped further. "And also free of every protection the oath ever gave you. Free of the guild's standing. Free of—" he hesitated, "—whatever else He wove into you. Are you sure there isn't anything else woven in?"

Paloma thought of the half-heard warmth in their ear during prayer. The sense, these past weeks, of being *known* — thoroughly, tenderly, the way a butcher knows a carcass. They thought of Sera's face, and of how much of their life they'd spent mistaking devotion for love because the two had arrived in the same voice.

"No," Paloma said honestly. "I don't know what else is woven in. But I know I'm done being collateral."

"It won't be painless. Cutting a true name is—" Nix grimaced. "Imagine the worst hangover of your life, except what hurts is *having been yourself*. For days. Some people don't come all the way back. They come back slightly smaller." He shrugged. "Most of us are slightly smaller anyway. It's called aging."

"And in exchange you give me the page, and you come back to the guild."

"In exchange I give you the page, and I *consider* coming back to the guild, pending assurances that we're not actually planning to open a door for a god who eats memories." Nix stood, joints cracking, and held the page out over the small fire — not dropping it, just holding it there, an offer balanced above destruction. "Last chance to bargain, ledger-keeper. I warn you, I drive a hard trade. Ask anyone whose name I own."

"Ask anyone whose name you *owned*," Paloma corrected. "You sold most of them back. Besh told me. You've returned forty-one names in six years and charged nothing, and you think nobody's noticed because you're insufferable in public."

Nix blinked. For the first time, the thief of names looked genuinely caught — a man discovered mid-kindness, which is the most embarrassing way to be discovered. "...She talked," he said weakly.

"She talks to everyone. It's her superpower. Right after the broth."

There was a long moment. Rain drummed the dead roof. And then Nix Aulde began to laugh — really laugh, doubled over, wheezing, the laugh of a man setting down something heavy after carrying it a very long way. "Gods," he gasped, "twenty years with Ivetta and you're still the most frightening person in that building. Fine. Fine! The page for the name. One cut, clean, no interest accrued." He straightened, wiped his eyes, and his expression sobered into something almost gentle. "One condition of my own, freely given, no charge. When you make your choice about Vess — and you will have to make it soon, He's getting louder, I can hear Him from *here* — remember what I told you tonight."

"Hiding, not imprisoned."

"Hiding, not imprisoned." Nix nodded. "Because a prisoner, Paloma, you can pity. You can rescue. A coward with a god's appetite is another matter entirely. And when He speaks to you — and He will, He's been saving His voice for you specifically, I'd bet every name I own — listen to *how* He sounds. Not what He says. How." He held out the page. "Does He sound like something locked in a cage? Or does He sound like something hoping you'll never ask what's buried in the yard?"

Paloma took the page. The ink writhed under their fingertips, warm as a living thing, and two hundred and six names shifted against each other like fish in a net.

Then Paloma closed their eyes, reached down into the place where their oath lived — the warm thread behind the sternum, humming faintly, faithful as a heartbeat — and spoke their true name aloud for the sixth time in twenty years, and the last time in any.

Nix caught it out of the air like a moth.

The cutting was exactly as bad as advertised.

When Paloma could stand again — minutes or hours later, kneeling on the charred floor while the rain dripped its broken clockwork around them — the warmth behind their sternum was gone. Not torn out. Simply *absent*, like a tooth, like a season. The silence where Vess had lived in them was enormous, and cold, and theirs.

"There," Nix said softly, folding the stolen name away into some pocket that wasn't a pocket. He sounded shaken, though he clearly wished not to be. "Done. You're the freest person in Corvane, and I'd wager the loneliest. Congratulations on both."

Paloma pushed themselves up, clutching the page. "Come back to the guild, Nix. Ivetta needs—"

The words died.

Because far away — across the city, across the salt marshes, beyond the edge of every map — something noticed the thread go slack. Something vast and patient and terribly fond turned its attention toward the place where its claim used to be, and found a hole shaped like Paloma Eriksen.

And in the enormous silence where their god used to live, Paloma heard — faint, unmistakable, closer than breath —

*"Oh,"* said Vess, in a voice like a mother waking to an empty crib. *"Oh, my child. What have you let them take from us?"*

Nix took one look at Paloma's face, snuffed his little fire with his bare hand, and said, "Run."
