## 12. Vess Speaks

The stolen page sat on Paloma's desk for three hours before they dared touch it.

They had lit every candle in the guildhouse first — eleven of them, arranged in the old protective ring that Ivetta called superstition and the priest of three dead gods called "adequate." They had brewed the bitter tea Sera used to drink before interrogations, though Paloma had no idea why, except that some habits outlive their reasons. And they had locked the door, which was theater, because a door had never once stopped anything that mattered.

The page lay flat under a paperweight shaped like a sleeping cat. It was Merrow's Bend. Of course it was. The thief of names — a wiry, apologetic man called Osk who stole things the way other people sneezed, involuntarily and at the worst possible moments — had taken it because it was the guild's most dangerous page, and dangerous pages were his favorite flavor. Paloma had bought it back with a bargain they still hadn't told Ivetta about, a bargain involving their own true name, spoken aloud into Osk's cupped hands like water poured into a dry well.

*Later,* Paloma told themselves. *Worry about that later. There is a long tradition in this guild of worrying later.*

The ink on the page was moving again. Not the slow crawl of chapter four, when Paloma had first found the debt recorded and uncollected — *one village, extinguished; payment deferred* — but something livelier now. The words shifted like fish under ice. The numbers rearranged themselves. Where the entry had once read simply *Merrow's Bend*, it now read, in handwriting that was not Paloma's and yet knew Paloma's hand better than Paloma did:

*Merrow's Bend, and all bends like it. Payment due at opening.*

Paloma sat down. The chair complained. Everything in Corvane complained if you asked it to hold your weight while your world tilted.

"Right," they said aloud, to the page, to the candles, to the silent god who had been silent for twenty years and was apparently only now getting around to checking his mail. "Let's you and I have a conversation."

Nothing happened, for a while. The candles burned. Somewhere below, Ivetta was arguing with the cartographer about whether a valley could be said to exist if no map admitted to it, an argument that had been going on, in one form or another, since before the conquest. The sounds of the guild rose up through the floorboards like a heartbeat: the scratch of pens, the clink of bottled grief being inventoried, the thief's distant voice saying *I'm sorry, I'm sorry, give it back, I said give it back—*

Paloma picked up the page.

And Vess spoke.

---

It did not come as thunder. Paloma had spent twenty years imagining what a god's voice would sound like when it finally came — they had imagined avalanches, they had imagined bells, they had imagined the low grinding of tectonic plates. What came instead was warmth. A hearth-warmth, a bathwater warmth, the warmth of a hand pressed between shoulder blades at exactly the moment grief crests. It came up through the desk, through the chair, through the soles of Paloma's boots, and it said:

*Little ledger-keeper. You kept count for me all these years.*

Paloma's eyes were wet before they decided to be. That was the obscenity of it. Twenty years of silence, twenty years of prayers dropped into a well with no bottom, twenty years of carrying out atrocities in the name of a god who gave nothing back — and the first thing Vess did was make them cry with tenderness.

"You left," Paloma said. Their voice came out steady, which surprised them. "You left us in the dark. You let them erase you, and erase everything with you, and we — I —"

*I did what love required,* Vess said, and the warmth deepened, wrapped closer, and it would have been easy — it would have been so easy — to sink into it and never come up for air. *Every step of it. You know this. You felt my heart in yours when you walked into Merrow's Bend. You were never alone there, little one. Not for one moment.*

"No," Paloma said. "That's rather the problem."

The candles flickered. The warmth paused — a held breath, a mother listening for her child's tone.

*Explain,* Vess said, gently. Gently, as if Paloma were a student who had gotten a sum wrong. As if the error were charming.

"Merrow's Bend," Paloma said. "Two hundred and six souls. I counted them. I always count them, you made sure of that — you put the counting in me when you took my oath, so that even the killing would be tidy. A fisherman who sang off-key. A woman who was eight months pregnant. Children whose names I wrote down afterward because no one else would ever write them down at all. I did that in your silence, believing you'd want it done, believing that faith meant acting as if the god were watching even when he wasn't. And now you're awake, and the first thing you ask—"

*Is what I have always asked.* The warmth did not cool. That was the terrible thing. It stayed warm. It stayed warm the way fever stays warm. *Open the Hollow, little keeper. Open the door to my country, where they buried me alive with everything I loved. But doors are hungry things, and mine has been shut for twenty years, and it will not open on arithmetic alone. It opens on payment. You know the currency. You've carried it since Merrow's Bend.*

Paloma looked down at the page. The ink had stilled, waiting, attentive, like a dog that knows the word *walk*.

"A village," Paloma said quietly. "You're asking for another village."

*A bend,* Vess corrected, with the mild precision of a god correcting a clerk's grammar. *A gathering. Two hundred souls, more or less. Given freely or taken cleanly — I leave the method to your conscience, which I have always admired. Their memories will feed the threshold. Their forgetting will be my remembering. This is how gods eat, little one. It is how I have always eaten. How do you think your prayers were answered, all those years?*

Something went cold in Paloma's chest, and it was not the god's doing.

"The answered prayers," they said. "The fisherman's wish that drowned his neighbors. The child's mother, come back wearing her face. That was you."

*Borrowed,* Vess said, and there was pride in it, warm and awful. *I had nothing, you understand. Nothing but what worshipers remembered. So I borrowed. I am paying it back — I keep my ledgers too, little keeper, I learned bookkeeping from watching you — but a door must be fed, and the free cities are full of memories, and full of mouths that pray without thinking what prayer costs.*

Paloma stood up. The chair skidded back. The candles guttered in a wind that was not wind.

"I refuse."

The word came out smaller than they wanted. So they said it again, louder, to the ceiling, to the hearth-warmth coiled in the room like a patient animal.

"I refuse. Do you hear me? Twenty years I served a silence. I built my whole life inside that silence — the ledgers, the oath, the counting, the — the massacre, gods forgive me, the massacre I committed because I thought devotion meant filling in for an absent god. And now you're here, and you're warm, and you know what? Warmth isn't the same as goodness. I've sold enough bottled grief to know the difference. You want me to walk into some border village and do Merrow's Bend again, tidily, with my conscience admiring itself the whole time, so your door can open. And I am telling you — for the first time in my life, Vess, the actual first time — no."

Silence.

Not the old silence. The old silence had been absence, a well with no bottom, a god presumed dead. This silence was presence. This silence was a listener deciding.

Then, softly, with a tenderness that curdled even as it soothed:

*You are tired, little one. You have carried so much. Give me the page. Give me the ledgers. Rest. I will carry the arithmetic now.*

"No."

*You love them,* Vess said. *The free cities. The refugees. The old woman selling charms who cannot remember her own village. I know you love them. It is the finest thing about you. And because you love them, you will see reason: the armies are coming, little keeper. The usurper's vanguard is three days out. When they arrive, everyone you love burns or flees, and the door is the only way out, and the door is hungry, and I am offering you a small price against an ocean of salvation. Refuse me, and you choose the fire. Is that what love looks like?*

"It's what arithmetic looks like," Paloma said. "And I've finally read the whole column. You know what I noticed at the bottom? The sums don't work. They've never worked. Every answer you ever gave cost more than the prayer was worth. Every miracle was a loan shark's kindness. You didn't die for us, Vess. You hid. You erased a whole country to escape a bill, and now you want me to pay it for you, in villages."

The warmth pulled back — just slightly, just an inch, the way a lover pulls back when you've said the true thing.

*Careful,* Vess said.

"No," Paloma said, and found, to their astonishment, that they were smiling. It was not a happy smile. It was the smile of a person setting down a weight they'd forgotten they were carrying. "No, I don't think I'll be careful. I've been careful for twenty years. Careful got me two hundred and six names in a ledger and a god who calls a massacre *a bend*. Take the true name Osk is holding, if you can reach it. Take the oath. Take everything. But I will not feed your door with anyone's children. Burn the free cities yourself, if that's your love. I'm done committing your atrocities on credit."

The candle ring went out — all eleven flames at once, pinched by nothing.

In the dark, Vess spoke one last time, and the warmth was gone from it entirely, and what remained was vast and old and hurt, which was somehow worse than monstrous:

*Then you are oath-broken, little keeper. And I withdraw what I lent you.*

"What did you—" Paloma began. "What did you lend me?"

But the dark was already answering. It came up through the floor like cold water rising in a hull. It took the edges of the room first, then the middle. Paloma heard, very far away, Ivetta's voice calling their name, and reached for the desk, and the desk was not where desks kept themselves—

The last thing they saw was the page, ink writhing, and the last line writing itself in that handwriting that loved them:

*Account closed.*

Then the world went out like a snuffed wick, and somewhere beneath the blackness, faint and receding, Paloma heard the sound of a door beginning, very slowly, to breathe.
