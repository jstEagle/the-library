## 13. Excommunication

The silence had a shape.

Paloma had spent twenty years inside Vess's silence and had thought themselves an expert on it — its texture, its patience, the way it sat in the chest like a stone dropped into deep water. But this was different. This was not the god being quiet. This was the god turning away, and taking the light with it.

They had been struck mute on the steps of the guildhouse. That much they remembered: the page recovered from the name thief's coat, Vess's voice pouring through them like warm honey, and then their own refusal — one word, *no*, spoken aloud for the first time in two decades of service — and the world going out like a lamp pinched between wet fingers.

Now there was a ceiling above them. Whitewashed. A crack running through it like a river on a map of nowhere.

*Half-blind,* someone had said. Voices came to them as if through wool. The left eye saw candle-glow and shadow; the right saw only a soft grey fog, the color of a country that had been unwritten.

And no voice of their own. When they tried to speak, the word simply wasn't there. Not blocked — *absent*. As if Vess had reached down their throat and collected even that small debt.

A chair creaked nearby. Someone was sitting vigil.

Paloma turned their head — slowly, everything was slow now — and found Sera Halvard asleep in a kitchen chair pulled up to the bedside, still in her inquisitor's grays, her boots muddy, her sword laid across her knees like a cat she didn't trust.

Of all people.

Sera, who had cornered them in the archive three nights ago with a confession trembling on her lips. Sera, who had offered them escape in exchange for the ledgers. Sera, whose armies were even now grinding up the salt road toward Corvane.

She'd stayed.

Paloma lay very still and watched her sleep, because it was the first time in eleven years they had been permitted to look at her without a god or a guild or a war standing between them, and some petty, stubborn corner of their heart intended to take full advantage.

Her hair had gone grey at the temples. She'd have hated that, once. She'd probably hate it now. There was ink on her fingers — she'd been writing, or reading, or both. Her left hand, the one that used to find Paloma's in the dark of the drowned chapel, was curled loosely near the edge of the mattress, close enough that if Paloma had any voice at all, they might have said something unforgivably sentimental about it.

The candle guttered. Sera woke the way soldiers wake — all at once, hand to sword, then blinking, then seeing Paloma awake, and going pink to the ears.

"You're up," she said. "Good. That's — good." She cleared her throat. "You've been asleep for two days."

Paloma raised an eyebrow. It was, they discovered, the only eloquence remaining to them, and they resolved to make it count.

"Don't look at me like that," Sera said. "I couldn't leave you on the steps. People were stepping over you. One man took your coat."

*My coat,* Paloma mouthed, outraged.

"I got it back," Sera said quickly. "Mostly. He kept the buttons. I think he felt entitled to them, under the circumstances."

Paloma closed their eyes. Eleven years of longing, and here she was, arguing about buttons. It was so perfectly, unbearably *her* that something in their chest cracked open sideways, and if they could have wept loudly they would have. Instead a single tear escaped down the working side of their face, which was somehow worse.

"Oh," Sera said softly. "Oh, don't. Please don't do that, I'm terrible when people cry, you remember. I once made a grieving widow laugh at her husband's funeral by accident. She threw a shoe."

Paloma's shoulders shook. It might have been laughter. It might have been grief. With Sera it had always been both, in roughly equal measure.

* * *

Sera's hands were steady. They had always been steady — steady enough to hold a blade at the usurper's coronation, steady enough to sign the orders she hadn't wanted to sign. But holding a cup of broth to Paloma's lips, they trembled, just slightly, and she hated herself for it.

*He can't hear me,* she reminded herself, for the hundredth time since she'd carried Paloma up the back stairs of a rented room above a chandler's shop. *He can't speak, can't write, half-blind. Whatever's left of him in there, it isn't reporting to anyone.*

That was the official reasoning, anyway. The reasoning she would put in a report, if she ever wrote another report, which she would not, because three days ago she had done the arithmetic every inquisitor eventually does and found that the sum of her obedience came to more than she was willing to pay.

The truth was simpler and more shameful: she had seen Paloma fall, and her body had moved before her doctrine could stop it.

"Slowly," she murmured, tilting the cup. "You're gulping like a gull."

Paloma glared at her with their one good eye. It was a remarkably expressive glare. Sera had forgotten how much of Paloma lived in their face — how the ledgers and the god and the oath had all been furniture arranged around that quick, wry, watchful expression, like a lamp in a window.

"You know," she said, because the silence needed filling and she had never been good at holy quiet, "when I found you, my first thought was that you'd finally been killed by your own accounting. All those ledgers. I used to say you'd die of a paper cut."

Paloma's mouth twitched.

"My second thought," Sera went on, quieter, "was that I'd spent eleven years learning exactly where everyone in this country was, so that I could protect the conquest, and the moment it mattered — the moment *you* mattered — I knew precisely where you were and I still almost stepped over you on the stairs."

Paloma set down the cup with great deliberation, took her ink-stained hand, and pressed it flat against their own chest, over the heart. Then they looked at her, level and unblinking, and mouthed, with exaggerated clarity:

*Liar.*

"I am not—" Sera stopped. Breathed. "All right. Yes. Fine. I hesitated. Four seconds. I counted them later, which is its own kind of damning." She didn't pull her hand away. That was the thing she couldn't explain, wouldn't be able to explain to any tribunal, earthly or divine: that she hadn't pulled her hand away. "Four seconds, and then I picked you up, and I've been here since, and I haven't sent word to anyone, and I should be hanged for it."

Paloma squeezed her hand. Once. Firmly. The gesture of a bookkeeper confirming a figure.

"Don't," Sera said. "Don't absolve me. You're mute, not ordained."

But Paloma's eye was shining, and their thumb had moved — just barely — across her knuckles, in the old pattern, the one they'd invented in the drowned chapel years ago, four strokes meaning what neither of them had ever managed to say aloud in daylight.

Sera looked away first. She always had.

* * *

The days took on a shape of their own, and the shape was domestic, which frightened Sera more than the armies did.

She learned the grammar of Paloma's silence. The raised eyebrow for *go on*. The slow blink for *you're being an idiot, but affectionately*. The flat stare for genuine disapproval, deployed most often when Sera tried to feed them fish. And writing — Paloma could still write, though the letters came out wrong-handed and wavering, and after the third attempt at a sentence dissolved into something resembling a spider's funeral, they'd flung the pen across the room with surprising force.

Sera retrieved it. "You always did have a temper about handwriting."

*I have a temper about EVERYTHING,* Paloma wrote, in enormous crooked capitals, *and you KNOW this.*

"I know this," Sera agreed solemnly, and then ruined the solemnity by laughing, and then Paloma was laughing too — soundlessly, which was somehow funnier, their whole body shaking while their face did all the work of mirth at double volume.

It was, Sera thought later, the strangest week of her life, and she had once spent a month cataloguing relics that screamed. Here she was, an inquisitor of the Usurper's Holy Office, sharing bread and bad wine with a heretic, a godsworn killer, a person whose ledger she had read — she had *read it*, during those four seconds of hesitation, or perhaps she was lying to herself about when — and instead of horror she felt something she had no doctrinal term for.

Comfort. Unlicensed comfort.

On the fifth night, Paloma wrote a question and held it up to the candlelight:

*Why are you really here?*

Sera took the slate. She held it a long time. Then she wiped his question away and wrote her answer underneath, because some things were easier written than said, and she suspected Paloma of all people understood that.

*Because I heard him too. In the archive. Vess. He asked me for your name, and I said no, and I have never said no to anything in my life, and it felt like being born.*

Paloma stared at the words for a long moment. Then, slowly, they reached out and touched the slate, right over the word *no*, the way you'd touch a scar that had finally stopped hurting.

Then they wrote: *What did it cost?*

Sera laughed, but it came out wrong. "Everything, presumably. It usually does. Ask me again when the bill arrives."

* * *

On the seventh night, the fever came.

It swept through Paloma like a tide, and with it came fragments — Sera caught them in snatches as she sponged their brow and changed cool cloths and cursed the chandler downstairs for burning tallow that smelled like a burning fleet. Paloma spoke in the fever. Not with their stolen voice, but with their whole body, thrashing, mouthing words at the ceiling, and Sera bent close and caught pieces.

"—the village— I didn't ask what the rite was for—"

"—Merrow's Bend—"

"—he was so *warm*, Sera, he was warm the whole time, that's the worst of it—"

Sera froze, cloth in hand.

She knew that name. Every officer of the Holy Office knew that name, or rather knew the absence of it — Merrow's Bend appeared in no census, on no map, in no record, and the fact that it appeared nowhere was itself classified. She had signed the erasure order's *confirmation*, years ago, one signature among hundreds, bureaucratic and bloodless.

And Paloma had carried it out. She understood that now, the way you understand a wound is yours: completely, and too late.

When the fever broke near dawn, Paloma lay grey and spent, and Sera sat beside them and made a decision that no inquisitor in the history of the office had ever made sober, and she was entirely sober.

She unclasped the gray badge from her collar — the eye within the circle — and set it on the table where Paloma could see it.

"I'm done," she said. Her voice didn't shake, which surprised her. "I sent my last report four days ago. It said I was pursuing a lead south. By tomorrow they'll know that's false. By the day after, there will be a warrant with my face on it." She turned the badge over. The metal caught the candlelight. "Eleven years I told myself I served the law. But the law burned villages and then burned the *memory* of villages, and called the ash peace. And I signed things." She looked at Paloma — at the ruin of them, the mute and half-blind wreck of the person she had loved across a war — and found, to her astonishment, that she was smiling. "So. I'm unemployed. I'm treasonous. I'm sleeping in a chair above a chandler's shop. And I have never in my life been more certain that I'm in the right place."

Paloma's good eye was wet. Their hand crept across the blanket, and Sera met it halfway, and for a long time neither of them moved, and no god watched them, and no ledger recorded it, and it was the holiest moment either of them had ever known.

*Say something,* Paloma mouthed at last, wickedly. *You're terrible at silence.*

"You're one to talk," Sera said, and kissed their knuckles, and let herself laugh at the absurdity of it — the two of them, excommunicate and deserter, hiding above a candle shop while the world ended politely outside.

* * *

It was near noon when the knock came at the door — three sharp raps, unhurried, in a rhythm Sera did not know and Paloma, propped on pillows, recognized instantly with a lurch of dread.

Sera drew her sword and opened the door a hand's width.

Ivetta stood in the corridor, guildmaster's rings on every finger, flanked by nobody at all, which was somehow more intimidating than an honor guard. Her eyes went from Sera's drawn blade, to Sera's bare collar, to the gray badge gleaming on the table, and her expression performed a complicated journey that ended somewhere near grim delight.

"Well," Ivetta said. "The inquisitor. Holding the door for me. There's a parable in that, and I haven't the time to write it." She stepped past the sword as if it were a curtain. "Paloma. You look dreadful. Good. Dreadful suits you; it means you're finally taking something seriously."

Paloma raised the eyebrow. *Go on.*

"The Hollow opens in six days," Ivetta said. "The valley, the stones, all of it. We begin settling accounts tonight — every debt in the ledgers, called in, paid, closed. Except." She paused, and for the first time since Paloma had known her, the guildmaster looked uncomfortable holding a piece of news. "Except yours. Yours has grown, Paloma. Interest compounds. And there's a new entry — written itself into the book overnight, in ink none of us mixed. Would you like to know what it says?"

Paloma nodded slowly. Beside them, Sera had gone very still.

"It says," Ivetta read, from memory, her voice flattening, "*One soul, owed to Vess, payable at the door.*" She folded her hands. "It doesn't say whose soul. But the ink is your handwriting, Paloma. Down to the way you loop your sevens."

Outside, far off along the salt road, a horn sounded — once, low, patient. The vanguard, closer than anyone had admitted.

Sera's hand found Paloma's without looking. "Six days," she said quietly. "Then we settle it ourselves. Whatever it costs."

Paloma looked at the slate, at the pen lying where they'd flung it, at the woman who had chosen them over a crown, and picked up the pen.

*Then we'd better start counting,* they wrote.
