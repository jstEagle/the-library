## 14. The Ledger Burns Slowly

Ivetta did not shout. That was the worst of it. Paloma had prepared themselves for shouting—had rehearsed defenses in the dark hours after Vess's voice had left them hollowed and half-blind, arguments stacked like sandbags against a flood. But Ivetta simply sat behind her desk in the guildhouse at Corvane, her hands folded on the great ledger of the guild itself, and looked at Paloma the way an accountant looks at a column that will not balance.

"Say it," Paloma said. Their voice was still rough from the muteness Vess had left behind, like a door scraped open over stone.

"You are oath-broken." Ivetta said it quietly, the way she recorded deaths. "You refused a direct commandment of the god to whom you are bound. You refused it *publicly*, before witnesses, which means half the guild heard it and the other half will hear it by supper. And you did it while holding the page that opens our only way out of this country." She turned the ledger toward them. There was a line already written there, in her own hand, ink still wet enough to shine. *Paloma Eriksen. Struck from the rolls. Barred from the Hollow.*

"I refused to burn another village," Paloma said.

"Yes," Ivetta agreed. "That's what makes it so difficult. If you'd refused out of cowardice, I could have had you flogged and kept you on. If you'd refused out of ambition, I could have had you killed and slept well. But you refused because you were *right*, and rightness is contagious, and I cannot have it walking through my guildhall." She pressed her thumb below the entry, sealing it with wax from the candle at her elbow. The wax was guild-red, the color of contracts. "You may keep your rooms until the end of the month. You may not approach the Hollow, the valley, or any partner of this guild on guild business. And Paloma—" for one moment something old and tired moved behind her eyes, something that remembered a younger mercenary who had once carried her through a fever in the Salt Marshes, "—I am sorry. Truly. It changes nothing, but I am sorry."

Paloma nodded. They had expected the apology to hurt more than the sentence. It didn't. Perhaps they had less room left inside them for hurt than they'd thought. They would learn, over the coming weeks, exactly how much room they had—and exactly how much of it they were willing to sell.

* * *

The first debt they settled was small, which felt fitting. You do not begin a war with your heaviest artillery; you begin with reconnaissance. Besides, Paloma's hands needed something to do, and idle hands, in their experience, tended to start hearing gods again.

The debtor was a cooper named Anselm, three streets down from the guildhouse, who owed the guild four crowns for a vial of borrowed courage he'd used to propose to his wife eleven years ago. She had said yes. He had never managed to repay it, because courage, once spent, does not leave much change.

Paloma knocked on his door on a gray morning, mute-eyed still, their vision at the edges soft as wet paper since Vess's withdrawal. Anselm opened the door, saw the guild colors, and went the color of his own unfinished barrels.

"I can't pay," he said immediately. "I've told your people, I've told everyone—I'll pay, but I can't pay *now*—"

"You're going to pay now," Paloma said. "But not in coin."

They sat in his workshop among the shavings and the smell of oak, and Paloma explained the trade as gently as they knew how—which was not very, but honestly. The ledgers were burning. Not literally, not yet, though Paloma had begun to think of fire the way other people thought of weather, as a thing always somewhere nearby. Every account had to be settled before the Hollow opened, and coin was no longer the currency that mattered. Memory was. The god had answered prayers with stolen memories; the debts, therefore, would be paid in memories given freely, willingly, one per account, and Paloma would stand as the counterparty for every one.

"And where do *you* get memories to give?" Anselm asked, because coopers are practical people.

"From myself," Paloma said. "One for each account I settle."

Anselm stared at them. Then, with the slow dawning of a man doing sums he doesn't like: "How many accounts are there?"

"In the full ledger? Eleven thousand, four hundred and six."

The silence in the workshop was long enough that a spider crossed the floor and left again, disappointed.

"That's—" Anselm began.

"I know how many it is," Paloma said.

They took the memory of Anselm's wedding day—not all of it, just the moment when his wife had stepped through the chapel door and every plan he'd ever made had rearranged itself around her face. In exchange, Anselm's debt burned off the ledger page in Paloma's satchel, the ink lifting like smoke. Paloma walked home afterward feeling strangely light, and realized halfway up the guild steps that they no longer quite remembered what the inside of that chapel had looked like. They remembered that it had mattered. They remembered the shape of the mattering. The rest was gone, paid out like coin from a purse.

It should have frightened them more than it did. That, too, was a kind of payment—the god's withdrawal had taken their fear's sharp edges first, leaving something blunter, easier to carry. Or perhaps, Paloma thought, climbing toward their cold room, they had simply been afraid for so long that fear, too, had a balance outstanding, and some part of them was settling it early.

* * *

Word traveled, as word does in free cities, faster than any courier. By the end of the week, people were coming to *them*.

They came to the little rented room above the chandler's shop that Paloma had taken after leaving the guildhouse—a room that smelled of tallow and held a bed, a desk, and a window with a view of a wall, which Paloma found honest. They came with their ledger pages clutched like tickets. A widow who owed for a year of borrowed sleep. A dockhand who'd bought luck against drowning and then, in a fit of conscience, tried to return the unused portion. A boy of maybe sixteen who owed for a stolen name—he'd bought a dead man's name to escape conscription, and the guilt had been eating him alive ever since.

"The thing about names," the boy said, twisting his cap in his hands, "is that I keep answering to him. Marten. Someone calls Marten in the market and I turn around before I know I'm doing it. Is that—is that normal?"

"No," Paloma said. "But it's fixable."

They settled his account and took, in payment from themselves, the memory of learning to ride. It seemed fair. Horses had never liked them anyway, and the feeling had evidently been mutual enough to survive even the loss of the memory, because the next morning a carthorse in the street below tried to bite them through the window.

The gentle humor of it—that was what kept them upright, in those weeks. That was the rope along the cliff edge. Because the work was otherwise unbearable, if you let yourself look at it straight. Every evening Paloma sat at the desk and took inventory of themselves the way a merchant takes inventory before a long voyage, cataloguing what remained, deciding what could be spared. The taste of river water in high summer. Kept. The smell of Ivetta's office, pipe smoke and sealing wax. Kept, for now. The sound of rain on the drowned chapel roof, the day they swore their oath. Spent—spent on the dockhand's account, and good riddance, though they missed it within days, the way you miss a tooth.

And Sera. Sera was the problem. Sera was the whole problem, arranged in the shape of a woman.

* * *

It happened on the nineteenth day, on the account of a ferryman named Odile who had borrowed twenty years of safe passage and whose debt was too large for anything small. Paloma had been putting this account off. They were not a coward—they had refused a god to his face, or to whatever a god keeps instead of a face—but they had put this one off, and they knew why, and the knowing sat in their chest like a swallowed stone.

Because the ledger required payment proportional to the debt, and Odile's debt was enormous, and the only things in Paloma's inventory large enough were the years with Sera. All of them. The whole archive.

They sat at the desk by lamplight and laid it out with a bookkeeper's terrible clarity. If they paid Sera in pieces—one meeting here, one night there—the debt would take six accounts to settle, and each partial payment would leave them holding fragments of a person, shards that wouldn't fit together, a love remembered the way you remember a song someone hummed once in another room. Worse than forgetting. Forgetting cleanly was a mercy. Fragments were a haunting.

Or they could pay it all at once. Every memory of Sera Halvard, surrendered in a single transaction, and walk out of this room not knowing they had ever loved anyone at all.

Paloma took out a fresh sheet of paper, because they were still, at heart, a keeper of ledgers, and wrote at the top: *Account of Odile, ferrywoman. Payment tendered.* Then they sat for a long time, pen hovering, and finally wrote beneath it, in a hand that only shook a little:

*One (1) face. Hers. As it was at dawn, at dusk, at the moment she told me she'd been assigned to the conquest's inquisition and asked me not to hate her, and I said I could never, and we both knew I was lying about the future but not about the present. One (1) laugh, the real one, the ugly snorting one she denied having. One (1) winter, the good one, before everything. One (1) scar on her left palm and the story of how she got it, which she told badly on purpose because she liked watching me correct the details. All of it. The lot. Consider the account closed.*

They read it twice. Then they folded it and put it in the drawer where they kept the pages of settled debts, because they could not bring themselves to burn it yet, and because some part of them—the part that was still doing arithmetic in the dark—whispered that a written record is a kind of anchor, and someday, someday, someone might read it back to them.

Then they closed their eyes and paid.

* * *

The transaction itself was almost nothing. That was the obscenity of it. No thunder, no tearing. Just a sensation like a page turning somewhere behind their eyes, and then—

They opened their eyes in the rented room above the chandler's shop, and there was a sheet of paper on the desk in their own handwriting, and they read it, and understood that they had loved someone named Sera Halvard, inquisitor, once their lover, currently hunting them across a country at war. They knew the facts the way you know facts from a report. What they could not find, anywhere in themselves, was her face.

They knew she had a scar on her left palm. They did not know what her hands looked like. They knew she laughed—there was a note about a laugh, an *ugly snorting laugh*, and Paloma sat with that for a long moment, grieving something they couldn't picture, mourning a sound they couldn't hear. It was like being handed a map of a country that had sunk beneath the sea. Here, the coastline. Here, the mountains. All of it underwater now, all of it gone, and you standing on the shore holding paper.

"Well," Paloma said aloud, to the empty room, because the alternative was not speaking at all. "That was phenomenally stupid."

And then, because grief without humor is just drowning, they added: "At least the ferrywoman's square."

Odile's debt burned off the ledger. Word would spread again. There were eleven thousand accounts, give or take, and Paloma had settled forty-one, and at this rate they would be finished sometime after the sun burned out. But the rate didn't matter tonight. Tonight what mattered was that they had done the hardest one—or what they'd believed was the hardest one—and survived it, mostly, minus a face.

They blew out the lamp. In the dark, they found they were still crying, though they couldn't have said for whom, and that seemed somehow the truest prayer they'd ever offered: tears with no god attached, no name, no debt. Just water, falling freely, owing nothing to anyone.

* * *

Three days later, the survivor from Merrow's Bend found them.

Paloma was in the market buying candles—they went through candles now, working late over the ledgers—and they saw her across the stalls, an old woman at a charm-seller's table, and something in Paloma's chest lurched with recognition that had no content, like a dog hearing its name in a language it has forgotten. They knew her. They knew they knew her. The knowing arrived without a single fact attached, stripped bare by some earlier payment, and it pulled them across the market with their heart hammering.

She looked up as they approached, and her eyes—her eyes were wrong, or rather they were *right* in a way nothing else in Corvane was right anymore. They were full. Full of remembering. Around her, other market-goers had stopped, and were looking at her, and were touching their own temples, and one man had sat down abruptly on a crate with the expression of a man seeing a house he grew up in burn.

"You're the one paying," the old woman said. Not asking. "The one trading yourself away, account by account. They talk about you in the border towns like a saint." She smiled, and it was not a kind smile, but it was not unkind either. It was the smile of someone holding a lit match in a dry field. "I don't need a saint. I need a witness. My village is coming back to me, guildkeeper. All of it. Every house, every name, every grave." She leaned closer, and lowered her voice, and the market around them had gone very quiet indeed. "And it isn't coming back alone. Do you understand? The usurper's founding crime is coming back with it. Merrow's Bend remembers now. And memory, mercenary, is *contagious*."

Behind her, across the market square, a woman began to weep for a village she had not known she had lost. Then another. Somewhere a bell began to ring—not the alarm bell, not yet, but the old bell, the one that rang for assemblies, for gatherings, for the things a city must decide together.

Paloma stood in the candle-smoke with their half-empty self and their full satchel of settled debts, and understood that the ledger was no longer the only thing burning.

The old woman tucked a charm into their hand—refusing payment, the first person in weeks to give them anything—and said, "Come to the border market tomorrow. Bring your pen. Someone ought to write down what's coming back before it burns the country down getting here."

The bell kept ringing. The crowd kept remembering. And Paloma, oath-broken, barred from salvation, paid out in pieces to strangers, followed the sound toward whatever justice looks like when it arrives outside any law at all.
