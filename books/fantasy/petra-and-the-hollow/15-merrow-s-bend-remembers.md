## 15. Merrow's Bend Remembers

The first one was a baker in Corvane named Odile Fenn, who dropped a tray of morning buns because she suddenly remembered a sister she had never had.

She stood in the flour-dusted doorway of her shop with her hands still shaped around the absent tray and wept without knowing why, the way you cry at a funeral for someone whose name you can't quite find. Her sister. A girl with a gap between her teeth who used to steal the heel ends of the loaf. A village called Merrow's Bend, salt marshes, a chapel with a crooked bell. Odile had never been to Merrow's Bend. She was quite sure of that. She was equally sure of the gap-toothed grin, the smell of the marsh at low tide, the sound of the bell the day it stopped ringing forever.

Her husband found her like that and asked what was wrong, and she said, "I don't know, but I know it happened," which is either madness or memory, depending on how many other people are saying it.

By noon, four more people in Corvane were saying it.

* * *

In the fishing town of Brackwater, old Tam Voss — who had spent sixty years insisting he'd been born on the water, born to nets, born to nothing else — woke from an afternoon nap remembering a farmhouse. An orchard. A mother who sang off-key and a father who mended fences and called him *little sprout*, which no fisherman would ever call anyone. He sat up in his cot, drenched in sweat, and said aloud to his empty room: "I had a grandmother. I had a *grandmother*."

His granddaughter thought he'd gone soft in the head until he described the grandmother's house so precisely — the blue door, the chipped step, the row of clay charms hung above the lintel against bad weather — that when she went looking, following his directions down roads she'd never noticed, she found the foundation stones. Just the stones. No door, no step, no lintel. But the ground remembered the shape of them, the way ground does, and Tam's granddaughter knelt there in the weeds with her hand flat on a stone that should not exist and felt something in her chest come loose.

That evening she told her neighbors. The neighbors told theirs. By the third night, half of Brackwater was dreaming of villages they'd never seen, and by the fifth night they were comparing dreams in the tavern like trading cards, arguing cheerfully over details — *no, the mill was on the east side* — the way families argue, which is to say fiercely and with love.

The usurper's tax assessor tried to break up one such gathering and was gently but firmly handed a mug of cider and told to sit down, because his great-grandmother had lived in a place called Hollowmere and someone was about to tell him what had happened to it.

He sat. He drank the cider. He listened. Then he went home and wrote a letter of resignation that his superiors never received, because he burned it and joined the crowd instead.

* * *

It moved like weather after that. Like fire through dry grass, if fire could be kind and terrible at once.

A washerwoman in Saltcross remembered the name of a road that appeared on no map and walked it anyway, and found forty people already standing on it, all of them drawn by the same ache. A schoolteacher in Corvane began keeping a list — names of erased places, offered by children mostly, who recalled things adults had trained themselves not to. The children remembered easily. Children had never learned that some doors are supposed to stay shut.

And everywhere, underneath the names and the places and the recovered dead, ran the same dark thread: soldiers in grey-and-gold. A founding crime. A throne built on a massacre that had been scrubbed so clean even the blood had agreed to forget itself.

They remembered the burning of Aldenmoor. They remembered the Sack of the Nine Villages. They remembered the young general — not yet the Usurper, just a hungry man with a good smile — kneeling in a temple and swearing before witnesses that the land would be *cleansed of false gods*, and understanding, in the way crowds understand things, that "false gods" meant anyone who prayed differently than he did.

Justice arrived outside any court, because courts require a country, and the free cities' courts had been dissolved by treaty twenty years ago. What arrived instead was simpler and older: people in the streets, holding candles, holding each other, holding signs painted by hand — *WE REMEMBER MERROW'S BEND* — and refusing, with the particular stubbornness of ordinary folk, to go home.

The usurper's garrisons were ordered to disperse them. Some soldiers did as ordered. Many did not, because the soldiers were remembering too — their own grandmothers, their own blue doors — and a man cannot beat a crowd for grieving his own family. One garrison captain in Saltcross read the riot order, looked at the faces below his balcony, and instead announced the names of the Nine Villages, one by one, in a voice that carried three streets. The crowd answered each name back like a call-and-response hymn. Nobody rioted. It was worse than a riot. It was a reckoning, and reckonings do not burn out by morning.

Though some of it did burn, of course. The customs house in Brackwater, where the erasure orders had been filed, went up in flames that glowed a strange, satisfied gold. The statue of the Usurper in Corvane's trade square did not fall so much as get *edited* — by dawn its head wore a child's clay charm, hung there by person or persons unknown, and no authority dared remove it, because everyone who looked at it could suddenly remember exactly why it was there.

* * *

Sera Halvard watched it happen from the window of the Wayhouse of the Broken Compass, and understood, with the cold clarity of her profession, that she was watching the end of two things at once: the Usurper's founding lie, and any chance of containing this quietly.

She had come to Corvane under a truce banner to collect records. Records could be confiscated. Records could be burned. But this — this was not paper. This was *people*, thousands of them, walking around with contraband history in their skulls, and there was no inquisitorial method yet devised for searching a mind that refused to be emptied.

"You're doing the thing," said Brother Anselm, the priest of three dead gods, setting a cup of tea beside her elbow. He had adopted her sometime in the last two days, in the way of men who have buried three deities and therefore fear very little. "The staring-at-the-window-like-it-owes-you-money thing."

"I'm assessing the situation."

"You're watching a city remember itself and wondering whether to report it." He sipped his own tea. "I'll save you the trouble. Report it. They'll send more soldiers. The soldiers will remember their grandmothers. Repeat until the army dissolves into the world's largest family reunion."

"That is not how armies work."

"It is how *these* armies work," Anselm said. "Yours are conscripts, Halvard. Half of them are from towns that got erased-adjacent. You can't order a man to suppress a memory he's currently having."

Sera didn't answer. Below, in the street, a procession was passing — hundreds strong, candles in jars, singing something wordless and low. At its center walked an old woman selling charms from a tray, though nobody was buying; they were only touching her sleeve as she passed, the way pilgrims touch a relic. Merrow's Bend's survivor. Paloma's work. Sera knew it the way she knew her own heartbeat, which was to say painfully and without permission.

Paloma had done this. Given one woman back her village, and the village had caught, the way true things catch.

"She'll want to stop it," Sera said quietly.

Anselm raised an eyebrow. "Stop people remembering?"

"Contain it. Control it. Truth is a weapon and my former— " she caught herself, "—my former colleague knows better than anyone that weapons need aiming. If the Usurper's throne falls to a mob, the mob will want a new god by winter. And I know exactly which god is applying for the position."

The priest was quiet for a moment. Somewhere below, the singing swelled, and a child's voice rose clear over it, reciting the names of the Nine Villages in perfect order, learned by heart in a single afternoon.

"You think the silent one is waking," Anselm said. Not a question. He was a priest of three dead gods; he could hear the fourth one knocking.

"I think," Sera said, "that every prayer being answered right now is being answered by something, and I have read enough ledgers to know that nothing answers for free." She turned from the window. "I also think Paloma Eriksen is about to make a choice that will decide which side of this history we all end up on, and I intend to be standing next to them when they make it. Even if they hate me for it. *Especially* if they hate me for it."

"Romantic," said Anselm. "Deeply unwise, but romantic."

"The two are rarely separate."

Below, the procession reached the trade square and stopped beneath the defaced statue, and the old woman with the charm tray lifted her voice — thin, cracked, absolutely steady — and gave the crowd back its first full sentence in twenty years:

"They told us it never happened. *It happened.*"

The sound that came off the square was not a cheer. It was deeper than cheering — a long exhale from a country that had been holding its breath since the conquest, and Sera felt it pass through the floorboards, through her ribs, through the careful walls of everything she had built her life out of.

Then the bells of Corvane began to ring — all of them, unsanctioned, unpermitted, including the crooked-bell pattern that belonged to a chapel that officially did not exist — and Sera Halvard, Inquisitor of the Usurper's Throne, traitor-in-waiting, picked up her coat and went to find the one person in the world who could tell her what came next.

She found the guildhouse door open, the hall beyond it lit, and Ivetta the guildmaster waiting on the steps with her arms crossed and an expression that suggested the bill for everything had just come due.

"You," Ivetta said. "Good. We need to talk about what your emperor does when he learns his kingdom is remembering."

Sera looked past her, into the warm light of the guildhall, where maps were being unrolled and pages were burning in a brazier, and felt the future tilt beneath her feet like a deck shifting at sea.

"He burns it back," she said. "All of it. And he's coming here first."
