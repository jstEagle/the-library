## 16. Sera's Choice

The order came at dawn, written in the usurper's own hand, which was how Sera Halvard knew it was meant to frighten her.

Inquisitors received orders the way millstones received grain—by the sackful, stamped and countersigned by clerks who had never seen a heretic in their lives. But this one bore the imperial seal unbroken, the black wax still sharp-edged, and beneath it a single line in the Emperor's cramped, patient script:

*Burn the archives of Corvane. Take the Hollow by force. Leave nothing for the god to remember us by.*

She read it three times in her quarters above the garrison chapel, standing because sitting felt too much like agreement. Outside her window, Corvane was waking up the way it always did: bakers arguing with their ovens, a fishwife singing off-key to her mule, two children racing a hoop down the Salt Road while their mother shouted after them about mud. A city that did not yet know it had been sentenced.

*Leave nothing for the god to remember us by.*

As though memory were a warehouse you could put to the torch. As though Vess had ever needed paper.

Sera set the letter on the desk and looked at it the way she had once looked at suspects—patiently, waiting for it to confess what it really was. It was not an order, she decided. It was a test. The Emperor had heard the rumors out of the free cities: prayers answered, names returned, a door breathing open in some unmapped valley. He had heard that his own Inquisitor Halvard had spent three weeks in Corvane and come back with nothing but a sunburn and a reputation for drinking with booksellers. And so he had sent her this—not because he wanted the archives burned by her specifically, but because he wanted to see what she would do when handed the match.

Loyal officers burned cities. Loyal officers marched on valleys full of weeping refugees and called it sanitation.

She had been a loyal officer for eleven years. She had been good at it, the way a knife is good at cutting—without opinion, without appetite, without asking the bread whether it minded.

And then Paloma Eriksen had looked up from a ledger in a guildhall smelling of ink and old tea, and said her name like it was a question they'd been carrying for a decade, and every clean edge she owned had gone dull at once.

* * *

The archives of Corvane occupied a former granary near the river, three floors of shelving that smelled of dust and lamp oil and the particular sweetness of paper that had been loved too long. Sera had visited twice during her official inquiry, ostensibly cataloguing pre-conquest religious materials. In truth she had spent those visits reading love letters.

Not hers. Corvane's. The city wrote to itself, century after century—petitions, complaints, marriage contracts, recipes, a truly alarming quantity of poetry addressed to a woman named Bess who appeared to have broken hearts across four generations. The archivists knew every shelf by nickname. There was the Widow's Row, where women who outlived their husbands recorded everything from grief to grievances; there was the Flood Shelf, salvaged from a drowned parish, its documents warped into little waves as if the water had tried to keep them.

The head archivist was a small round man named Odo who wore his spectacles pushed up into his hair like a second pair of eyes and greeted Sera, on her first visit, with "You have the look of someone about to ask me an uncomfortable question. May I offer you tea first? Uncomfortable questions go down easier with honey."

She had liked him immediately, which should have been her first warning.

Now she stood in his doorway at midmorning with the Emperor's letter folded against her ribs like a knife she hadn't drawn yet, and Odo looked up from his cataloguing and said, "Ah. Inquisitor. You have the look again."

"Odo."

"That's worse than usual," he said, setting down his pen. "Tea?"

"No."

"Honey?"

"Odo." Her voice came out steadier than she deserved. "If I asked you how long it would take to evacuate these shelves—if every document, every scrap—how long?"

He took his spectacles down from his hair and polished them on his sleeve, which he did when he was buying time, and she realized he already knew. Of course he knew. Archivists always knew; it was the whole profession. Somewhere in those shelves there was probably a file on her, cross-referenced under *Imperial Functionaries, Suspicious Behavior Of.*

"You're asking me," he said carefully, "as an officer of the conquest."

"I'm asking you as—" She stopped. Started again. "I'm asking."

Odo put his spectacles back on and looked at her through them, and whatever he saw made him nod slowly, the way men nod at weather they've been expecting all week.

"With carts and willing hands? Two days. Without carts?" He glanced at the stairs, at the city beyond, at the rumor of war hanging over it like smoke. "We'd carry it out ourselves, book by book, if we had to. We've done it before, you know. When the old temple fell, the brothers saved the psalter pages one at a time in their robes. Took them a year. Half of them were caught." He smiled faintly. "The other half are filed under Martyrs, Alphabetical."

"Don't be a martyr, Odo."

"I've no talent for it. Too fond of lunch." He rose, brushing biscuit crumbs from his robe, and for a moment the humor dropped away and she saw the man underneath—the keeper, the one whose entire life was the stubborn belief that paper could outlive fire. "Inquisitor Halvard. Why are you asking me this?"

Because last night, she almost said, I dreamed of a village called Merrow's Bend that I have never visited, and I woke up knowing the name of its well. Because the god my emperor fears is returning memories to people like a thief returning stolen goods, and every returned memory is an indictment with his seal on it. Because a mercenary with ink-stained fingers is holding a door open at the edge of the world, and I have spent eleven years pretending I don't know exactly why they're doing it.

"Because," she said, "I need to know what I'm burning."

* * *

She went to the garrison commander next, because treason, done properly, begins with paperwork.

Commander Brase was a solid, unimaginative man who had risen through the ranks by never once having an idea, and Sera had always found him restful. She laid the Emperor's letter on his table and watched him read it, watched his jaw work through the words *take the Hollow by force*, and waited.

"They want us to march on a valley," he said finally, "that isn't on any map."

"The cartographers will find it."

"The cartographers say it *moves*." He rubbed his face. "Halvard, I've got six hundred men, half of them conscripts who signed on for bread, and you're telling me to assault a doorway guarded by—what, exactly? Refugees? Guildsmen? A god?"

"A god," Sera said. "A very hungry one."

"And the archives?"

"Burned before we march. That part's mine."

Brase looked at the letter again, then at her, and she saw the thought move behind his eyes like a fish under ice: *she has been strange since Corvane.* "You'll want a detail for the fires."

"I'll handle it personally."

He nodded, relieved to have the problem shrink back down to something with a procedure. "Good. Report when it's done." Then, because he was not entirely without imagination, he added quietly: "Halvard. Whatever's eating you—it'll keep till after the campaign. See to your duty first."

"Yes, Commander," she said, and meant none of it, and hated how easily the lie fit, like a glove worn soft with use.

* * *

That night she sat in her quarters with the invasion plans spread across her bed, because her desk had begun to feel like an altar and she was no longer sure to whom.

Six hundred men from the Corvane garrison. Two thousand more coming up the Salt Road within the fortnight, with siege engines disassembled into wagons. Cavalry to hold the valley mouth. Orders to take the threshold intact—the Emperor wanted the door, wanted whatever country lay beyond it, wanted a god in a cage more than he wanted a victory—and orders, in a separate sealed annex she had opened with a hot knife and a steady hand, for what happened to the refugees afterward. The annex used the word *relocate*. The annex always used that word. It was the favorite word of men who had never relocated anywhere except other people.

Sera read until the candles guttered, memorizing what she intended to forget deliberately later, and somewhere past midnight she caught herself doing arithmetic she hadn't authorized: two thousand soldiers, six hundred here, how many would it take to hold the Salt Road if the road suddenly developed, say, a collapsed bridge, a burned supply train, a case of thoroughly forged marching orders—

She laughed, alone in the dark, and it came out slightly unhinged, and she thought: *this is what Paloma must feel like all the time. This is what it costs to keep two ledgers.*

Then she thought of Paloma's face when they'd told her about Merrow's Bend—not the confession, that had been earlier, terrible and necessary—but the moment after, when Sera had not flinched, and Paloma had looked at her like a man looking at dry land from deep water. They had chosen the door over the god. They had chosen strangers over their own soul, and paid for it in pieces of themselves, and were paying still.

And she had gone back to her post. Had written her reports. Had worn the glove.

No longer.

* * *

Getting the plans out of the garrison was almost insultingly easy, which told her something bleak about empires: they trusted their own so completely that they never imagined betrayal might arrive wearing the uniform and carrying the correct countersigns. She walked out the gate at dawn with a satchel of maps and annexes under her cloak, nodded to the sentry—who saluted her, gods help him—and turned toward the guildhall with her heart going like a drummer boy's.

The Guild of Impossible Things kept its doors locked these days, but the lock knew her now. She knocked the pattern Ivetta had grudgingly taught her—three, pause, two, pause, one, "and stop dawdling"—and the door opened onto the dim hall where the partners held their councils.

Ivetta was waiting, which meant word had traveled ahead of her, which meant the thief of names had eyes everywhere or the priest of three dead gods had dreamed her arrival, and Sera had stopped asking which miracles belonged to whom.

"You look terrible," Ivetta said. She was a broad woman with silver in her braids and the expression of someone who had audited better liars. "Sit down. Tea?"

"Do you people put honey in everything?"

"It's a free city. We can afford kindness." Ivetta poured two cups anyway, and Sera realized her hands were shaking only when she wrapped them around the warmth. "Well, Inquisitor. You've been walking past our door for three days looking like a woman rehearsing a speech. Say it."

Sera set the satchel on the table between them.

Inside were the invasion plans. The troop counts. The route of the Salt Road column. The cavalry assignments. The sealed annex, its cut wax still bearing the scar of her knife. Everything the Emperor intended for the Hollow, for the refugees, for the door and everyone standing in it.

Ivetta looked at the satchel for a long moment. Then at Sera. Then back at the satchel, with the particular reverence of a merchant appraising an item she had never expected to see in stock.

"Do you know what this is worth?" she said quietly.

"Treason," Sera said. "It's worth treason. I've checked the price. It's everything."

"Mm. And what do you want for it?"

"Nothing." Her voice cracked on the word and she let it. "That's the point. I'm not selling. I'm not trading. I'm not settling a debt, Ivetta—I'm ending one. Eleven years I served a law that burns villages and calls it order. I told myself it was the lesser evil. I told myself someone had to hold the line. And then your people started remembering things, and I found out what the line was *for*, and I—" She stopped. Breathed. "Paloma pays for everyone. One memory at a time. I watched them trade away their own past like coins, and I thought, someone should pay for *them* for once. So. Here I am. Paying."

Ivetta was silent. Around them the guildhall creaked and settled, and somewhere upstairs a clerk dropped something and swore with real artistry, and the ordinary sounds of ordinary people keeping impossible accounts went on.

"You understand," Ivetta said at last, "that I can't protect you. If the Emperor learns—"

"He'll learn. That's rather the design." Sera stood, straightening her uniform out of habit, then stopped, fingers on the buttons. After a moment she unpinned the inquisitor's badge—a small iron sun, the light that sees all things—and set it on the table beside the satchel. It made a small, final sound, like a coin landing.

"There," she said. "Now it's official. I'm nobody. No rank, no warrant, no law." She managed something that was mostly a smile. "Just a woman with stolen paperwork and nowhere to be."

Ivetta studied her, and then, unexpectedly, laughed—a short, rough bark. "Nowhere to be. Halvard, you're standing in the one place in the world where *nowhere* is a growth industry." She slid the satchel toward herself and stood, tucking it under her arm like contraband, which it was. "The cartographer will want the routes tonight. The priest will want to pray over them, ignore him, he prays over everything. And the thief—" her mouth thinned— "the thief owes us a page and a betrayal, and thanks to you, we may yet collect both before the armies do."

"Sera."

They both turned. Paloma stood in the doorway to the back rooms, ink on their fingers as always, a ledger under one arm, looking at her the way they had looked at her across ten years and one burned bridge—as though she were a question they'd stopped expecting answers to.

"You heard," Sera said.

"I hear everything in this building. Occupational hazard." Paloma crossed the hall slowly, and stopped close enough that Sera could see the exhaustion carved under their eyes, the places where traded memories had left gaps like missing teeth in a smile. "You gave up your whole life. Your law, your rank, your—everything. For a door. For strangers."

"For you," Sera said simply. "For the strangers *because* of you. It seemed efficient."

Paloma made a sound that was half laugh, half something else entirely, and reached out and took her hand—ink-stained fingers closing around hers, warm and trembling slightly, the hand of someone who had given away so much and still had this left to give.

"The ledgers say I owe you," Paloma murmured. "Eleven years of interest."

"The ledgers are wrong," Sera said. "This one's free."

Behind them, Ivetta snorted. "Nothing in this guild is free, and I include the tea. Now both of you stop being moving and start being useful—we have an army arriving in twelve days, a door that breathes, and a god on the far side of it getting hungrier by the prayer." She paused at the foot of the stairs, and for just a moment her voice lost its armor. "Halvard. What you did today took more spine than anything the Empire ever asked of you. Remember that, when it comes time to pay."

When Sera looked back at Paloma, they were watching her with an expression she hadn't seen since before the conquest—unguarded, startled, hopeful, as if the world had just rearranged itself around a fact neither of them had dared write down.

"Twelve days," Paloma said softly. "Then the valley. Then the door."

"And Vess," Sera said.

Paloma's grip tightened on her hand.

"And Vess," they agreed. "But this time, we walk in together—and this time, the god answers to *us*."
