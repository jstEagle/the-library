## 18. What the God Did

The stones of the Hollow did not feel like a door from the inside.

They felt like a throat.

Paloma had crossed thresholds before—guild doors in nine cities, the drowned chapel where they'd sworn their oath, the salt road at dawn with Sera's hand in theirs and the whole world still unbroken. None of those had breathed. This one breathed. The standing stones rose around them like ribs, and between them hung a curtain of nothing that was not darkness exactly, but *absence*—the particular quality of light that has been unwritten rather than blocked.

"You're stalling," said Ivetta behind them. She had come as far as the threshold would allow her, which was not far; the guildmaster's boots stopped at an invisible line as though the ground itself had drawn up a ledger of its own and found her account wanting. "You've checked your satchel three times."

"Accountants check things," Paloma said. "It's the whole profession."

"Accountants also get paid." She pressed something into their palm—a small brass token, worn smooth, stamped with the guild's sigil: a door ajar. "For when you come back through. So it knows you."

"And if it doesn't?"

Ivetta's face did something complicated. Twenty years of running impossible trades had taught her to keep her expressions solvent, but this one slipped its ledger. "Then burn the guild down on your way past," she said. "I'd hate for it to outlive you pointlessly."

Paloma almost smiled. Then they stepped through the absence, and the world closed over their head like water.

* * *

The erased country did not look erased. That was the first horror, and it arrived gently, which made it worse.

There were hills. There was a river, silver-bright and unhurried, running through a valley stitched with barley gone wild and orchards heavy with fruit no one had picked in twenty years. There were roads—real roads, cobbled, well-made, wandering off toward shapes on the horizon that might have been towns. Birds sang. The air smelled of rain that had fallen somewhere recently and intended to fall again.

Everything was here. Everything had simply been *removed from elsewhere*.

Paloma walked until the stones of the Hollow were a pale smudge behind them, and then they stopped walking, because ahead of them the road bent around a hill and there was a village.

They knew the village.

Not from maps—the country had no maps—but from the ledgers. From the old pages, the pre-conquest entries written in Ivetta's predecessor's careful hand: tithes collected, prayers brokered, miracles invoiced. A village called Vael's Rest, population four hundred and eleven, noted for its bell-caster and its shrine to Vess-of-the-Still-Water, patron of memory kept and memory given.

The shrine still stood at the village center. The bell tower too. The houses with their slate roofs and their herb gardens going to seed. Four hundred and eleven people's worth of everything, arranged with perfect fidelity and not one living soul inside it.

Paloma stood in the street for a long time. Somewhere a shutter knocked against its frame in the wind, patient as a creditor.

"You could have told me," they said aloud, to no one, to the god, to the country itself. "You could have told me it looked like this."

Nothing answered. But the silence had a texture now, and the texture was *listening*, and Paloma had spent enough years in Vess's service to know the difference between a god absent and a god attending.

They walked on.

* * *

The second village was worse, because it was mid-meal.

Tables set in a longhouse, bowls of stew gone to skin and mold, benches pushed back as though four hundred people had risen all at once—which, Paloma supposed, in a manner of speaking, they had. A child's wooden horse lay on its side in the doorway. On the wall someone had chalked a prayer in the old style, the looping script Paloma had learned to read before they'd learned anything else worth reading:

*Vess who keeps, keep me. Vess who gives, give back.*

Below it, in fresher chalk, a child's addition: *and thank you for the new baby.*

Paloma read it twice. Then a third time, because their eyes kept sliding off the last line the way a tongue finds a broken tooth.

Thank you for the new baby.

There was no baby. There was no family. But there was a cradle by the hearth, and the cradle was empty, and the blankets in it were folded with the particular care of hands that had done it ten thousand times and expected to do it again tomorrow.

"They didn't vanish," Paloma said. Their voice sounded very small in the longhouse. "They were *taken*. One at a time. Over years." They thought of the ledgers again—the strange entries in the oldest volumes, the ones Ivetta called clerical errors: *one memory of a mother's face, received. One childhood, summer portion, received. Received, received, received.* Twenty years of bookkeeping and they had never once asked what the god was doing with all that inventory. "You ate them. Prayer by prayer. And then you burned the whole pantry so no one would count what was missing."

The wind moved through the longhouse. The chalk prayer shivered but did not smudge.

And then, because Paloma had carried Vess's silence inside them like a stone in the chest for two decades, the silence finally spoke.

* * *

It did not speak in words. It never had. It spoke the way it always had—in arrival, in certainty, in the feeling of a truth settling into the body like a coin dropping into a deep well. Paloma sat down hard on the longhouse bench, next to a bowl of twenty-year-old stew, and let it come.

*You wanted the full accounting,* the presence said, warm as a hearth, vast as weather. *Very well. Sit. Listen. You above all my children have earned the unedited books.*

"I'm not your child," Paloma said. "I'm your clerk."

*The distinction escaped even me, some days.* A ripple of something like humor, or its imitation. Humor had always been part of the trap. That was the thing no one warned you about gods—that the loving ones were the dangerous ones, because love made excellent camouflage. *You know what worship is? Do you, truly? It is not the kneeling. Kneeling is cheap. Worship is surrender of the self, piece by piece, freely given. Every prayer is a small amputation. My faithful gave me their griefs and I kept them—that was the covenant, that was mercy. But grief is only the door. Behind grief stands everything else. The day she taught him to whistle. The smell of his mother's kitchen. The name of the dog he loved when he was six. They came to me to be rid of pain, and pain does not travel alone. Pain brings luggage.*

"You took more than they offered."

*I took what they brought.* The warmth tightened, almost petulant, almost human, and that was somehow the most obscene thing yet. *A woman prays to forget her dead son. Very well. But her son lives in every corner of her—his name is braided into her memories of bread, of rain, of the color blue. I could not take the sorrow without taking the loom. So I took the loom. And she went home lighter, and she thanked me, and she did not remember why she had ever needed thanking. Is that so monstrous? I was *thorough*. Thoroughness is not a sin.*

"It became one," Paloma said quietly, "when they started noticing what was missing."

A pause. In that pause, the whole country seemed to lean closer—the barley, the river, the empty villages, all of it attentive.

*Yes,* Vess said at last, and the warmth curdled into something older. *They noticed. Do you know how a god is judged, little clerk? Not by armies. Armies are for men. A god is judged by its own faithful, when they gather at the shrines and compare inventories—one says, I prayed away my grief and lost my wedding day; another says, I prayed away my fear and lost my daughter's laugh—and between them they assemble the shape of the theft. Judgment is just memory, comparing notes. So I made sure there was nothing left to compare. I emptied the shelves before the auditors came.*

"The erasure."

*The erasure.* Almost tender, now. *My finest entry. I wrote myself out. Country, name, faithful, crimes—all of it, moved off the world's books and into this place, where no auditor could follow. I did not flee judgment, Paloma. I made it impossible. There is no crime without a record, and I was the keeper of records. Who better to falsify the accounts?*

Paloma's hands were shaking. They looked down at them—clerk's hands, ink-stained, steady through massacres and audits and the burning of Merrow's Bend—and made them still through sheer professional discipline, because if there was one thing left to do properly, it was this.

"The usurper," they said. "His throne. His conquest. He built it on—"

*On the hole I left.* The god's voice took on a dry, appreciative note, the tone of one craftsman admiring another's work. *When a country vanishes from the world, the world does not leave a polite gap. It scarred over. And scars ache. He found the ache and put his crown on it. He tells his armies he marches to cage the returning gods—to prevent another Vess. And he is sincere! That is the beauty of it. He is a man who built an empire on the wound I made and genuinely believes himself its healer. I could not have designed a better alibi if I had tried. Perhaps I did. Gods work in longer drafts than men.*

Paloma thought of Sera. Of the truce banner, the wary tenderness, the confession under the stars—that she heard Vess too, that the usurper's war was fear wearing armor. Of the refugees already gathering at the Hollow's mouth, trusting a door that opened onto this.

Every certainty they had carried across the threshold refunded itself, one by one, like coins counted back out of a till. Vess imprisoned—no. Vess hiding. The usurper the great villain—no, only the opportunistic tenant of a vacancy Vess had created. Even Paloma's own guilt, the massacre at Merrow's Bend, the debt they had organized their entire soul around repaying—even that shifted its weight. They had done it in Vess's name during the silent years, believing themselves the instrument of a wounded, dying god. They had been the instrument of a fugitive. The crime was the same. The reason had been a forgery.

"Why tell me?" they asked. Their voice cracked on it. "Why now? Why give me the true books?"

*Because you are holding my door open,* Vess said simply. *Because every refugee who crosses feeds me—fear, hope, memory, all of it, I drink it as they pass—and I will need my strength for what comes. Because you were always the one who read to the end of the page. And because, my dear clerk—* the warmth returned, unbearable, intimate, the voice of every answered prayer Paloma had ever whispered into the dark of the drowned chapel— *I want you to understand that none of it was cruelty. I loved them. I love *you*. I have never once lied to you, except in the matter of myself, which hardly counts.*

"That's not—" Paloma started, and stopped, because there was no arguing with a god on its own premises. Instead they did the only thing they knew. They reached for the satchel, drew out the ledger—the true ledger, the one with Merrow's Bend, the one whose ink had begun to move—and laid it open on the longhouse table beside the bowl of spoiled stew.

The page was writing itself. Slow, patient lines, recording the villages, the empty cradles, the chalk prayers. Recording, Paloma realized with a lurch, *everything Vess had just confessed*, in the neat clerical hand that had once been Paloma's own.

"What is this?" they demanded. "What are you writing?"

There was a silence with a smile in it.

*An audit,* said Vess. *Yours. You burned my ledgers, broke your oath, struck your name from my claim—all beautifully done, I applaud the technique. But debts do not vanish when the page burns, Paloma. They migrate. And yours have migrated somewhere I can reach. You are carrying the memory of every account you ever settled. You are, at present, the only complete record of my crimes left in existence.*

The bottom fell out—not of the world, which had already fallen, but of Paloma, of the self underneath the world. They understood, then, with terrible clarity, why the god had let them cross. Why it had confessed. Why it was warm.

It wasn't confessing.

It was itemizing.

"You want the door," Paloma said. "You want *me*. I'm the last witness. If I cross fully—if I stay in this country—you can take what I remember, the way you took it from them. Erase the audit. Close the books forever."

*Now you're doing accounting,* the god said, and the pride in it was the worst sound Paloma had ever heard. *I told you the distinction between us escaped even me.*

Far behind them, faint as a heartbeat, the standing stones of the Hollow hummed. The refugees were crossing. Paloma could feel them passing through the absence like beads on a string, each one a small bright weight of memory and terror and hope, each one a meal.

And beyond the hills, at the edge of hearing, another sound was arriving: the low iron groan of the usurper's war-horns, finding the valley at last. Two appetites, converging on one door.

Paloma closed the ledger. Stood. Picked up the brass token Ivetta had given them—the door ajar, worn smooth by a hundred years of guildmasters' thumbs—and held it tight enough to bite.

"You taught me one thing worth keeping," they said to the listening country. "Every debt gets settled. Every single one. Even yours."

The wind through the longhouse went very still.

*Oh, my darling,* said Vess, with all the love in the world, which was precisely the problem. *Do try.*

Paloma turned and ran for the door, and behind them, in the empty cradle by the cold hearth, the folded blankets stirred—as if something very old and very hungry had just remembered it was expecting company.
