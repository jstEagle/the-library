## 19. The Last Entry

The guildhall of Corvane had never been quiet, not once in the nineteen years Paloma had kept its ledgers. Even at three in the morning there was always something: Ivetta's boots on the stairs, the cartographer muttering to a map that muttered back, the thief of names—before his defection—whistling through the gap in his teeth while he counted other people's lives into little boxes.

Now it was quiet in the way a held breath is quiet.

Paloma sat at the long counting table with every ledger the guild owned stacked around them like a fortress wall. Forty-one volumes. Some were older than the conquest, older than the free cities themselves, their spines cracked and their pages soft as cloth. The ink on some of them was still moving. That was the part Paloma tried not to look at directly, the way you try not to look at a wound on your own hand.

They dipped the pen. They did not write yet. There was an order to these things, and if nothing else in Paloma's life had survived intact—their god, their country, their name, their eyesight on the left side, which Vess had taken as a parting gift when they refused Him—then the order would survive. Debts first. Then transfers. Then the entry itself, last, because a final entry written before its time was just a lie with good penmanship.

* * *

The first ledger was the small one, the one Ivetta called the Kindness Book, though she'd deny it under torture. It held the debts nobody could pay in coin: the widow who owed the guild for the winter her son didn't starve; the ferryman who'd been given a second chance at a river he'd once failed; the child in Saltrow whose fever the guild's priest-of-three-dead-gods had argued out of her body, and who now owed a favor no one had ever named.

Paloma went down the column, line by line, and wrote beside each one:

*Transferred. — P.E.*

It took four hours. Their hand cramped twice. Once, on the widow's page, they stopped and sat with the pen hovering, because the widow was Maren Ostrey, and Maren had once smuggled Paloma a heel of bread and a flask of plum brandy through a checkpoint during the worst winter of the occupation, and had refused payment with a laugh that said *you'll owe me forever, mercenary, and I intend to collect.*

She was dead now. The debt wasn't. That was the thing about debts that people who hated accounting never understood: a debt was not a cruelty. A debt was proof that someone had once decided you were worth saving. To erase it was to erase the saving.

So Paloma transferred it. All forty-one volumes. Every impossible trade, every borrowed luck, every bottled grief, every year of somebody else's childhood bought back at scandalous markup. Transferred to the account of Paloma Eriksen, keeper of ledgers, oath-broken, half-blind, formerly beloved of a god.

By dawn the stack of ledgers looked exactly as it had before, which felt right. Absolution should not change how things look. Only what they weigh.

* * *

Ivetta found them there when the light came up gray through the shutters. She stood in the doorway of the counting room for a long moment, arms crossed, wearing the expression she wore when a client tried to renegotiate terms—which is to say, the expression of a woman calculating exactly how much violence the situation justified.

"You haven't slept," she said.

"I slept in Merrow's Bend," Paloma said. "I slept in the erased country. I slept in a doorway in the Hollow while a god breathed on the other side of the stones. I'm very well rested, comparatively."

"Don't be clever with me, Eriksen." But her voice caught on the name, and Paloma knew why. *Eriksen* was the true name they'd given to the thief of names, the payment that had unhooked Vess's claim from them—and then, when the truth came out about what Vess had done, the thief had given it back. "You can't have it both ways," he'd said, pressing the scrap of paper into their palm like a man returning a knife. "You can't sell yourself to save everyone unless you're still somebody worth selling." Gentle humor, from a man who stole names for a living. Paloma missed him the way you miss a tooth.

Ivetta crossed the room and put her hand flat on the top ledger. "These are guild property."

"They were."

"They are. And you are barred. I said the words myself, in front of witnesses, because you broke your oath to a god in front of a valley full of refugees, and oaths are the only currency this guild has ever honored." Her jaw worked. "You made me say it. You knew I'd have to say it, and you made me anyway, so that when the armies came there'd be no question of the guild harboring an oath-breaker. You planned your own excommunication like a market transaction."

"It was a market transaction," Paloma said. "I purchased your deniability. Fair rate."

"Fair—" Ivetta laughed, one sharp note with nothing in it, and pulled out the chair across the table and sat down heavily, which she almost never did, because sitting down in front of a subordinate was surrender and Ivetta had not surrendered to anything since the conquest. "Forty-one volumes," she said quietly. "Every account. Transferred to yourself. Do you know what that means? Do you have any idea what you've just done?"

"I've done the arithmetic."

"The arithmetic." She pressed two fingers to the bridge of her nose. "The debt of Merrow's Bend alone. Four hundred and six souls, and the memory of a village, and twenty years of interest compounding in moving ink. The debt of the fisherman's neighbors. The debt of the girl in Saltrow whose mother came back wrong. And now—" her voice dropped, and Paloma watched her decide whether to say it, and watched her lose that argument with herself, "—now the debt of a god. You've written yourself liable for Vess. For whatever He's owed. For whatever He takes."

"He's owed everything," Paloma said. "He told me so Himself, warm as a hearthfire. Everything His worshippers ever gave Him, and everything they would have given, and the memories He ate to make Himself strong enough to vanish. That's the bill. Someone has to hold it."

"And you think holding it means paying it?"

"I think," Paloma said, "that a debt held by no one is a debt that collects itself. That's what happened to the usurper's throne. That's what happened to the erased country. Nobody held the accounts, so the accounts reached out and held everybody." They turned the top ledger toward her, open to the last blank page. "A debt held by one person, willingly, with witnesses—that's just a promise. And promises can be broken, or paid, or forgiven. It's the only form absolution comes in, Ivetta. I've checked every other shelf."

Ivetta stared at them for a long time. Outside, faintly, came the sound Paloma had been hearing all night underneath everything else: the far-off grind of the usurper's siege engines being turned toward the outer city walls, patient as tide.

"You're going to burn them," Ivetta said at last. Not a question. "After the transfer. You're going to burn forty-one volumes of guild history."

"After the refugees are through. After the door holds. Yes."

"That's—" She stopped. Started again. "My mother helped found this guild. In a cellar, with three other smugglers and a stolen scale. These books are the only proof any of it happened."

"No," Paloma said gently. "You're the proof it happened. The books are just the receipts." They slid the pen across the table. "One more signature, guildmaster. Then I'll get out of your hall and stop being your problem."

Ivetta looked at the pen for a long moment. Then she laughed again—a different laugh this time, wetter, angrier at itself—and picked up the pen, and instead of signing anywhere sensible she leaned over the ledger and wrote, beneath Paloma's transfer entries, in her own fierce hand:

*Account reopened. Keeper reinstated. Effective immediately, retroactive to birth. — I.V., Guildmaster*

"There," she said, blinking hard and daring Paloma to comment. "Now you're not an oath-breaker settling scores on your own recognizance. Now you're guild business. And the guild does not abandon its own accounts." She slammed the ledger shut. "Even the ones that insist on being martyrs."

* * *

They spent the rest of the day doing what the guild did best: impossible things, quickly.

The outer city had fallen at noon—not stormed but simply swallowed, the way the usurper's army took things, in silence and in order, street by street, until the bells stopped ringing one district at a time like a song running out of notes. By late afternoon the inner city knew. You could tell by the sound of it: too many footsteps, all of them going the same direction, toward the valley where the standing stones breathed with the erased country beyond.

Ivetta threw open the guild doors at dusk. Not for business—for refuge. She stood on the steps with the guild's seal held high and shouted herself hoarse telling the fleeing city that anyone with a debt on the books, anyone owed a kindness, anyone who'd ever traded with them honestly or dishonestly, was welcome inside, and that the guild would move them through the Hollow before the armies arrived, and that the fee was nothing, absolutely nothing, and if anyone asked her to repeat that she'd do it louder.

Paloma worked the tables inside. It was strange work and holy work at once: matching faces to entries, finding the widow's granddaughter in the crowd and telling her the account was settled, telling the ferryman's daughter that her father's second chance had been paid forward and she owed nothing, watching people weep over arithmetic. A boy of maybe nine pushed through the press of bodies and demanded to know if the guild had an account for his dog, who had died, and whether dogs could be entered in ledgers, and Paloma—half-blind, exhausted, carrying the liability of a god—crouched down and said, gravely, "What was his name?" and opened a fresh page.

"Biscuit," said the boy.

"*Biscuit,*" Paloma repeated, writing it. "Debt of companionship, outstanding. Transferred to the keeper. He'll be remembered somewhere safe. That's the best any of us get."

The boy considered this with the terrible seriousness of the young, nodded once, and ran off, satisfied. Paloma watched him go and thought: *this is what the god never understood.* Vess had eaten memories because He thought love was a substance, something you could hoard, grow fat on. But it wasn't. It was a ledger. It only meant anything while it moved between hands.

* * *

Near midnight, Sera found them.

Paloma heard her before they saw her—the particular cadence of her boots, the one Paloma had known in another life, before the conquest split them, before the truce banner and the inquisitor's gray coat. She came through the crowded hall like a blade through water, and the crowd let her, because even now, even having delivered the invasion plans to Ivetta and burned her own commission in front of witnesses, Sera Halvard moved like authority, and crowds part for authority the way water parts for a keel.

She set down on the table, without a word, a single sheet of paper. The vanguard's route through the inner city. Timed. Precise. Dated for tomorrow, an hour past dawn.

"They'll reach the valley by midmorning," she said. "He's not sending the whole army. Just enough. He wants the door intact—he wants to go through it himself, take the erased country back, and find whatever's left of his founding crime waiting for him on the other side." Her mouth twisted. "He thinks he can arrest a memory."

"Can he?"

"No. Neither can you, Paloma. Whatever you're planning—I can see you planning, you have the same face you had when you were about to do something unforgivable in service of the forgivable—you can't hold that door against soldiers."

"I'm not going to hold it against soldiers," Paloma said. "I'm going to hold it open for them too, if they come through asking. Everyone gets an entry. That's the whole point."

Sera stared at them. In the lamplight her face did several things at once, most of them old. "You'll burn yourself up," she said finally. "The anchor. The door needs an anchor, and you mean to be it, and anchoring means burning memories, and you've already spent—" her voice frayed, "—you've already spent mine. Your memory of my face. Ivetta told me. You paid a stranger's debt with me."

"I did," Paloma said. "And I'd do it again, and I'm sorry, and those are both true, and neither of us has time to fight about it tonight."

Sera's jaw tightened. Then, unexpectedly, she huffed—a small, reluctant, familiar sound, the ghost of a laugh from a life with no inquisitors in it. "You always were insufferable about being right at the worst possible moment."

"It's in my ledger. Under faults. It's a long entry."

"Is there room in it," Sera said, and her voice went quiet and steady, the way it went when she was about to commit treason or tenderness and saw no difference between them, "for one more name? Written down somewhere safe. So that whatever you burn tomorrow, whatever you spend—one thing stays on the books. One thing the fire can't collect."

Paloma looked at her. Half-blind, they saw her the way you see a candle through fog: bright, blurred, unmistakable.

"Give me a name," Paloma said, and opened the ledger to Biscuit's page, and beneath the dog wrote *Sera Halvard, debt of love, outstanding since before the war, transferred to the keeper, non-negotiable, permanent,* and read it aloud once so it existed in the air and not only in ink, and watched Sera close her eyes like a woman setting down something heavy she'd carried for twenty years.

Then the bells began.

Not the falling-silent bells of the outer city. The alarm bells, all of them at once, ragged and urgent, and beneath them a new sound rolling up from the dark: hooves, many, and the low iron voice of the vanguard's horns. Ahead of schedule. Of course ahead of schedule. Wars, like gods, had no respect for bookkeeping.

Ivetta appeared at the head of the stairs, seal in hand, and called down into the hall in a voice that carried over panic like a keel over water: "Everyone with a settled account, to the valley! The guild honors its debts *tonight!*"

Paloma closed the ledger. Forty-one volumes plus one, every debt in the free cities resting now on a single name, and outside, the armies coming to collect.

"Sera," they said, standing, tucking the ledger inside their coat against their heart like a second heartbeat. "Walk me to the door. I want to write the last entry while there's still ink left."
