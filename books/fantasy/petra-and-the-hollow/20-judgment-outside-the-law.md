## 20. Judgment Outside the Law

The valley had no name, which was fitting, because neither did what came next.

Paloma stood at the threshold of the Hollow—the ring of standing stones breathing like something asleep—and watched two crowds gather on either side of them. On one side, the refugees of the free cities: fishwives and clerks, children clutching charms they'd bought from an old woman who remembered everything now. On the other, coming down the salt road in a glittering line that looked almost beautiful if you didn't know what it was, the usurper's army.

Between them, filling the stones like water filling a cup, Vess.

The god had stopped pretending to be a presence at the edge of things. It stood—there was no other word for it—in the center of the circle, tall as grief, wearing a shape it had borrowed from Paloma's own memory: the drowned chapel's statue, but warm-eyed now, smiling, arms open. It had worn that smile for twenty years of silence, and before that for centuries of worship, and Paloma understood suddenly that the smile was the oldest thing about it. Older than the hunger. Maybe *was* the hunger.

"Little ledger-keeper," Vess said, and its voice was kind, which was the worst thing about it. "You brought me such a crowd."

"I brought you an audience," Paloma said. Their voice came out rough. Half-blind since the excommunication, half-mute until Sera's stubborn nursing had coaxed language back into them like frostbite thawing, they were not made for oratory. But then, this wasn't going to be oratory. It was going to be accounting. "There's a difference. An audience watches. A crowd is owed things."

Behind them, Ivetta stood with the guild's remaining partners, arms crossed, her face carved into the expression she used when a client tried to renegotiate a contract mid-delivery. The cartographer had unrolled his impossible map across a flat stone. The priest of three dead gods was quietly praying to all three, hedging his bets even now, and Paloma found that oddly comforting. Some habits deserved to survive anything.

And beside Paloma, refusing—absolutely refusing—to stand anywhere else, Sera Halvard, former inquisitor, current traitor, her truce banner folded under one arm like a tablecloth after a dinner party that had gone badly.

"You don't have to do this," Sera murmured. "I can arrest it. I still technically have jurisdiction over gods. It's a very old statute."

"That's the funniest thing you've ever said," Paloma said, "and I once heard the name thief try to steal his own name."

"It worked, briefly."

"It did *not* work briefly."

Vess waited through this with divine patience, because patience was free when you were certain. "Paloma," it said gently. "You are tired. You have given so much. Your memories, your sight, your name. Come inside the door with me. Rest. I will carry the rest."

And here was the trap, laid out in the open, because Vess had never needed to hide its traps—it only needed to make them comfortable. Behind the god, past the threshold, the erased country shimmered: green hills that remembered being a nation, rivers that ran with names no treaty could drown. Refugees were already crossing. A child went through laughing, because the door felt like summer to those it welcomed. Every soul that passed fed the god a little. Every prayer whispered by the frightened—*let us get through, let us get through*—poured into it like coin into a purse.

That was the arithmetic Paloma had spent sleepless nights arriving at. The Hollow wasn't just a door. It was a collection plate.

"You want me to come inside," Paloma said. "You want all of us to come inside. And every one of us who does makes you stronger."

"I am a god of memory," Vess said, spreading its borrowed hands. "What else should sustain me?"

"Not memories," Paloma said. "*Stolen* ones. That's the entry we're here to reconcile."

They lifted the ledger.

It was the last one. The others had burned slowly, page by page, each debt settled with a piece of Paloma—the taste of their mother's bread, the color of the sea off Corvane, the sound of rain on the guildhouse roof. They missed these things the way you miss a tooth: constantly, and mostly at night. What remained was one book, one final account, written in their own hand in the last hour before dawn, when Sera had slept against their shoulder and the armies' watchfires had dotted the hills like fallen stars.

"No trial," Paloma said, loud enough to reach the first rows of refugees, who had begun to turn back toward the stones. "No throne. No tribunal of gods, because look around—we couldn't raise a quorum." A few wet, exhausted laughs from the crowd. Good. Fear made people stupid; laughter made them *witnesses*. "Just a reading of accounts. As my oath required. As my oath—" their voice caught, and they pushed through it, "—as my oath *should always have required*."

Vess's smile did not waver, but the light behind its eyes flickered, the way a lamp flickers when someone opens a door somewhere else in the house.

"Entry one," Paloma read. "Merrow's Bend. Two hundred and six souls. Debt incurred: massacre, committed by the undersigned at the god's command. Debt owed: everything."

The words left the page as they spoke them. Not metaphorically—Paloma had learned, in these terrible weeks, that ink in a guild ledger was never merely ink. The names rose off the paper like heat-shimmer, like breath in winter, and drifted out over the crowd.

A woman near the front gasped. She pressed her hands to her mouth. "I had a sister," she said, bewildered. "I've been trying all week to remember why I came here, why I'm running, and—I had a *sister*, she had a laugh like a cart on gravel, and there was a village—" Her eyes went wide and wet and furious all at once. "There was a village called Merrow's Bend."

"Entry two," Paloma said, and kept reading.

This was how it worked; the survivor had taught them. Memory returned to its owner the way water returns to a dry well—not gradually, but all at once, with a sound. Each name read aloud flew home. And Vess starved by exactly that much.

The god took a step forward. The ground did not shake, because Vess was too proud for theatrics, but the standing stones groaned like ship's timbers.

"Paloma." The kindness was gone now, or rather it had turned itself inside out, showing its bones. "You would read *my* worshippers out of *my* keeping? They gave freely. They prayed. Prayer is consent."

"Is it?" Paloma turned a page. "Entry forty-one. A fisherman of Corvane prayed for a full net. You answered by drowning his neighbors, because grief makes rich silt for memory-growing. He didn't consent to that. Entry fifty-three. A child prayed for her mother. You sent her something wearing her mother's face, stitched together from stolen recollections. She was *six*, Vess. Six-year-olds will consent to anything if you smile right. I know. Someone smiled at me for twenty years."

"I loved you."

"I know," Paloma said, and meant it, and hated how much they meant it. "That's what makes it theft instead of robbery. Robbers don't tuck you in first."

They read on. And the crowd began to remember.

It moved through them like fire through a dry field, like the survivor's restored memory had spread through the free cities—person to person, name to name. A ferryman suddenly recalled a festival that had never happened in any city he knew, because its city had been unwritten. An old soldier wept and could not say why, and then could, and the why was a village and a god and a founding crime the usurper's throne was built upon. People turned toward the army on the salt road and shouted the names of dead places at the soldiers, and some of the soldiers—conscripts, farm boys, the usurper's own erased—shouted names back.

Sera stood at Paloma's shoulder, and Paloma heard her whispering along with some entries, because she had helped compile them, sitting up nights with tea and terrible honesty. Once, when Paloma stumbled over a name—a name they'd traded away their own memory of, and could only read because the ledger remembered for them—Sera's hand found theirs and steadied the page.

"Entry one hundred and nine," Paloma read. "The god Vess, debtor. Owed: the childhood of Tomasin Wren, age four years at taking. Owed: the last words of Maren Ostrova, taken before her daughter could hear them. Owed: the smell of the harbor at Vellich, taken entire, from everyone, forever—"

"*Enough.*"

The god's voice cracked across the valley like ice giving way. The stones shuddered. Somewhere in the refugee crowd a horse screamed and was calmed. Vess had grown taller without moving, or the world had grown smaller, and its borrowed face was running like wax, the smile sliding sideways into something older and hungrier and infinitely more tired.

"You cannot starve a god with bookkeeping," Vess said.

"You'd be surprised," said Ivetta, from the stones, in the tone she used for closing a deal. "I've ruined men with less."

But Paloma felt the truth of the god's words in their own hollowed-out head. This was the risk they'd carried up the valley like a stone in their chest. Reading the debts aloud returned the small memories, the human ones. But the deepest hoard—the centuries of worship, whole generations of devotion poured into the god willingly, gladly—that debt was different. Who could take back a lifetime of love?

Vess knew it too. The god straightened, gathering itself, reaching for the vast deep accounts. "Everything they gave me, they gave with open hearts. Read that entry, little keeper. Read the entry where they *loved* me."

Paloma turned to the last page. The final entry. The one they'd written at dawn with Sera's breath warm on their neck.

"I did," Paloma said. "Read it closely."

And they held the ledger up—not toward the god, but toward the crowd—and read their own handwriting aloud:

*"Final entry. All debts herein, including the debt of the god Vess, are transferred to the undersigned: Paloma Eriksen, keeper of ledgers, oath-broken, last creditor of record. The god's accounts are closed. Whatever it holds, it holds as a borrower now. And a borrower," Paloma said, looking up, meeting the god's melting eyes, "can be collected from."*

Silence. The particular silence of several thousand people understanding something simultaneously.

Then the old woman from the border market—the survivor of Merrow's Bend, charms rattling in her pockets—stepped forward. "You mean," she said, in the voice of someone testing a floorboard, "everything it took from us is ours again? All of it? Even the old stuff? Even what our grandmothers gave it?"

"Every entry transfers," Paloma said. "Guild rules. I don't make them. Well." A beat. "Today I do."

The woman turned to the crowd, held up one of her charms—a cheap tin thing, a saint nobody could identify—and said, "Then take it back."

And the crowd, which had crossed a burning country to get here, which had lost villages and languages and the names of their own grandparents to a god's appetite and a tyrant's treaty, reached out with ten thousand invisible hands and *collected*.

It did not look like violence. That was the astonishing thing, the thing Paloma would remember—if they survived to remember anything. It looked like weather changing. It looked like morning. The god Vess, standing in the ring of stones, dimmed the way a bonfire dims when everyone finally picks up their own logs and carries them home. Its borrowed face smoothed into nothing. Its open arms stayed open, embracing a shrinking nothing, and the prayers that had fed it all day reversed course and flowed back to the mouths that had made them, and people staggered and laughed and wept as decades of devotion came home to roost in ordinary mortal chests.

Vess's last act was not rage. Paloma had braced for rage. Instead the god looked at them—at *them*, specifically, out of the last of its gathered light—and said, very quietly, in a voice suddenly no bigger than a person's:

"You were the best of them, little keeper. That was always the problem."

Then it was gone—or nearly gone, a residue, a stain, a cold spot in the air between the stones—and the threshold stood empty for the first time in living memory, breathing softly, open to the unnamed country beyond.

Paloma sat down. Right there, in the grass, ledger in their lap, legs simply declining further service. Sera sat down next to them with the graceless thump of a woman who had been standing at attention for thirty years and had just resigned from attention permanently.

"You did it," she said.

"We did it."

"I arrested no gods today. I want that noted."

"Noted in the margin," Paloma said, and managed a smile that cost them most of what they had left.

But the valley was not done with them. Down the salt road, the usurper's vanguard had stopped—not retreating, not advancing, but *wavering*, because half those soldiers had just remembered villages of their own, and an army that remembers is a very different animal from an army that has forgotten. Horns sounded uncertain orders. And above the stones, the Hollow began to hum—a low, rising note, the sound of a door that had lost its keeper's hand and was starting, slowly, to swing shut.

Ivetta appeared over them, silhouetted against the shimmer, and her face was grim as a winter audit.

"Keeper," she said. "The door needs an anchor. It always did. And you've just transferred the god's claim to yourself—which means, by guild law, by *your* law—" she paused, hating every word, "—the anchor is you."

Paloma looked at the ledger. One blank page left. Then at the refugees still streaming through, hundreds of them, thousands, the free cities emptying themselves into a country with no name. Then at Sera, whose hand had found theirs again and was not letting go.

"How long," Paloma asked quietly, "can I hold it open?"

* * *

The answer, Ivetta said, depended on what they were willing to pay.
