## 21. Holding the Door

The stones were singing out of tune.

Paloma felt it in their teeth before they heard it with their ears—a wrongness in the valley's hum, like a bell struck by a dying man. The Hollow had held for three days and two nights while the free cities emptied through it: farmers with their goats, archivists hauling carts of rescued books, a whole street of candlemakers from Corvane who refused to leave without their molds and had to be carried instead, protesting the entire way.

Now the standing stones were leaning. Not falling—*leaning*, the way a tired person leans against a wall, the way a door leans on a hinge that was never meant for this much use.

"It's the anchor," Ivetta said beside them. Her voice was steady, which meant she'd already done the arithmetic. She always did the arithmetic first; grief came second, filed neatly under later. "Vess was supposed to hold this side. A god's weight on one end, a country's weight on the other." She looked at Paloma, and her eyes did something they almost never did—they apologized in advance. "The god is starving. You saw it happen."

Paloma had seen it. Yesterday, at the threshold, Vess had stood among the stones like a figure made of weather, gathering strength from every prayer that crossed—and then the crowd's memories had come home to them, all at once, read aloud from a ledger in a voice that shook, and Vess had begun to thin. Not die. *Thin.* Like smoke remembering it used to be a fire.

"The door needs an anchor," Ivetta said. "Something with weight. Something with—" she hesitated over the word, hating it, "—*debt*. The Hollow runs on unsettled accounts, Paloma. It always has. That's why only settled debts could open it, and why an unpaid one can hold it shut."

"And you're telling me this now," Paloma said, "because you've already decided whose debt is biggest."

Ivetta didn't answer. That was answer enough.

The last of the refugees were crossing in a long gray ribbon—the old ones first, then the children, then everyone else, sorted by the guild's own merciless kindness. On the far side of the threshold, the erased country breathed. Paloma could smell it even here: rain on stone that had never been rained on, grass growing where no treaty had ever said grass could grow. A country with no name yet. A blank page the size of a nation.

Their ledger hung at their hip, mostly ash-stained now, mostly empty. They had traded away so much of themselves to settle the free cities' accounts—one memory per debt, paid gladly, paid until the paying hurt in ways they couldn't name because they'd forgotten what they'd lost. They knew they had loved someone once. They knew the someone's name was Sera. They could not, no matter how they reached, remember her face.

They remembered that they missed it. Missing without an object. Grief with the address torn off.

"Paloma," Ivetta said quietly. "There's another way. There's always another way. We could close the door early, save the anchor, lose maybe a thousand people still on the roads—"

"No," Paloma said.

"—or we could find another anchor, some other poor soul with debts—"

"*No.*"

"Then say it plainly," Ivetta said, and her voice cracked at last, just once, like ice on a pond. "Say what you're going to do, so I can hate it properly."

Paloma looked at the stones. They looked at the ribbon of people. They took out the ledger and opened it to the last clean page.

"I'm going to make the final entry," they said. "All remaining debts, transferred to me. Including the god's."

* * *

Sera found them at dusk, when the valley had gone violet and the usurper's fires were visible on the salt road—hundreds of them, thousands, a constellation fallen low and marching. She came down the slope at a run, armor half-strapped, helmet under her arm, hair escaping its braid in the way it always had, and Paloma's heart did the thing hearts do when they recognize what the mind has lost: it *ached toward her*, specific and helpless, like a compass needle finding north through a wall.

"You're doing it," she said, skidding to a stop. "You're actually—I can see it in your face. You're taking his place as the anchor."

"Somebody has to hold the door."

"Somebody has to be *stupid*, you mean." She grabbed their shoulder, and her grip was furious and careful at once, the grip of a woman who had spent twenty years learning exactly how much pressure things could take before they broke—including herself. "Paloma. Listen to me. I've seen what anchoring does. I read the cartographer's notes. The door doesn't just take your debt—it takes your *weight*. Everything you are gets poured into the threshold until there's nothing left over to be a person. You'll stand there forever, holding a country open, and there won't be anyone home behind your eyes."

"I know."

"You don't—you can't—"

"Sera." Paloma covered her hand with theirs. Her knuckles were scarred; they knew that much. They had learned her hands again over these weeks, relearning by touch what memory wouldn't give back. "How many people are still on the road?"

She closed her eyes. "Two thousand. Maybe more. The rear guard is burning villages behind them to slow the vanguard. If the door closes tonight—"

"Then the door doesn't close tonight." They said it simply, the way they wrote entries: number, name, amount owed, amount paid. "I burned the ledgers to unbind a god. I can burn myself to bind a door. It's the same arithmetic."

"It is *not* the same arithmetic—"

"It's exactly the same arithmetic, and you know it, because you taught it to me." They smiled, and were surprised to find the smile was real. "You used to say it over your reports. Every choice costs. The only question is who pays. Well." They tapped the ledger. "This entry says I pay."

Sera let go of them. She stepped back. And then, to Paloma's astonishment, she began unbuckling her sword belt.

"What are you doing?"

"What does it look like?" She laid the belt across a stone, then her gauntlets, then the inquisitor's seal she'd already broken weeks ago and kept carrying anyway out of habit. "I'm staying."

"No."

"Yes."

"Sera, the anchor takes everything—"

"Then it takes everything." She said it lightly, terribly lightly, the way people talk when they have made a decision so large that smallness is the only way to carry it. "You're going to stand in a doorway until you dissolve, and you thought I would walk through to the other side and start a new life? Plant a garden? Learn to bake?" Her voice finally broke open. "I did that already, Paloma. After the conquest. I built a whole life on the far side of losing you, and it was a *shallow* life, it was a coat I wore over a hole, and I am not doing it twice. I am not walking through that door knowing you're on this side of it. Refuse the offer. I refuse yours. We're even."

"That's not how debts work."

"It's exactly how debts work. You taught me that too." She wiped her face with the back of her hand, briskly, like closing a ledger. "Besides, someone has to keep the armies off the threshold while you do your dramatic final entry. You can't fight. You're half-blind and you gave away your sword arm's memory of how to swing it—I watched you try, it was like watching a man butter bread with his elbow."

Despite everything, despite the fires on the road and the stones groaning overhead, Paloma laughed. It came out wet. "That bad?"

"Worse. The bread survived. Barely."

* * *

They worked through the night.

The guild's remnants formed a line at the threshold—thieves and archivists and one priest of three dead gods, all of them exhausted, all of them refusing to leave until the last refugee crossed. The thief of names, forgiven and unforgiven in equal measure, moved along the line whispering to frightened children, giving them back the names fear had stolen. The cartographer stood at the stones with her instruments, calling corrections into the dark: *"Hold the western stone! The angle's slipping! Someone lean on the western stone!"*

And Paloma sat cross-legged before the threshold with the ledger open on their knees, making the final entry by candlelight while Sera stood guard above them with a borrowed spear.

*Entry the last,* they wrote. *Let it be short.*

*Debts outstanding: all of them. Debtor: Paloma Eriksen, keeper of ledgers, oath-broken, excommunicated, formerly beloved of a silent god and currently beloved of no one who will admit it.*

*A correction, in Sera's handwriting, added beneath:* *Beloved of one who admits it constantly and is ignored.*

Paloma read that line four times. Then they kept writing, because if they stopped they would stop entirely.

*Terms of settlement: my weight, my name, my remainder. To be paid into the threshold of the Hollow and held there until every soul owed passage has passed. Consider the account closed when the door closes. Consider me—*

They paused. The candle guttered. Somewhere below them in the valley, the vanguard's horns sounded, closer now, hungry.

*Consider me paid in full,* they finished, *and forgive the untidy hand. My teacher always said my penmanship would be the death of me. She was nearly right.*

Sera read it over their shoulder. She was quiet a long moment.

"She?" she said finally.

"Hm?"

"My penmanship. You called me *she*." Her voice had gone strange. Careful. Hopeful in the way hope is dangerous, the way a raised floorboard is dangerous. "You remembered something."

Paloma searched themselves, the way they'd learned to—gently, patiently, feeling along the walls of all the empty rooms inside. "Did I?"

"You did. You said *my teacher*. You said *she*." Sera crouched beside them, and her eyes were bright and fierce and wet. "Paloma. The anchor takes your weight. But you've been trading memories away for weeks, and some of them *came back*. Little ones. Fragments. The priest said it might happen—that debts paid freely return interest. What else comes back? Tell me. Tell me everything."

"A smell," Paloma said slowly. "Rain on hot stone. I think it's from Merrow's Bend, before. I think I smelled it the day we—no. No, I won't keep that one. I'll trade it again." They shook their head. "And a voice saying 'your penmanship will be the death of me.' And—" they frowned, reaching— "*warm bread. Someone stealing the ends of the loaf and blaming the cat. There was no cat. There was never any cat.*"

"There was no cat," Sera whispered. "It was me. It was always me. You bought a loaf every market day and I ate the ends and blamed a cat for six years and you *knew*, you told me you knew, you said—" she laughed, and it turned into something between laughing and crying, "—you said you kept buying them because you liked watching me lie about it."

"I did?" Paloma said. And then, feeling it surface like a fish rising: "I did. Gods. I did. You lied with such *conviction*, Sera. You'd have made a terrible honest woman and a magnificent criminal."

"I became an inquisitor instead. Close enough."

The horns sounded again. Closer. Below them in the dark, torches were spilling into the mouth of the valley like wine from a tipped cup.

* * *

The last refugees crossed at dawn.

Paloma stood at the threshold and poured themselves out.

It did not feel the way they expected. It felt like writing—an entry being made of them, line by line, their weight flowing down through their feet into the stones, which straightened, which sang, which *held*. They felt the two thousand souls pass through them in some sense, or past them, or perhaps through the door they had become; the distinction stopped mattering. They felt the erased country breathing on one side and the burning free cities on the other, and they held both, the way a ledger holds both columns, loss and payment, and refuses to flinch at either.

Memories went. The bread. The rain on stone. Their mother's kitchen—had there been a mother's kitchen? There had been. There had been. Gone. A dog, some childhood, a dog whose name was—gone. The taste of the first prayer they ever answered wrongly. Gone. They burned steadily, calmly, like a candle that had finally been given permission.

What remained was surprisingly small. A name. A habit of arithmetic. And Sera—Sera as a fact, if not a face. *Someone stayed. Someone chose the shallow life over the hole and then chose the hole over the life. Someone is shouting orders on the ridge right now, holding the vanguard off the stones with thirty guild stragglers and a stolen spear, and she is magnificent, and you do not know her face, and you love her anyway, which proves love was never about the face.*

The door shuddered. The armies broke through the picket line—she was retreating up the slope, fighting backward, glorious, doomed—and Paloma poured faster.

And then Sera was there, at the threshold, bleeding from a cut above her eye, and she did not go through.

"Sera," Paloma said—or tried to say. Their voice was mostly stone now. "Go. *Through.*"

"No," she said.

"*Please.*"

"No." She planted herself in the doorway, in the wind of two countries, and faced them—faced the thing they had become, which was less a person now than a place, a standing weight, a held breath. "You want to know the difference between us? You think everything is a debt. Something given must be repaid, something owed must be collected, every gift is a transaction wearing a smile." She put her hand flat against their chest, against whatever chest remained, and Paloma felt it like sunlight feels a wall. "But love isn't a debt, Paloma. Love is the one entry that never balances. You can't pay it forward and you can't pay it back and you can't burn it, because it isn't *yours*—it's ours, it lives in the space between us, and I am not leaving the space between us. So." She set her feet. She wiped the blood from her eye. "Hold your door. I'll hold mine."

The stones screamed. The valley filled with soldiers. The erased country pulled at them both like a tide going out.

And Paloma, anchor of the Hollow, last unpaid debt of a dead god, discovered one final truth about arithmetic—the one lesson their teacher had saved for last, the one that wasn't in any ledger:

Some things cannot be counted. Some things insist on staying.

The door began to close around them both—

and neither of them moved.
