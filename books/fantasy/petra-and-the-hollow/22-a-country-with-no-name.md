## 22. A Country With No Name

Paloma woke to the smell of rain on warm stone and no idea who they were.

This was not, they would later learn, an unusual way to begin a day in this country. But it was their first day in it, and so the experience was entirely novel: a self with nothing behind it, like a house swept clean to the rafters, every room empty, every window open.

They lay on a hillside of soft grey grass beneath a sky the color of unpolished silver. Somewhere below, water moved — a river, unhurried, going about its business without any opinion of them whatsoever. The grass smelled faintly of salt, though there was no sea in sight. Everything here smelled faintly of salt, as if the whole land had been wept over once, long ago, and had decided to keep the scent as a souvenir.

Paloma sat up. Their body worked. That was something. They knew how to walk, how to speak, how to do arithmetic in their head — three times seven was twenty-one, and for some reason that felt like a small triumph, as though arithmetic were a friend they'd been reunited with rather than a fact they'd never lost at all.

What they did not know was their own name.

They checked, carefully, the way one checks a pocket for keys. *I am* — and then nothing. A doorway with no door in it. They said aloud, to the hillside, "Hello."

"Hello," said the hillside, or rather a woman sitting ten feet away on a flat stone, who had apparently been there the whole time, watching them wake with the patient expression of someone who has done this before.

She had grey eyes and a sword across her knees that she held the way other people hold a cat — comfortably, out of habit, without any immediate intention of using it. Her hair was dark shot through with early silver. There was a scar along her jaw, old and pale, and when she saw Paloma looking at it she touched it absently, the way you touch a thing you've stopped noticing years ago.

"You're awake," she said. "Good. You slept two days. I was starting to take it personally."

"Do I know you?" Paloma asked.

"No," she said, and smiled, and something in the smile went through Paloma like a needle finding an old seam — a stitch pulled tight somewhere in the chest, in a garment they couldn't remember wearing. "Not yet. My name is Sera."

"Sera," Paloma repeated. It sat strangely on the tongue, familiar as a coat that fits but belongs to someone else.

"And yours?"

"I don't have one."

Sera nodded, entirely unsurprised, which was either very reassuring or very alarming. "That's common, here. On the far side." She gestured at the country around them — the hills, the river, the distant suggestion of rooftops down in a valley where smoke rose from chimneys in thin, friendly columns. "The crossing takes your past. All of it. Everyone who comes through arrives the same way you did: blank, and hungry, and a little offended about it."

"I'm not offended."

"You will be. Give it an hour."

*

They walked down toward the valley together, because Sera was going that way and because Paloma could think of no reason not to follow a stranger with a sword except all the obvious ones, none of which seemed to apply. The country unfolded as they walked, and it was — there was no other word for it — *unfinished*. Not broken. Unfinished. Hills that ended in soft edges, as if drawn by someone running out of ink. A forest where the trees grew in rows too straight to be wild and too irregular to be planted. Roads that appeared only when you looked at them directly, shy as deer.

And people. That was the strangest part. Refugees from the free cities, hundreds of them, scattered across the valley like seeds from a split pod — and every one of them nameless, every one of them beginning again. Paloma watched a man by the river teaching a woman to skip stones, both of them laughing, neither of them knowing each other's names or their own, and thought: *this is what a country looks like the morning after it forgives itself.*

"What is this place called?" Paloma asked.

"It isn't. Yet." Sera kicked a stone off the path with unnecessary violence. "The treaty unwrote it. The god's erasure hollowed it. And now everyone's too polite or too frightened to be the first to name it. There's a committee."

"A committee."

"A naming committee. They meet on Thursdays. They've been meeting for six weeks. Current leading candidates are 'New Corvane,' which everyone hates, and 'The Valley,' which is less a name than a shrug." She glanced sideways at Paloma, and again the needle-seam feeling, the stitch pulling. "You strike me as someone with opinions about names."

"I wouldn't know," Paloma said. "I don't have any opinions. I checked."

But it wasn't quite true, and they both knew it wasn't quite true, because walking beside this woman felt like walking beside a fire in winter — warmth without explanation, comfort without provenance. Once, when their hands brushed reaching for the same low branch, they both stopped. Stood there. Looked at each other.

"I feel," Paloma began slowly, "like I've forgotten something important."

"Everyone here feels that," Sera said quietly. "It fades. Mostly."

"Mostly?"

"Mostly," she said again, and turned up the path, ears slightly pink, and Paloma followed, wondering what exactly they had forgotten and why forgetting it felt so much like keeping it.

*

The settlement in the valley had been a town once, back when the country existed, and now it was a town again, in the way that a skeleton is a person again once enough people agree to stand inside it. Refugees had raised roofs, reopened wells, planted gardens in the ruins with the fierce practicality of people who had crossed a door between worlds and intended to make the crossing worth the fare.

There was a guildhall. Of course there was a guildhall; some things survive the death of everything. Its sign had burned or rotted or been deliberately removed, and above the door hung a blank board, waiting.

Inside, at a long table scarred by centuries of elbows, sat a broad-shouldered woman with iron-grey hair and a ledger open before her, and when she looked up at Paloma, her face did something complicated — grief and relief and recognition all fighting for space — before settling into brisk authority.

"You're awake," she said. "Good. We need hands."

"Do I know you?" Paloma asked, for the second time that day.

"No," said the woman, and her voice caught on it, just slightly, like a cart wheel hitting a rut. "No, you don't. Ivetta. I keep the accounts. Or I did. Or—" She pressed her lips together, restarted. "Someone has to keep the accounts. That's the whole of the law here so far: *someone keeps the accounts.*"

She turned the ledger around and pushed it across the table.

It was blank. Every page, front to back, blank as new snow. Except — Paloma leaned closer — the first page bore a single line of writing, in a hand they did not recognize and yet somehow did:

*All debts transferred to the keeper. Paid in full. Let the first entry be written by whoever comes next.*

Below it, unsigned.

Paloma stared at it for a long time. Something in the handwriting made their chest ache, the way music makes chests ache — recognition without recollection. They felt, absurdly, like crying, and had no idea why, and no memory to blame it on.

"Who wrote this?" they asked.

"A person," Ivetta said. "Who paid everything they had so that the rest of us could start at zero." She cleared her throat. "The guild's books are clean. Every debt settled, every account closed. Which means, for the first time in the guild's history, we're not trading in what people owe. We get to decide what we trade in instead." She looked at Paloma with an expression of terrible hope. "I'm told you have a head for numbers."

"I have a head," Paloma said. "What's in it is another matter."

"That's the spirit."

*

* * *

They learned the country the way you learn a lover: awkwardly, incrementally, with occasional collisions.

Paloma learned that they could read three languages and cook exactly one dish, badly. That they flinched at the word *Vess* without knowing why, though nobody said it much anymore; the god had come through the door with the last of the refugees and then simply — thinned. Faded into the hills like mist into morning. Some said it starved. Some said it forgot itself, which the survivors agreed was a gentler fate than it deserved and a better one than it feared. Either way, the prayers stopped being answered, which everyone had decided to consider a blessing.

Sera learned Paloma. Or relearned them, though neither of them knew that.

They fell into it without deciding to, the way water finds the low ground. She taught them to hold a sword; they discovered their body remembered the forms even when their mind didn't, and Sera watched them run the drills with an expression she tried and failed to keep neutral. "You fight like someone with a grudge," she observed.

"Against whom?"

"No idea. Whoever taught you, probably."

They ate dinner together because the mess hall was crowded and two seats were easier to find than one. Then they ate dinner together when the hall was empty, because habit had formed. Then they ate dinner on the wall overlooking the river, because the view was good and because neither of them mentioned the alternative, which was not eating dinner together, and the alternative had begun to seem unthinkable in a way that made them both careful and slightly formal, like diplomats negotiating a treaty neither wanted to sign.

"You laugh at my jokes," Sera said one evening, "before I finish them."

"I'm quick."

"You laughed at a joke yesterday that I hadn't told yet. I was saving it. You ruined it."

"Then tell me a different joke."

"That was my best one. I'd been workshopping it for a week." She threw a crust of bread at their head, and Paloma caught it without looking, and they both went quiet, because catching things without looking was not the act of a stranger, and they were strangers, officially, on paper, in every way that either of them could prove.

Once — only once — Paloma woke in the night from a dream of fire and salt and a voice saying *you owe me, little ledger-keeper, you owe me everything*, and came out of it shaking, and found Sera already there in the doorway with a lamp, as if she'd heard the dream from across the courtyard. She didn't ask what it was about. She just sat with them until the shaking stopped, and near dawn, half-asleep against their shoulder, she murmured, "Whatever you did, whoever you were — it's paid. Someone made sure of it. You're allowed to rest."

"How would you know?" Paloma whispered.

"Call it faith," Sera said. "I used to have it professionally."

*

* * *

Spring came to the unnamed country the way spring comes everywhere: rudely, all at once, without asking permission. The valley turned green. The refugees' gardens came up in crooked rows. Children were born who would never know there had ever been a door, and children arrived through the door who would never fully believe the world on the other side of it had been real.

On a bright morning in the fourth month, Sera found Paloma in the guildhall, hunched over the great ledger, chewing the end of a pen.

"You look troubled," she said.

"I'm trying to write the first entry. Ivetta says it's tradition. The first entry sets the character of the whole book." Paloma gestured at the blank page with despair. "Everything I write sounds wrong. *Inventory: forty sacks of barley, received.* Fine. Accurate. Dead as a stone. This book deserves better than dead."

"What does it deserve?"

"Something true." Paloma looked up at her, and the needle-seam feeling came again, stronger than it had ever come, strong enough that they set down the pen before their hand could shake. "Sera. I need to tell you something, and I want you to not make it strange."

"I make nothing strange. I am famously normal."

"Every day since I woke on that hillside, I've felt like I left something on the other side of the door. Not a thing. A — a weight. A debt. And every day since I met you, I've felt like whatever it was, it's been paid." They stood. "I don't remember loving you. I want to be clear about that. I have no evidence. No history. For all I know we met for the first time on that hillside and I've invented the rest out of loneliness and good dinners."

"All right," Sera said, very steadily, in the voice of someone holding a rail in a storm.

"But I keep almost remembering," Paloma said, "and what I almost remember is that it cost me everything, and I'd pay it again. So." They took a breath. "Here's my proposal. We don't try to recover anything. We don't dig. Whatever we were, it's settled — the book says so, in somebody's hand, and I trust that hand more than I trust my own memory. Instead we start here. Blank page. New entry. Two strangers who keep almost recognizing each other, and decide to stop almost."

Sera was silent for a long moment. The lamplight caught the old scar on her jaw.

"For the record," she said finally, and her voice cracked on the second word and she let it, "I also have no evidence. And I also keep almost remembering. And last week I caught myself watching you sleep and thinking, *oh no, not again,* which implies a *first time,* which implies—" She stopped. Laughed, wetly. "You're right. We stop almost. We start actual."

"Is that a yes?"

"That's a yes, you impossible—"

She kissed them, and it was not the kiss of strangers, and it was not the kiss of the people they had forgotten either. It was a third thing, brand new, belonging to no ledger and no country and no god: the kiss of two people choosing each other with the full knowledge that they were choosing blind, which is, a wise priest of three dead gods once said, the only honest way anyone chooses anything.

When they broke apart, Paloma turned back to the ledger. The pen felt lighter in their hand.

"What will you write?" Sera asked.

Paloma considered the blank page — the whole blank country behind it, the hills waiting for names, the committee still meeting on Thursdays, the river going about its business. Then they wrote, in a clear and careful hand:

*First entry. New account, opened jointly. Assets: one country, unnamed; one book, blank; two people, unencumbered. Debts: none outstanding. Terms: to be negotiated daily, in good faith, for as long as both parties shall live.*

They signed it. Sera signed beneath, and her hand shook, and she let it.

"There," Paloma said. "The book's begun."

Outside, the bells of the unfinished tower rang noon, and down in the valley someone was shouting — joyfully, urgently — that the naming committee had finally, *finally*, after four months and forty-one Thursdays, narrowed the candidates down to two.

Paloma capped the ink, took Sera's hand, and went out to see what the world would call itself.

The end — and the first line of everything else.
