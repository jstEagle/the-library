## 1. The Night the Stars Went Out

The trouble with a small life, Bram had learned, was that it fit you like a too-tight coat. You could wear it. You could even get comfortable in it. But every morning when you buttoned yourself into it, you felt the seams straining, and you knew—one day they'd give.

Twelve years of smallness had taught Bram Coldharbour exactly how to keep the seams from splitting.

By day, they sold charms in a crooked little shop on Tallow Lane: luck-knots braided from river grass, dried lavender against bad dreams, copper pennies hammered flat and stamped with a hare for safe travel. Harmless things. Things that worked because people believed in them, which was the only magic Greyhollow's charter permitted anymore. Bram priced everything fairly, smiled at everyone, and never once let their voice drop below the register that human ears could hear.

Because Bram could speak to beasts. Had been able to since before they could write. And for twelve years—since the night the Edict fell and the witch-finders came with their iron collars and their ledgers of names—that gift had been a death sentence folded up small enough to hide behind the ribs.

So at night, when the shop was shuttered and the lamps of Tallow Lane burned low, Bram went out through the back door into the alley where the strays gathered, and they whispered.

"Old Tom," they murmured to the scarred grey tomcat who ruled the rooftops behind the chandler's, "you've got a burr in your left haunch. Hold still."

Tom held still. He always did. There were maybe six cats in Greyhollow who knew what Bram was, and they kept the secret with the particular contempt cats reserved for all human affairs. *Your wars are boring,* Tom liked to say. *But your hands are warm.*

That was the whole of it. That was the life. Charms by day, whispers by night, and never—*never*—a word spoken aloud where a window might be open or an ear might be sharp. Bram had gotten so good at silence that sometimes they wondered if the silence had eaten the rest of them, if underneath all that careful quiet there was nothing left but a shopkeeper-shaped hole.

They were thinking about this, more or less, on the night the stars went out.

* * *

It happened between one customer and the next, in the blue hour just past closing, when Bram was sweeping grit from between the floorboards and the last light was going amber in the west-facing window.

Bram stepped outside to pull the shutters—and stopped.

The sky over Greyhollow was empty.

Not clouded. Not hazy. *Empty.* Every star, gone, as if a hand had reached down and wiped the black clean. Bram stood with their hand on the shutter and felt something in their chest tilt sideways, some animal part of them older than thought, older than names, screaming a warning their mind couldn't parse.

Then the wards broke.

Everyone in Greyhollow heard it. There was no mistaking it—the border wards had hummed beneath the city for three hundred years, so constant that folk forgot it was there, like the sound of your own blood. Now the hum climbed, and climbed, and *shattered*, a sound like a cathedral's worth of glass breaking somewhere beyond the hills, and the pressure of it rolled through the streets hard enough to rattle shutters and knock pots off hooks. Dogs began to howl, every one of them at once, and then—this was the part Bram would remember later, the part that would keep them awake for years—the dogs stopped howling mid-note. All together. As if someone had cut the sound off with shears.

As if they'd forgotten what they were howling about.

Bram's hands were shaking. They looked down the alley toward the chandler's roof, where Old Tom should have been sitting his usual post.

"Tom?" they said, barely a breath, in the voice they used for no one.

Nothing answered. The silence where the city's small voices should have been was the loudest thing Bram had ever heard.

* * *

They didn't sleep. Of course they didn't sleep. They lit every lamp in the shop and sat behind the counter with a knife they'd never once needed, watching the door, listening to a city that had gone wrong in its bones. Around midnight, neighbors' voices drifted through the walls—frightened, argumentative, grasping for ordinary explanations. Clouds. Soot from the foundries. A trick of the usurper's new engines. Nobody said the word *magic*, because nobody in Greyhollow said that word anymore unless they wanted a visit.

Near dawn, exhausted and hollow-eyed, Bram finally drifted into a doze with their cheek on the counter—

And woke to the bell above the shop door ringing.

A fox stood in the doorway.

It was red, or had been; now its coat was matted grey with ash and something darker. It swayed on its feet like a drunkard, and its eyes—foxes' eyes were quick and bright and full of commentary, Bram knew that better than anyone alive—its eyes were *empty*. Not dead. Worse than dead. It was the look of a creature standing in a house where all the furniture had been carried out, trying to remember what the rooms were for.

"No," Bram whispered. "No, no—"

The fox took another lurching step and collapsed on the floorboards, and Bram was around the counter and on their knees beside it without any memory of moving. Their hands hovered over its ribs, feeling the shallow, stuttering breath, the heartbeat running wrong, like a clock winding down.

"*Speak to me,*" Bram said—and then caught themselves, glancing wildly at the shuttered windows, the thin walls, the whole listening city. Twelve years of discipline warred with twelve seconds of terror, and terror won. They bent close, until their lips nearly touched the ruined ear, and gave the fox the only thing they had left to give.

Their true voice.

"*Little brother. Little brother, I'm here. What happened? What's happening?*"

For a long moment, nothing. Bram felt the life guttering under their palms like a candle in a draft, and grief rose in them, huge and stupid and useless—

Then the fox's eye rolled toward them, and focused, and *found* them, and a voice scraped up out of it thin as wire:

"*Gone. All of it. The hedge-voices, the hill-voices, the old agreement. Severed. Cut at the root.*"

"The covenant," Bram breathed. "The Oath. Who—who could cut the—"

"*He did. The man on the throne. And without it we are forgetting ourselves, speaker. Every day we forget more. My name—I had a name—*" The fox shuddered, a full-body spasm. "*I can't find my name.*"

Bram's eyes were burning. "Hold on. I can help, I know herbs, I—"

"*Listen.*" The voice cracked like ice on a pond. "*There isn't time. It has to be reforged. At the stone. The first stone. The—*"

The fox's body arched. Its legs scrabbled once against the boards.

"*Oathstone,*" it said, in a voice that was suddenly, terribly clear, and then it said nothing at all.

* * *

Bram knelt on the floor of their shop for a long time afterward, with dawn coming grey through the shutter-cracks and a dead fox under their hands and one word ringing in their skull like a struck bell.

*Oathstone.*

They should burn the body. They should open the shutters, sweep the floor, put the kettle on, be Bram Coldharbour, charm-seller, harmless man-or-woman-or-nothing-at-all, the smallest life in the smallest corner of a city that had learned not to look too closely at anyone. That was the sensible thing. That was the survivable thing.

Instead, Bram looked down at the fox and made a promise they didn't know yet they'd spend everything keeping.

"I'll find my name too," they whispered. "And yours. Both of us."

Outside, boots were already marching in the street. Lots of boots. In twelve years, Bram had learned the sound of a patrol, and this wasn't a patrol—it was a column, dozens strong, moving up Tallow Lane with the deliberate rhythm of men following a list of addresses.

Someone, somewhere, had noticed the same things Bram had noticed. And the usurper's answer to a broken sky, as always, would be iron and fire and a ledger of names.

Bram closed the fox's eyes, snuffed the lamps, and slipped out through the back door into the alley where the strays gathered—where six pairs of eyes should have been waiting, and weren't.

Behind them, fists began hammering on the shop's front door.
