## 2. Witch-Finders at the Door

They came at dawn, the way rot comes: quietly, then all at once.

Bram heard the boots first — not the scattered tread of the night watch but a rhythm, many feet moving in unison down Candlemaker's Row. They were still lying on the cot behind the shop counter, one hand curled around the fox's cold ear, when the first door in the row splintered.

The fox had died an hour before sunrise. Bram had closed its eyes and wrapped it in sacking and told it, uselessly, *you did well, you found me, rest now* — words that meant nothing to a dead thing but everything to the living thing saying them. The word it had carried still sat in Bram's chest like a swallowed coal. *Oathstone.* Spoken in a voice gone thin as smoke, in the old tongue of the wild that no human throat should have understood.

And now the boots.

Bram rose without sound, a skill learned young, and pressed their eye to the shutter crack. Greyhollow's morning fog was burning off in grey rags, and through it came the witch-finders — twelve of them, maybe more, in the usurper's black-and-copper, faces hidden behind mesh masks that turned every mouth into a grille. They moved door to door with the bored efficiency of men who had done this in a hundred cities. One carried a caged bird. Not a pet. A *listener* — a wren charmed, or warped, into screaming when magic touched the air near it.

Bram's hand went to their throat, to the little bone whistle they'd worn since childhood. Harmless, they'd always told themselves. Trinkets. Luck-charms. Nothing that would make a wren sing.

But last night they had knelt in this shop and spoken aloud with a dying fox, and the wards had fallen around them like glass, and the whole city's air was thick with the residue of broken covenant. If the wren could hear anything, it could hear everything.

Across the row, old Maren Ostrey opened her door before they could break it. That was Maren — proud, foolish, kind, the woman who brought Bram soup in winter and pretended not to notice the cats that came and went through the shop like customers. She stood in her doorway in her nightdress with her arms crossed and said something too quiet for Bram to catch.

The witch-finder with the cage raised it toward her.

The wren screamed.

It was a horrible sound, high and mechanical, and it went on and on while Maren stood very straight and the witch-finders closed around her like water closing over a stone. Bram saw her say something else — saw her chin lift, saw her look directly across the row at the shuttered charm-shop, one long deliberate look, as if she knew exactly who was watching and wanted them to know she wasn't afraid.

Then they dragged her into the street, and her charm-work spilled out of her pockets as she struggled — little woven blessings for sore joints and safe roads, the most harmless magic in Greyhollow — and the wren screamed at every piece, and a witch-finder ground each one under his boot like a beetle.

Bram stepped back from the shutter. Their heart was going so hard they could feel it in their teeth.

*Run,* said every instinct they owned. Twelve years of instincts. Twelve years of keeping small, keeping quiet, keeping the gift folded up inside like wings that had never been allowed to open. Run out the back, take the sewers, be a ghost again.

But Maren had looked at them. Maren had looked at them *on purpose.*

Bram took the bone whistle from around their neck, kissed it once — an old habit, half prayer, half apology — and tucked it deep into their boot. Then they pulled up the floorboards over the cellar hatch and dropped into the dark.

* * *

The sewers under Greyhollow were older than the city above them, older than the free cities themselves, some said — cut by hands no history book named. Bram had learned them the way other children learned prayers: from the rats.

The rats met them at the bottom of the ladder, a dozen pale eyes catching the light of Bram's shuttered lantern.

*Down,* said the eldest, a scarred female with a torn ear, in the flat practical voice rats used for anything that wasn't food or fear. *Down and east. The black-coats are at every grate above. They have the birds.*

*How many birds?*

*Too many.* The rat groomed her torn ear, fastidious even now. *The air tastes wrong everywhere. Everything magic screams. Even you scream, small-speaker, though you try to be quiet.*

*I'm always quiet.*

*Yes,* said the rat, without sympathy. *That is what is sad about you.*

Bram didn't answer that. They followed her east along the walkway at the channel's edge, ankle-deep in shadow-water, past arches furred with moss that should have glowed faintly with ward-light and didn't. The whole city's underdark felt deaf. For twelve years the wards had hummed down here, a low note Bram had never let themselves love because loving things made losing them louder. Now the silence where the hum had been was worse than any noise.

They had gone perhaps half a mile when the rats stopped so suddenly that Bram walked into the dark ahead of them.

*Stop,* the scarred female said. *Small-speaker. Stop stop stop.*

Bram froze. Ahead, where the eastern passage bent toward the river gate, torchlight bloomed against wet stone. Voices. The mesh-masked faces of two witch-finders swam in the glow, and between them, held aloft in a wire cage, the wren.

It hadn't screamed yet. It was listening.

Bram killed the lantern. Stood absolutely still. Breathed through their mouth, slow and shallow, the way you breathe when you're trying to be nobody. Below them the water whispered. Above, the wren's head turned — a small mechanical horror, tracking something it could hear but not see.

Magic left traces. Everyone knew that. What everyone didn't know — what only Bram and the beasts knew — was that beast-speech left the loudest trace of all, because it wasn't cast at all. It was just *true*, and truth rang.

The wren opened its beak.

Bram ran.

They went west, blind, splashing, the rats streaming past them in a grey ribbon — *not this way, small-speaker, the grates, the grates* — and behind them the wren's scream tore the tunnel apart, and boots answered it, many boots, and a crossbow bolt sparked off stone a hand's width from Bram's ear.

They burst up through a maintenance grate into a courtyard and there were three more of them, black-and-copper, waiting — because of course there were; the birds had sung from three directions and the net had closed hours ago. A masked face tilted. A gloved hand pointed.

"On your knees," someone said. "Hands where we can see them."

Bram knelt. The courtyard fog curled around their wrists. Somewhere beyond the wall, Maren Ostrey was in a wagon heading for whatever the usurper did with people whose charms sang, and Bram thought, with a clarity that felt like a knife going in: *this is what hiding bought you. You hid, and she paid.*

"Search it," the nearest witch-finder said. "Careful. The bird flagged something big."

The second one reached for Bram's collar —

— and stopped, because a voice from the courtyard gate said, flat and carrying:

"That one's mine."

* * *

She came through the fog like a correction. Tall, weathered, mid-thirties maybe, with a sword worn the way soldiers wear swords they've actually used, and on her shoulder the badge of a ward-keeper — except the badge had been defaced, the copper sigil scratched out, leaving only a raw silver scar. Her cloak was travel-stained. Her eyes did the arithmetic of Bram's kneeling body in about a second and clearly found it wanting.

"Warden Vane," the senior witch-finder said, and there was something sour in it. "You're stripped. You've got no authority here."

"I've got a writ." She held up a folded paper. "Marshal's office. This one's flagged for interrogation regarding the ward collapse. Night of the stars. You want to explain to the marshal why you processed their prisoner into the pyre carts?"

A silence. The wren in its cage screamed once, experimentally, at Petra Vane herself — and she didn't flinch, which told Bram something interesting: either she was clean, or she was very good at standing still.

"The bird flagged this one," the witch-finder insisted.

"The bird flags everything lately," Vane said. "Half the city's been reported by their own grandmothers. Your cages are singing at rain barrels." She stepped closer, and her voice dropped into something colder. "Or would you rather argue jurisdiction with the marshal? Because I'll wait."

They didn't argue. Bram watched hatred move through the masked faces like weather, watched them hand over a prisoner they believed belonged to someone else, and then the courtyard was empty except for fog and the woman who had just claimed Bram like confiscated goods.

She hauled Bram up by the arm — not gently — and marched them out the gate and three streets east before she spoke.

"You're the charm-seller off Candlemaker's Row."

"Bram."

"I know what you are." She stopped in the mouth of an alley, checked both ends of it, and then looked at Bram with eyes the color of slate water. "You're a beast-speaker. Don't bother denying it; I watched you listen to the rats coming out of that grate. Nobody listens to rats like that unless the rats are talking."

Bram said nothing. Twelve years of saying nothing. But their silence, for the first time, felt less like armor and more like a wound.

"Here's what happens now," Vane said. "You're going to come with me, voluntarily, because the alternative is those gentlemen downstairs realizing their writ is forged in about an hour, and they don't forgive being made fools of. And you're going to tell me what the fox said to you last night, because I've spent twelve years trying to learn why the wards fell, and you're the first person in this city who's heard anything from the wild side of it."

"How do you know about the fox?"

"Because I've been watching your shop for three days." She said it without embarrassment. "Because the wards didn't just fail, they were *severed*, and severed covenants leave a scent, and everything with four legs in Greyhollow has been walking in circles howling since the stars went out — except toward your street. Toward *you*." She paused. "And because I need you, and I hate needing anyone, so let's skip the part where we pretend this is comfortable."

Bram finally found their voice. It came out rougher than intended. "Ward-keepers arrested my mentor. Sela Marsh. Twelve years ago. So you'll understand if 'ward-keeper' isn't a word I trust."

Something crossed Vane's face — quick, buried, but not buried enough. Guilt has a shape, and Bram had seen it often enough on grieving animals to recognize it here.

"I know who Sela Marsh is," Vane said quietly. "I know more than you think I do. And I'm not a ward-keeper anymore — they took the rank when I refused to sign reports saying the border wards were sound. I watched them decay for six years and I watched my superiors bury the numbers." Her jaw tightened. "So call me what you like. But right now there are wagons full of your neighbors rolling toward the citadel, the wild has gone silent as a grave, and you and I are the only two people in Greyhollow who know this is bigger than a purge."

Above the rooftops, the starless sky was going pale with a dawn that lit nothing. Bram thought of Maren's lifted chin. Of the fox's single word sitting in their chest like a coal. Of twelve years of smallness, and where smallness had gotten everyone they loved.

"If I come with you," Bram said slowly, "it's not forgiveness. Whatever you did, whoever you were — that account stays open."

"Fine," said Petra Vane. "I'm good at debts."

She turned east, toward the river gate and the road beyond it, and Bram fell into step beside her — hating it, needing it, the coal in their chest giving its first faint heat.

Behind them, at the mouth of the alley, a caged wren began, very softly, to sing.
