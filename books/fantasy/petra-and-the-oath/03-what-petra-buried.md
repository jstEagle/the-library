## 3. What Petra Buried

The cellar under the tannery smelled of salt and old blood, and Petra Vane had been sitting in it for six hours, listening to the witch-finders' boots cross the street above like a slow drum.

Bram Coldharbour sat across from her on an upturned barrel, arms wrapped around their knees, watching her the way a watched animal watches—every blink catalogued, every breath measured. The rats had led them both here. That was still strange to Petra, that the rats had *led* them, but she was learning to stop flinching at strangeness. She'd had twelve years of practice.

"You could have let them take me," Bram said finally. "Back in the alley. You didn't have to break that finder's nose."

"No."

"Why?"

Petra looked at her hands. They were good hands, once. Hands that had been trusted with the wards of Greyhollow's eastern gate, hands that had signed reports and drawn ward-lines and once, twelve years ago, closed around the wrist of a woman named Sela Marsh while other hands put the irons on.

"Because I've spent my disgrace buying back one honest act at a time," she said. "And I'm not done yet."

Bram said nothing. The lamp between them guttered, steadied.

"I know who you are," Petra went on. "Not your name—I didn't know your name until tonight. But Sela Marsh kept a shop on Tanner's Row before they took her. Charms. Quiet ones. And she had a student who came after hours, who the neighbors said talked to cats." She met their eyes. "I helped arrest her. I held her arm while they did it."

Something moved behind Bram's face—not surprise. Worse than surprise. Confirmation.

"I knew it," they said quietly. "When you pulled me out of that alley, some part of me knew it. Nobody saves a stranger for free."

"I'm not asking you to forgive me."

"Good. Because I don't."

The word landed clean, no heat in it, and somehow that was worse than shouting. Petra nodded. She deserved no better and had expected nothing else, and still it went through her like a splinter finding old wood.

* * *

She opened her pack because there was nothing left to do but open it. Under the spare shirt, the oilskin, the knife she'd stopped carrying when they stripped her rank—the journal sat where it had sat for twelve years, wrapped in waxed cloth, heavier than any book had a right to be.

Sela Marsh's journal. Confiscated evidence, inventory number 4471, logged into the Ward-Keepers' archive the night of the arrest. Petra had stolen it out of the archive three years later, the same week the council asked her to falsify her first report about the border wards. Two crimes in one lifetime. Only one of them shamed her.

"What is it?" Bram asked. Suspicion had sharpened their voice.

"The only record of what happened to the covenant," Petra said. "Or the closest thing to one. Sela wrote it in cipher. The archivists tried everything—scholars' ciphers, military ciphers, merchant code. Nothing fit." She turned the journal over in her hands, feeling the spine crackle. "It took me four years to understand why nothing fit. It's not a cipher for eyes. It's written so it can only be read by someone who can hear the shapes words make underneath words. A beast-speaker's cipher." She held it out. "Only you can read this. Maybe only you in all the free cities."

Bram didn't take it.

"Why carry it?" they asked. "Twelve years. Why not burn it, or sell it, or bury it somewhere and get on with your life?"

Because guilt is a stone you either set down or build with, and Petra had chosen, without ever quite deciding, to build.

"Because the night the stars went out," she said, "the wards didn't fail. I know the difference. I spent nine years maintaining those lines, and what fell over Greyhollow wasn't decay or accident or sabotage at a single anchor-point. It was severance. Something cut the covenant whole, root to branch, and everything built on it came down at once." Her voice stayed level. It cost her. "The council knows. The council has known for years that the wards were thinning, and they buried my reports because the truth was inconvenient. So if the institutions won't look for answers, then I will. And this—" she lifted the journal "—is the first page of them."

Bram stared at the oilskin bundle as though it might bite. Maybe, Petra thought, it would. Maybe it already had; maybe the word *Oathstone*, spoken by a dying fox, had teeth marks in them already.

"My mentor is in there," Bram said. "What happened to her. Whether she's alive."

"Yes."

"And you want me to read it with you. After you put the irons on her."

"After I put the irons on her," Petra agreed. "Yes."

The silence stretched. Somewhere overhead, boots passed again, slower now, moving off toward the river district. The hunt was widening. By morning the finders would have the alley witness statements, the description of a tall figure in a ward-keeper's coat that no longer existed, and a charm-seller from Tanner's Row whose shop door stood open to the rain.

"They'll come here by dawn," Petra said. "We can't stay."

"We?" Bram's mouth twisted. "There's no we. There's you, and there's me, and there's a reason I've hidden what I am since I was fourteen years old, and that reason wears your old uniform."

"I know."

"Do you? Do you actually know what it was like?" For the first time, heat came into their voice, low and banked and terrible. "Every day of my life, I have made myself small. I sold charms that were nothing, sugar and string, because real work would draw the finders. I whispered to strays in drainage ditches because it was the only place my voice fit. My mentor taught me that hiding was survival, and then your people took her, and I learned she'd been right all along. Hiding is survival. Belonging is a luxury for people whose gifts are legal." They stood, and the barrel rocked. "So no. I'm not traveling anywhere with you. Find another beast-speaker."

"There isn't one," Petra said. "That's rather the point."

Bram laughed, short and bitter, and headed for the stairs—and stopped. Because the rats were there. A dozen of them, lined along the bottom step in the lamplight, tails still, whiskers forward, all of them looking at Bram with an attention that had nothing animal-simple in it.

Petra watched them watch each other, girl and beasts—or whatever Bram was, person and beasts—and saw the moment the argument died in their throat.

"They've been doing that since the fox spoke," Bram said, very quietly. "Since it said Oathstone. Everything wild within a mile turns to look at me like I'm supposed to *do* something." Their hand flexed at their side. "I don't know what they think I am."

"I think," Petra said carefully, "that whatever broke the covenant broke it in a way that left a shape behind. A missing piece. And I think the wild knows its own missing piece when it sees one." She rose, joints stiff from the cold floor, and held out the journal again. "Read it. Not for me. Read it for her. If there's even a chance Sela Marsh is alive—if there's a chance she left the truth in these pages—then you're the only person in the world who can pick it up."

Bram looked at the journal. At the rats. At her.

"If I read it," they said, "and it tells us where to go—you'll follow? Even if it's somewhere bad. Even if it's Vey's own citadel."

"Especially there."

"And if it tells us something I don't want to know? About her? About me?"

Petra thought about twelve years of inventory numbers and falsified reports and a woman's wrist under her hand, and said, "Then we'll know it together. That's the only thing I can offer you. Not forgiveness. Company."

A long moment. Then Bram took the journal out of her hands, and the weight of it seemed to travel through them—they swayed slightly, caught themselves, pressed their palm flat against the cover as though listening through it.

"It's warm," they murmured. "After twelve years in a cold archive, it's *warm*."

"She wrote it to be found," Petra said. "By you, I think. She just didn't know your face yet."

Bram sat back down on the barrel. They unwrapped the oilskin with the care of someone handling a sleeping animal, and opened the journal to its first page, and went still.

Petra waited. The lamp burned. Above them, Greyhollow lay dark under a sky with no stars in it, and somewhere in that darkness the witch-finders were sharpening their questions.

"Oh," Bram said at last. Their voice had gone strange, hushed, the voice of someone reading aloud inside their own skull. "Oh, Petra. She's not dead."

Relief hit Petra like cold water, and she nearly staggered with it.

"She's alive," Bram went on, and now the hush curdled into something else, something afraid. "She's alive, and she's writing from inside it—from wherever they're keeping her. And there's a word she uses over and over, on every page, under everything else." They looked up, and their eyes in the lamplight were wide and wet and certain. "*Successor.* She keeps writing *successor*. She's telling whoever reads this that the covenant can't be reforged by her alone. It needs a second voice. It needs—" They stopped. Swallowed. The rats along the stair had all gone motionless, dozens of small black eyes fixed on Bram Coldharbour like a congregation waiting on a name.

"It needs what?" Petra asked.

Bram closed the journal slowly, holding it against their chest like a shield, or a wound.

"It needs someone the wild will answer," they said. "Someone it already thinks is its own. And Petra—it's been calling me that all night."
