## 4. The Road of Quiet Beasts

The wild was supposed to be loud.

Bram had grown up on stories of it—the chattering hedgerows, the argumentative crows, the whole green world talking over itself like a market at noon. Sela used to say you couldn't walk three paces past the city walls without being insulted by a jay. Bram had never actually heard it. By the time they were old enough to wander, the gift was already a crime, and so was anything that talked back.

But they'd imagined it. All their life, they'd imagined it.

What they walked into instead was silence with teeth.

Three days out from Greyhollow, following the old drover's road north through country that should have been thrumming with spring, Bram counted every living thing they saw. It was a habit now, a tally kept in the margins of their mind: one hare, fleeing before they'd even crested the hill. A murder of crows that did not caw—did not *anything*—just lifted off a dead field in perfect, frightened unison, like puppets yanked by the same string. Deer sign everywhere and no deer visible. The birds sang, technically. But it was the wrong singing: thin, mechanical, a song played from memory by something that had forgotten why.

"They're not speaking," Bram said, for the fourth time that morning.

Petra walked half a pace ahead, the way she always did, as if the road might ambush her. She'd been a ward-keeper long enough that her body hadn't learned to stop patrolling. "Birds sing. That's not speech."

"Birds *chatter*. Jays insult you. Wrens gossip. There's a wren up there right now singing the same four notes over and over like a man who's lost the rest of the tune." Bram stopped in the middle of the road. The hedges on either side stood blackened at the edges, frost-burned in a season that had no business having frost. "It's not that they've gone quiet. It's that they've gone *dumb*. There's a difference."

Petra turned. She had a way of looking at Bram when Bram said things like this—not skeptical, exactly. Recalibrating. Twelve years of arresting people like Bram had left grooves in her, but she'd spent those same twelve years digging at the wards' decay, and she knew better than most how much the official story was worth.

"Say more," she said.

"The speech isn't words. It's not like I talk to a cat and the cat talks back in little human sentences. It's—" Bram gestured helplessly. "It's meaning. It comes through as feeling first, then shape, then—if the beast is willing—words. And out here there's nothing coming through. Not hostility. Not fear, even. Fear I could work with; fear is just noise pointed the wrong way. This is..." They searched for it. "*Emptiness.* Like knocking on a door and the room behind it has been emptied. Furniture gone. Hearth cold. Nobody home, and nobody home for a long time."

"And in Greyhollow?"

"In Greyhollow the strays still talk. Barely. Whispers. Half-sentences." Bram started walking again, faster, because standing still made the silence press closer. "The cities are full of old wards, old stones, old promises. Whatever's failing out here failed *here* first. The wild is going mute from the edges in."

They camped that night in the shell of a drover's inn, its roof fallen in, its hearth full of seasons of leaves. Petra built a fire with the economy of someone who'd slept rough on assignment for years. Bram sat with their back against the wall and listened to nothing.

Not nothing. That was the horror of it. Bram listened the way only a beast-speaker listens—ears open, gift spread wide like a net—and caught the faintest thread, far off in the dark hills. Something alive. Something *straining*.

"It's dying," Bram said quietly.

"What is?"

"The speech itself. The covenant—it's not just broken, whatever broke it. It's *starving*. The animals aren't refusing to speak. They're forgetting how. Every day that passes, another generation hatches that's never heard the old words, and the ones who remember are losing them." Bram wrapped their arms around their knees. "I used to think my gift was like a language I happened to know. It's not. It's a language somebody has to keep speaking or it dies. And nobody's been speaking it. Except me. Alone. For twelve years, in alleys, to stray cats, whispering."

Petra fed a stick to the fire. When she spoke, her voice was careful. "You kept it alive."

"I kept a *coal* alive. In my pocket. Under my coat. While the whole house burned down around me because everyone with a louder voice than mine got arrested or worse." The bitterness came up sharp and surprised them both. Bram looked away. "Sorry."

"Don't be." Petra's jaw worked. "It's true. I helped make it true."

They didn't speak after that. The fire crackled, an animal sound with no animal behind it.

* * *

On the fifth day they found the elk.

They heard it before they saw it—or rather, Bram felt it, a faint wrongness in the net of their gift, like a single live coal in a field of ash. Off the road, down in a hollow where a stream had cut through alder scrub, something large lay half-hidden in the bracken.

It was a bull elk, and it was dying. An arrow stood in its shoulder, snapped off short—a military fletching, grey-feathered, the kind the usurper's conscripts carried. The wound had gone bad; the smell of it reached them ten paces out. The elk's great head was lowered into the ferns, antlers like a bare thicket, and its sides heaved in slow, laboring bellows.

Bram slid down the bank before Petra could say a word.

"Careful," Petra hissed. "Wounded herd beasts—"

"He's alone," Bram said. "That's the point. Herds don't leave a wounded bull unless there's no herd logic left. Unless the *knowing* is gone." They knelt in the wet ferns, close enough to see the elk's eye—dark, deep, and flickering with something worse than pain. Confusion. The look of a creature that had forgotten, mid-suffering, what suffering meant.

Bram opened their gift.

For twelve years they'd done this gently, in whispers, in doorways: a soft touch, a polite hello, *I'm a friend, may I sit with you?* Now they pushed everything they had through the contact—and hit the emptiness again, vast and cold, the elk's mind a house with the hearth swept cold.

Except. Deep down. Beneath the forgetting.

A ember. A word-shape, half-dissolved, turning over and over like a leaf in a slow current.

*I remember,* the ember said, in a voice like wind through winter branches. *I remember. I remember.*

"Oh," Bram breathed. Their eyes stung. "Oh, old one. *Yes.* Tell me what you remember."

The elk's good ear swiveled toward them. His eye focused—slowly, with enormous effort, like a man surfacing from deep water. And then, in a voice so worn it was nearly transparent, the last speaking elk in those hills began to talk.

*Cold came into the words. Cold came in from the human side.*

"I don't understand," Bram said aloud, and Petra, above them on the bank, went very still. "Cold from which side?"

*The oath had two voices.* The elk's thought-voice drifted, fraying at the edges, and Bram leaned closer, drinking it in, terrified of losing a syllable. *Two voices, two keepers. The stone remembers both or the stone forgets all. Human voice and wild voice, wound together, each one holding the other's name. My grandmother's grandmother held the wild half of it. She spoke the names of the river and the birch and the small crawling nations. And across the stone stood the human keeper, and they spoke the names of the roads and the roofs and the small walking nation. Two voices. One vow. Each voice a lock upon the other.*

"Broken from the human side," Bram whispered. "You're saying a human broke it."

*One voice walked away. One voice was silenced—or silenced itself—and the other could not hold the vow alone. No more than one hand can hold a rope that must be tied.* The elk shuddered, a wave passing down his enormous body. *And when the human voice fell silent, the wild voice grew quiet too. What is a promise with no one to promise it to? We forgot our own names first. Then we forgot we had forgotten.*

Bram sat back in the wet ferns, cold all through despite the mild air. Above them, Petra had come down the bank without making a sound, and stood with her hand resting on her sword-hilt out of pure habit, her face pale.

"Two voices," she said. "The wards were built on a two-key system. I always wondered why the old ward-stones had paired sockets—human-craft and wild-craft side by side. Everyone assumed the second socket was ceremonial." She looked at Bram. "It wasn't ceremonial. The wards didn't decay. They were *unlocked*. From our side. Deliberately."

*The stone,* the elk said suddenly, urgently, his fading mind seizing on the one thing that mattered, the way the dying seize on what matters. *The Oathstone. Where the voices met. It still stands. It still waits. It is hungry and patient and it has waited twelve years for a voice to come home.* His breath rattled. *Find the other half. Find the voice that was silenced. Before the last of us forgets the way to the stone itself.*

"Where?" Bram gripped the velvet of his nose, heedless of the wound-smell, heedless of everything. "Where is the stone? Tell me where—"

But the ember was guttering. The elk's eye had begun to dim, the great machinery of him slowing, and the last thing that came through was not words at all but a direction—a bearing, felt rather than spoken, north and west, toward mountains Bram knew only from maps. Toward the high pass. Toward—

Then it was gone. Not death, exactly. Worse. The light in the elk's eye settled into simple animal blankness, the peace of a creature with no memories left to lose. He breathed. He would go on breathing for some time yet. But no one was home, and no one ever would be again.

Bram knelt there in the ferns for a long moment, hand still resting on the great dumb head, and grieved. Not for the elk—for the *speech*, for the grandmother-elks who had held the wild half of the vow, for every wren singing four notes of a song whose ending had been amputated. This was what Vey's war had actually cost. Not fields burned, not towns taxed. A whole hemisphere of the world going dark and dumb while the cities argued about borders.

Petra crouched beside them. She didn't touch Bram's shoulder. She wasn't good at comfort; she was good at *next steps*.

"You heard a bearing," she said. "Northwest. High country."

"Past Marrowgate." Bram wiped their face with a dirty sleeve. "The stone wants a voice that was silenced. A human voice, Petra. Someone who once held the other half of the oath and stopped." They looked up, and the thought landed even as they said it, heavy and inevitable. "Sela. It has to be Sela. Your journal, the cipher—she wrote in beast-speech cipher because she was writing about the *oath*. She knew. She knew what was broken and who broke it."

Petra was silent for a long moment. When she answered, her voice was low and steady, the voice of a woman stepping off a cliff and calling it a path.

"Then we go find her. And Bram—" she hesitated, and for once the ex-ward-keeper looked less certain than the beast-speaker, "—if the covenant needs two voices, and one of them is Sela... what if the wild half needs to be someone too? What if that's why your gift is different? Why you could hear the elk when everything else out here has gone empty?"

Bram opened their mouth to deny it.

Nothing came out. Because in the silence of the mute hills, in the hollow where a dying elk had remembered enough to point them north, the question fit something in Bram perfectly—like a key finding a socket that had been waiting, empty, for twelve years.

Far off to the northwest, thunder rolled over mountains they couldn't yet see.

Or perhaps it wasn't thunder.
