## 5. The Usurper's Harvest

Petra had spent twelve years learning to read the land the way she'd once read ward-lines: by what was missing.

The hedgerows were the first tell. For three days they'd walked the old drover's road south of Greyhollow, and for three days the hedgerows had been wrong—not overgrown, not dying, but *absent*, cut back to black stumps in long straight lines, as if someone had measured the wild and decided exactly how much of it to kill. Ward-keepers used to trim hedges that way. Petra herself had signed off on such orders, back when trimming was maintenance and maintenance was duty. But this wasn't maintenance. This was amputation.

"They're harvesting," Bram said quietly, from the ditch beside her.

"I know."

"No—I mean it literally." Bram's voice had that strained quality it got when they were listening to something Petra couldn't hear, which, these days, was almost everything. "The birds stopped singing a mile back. Not scattered—*stopped*. Like someone closed a door on them. And the hedges—" They swallowed. "There are things living in hedges. Lived. Wrens, hedgehogs, the small folk. The stumps are still warm with them. Or they were."

Petra lay flat against the cold earth at the lip of the rise and looked down into the valley, and understood all at once why the road south had been so quiet.

The column filled it like a wound fills with blood.

She'd seen conscript columns before, during the border wars—mud-colored lines of men and women who hadn't chosen their uniforms, marching where they were pointed. This was shaped like one of those but wrong in every proportion. The wagons came first, forty of them, fifty, each drawn by oxen whose eyes had been sewn shut with red thread—a practice Petra recognized from the old witch-finder manuals, meant to blind the beasts to whatever they carried. And behind the wagons, flanking them, marching in files too neat for conscripts: soldiers in ash-grey livery, and above them, on a raised command wagon, a brazier burning something that gave off light the wrong color. Greenish. Hungry.

Cages hung from the wagons' frames. Dozens of them. Small cages, bird-sized; large ones, elk-sized; some so large they needed their own cart, and those held shapes Petra couldn't name—long-bodied things that pressed against the bars and made no sound at all, because there was no sound left in them to make.

"Speaking beasts," Bram breathed. "They're caging speaking beasts."

"Not speaking," Petra said. Her mouth had gone dry. "Look at them. Whatever speech they had is already gone. Vey isn't collecting talkers. He's collecting what's left when you take the talking out."

Bram went very still beside her. Petra had learned, in the weeks since Greyhollow, to read that stillness. It wasn't calm. It was the stillness of someone holding a scream behind their teeth because letting it out would cost everything.

Below, the work of the column continued with the unhurried rhythm of a harvest indeed. Soldiers moved along the blackened hedge-lines with long iron rakes, dragging them through the rootbeds, and where the rakes passed, the earth itself seemed to dim—as though someone had turned down the sun over just that strip of ground. The harvested something traveled up chains into the wagons, into copper vessels that glowed faintly, and the glow fed upward through conduits of braided wire toward the command wagon and its green-burning brazier.

"That's war-magic," Petra said. She hated how steady her voice came out. Steadiness had been drilled into her until it was indistinguishable from numbness. "I've seen the citadel's engines. I told myself they were running on coal and captured fire-salts like everyone said. Nobody runs engines on *this*."

"On what is this?" Bram asked, though Petra suspected they already knew. Beast-speakers always knew first. That was the whole curse of them.

"Power," Petra said. "Raw. The kind the wards used to drink. The kind the covenant used to *be*." She watched a soldier rake a line through a hedge-stump and saw a wren fall out of the branches above it, dead before it landed, small and weightless as a dropped coin. "He didn't break the covenant to punish the wild. He broke it to *drain* it. Every ward that failed, every beast gone mute, every star snuffed out—that power didn't vanish. It's in those wagons. It's in his engines. He's starving the wild to feed his army."

Beside her, Bram made a sound Petra had never heard from them—not grief exactly, and not rage, but the place where the two met and fused into something harder than either.

* * *

They followed the column for two days.

It was Bram's idea, which surprised Petra; she'd expected them to want to run, or worse, to want to do something reckless and half-formed with whatever small magics they still allowed themselves. Instead Bram had gone quiet in the way that Petra was beginning to understand meant *thinking*, and had said only: "If we're going to the Oathstone, we need to know what he knows. Columns like this have orders. Orders have officers. Officers talk."

So they followed, wide of the road, sleeping in ditches and culverts, moving when the column moved and holding still when it halted. Petra taught Bram the old scout-craft—the ward-keepers' craft, the one she'd been stripped of—and found, to her discomfort, that Bram took to it faster than any recruit she'd ever trained. Of course they did. Someone who could hear a fox thinking two fields away didn't need to learn awareness. They just needed permission to use it.

On the second evening, the column made camp in the ruins of a village that had, until recently, been called Thistlebrook. Petra knew the name from her ward-maps. She knew it more intimately now: the well had been filled with stones, the orchard burned to charcoal skeletons, and every doorway marked with the ash-grey sigil of requisition—which was the usurper's polite word for theft.

The campfires lit the valley floor like fallen stars, and Petra thought, with a bitterness that surprised her, that these were the only stars Aldric Vey intended anyone to see again.

"We should go around," she murmured. "We've seen enough. More than enough."

But Bram wasn't listening. Bram had gone rigid, head cocked, eyes fixed on the command wagon at the center of the camp.

"What?" Petra whispered. "What is it?"

"The horses," Bram said. "The officer's horses. They're not sewn-shut like the draft teams. They're *his*. And they're screaming." A pause. "Not loudly. Horses don't scream loudly when they've given up. But they're saying a name. Over and over. They're saying it the way animals say things they've been told never to say."

"What name?"

Bram turned to look at her, and in the dusk their face was unreadable, but their voice wasn't. It was careful, the way you're careful carrying something with a blade's edge.

"*Vane,*" they said. "They're calling for Vane."

The world tilted slightly. Petra put her hand flat on the cold ground and waited for it to steady.

"There's no Vane in that army," she said. "I made sure of that. When I was stripped of rank, my name was struck from the rolls. No one serves under—"

"Petra." Bram's voice was gentle, which was somehow worse. "The horses aren't calling your name. They're calling the name of the person who *used to be* Vane's. Someone they loved. Someone who left." Their throat worked. "Animals remember who was kind to them. Even after."

And then, because the world had not finished tilting, the tent flap of the command wagon opened, and a figure stepped out into the firelight, and Petra Vane looked down into the valley at the face of Captain Ilse Marren—her protégé, her best student, the girl she'd personally recommended for commission eleven years ago, the one she'd once said, in a moment of pride she now wanted to claw out of her own memory, *will be a better keeper than I ever was.*

Ilse stood at the wagon rail with a ledger open on her arm, dictating numbers to a clerk. Harvest tallies. Petra could read the shape of the work from here; she'd audited enough supply columns to know bookkeeping when she saw it, even when the goods being counted were the last voices of the wild.

"She was sixteen," Petra heard herself say. "When I took her on. She cried the first time she had to put down a rabid dog, and she thanked me afterward for making her do it herself. She said—" Petra stopped. Started again. "She believed in the wards. She believed in them the way you believe in water. You don't think about it. It's just *true*."

"And now she counts cages," Bram said softly.

"Now she counts cages."

Petra watched Ilse Marren close the ledger, say something to the clerk that made him laugh, and turn back toward the tent—and in the turning, the firelight caught her face full-on, and Petra saw what she would spend the rest of her life seeing: that Ilse was not hollow. She was not broken or coerced or secretly weeping into her pillow. She was *content*. She had found a duty that fit her hands, and she performed it well, and she slept soundly, and the horror of that was greater than any monster Petra had ever been sent to hunt.

Because Petra had trained her. Petra had taught her that duty was the highest thing, that obedience to office was its own virtue, that the report mattered more than the truth it contained. Ilse Marren had simply believed the lesson completely, without Petra's late-arriving doubts. She was what Petra would have become, if disgrace hadn't knocked her off the path.

"You blame yourself," Bram said. Not asking.

"I built her." Petra's jaw ached; she realized she'd been clenching it. "Every principle she's serving under, I handed her polished. Vey didn't corrupt her. He just gave her a bigger office."

"Then you know how she thinks." Bram's eyes had gone sharp, catching the distant firelight. "You know what she'll do next, what she won't do, what she can't refuse. Petra—you're the only person alive who can predict that woman, and she's commanding the column that stands between us and the Oathstone road."

Petra stared down at the fires, at the wagons, at the mute cargo breathing its stolen silence into copper vessels, and felt something shift in her chest that she recognized from the worst day of her life—the day she'd arrested Sela Coldharbour, the day she'd done her duty perfectly and known, even then, in the marrow-deep place beneath the drills and the doctrine, that she was on the wrong side of something enormous.

For twelve years she'd told herself the feeling was doubt. Doubt could be managed. Doubt could be filed in a report.

This was not doubt. This was direction.

"We're not going around," she said.

Bram blinked. "Petra—"

"The column moves north at dawn. North is the Oathstone road. Which means sooner or later, we cross it—or it crosses us." She was already pulling back from the ridgeline, low and smooth, scout-craft moving through her body like an old song. "And when it does, I want to be the one choosing the ground. Get your rats ready, Coldharbour. Tonight you're going to teach me everything the animals know about that camp—and tomorrow, we're going to show Captain Marren what happens to a lesson I taught badly."

Above them, in a sky still scoured of stars, the first thin cry of a caged thing rose from the valley—faint, wordless, and unmistakably, desperately human.

Bram heard it. So, for the first time in twelve years, did Petra.

She did not look away.
