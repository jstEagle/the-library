## 6. Sela's Cipher

The waystation had been a tollhouse once, back when the roads belonged to travelers instead of soldiers. Now its roof sagged like a tired animal and the hearth was black with twelve years of nothing, but the walls stood, and the door still barred from the inside, and after three days of sleeping in ditches with one ear open for hoofbeats, Bram would have called a stone box with a broken window *home*.

Petra built the fire small. She always built it small — cupped it in the old hearthstone like something she could smother in a heartbeat if she had to. Old habits of a woman who'd spent her career hunting exactly the kind of light they were making.

"Rats say the road's empty until dawn," Bram said, settling cross-legged on the floor. "A patrol passed at moonrise heading south. Two riders, bored, arguing about dice."

"You talked to rats about dice."

"I talked to rats about everything. Rats are very invested in everything." Bram pulled the journal out of their coat, where it had lived against their ribs since Petra first pressed it into their hands on the sewer stairs. It was heavier than a book that size should be. Grief usually is.

Petra looked at it the way she looked at most things: like she was deciding whether to apologize again. She'd apologized twice already for arresting Sela Voss. Bram had accepted neither apology, because acceptance felt like a door swinging open onto a room they weren't ready to enter.

"Take first watch," Bram said. "I'm going to read."

"Can you? Read it?"

Bram turned the journal over. The cover was oxblood leather gone soft as skin, stamped with no sigil, marked by no owner's name. Sela had been careful. Sela had taught Bram to be careful — *a gift unused is a gift unglimpsed, little wren; the world can't burn what it can't see.*

Twelve years of being unseen. And now here was her handwriting, and it wasn't hiding at all.

"No," Bram said honestly. "Not yet. But I will."

The cipher wasn't ink-deep. That was the first thing Bram understood when they finally opened the book by firelight and let their fingers rest on the first page. To any reader — to Petra, to a witch-finder, to Aldric Vey himself — the pages held a dull inventory. Feed stores. Boot counts. Toll ledgers from a dead road. The most boring book ever confiscated.

But under the ledger lines, in the spaces between the numbers, there were other marks. Faint ones. Not written so much as *pressed* — the ghost-pressure of a pen lifted between strokes, the indentations left where a hand had paused too long.

And those marks weren't letters at all.

They were sounds.

Bram went cold all over. Because they knew this language. They'd known it before they knew their own name. It was the speech of hares — that quick, breathless patter that ran beneath words the way water runs under ice. Sela had written her true journal in hare-speech, pressed into paper, invisible to every eye in the world except the eyes of someone who could *hear* it.

She hadn't hidden the journal from readers. She'd hidden it from everyone who couldn't listen.

"She wrote it for you," Petra said quietly, from the doorway. Bram hadn't heard her move. "Or for whoever came next."

"She wrote it for a beast-speaker," Bram corrected. Their voice came out strange. "There's only ever one at a time. You know that?"

"I didn't even know there was one at a time until I met you."

"There's always one." Bram traced the first pressed line, and the old language rose up through their fingertips like warmth through frozen ground. "One voice in each generation, born to speak for the wild. When one dies, the gift passes — nobody knows how, it just *moves*, like a bird leaving a dead tree. Sela used to say we're not people who have a gift. We're a gift that has people. One at a time."

"And if the gift has you now—"

"Then she's—" Bram stopped. Swallowed. Started again. "Then either she's dead, or she gave it away. And Sela Voss never gave anything away in her life except trouble."

* * *

Reading the journal took the whole night, and it took something else too — something Bram didn't have a name for until dawn grayed the broken window. Hare-speech was fast, giddy, all leaps and reversals, and Sela had written it the way she'd done everything: at a sprint, laughing at the cliff edge. Bram had to slow it down, translate the leaps into human thought, and every translated line landed in their chest like a stone dropped down a well.

The early entries were ordinary. Ward reports. Complaints about her knees. A joke about a mule named Duchess who could curse better than a dockworker — *and I never taught her a word of it, she learned it listening at tavern walls, which proves the wild hears more than we do and repeats the worst of it.*

Then, eleven years ago, the entries changed.

*I've been summoned to Coldmere. The new Lord Protector wants ward-keepers to report on "anomalous fauna." Anomalous fauna. He means speaking beasts. He means us. He asked me — me, by name — whether the covenant could be measured. Whether it could be cut. I told him the covenant isn't a rope, it's a conversation, and conversations don't survive being cut. He smiled like a man writing down my answer for later use.*

*I don't like him. I've liked few men less. There's a hole in him shaped like something he lost, and he's decided the whole world owes him a replacement.*

Bram read that line four times. Around them the waystation breathed — wind in the rafters, mice in the walls (the mice had opinions about the mice), Petra's slow deliberate breathing by the door as she pretended to sleep and listened to every word Bram murmured aloud without meaning to.

Then the entries darkened fast, the way weather does.

*He's cutting it. Gods help us, he's actually doing it. Not severing the covenant — you can't sever a conversation — but starving it. Every speaking beast his finders cage is a voice taken out of the chorus. The Oathstone doesn't hear the wild anymore because he's killing the wild's ability to speak. And the wards were never human magic. They were the covenant's shadow on our side of the border. Starve the conversation and the shadow dies. The stars going out — that's the wild holding its breath.*

*I refused to name my successor. He demanded it. Can you imagine — he understands the succession better than the free cities do, he knows the gift moves, he wants the next voice born inside his cages where he can raise it like a dog. I told him nothing. He put me in the citadel at Ashfall. The cells there are warded against speech itself — I can feel my gift going quiet the way a limb goes quiet under snow. I am writing this in the last week I'll be able to think clearly, pressing it into confiscated ledgers, hoping some clerk's lazy hands deliver it somewhere a listener might find it.*

*If you're reading this, little listener, then the gift has moved, and I'm sorry. I'm so sorry. Because of what comes next.*

Bram's hands had stopped moving. They realized it distantly, the way you realize your own heartbeat — from the outside.

*Here is what I could never tell the ward-keepers, what I kept even from Petra Vane, who was honest when none of them were and paid for it: the covenant needs two voices. One human, one wild. But the wild's voice is not one beast. It is ALL beasts. Every speaker who ever carried the gift carried a piece of the whole chorus, and the strongest of us — the ones born wrong, born too loud, born hearing hares and wolves and whales in the same breath — we carry almost all of it. The rite at the Oathstone requires a voice that can speak for every surviving beast AT ONCE. Not a piece. The whole.*

*I was strong enough, once. Nearly. My years in his cells are taking the rest.*

*So if the gift has moved to you, little listener, forgive me for what I'm about to say, because I chose you without meaning to and I know what it will cost:*

*You were never supposed to fit. You were supposed to be the keystone. The one who doesn't fit anywhere is the one the arch rests on.*

*Come to Ashfall. Or don't come to Ashfall and let the world go dark forever. Either way, know this: you were never broken. You were load-bearing.*

* * *

Bram didn't remember closing the journal. They remembered the sound it made when it hit the floor — a flat, final slap, like a door.

They were standing. They didn't remember standing either. The fire had burned to embers and the light through the broken window was full morning, and Petra was on her feet with a knife already drawn, because of course she was, because twelve years of witch-finders teaches a person to wake armed.

"Bram."

"They have her." Bram's voice came out scraped raw. They must have been talking in their sleep, or shouting. "Ashfall. The citadel. She's alive, Petra, she's *alive*, and he's keeping her because she wouldn't give him a name, because he wanted to breed the next voice in a cage like a — like a *dog* —"

Petra lowered the knife slowly. "Bram. Breathe."

"Don't tell me to breathe." Bram laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "You want to know what she wrote? What she pressed into these pages in hare-speech because she knew only one kind of creature in the whole world could ever read it?" They picked the journal up off the floor and held it out, and their hands were shaking. "She wrote it for me. Twelve years ago she wrote it for *me*. The covenant needs two voices, and the wild's voice isn't one beast, it's all of them, and the rite needs someone who can speak for every living thing at once. Every beast. All together. In one throat."

The silence after that was very long. Somewhere outside, a crow called — an actual call, actual speech, thin and frightened: *cold, cold, the sky is cold, where did the warm lights go.* Even the birds were asking.

"That's why you're different," Petra said. Not a question. She'd seen it before Bram had, maybe. Seen the way stray animals crowded toward them, the way Bram flinched at crowds not because of the people but because of the *chorus underneath* — dogs and rats and starlings all talking over each other in a noise no one else could hear. "That's why you hear everything."

"I hear everything," Bram whispered. "I've spent my whole life turning it DOWN. I built a life out of being half-deaf to myself. Charms for coin. Cats for company. Small. I made myself *small*, Petra, because the bigness scared me, and now it turns out the bigness is the point. The bigness is the *only thing that works*. She's right. I'm the keystone. I'm the thing the arch rests on, and keystones don't get to be small, keystones get to hold up the entire—"

Their voice broke. They sat down hard on the cold hearthstone, journal clutched to their chest like a shield, and for a while they couldn't do anything but shake.

Terror is a strange animal. It had worn many shapes in Bram's life — the witch-finder's knock, the fox bleeding out on the shop floor, the word *Oathstone* spoken in a dying voice. But this shape was the worst one yet, because it wasn't fear of dying. It was fear of being *chosen*. Of spending twelve years learning to disappear and discovering that disappearing had never once been possible — that the world had simply been waiting, patient as winter, for the loud thing inside them to become necessary.

Petra crouched in front of them. Close enough that Bram could see the exhaustion carved under her eyes, the old scar through her eyebrow, the stubborn set of a jaw that had already burned one commission and would clearly burn more.

"Ashfall is a fortress," she said. "Vey's seat. Ten thousand soldiers, warded cells, and the man himself."

"I know."

"Sela's in there."

"I know."

"So." Petra held out her hand — the same gesture, Bram realized, that had started all of this on the sewer stairs, an offer made to a stranger in the dark. "Do we go?"

Bram looked at the hand. Then past it, at the journal, at the last lines they hadn't said aloud, the ones still burning behind their ribs:

*You were never broken. You were load-bearing.*

Outside, the crow called again — *cold, cold* — and beneath it, faint and far and growing, Bram heard something they'd never heard before: beasts answering. Not words. Just voices, rising across the mute countryside like sparks catching, all of them saying the same thing in a hundred tongues.

*Coming. Coming. Coming.*

"Yes," Bram said, and took her hand. "We go to Ashfall."
