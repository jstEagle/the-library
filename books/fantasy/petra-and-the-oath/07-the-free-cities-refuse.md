## 7. The Free Cities Refuse

Marrowgate smelled of salt and rot, the way Petra remembered it from her first posting twelve years ago. The city clung to its cliffs like a barnacle, all grey stone and green copper roofs, its harbor choked with fishing boats that had stopped going far out to sea since the stars went out. Fishermen didn't like water that reflected nothing back at them.

Bram walked beside her through the Salt Gate, hood up, hands in their pockets, radiating the particular tension of someone trying very hard not to be noticed. Petra had learned to read them over the weeks on the road—the set of their shoulders when they heard something wild and wordless, the way their fingers twitched toward speech they couldn't afford. In Marrowgate, with witch-finders' notices pasted on every wall bearing a charcoal sketch that was only a poor likeness but a likeness nonetheless, not being noticed was survival.

"The council meets at dusk," she said. "We have until then to convince them."

"And if they won't be convinced?"

"Then we've lost nothing but time." She didn't believe that and knew Bram could hear the lie in her voice. Time was the one thing they didn't have. Sela's journal sat against Bram's ribs beneath their coat, and every day they delayed was another day Vey's harvest columns burned deeper into the wild.

The Council Hall of Marrowgate had been a lighthouse once, before the harbor silted and the city grew up around it. Its great chamber still bore the soot-scars of the old beacon fire, and beneath those scars hung the thing Petra had come to see: the Commission Wall, where the names of every ward-keeper sworn under the old covenant were carved into slate, rank by rank, posting by posting.

Her own name was there. *Petra Vane, Third Circle, Greyhollow District.* Struck through with a single chiseled line the day they stripped her rank—*for insubordination and falsification of reports*, the tribunal record said, though everyone in that room had known the truth was the opposite. She had refused to falsify. That was her crime. The wards were dying and she had put it in writing, and institutions do not forgive accurate reports.

She stood before the wall while the chamber filled around her, watching her own name like a scar she couldn't stop touching.

"You shouldn't be here," said a voice behind her—young, wary, familiar. "If the council sees you—"

"I'm the reason the council called an emergency session, Tam." She turned. Tam Wren had been nineteen when she'd last seen him, a gangly recruit who could raise a ward-lantern on his first try and apologized to doors he bumped into. He was twenty-one now, thinner, with the hollowed look of someone who had spent two years maintaining wards he knew were failing and writing reports saying otherwise. "You sent the request for aid. I answered it."

"We asked for a *consultant*. Not—" His eyes flicked to Bram, to the hood, to the wanted-notices' face made flesh. "Not this."

"This is exactly what you need," Petra said. "Whether you know it yet or not."

The session bell rang.

* * *

The Council of Marrowgate sat nine strong behind a crescent table of black driftwood, and Petra recognized four of them from her service years—merchant-princes and harbor-masters and one retired warden grown soft and rich on the peace the wards had bought. At the center sat First Speaker Odile Ferrance, a woman built like a ship's keel, whose reputation for pragmatism had reached even Greyhollow.

Pragmatism, it turned out, was another word for surrender.

"Vey's envoys arrived by fast courier three days ago," Ferrance said, without preamble, once the chamber doors closed. "His terms are generous. Marrowgate keeps its fleet, its markets, its self-governance in matters of trade. In exchange, we open our gates, register any persons of gifted blood, and accept garrison oversight."

"Generous," Bram repeated softly, from the gallery where Petra had insisted they sit. The word carried an edge that made two councilors look up.

"The alternative," Ferrance continued, "is what happened to Coldmere. You've seen the reports. A city of forty thousand, and after Vey's war-engines finished with it, a smoking hole. We have no wards. The covenant is broken—we all felt it break, that night the sky went black. Without the wards we cannot fight him. Therefore we do not fight him."

Petra stepped forward into the well of the chamber. "First Speaker. May I address the council?"

"You may address us as a private citizen," Ferrance said coolly. "Your standing was revoked. You know that better than anyone."

"My standing was revoked because I told the truth about the wards." Petra let her voice fill the old lighthouse the way the beacon-fire once had. "And I'm going to tell you more truths now, and you can revoke whatever else you like afterward. One: Vey did not merely benefit from the covenant breaking. He broke it. Deliberately. With engines and rites I have seen the wreckage of with my own eyes. Two: the power he harvested from the wild is finite. It burns hot and it burns fast, and when it runs low he will need more, and the terms he offers you today will not be the terms he honors tomorrow. Three—"

"Three," interrupted Councilor Hask, the retired warden, "you will tell us you can restore the wards. Every disgraced keeper says so. It's how they beg their way back inside the walls."

"I don't want back inside your walls." The words came out of her harder than she intended, and truer than she'd planned. "I want you to understand that your walls are already down. The border wards fell the night the stars went out. What you're maintaining in your lantern-towers is theater—residual charge, guttering. I've read the decay curves. You have perhaps a season of flickering light left, and you're spending it negotiating with the man who snuffed the sky."

Silence. Somewhere above, gulls cried against the glass of the dome.

Ferrance leaned forward. "And your alternative, Keeper Vane? Since you're so free with truths. What would you have us do? March out and die loudly instead of quietly?"

"There is a way to reforge the covenant. There is a place—the Oathstone—and there are people working toward it right now, at cost of everything, while this council drafts surrender terms with comfortable margins."

"A fable," Hask snapped. "The Oathstone is a story grandmothers tell. The covenant needed two voices, human and wild, and the wild has been silent for twelve years. Who speaks for the wild now?"

Petra looked back at the gallery. At Bram.

Bram had gone very still, the stillness of prey deciding whether to bolt. And Petra saw the exact moment they chose—not to speak, not to reveal themselves, but to shake their head, small and sharp. *Not here. Not like this.*

She turned back to the council alone.

"They're right," she said, and something in her chest tore along an old seam as she said it. "The wild has been silent. But silence isn't death. And I have walked through that silence for six weeks, and I have heard things in it that this council should pray it never has to hear. I came here asking Marrowgate to stand. You've asked me instead to help you kneel politely. So I'll leave you with the last truth I have: Aldric Vey does not want your gates. He wants your children—the gifted ones, the ones you'll hand over in your registration lists. You aren't buying peace. You're filling his quota."

She unclasped the case at her belt.

Inside, folded into eighths, was her commission—the actual vellum, signed by the Warden-General, the document she had served under for ten years and been stripped of for two. She had carried it through disgrace and exile and sewers and burning hedgerows because some part of her had always believed the institution could be redeemed, that duty was a place you could return to.

Standing in the chamber of the institution she had loved, listening to it negotiate away children, she understood at last that duty had never lived in the paper. It lived in what you refused to do.

She held the commission high, and the nearest lantern-flame caught it, and she let it burn.

The vellum curled and blackened between her fingers. She held on until the fire kissed her skin, then let the last corner fall, a scrap of ash drifting down onto the black driftwood table like a dead moth.

"Petra Vane," she said, "Third Circle, resigns. From everything."

* * *

The uproar lasted eleven minutes. She counted.

Guards moved toward her; Tam Wren moved faster, planting himself in their path with a ward-lantern raised—not charged, just raised, the gesture itself an argument. Ferrance called order twice, failed, and finally dismissed the session with a gesture like a woman cutting a mooring line.

In the corridor outside, Bram fell into step beside her, hood thrown back now, done hiding in this building at least. "That was either the bravest or stupidest thing I've ever watched."

"It can be both."

"You burned your commission." Bram's voice caught on something. "You loved that commission. You told me—on the road, the second night—you told me what being a ward-keeper meant to you. That it was the first time you'd ever belonged anywhere."

"It was." Petra flexed her burned fingers. The pain was clean, clarifying. "And I kept belonging to it long after it stopped deserving me. That's the trap, isn't it? Belonging. You'll hold onto the wrong thing forever because it's the only thing that ever held you." She glanced sideways at them. "I watched you do it for six weeks. Staying small. Staying hidden. Belonging to your own invisibility because it was safer than belonging to anything that could take you."

Bram said nothing, but their jaw tightened, and Petra knew the words had landed somewhere true.

They had nearly reached the Salt Gate when running footsteps echoed behind them—two sets, then five, then more. Tam Wren rounded the corner first, breathless, and behind him came four others in ward-keeper grey: a stocky girl with a lantern-scarred forearm, twin brothers barely shaving age, an older woman with a tribunal pin torn off her collar.

Tam sketched the old salute—fist to heart, fingers opening outward, the sign of the open gate. "Eight of us, total. Three couldn't get clear; they'll follow by the coast road. We heard what the council voted after you left." His young face was grey. "They accepted the terms. Registration lists start tomorrow. They're handing over names, Captain."

"Not captain," Petra said. "There's no corps to captain anymore."

"Then what do we call you?"

She looked at them—five young faces lit by lantern-light, frightened and certain in the way only the young can manage, having chosen her the way she had once chosen an institution that betrayed them both. Behind them, through the arch of the Salt Gate, the sea lay black and starless, and beyond the sea, eastward, the road ran toward the citadel where Sela waited in a cage, and beyond that, the Oathstone.

Something truer than rank settled over her shoulders, weightless and heavy at once.

"Follow me," she said. "That's all. Just follow me."

They went out through the Salt Gate in the dark, eight ward-keepers and one beast-speaker and one woman with burned fingers and no commission at all, and behind them Marrowgate's lanterns flickered along the walls like a promise the city no longer intended to keep.

They made camp that night in the ruins of an old signal tower, and Petra took the first watch, looking east. Toward the citadel. Toward Sela.

Toward the man who had taken everything from her twice—her mentor, her institution, her belief—and who did not yet know that everything he'd left her was walking toward him with a fire still in its hands.

Bram joined her at the parapet, silent a long moment, watching the same black horizon.

"They'll come for us now," Bram said. "Marrowgate's lists. Our names will be on them."

"Yes."

"Are you afraid?"

Petra thought about it honestly, the way she was learning to think about everything since the night the stars went out.

"No," she said. "For the first time in twelve years—I'm not."

Far to the east, on the horizon where no stars shone, a light kindled that was not a star: red, pulsing, patient. The beacon of Vey's citadel, answering the dark.

It had seen them coming.
