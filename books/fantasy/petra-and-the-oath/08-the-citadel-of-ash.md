## 8. The Citadel of Ash

The citadel did not look like a fortress. It looked like a wound that had learned to stand upright.

Bram saw it first from the ridge above the ash-plain, where the defectors crouched in the bracken and Petra traced the walls with one finger against her knee. Black stone, windowless on the lower floors, and around its base a moat not of water but of burned ground — a hundred yards of nothing, as if the earth itself had been scraped of every root and worm. No birds crossed it. Even the wind seemed to lose its voice over that dead ring, arriving at the walls flattened and dumb.

"They call it the Harvest Yard," Petra said quietly. "Everything the covenant fed on, he raked out and burned. The wild can't touch this place. Nothing living can."

"Then how do we get in?"

Petra's mouth curved, and it was not a kind expression. "The same way everything gets into a fortress. As cargo."

* * *

The grain wagons came up from the south at dusk, three of them, rolling through gates wide enough for siege engines. Bram rode in the second, buried to the chin in barley sacks, with Sela's cipher-journal pressed flat against their ribs like a second heartbeat. Above them, sitting openly on the sacks with a conscript's dull cap pulled low, sat Wren Ashfall — nineteen years old, a ward-keeper's daughter from Marrowgate, and the best liar Bram had ever met.

"Remember," Wren murmured through the side of her mouth, "if they search, you're a runaway tithe-boy I'm smuggling for coin. I found you. I own you. Cry if you have to."

"I don't cry."

"Everyone cries in a barley sack." She said it without malice, and Bram understood she was right, and hated it.

The gate guards barely looked at the wagons. That was the horror of it — the usurper's own men were bored here, bored at the center of the thing that was unmaking the world, because Aldric Vey had built his citadel on certainty. The wards above the gate hummed with stolen power, thick and greasy, and Bram felt them pass over the wagon like a hand feeling for warmth and finding none. Beasts had no warmth here. Neither, apparently, did smugglers.

They rolled into the inner yard. Bram slipped from the sacks while Wren argued loudly about her pay, and folded themselves into the shadow between a granary and the wall, exactly where Petra's map — drawn from memory of a citadel she'd once inspected and been refused entry to — said the old servant stairs began.

The plan was simple, which meant it would go wrong in an interesting way. Petra and the defectors would make noise at the east wall at moonrise — fires, shouting, a feint at the harvest-vaults where Vey kept the caged beasts. Every guard who could walk would run east. Bram would go west, down, into the deep cells, and bring Sela out.

Simple. Bram whispered it to themselves like a charm all the way down the stairs: *simple, simple, simple.*

* * *

The cells were older than the citadel. Vey had built his black towers on the bones of something else — Bram could see it in the walls, older masonry beneath newer, and carved into the oldest stones, half-chiseled away, the faint spiral marks of oath-work. He had built his empire on top of the covenant's grave and called it a foundation.

Bram counted doors. Twelve. Thirteen. At the fourteenth, a smell stopped them — lavender gone stale, and under it the particular scent of a person who has stopped expecting anything.

"Sela?" Their voice cracked on the name.

Silence. Then, from behind the door, dry as paper: "If you're a witch-finder playing games, I'll tell you nothing new. You've taken everything worth telling."

"It's me. It's Bram. From Greyhollow — you taught me the night-songs when I was small. You said—" Bram's throat closed. They pressed their forehead to the cold iron. "You said the foxes knew my name before I did."

A long silence. Then a sound Bram had never heard from Sela Voss, who had once stared down a ward-keeper tribunal without blinking: a small, broken laugh.

"The foxes," Sela said. "Oh, child. Open the lock, then. Third pin from the left is rusted; be gentle with it."

Bram had never picked a lock in their life. But the rats had followed them down from the yard — the citadel's rats, thin and desperate and still, barely, able to speak — and they told Bram the tumbler's shape in squeaks and whisker-touches, and Bram's hands translated. The lock opened like a held breath released.

Sela Voss in the flesh was smaller than the legend. White-haired, stooped, wrists scarred from twelve years of iron. Her eyes were the same, though — sharp grey, and they went straight past Bram's face to the journal-shaped bulge at their ribs.

"You read it," she said. Not a question.

"Some of it. Enough."

"Then you know what you are."

"I know what I'm afraid of being."

Sela reached out and took Bram's face in both scarred hands, and her grip was stronger than her body suggested. "Listen to me. Fear is just the gift knocking on the wrong door. We'll open the right one together. Now get me out of here, because I have spent twelve years listening to the walls, and I know exactly where Aldric Vey keeps his heart."

* * *

They almost made it. Of course they almost made it.

The diversion roared to life on schedule — orange light bloomed against the east windows, horns, screaming, the drum-beat of boots overhead — and Bram got Sela up two flights of the servant stairs before the corridor ahead filled with torchlight and a single figure walked toward them unhurried, alone, his guards held back by a raised hand.

Aldric Vey was taller than his portraits and much more ordinary. Grey-cloaked, grey-eyed, a man you'd pass in a market without a second look — except for his left arm, which ended at the elbow in a smooth cap of pale scar tissue, and except for the way the air around him was utterly silent. No rats in the walls near him. No insects. Bram realized they could hear their own blood.

"So," Vey said. His voice was quiet, almost gentle. "The second voice comes to me on its own feet. Sela, you never named your successor, but you didn't need to. I've known since the stars went out. Something answered the silence, out there in the dark. Something small and stubborn."

"Vey." Sela stepped forward, placing herself half in front of Bram, an old teacher's reflex. "Twelve years I've wondered what could make you do it. Sever the covenant. Burn the wild's half of the world. And now I see your arm, and I understand, and I want you to know that understanding changes nothing."

"Don't." For the first time his voice frayed. "Don't look at me like I'm a monster to be studied."

"What are you, then?"

He looked at his ruined arm. When he spoke again, it was to Bram, not to her, as if she had already stopped mattering.

"I was like you. Before the purges, before the Oath was even outlawed — I was a boy in the fen-country who talked to herons. Do you know what the wild asks in return for its friendship? Everything. Your privacy. Your hiding places. There is no *quiet* inside a beast-speaker's head; the whole world leans on you wanting things. I loved it. Gods help me, I loved it, and I told one person. One person I trusted." His jaw worked. "She sold me to the first witch-finder who paid. They took the arm — the arm that touched the herons last, they said, the guilty limb — and they burned the gift out of me with hot iron and prayer. And the wild watched. Do you understand? The wild *watched*, and did nothing, because the covenant bound it to neutrality. Bound it to silence while they maimed me."

Bram's fury rose like floodwater — and then caught, snagged on something underneath. Because they knew. They knew what it was to hide, to flinch from every knock, to shrink a life down small enough to survive. Vey had simply gone the other direction down the same road. Had decided that if he could not have the gift, no one would, and had spent twenty years building an engine to make the whole world as silent and safe and dead as his arm.

"You broke the covenant," Bram said slowly, "because it failed you."

"I broke it because it was a lie." Spittle flew now; the gentleness was gone. "Two voices, equal halves, mutual protection — where was its half of the bargain the day they took my arm? Where was the wild's great loyalty? It stood aside. So I severed it, and I fed its power into war-magic that answers to *me*, that will never abandon me, and when the last speaking thing is caged or culled, no child anywhere will ever again be what I was. No child will ever lean on a world that won't defend them. I am not destroying the wild, beast-speaker. I am *protecting* everyone from it."

"You're protecting them from ever belonging," Bram said. "You're doing to the whole world what they did to you. You cut off everyone's arm."

For a moment — just a moment — something moved behind Vey's eyes that was not triumph or madness. It was recognition, and it was grief, and it was twelve years old and still bleeding.

Then boots thundered in the stairwell below, and the moment closed like water over a stone.

"Take them both to the harvest-vaults," Vey said to his guards, turning away. "The woman can watch the ritual fail. And the beast-speaker—" he paused at the corner, and did not look back— "the beast-speaker I want alive. Whatever answers the Oathstone, I want to hear it beg the wild for help and hear the wild say nothing. Just as it said nothing to me."

They dragged Sela and Bram down through the burning citadel, past windows full of Petra's fires, and Bram fought the whole way — not to escape, but to memorize. The route to the vaults. The number of guards. The place where the wall-stones changed from black to old grey oath-work, where the citadel's foundations were thinnest.

Because Sela had whispered it against their ear as they were seized, so softly only a beast-speaker could have caught it under the shouting:

*"The stone is below us, child. And the stone has been waiting twelve years for someone loud enough."*

* * *

The harvest-vaults were beneath everything, deeper than the cells, a cavern where the air itself felt skinned. Cages lined the walls in tiers — hawks, wolves, a bear with a muzzle of cold iron, a fox with eyes like Bram's own reflection — dozens of them, every speaking beast Vey's armies had taken alive, brought here to be drained at the ritual. The silence in that room was not absence. It was weight. It pressed on the chest like deep water.

And at the center, on a plinth of black glass, sat the thing Vey had torn from the world's heart: a shard of the old covenant, drinking light and giving none back.

Bram was thrown into a cage. Sela into another. The doors boomed shut, and above them, muffled through tons of stone, the battle for the east wall raged on — Petra holding her line, buying hours with fire.

Sela's fingers found Bram's through the bars. Old hands, scarred hands, steady as roots.

"They think they've won," she whispered. "Good. Frightened men gloat, and gloating men monologue, and meanwhile—" her grey eyes flicked to the fox in the neighboring cage, then to Bram, and the ghost of her old teaching-smile surfaced through the ruin of her face— "meanwhile, you and I have a vault full of students, and one lesson left to teach."

Beyond the walls, far above, the false stars wheeled in a sky that had been empty for twelve years.

By dawn, they would not be.
