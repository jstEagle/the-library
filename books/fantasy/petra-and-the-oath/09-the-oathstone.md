## 9. The Oathstone

**Bram**

The Oathstone was smaller than Bram had imagined. Twelve years of whispered legends had built it into a mountain, a cathedral, a thing that scraped the sky — and here it was, a grey monolith no taller than a man, standing alone in a bowl of dead grass at the heart of the wild. Frost clung to its flanks even though the season was wrong for frost. It looked like a grave marker.

Around its base, carved so deep the grooves still held shadow after centuries, ran two spirals of script. One wound clockwise. One wound counter. Human hand and wild hand, meeting and never touching, an inch of stone between them all the way around.

"Two voices," Sela said. She stood with her palm flat against the stone, and Bram saw how thin her wrist had gotten in Vey's citadel, how the tendons stood out like roots through soil. "One to speak for the realms of humankind. One to speak for everything else." Her eyes found Bram's, and there was something in them Bram had never seen from her before — not in the old days, not in the shop, not once in twelve years of careful silence. Fear. "I need you to stay back, little wren. Whatever happens."

"You said you could do this."

"I said I would try." Sela turned back to the stone. "The gift isn't muscle, Bram. It's breath. And they took mine a lungful at a time, every year, every interrogation, every—" She stopped herself. Pressed her forehead briefly to the cold granite. "Stand behind me. That's all I ask."

Behind them, down the slope where the bowl of dead grass met the treeline, Petra was shouting orders in a voice that carried like a whipcrack. Forty-one defectors. Two ward-keeper veterans who'd walked out of Marrowgate with their commissions in their fists. A handful of free-city volunteers armed with hunting bows and fury. Against whatever Vey had left, which by Petra's last count was three thousand spears and a war-mage corps that drank stolen wild-power like wine.

"They're coming," Petra called up. No panic in it. Just fact, delivered the way she delivered everything, as if dread were a supply inventory. "An hour. Maybe less."

"Then we have an hour," Sela said, and began the rite.

* * *

**Petra**

Petra had held lines before. She'd held the river line at Cael Ford with eleven ward-keepers against a raiding company, and she'd held the gate at Greyhollow during the bread riots, and she had learned one truth about holding: you did not hold ground. You held people. Ground was just where they happened to be standing.

So she put the bow-armed volunteers on the eastern rise where the terrain funneled attackers into a kill-lane. She put the two veterans with her at the center, where the pressure would come hardest, because they were the only ones who wouldn't break when the war-mages started singing. She gave the youngest ones the rear guard and told them, plainly, that running was permitted and survival was the order.

And then she waited, watching the treeline, listening to Sela's voice rise behind her in words that predated every language Petra had ever been taught.

The first horn sounded forty minutes later. The second answered it from the north, out of position, which meant Vey wasn't sending his column — he was surrounding them. Of course he was. He didn't need to win here. He needed only to keep anyone from finishing what Sela had started.

They came out of the trees at dusk, and the sight of them stopped even Petra's breath for half a second. Not because of the numbers. Because of the cages. Great iron wagons rolled among the infantry, and in the cages — wolves, elk, a bear with its muzzle strapped shut, hawks with their eyes hooded — the usurper's harvest, dragged to the Oathstone so that no voice of the wild would be within reach of the rite. So that the covenant, if it reforged, would reforge over corpses.

"Hold," Petra said, and drew her blade, and the world narrowed to the width of the kill-lane.

The war-mages sang first. Petra felt it hit the ward-line she'd thrown up — a borrowed, ugly pressure, power that had been ripped out of living things and wore the wrongness like a smell. Her wards held. Barely. Cracks spidered through the light, and beside her, the veteran Corvin grunted and spat blood.

"They're feeding off those cages," he snarled. "Every beast in there is a battery."

"Then we get to the cages," Petra said, and led the countercharge herself, because if you asked people to go somewhere you had never gone first, you deserved to lose them.

* * *

**Bram**

Bram watched Petra charge and understood, with terrible clarity, that they were about to watch her die.

The rite was failing. That was the other thing, the thing underneath everything, the thing Bram's whole body knew before their mind let it form a word. Sela's voice rose and fell and rose again, and the stone drank the sound and gave back nothing. The carved spirals stayed dark. Frost crawled. Once, a single rune flickered — a pulse of gold, there and gone, like a heart skipping — and Sela staggered as though something had reached out of the rock and taken blood.

"Again," Bram heard her whisper. "Again. Again—"

The third attempt broke her. Sela went down on her knees in the dead grass, palms pressed to the stone, and the sound she made was not pain exactly. It was grief with nowhere left to go.

"It's not enough," she said. "My voice. They thinned it. Twelve years, Bram, they thinned it, and I can't — the stone needs a voice that carries the whole wild, every throat at once, and mine is—" She looked up, and her face was the face of someone asking permission to die. "There's no one else. There was never going to be anyone else. I'm sorry. I'm so sorry. I didn't know until now that it was you, but some part of me knew, and I brought you here anyway—"

"Sela." Bram knelt beside her. Put their hand over hers on the freezing stone. "Stop apologizing. Start telling me what to do."

Sela stared at them. And then, slowly, she laughed — a cracked, astonished sound — and pressed both their hands harder against the spiral carvings.

"Speak," she said. "Not as yourself. Let them speak. All of them. Every beast that remembers. Don't hold anything back, do you hear me? Not your fear, not your name, not your hiding. The oath takes everything or nothing."

Below, the battle was collapsing. Petra's line bent. War-mage fire lit the dusk green and wrong. And somewhere in the chaos, in a cage wagon being cut open by a veteran's axe, a wolf lifted its head and howled — and Bram heard it not as sound but as speech, desperate and clear:

*Let us help you.*

Bram closed their eyes. Stopped hiding. For the first time since they were nine years old, since the night magic was outlawed and their mother taught them that speaking to cats was a death sentence, Bram opened every door inside themselves at once.

It was like drowning in reverse.

The voices came up through the stone, through their hands, through the soles of their feet buried in the dead grass — hundreds of them, thousands, the wolf and the elk from the road and the rats of the sewers and the stray cats of Greyhollow and hawks wheeling over battlefields and salmon in dark rivers and the last old bear dreaming in the far mountains, every surviving beast that remembered the covenant, all of them speaking through Bram's single small body, and it should have torn them apart. It nearly did. Their ribs creaked like a ship's hull. Their mind stretched past its own edges until *Bram* was a word spoken in a language much larger than themselves.

And then the stone caught fire.

Not flame. Light — gold and green braided together, pouring up through the twin spirals, human-script and wild-script blazing at last into contact, the inch of separation between them burning away. The frost exploded off the monolith in a ring of steam. The dead grass beneath Bram's knees turned green so fast it seemed to be growing in fast-forward, seasons cycling in seconds.

Through their own mouth — through mouths that were no longer only theirs — Bram spoke the old words and new ones together, and the sky heard.

* * *

**Petra**

Petra was on one knee in the mud with a war-mage's hex burning through her shield-arm when the stars came back.

She would remember it always as a sound before a sight — a vast, collective inhale, as if the whole world had been holding its breath for twelve years and finally let it go. Then light. Every star overhead reigniting at once, hard and white and close, and the green wrongness of the war-mages' power guttering like candles in a gale.

The beasts came out of the dark.

The caged ones first — freed, unbound, their voices suddenly *voices*, roaring words in languages older than any city. Then the wild beyond the treeline, waking all at once, a tide of eyes and movement and speech flowing downhill toward the battle. The infantry broke. Three thousand men trained to fight armies turned and fled a forest that had started talking to them.

Petra got to her feet. Looked up at the Oathstone, blazing like a second dawn, and at the figure standing before it wrapped in light and every voice at once — and felt something crack open in her chest that had been sealed since the day she'd arrested Sela Coldharbour and told herself it was duty.

"Bram," she whispered. "Hold on. Hold on, I'm coming—"

She ran uphill through the green grass, through the drifting embers of a broken army, toward the voice that could never hide again.

* * *

**Bram**

When it ended, Bram was on their knees in grass that smelled of summer, and the stars were burning overhead, and every voice had withdrawn except one — their own, strange and enormous now, echoing faintly with wolf and hawk and salmon-deep river-speech. They could feel the wild in every direction, awake and aware and *listening*.

Sela was weeping, laughing, gripping their shoulders. Somewhere below, Petra was climbing the hill toward them, sword still drawn, blood on her face, alive.

But at the edge of the battlefield, where the fleeing columns parted around a lone rider on a black horse, Aldric Vey sat unmoving, staring up at the reignited sky. His maimed hand hung at his side. And as the last echo of the covenant faded from the air, he slid from his saddle to his knees in the new grass — and bowed his head.

Toward the stone. Toward Bram.

And across the valley, ten thousand soldiers stopped running, and turned, and waited to see what the voice of the wild would say next.
