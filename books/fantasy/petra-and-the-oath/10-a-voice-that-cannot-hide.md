## 10. A Voice That Cannot Hide

The silence after the stars came back was the loudest thing Bram had ever heard.

They stood on the Oathstone's crown with their hands still raised, their throat raw from a voice that had not been only theirs, and below them the battlefield simply... stopped. Ten thousand soldiers of Aldric Vey's host stood in the ash and trampled heather with their weapons half-lifted, staring upward at a sky that had been black for twelve years and now burned with more stars than any of them had ever seen. Somewhere in the ranks a man began to weep. Then another. The sound spread like fire through dry grass.

Bram's knees buckled. Petra caught them before they hit the stone.

"I've got you," she said, and her voice cracked on it. "I've got you."

"You should see the sky," Bram managed. Their own voice sounded strange now—thinner, human, but underneath it something vast still echoed, like a cathedral after the choir has gone home. "Petra. You should—"

"I see it." She was crying without seeming to notice. "I see it."

Below, the wild was waking. It came up out of the ground like water finding its old channels: foxes trotting openly between the frozen soldiers, elk stepping out of the treeline with their antlers silvered by starlight, crows wheeling overhead in patterns that were unmistakably language now, unmistakably *speech*. A wolf sat down ten feet from a Vey conscript who had spent his whole life fearing exactly that, and the wolf yawned, and the boy dropped his spear and laughed—a broken, astonished laugh—and the wolf laughed back, or something very like it.

Bram felt all of it. That was the price and the gift both. Every waking creature rang through them like a struck bell, and they could no more shut it out than they could shut out their own heartbeat. They had spoken for every beast alive, and so every beast lived now in the corner of their ear, forever.

*It doesn't hurt,* said an old voice in their mind—not Sela, but the wounded elk from the Road of Quiet Beasts, whose name Bram finally knew now, whose name was *Harrow-of-Winters*. *You thought it would hurt forever. It doesn't. It only hurts when you try to be alone.*

"Okay," Bram whispered, to the elk, to Petra, to themselves. "Okay."

* * *

Aldric Vey surrendered without a sword drawn.

He came up the hill alone at dawn, unarmored, his war-magic guttering out around him like a lamp running dry—the stolen power of the severed covenant bleeding away now that the covenant itself was whole again. He walked past Petra's line of defectors, past Sela standing straight-backed despite her stolen years, past the wolves and crows who watched him with ancient patience, and stopped at the base of the Oathstone.

Bram looked down at him and heard everything at once: the man's ragged breath, the hum of the stone, the distant chatter of sparrows, the low tide of their own doubled voice.

"You were one of us," Bram said. Not a question. They had seen it in him last night, in the citadel, in the moment before the rite—seen the maimed place where his gift had been cut out of him, and the twelve years of fury grown over the wound like bark over a broken branch.

"I was a child who talked to horses," Vey said. His voice was flat, emptied out. "And my father's priests held me down and burned it out of me, because a boy who speaks with beasts cannot inherit a throne. So I made certain no one would ever have what I lost." He looked up, and his eyes found the new stars, and something in his face finally broke—not into rage, but into something older and tireder. "And then you stood there and spoke with every living thing at once, and I understood that I never broke anything. I only delayed it. The wild keeps its appointments."

"The free cities will want you tried," Petra said, coming to stand beside Bram. Her hand found Bram's without either of them deciding it. "There will be a trial. There should be."

"There will," Vey agreed. "Do you know what I heard, ward-keeper, when your beast-speaker spoke? Not an army. Not a weapon." He shook his head slowly. "I heard every horse I ever loved, saying my name. Take me to your cities. Let them do what they will. But put me somewhere I can hear them."

Sela stepped forward then, old and thin and luminous, and looked at her former captor for a long moment. Whatever passed between them, she kept it behind her teeth. "Bind his hands," she said at last. "Gently. Even he is part of the oath now. Even him."

* * *

The weeks that followed were stranger than the battle, as weeks after miracles always are.

The armies scattered the way frost scatters in morning sun—not defeated, simply *melted*, men walking home across country that spoke to them as they went. Some fled from the talking animals. Most, Bram learned, did not. There was a hunger in people, it turned out, older than the outlawry, older than the fear: a hunger to be answered by the world. Twelve years of silence had not killed it. Nothing had killed it.

The free cities convened what they called the Concord at Marrowgate, and for the first time in history the speaking wild sent delegates: Harrow-of-Winters for the elk-herds, a crow named Ninefold-Tongue for the birds, a river-otter who spoke for the waters and refused, pointedly, to use anyone's titles. The councils squirmed. The councils bargained. The councils discovered, to their horror, that the beasts negotiated better than they did.

Sela went where she was needed most, which was everywhere. She stood before the Concord and taught. She walked the roads and found gifted children hiding in cellars and haylofts, children who had spent their whole lives whispering to cats and crows and hoping no one heard, and she knelt down to them and said the words Bram had waited twelve years to hear anyone say aloud:

"Your gift is not a curse. It is half of an oath. Come and learn the other half."

She had offered Bram a place beside her as teacher. Bram had considered it—honestly, painfully considered it—and then looked at Petra, and at the road south, and at a falling-down waystation with good bones and a wide yard, and made a different choice.

"Not Greyhollow," Bram had said. "Not a city. Out here, where the wild can sit in on the lessons."

* * *

The school had no name yet, though the children had already given it three, and argued about them daily.

It stood a day's walk from the Oathstone, in the shell of the old waystation where Bram had cracked Sela's cipher with shaking hands. They'd rebuilt the roof themselves, Petra hauling beams while Bram directed operations from atop a ladder, arguing cheerfully about load-bearing walls with a family of martens who had opinions. The first students arrived before the roof was even finished: a badger-speaker girl from Marrowgate who flinched at loud voices; twin brothers from the river country who spoke to fish and hadn't spoken to people in years; a silent boy of maybe nine who turned out not to speak to beasts at all, but to stones, which nobody had known was possible, and which delighted Sela so much she stayed a full month just to begin teaching him.

Bram's voice carried now. That was the truth they lived inside. When they spoke, every animal within a mile heard, and most of them listened, and word traveled outward from there in rings. There was no more slipping through markets unnoticed. No more harmless charms sold to strangers who never looked twice. A sparrow on the fence would announce them to the next county. Children pointed. Adults stared—some with fear, most, increasingly, with something else.

On the first day of term, Bram had stood before eleven students with their heart hammering, certain the old dread would come back, the craving to be small.

Instead they had opened their mouth and heard the yard answer: hens, martens, a curious elk at the treeline, a crow on the ridgepole saying *begin, begin, begin.*

"Good morning," Bram had said, loudly, to all of them. "My name is Bram Coldharbour, and I am going to teach you the other half of your voice."

* * *

Autumn came gold to the valley. On an evening in late harvest, Bram sat on the schoolhouse steps with Petra's shoulder warm against theirs, watching the students' lanterns bob in the yard as they said goodnight to the animals—each child kneeling to some creature or other, trading the day's last words.

"Tired?" Petra asked.

"Constantly." Bram laughed. "I hear everything now. Did you know hedgehogs gossip? Did you know *how much* hedgehogs gossip?"

"I did, actually. You told me last week. And the week before."

"Hedgehogs bear repeating."

Petra smiled and leaned her head against theirs. Below in the yard, the badger-speaker girl—Wren, fearless Wren, who no longer flinched—was teaching the stone-boy to whistle to starlings, and the starlings were coming down out of the dark in a spiral, dozens of them, whistling back. Sela's old journal lay open on Bram's knee, its cipher long since translated, its last page bearing a single line in Sela's hand that Bram read again now, as they did most evenings:

*A covenant is not a wall. It is a conversation. Keep talking.*

Down the valley, toward Marrowgate, a rider was coming up the road with a lantern swinging—too fast for evening, too urgent for harvest. Beside the rider loped a grey wolf, and even at this distance Bram could feel the wolf's message arriving ahead of it, ringing up through the wild and into their chest like a bell struck far away:

*Riders from the northern holds. Old blood, old pacts, oaths of their own that predate yours. They say the new covenant was made without them. They say they are coming to argue.*

Petra felt Bram go still. "What is it?"

Bram watched the lantern light grow closer, and felt the whole listening world lean in around them, and found—to their own surprise—that they were smiling.

"Company," they said. "The wild keeps its appointments. Apparently, so does everyone else."

They rose together and went down into the yard to meet it, loudly, and were not afraid.
