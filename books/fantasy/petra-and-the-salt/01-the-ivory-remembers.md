## 1. The Ivory Remembers

The bone had been soaking in lye water for three days, and still it smelled faintly of the child.

August Villalobos knew better than to hurry that part. Bone held what flesh let go of too easily. Flesh forgot, or pretended to; bone remembered everything, patiently, the way snow remembers every footstep until spring. So they changed the water each morning, murmured the small apology their grandmother's people used to murmur over anything taken from a body—*forgive the taking, forgive the making, forgive the hands*—and waited for the smell to fade into something clean and mineral, like chalk, like winter air in a high place.

It never faded entirely. August had stopped pretending it did.

The workshop was a single room above a cooper's shop on Tallow Lane, chosen precisely because no one chose Tallow Lane. The cooper below was half-deaf and wholly incurious. The window faced a brick wall furred with moss, so that even August's lamplight stayed private. On the bench lay the tools of a modest trade: knives of varying temper, rasps fine as insect legs, a bow drill, beeswax, linseed oil, and a shallow dish of river stones worn smooth, which served no purpose except that August liked to hold one when the work went badly. There were carvings everywhere—a shelf of buttons, a rack of combs, a small herd of bone animals meant for the market stall—all of them ordinary, all of them deliberately dull, because dull work kept the guild assessors uninterested and the tax collectors bored and the neighbors from wondering why the bone-carver on Tallow Lane worked with the shutters closed.

August had spent eleven years being nobody in Lowmark. It was the finest achievement of their life.

The whistle itself would be simple: a fipple flute, six finger-holes, carved from the femur of a girl named Wren Halloway, who had drowned in the millrace in the last week of harvest, aged nine. Her mother had come to the shop four days after the burial, before the frost could set the ground hard enough to make exhumation impossible, and she had not wept. That was how August knew the woman had been saving up her coin for months, maybe years. Grief that has been budgeted is grief that means business.

"A whistle," Maren Halloway had said, standing very straight in the doorway with her shawl clutched at her throat like a wound. "The kind children play. She wanted one. I couldn't—" The sentence had collapsed there, and August had caught it, the way you catch a falling cup, and set it down gently on the counter between them.

"I can do that," August had said. "One mark. No more."

Because there were rules. August had made them early and kept them like scripture, and the first rule was this: *one commission per dead soul, and only when the living ask.* No fishing bones out of graveyards. No carving for curiosity. No listening for profit. The gift—if you could call a thing a gift when it arrived uninvited and never left—worked only through worked ivory and bone, only through material shaped by human hands, and only ever gave back what the dead had carried closest to the end. A song, usually. Sometimes a name. Once, memorably and horribly, an argument about turnips.

Maren Halloway paid in advance, in copper counted out twice, and left without looking at the shelf of bone animals, which was its own kind of discipline. August respected her for it.

Now, on the third evening, with the lye water poured off and the bone dried and warmed in August's cupped palms until it lost its graveyard chill, the carving began.

* * *

Working bone was like listening to someone talk in their sleep. You had to go slowly. You had to ask rather than demand. The rasp took off the outer layer in pale curls that August collected in a linen cloth—not scraps to be swept away, never that—and beneath it the bone opened up, dense and honeycombed at the core, and August felt the familiar pressure behind the eyes, the sensation of a door standing ajar somewhere in the skull.

Wren Halloway came through gradually, the way weather comes through a wall.

First a rhythm: skipping. Rope-skipping, probably, or the hopscotch grids chalked on the Lowmark cobbles all summer. Then a smell—woodsmoke and wet wool and something sweet, burnt sugar, a fair-day treat. Then, faint as a finger drawn in fog, a voice. A child's voice, mid-song, the tune broken and restarted the way children break and restart songs, inventing verses, discarding them.

August carved the bore with the long drill, turning it by lamplight, and the song grew clearer with every pass of the bit. This was the part no one understood, the part August could never explain without sounding either mad or monstrous: the hearing was not grief. It was closer to eavesdropping. The dead did not speak *to* August. They simply continued, briefly, in the material that had carried them, and August happened to be the sort of instrument that picked them up. A tuning fork struck near a piano string. That was all. That was all it had ever been, August told themselves, eleven years of telling themselves, and some nights the telling almost held.

The song resolved. It was a counting song, the kind sung to skip-rope:

*One for the bread, two for the bee,*
*three for the bird in the alder tree,*
*four for the door left open wide—*

and there the verse broke off, because that was where the millrace had broken it off, and August's hands went still on the drill, and the workshop was suddenly very quiet, and the river stone in the dish might as well have been on the moon.

They finished the verse themselves. Not aloud. Just in the shape of the cutting—the last two holes placed where the melody wanted them, the mouthpiece angled so the breath would carry the phrase home instead of dropping it. It was a small dishonesty, a kindness smuggled into the acoustics, and August had done it before and would do it again and hated themselves a little each time, because the dead deserved their endings whole, even the ones that weren't endings but interruptions.

By midnight the whistle was done: sanded smooth, oiled once, warm-toned, plain. A child's whistle. Nothing about it announced that it had been a leg once, or a girl, or a last afternoon of rope-songs by the mill. August wrapped it in wool and sat for a while with both hands flat on the bench, breathing, letting Wren Halloway drain out of their head the way water drains from a lock, slow and level, so nothing tore.

This was the curse, if curse it was: not the hearing, but the carrying. Every commission left sediment. Eleven years of sediment. August sometimes thought of their own skull as a riverbed, silted deep with other people's last songs, and wondered what would happen if the river ever rose.

They blew out the lamp. In the dark, the bone animals on the shelf watched with their drilled, indifferent eyes, and August slept badly, as usual, and dreamed of counting songs, and woke at dawn with the taste of salt on their lips.

* * *

Maren Halloway returned on market day, which was tactful of her, since everyone in Lowmark was too busy haggling over turnip prices to watch a woman collect a dead child's whistle.

She stood in the workshop holding the thing in both hands, turning it, and August watched her face go through the stages: the craft-appreciation, the horror of the craft, the guilt of the horror, and then—when she raised it to her lips, out of some instinct older than decorum—the stillness.

She played three notes. Badly. Her fingers didn't know the holes.

But the whistle answered her anyway, because August had tuned it true, and in the answering tone there was—there always was, for the bereaved, a fraction of something. A harmonic. An echo with intent. Maren Halloway lowered the whistle and looked at August with an expression that August had seen many times and dreaded every time: the expression of a person deciding whether to ask the question that must not be asked.

"Is she in it?" Maren whispered.

Here was the fork in the road, the one August stood at with every client. Say yes, and become a medium, a fraud, a comforter of the living at the expense of the dead. Say no, and hand a mother a piece of polished emptiness. August had learned, over eleven years, to say neither.

"It holds what she loved," August said. "Songs. Fair-days. The sound of the millrace, I think, though she'd have argued it was the race that owed her an apology." A pause. "Play it on her birthday. Play it badly. She'd have laughed at you."

And Maren Halloway laughed—or made the sound that stands next to laughter in a grieving throat, cracked and startled—and clutched the whistle to her chest, and paid the second half of the fee though she'd already paid in full, pressing the coins into August's palm and closing their fingers over them with both her hands, as if sealing a bargain with something larger than a bone-carver.

"Thank you," she said. "Whatever it is you do. Thank you."

Then she was gone, down the stairs, past the deaf cooper, out into the market noise, and August stood alone with coins they hadn't earned and a silence that rang.

That was the trouble with the work. Not the dead. The dead were honest, mostly, and brief. It was the living who left things in the room afterward—gratitude like a debt, hope like a hook. August dropped the copper into the jar on the shelf, the jar that fed no one and bought nothing, the jar that existed purely so that August could say they charged and mean it, and turned to the morning's dull work: forty buttons for the mercer, plain as pebbles.

They had gotten through perhaps six buttons when the knocking started.

Not at August's door. Down the street, at the chandler's, then the dyer's, then the house with the blue shutters—knocking, voices, the particular official cadence of questions being asked by men who already knew the answers. August put down the button knife and went to the gap in the shutters and looked out, and saw them.

Three figures in white. Not cloth-white, not linen-white, but white the way salt is white, a white that seemed to generate its own lightless glare, and even across the street and through a crack in the shutters, August's teeth ached with the wrongness of it. Salt-white enforcers. August had heard of them the way one hears of wolves: as rumor, as cautionary tale, as a reason to stay north of Thornfell and never, ever take commissions connected to the mountain trade. They were not supposed to be in Lowmark. Lowmark was nobody's town. That was the entire point of Lowmark.

At the chandler's door, one of them held up something small and dark and rolled—a hide, a scroll—and the chandler shook his head, and the enforcer wrote something down, and moved on.

Moving door to door. Moving toward Tallow Lane.

August stepped back from the shutter, heart going hard and slow, and did the arithmetic of invisibility: no guild marks, no ledger under their own name, no letters, no family, no debts, no history anyone could pull like a thread. Eleven years of being nobody. Nobody was safe. Nobody was passed over. Nobody was—

A fist struck the cooper's door below, and the whole stairwell shuddered with it, and August stood in the middle of their quiet room full of dull, honest bone, and understood with perfect clarity that the knocking was coming up.
