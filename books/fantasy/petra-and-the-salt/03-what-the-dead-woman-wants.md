## 3. What the Dead Woman Wants

The dead woman did not stop at the workshop door.

August had buried her—properly, quickly, in the hour before dawn, wrapped in sailcloth behind the tannery where the ground was soft and no one asked questions. They had said the words over her the way they said them over every stranger: *go home, go quiet, go where the carving can't reach you.* It was a lie, of course. The words never worked. But they were a kind of courtesy, and August had few enough courtesies to offer.

By midmorning she was speaking through the whistles.

It began with the memorial whistle—the child's bone, the one from yesterday, still sitting on the bench where August had left it after the mother's coin changed hands. August had meant to clean the bench. Instead they had picked up the whistle without thinking, turning it in their fingers the way a carver does when the hands need work but the mind is elsewhere, and the girl's song had risen sweet and small out of the ivory—

—and then it had broken off, mid-phrase, as if a hand had been laid over a mouth.

*Not you,* said another voice, deeper, cracked with cold. *Move aside, little one.*

August dropped the whistle.

It rolled across the floorboards and came to rest against the leg of the worktable, and from that distance of three feet, it went on speaking anyway. That was wrong. That was deeply, professionally wrong. Bone carried voice only through touch—through the carver's hands, through skin and callus and the long apprenticeship of listening. A bone on the floor was a bone at rest. A bone on the floor was silent.

*You have my map,* the dead woman said. Her voice had a frostbitten quality to it, syllables slurred at their edges like flesh gone grey. *And I have your name.*

"I don't take commissions," August said aloud, which was absurd, because there was no client in the room, and because the dead did not commission, they *insisted*, and because talking to an empty workshop was exactly the sort of thing that got a bone-carver noticed in a town like Lowmark.

They left the whistle where it lay and went to the window instead, easing back the shutter a finger's width. The street below ran brown with meltwater. The enforcers' cart was still parked outside the Salt Gate inn, its white-painted boards catching the morning light so brightly it hurt to look at. Two figures stood beside it, motionless in the way that only salt-white men could be motionless—not relaxed, not waiting, just *held*, like things set down by a larger hand. Their faces were smooth as river stones. Their coats made no sound when they moved, and they moved very rarely.

They had knocked on eleven doors since dawn. August had counted. At each door they spoke briefly, softly, and the householder would shake their head, and the white men would bow—a strange, precise bow, all spine—and move on. No one in Lowmark had ever seen the court's enforcers go door to door. The court bought souls quietly, through brokers and desperate mothers and contracts signed in wax. The court did not come looking. Whatever the courier had carried down out of Thornfell, it was worth breaking a hundred years of silence for.

*They will find me,* the dead woman said through the whistle on the floor. *I am in every bone you own now. Do you understand? You touched me. You carved near me. You are soaked in me, bone-carver, and everything you touch will remember.*

---

August tried, first, the sensible things.

They built a fire in the yard and burned the courier's outer clothes, the ones stiff with frost and crusted at the collar with salt. The smoke smelled of nothing at all, which was itself unnatural—wool should smell of wool, even burning—but August told themselves it proved nothing.

They scrubbed their hands with lye until the knuckles split. The dead woman's voice faded to a whisper during the washing and came back full-strength the moment August touched the doorframe.

They took the map out from under the third floorboard—the loose one, the one every hiding place in Lowmark used, which was why August kept their real valuables in the chimney flue—and considered, seriously and at length, feeding it to the fire.

The hide lay curled on their palms like something sleeping. It was good leather, mountain-tanned, and along its edges ran lines of script in a hand too fine to be human: columns of tiny characters that seemed to rearrange themselves when looked at directly, so that reading it was like trying to count rain. In the center, drawn in what August sincerely hoped was ink, the mountain ranges of Thornfell spread in blue-grey ridges, and beneath the tallest peak—Cindrel, though no surface map named it so—a staircase descended into the dark in careful, numbered steps. Three hundred and forty-one steps. Someone had wanted whoever followed this map to know exactly how deep they were going.

And there, at the bottom of the stair, drawn small and precise as a signature: a gate, and beside the gate, a word in that shifting script that August's eyes refused to hold but their bones, apparently, understood perfectly.

*Villalobos.*

The name sat in August's chest like a swallowed stone. They had heard it twice now—once from a dying woman's salt-crusted lips, once whispered up through the grain of a whistle—and both times it had felt less like a name than a hook, set deep, line running down into water they couldn't see.

"It's not mine," August told the map. "Whatever it says. I don't have a family. I don't have a before. I'm Dunmore's boy from the foundling row, and I carve dogs and memorials and I keep my head down, and whatever this is—" they shook the hide, "—it isn't mine."

The map said nothing. Maps were honest that way.

But the whistle, from the floor by the table, said: *Liar.*

---

The second day was worse, because the second day the voice learned the whole workshop.

Every finished piece became a throat. The dog figurines August carved for tourists—little hounds with their tails curled, harmless, charming, the bread-and-butter work that kept a nobody fed—began murmuring in unison whenever August passed the display shelf, a low chorus of frost-cracked syllables: *the map, the map, you have my map.* The box of raw bone in the corner hummed. When August picked up a carving knife, the handle itself seemed to carry a faint static whisper, though horn and steel held no voices at all, or never had before.

Worst was the old piece under the bench. August's own first carving, done at nine years old with stolen tools: a rough bird, half-finished, kept out of sentiment or cowardice or whatever you called the thing that wouldn't let you throw away the only object that proved you'd once been young. August hadn't touched it in years. They didn't need to. From beneath the bench, in the bird's unfinished wing, the dead woman said, gently, almost kindly:

*I know what you were sold for.*

August stopped eating. Stopped sleeping. Sat in the middle of the workshop at three in the morning with their arms wrapped around their knees, listening to a dead courier explain, in patient detail, that the Salt Court kept ledgers, and that ledgers had entries, and that somewhere beneath Mount Cindrel an entry existed with August's birth-date in it and a sum paid in winter grain.

"You're lying," August said to the empty air. "Dead women lie. Grief lies. Frostbite rots the brain before it rots the toes—I've seen it."

*I was a courier twenty-two years,* the voice said. *I carried everything the court wanted carried and asked nothing about any of it, because asking is how couriers die. But I read what I carried, bone-carver. I always read. And what I carried south last month was a copy of the intake ledger, and your name was in it, and I am sorry—do you hear me? I am sorry—that the truth travels badly.*

"Then why give me the map?" August's voice cracked on it. "If you're so sorry. Why hand it to a stranger in a doorway?"

*Because I was dying, and you were there, and you have hands that can do what mine couldn't.* A pause, and when she spoke again the frostbitten slur had thickened, the way voices thinned as the days since death lengthened. *Because it belongs to you. Not the map. What the map leads to. Go and see. Go and see, and then hate me if you still want to.*

---

On the third day, the enforcers reached August's street.

August watched from the loft window as the white men worked their way up from the tannery end, unhurried, precise, bowing at each threshold. The neighbors had begun to gather at corners to watch them pass, the way people watch a funeral they weren't invited to. Old Marta from the dye-house crossed herself with a dyed-blue thumb. The tanner's boys threw a pebble that struck one enforcer's shoulder and simply fell, and the enforcer did not turn, and the boys stopped laughing very suddenly.

Four doors. Three doors.

August looked around the workshop—at the whistles, the dogs, the bird under the bench, all of them whispering now, a whole congregation of bone murmuring *the map, the map*—and understood, with the flat clarity of a blade coming down, that invisibility was a currency they had spent. Twenty-six years of being nobody, of carving small and signing nothing and paying every toll in silence, and it had bought them exactly as much time as it had. The court knew a Villalobos existed. The court knew a courier had died carrying proof of it. And a town of four hundred souls contained perhaps six bone-carvers, and only one who took no guild mark and signed no work.

There was no version of the next hour in which August stayed.

They packed the way they'd always promised themselves they could: fast, light, nothing that mattered. Knife roll. Flint. Dried fish and hardbread into a sack. Coin from the chimney flue—all of it; if the court was going to burn their life down, they might as well rob themselves first. The map went inside their shirt, against the skin, where it lay cold as a fish.

They hesitated over the bird. Then they took it, because leaving it felt like leaving a hostage, and wrapped it in cloth at the bottom of the sack.

At the door, August stopped, and did the one thing they had sworn never to do in Lowmark: they spoke to the room.

"If I go," they said, "will you be quiet?"

Silence. Then, from every shelf at once, soft as snowfall: *No. But I will be quieter. Walk fast, bone-carver. They knock lighter than they enter.*

---

The Widow's Stair began behind the salt sheds, where the cliff above Lowmark sheered up four hundred feet in grey limestone, and the townsfolk had cut—or someone had cut, centuries ago, no one remembered who—a zigzag of steps into the rock, narrow enough that two people could not pass, steep enough that a fall meant the tide pools below. Widows had thrown themselves from it in the famine years. Hence the name, delivered always with the particular relish Lowmark reserved for its own grimness. No guard watched it, because no smuggler used it—too exposed, too slow, too visible from the sea road—and no tax collector bothered, because nothing worth taxing went up a widow's drop.

Which made it, for a man who needed to leave a town without being seen leaving, the only road in the world.

August climbed with the sack strapped tight and the wind pulling at their coat, and below them Lowmark shrank to its true size: a smear of brown roofs, a harbor like a cracked dish, the white cart by the inn no bigger than a tooth. Somewhere down there, a white-knuckled fist was knocking on a workshop door and finding it empty, and the finding-empty would be reported upward, and the reporting would not stop.

Halfway up, on the fourth landing where the steps turned and the cliff offered a ledge barely wide enough for both feet, August had to stop and breathe, and in the breathing, the voice came again—and this time it did not come from the sack, or the knife roll, or the bird.

It came from the cliff.

From the limestone itself, faintly, the way a shell holds the sea: *Careful. The fourth step of this landing is loose. It has been loose for sixty years and it has taken three people.*

August went very still. "That's not possible. Stone doesn't remember. Stone was never alive."

*Everything was alive,* the dead woman said, and there was something in her voice now that August had not heard before—not urgency, not grief, but a vast tired patience, the tone of someone explaining the sea to a fish. *Everything that grew, everything that bled, everything that was born and ended. The mountain remembers its own deaths. Why do you think the court built itself underground, in the oldest stone it could find? Why do you think they mint souls into salt? Because salt keeps, bone-carver. Salt and bone and stone—they are the world's memory, and the court has learned to spend it.*

August looked down at the step she'd named. It looked like every other step. They put their weight on the edge of the landing instead, hugging the living rock, and as they swung past, the fourth step groaned and shed a flake of limestone the size of a dinner plate, which dropped silently away into the wind and was gone.

August's heart hammered against the map against their ribs.

"You're doing this," they said. "Somehow. Some trick of echo, some—"

*I am doing very little,* she said. *I am mostly dead. But you—you are doing more than you know. Every step you climb, you are hearing wider. Half a soul hears thin, bone-carver. And yours is thinner than most. They took their share at the start. That is why the voices come to you so easily. That is why you have never once, in twenty-six years, been able to sit in a crowded room without feeling like you were drowning.*

The wind took the rest of it, or she let it go. August climbed.

---

Near the top, where the stair crested the cliff and the moorland opened out—brown and silver and endless, the Thornfell passes a white-toothed smudge on the northern horizon—August finally stopped and let themselves feel it, because there was no one to see, and because a man cannot carry a thing forever without setting it down.

Grief came first, and it surprised them with its direction. Not grief for the life abandoned—Lowmark had never been a home, only a hiding place, and hiding places are not mourned. The grief was for the courier herself, for the woman whose name August had never thought to ask, who had walked out of the mountains on frozen feet to deliver a message to a stranger, and died of it in a doorway smelling of bone-dust. Who was now alone in a shallow grave behind a tannery, wrapped in sailcloth, while her voice rattled around in a fleeing carver's luggage like a coin in a jar.

"I'll carry your bones home," August said to the wind, and hated themselves for saying it, because it was a promise, and promises were debts, and debts were names, and names were exactly what they had spent a lifetime avoiding. "Wherever home is. If we get through this. If any of this is real."

*It's real,* said the bird, from the bottom of the sack, in her voice. *And denial is a luxury of the settled. Climb.*

So August climbed—up off the Widow's Stair and onto the open moor, where the wind came straight off the high passes carrying the first smell of true winter, and the road north ran pale and empty toward Thornfell.

Behind them, far below, a thread of smoke was rising from Lowmark. August told themselves it was a chimney. Told themselves it was the tannery. Told themselves, with diminishing conviction, all the way to the first bend in the road.

And ahead—when the wind shifted, when the moor went quiet—even here, even miles from any grave or any carving, so faint it might have been their own pulse playing tricks: *Come north, bone-carver. Come and see what you were sold for.*

August pulled their collar up, fixed their eyes on the white teeth of the mountains, and walked.
