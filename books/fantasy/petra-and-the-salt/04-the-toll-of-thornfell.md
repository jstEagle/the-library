## 4. The Toll of Thornfell

The Widow's Stair ended where the mountain began, and August had hoped—foolishly, perhaps—that the two would feel different underfoot.

They did not. Both were stone worn smooth by other people's desperation. Both demanded that you keep moving or admit you had nowhere to go. By the second day above the tree line, August's boots had gone soft at the seams, and the wind off the high passes had found every gap in their coat the way water finds a crack in a wall: patiently, inevitably, with an old familiarity.

The courier's map rode against August's chest, wrapped in oilcloth beneath three layers of wool. They had not looked at it since Lowmark. They had not needed to. The dead woman's voice had stopped speaking through the bones they touched somewhere on the third morning of the climb—not gone, August suspected, but waiting, the way a debt waits. She had said what she came to say: *the map leads to something that belongs to you.* Everything after that was argument, and she seemed content to let August lose it slowly.

What belonged to them. August turned the phrase over as they climbed, the way a tongue worries a broken tooth. They owned a knife, a roll of good carving chisels wrapped in felt, forty-one coins sewn into a belt they never untied in company, and a reputation for being no one worth robbing. That was the whole estate. Whatever else might belong to them had been left deliberately, like a snake leaving its skin, and August had never once looked back at it.

So why was the mountain pulling at them like a hook through the cheek?

Because the voice had been right about one thing already. It had known the name *Villalobos* before August said it aloud—and August had never said it aloud.

* * *

The pass narrowed in the late afternoon, walls of grey stone rising on either side until the sky became a ribbon overhead, cold and thin as knife-blade steel. August knew this place. Everyone who worked the border knew this place, the way sailors know a reef. The Narrows of Thornfell, where the smugglers' road pinched down to a single file of footsteps, and where—for eleven years, before his exile—the toll had been collected by a man named Brannoc Hale.

August had told themselves they would not come this way. There were other passes, worse ones, longer ones, passes where the only toll was weather and bad luck. But the Ashwife's reading had shaken something loose in their sense of direction, and grief makes a poor navigator. August had walked east when they meant to walk north, and north when they meant to walk anywhere but here, and now the walls closed in and there was no pretending anymore.

The chain came down at dusk.

It was iron, rusted at the links, hung across the Narrows between two iron stakes driven into the rock, and it sang a low note when it settled—a sound August felt in their teeth before they heard it. Beyond the chain, in the shadow of an overhang that had been someone's toll-house once, a fire burned small and disciplined. And beside the fire stood a shape August would have known anywhere, in any light, alive or dead: broad-shouldered, heavy-handed, wearing a fur cloak that had been mended too many times to be warm anymore.

"Evening," Brannoc called, and his voice hadn't changed either. Gravel over honey. "Toll's two silver, or its weight in truth."

August stood very still. The wind moved through the Narrows and carried woodsmoke and old leather and, underneath both, the smell of him—pine tar and iron, the smell of a man who worked chains for a living. Eleven years since Lowmark. Nine since the last letter, which August had not answered. Four years since word came down the mountain that Brannoc Hale had been exiled from Thornfell for reasons no one would say plainly, and August had told themselves the news meant nothing, and had believed it for almost a full day.

"You're supposed to be gone from here," August said.

"I am gone from here." Brannoc stepped forward into the last of the light, and August saw what exile had done to him: grey in the beard now, a scar through one eyebrow that hadn't been there before, hands that looked like they'd been holding things heavier than chains. "This isn't Thornfell. This is the rock above Thornfell, which belongs to nobody, which is why I'm allowed to stand on it." He smiled, and it was the old smile, crooked and tired at the same time. "Hello, August."

"Don't." The word came out sharper than intended. "Don't do the greeting. Take your toll or open your chain."

"Two silver, then."

August crossed the frozen mud, untied the belt with fingers that wanted to shake and refused to, and counted out the coins into his palm without touching it. Brannoc weighed them out of habit, bit one, and dropped all of them into a pouch that clinked far too full for a man living alone above the tree line. He was collecting more than tolls up here. He was collecting something else, and August didn't want to guess what.

"The chain stays down till morning," Brannoc said. "No one crosses the Narrows in the dark, not even people paying double. You know that."

"I'll walk around."

"There is no around. There hasn't been since the slide took the west shelf, four winters back." He tilted his head. "You didn't know that?"

"I don't keep track of the mountain's moods."

"No," Brannoc said quietly. "You keep track of nothing. That's always been your genius, August. You can make yourself forget anything if it costs you something to remember."

There was a flat stone near the fire, and a second one across from it, and the arrangement was so obviously deliberate that August understood he had been expecting someone—not necessarily them, but someone, some traveler, some excuse for a fire and two stones facing each other. Exile was loneliness with better scenery. August sat down because sitting was less revealing than standing, and held their hands to the flames, and hated how good the warmth felt.

Brannoc put a kettle on. Of course he did. He had always been the one who fed people, who fixed straps, who remembered which side of the bed the draft came from. In the years when they had been—whatever they had been—he had kept August's chisels oiled without being asked, and August had carved him a whistle from elk bone that he had carried, so far as August knew, until the day they stopped speaking. The thought of that whistle, wherever it was now, made something in August's chest pull tight and sour.

"You're heading up-mountain," Brannoc said. Not a question.

"I'm heading through."

"Toward Cindrel."

The name landed like a dropped chisel. August kept their face still, which took work. "I'm going where the road goes."

"The road doesn't go to Cindrel. Nothing goes to Cindrel. That's rather the point of Cindrel." He poured the tea—black pine tea, bitter, the kind that kept frostbite out of your blood—and handed a tin cup across the fire. Their fingers brushed around the cup's rim, neither quite touching, both aware of it. "Salt-white men came through the low passes three weeks ago asking questions. Asking about a courier. Then, a few days later, asking about carvers. Bone-carvers, specifically. One bone-carver, specifically." He drank. "I told them I hadn't seen anyone. Which was true. But I wondered, afterward, why they thought I might have."

"They ask everyone."

"They asked me by name, August. They knew my history. They knew—" he paused, and the fire cracked in the silence, "—they knew things about me I'd prefer strangers not to know. Someone gave them a list, and my name was on it under yours."

The tea tasted like smoke and pine needles and the end of something. August set the cup down on the stone with great care, as though it might detonate.

"What are you doing up here, Brannoc? Truly."

"Earning back what I owe." He said it simply, without drama, and somehow that was worse than drama. "You've heard the story by now, surely. Everyone on the border tells it differently, but the spine of it's always the same. Toll-keeper takes bribes to look away from certain cargo. Toll-keeper gets caught. Toll-keeper gets exiled." He looked into the fire. "Nobody ever asks what the cargo was."

August's hands went still around the cup. "Tell me."

"Souls." He said it the way you'd say *grain*, or *salt*, ordinary as freight. "Small ones, mostly. The court pays by weight and the poor sell cheap. My part was only to count the crates and mark the ledger and never ask whose names were stenciled on the sides. I told myself it was just paper. Just numbers." His jaw worked. "Then one winter a crate came through with a village's name on it. A whole village, August. Flooded out, starved out, bought for the price of a road. I opened it. I shouldn't have—I knew I shouldn't—but I opened it, and I heard them."

The wind screamed thinly through the Narrows above them. August did not move. Every bone in their body, every carved thing they had ever touched, sat up and listened.

"You heard them," August repeated. "Through the crate."

"Through the salt-glass. It carries sound, if you're the sort who hears." Brannoc finally looked up, and his eyes in the firelight were wet and furious and old. "You're the sort. You always were. Why do you think I'm telling you this instead of lying to you like a normal man?"

"Because you want something."

"I want to give you back your letter," Brannoc said. "The one you never answered. I wrote nine drafts and sent none of them, and the reason I sent none is that every one of them was a lie, and I couldn't bear to lie to you in writing where you could reread it." He set down his cup. "Here is the truth instead. I took the court's coin for six years. I counted souls like sacks of barley. And when they caught me, they didn't exile me—they hired me. Because a man who knows the smuggling roads and has nothing left to lose is worth ten enforcers, and cheaper to feed."

The cold came into August then, from the inside, spreading outward like frost through a windowpane. "You're working for them."

"I'm *near* them. There's a difference, and it's the difference I've spent four years building, and it's thinner than this chain." He gestured at the iron stretched between its stakes. "They send me up here because I know every path over Thornfell, and because travelers trust a fallen man. They think shame makes people honest." A terrible pause. "And they told me, three days ago, that a bone-carver out of Lowmark was walking toward the mountain carrying stolen property. And they asked me to stop that carver. Or, failing that, to report which road they took."

* * *

For a long moment there was only the fire, and the wind, and the enormous indifferent dark pressing down on the Narrows from both sides.

August thought about running. There was no running; there was the chain, and the walls, and four thousand feet of scree on either side, and a man between them and all of it who had once carried them, laughing, across a flooded river in spring. August thought about the knife at their hip and was ashamed of the thought within the same heartbeat it arrived.

Mostly, August thought about the elk-bone whistle. About whether he still had it. About whether it mattered.

"You should turn back," Brannoc said gently. "Whatever's on that map—and don't look at me like that, August, I've watched you touch your chest every time you shift your pack, you've been guarding it like a wound—it isn't worth what the court does to thieves. I've seen the vaults' ledgers. I've seen what comes back from Cindrel when anything comes back. Turn around. Go south. Carve your whistles. Be nobody again. It's a good trick. You're the best I ever saw at it."

"That's not a life. That's a hiding place with a roof on it."

"It kept you alive twenty-six years."

"And it cost me—" August stopped. The words were right there, crowding, and the firelight made it easy, dangerously easy, to say true things. "It cost me everything I don't remember. Somebody sold me, Brannoc. Before I could walk. Before I could *want* anything. They sold whatever I am to that mountain, and I found out eight days ago, and the woman who told me died with salt on her lips getting the proof to my door." Their voice cracked and they let it, because hiding it would have cost more. "You want me to turn back and be nobody. Fine. But nobody is exactly what they made me, and I am done being their product."

Brannoc had gone very still. When he spoke, his voice was rough. "Your own parents?"

"Someone's. Whoever signed."

He closed his eyes. For a moment he looked every year of his age and several he hadn't earned yet. "Gods. I counted crates for six years and never once wondered if one of them had a name I loved in it." He opened his eyes. "That's the real reason I'm telling you everything. Not warning you. Confessing. You deserved it nine years ago and you're getting it now, on a rock, in the cold, because I'm still a coward but I'm a slightly better one than I was."

August looked at him across the fire—at the mended cloak, the scarred brow, the hands that had counted the stolen dead and never forgotten a single one—and felt the old loyalty and the new necessity grind against each other like millstones, with their heart caught between.

"If you report me," August said, "they'll take the map, and they'll take me, and whatever's mine stays in their vault forever. If you don't, they'll do to you what they do to everyone who fails them. I'm not asking you to choose me. I'm telling you what choosing looks like."

"I know what it looks like." Brannoc rose, joints cracking, and went to the chain. He lifted the great iron links with both hands, easily, the way he'd lifted everything his whole life, and began winding them back around the stake. "Cross at first light. The snow will cover your tracks by noon." He didn't look at August while he worked. "But hear the terms, because I only get to be generous once."

"Terms."

"One: you were never here. If the white men ask, I stopped a carver, questioned them, and sent them south weeping. I'll show them a false ledger entry to prove it. Two:" he straightened, and now he did look at August, and the firelight made his eyes hard as the rock around them, "you tell no one I helped you. Not the smuggler you'll eventually need—because you will need one, no one gets into Cindrel alone—not the witch, not anyone. My usefulness depends on my shame, and the moment they suspect my shame is fake, I'm dead, and worse, I'm *useless*. Three."

He hesitated. The chain hung slack in his hands.

"Three," August prompted.

"When you get there—and you will, you're the most stubborn person I've ever loved—" the word fell between them like a struck bell, and neither of them reached for it, "—when you find whatever jar or contract or scrap of paper has your name on it, you read the whole page. All the signatures. All the witnesses. Contracts like that need witnesses, August, that's how the law works even for monsters. And when you see whose name is at the bottom, I want you to remember that I told you the truth tonight, before you asked, when lying would have been easier and safer." His voice dropped. "Because I was there, August. Years ago. I was there for all of it, and I didn't know what I was witnessing, and that is either the worst excuse in the world or the only honest one, and you'll have to decide which. Not me. I decided long ago and I chose wrong."

The fire popped. Somewhere above them, snow began to fall, fine and dry, the first grains of the season sifting down through the ribbon of sky.

August stood. Walked to the edge of the firelight. Stopped.

"Thank you," they said, because gratitude was owed even when it hurt to pay. "And Brannoc—when I read that page, whatever's written on it, I'll remember that you told me the truth first. I don't know yet if that saves you. But it counts. It counts for something."

"It counts for everything," Brannoc said quietly. "It's the only coin I have left."

August slept that night against the toll-house wall with the map over their heart and one hand on their knife, and dreamed of a ledger with endless pages, and a child's name at the top of each one, and at the bottom of every page, in a hand they almost recognized, a signature like a scar.

At first light the chain lay coiled and open, and Brannoc stood beside it looking north, not at August, and said only:

"The white men patrol the high shelf by week's end. Whatever you mean to do under that mountain—do it fast. Winter's coming early this year. I can feel it in the chain."

August crossed the Narrows and did not look back, which was a lie, because they looked back twice.

And behind them, faint on the wind, they heard the dead woman's voice for the first time in days—not angry, not insistent, almost gentle:

*Now you understand what it costs. Keep walking.*
