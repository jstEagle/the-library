## 5. Salt on the Wind

The Widow's Stair let August down out of Thornfell in three days of wind and bad footing, and by the fourth morning they had almost convinced themselves that nothing was following them.

Almost.

It was the birds that gave it away. The high meadows below the pass should have been loud with pipits and the small quarrelsome business of finches in the gorse. Instead the valley lay silent in a way that had nothing to do with winter. August stood at the edge of a shepherd's track with their pack straps biting into their shoulders and listened to the absence, and understood that silence like this was not empty. It was *listening*.

They did not look back. Looking back was a confession; whatever walked behind them would read it as one. Instead August did what they had done all their life, what had kept them alive through every hard season since they'd first understood that being nobody was a skill you could practice: they became unremarkable. They slouched. They let their hood come forward. They moved at the pace of a tired carver going between towns, which was not far from the truth, and they turned off the shepherd's track onto a lower road that smelled of peat smoke and wet wool, heading for the one place in these hills where a fire burned that no enforcer's cold could put out.

The Ashwife lived on the burnt side of the fell, in a house everyone said had burned down twice and stood up again both times, grinning soot-black through its own scars.

* * *

August had traded with her before—bone needles for burn-salve, a carved lintel-ward for a night under her roof when the snow came early two winters back. She had never asked their name. That was why August was walking toward her chimney smoke now instead of toward any of the warm, sociable villages along the river, where a stranger asking after wax seals would be remembered by supper-time and described by morning.

The house sat in a bowl of ash-grey grass, and even from the track August could see something wrong with the air above it. Heat-shimmer, in autumn. The chimney breathed a column of bent light, like the world seen through water.

They knocked the way she liked: three slow raps, then the flat of the palm held against the door until she decided to answer it. The wood was warm. Not sun-warm. *Hearth*-warm, deep in the grain, as if the whole door were a banked coal.

"I'm not buying," said a voice through the planks, "and I'm not selling, and I buried my last husband twice."

"It's the bone-carver from Lowmark."

A pause. Then bolts—three of them, iron, drawn back in an order that sounded almost like music—and the door swung open on a wall of heat and a woman built like a hearth herself: broad, dark, silver-streaked hair pinned up with what appeared to be actual nails. Marta Ashwife looked August over once, head to boots, and her eyes narrowed at something behind them.

"You're leaking," she said.

"Leaking?"

"Cold. You've got cold coming off you like a wound bleeds. Something's been *reading* you." She stepped aside. "In. Quickly. And don't touch the third kettle, it's holding a grudge."

August crossed the threshold and felt the pursuit-fear drop away from their shoulders like a wet cloak set down by a fire. The single room of the Ashwife's house was impossible—too large inside than out, the way old houses of old women sometimes were—and every surface held fire in some form or another: coals under the floor stones, a hearth wide enough to roast an ox, lamps without wicks burning small steady flames the color of different hours of the day. The heat pressed against August's skin like a hand checking them for fever.

Marta barred the door, then did something to the bar—spat on her thumb and drew a line down it—and the warmth in the room tightened, gathered, became deliberate.

"Now," she said, turning. "Bone-carver. You've got frostbite on your ghost and salt in your pack, and you've come to me because there's no one else left who doesn't ask names." She jerked her chin at August's bag. "Show me."

"How did you know about the—"

"The wind told me. Wind tells me everything; it's a gossip." She held out one broad hand, fingers scarred like candle-wax. "The map, carver. Or the thing wrapped in the map. Whichever's making your hands shake."

August hadn't known their hands were shaking. They unbuckled the flap of the pack slowly, and the smell rose up out of it—that smell, dry and mineral and old, the smell of the courier's dying lips. Salt.

The rolled hide came out heavier than hide should be. August laid it on Marta's table, between a mortar of red powder and a lamp burning flame the color of late afternoon, and unrolled it.

The map spread itself with a sound like a sigh. It showed mountains—the Thornfells, unmistakably, though drawn as no living surveyor drew them, the peaks rendered as teeth, as vertebrae, as the knuckles of some enormous buried hand. Rivers ran in silver leaf. And threaded through everything, stitched across passes and valleys in lines of dried white crystal, ran roads of salt.

Roads August had never heard of, though they had crossed these mountains their whole life. Roads that converged, all of them, every white thread in the weave, on a single point beneath the massif at the map's heart—a mountain drawn larger than any mountain August knew, its slopes marked with a symbol like a closed eye.

Beneath it, in letters of pressed salt, words August could not read. The script was wrong somehow—letters that seemed to rearrange themselves when looked at directly, meaning sliding off them like water off fat.

"Wax," Marta said quietly.

"What?"

"The seals. Along the edge." Her finger traced the map's border, where at intervals small discs of grey-white wax had been pressed into the hide, each stamped with the same mark: a circle bisected by a vertical line. "There are seven. No—eight. One's been broken." She looked up, and for the first time since August had known her, the Ashwife looked afraid. "Where did you get this?"

"A courier. Dying. She pressed it into my hands and died on my floor with—" August stopped. "With salt on her lips."

"And she said a name. Your name." It wasn't a question. The wind, presumably, had told her that too. "Sit down, carver."

"I'd rather stand."

"I'd rather you didn't. When I open this, you'll want to be sitting." Marta pulled a stool to the table with her foot and waited, immovable as her own hearthstone, until August sat.

Then the Ashwife began to work.

She took a lamp whose flame burned the color of midnight—not blue, but *dark*, a flame that gave light and took it back again—and tipped it over the first seal without touching wick to wax. The flame poured. That was the only word for it: it poured off the lamp like honey, like molten glass, and lay across the seal in a thin bright sheet, and the wax began to speak.

Not in words. In images. The fire on the wax threw shadows upward, and the shadows moved: August saw a gate, vast and white, carved into the root of a mountain. Saw figures passing through it—figures that cast no shadows of their own, though the gate's shadow swallowed them whole. Saw hands, hundreds of hands, pressing small glass jars into long shelves in the dark.

And heard, faintly, under the crackle of the flame, a sound like coins being counted. Except the counting was wrong. Coins don't whisper. Coins don't *plead*.

Marta moved the midnight flame to the second seal, and the vision shifted: a hall of white ministers, robed in salt-crusted cloth, and before them a line of the living—poor ones, hollow-cheeked, holding contracts they could barely read—signing themselves away. A farmer's weathered thumbprint pressed into wax. A mother's. A child's, guided by an adult hand.

August's stomach turned over. They thought of the courier's cracked lips. They thought, unwillingly, of their own hands—of twenty-six years of carving bone and hearing the dead sing through it, and never once asking themselves the question that now rose like floodwater:

*If souls can be sold... where do the sold ones go?*

The third seal showed the minting. August would carry that image the rest of their life, whichever length that turned out to be. A great wheel of white stone turning in darkness, and souls—visible now, visible as threads of pale light—fed into it, and ground, and pressed, and stamped, and what dropped from the wheel's other side was coinage. Small white coins, each one humming faintly, each one *singing*, in voices so worn down by the pressing that only fragments of song remained.

"That's Mount Cindrel," Marta said. Her voice had gone very quiet, the voice people use in sickrooms. "The closed eye. There's an old story—my grandmother's grandmother told it—that Cindrel is hollow, and that what's hollowed out of it isn't ore." She straightened. "The Salt Court. The ministers under the mountain. Souls minted into currency, carver. Every white coin that buys grain in Lowmark, every salt-mark that pays a toll on the passes—each one is somebody. Somebody's lifetime. Somebody's love. Ground small enough to spend."

The fire on the third seal guttered and went out. Marta's hand was trembling. August had never seen the Ashwife's hands tremble.

"There are five more seals," August said.

"And I'll read them all. But not tonight—tonight the fire needs feeding and so do we, and you need to sleep before I show you the rest, because the rest is worse." She paused at the hearth, ladle in hand, and did not look around. "Except the last one. The broken one. I'll read that now, because you'll get no sleep at all otherwise, and neither will I."

She lifted the broken seal from the map's corner—a disc of wax cracked clean in half, still joined to the hide by a thread of it—and held the midnight flame over it.

The shadow that rose was small. Just a contract. Just a page, rendered in fire-shadow, columns of script and a date and two signatures and—

A name.

August leaned forward before they could stop themselves. The name at the top of the contract, written in a clerk's careful hand, the name of the soul being sold, the name of a newborn twenty-six years ago—

The flame flickered. The shadow-script swam. But not before August read it. Not before the letters burned themselves into the backs of their eyes, into the place where their own name should have lived and never quite had.

*Villalobos.*

Their family name. Their father's name, their mother's name, the name the courier had gasped with her dying breath. And beside it, in the space where a given name should have been written, the clerk had entered not a name but a word, small and neat and damning:

*Petra.*

The flame went out. The room came back—the hearth, the kettles, Marta's face grey above her scarred hands.

"Petra," August said. Their mouth was full of salt. "That's—"

"Yours." Marta set the lamp down with exaggerated care, the way one sets down something that might go off. "Or it was. Before they took the rest of you." She finally looked up, and her eyes were kind in a way that was almost unbearable. "You weren't just born, carver. You were *issued*. And somewhere under Cindrel there's a shelf with a jar on it, and in that jar there's a piece of you that's been singing for twenty-six years, waiting for someone who could hear it."

Outside, the wind changed.

August felt it before they heard it—a pressure against the north wall, a cold that slid under the door despite the bar, despite the spit-line, despite the hearth's deliberate warmth. The kettles stopped muttering. Every lamp in the room leaned sideways at once, flames flattening as if ducking.

Marta's head came up. "Wind's wrong," she said. "Wind's been *paid*."

Through the gap under the door, across the warm floor stones, something white began to spread. Not snow. It crept like frost but moved with purpose, a thin rime of salt crawling inward from the threshold, and where it touched the ashes scattered by the hearth the ashes hissed.

"They followed the map's scent," Marta said calmly, terribly calmly, already moving—pulling a sack from under the floor, filling it with quick, economical motions: a paper of salt, a twist of dried herbs, a clay pot sealed with wax that August deeply did not want to ask about. "One of them. Maybe two. They won't knock, carver. They don't knock. They arrive, and they take, and the houses they visit stand empty after." She thrust the sack into August's arms. "Back door. Down through the ash-field, follow the hot stones—I keep them warm for exactly this—and don't stop until you reach the river road."

"Marta—"

"Don't thank me, don't apologize, and don't you dare die on my fell, I've buried enough husbands." She was already turning to face her own front door, rolling up her sleeves, and the firelight made her shadow enormous on the wall—a shadow with broad shoulders and burning hands. "I've been waiting thirty years for one of these white-handed bastards to try my door. Go. *Go.*"

August went—through the back into the shrieking dark, the sack clutched to their chest, the name *Petra Villalobos* ringing in their skull like a struck bell. Behind them, through the walls, they heard the front door burst inward and heard Marta Ashwife say, conversationally, to whatever had entered:

"Take off those white gloves or lose the hands."

Then the night took August, and the hot stones led them down through the ash-field toward the river, and the wind at their back carried the smell of salt—and underneath it, faint and furious, the smell of burning.
