## 6. The Name I Was Sold Under

The Ashwife's hearth had no chimney, and the smoke went where it pleased.

August watched it curl along the ceiling beams of the crooked little house, pooling in the corners like something patient, something waiting to be asked a question. The fire itself burned low and blue at its heart, the color of salt held to flame, and the woman who tended it moved with the unhurried certainty of someone who had spent decades learning exactly which lies her fire would tolerate.

She was called the Ashwife, though August suspected she'd had other names before—names she'd fed to the coals one by one until only the title remained. Her hair was white as frost-fern, cropped close to her skull, and her hands were stained gray to the wrist, as if she'd been kneading ash into bread all her life. She wore a man's coat too big for her shoulders and she moved through her own house like a guest who had long ago stopped apologizing for it.

"You keep looking at the door," she said, without turning from the hearth.

"I'm not."

"You are. You've looked at it four times since you sat down." She picked up the poker, stirred the blue heart of the fire, and the flames flared briefly green. "The enforcer lost your scent at the river, but scent is not the same as knowing. The court's white hands don't need tracks. They need only a name, and names travel faster than feet." She set the poker down. "So we will make sure they have nothing to find. Sit properly, bone-carver. We're going to do a reading."

August's hand went, without permission, to the satchel at their hip—the courier's map rolled inside it, wrapped now in oilcloth, heavy as a sleeping snake. "I told you. I want to know what the map says. Where it leads. That's all."

"That is what you *told* me." The Ashwife turned at last, and her eyes were pale as woodsmoke, pale as the smoke pooled above them. "But the map is wax and hide and other people's seals. It will tell us what the court wants us to know. If you want truth—" she extended one gray-stained hand, palm up, an old woman's gesture of asking, or begging, or receiving alms, "—we read the bone. Your oldest piece. The one you never carve, never sell, never let another living soul touch. Every carver has one. Don't bother denying it; I can smell the sentiment on you like cheap tallow."

August did have such a piece. They had carried it for eleven years, through every border town and smuggler's road, through the Thornfell passes and the long gray years of being nobody. A sliver of rib-bone, yellowed with age, carved on their sixteenth winter with a pattern so simple it was barely a pattern: three lines, a circle, three lines again. Their first work. The work of a child who had not yet learned that bones remember better than the living do.

They had never heard anything in it. That was the point of it. It was the one silence in a life full of voices.

"It's nothing," August said. "It's blank. I've held it a thousand times and it's blank."

"Everything is blank," the Ashwife said, "until it isn't." Her fingers curled once, beckoning. "Give it here, child. The fire is hungry tonight, and I'd rather feed it truth than guesswork."

Every instinct August owned said no. Give away the oldest thing, the quietest thing, the one object that had never lied to them because it had never spoken. But the dead woman's voice had been whispering through every bone they touched for two days now—*it belongs to you, it belongs to you*—and the enforcers were behind them, and the mountain was ahead, and August was so tired of carrying questions the way other people carried stones.

They took out the sliver of rib-bone and laid it in the Ashwife's palm.

Her fingers closed around it gently, the way one holds a bird that might be dying. She carried it to the hearth and held it—not in the flame, but above it, in the column of hot air rising from the blue heart—and began to murmur words that were less language than rhythm, a lullaby sung backward.

The smoke changed.

It had been drifting lazy and directionless; now it bent toward the bone, gathered around the Ashwife's fist like moths around a lantern, and began to move in shapes. August saw a door. A road. A small figure walking away from a house with its roof on fire—or perhaps not on fire; perhaps the smoke simply didn't know how else to draw grief.

And then the voice came, and it was not the dead courier's voice, and it was not the girl from the whistle, and it was not any voice August knew.

It was a woman's voice, young and exhausted and cracked down the middle, and it said:

*"Please. She'll die if we stay. Please, there must be another way."*

The Ashwife's eyes flicked up. "A memory," she murmured. "The bone kept it. The oldest ones always keep the beginning." She turned the sliver over in her fingers. "Whose voice is this, bone-carver? Think carefully before you answer."

"I don't know," August said, and the lie tasted like copper.

The smoke made a room. Low ceiling, a bed, a bundle in the bed that cried with a thin, furious hunger. Two figures bent over the bundle—a man with his back half-turned, a woman holding the crying thing against her chest as though someone were trying to take it from her.

Someone was trying to take it from her.

*"Twenty-six years of luck,"* the man's voice said, and he sounded young too, young and scared and trying to sound practical. *"That's what they're offering. Grain through the famine, medicine, passage south. And all they take is—"*

*"You know what they take."* The woman's voice again, cracking wider. *"They take HER. Not her body. Not her breath. The part that—"*

*"The part that doesn't keep us alive,"* the man said. *"Mara. Look at her. Look how small. The famine took the Vell children last winter. All three. I won't bury my daughter."*

And the bundle in the bed—the bundle that was not a bundle, that was a newborn, that was a baby girl with a name already given and already being traded away—cried on, thin and furious, while the smoke drew the man signing something in white, drawing his finger through a line of salt on a table, while the woman turned her face to the wall.

*"What did you name her?"* asked a third voice, dry and official, the voice of a clerk or a minister. *"For the ledger."*

*"Petra,"* the woman whispered, to the wall. *"Her name is Petra."*

*"Petra Villalobos,"* the clerk repeated, and somewhere in the smoke a quill scratched, and somewhere in the smoke a jar was sealed, and the woman in the burning-looking house began to cry without any sound at all.

The bone went silent in the Ashwife's hand.

August did not remember standing up. They were standing, though, halfway across the room, and the hearth-smoke was still hanging in its shapes—the room, the bed, the salt line on the table—and the Ashwife was looking at them with an expression terrible in its gentleness.

"Petra," she said quietly. "Villalobos."

"Don't." August's voice came out wrong, scraped raw. "Don't say it."

"The courier died saying your family name. The map bears your name in its seals—I showed you the mark, the doubled-V sigil, the contract-holder's sign. And your own first bone keeps the memory of the day you were sold." She rose, slow, her joints cracking like green wood. "Child. You weren't hunted by accident. You weren't given that map by chance. The court sold you before you could walk, and someone up that mountain has been keeping your soul in a jar for twenty-six years, and the dead woman carried proof of it in her teeth when she crawled into your workshop."

"They're wrong." August heard themselves say it from very far away. "It's a mistake. Some other family. Some other name. My parents—" and here their voice failed entirely, because what parents? What family? August had been raised by a knife-sharpener who drank, then by nobody, then by the road. There had never been a mother named Mara turning her face to a wall. There had never been—

There had always been. The bone remembered. Bones remember better than the living do.

"They sold me," August said. The words came out flat, factual, obscene. "Before I could talk. Before I could *want* anything. They signed a line of salt and got grain and a passage south and I got—" their hand closed around nothing, around the shape of a jar they'd never seen, "—I got twenty-six years of being nobody. Of having nothing inside me worth naming."

"August—"

"That's not even my name, is it? It's the name I *made*. The name I built out of driftwood because nobody gave me one that wasn't already mortgaged." They were shaking. They could feel it starting in their hands and moving up their arms like cold water rising. "The dead woman kept saying *it belongs to you.* I thought she meant the map. She meant *me.* She meant the jar with my name on it, sitting on some shelf under the mountain, next to a farmer's lifetime and a winter's grain—"

The nearest thing was a shelf. August's hand found it without their eyes' help, and their arm swept it clean.

Clay pots burst against the far wall. Dried herbs rained down. A jar of rendered fat caught the edge of the hearthstone and split, and the fat ran toward the fire and went up in a soft whump of orange light, and August stood in the middle of the Ashwife's kitchen breathing like a bellows and wanting, with a clarity that frightened them, to break everything that could be broken.

So they did.

The stool went first, then the second shelf, then the row of hanging bundles that showered the floor with dried nettle and sage. The Ashwife did not stop them. She stood by the hearth with the little bone still cupped in her palm, watching, letting the storm spend itself, and when August's boot came down on a clay bowl and the crack of it rang through the room like a shot, the old woman finally spoke.

"Break it all," she said. "Then sit down, and listen, because breaking is easy and what comes after is not."

August stood in the wreckage, chest heaving, knuckles bleeding from a pot they didn't remember striking, and the rage in them hit the wall of the Ashwife's calm and rebounded, curdled, sank. The firelight lay orange across the destruction. Somewhere under the smell of burnt fat and crushed herbs, the smoke still hung faintly in the shape of a woman turning her face to a wall.

"She cried," August said. Quieter now. Worse than shouting. "My mother. She cried and she still signed. Or he signed and she cried. Either way—" their jaw worked. "Either way I was *currency* before I was a person. Twenty-six years of grain. That's what I cost. That's what I was *worth.*"

"No." The Ashwife crossed the wrecked kitchen, stepping over the shards without looking down, and took August's bleeding hand in both of hers, gray-stained and strong. "That is what you *sold for.* Worth is a different arithmetic entirely, and the court has spent centuries making sure people confuse the two. That is the whole of their economy, child. They buy the souls of the desperate at famine prices and sell them dear, and everyone starves twice—once in the belly and once in the ledger." Her grip tightened. "Your parents were starving and afraid. That does not absolve them. But it explains the price, if not the sale."

"And mine?" August pulled their hand free. "What am I selling *for*, right now? Because that's what this is. The courier, the map, the enforcers—I'm not choosing any of it, I'm just being *spent*, the same as I was spent the day I was born. Someone signs a line of salt and August goes wherever August is needed."

"Then un-sign yourself."

The words dropped into the room like a stone into a well. August stared at her.

"You cannot un-sell a soul by wishing," the Ashwife said. "But you can go and take it back. That map in your satchel leads to Mount Cindrel, and beneath Mount Cindrel is the Salt Court, and in the Salt Court is a vault, and in that vault—" she nodded at the little rib-bone in her palm, "—is a jar with Petra Villalobos written on it in a clerk's neat hand. Twenty-six years of interest, child. The court does love its ledgers. Everything owed comes due."

August looked at the map. It lay in its oilcloth on the table where they'd dropped it during the reading, and the sight of it now made their skin crawl—that innocent roll of hide, that folded cargo of ruin. The dead woman's voice had gone quiet since the reading, as if even she understood that some things needed silence, but August could feel her presence the way you feel weather coming.

They picked up the map. Carried it to the hearth.

Held it over the blue-hearted flame.

It would be so easy. Burn it, and the court's hunters lose the trail, and the name Petra Villalobos stays buried where it belonged, and August goes on being nobody, which had been enough for eleven years, which had kept them alive—

Their hand shook. The corner of the hide began to brown.

In the firelight, faint as breath on glass, the doubled-V sigil glowed up at them from the wax seal. Contract-holder's mark. Family mark. *Their* mark, whether they wanted it or not.

And beneath the roar of the fire August heard—not through any bone, just heard, the way you hear your own pulse—the dead woman's voice one more time, very clear, very tired, and very certain:

*It belongs to you. Come and take it.*

August pulled the map back from the flame. The scorched corner crumbled between their fingers, and they watched the ash of it drift down onto the wreckage of the kitchen, settling among the shards of the life they'd broken open.

"Grim purpose looks like that," the Ashwife observed, "from the outside. Like almost burning the thing you need most."

"What comes after breaking?" August asked. Their voice was steady now, and that steadiness frightened them more than the shaking had. "You said breaking was easy. What comes after?"

The Ashwife set the little rib-bone down on the table, next to the map, side by side—the oldest silence and the loudest secret August owned.

"Now?" She smiled, and it was not a comforting smile. "Now you plan a theft, bone-carver. The court stole from a starving family, and the family's debt has come due with interest, and you are going to walk down a mountain and collect." She began sweeping shards into a pile with the side of her foot, briskly, as if the world were ordinary. "And for that you will need three things. A guide who knows the flooded mines. A key that opens what should stay shut. And a lie good enough to get you past the white ministers."

August looked at the map. At the bone. At their own reflection, warped in a puddle of spilled fat, wearing a face they suddenly did not recognize—because it had never been theirs alone. It had been collateral all along.

"Petra," they said aloud, testing the name like a blade's edge, hating it, needing it.

Outside, the wind turned, and came down off the mountain smelling of salt.

* * *

They worked through the night, the two of them, in the wrecked kitchen the Ashwife refused to tidy. She spread the map fully at last, weighting its corners with clay shards, and read the wax seals one by one with methods she declined to explain—holding each to the blue flame, sniffing the smoke, occasionally pressing a seal to her tongue and grimacing.

"Flooded mines," she said, tapping a route inked in faded brown along the mountain's western flank. "The drowned stair. Smugglers' road, closed these ten years—or said to be. Whoever drew this knew it well." Her finger moved lower, to a cluster of marks near the mountain's root. "Antechamber. Auction floor. Archive. Vault." She looked up. "You'll need a guide for the stair. No carver walks black water on maps alone."

"I know a name," August said, and hated that they did. "Sefa. Salt-smuggler. Ran the Widow's Stair routes before she was disgraced. If anyone knows the drowned mines—"

"Sefa." The Ashwife snorted smoke. "Half the border towns have a Sefa, and the other half have a warrant for one. Find yours carefully."

Later, near dawn, while the Ashwife slept in her chair, August sat alone with the rib-bone in one hand and the map in the other, and did the only carving they had done in days: three lines, a circle, three lines again, cut fresh into the bone's end with a trembling blade. The old pattern. The child's pattern. Except now, when they held it, the bone was not silent.

It hummed—faintly, warmly, like a struck wire—with a voice that was almost their own, and was not, and would not be until they went down the mountain and took it back.

August closed their fist around it, and the rage in them settled at last into something harder and colder and far more useful.

Three things. A guide, a key, and a lie.

Come morning, they would go find the guide.
