## 7. Three Roads Down

The Ashwife's fire had burned down to a red heart by the time August finished counting what they had.

It was not much. A bone-carver's kit wrapped in oilcloth. Fourteen silver coins and a handful of coppers, earned from whistles and memorial beads in towns that would never remember their name. The map, rolled tight and smelling of brine and old wax. And a dead woman's voice, patient as frost, waiting in the marrow of every piece of ivory August touched.

*A guide,* the courier's voice had said, three nights ago, through the teeth of a sheep's jaw August was carving for a shepherd's dead son. *A key. And a lie. You'll need all three, carver, or you'll die in the dark with your own name in your mouth and no one to hear it.*

August had thrown the jawbone across the room. It had struck the wall, rolled, come to rest pointing at the door like a compass needle. The dead were rarely subtle.

Now they sat in the Ashwife's kitchen while the old woman stirred something that smelled of iron and pine resin into her tea, and August said aloud, for the first time, the shape of the plan they'd been turning over like a stone in the pocket.

"I need someone who knows the flooded mines under Cindrel."

The Ashwife did not look up from her stirring. "You need Sefa Quill."

"You know her?"

"Everyone on this side of the mountain knows Sefa Quill." The Ashwife set down the spoon. Her eyes, milk-pale and unblinking, found August's face with unsettling precision for a woman who claimed to see mostly through fire. "Salt-smuggler. Best there was, before she wasn't. She ran the drowned stair for eleven years—moved more salt past the toll-keepers than the guild moved through its own gates. Then one winter she came up out of the mines alone, with a full cargo and no crew, and she never went down again."

"What happened to her crew?"

"That," said the Ashwife, "is the question you'll want to ask her gently. Or not at all." She rose, joints cracking like green wood, and fetched a jar of honey from a shelf crowded with jars of less identifiable things. "She keeps a room above the tannery in Hallowford. Drinks. Carves notches into her table—one notch for each of them, I'd wager, though she'd deny it if you asked. She'll tell you no."

"All right."

"She'll tell you no twice. She'll tell you no until you're halfway out the door, and then she'll tell you yes, because that woman has been waiting six years for someone to give her a reason to go back down, and she hates herself for it." The Ashwife spooned honey into August's tea with the tenderness of someone dosing medicine. "Drink that. You look like a man who hasn't slept since the Widow's Stair."

"I'm not a man."

"No," the Ashwife agreed placidly. "You're something rarer and more inconvenient. Drink your tea."

* * *

Hallowford stank of hides and lye, the way river towns always did when they made their living off what animals left behind. August came down the hill road at dusk with their hood up and their hands in their sleeves, practicing being nobody again after two weeks of being hunted, and found the tannery yard quiet except for one lit window on the upper floor.

Sefa Quill turned out to be smaller than her reputation. She was a broad-shouldered woman gone soft at the middle, with rope-scarred forearms and a face that might once have been handsome before drink had redrawn its lines downward. She sat at a table scarred with hundreds of small notches, a bottle at her elbow, a knife in her hand—and she was carving another notch when August knocked, so absorbed in the ritual that the knock had to come three times before she looked up.

"We're closed," she said.

"I'm not buying leather."

"Then you're lost." But her eyes had already done their work: taken in August's ink-stained fingers, the fine bone dust still caught in the creases of their knuckles, the way they stood with their weight back, ready to leave. Smugglers' eyes, even pickled ones. "Carver. What does a carver want with me?"

"A guide."

Sefa laughed, and it was a terrible sound, like something breaking underwater. "No."

"The flooded mines under Mount Cindrel. The drowned stair."

The knife stopped moving. For a long moment the only sound was the tannery vats below, slopping and sighing like slow lungs. Then Sefa set the knife down with exaggerated care, the way a sober person handles a thing they might otherwise throw.

"Get out."

"Not yet." August sat down across from her without invitation, which was either courage or stupidity, and laid the map on the table between them—not unrolled, just resting there, a tight roll of stained hide with wax seals glinting dully at its ends. "I'm going into the Salt Court. I need someone who knows the ways water forgot."

Sefa stared at the map the way a drowning woman stares at a rope thrown to someone else. August watched her hands. They had gone white-knuckled on the table's edge, and one thumb kept moving, tracing an absent pattern—counting, August realized. Counting notches.

"There were nine of us," Sefa said, to no one, to the table, to the notches. "Eleven years running salt through the drowned galleries. I knew those tunnels better than my own mouth. And then—" She stopped. Drank. Started again, quieter. "And then the court raised the water out of season. Middle of a dry autumn, the black water came up the stair like it had been waiting for us. Like it *knew.* Eight of mine drowned in the dark. I came up with the cargo because the cargo floats, and men don't." Her eyes came up, bloodshot and flint-hard. "So no. Find another guide. Find a corpse and ask it. Get out of my room."

August didn't move. "The court raised the water because it knew someone was coming," they said quietly. "It's doing it again. Right now. The passes are sealing early; the enforcers are sweeping every town from Lowmark to the coast. Whatever window used to exist into that mountain is closing, and when it shuts, it shuts for a generation." They tapped the map once, lightly. "You've spent six years carving notches because you couldn't carve a way back down. I'm offering you the way back down."

"To do what? Drown properly this time?"

"To take something back."

Something moved behind Sefa's face—grief turning over, showing its other side, which was hunger. She reached for the bottle, stopped herself, reached again, and then shoved the whole thing away hard enough to crack it against the wall. Amber liquor spread across the floorboards, smelling of ruin.

"One condition," she said hoarsely. "If we find them—the eight—if there's anything left of them down there, anything the court took, we carry it out. Even if it costs the job. Even if it costs *you.* Swear it."

August thought of a vault full of humming jars. Thought of a voice in the bones saying *you'll want to take more than yours, carver, that's the trap and that's the truth.*

"I swear," they said.

Sefa looked at them for a long time. Then she pulled a fresh knife from her belt—a smuggler's short blade, salt-rimed at the hilt—and began, deliberately, to scrape away the first notch on the table's edge.

"No," she said, when August raised an eyebrow. "Not erasing them. Making room. There's going to be eight more marks when I come back up, and I'll need somewhere to put them." She scraped, and wood-dust fell like snow. "We leave tomorrow, before I get sensible again. Now get out of my room, carver. I have to say goodbye to a bottle."

* * *

That night, in the loft above the tannery where Sefa had grudgingly spread them a blanket, August took out the oldest piece of carved bone they owned—a small round token, worn smooth, the first thing they had ever carved with intent rather than accident, back when they were eleven and still believed names were free.

They held it in their palm and waited.

The courier's voice came slowly tonight, tired, like a wind working itself up to strength. *You saw the smuggler's hands. Did you watch them shake?*

"They shook."

*Good. Steady people make poor guides underground. The steady ones trust the tunnel, and the tunnel lies.* A pause, and when the voice resumed it had shifted, the way it did when it was circling toward something it wanted. *You have your guide. You don't have your key or your lie.*

"The lie I can manage. I've been nobody for twenty-six years; I can be a bone-merchant for a week. Veyra's name will open the antechamber—the Ashwife read it off the seal. But the vault needs a minister's key, and ministers don't hand those out to merchants."

*No,* the ghost agreed. *They don't.* The silence stretched, and August felt the token warm in their palm, felt the presence gathering itself the way a diver gathers breath. *But a voice can pass where hands cannot. Every court has its deaf spots, carver—rooms where the ministers sealed away what they didn't want to hear. I know where they are. I died finding one.*

August sat very still. "You want to come inside."

*I want to come home.* The words cracked, briefly, into something raw and young. *My bones are in a courier's satchel in a waystation outside Thornfell's foot—I paid a boy to bury me decent and he buried me shallow and I have felt every rain since. But my daughter is in that vault. I heard her jar humming the night I died; I heard it through the ice in my own chest. She was sold at fourteen, carver. Fourteen, for a season's coal.* The voice steadied itself with visible effort, frost reforming over water. *I can be your ears in the court. I can tell you which ministers lie, which corridors remember, where the deaf spots are. I can speak through any bone you carry, and no enforcer thinks to search a dead woman's voice. In exchange—*

"In exchange I carry your bones home after."

*After,* the ghost repeated, tasting the word. *To the waystation. Properly deep, below the frost line, with a stone that says my name. Maren. My name was Maren Ostreth. Say it.*

"Maren Ostreth."

*Again.*

"Maren Ostreth. I'll carve it myself. Deep enough to outlast the weather."

The token in August's palm grew almost too warm to hold, and then, gradually, cooled to something like a hand relaxing. When Maren spoke again, her voice had changed register entirely—all business now, brisk as a courier reading a route sheet, and August understood suddenly that this, too, was grief: the dead learning to fold their sorrow small enough to carry.

*Then here is how it goes. The court's antechamber admits merchants by seal and sample—you'll show bone-work, and I'll tell you what the ministers are listening for, because they listen for souls in the grain of everything and a clever carver can fake the hum. The deaf spot I found is behind the third gallery of the minting floor; the enforcers can't hear anything said or done within ten paces of it. That's where you'll plan. That's where you'll hide what you take.* A pause. *And carver—when you're inside, when you hear them, the thousands of them in the walls—*

"Yes?"

*Don't answer them. Not at first. If you answer, they'll know you can hear, and they will beg, and you are not strong enough yet to hear a thousand people begging and keep your own shape. Promise me that too.*

August looked down at the token, at the faint worn spiral they had cut into it twenty-six years ago with a child's blunt knife, and thought about how many promises they had made in the last two weeks—to Sefa, to the Ashwife, to a dead woman in the marrow of things. Promises were supposed to be for people who had futures.

"I promise," they said. "For now."

*For now,* Maren echoed softly. *That's all any of us ever manage.*

Below, in the tannery yard, a dog began to bark—not the idle barking of dogs at shadows but the flat, urgent rhythm of a dog that has seen something real. August blew out the candle and moved to the window, and down in the street, pale as a spill of milk in the lamplight, stood a figure in white, motionless, its face tilted upward toward the tannery windows one by one.

Counting doors.

August drew back into the dark, heart hammering, and felt Maren's voice rise cold and sharp through the token still clenched in their fist:

*It's begun early. We leave tonight.*
