## 8. The Drowned Stair

The water was wrong before they ever touched it.

Sefa knew the Drowned Stair the way other people knew their mother's face—by touch, by dark, by the particular cold of it. She had gone down these mines forty times in her smuggling years, and the water always behaved. It sat at the third landing in high summer, lapped the fifth in autumn, and never—*never*—rose out of season. The mountain kept its own calendar, and the calendar said: dry stair until first thaw.

But standing on the second landing with a hooded lamp held low, she could hear it. A sound like breathing. Slow, patient, wet.

"Tell me that's an echo," August said behind her.

Sefa didn't answer. She crouched and put two fingers into the black water pooling on the step, and the cold of it went up her arm like a confession. Too high. Two full steps too high, and moving—not rushing, not yet, but moving with the terrible unhurried confidence of something that had all the time in the world.

"It's not an echo," she said.

Behind her, the courier's bones shifted in the pack on August's shoulders—a bundle wrapped in oilcloth that clinked faintly when they moved, like a jar of spoons. Sefa still wasn't sure how she felt about traveling with a dead woman. She'd smuggled plenty of things down this stair: salt, brandy, fugitives, once a live snow-owl in a hatbox. Never a passenger who talked back through someone else's knuckles.

"She says the water knows we're coming," August said. Their voice had that flat carefulness they used when relaying the ghost, as if reporting weather. "She says it rose for the last people who came down here too."

"When was that?"

A pause. In the lamplight, August's face was all hollows and one bright eye. "Eleven years ago," they said. "She doesn't remember what happened to them."

Sefa stood up and wiped her hand on her coat, though the water didn't wipe off so much as it seemed to want to stay, a thin slick of cold that no cloth would take. Eleven years ago. She'd been seventeen, new to the trade, still believing that the mountain was just a mountain and the court beneath it was a story smugglers told to keep the green ones honest.

Eleven years ago was the year of the betrayal. Of course it was.

"We can still go up," she said, and hated how quickly the words came. "There are other ways in. Slower ones."

"No." August adjusted the lamp. "You said the flooded gallery connects to the old ventilation shafts. You said you knew the way."

"I did say that." Sefa looked down the stair, where the water waited. "I say a lot of things."

* * *

They went down anyway, because August Villalobos had the kind of stubbornness that didn't argue—it simply started walking, and let everyone else catch up or be left. Sefa followed, because following was easier than admitting she wanted to turn around, and because there was a debt between them now that she hadn't named aloud: three days ago, in the smuggler's den at Cindrelfoot, August had paid her price without haggling, which no one had ever done, and then asked her one question—*what did you lose down there?*—which no one had ever dared.

She hadn't answered. She was going to have to answer eventually. The mountain would make her.

The Drowned Stair itself was older than the mining roads above it—steps cut before anyone thought to square them, worn into shallow bowls by feet long dead. The walls sweated. Somewhere below, water moved through galleries with a sound like a crowd murmuring in another room. Sefa counted landings the way a drowning woman counts waves: third, fourth, fifth, each one wetter than the last, the water climbing to meet them instead of falling away beneath them.

At the sixth landing, the lamp guttered.

Not from wind. There was no wind down here. It guttered the way a flame gutters when something breathes near it, and in the stuttering light Sefa saw the water on the step ripple outward from a single point, ring after ring, as if something far below had exhaled.

"The air pressure's changing," she said, and heard her own voice come out too steady, the voice she used for clients. "That means the lower galleries are flooding ahead of us. Fast."

August had stopped. They were holding one hand against the wall, palm flat against the stone, and their expression had gone distant—the listening look. Sefa had seen it twice now and it still made her skin crawl, the way August's whole body tilted toward the rock like a plant toward sun.

"There are voices in the water," August said quietly. "Not the courier. Others. They're saying—" A frown. "They're saying *turn back*. But not kindly. They're saying it the way you'd say it if you were being made to say it."

The lamp steadied. Sefa found she had backed up two steps without deciding to.

"The court knows," she said. "Doesn't it. That's why the water's up. They've opened the sluices."

"You tell me. You're the one who knows this place."

"I knew this place eleven years ago." Sefa turned away and started down, because standing still was worse. "Water's water. It rises, it falls. We move fast and stay ahead of it, and we don't stop again no matter what your dead friend hears."

The courier's bones clinked. And through August's mouth, in a voice that was almost but not quite August's—lighter, quicker, with a laugh buried in it like a coin in bread—the dead woman said: *"Listen to her. She lies beautifully. Ask her about the ninth landing."*

Sefa's foot slipped on the wet stone. She caught herself hard, wrist screaming, and stood there in the lamplight with her heart going like a fist on a door.

"What," she said carefully, "did she just say?"

August looked at her. In the hooded light their face was unreadable, which was its own kind of answer—they'd heard it too, and they were deciding what to do with it.

"She says you know this stair better than you told me," August said. "She says ask you about the ninth landing."

* * *

The ninth landing was where the betrayal had happened, and Sefa had been walking toward it for an hour pretending she wasn't.

She told the story the way she'd never told it, in pieces, while they waded the eighth gallery thigh-deep in water that pulled at them like hands. Better to talk than to think. Better to fill the dark with her own voice than listen for the voices underneath it.

"There were four of us," she said. "Me, my brother Tam, and a couple out of Lowmark—a man called Odo and his wife, Merrin. We ran salt down the drowned road and sold it to the hill villages at prices the guild couldn't touch. Good work. Honest-ish work." Her boot found a submerged step; she hauled herself up and reached back for August without looking, an old reflex, and August took her arm with a grip stronger than she expected. "Then Tam got sick. Lung-rot, from the dust. And there was a cure—there is a cure—but it costs more than four smugglers see in a year."

The water deepened. The lamp threw their shadows huge and wavering across the ceiling, two giants walking on the roof of the world.

"And the court pays," August said. Not a question.

"The court pays," Sefa said. "In coin, real coin, minted from—you know what it's minted from. I didn't know then. Or I knew and didn't let myself know, which is worse. There's a man at the ninth landing gate, or there was—a factor, white-robed, polite as a priest. He buys passage-silence. You let a shipment through unremarked, you look the other way for one night, and he weighs out silver like sugar." She laughed, and it came out awful. "One night. That's what he asked for. One night, don't light the warning beacon at the stairhead, because a party was coming down the mountain road and the court preferred not to be observed."

August had gone very still behind her. "And the party?"

"The party was a family. Fleeing south with a sick daughter, avoiding the toll roads." Sefa's voice flattened out, went smooth as the black water. "I lit the beacon. Afterward. When I understood—or half-understood—what I'd done. Too late to matter. The factor had already weighed my silver, and Tam got his cure, and he lived nine years on it before the rot took him anyway, and every one of those nine years I heard that family's footsteps going down the mountain road in the dark."

The water was at their waists now, and rising as they watched—she could see it climbing the wall in slow pulses, each wave a finger's width higher than the last.

"The courier," August said. "Your dead woman. She was—"

"Merrin," Sefa said. "Merrin was the wife. And the sick daughter was hers, and I have spent eleven years telling myself the factor's shipment and that family were nothing to do with each other, and eleven years knowing better." She turned in the water to face August, and the lamp between them turned both their faces the color of old wax. "So yes. I know the ninth landing. I know it like a scar. And I'll thank you and your passenger both to stop dragging me over it."

For a moment nobody spoke—not even the dead—and the only sound was the water, climbing.

Then August said, quietly, without any judgment in it at all, which was somehow unbearable: "You came back down here anyway. To help me steal souls out of the place that paid you."

"Don't make it noble. I took your money."

"You took my money," August agreed. "Come on. The gallery bends left ahead. If your memory serves."

Her memory served. That was the problem with memory; it always served, whether you'd invited it or not.

* * *

The ninth landing announced itself by smell before sight: a mineral reek, salt and iron and something older, like the inside of a shell. The gallery opened into a vaulted chamber where the stair met the drowned road—a wide causeway running off into darkness, its surface just breaking the water, and set into the far wall, the gate.

It was bronze, tall as three men, crusted green and white, and it stood open. That was the first wrong thing. The second was the chain—snapped, links scattered on the causeway like a broken necklace, each link furred with fresh frost though the chamber was not cold enough for frost.

The third wrong thing was the level of the water, which had risen past every mark Sefa had ever known it to reach, and was still rising, and was doing so in rings. Outward from the center of the chamber. As if something below were stirring it.

"Lamp high," she said. "Now."

August raised it. The light swept the chamber and caught, at the far edge, a line of white.

Enforcers. Three of them, standing on a ledge above the waterline, salt-white robes hanging motionless, faces smooth as poured tallow. They weren't advancing. They were waiting, the way you wait at a door you've locked, and the tallest of them lifted one hand—not a threat, a gesture, almost courteous—and pointed down.

At the water.

Sefa looked down, and the lamp's reflection looked back up at her, and beneath the reflection, deep under the black, something pale was turning over. Vast. Patient. The drowned road's old flood-chamber, she realized—the sump below the landing, the place the mine water gathered before the sluices bled it off. Except the sluices were closed. She could see them, the great bronze doors in the chamber floor, shut fast, and the water rising against them like a tide against a dam, and somewhere in the dark below, whatever the court kept down here to mind its drowned threshold was waking up and stretching.

"They're not trying to catch us," August breathed. Beside them, the oilcloth bundle had begun to rattle, softly, continuously, like teeth chattering. "They're going to drown the stair. Seal it behind us. If we go forward—"

"If we go forward we're committed," Sefa finished. "Past the gate there's high ground. The ventilation shafts start dry. But the causeway's already under, and it's fifty yards of open water to the gate, and that thing in the sump is between us and it."

The courier's voice came through August then, and for once it didn't sound like laughter. It sounded urgent, stripped bare:

*"The beacon chain. On the wall. Smugglers' rope, tarred—it'll hold a soul's weight, and I should know, I'm carrying one."*

Sefa spun. There—above the snapped gate-chain, bolted to the rock, a length of greasy black rope, the old smugglers' lifeline, run through iron rings along the chamber wall from the landing to a point above the gate. She'd used it a hundred times. Every smuggler had. It was the first thing you learned and the last thing you trusted, because it was older than anyone alive and tarred by hands long dead.

"It'll hold," she said. "Maybe."

"Maybe is generous, coming from you today," August said. But they were already shrugging the courier's bones higher on their shoulder, already pulling the knife from their belt to cut a handhold loop, and Sefa understood that the decision had been made somewhere in the last minute without either of them saying it aloud.

The water shuddered. Below the surface, the pale vast thing turned again, closer, and the rings spread wider, and on their ledge the three white figures did not move at all.

"Sefa." August's voice, just their own, no ghost in it. "The ninth landing. You lit the beacon too late to save them. Is that the shape of it?"

"That's the shape of it."

"Then here's the difference." They tied the loop around her wrist themselves, quick and rough, their fingers steady as a surgeon's. "This time you get to be early."

Sefa looked at the water, and the water looked back, and something enormous began to rise beneath it, shedding darkness like a diver shedding depth.

She stepped off the causeway into open black, and climbed.
