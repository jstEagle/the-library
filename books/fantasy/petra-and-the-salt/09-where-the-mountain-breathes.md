## 9. Where the Mountain Breathes

The water let them go all at once, like a hand opening.

One moment August was swimming—or something like swimming, more accurately being carried by black water through a throat of stone with Sefa's rope biting into their wrist—and the next their boots found rock that rose in shallow terraces, and the flood was draining away around their ankles with a sound like a long exhale.

August stood in the dark and breathed, and immediately wished they hadn't.

The air tasted wrong. Not foul—wrong the way a stranger's house smells wrong, every breath full of someone else's life. Salt, yes, thick enough to coat the tongue, to crust in the corners of the lips. But under the salt was something older and warmer, something that made August think unbidden of their mother's kitchen they had never known, of woodsmoke and bread and a lullaby hummed off-key. Memories that were not theirs, arriving whole and unwelcome, the way a splinter arrives under skin.

"Sefa," they said. Their voice came out flattened, swallowed. The cavern ate echoes the way the flooded mines had eaten light. "Sefa. Where are we?"

"Give me a moment." A splash, a curse, the scrape of flint. "I'm counting my fingers and deciding how many I still have."

Light bloomed—a stub of tallow candle cupped in Sefa's scarred hands, flame bending toward August as if it too wanted to lean on something solid. The smuggler's face floated above it, gray-lipped, hair plastered flat, eyes red-rimmed from the drowning dark of the stair. She looked ten years older than she had at the Widow's Stair. So, August suspected, did they.

Then the candlelight reached the walls, and neither of them spoke for a long time.

* * *

The cavern was not a cavern. Caverns were pockets, hollows, accidents of water and time. This was architecture. The ceiling arched overhead at a height that made August's neck ache and their mind refuse the measurement, ribbed with columns of pale stone that rose from pools of standing brine like the ribs of some god-sized animal. And the pale stone was not stone.

It was bone.

August knew bone the way a priest knows scripture. They could read the grain of a femur the way farmers read weather, could tell a child's knuckle from a sparrow's skull by touch alone in a pitch-dark room. What rose around them now was bone grown vast, fused, mineralized into something between ivory and marble, and every column, every arch, every inch of the vaulted dark was veined with a faint, sourceless luminescence—the color of moonlight seen through milk, the color of the salt-glass jars the Ashwife had described, the color, August realized with a slow cold turning in their gut, of a soul seen from the inside.

"It's the mountain," Sefa whispered. Her candle trembled. "Cindrel. The old songs say the mountain was alive once. That it *breathed*."

"The old songs," August said, "were written by people who'd never been sold."

They began to walk. There was nothing else to do; the drained floodwater had left only one path, a causeway of bone running out across the cavern floor between still black pools, and the air itself seemed to pull them forward, each breath carrying those borrowed memories deeper. August kept their jaw clenched against them. A memory of snow falling on a wedding. A memory of a dog's ears under a calloused hand. A memory of dying—not the fear of it, but the fact of it, quiet as closing a door—over and over, layered like sediment, until August understood what they were breathing.

Souls. Ground fine as flour and worked into the very air. The Ashwife had said the court minted souls into currency, and August had imagined coins, ledgers, vaults. They had not imagined this: that the wealth of the Salt Court was not stored here but *dissolved*, that the mountain itself had been fed on the dead poor until its bones gleamed, until the air was a broth of everyone who had ever been traded, spent, and forgotten.

The dead woman's voice had been silent since the drowned stair. August missed it with an ache that frightened them. For three weeks the courier—Vess, she had finally given them her name somewhere in the black water, gasped it between one flooding gallery and the next—had been a constant presence, sharp-tongued and frightened and stubbornly alive in the way of ghosts. Now there was nothing but the chorus.

And the chorus was everything.

* * *

It started as a pressure behind the eyes. Then a ringing, like the after-hum of a struck bell that would not fade. Then voices.

Not one voice. Not ten. Thousands.

They came up through August's bones the way water comes up through chalk, unstoppable, finding every seam. Each soul in the walls, in the air, in the luminous veins of the bone, was still *sounding*—still echoing the shape of the life it had been, the way a bell keeps the shape of its note long after the strike. A farmer's patience, humming low and brown as loam. A child's quick silver flicker, gone before August could turn toward it. An old woman's grief worn smooth as river stone, repeating, repeating, repeating. They layered and braided and contradicted one another, a chord with ten thousand notes, and August's gift—which had always been a narrow door, one dead voice at a time through one carved piece of ivory—was kicked wide open.

They heard everything.

They heard a man who had sold his own winter so his daughter wouldn't go hungry, and they heard his daughter forty years later selling hers for the same reason, and they heard the terrible arithmetic of it, generation upon generation, the mountain's economy built out of love turned inside out. They heard names. Hundreds of names, thousands, pouring through them faster than any mind could hold: *Maret, Joss, Ilsette, Brannic, Tam, Tam, another Tam, Wren, Wren, oh, Wren—*

August stopped walking.

The causeway went on ahead of them, pale and straight, and the pools on either side had begun to glow faintly where August passed, brightening like coals under a bellows, because the chorus had noticed them. The dead had noticed someone who could hear. The hum swelled. It pressed inward, tender and enormous, and August felt the borders of themselves going soft as wet paper.

*Here,* said a voice that might have been anyone, that might have been everyone. *Here is no hunger. Here is no debt. Here you can rest, little carver, here you can set down the weight, here you can be part of the song instead of alone outside it—*

It would be easy. That was the horror of it, the thing August would remember all their life: how *easy*. Their hands were already loosening. Their name—they had never had one, that was the whole wound of them, Villalobos was a label on a contract, Lowmark's bone-carver was a function, nobody, nobody, nobody—their non-name was dissolving, and the chorus rushed into the vacancy like water into a footprint on sand, warm, welcoming, offering the one thing August had wanted since before they could want it: to belong to something larger than themselves.

Their knees touched the bone floor. They did not remember kneeling.

Somewhere very far away, Sefa was saying their name. It sounded like it came through a wall of glass.

Then, closer than Sefa, closer than August's own heartbeat:

*"Get up."*

Vess. The courier's voice, arriving not through the air but through the small knuckle-bone August carried in their breast pocket—her anchor-bone, the piece of her August had promised to carry home, and which had warmed suddenly against their sternum like a coal.

*"August. Listen to me. That warmth you feel—that's them loving you. That's real. But the rest of it, the softness, the belonging—" * the ghost's voice cracked, and for a moment she sounded exactly like the frostbitten woman who had died on August's workshop floor, twenty-six years old and furious about it. *"I felt it too, at the end. In the water. It offered me the same thing. And I nearly took it, and then I remembered my daughter is still in a jar down here, and I chose the ache instead. Choose the ache. The ache is yours. The softness is theirs. GET UP."*

August got up.

It was the hardest physical act of their life. Harder than the Widow's Stair in an ice storm, harder than the first whistle they ever carved from a dead child's bone while the mother watched. They planted their palms on the glowing floor and pushed, and the chorus screamed—not in anger, August understood dimly, but in loss, the way a crowd moans when a dancer leaves the circle—and they stood, and they said aloud, in a voice scraped raw:

"My name is August Villalobos. I am nobody's. I am *mine*. And I am here for what you were robbed of—all of you—but I have to be *me* to do it."

The hum faltered. Softened. Somewhere in the vast dark, one small silver flicker—the child, Wren, or a Wren, there had been two—flickered once, brightly, almost like a wave.

Sefa caught August's arm as they swayed. Her grip was iron and her face was wet, though whether from the flood or otherwise she would have denied to her grave.

"You went somewhere," she said. "Your eyes went white as the enforcers' coats. Don't do that again."

"I'll try," August rasped, "to schedule it better."

* * *

They walked on, and the walking helped. Bodies are good anchors; August had learned that young, when the voices first came and the only cure was work—carve, plane, sand, breathe. Now they counted their steps and named the muscles of their feet as they moved and kept Vess's knuckle-bone in their fist, and slowly the chorus receded from a flood to a tide, from a tide to a distant surf. Still present. Always present now, they suspected, for the rest of their life. But bearable, if they held the bone.

The causeway climbed. The pools gave way to terraces, and the terraces to galleries, and the galleries showed them the scale of what lay beneath Mount Cindrel. Whole districts of the deep dark, lit by that milky soul-light: streets of bone, bridges of bone, stairs spiraling up into vaults above and down into depths below. Empty, all of it empty, a city built for multitudes and holding only wind and echo—and yet not empty, never empty, because the walls themselves were the population. A city of the mined dead, still singing their lives into the stone, waiting for a mint, a market, a buyer.

"They built heaven down here," Sefa said quietly. She had stopped pretending her eyes were dry. "Look at it. All that beauty. And it's a *larder*."

"No," August said. Something had shifted in them on the causeway floor, some load-bearing wall of grief giving way and revealing a room behind it they hadn't known they had. Their voice came out level and cold and certain. "Heaven doesn't keep accounts. This is a bank wearing heaven's face."

Ahead, the causeway ended at a gate.

Even from a hundred paces, even in the strange diffuse light, August could see it was different from everything else in the cavern. Where the city was grown, organic, curved, the gate was *cut*—a massive slab of translucent salt-glass framed in bone polished mirror-bright, and through its surface glowed a steady amber radiance, warm as lamplight, warm as the lie the chorus had offered. Beyond it, faintly, came sounds that had no business existing under a mountain: the murmur of a marketplace. The clink of coin. A crier's voice, lifted in auction-song.

And on the threshold, motionless as statuary, stood figures in white.

Three of them. Tall, featureless, their coats seamless as frost on a window, their faces smooth where faces should be. They did not patrol. They did not shift. They simply stood at the gate of the Salt Court, and August understood with absolute certainty that they had been standing there since before August was born, and would stand there after, and that they were waiting.

Sefa's hand found August's sleeve. "That's the merchant's entrance," she whispered. "Disguises. We agreed. Bone-merchant and porter. You have the samples?"

August touched the satchel at their hip—the carved pieces, the memorial whistles, the small beautiful lies of their trade. Inside, wrapped in wool, sat the oldest bone they owned, the reading-piece, the one the Ashwife had used to pull their true name out of the dark. And beside it, warm as a sleeping animal, Vess's knuckle-bone pulsed once, twice, like a second heart.

*"They're waiting for us,"* the ghost said softly. *"Of course they're waiting. The mountain told them the moment we surfaced. But listen, August—"* and her voice took on the crisp edge of the courier she had been in life, a woman who had crossed the Thornfell passes in winter because no one else would carry what she carried—*"they know *someone* is coming. They don't know it's you. Twenty-six years, and they've never once wondered what happened to the infant whose soul they bought. To them you were never a person. You were a line in a ledger."*

August looked at the white figures, and at the amber gate behind them, and felt their half-formed plan—the disguise, the lie, the porter's lowered head—harden into something with edges.

"Good," they said. "Ledgers can be stolen from."

They pulled up their hood, shouldered the satchel of bones, and walked toward the gate where the mountain's breath tasted of everyone they had come to save.
