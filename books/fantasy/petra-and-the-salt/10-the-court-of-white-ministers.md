## 10. The Court of White Ministers

The disguise was Sefa's idea, and like most of her ideas it smelled faintly of brine and desperation.

"You're not a thief down here," she'd said, lashing the last of the wax seals to August's collar with a length of gut-cord. "You're a merchant. Merchants are welcome everywhere in Cindrel. Thieves get dissolved." She had tapped the bone-handled knife at August's belt. "Leave that. A carver's hands don't need steel."

So August had come up out of the smuggler's warren wearing a coat of dyed goatskin that fit them wrong in every way a coat could fit wrong, carrying a satchel of carved pieces they had made by candlelight over three sleepless days—whistles, buttons, a small lion with a chipped ear—and telling themselves, with each step deeper into the mountain's white heart, *I am nobody. I am a trader in dead things. Nobody looks twice at a trader in dead things.*

The lie held for exactly as long as it took to reach the antechamber.

* * *

The Salt Court did not look like a court. It looked like the inside of a tooth.

August had imagined vaulted halls, thrones, banners—the trappings of the surface kingdoms they had spent a lifetime avoiding. What they found instead was a cavern so vast the ceiling was a rumor, its walls ribbed and glistening, every surface crusted with salt that had grown in slow geological whorls, like the rings of a tree that had been weeping for ten thousand years. The light came from nowhere and everywhere: a pale luminescence rising from the floor itself, as though the stone remembered sunlight and could not stop dreaming of it.

And the air—

The air was the worst part. August had tasted salt on the wind since the Widow's Stair, but here the salt had *flavor*, and the flavor was memory. One breath brought their mother's kitchen—not their real mother, the one who had sold them, but the foster-woman in Lowmark who had taught them to hold a blade—and the smell of barley porridge. The next breath carried woodsmoke from a hearth they had never sat beside, a lover's name they had never spoken, the particular green smell of a summer meadow that belonged to someone else's childhood entirely. Thousands of lives, steeping into the stone like tea.

They staggered against a salt pillar and pressed their palm flat to it, and the pillar *sang*.

Not aloud. Not in words. But August's gift had never needed words; it needed only bone, and salt was the oldest bone there was, the mineral skeleton of the world itself. Under their hand the pillar hummed with a woman's patience, decades of it, the long endurance of someone who had hauled water and buried children and kept going anyway, and then—abruptly, at the end—a confusion, a falling, a cold that was not winter.

"Steady," murmured the courier's voice, close against August's ear where no one else could see her. Her ghost rode in the courier-bag now, in the fingerbones August had promised to carry home, and she had learned to speak through them even muffled in canvas. "Breathe through your mouth. Don't touch anything you don't have to."

"I'm not touching anything," August whispered back. "It's touching me."

"Then be touched politely. You're a merchant, remember? Merchants are made of money, not grief."

August straightened. Around them, the antechamber moved with a crowd that should have been impossible this far under a mountain—hundreds of figures in furs and wool and silk, merchants from every road August had ever sworn off, clustered in knots around pillars hung with lanterns of salt-glass. Their breath fogged and glittered. Their voices echoed strangely, arriving half a heartbeat after their lips moved, as if the cavern were considering each word before passing it along.

And moving among them, unmistakable, were the white ones.

The enforcers did not walk so much as *proceed*, in robes of undyed wool gone grey-white with salt frost, their faces wrapped to the eyes in cloth so that only their gazes showed—pale irises, pale lashes, eyes like chips of the ceiling. They carried no weapons that August could see. They didn't seem to need any. Wherever one of them passed, the crowd parted with the smooth practiced motion of water around a stone, and conversations resumed a register lower.

One of them stopped near August. Stopped, and turned its wrapped face, and regarded them for the space of four heartbeats.

August did what a merchant would do. They lifted the flap of their satchel, drew out the little lion with the chipped ear, and held it up into the lantern-light with the weary pride of a craftsman showing wares. "Carved mammoth ivory," they said, pitching their voice into the bored drawl they used on tourists in Lowmark. "From the far north trade. Fine detail work. You won't find better grain below the snowline."

The white gaze moved from the lion to August's hands—to the scars, the calluses, the fine old nicks of thirty years at the blade—and something behind those pale eyes seemed to weigh them, the way August themselves weighed a bone before cutting, judging what it would bear.

Then the enforcer inclined its head, almost courteously, and moved on.

August let out a breath they felt all the way down in their half-hollow chest. In the bag, the courier's bones gave a dry rattle that might have been laughter.

"You're better at this than you think," she said. "Dead men make honest liars."

"I'm not dead."

"No," she agreed, and there was something in her voice August couldn't parse—pity, maybe, or its opposite. "But you're halfway to being merchandise. Look around you, carver. Really look."

* * *

So August looked.

The antechamber narrowed ahead into a colonnade of salt pillars, and beyond the colonnade, sunk into the cavern floor like an amphitheater drowned in milk, lay the auction floor. There were no seats for spectators—or rather, there were, tiers of them cut into the salt, but they stood empty, because the buyers preferred to stand, to mill, to pretend they were not here for what they were here for. At the center of the floor rose a block of white stone, waist-high, worn smooth as soap by centuries of use.

A minister stood beside it.

He was not white-robed like the enforcers. He wore dove-grey, immaculate, with a chain of office made of interlocking salt-glass beads, each bead the size of a plum and each one, August realized with a lurch, *occupied*. Faint lights moved inside them, slow as fish under ice. His face was kind. That was the horror of it—it was genuinely, warmly kind, the face of a village physician or a favorite uncle, arranged in permanent gentle concern.

"Friends," the minister said, and his voice filled the cavern without effort, the acoustics handing it from wall to wall. "We have a late addition to today's schedule. A consignment come down from the high valleys—arrived only this morning, and fresh."

Murmuring. Shifting. Somewhere to August's left, a fat man in sable licked his lips.

Two enforcers led the consignment forward.

He was a farmer. There was no other word for him. Big-knuckled, sunburnt even now, even here, with the deep tan of a man who had spent his life bent over open ground under an open sky. He wore his good coat, the one with the brass buttons, and he had combed his beard, and he walked between the white-robed figures with the dazed obedience of a man who has been told so many times that this is normal that some load-bearing part of him has begun to believe it.

His eyes found the crowd. Found the faces. And August watched him do the arithmetic that the poor are forced to do all their lives: *which of these people might help me, and what will it cost me to ask.*

Nobody met his eyes. August did not meet his eyes either. Later—they would carry this later like a stone in the boot—they would tell themselves it was because meeting his eyes would have broken the disguise, would have undone everything, would have doomed the souls in the vault and the courier's daughter and their own stolen self. It was even true. It was true, and it was also the most cowardly moment of their life, and both things lived in them at once, the way two things can live in one body when the body is what's for sale.

"Subject is male," the minister said pleasantly, consulting a slate. "Fifty-one years of age. No debts outstanding—that's rare, friends, let us appreciate it. A lifetime of clean living, sober habits, no feuds, no blood-price. The appraisal came in this morning." He turned the slate so the front rows could see, and August, craning despite themselves, saw the figure written there in a clerk's neat hand, and felt the floor tilt beneath them.

Forty years of plowing. Forty years of harvests, of children raised, of a wife presumably waiting above in the high valleys with a supper going cold. Appraised, weighed, and entered into a ledger at a value that would not have bought the fat man's sable coat.

"The reserve," the minister announced, "is one winter's grain. Milled. Delivered to the subject's household, with our compliments, annually, for the natural term of his family's need."

The farmer's head came up. "My daughter's got the lung-rot," he said, to the crowd, to anyone. "The physicians wanted forty silver just to look at her. This way—" his voice cracked and he mastered it the way men of his sort master everything, brutally, privately—"this way she eats. Every year. However long she needs."

"That's right," the minister said warmly. "That's exactly right. Your family prospers, friend. And you—" he gestured, gentle as a priest, at the smooth white block— "you'll rest somewhere very safe. Very secure. Some of the finest souls in the province pass through this room, and yours is a good one. Clean. We can always tell."

The bidding began.

It began quietly, the way rot begins—a nod here, a raised finger there, the fat man in sable opening at a modest bid with the air of a man doing charity. A grain factor bid against him. A woman with a ledger-chain at her belt bid in scrip. The numbers climbed in units August could not see but could feel, the whole cavern leaning in with the particular hunger of respectable people at a respectable price, and the farmer stood on the block the whole time with his hands folded, watching himself be priced, and every few moments he glanced toward the entrance as if his daughter might have followed him down after all, as if love might arrive late and put a stop to it.

Love did not arrive. Bidding closed at two winters' grain and a cask of oil.

"Sold," said the minister, "to the house of Corvel," and the fat man in sable came forward and shook the farmer's hand, actually shook it, and said something low and hearty that made the surrounding buyers chuckle, and the farmer laughed too—laughed, God help him, relieved, *grateful*, shaking the hand of the man who had just bought the rest of his life, because his daughter would eat, because there had never been another door.

The enforcers led him away toward the inner galleries. He went the way oxen go, without being driven, having long ago accepted the shape of the chute.

* * *

August stood in the colonnade's shadow with the satchel clutched to their chest like a shield, and understood, in one long sickening wave, what they were standing inside of.

Not a court. Not a vault. Not some aberration, some secret evil hidden under the mountain. An *economy*. The grain that fed the high valleys in the bad years came from here. The scrip in the woman's ledger-chain, the oil in the cask, the sable coat, the salt-glass lanterns burning memory-light over their heads—all of it minted out of people. Out of the poor, always the poor, because the poor were the only ones whose lives could be bought for the price of staying alive. The mountain did not prey on the world above. It *underwrote* it. Every famine relieved, every debt forgiven, every physician's fee paid in the valleys within a hundred miles of Cindrel had been paid with somebody's mother, somebody's father, somebody's fifty-one years of clean sober living, appraised and auctioned and poured into the walls to flavor the air with other people's summers.

And somewhere in those inner galleries, in a jar on a shelf, was a soul sold twenty-six years ago by two frightened parents for—what? August had read enough contracts in the Ashwife's firelight to know the rates. A hard winter. A failed crop. Perhaps a physician's fee, for a daughter with lung-rot, or a son, or for themselves. The farmer on the block was not an exception. The farmer was the *pattern*. August's whole life—the foster-mother, the anonymity, the carving, the careful nothing they had made of themselves—had been built on top of a transaction, the way a house is built on a foundation, and they had never once felt the cold coming up through the floors.

"They don't even resist," August whispered. The courier's bones stirred in the bag.

"Why would they?" she said. "I nearly sold myself, the year my girl was small. Stood in a line outside a court annex in Marrowdale for six hours in the rain. The only reason I walked away is the man ahead of me fainted, and I saw what came out of him afterward." A pause, dry as old paper. "Fear is a luxury of people who still own themselves, carver. He wasn't brave or weak. He was just poor, in the exact way the mountain was built to farm."

"He shook his hand."

"What else was there to shake?"

August's hands had gone cold around the satchel strap. Inside it, their fingers found the little lion with the chipped ear, and the whistle they had carved from the dead child's bone in Lowmark—no, they hadn't packed that one, they'd buried it—but their thumb remembered it anyway, the fine grain of it, the last song caught inside. Everything they had ever carved had been a way of keeping the dead audible. Of saying: *you were here, you mattered, I heard you.* And here was a cathedral built to the opposite principle, an entire economy organized around making the dead silent and spendable, and the whole thing ran so smoothly, so *politely*, with ministers in dove-grey and buyers who chuckled, that the people being fed by it lined up in the rain for six hours to join it.

The moral horror did not arrive like a thunderclap. It arrived like water rising in a mine—quiet, level, patient, filling every low place—and August stood in it up to the chin and thought, with terrible clarity: *Sefa's flooded stair. Veyra's key. The courier's daughter humming in the dark. None of it matters unless I burn this whole thing down, and none of it is possible unless I first walk through these rooms smiling.*

"Minister," said a voice nearby, crisp and official. "The bone-merchant. The one the gate registered this morning. The Minister of Small Souls requests an inventory of the new stock."

August's blood stopped.

An enforcer stood at their elbow—how long had it been standing there?—and beside it, holding a slate and a stylus, a clerk with the harried expression of all clerks in all ages. Beyond them both, at the far end of the colonnade, a door of black salt-glass stood open onto a corridor lit in soft amber, and in that doorway waited a slight figure in dove-grey robes, hands folded, face kind, watching August with an attention that had nothing casual in it at all.

Veyra. Minister of Small Souls. The name Sefa had pried out of a drunk archivist three nights ago, the name that was either August's way into the vault or the trap that ended everything.

The courier's ghost breathed one word through the canvas, barely a sound: *"Go."*

August lifted the satchel, arranged their merchant's smile over the horror underneath like a cloth over a stain, and walked toward the door.
