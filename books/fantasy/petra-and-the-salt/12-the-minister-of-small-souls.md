## 12. The Minister of Small Souls

The minister's office was the smallest room in the Salt Court, and that was how August knew she was nothing here.

Everything else in the antechamber had been built to make a person feel like an insect beneath a cathedral: pillars of veined white salt rising into darkness the eye couldn't finish, floors polished until each step echoed back at you as if someone else had taken it. But Veyra's office was tucked behind a filing gallery on the third ring, low-ceilinged, lit by a single lamp of rendered tallow that smelled faintly of old grief. Shelves crowded the walls from floor to ceiling, and every shelf was packed with ledgers—thousands of them, spines cracked, corners furred with salt-bloom. A room for small accounts. A room where nobody important ever looked.

August had been waiting in it for two hours, seated on a stool too short for them, while ministers' footsteps passed and repassed the door like tide against a breakwater. They had come in as a bone-merchant, which was true enough now to be comfortable—a sample case of carved pieces riding their hip, each one holding a whisper they'd coaxed into it over three sleepless nights in the lodging cells assigned to visiting traders. The disguise was good. The disguise was the best work they'd ever done, because it wasn't a disguise at all.

When the door finally opened, the woman who entered was not what August expected.

They had imagined a minister as the enforcers were imagined: tall, salt-white, faceless. Veyra was small and gray and tired, with ink stains up both forearms and spectacles mended twice with wire. She looked, August thought, like a woman who had spent thirty years counting other people's thefts and had long ago stopped pretending any of it would add up to anything. She closed the door behind her with the particular care of someone who has learned that doors are the only privacy left in the world.

"You're the carver," she said. Not a question. "The one who's been leaving samples with the requisition clerks."

"I leave gifts," August said. "Merchants give gifts."

"Merchants give gifts hoping for contracts." She set her lamp down, sat behind her desk—a real desk, scarred oak, the only wood August had seen since entering the mountain—and folded her hands. "You gave a clerk a whistle yesterday that made him weep at his station. He couldn't say why. He thought he was ill." Her eyes came up, pale and level. "I know why. So let us not spend the evening pretending we are both something other than what we are. You are not a merchant. And I am not fooled. What do you want?"

*The courier's voice*, August thought, *would like this woman.* They said instead: "Access to the vault."

Veyra did not flinch. That was the first thing August truly noticed about her—that nothing moved in her face unless she put it there. "And what makes you think a minor minister of small souls has access to the vault?"

"Because you hate this place, and hatred is the only key anyone ever picks for themselves."

For a long moment the lamp hissed. Somewhere far above—or below; distance meant nothing in the Court—a bell tolled the change of watch, and the sound came through the salt walls softened, like a voice heard through snow.

"You have been here four days," Veyra said at last, "and you think you understand what hatred is worth in this mountain." She rose, went to the shelves, and ran one finger along a ledger spine without pulling it out. "Hatred is worth exactly what it can be traded for, and no more. It is not worth my life. It is not worth—" she paused, and something almost human crossed her face before she filed it away again, "—it is not worth certain other things. So. Before I decide whether to have you seized by the White Hands, tell me what you actually intend to do in the vault, and be precise, because I will know if you lie. I read contracts for a living. Lying has a texture."

August had rehearsed this. They had rehearsed it in the dark of the trader's cell with the courier's ghost murmuring at their shoulder, refining the words until they were smooth as river stone. But sitting under the tallow lamp, looking at ink-stained hands and wire-mended spectacles, they found the smooth words wouldn't come, and what came out instead was the truth, rough-hewn:

"There's a jar in there with my name on it. I'm going to take it back."

* * *

Veyra sat very still.

Then she laughed—not cruelly, but with a dry, exhausted sound, like wind through a dead reed bed. "Twenty-six years I've served this court. Do you know how many people have sat in that stool and told me they were going to rob the vault?" She held up fingers. "Three. One was lying to test me—he was a minister himself, checking my loyalty. One was mad. And one—" her hand closed slowly, "—one was serious, and I turned her in, and I have regretted it every day since, which is its own peculiar punishment, because the court does not reward loyalty, it merely consumes it."

"The third one," August said quietly. "A courier?"

Veyra's eyes sharpened. "You knew her."

"She gave me the map. She died giving me the map." August reached into their coat—not fast, telegraphing the motion, letting the woman see everything—and drew out the roll of hide, laying it flat on the scarred oak between them. The wax seals caught the lamplight, dull as old scars. "Her name was Maren Ostreva. She had a daughter. The daughter's voice is in the vault—I've heard it through the walls. Maren spent the last year of her life trying to buy her child's soul back out of this mountain, and your court hunted her across two ranges for it, and when she was dying she pressed this into the hands of a stranger because a stranger was all she had left."

The lamp hissed. Veyra looked down at the map for a long time. When she spoke again, her voice had changed texture entirely—the clerk's dryness gone, replaced by something older and wearier and more dangerous.

"Maren Ostreva came to me eleven months ago. Sat in that exact stool. Asked me for exactly what you're asking." Her finger traced the edge of the map without touching it. "And I told her the truth, which was that the vault cannot be robbed—it can only be bargained with, and the court does not bargain with thieves. And then I sent word to the Ministers of Seals, because I was afraid, and fear in this place always travels upward." She took off her spectacles and pressed the bridge of her nose. "She got out anyway. Slipped the cordon at the flooded stair. I told myself that meant the court hadn't acted on my word quickly enough, that her escape was luck, that my hands were still clean." A pause. "Her hands were clean too, once. Everyone's are, at the start."

"So you'll turn me in too," August said. "Is that what this is? A confession before the seizure?"

"I am deciding," Veyra said, "what kind of coward to be this time." She put her spectacles back on, and when she looked up, the decision had already settled into her face like sediment into water. "Show me what you can do. Then we'll talk about the vault."

* * *

What August could do, they did with the smallest thing in their sample case: a button-sized disc of bone, carved over three nights with a pattern so fine it looked at first like plain polish.

"This belonged to a man who died in Lowmark two winters ago," they said. "A cooper. He had a daughter he never told anyone about—born on the wrong side of a marriage, sent away north. He wrote her letters every year and burned them, because he didn't know where to send them and couldn't bear to keep them." They set the disc in Veyra's palm. "Hold it to your ear."

Veyra held it to her ear.

August watched her face while the bone spoke. It was the same everywhere, that listening—the way a listener's whole architecture of composure leans slightly toward the sound, the way the eyes go somewhere else. The cooper's voice was saying what he'd said in the last letter, the one he'd burned unopened even by fire: *If the north is cold, little wren, remember the workshop was warm, and you were warm in it, and warmth doesn't stop being true just because you leave the room.*

Veyra lowered the disc. Her hand was not quite steady.

"That's not possible," she said. "Souls don't echo. Souls are minted. The resonance is stripped in the rendering halls—that's the entire purpose of rendering. What's left is currency. Clean. Silent." She looked at the disc as though it might bite her. "How are you doing this?"

"I carve the memory into the ivory while the voice is still in it. The bone remembers. I just—give it somewhere to speak from." August hesitated. This was the part they'd sworn not to explain, the part that made their gift a curse as much as a livelihood, and yet they heard themselves go on anyway, because this gray tired woman had held a dying courier's name for eleven months and carried it like a stone. "It isn't only the dead I've known. Any bone holds something. The older the bone, the deeper the voice. In the galleries outside your office, the walls themselves are singing—all those mined souls worked into the salt. I can hear them. Thousands of them. If I hadn't had—" *a dead woman anchoring me*, they almost said, and didn't, "—if I weren't careful, they'd have pulled me apart by now."

Veyra set the disc down on her desk with exaggerated gentleness, the way one sets down something explosive.

"Do you know what you're describing?" she said. "Do you understand what you've brought into this mountain? The entire economy of the Salt Court rests on one axiom: that a soul, once minted, is silent. Property. A jar of value with no voice in it. Every contract, every auction, every winter's grain bought with a farmer's lifetime—all of it depends on the merchandise never being able to testify." She stood, walked to the window slit that faced the inner galleries, and stood with her back to August. Through the slit, faintly, came the sound August had been hearing for days: the vast, layered hum of the walls, thousands of stolen lives resonating in the salt like a wet finger on a glass rim.

"If the court learns that a carver exists who can make the merchandise speak," Veyra said, "they will not seize you. They will use you. There are ministers who would give a province to have the voices quieted forever, and others who would give more to have them *amplified*—to mint fear itself, audible grief, weaponized memory. You are not a thief, carver. You are a munition walking around in a merchant's coat." She turned. "Which means I have exactly two choices. Give you to the court, or help you get out of it before it understands what it's holding. And I find—" her mouth twisted, "—that I have already been the first kind of coward once."

* * *

They talked until the lamp burned low and Veyra fed it a second candle without either of them noticing.

The shape of it emerged slowly, the way a carving emerges—first the rough outline, then the details neither party had planned. Veyra could not open the vault; her seal was a minor one, good for records and requisitions, worthless past the Vault of Voices' outer door. But she could do something better than open it. She could *schedule* it.

"The vault is inventoried twice a season," she said, sketching the third ring's corridors on the back of a requisition form with quick, practiced strokes. "The next inventory falls in nine days, when the winter quotas are sealed. For one watch—four hours—the vault stands open and every senior minister is required to attend the sealing ceremony in the Auction Hall. The vault will be guarded, yes, but by juniors. Juniors whose rotation I write." A thin, humorless smile. "Thirty years of writing rotations. I know precisely which junior guards fall asleep, which ones drink, and which one—this is the important one—which one owes me, and for what."

"And the jars," August said. "Once I'm inside. How do I carry them out?"

Veyra stopped drawing.

"Ah," she said softly. "There it is. The question you haven't asked anyone, because everyone you've met so far has been either a smuggler or a ghost, and neither of them knows." She laid down the pencil and folded her hands again, and August saw that whatever she was about to say had cost her something to learn, and had stayed in her like a splinter. "Listen to me carefully, because this is the one thing I know that the court itself barely admits, and it will save your life. A soul in a jar is stable. A soul released into air evaporates—minutes, sometimes less, like breath on cold glass. The rendering halls exist partly to prevent exactly that loss; waste is sin, here. The only vessel that can hold a soul outside its jar—" she tapped her own sternum, once, "—is living flesh. Carry it in your body or lose it to the air. That is the law of it. No one knows why. The oldest ministers claim even the Founders didn't know; they only discovered it, horribly, in the first winter, when the first harvest was spilled in a tunnel collapse and every soul lost simply... went. All that value. Gone like fog."

August sat with that. Thought of the plan they'd carried down the drowned stair: jar in a pack, pack on a back, up through the mines, out into the snow. A bag full of breathing glass.

"And if the jar breaks on the way out," they said slowly, "the soul doesn't die. It just—unravels. Scatters."

"Scatters, evaporates, returns to whatever it returned from before the court learned to catch such things. Call it what you like. The accountants call it shrinkage." Veyra's voice was bitter enough to strip paint. "Four thousand souls in that vault, carver. Four thousand, give or take the quarter's auctions. Even if you could carry one in flesh—you, specifically, one soul in one body, and yours is already spoken for, which I suspect is the whole point of this expedition—how many jars fit in a smuggler's pack? And every one you spill on the stairs is a life gone past saving. Forever. Is that the theft you came to commit?"

August didn't answer. Because the honest answer was that they hadn't gotten that far—that the plan had ended, always, at the moment of taking the jar, the way a fever ends at waking, with nothing mapped beyond. Sefa would have an answer about routes. The courier's ghost might have an answer about the vault itself. But the question Veyra had just asked—the arithmetic of it, four thousand against one body—was a question no one had dared ask them yet, and it sat in their chest like swallowed ice.

"One more thing," Veyra said, and now her voice dropped, and she glanced at the door, and August understood they had arrived at the true price of the evening. "You will need work papers to move between rings on inventory night. I can forge them—but forged papers are matched against a worker's mark, and you have none. So you will need to *be* someone. And I happen to have a dead clerk on my rolls, a small-souls appraiser who died last spring of lung-rot, whose replacement paperwork I have—entirely legitimately—been delaying." She slid a blank ledger page across the desk, along with a stick of sealing wax the color of dried blood. "His name was Osten Ferrow. Learn it. Learn his hand; you'll sign in it. And learn this too, carver, before you agree to anything: if you are caught, I was never in this room. I will denounce you loudly, publicly, and with genuine feeling, because that is the only way either of us survives the night. Can you live with being betrayed by an ally as part of the plan?"

August thought of Brannoc, and of the message they'd sent up-mountain that had not yet been answered, and of all the ways a person could be betrayed on purpose and by accident and how rarely you got to choose which.

"Yes," they said. "I've had practice."

Something flickered across Veyra's face—recognition, maybe, of a fellow survivor of other people's choices. She melted the wax, pressed her minor seal into it, and pushed the finished paper across the desk.

"Nine days," she said. "Inventory night. Fourth watch. Be at the records gallery wearing Osten Ferrow's face, and bring your knives." She snuffed the lamp, and in the sudden dark her voice came dry and quiet and, for the first time, almost warm:

"And carver—if you hear a voice in these walls that sounds like a woman asking after her daughter—tell her the debt is being paid."

Outside the door, the corridors of the Salt Court hummed with their thousand stolen voices, and August walked back through them toward the traders' cells with a dead man's name in their mouth and nine days in which to become him—while somewhere above, unheard by anyone, the first flakes of the court's manufactured winter were already gathering against the mountain's crown.
