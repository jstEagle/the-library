## 13. Winter Falls Early

The water told her first.

Sefa had spent eleven years learning the language of flooded mines—the way a passage sighed before it swelled, the way cold came up through the stone like a warning whispered through a wall. So when the black water in the Drowned Stair went still as glass at midday, she knew something above had changed. Rivers did not go quiet. Mountains did not hold their breath unless something was coming down their throat.

She stood in the mouth of the third gallery with her lantern held high and watched the surface of the flood lie flat and perfect, reflecting her own face back at her: sharp-boned, salt-scoured, twenty-nine years old and looking fifty. The reflection stared at her the way her mother used to stare at her across the supper table—waiting for her to say the thing she'd sworn she'd never say again.

*You promised no one,* she reminded herself. *That was the whole point of you.*

But the water stayed still, and stillness in a mine was never peace. It was attention.

By dusk she had climbed to the seam of daylight where the stair's upper landing met the mountainside—a crack in the rock barely wide enough for a thin woman with a pack, opening onto a shelf of scree below the tree line. She had chosen this spot three days ago because it gave her sight of the pass and cover from it both, and because if the worst happened she could be down the stair and gone before anyone knew she'd hesitated.

The worst happened at full dark, and it was beautiful, which was how she knew it was the court's doing.

Snow began to fall—not drifting, not settling, but falling straight down in fat, deliberate flakes, as though the sky had been tipped like a jug. It came out of a cloudless night. Sefa watched stars vanish one by one behind it, and the temperature dropped so fast that her breath cracked in the air and the wet stone under her hands grew a skin of ice. In the space of an hour the pass that had been merely difficult became a white throat, sealed. She could hear it happening: the deep groan of cornices loading up on ridgelines, the tick and hiss of snow welding itself to rock, the silence spreading outward like ink in water.

No storm she'd ever ridden out had come down so fast or so clean. Storms were weather. This was policy.

"They've closed the door," she said aloud, just to hear a voice that wasn't hers answer with nothing.

The plan had been simple, the way plans made by desperate people are simple. August and the courier's ghost would go down into the Salt Court and come back up the Drowned Stair with what they'd stolen, and Sefa would hold the route—keep the water levels watched, keep the lanterns set, keep the boats patched—and then they would all walk out over Thornfell together before the winter gates shut, and Sefa would take her cut and her bones south to Merrow Bay and never think about salt again.

It was a good plan. It had exactly one flaw, which was that it required the mountain to behave like a mountain instead of like a creditor.

Sefa sat down on the frozen scree with her back against the rock and did the arithmetic she'd been avoiding for three days. The passes were sealed now—weeks early, weeks *deliberately* early. Which meant the court knew someone was coming for them. Which meant they hadn't sent enforcers down the stair yet only because they wanted to know who and how many, or because they were setting the trap with care, or because—

Or because they already knew everything, and the snow was just tidiness.

Her hands were shaking. She looked at them without surprise. They'd been shaking since the second day, since the moment in the flooded gallery when the water rose out of season and she'd understood that somewhere below them, someone had turned a wheel.

*You could leave.* The thought arrived wearing her own voice, calm and reasonable, the voice she used on frightened clients. *Nobody would blame you. Nobody would even know. You wait until the stair floods proper, you take the long tunnel east to the Vent shaft, you're in Lowmark inside four days with your skin and your share of nothing. Villalobos is dead the moment they catch her anyway—you can't fight a court. You're a smuggler, not a martyr.*

It was a good argument. She'd have believed it from anyone else.

The trouble was the shape of the thing underneath it. Eleven years ago she had stood in a room very much like this one—cold, dark, a decision balanced on a knife-edge—and she had chosen herself, and a man named Odo Ferrin had drowned in the dark because of it, and she had told herself for a decade that he'd have done the same, that survival wasn't betrayal, that the water would have risen anyway. She had told it to herself so often and so smoothly that she'd almost sanded the truth flat. But the truth had edges. She could feel them tonight, all of them, every one.

Odo had trusted her. That was the whole of it. He had handed her the rope end and turned his back to swim the flooded crossing first, because she was lighter and he was stronger and that was how they'd divided the world between them, and she had let go of the rope.

Not dropped it. Let go. There was a difference, and she had spent eleven years not looking directly at the difference, and now it stood in front of her wearing snow.

"Damn you," she said—to Odo, to the mountain, to the woman currently walking into the Salt Court carrying half a plan and a dead woman's bones. "Damn every one of you."

The snow kept falling, patient as paperwork.

* * *

She went down the stair because standing still had become unbearable, and because there was work, and work was the only prayer she'd ever known how to say.

The Drowned Stair at night was a cathedral nobody had meant to build. Her lantern threw light along walls that miners had cut a century ago and the water had been finishing ever since—arched galleries where the flood rose and fell with the seasons, carved steps descending into black water, air shafts breathing faintly overhead. The stone here was streaked white with mineral bloom, so that in the lantern light the whole passage looked furred with frost, a winter that had nothing to do with the one sealing the passes above.

She checked the marks first. Three days ago she'd cut gauge-lines into the third gallery's pilings at the seasonal low-water level, and she checked them now with a fisherman's patience, holding the lantern close, watching for the water to breathe against the stone.

It didn't. The water lay against the lowest mark and did not move, and its stillness was worse than rising. Rising meant weather. Stillness meant a hand on the lever.

"All right," she murmured. "All right, you great white bastard. I see you."

She spent the night working, because her hands needed it. She patched the second boat's seam with pine pitch and waxed canvas. She moved the cache of dry clothes and lamp oil from the low landing to the high one. She re-rigged the guide ropes along the flooded crossing with new knots, double knots, knots she tied while telling herself she was tying them for the money, for the cut August had promised, for purely professional reasons, and heard her own mother laughing at her from thirty years away: *Sefa girl, you always did lie loudest to yourself.*

Near dawn she slept two hours sitting up, knife in her belt, and dreamed of Odo swimming, and woke with salt on her lips that wasn't hers.

On the second day the enforcers came—or nearly came. She heard them before she saw anything: a sound like sand poured slowly onto a drumhead, coming down the upper galleries from the direction of the court. Salt-white figures moving through the dark with a smoothness that made her stomach turn, four of them, walking the mine tunnels as if the tunnels had been built for them. They passed within forty feet of her hiding place behind a fallen roof-beam, close enough that she could hear the whisper of them, a dry granular hiss, like the sea rehearsing.

They stopped at the third gallery. One of them crouched and touched the water.

Sefa did not breathe. She watched the white figure dip its fingers into the flood and lift them, and though she couldn't see its face beneath the smooth blankness of it, she felt—she would swear this later, in the dark, to no one—the sensation of being read. Of the water reporting her. Of three days of warmth and lamplight and boat-patch rising up out of the flood like oil.

Then the figure stood, and the four of them walked on downward, toward the court, and did not turn aside to look behind the beam where a smuggler sat with a knife in her fist and her heart going like a drum in a parade.

They hadn't seen her. Or they had, and didn't care. Or they had, and were saving her.

Every possibility was worse than the last. She waited until the hiss of them faded entirely, then another hour, then let her breath out in a long shaking thread.

"They know," she said to the empty gallery. Her voice came back off the white-streaked stone, small and doubled. "Of course they know. They've always known."

And here was the thing she arrived at then, sitting in the dark with the water lying still as a mirror and the snow sealing the world above: knowing changed nothing about what she owed. That was the ugly, liberating arithmetic of it. If the court knew everything—if August was already caught, already opened up like a contract, already being weighed in some white minister's scales—then Sefa's escape bought nothing and cost everything, and staying was a fool's errand.

But if there was any chance—any at all—that August was still moving through those deep galleries with a jar in her arms and a dead woman's voice at her shoulder, then the only door out of the mountain was the one Sefa was sitting beside. And doors, in Sefa's long experience, were the one thing worth dying slightly for. People could survive losing gold, names, fingers, years. What killed them was a locked door at their back while footsteps came on.

Odo had died because of a door she'd opened too late. Or not opened. She still couldn't make the memory agree with itself.

"I'm not letting go of the rope," she said. "Hear me? Whoever's listening down there—I'm not letting go."

The water didn't answer. But somewhere far below, so far the sound was more pressure than noise, something groaned—a deep structural complaint, stone shifting on stone, the mountain clearing its throat. And the still water in the third gallery shivered once, all across its surface, like a face changing expression.

* * *

On the third day she made her choice official, the only way a smuggler knows how: she burned her own exit.

The Vent shaft was the eastern tunnel, the smuggler's insurance, the crawl-space she'd kept secret through two interrogations and one marriage, the hole that led to Lowmark and safety and the rest of her life. She'd kept it clear for eleven years the way other people keep savings. Now she took the last of her pitch and kindling and collapsed its mouth with a controlled fall of the rotten prop-timbers, bringing a hundred tons of loose shale down behind it in a rolling white thunder that shook dust from the ceiling of the whole eastern gallery.

When the echo died she stood in the settling cloud, coughing, eyes streaming, and felt—strangely—lighter. Lighter and more trapped, both at once, which she supposed was what loyalty actually felt like when you finally got honest about it. Not warmth. Weight, willingly carried.

"There," she said. "Now it's not a choice. Now it's just a fact."

She spent that day and the next making the stair ready the way a harbor-master makes ready for a fleet: lanterns hung at intervals along the flooded crossing, wicks trimmed, oil topped. Boats bailed and turned right-side-up. Dry blankets sealed in oilcloth. The guide ropes inspected twice. She marked the water hourly, and the water marked her back—it had begun, very slowly, to rise. A finger's width by noon. Two by evening. Out of season, out of reason, steady as a debt accruing interest. The court was filling the mines. Whether to drown intruders or seal the vault or simply tidy up, she couldn't guess, but the message was legible enough: whatever was going to happen down there should happen soon.

On the fifth day, the ghost found her.

She was asleep at the high landing when the cold came—not the ordinary cold of the mountain but a specific, localized cold, like a hand laid flat against her chest, and she woke with her knife already drawn and a woman standing at the edge of the lantern light.

The courier. She knew her at once, though she'd never seen her alive: the frostbitten face, the salt crusted at the corners of her mouth, the look of someone who had run a very long way and intended to finish the run regardless. Except she was translucent at the edges, and the lantern flame stood perfectly still through the place where her body should have bent the air.

"You're the smuggler," the dead woman said. Her voice was strange—like speech remembered rather than heard. "Sefa. August said you'd hold the stair."

"August says a lot of things." Sefa got to her feet slowly, knife still in hand, feeling foolish and keeping it anyway. "Is she alive?"

"For now." Something moved across the dead woman's face that might have been a smile, worn thin. "She has the jar. She has her half. And she has made the mountain very, very angry." The ghost turned her head, listening to something Sefa couldn't hear, and when she spoke again her voice had dropped, urgent. "They're coming up behind her. All of them. Every white hand in the court. She's maybe an hour ahead, no more, and she's carrying weight and she won't leave the others' jar even to run faster, so don't hope she'll be quick. Hope she'll be stubborn."

"The water's rising. The crossing will be chest-deep within the hour."

"Then we'd best get her across before it's past that." The ghost looked at the flooded stair below them, at the lanterns Sefa had hung, at the boats and ropes and the small stubborn architecture of readiness, and something in her translucent face eased. "You stayed."

"Don't make it a thing."

"It is a thing." The dead woman's gaze was terribly direct, the way the dead can afford to be. "I had a daughter. Did August tell you? She heard her in the vault." For a moment the courier's composure slipped sideways, and Sefa saw the whole human ruin of her, the mother under the messenger. "I ran messages through these mountains for nine years and I told myself every time I took the dangerous road that it was for her. And then I sold my soul's safekeeping to pay her school-fees, because I told myself that was for her too, and look where telling yourself things gets you." She lifted her chin toward the darkness below, where the water waited, rising. "You told yourself you'd leave. You didn't. That's all any of us get to be, in the end—the last thing we told ourselves, kept or broken."

Sefa's throat was tight. She busied her hands with the nearest rope, checking a knot that didn't need checking. "Get down to the crossing," she said roughly. "If she's coming, I want the boats in the water and the lanterns lit before she rounds the last bend. And you—" she pointed the knife at the ghost, aware of how it looked, refusing to put it away, "—you go meet her. Walk ahead of her. Be her lamp. That's what couriers do, isn't it? Deliver things."

The ghost smiled properly then, brief and bright as a struck match. "Yes," she said. "It is."

She went down into the dark without disturbing the air, and Sefa stood alone at the landing with the water rising below and the snow sealing the world above, and listened.

At first there was nothing. Then, faint through the stone, from the deep galleries where the court's foundations gripped the mountain's roots, came a sound she had no name for—a vast resonant humming, many-voiced, like a choir singing through a wall of granite. It was getting louder. It was coming closer. And threaded through it, unmistakable, was the crack and grind of something enormous beginning to break.

Sefa spat on her palms, took up the bowline, and started hauling the first boat to the water's edge.

"Hold on, bone-carver," she said to the mountain, to the dark, to whoever was listening. "Your door's open. Come find it."
