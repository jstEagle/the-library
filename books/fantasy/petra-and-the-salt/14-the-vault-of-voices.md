## 14. The Vault of Voices

The key was small enough to swallow.

That was August's first thought as they turned it over in the dark of the service gallery, feeling its weight settle into their palm like a pebble from a riverbed. Veyra had pressed it there an hour ago, in the shadow between two pillars, without ceremony, without even meeting their eyes. It was made of bone—of course it was bone, everything in this mountain was bone or salt or the terrible marriage of the two—and it had been carved so finely that the surface seemed to ripple when August moved, like water remembering a stone. Along one edge ran a line of characters too small to read by any light August dared carry. They did not need to read them. The key hummed against their skin with a note they recognized from their own workshop: the sound of something that had been loved by its maker and then used for purposes its maker would have wept at.

*Left at the third breath,* Veyra had said. *The vault does not have doors, August. It has lungs. You must enter the way you would enter a living thing—gently, on the exhale, never while it draws in.*

August had asked her what she meant, and she had looked at them then, really looked, with those minister's eyes that were white as unlit candles, and said: *You carve bone. You already know how to listen for the moment a body lets go.*

Now August stood before the third breath.

It was not a door. It was a seam in the wall where the salt had been allowed to heal imperfectly, a scar left deliberately unfinished, and as August pressed their ear against it they understood what Veyra had meant. The wall breathed. Slowly, enormously, the way a sleeping mountain might breathe if mountains slept—and between each exhalation there was a pause, a held emptiness, a silence so complete that August's own heartbeat sounded obscene inside it.

They waited through two breaths. Three. On the fourth exhale, when the seam loosened like a fist remembering how to open, August slid the key into the scar and turned.

The bone sang.

It was not loud. It was the opposite of loud—a note so pure and low that August felt it in their teeth, in the wet chambers of their ears, in the hollow behind their sternum where the courier's voice had lived for so many weeks. The seam widened. Cold air poured out, air that tasted of salt and old snow and something underneath both, something sweeter and more terrible: the smell of preserved time.

August went in on the exhale, gently, the way you'd slip your hand under a dying animal to lift it, and the wall closed behind them with a sound like a held breath finally released.

* * *

The vault was not what they had imagined, though they could not afterward have said what they had imagined instead.

It was round. That was the first wrongness—that such a place should be round, soft-arched, almost tender, when every other chamber in the Salt Court had been built to remind you of your smallness. The ceiling curved overhead like the inside of a skull. The floor gave slightly beneath August's boots, yielding as packed snow, and when they looked down they saw that it was not stone at all but centuries of fine white dust—the shed residue, perhaps, of everything stored here, the way a moth sheds powder from its wings.

And rising from that floor, in concentric rings, were the shelves.

They climbed toward the ceiling in tiers, hundreds of them, thousands, carved from salt-glass so clear it seemed less solid than the air itself. And on the shelves, arranged with a care that made August's throat close—arranged the way a mother lays out a child's clothes, the way a carver lays out finished work—stood the jars.

Each jar held a light.

Not firelight, not glowworms, nothing so explainable. Each jar held a small shifting radiance the color of dawn seen through fog, and each light moved—not flickering, but *moving*, turning slowly on itself like something asleep that still dreamed of dancing. And from each jar came a sound. A hum. Every one different. Some low and steady as a cello's open string; some thin and wavering; some broken into stuttering fragments, like a song remembered only in pieces. Thousands of jars. Thousands of voices. The combined sound should have been deafening, but instead it wove itself into a single vast chord, a chord that was not heard so much as *inhabited*, and August stood at the edge of it feeling the hair rise along their arms because they knew this chord.

They had been hearing its edges all their life. In every piece of ivory they had ever carved. In the whistle of a dead child. In the courier's voice rising through stolen bone. This was where the music came from. This was the well.

"Oh," August said aloud, and the word fell dead in the chord, swallowed whole.

*Careful.* The courier's voice, faint, coming not from the walls now but from the small bundle of bones August carried against their chest, wrapped in oilcloth. *Don't listen with your whole self. Listen the way you'd walk on river ice—one ear always on the crack.*

"I know," August whispered. "I've been nobody my whole life. I know how to hold myself apart."

But even as they said it, they felt the chord reaching for them, patient as water, and they began to walk.

* * *

The aisles had no labels. That was the second wrongness, and it took August longer to understand. In Lowmark, in every market town, goods were named, priced, claimed. But here the jars bore no names, no numbers, only small marks near their bases—tiny sigils pressed into the glass, and August realized after a long moment that these were not identifiers at all. They were *denominations*. The way coins are struck with the faces of kings. A farmer's lifetime here, a weaver's there, a soldier's, a midwife's—and the marks told you only what each soul was worth, never who it had been.

Because who it had been did not matter. That was the whole architecture of the place. The court had spent generations perfecting the art of making people into currency, and currency has no face, no name, no voice—except here, in the vault, where every coin screamed silently forever.

August walked until they found the shelf they needed. Veyra's map of the vault—drawn from memory, drawn in trembling charcoal on the back of a minister's inventory sheet—had placed the newest acquisitions on the innermost ring, lowest tier, where the salt-glass was thickest and the light of the jars fell directly onto the white dust floor. *New acquisitions,* she had written, and beneath it, smaller, as if ashamed: *I catalogued forty-one last winter. I did not ask where they came from. I am telling you this so you will not trust me more than I deserve.*

The innermost ring. Lowest tier. August knelt.

The jars here were younger—their lights brighter, their movements quicker, souls not yet settled into the long sleep of storage. August moved along the row, reading the denomination marks, hating themselves for reading them, hating the fluency with which they had learned the court's arithmetic in only days. And then, near the end of the tier, they saw it.

A jar slightly larger than the others. Its glass was older, yellowed at the shoulders like teeth, and the sigil pressed into its base was not a denomination mark at all but a name.

*Villalobos.*

August's own hand reached out and stopped, hovering, trembling, an inch above the glass.

Twenty-six years. Twenty-six years this jar had waited on a shelf in the dark, holding the part of them that had been sold before they could cry properly, before they could walk, before they could want anything at all. Someone had carried it down here—an enforcer, a clerk, some functionary with cold hands—and set it among the others, and the ledger would have recorded it in the court's neat accounting: *one soul, infant, unformed, value pending.* Value pending. As if August had been a debt still accruing interest. As if the person they had become—the hands, the gift, the fierce careful loneliness of their whole life—had been waiting here all along to be collected.

They picked it up.

It was heavier than it should have been. That was the first thing—the sheer physical weight of it, far more than glass and light could account for, as though twenty-six years of unlived weight had settled into it like sediment. And it was warm. Warm like a sleeping animal, like a bird cupped in two hands, and August felt the warmth travel up their arms and into their chest and something in them *turned over*, the way a foal turns in the womb, recognizing.

The hum of it was theirs. They knew it instantly, the way you know your own footsteps in a dark house. It was the note that had been missing from every silence of their life, the flat places in them they had always taken for temperament, for damage, for the simple cost of being born wrong-shaped for the world. It was none of those things. It had been here. It had been *here*, humming in the dark, all this time, and it knew them—it rose toward their touch the way a plant rises toward a window—

And that was when August heard the other voice.

* * *

It came from three jars down.

At first, in the vast chord of the vault, it was only one thread among thousands, indistinguishable. But August's gift had never been volume; it was *discernment*. They could pick a single dead voice out of a choir the way their mother—some mother, some woman who had sold them—might once have picked their cry from a nursery of infants. And this thread snagged on them and would not let go, because it was young, and it was frightened, and it was singing a lullaby.

Not just any lullaby. August knew it. Everyone from the Thornfell valleys knew it—the *Shepherd's Counting*, the song mothers sang to children afraid of the dark, counting sheep one by one until sleep came. But this version was sung wrong, sung the way a child sings it, skipping verses, inventing words, and August knelt before the third jar down and looked into its light and understood.

The courier. The woman who had died in August's workshop with salt crusted on her lips, pressing a map into their hands, gasping a name she should never have known. She had spoken of a daughter exactly once, in the early weeks, when her voice was still strange to August and full of static: *My girl is seven. If you find her jar—if you ever find it—tell her the song wasn't finished. Tell her I was counting.*

August had carried that promise like a stone in the boot for weeks, telling themselves they would deal with it later, after, when they were whole. Later. There was always a later, and it was always a lie.

August lifted the daughter's jar.

The light inside flared at their touch, startled, and the lullaby broke off mid-count—at nine, at *nine*—and became something else. Not words. The dead in the jars were past words, mostly, worn smooth as river stones by their long storage. But this one was young enough, new enough, and what came through was a feeling shaped like a question: *Mother? Is that—*

"No," August said, and their voice cracked in half. "No, little one. I'm not her."

The light dimmed. And then, hesitantly, it brightened again, and the feeling reshaped itself, and August felt it land in their chest like a small cold hand: *You're the one she talks about. You're the one she wouldn't stop talking about. She said you'd come.*

August sat down on the white dust floor, right there in the vault of the Salt Court, with their own soul warm in one hand and a dead child's in the other, and wept.

They wept the way they had not wept since Lowmark, since the whistle, since the mother who had paid them coin they didn't need for the last song of her girl. They wept for the child on the shelf who had been counting sheep in the dark for however long the court had held her. They wept for the courier whose bones lay wrapped against August's heart, whose voice had nagged and guided and refused them, who had died carrying proof of a stranger's theft because no one else would. They wept for themselves—for the flat places, the missing rooms, the twenty-six years of humming they had mistaken for silence.

And beneath the grief, quiet as frost forming, another feeling grew.

Rage. Not the hot rage of chapter six, of the shattered workshop and the nearly-burned map. This was colder. This was the rage of a carver looking at fine work done for evil purposes, and it said, in August's own voice: *There are forty-one new jars on this tier alone. There are thousands on the shelves above. Every one of them is somebody's daughter. Somebody's sold child. Somebody's counting.*

*I cannot take only mine.*

*You must,* said the courier's voice, sharp with fear, rising strong from the bundle at August's chest. *August. Listen to me. Veyra told you—reclaimed souls must be carried out in flesh or they evaporate. One soul, maybe two, hidden against your body. Not a jar. Not dozens. You'll kill us all trying to save us all, and the court will mint every one of us anyway.*

"I know," August said.

They looked down at their own jar. Warm. Patient. Humming the note that would make them whole, that would fill every flat place, that would let them walk out of this mountain finally and entirely themselves. All they had to do was take it and go. All they had to do was be, for once, the person everyone had always assumed they were: someone who survived alone, who owed nothing, who carried no one.

August had been that person for twenty-six years. They knew its exact weight, and it was lighter than a child's jar, and it was unbearable.

"Help me," they said—to the courier, to the daughter, to the thousand humming shelves. "Help me think."

And the vault, which had been holding its chord around them like a tide, changed.

It was subtle at first—a shift in the hum, the way a forest goes quiet before weather. Then jar by jar, ring by ring, the voices began to turn toward August, the way flowers turn toward a window. Not words. Not demands. Something older than either: recognition. The mined dead, the stored dead, the counted and catalogued and denomination-marked dead, sensing in the vault's still air the presence of someone who could *hear* them—truly hear, the way no one in this mountain had heard them in centuries.

The chord swelled. The salt-glass shelves rang softly in sympathy. Somewhere far above, August felt rather than heard, a deep structural groan pass through the mountain's bones, like a giant shifting in sleep.

*August,* the courier whispered, and her voice was shaking now, and not only with fear. *What are you doing?*

August looked at their own jar. At the daughter's. At the forty-one new lights on the tier, and the thousands beyond.

"I'm going to need a bigger plan," they said.

Far off, through miles of salt and stone, the vault's swelling chorus found something to resonate against—a fault, a weakness, a hairline fracture running through the mountain's roots—and the first faint tremor shivered up through the white dust floor, delicate as a plucked string.

Above them, muffled by a hundred feet of rock, an alarm bell began to ring.
