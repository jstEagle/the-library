## 15. Half a Soul

The jar was warm.

August had expected cold—salt and glass and the grave's long chill—but the moment their fingers closed around it, warmth bloomed up through their palms like a sleeping animal stirring in its bedding. Not body-warmth. Something older. The warmth of a hearth someone had tended for twenty-six years without ever being thanked.

*That's mine,* they thought, and the thought did not feel like triumph. It felt like reaching into a stranger's cradle.

The vault stretched away from them in ranks and terraces, shelf upon shelf of salt-glass jars, each one humming at its own pitch. Some hummed low, like a man snoring after a hard day in the fields. Some chimed high and thin, children's notes that made August's teeth ache. The courier's ghost stood beside them—or rather, her voice stood beside them, a presence in the marrow of every bone August carried, and she was quiet now, quieter than she had been since Lowmark, as if even the dead held their breath in this place.

*Her daughter is here,* the voice said at last, very soft. *Third terrace. The small blue seal.*

"I know," August whispered. "I know."

They had come for one jar. One. Their own name on their own contract, their own soul in its own glass, and out again before the ministers finished their evening counting. That was the whole of the plan, and plans were the only thing August had ever trusted more than silence.

But the vault was full of names.

They set their own jar down on the reading ledge with exaggerated care, as one sets down a sleeping child, and climbed to the third terrace. The jars there were smaller. Younger. August found the blue seal and read the label through the salt-frost: *Maren Villalobos-Errék, aged nine years, purchased from the estate of the flood-year, lot 114.*

Not the courier's daughter by blood. By debt. The woman whose bones rode in August's satchel had died carrying proof of a sale, and somewhere between Lowmark and the mountain she had told August—through ivory, through dreams, through the stubborn insistence of the dead—that the girl whose soul was sold alongside hers was not her daughter but her neighbor's, an orphan of the same flooded valley, and that she had promised the mother, dying herself, that the child would not be alone in whatever came after.

Dead women kept promises badly. They kept them forever instead.

August lifted the small jar. It weighed almost nothing. Inside, the salt-light moved like minnows.

"Lot 114," they said aloud, and their voice cracked on the number. "I'm sorry. I'm not your mother. I'm not anyone. But I know where the door is."

Behind them, the vault began to sing.

It started as a single note—one jar, high on the far wall, shivering in its niche—and then another answered it, and another, and within three heartbeats the whole chamber was ringing with alarm, if alarm could be said to have a melody. Because it did, here. August understood that with terrible clarity: the Salt Court had no bells, no horns, no shouting guards. The stolen souls themselves cried out. Every jar in every terrace flared salt-white, and the sound they made together was the sound August had heard once before, in a burning workshop in Lowmark, when a child's bone sang its last song—a chorus of everything taken, announcing its own theft.

*Run,* the courier's voice said. *August. Run now.*

"I'm trying," August said, clutching two jars against their chest—their own, heavy and warm; Maren's, light as a bird's skull—and the light was spreading, running along the seams of the shelves like fire along a fuse, and somewhere above, doors that had been silent all night began to open.

They made it four steps toward Veyra's corridor before the floor changed.

There was no other way to describe it. The stone under their boots went soft—not crumbling, not shifting, but *attentive*, the way bone goes attentive under a carver's blade, aware, responsive. August stopped. The singing jars dimmed to a watchful glow. And beneath their feet, in a voice that came up through the soles of their shoes and the bones of their ankles and settled, finally, behind their sternum, something spoke.

It was not a minister's voice. Ministers, August had learned in three days inside the court, spoke in ledgers: sums, lots, interest, yield. This voice had no numbers in it at all.

**You are holding what is owed,** it said. **Two debts leaving one debtor's hands. State your account.**

"The vault," August breathed. "The vault itself—"

**Every coin remembers the hand that spends it. Every jar remembers the hand that fills it. I am the remembering. State your account, thief.**

August's mouth was dry as chalk dust. Around them the salt-light pulsed in slow waves, and they could feel it now—feel *it*, the vast patient pressure of ten thousand stored lives, all of them listening through the glass the way August listened through ivory. All their life they had been the one who heard. Now they were heard.

"My account." They laughed, and it came out wrong, half sob. "Fine. My account. Twenty-six years ago my parents sold me. Lot—I don't even know my own lot number. That's my account. I was sold before I could hold a spoon, and I've spent my whole life being nobody so nobody could spend me, and last night I walked into the belly of the thing that bought me and took myself back, and—" they lifted Maren's jar, "—and I couldn't leave her. That's the whole ledger. That's everything I have."

Silence. The kind of silence that weighs.

Then: **You are correct. You are lot 3,091. Your balance was paid in full at minting. You are therefore not stealing. You are withdrawing.**

"That's—" August blinked. "That's good? That means I can go?"

**Withdrawals require the depositor's key or the depositor's death. Neither condition obtains. Therefore the withdrawal is irregular. Irregular withdrawals incur penalties.**

The temperature of the room dropped. Frost feathered across the nearest jars, and the warm weight against August's chest—their own soul, their own jar—grew suddenly, terribly cold, as though something were reaching into the glass and taking hold.

**Penalty schedule:** the vault continued, in the tone of a clerk reading weather. **Option one: return both jars. No penalty. Option two: proceed. Penalty assessed against the withdrawing party.**

"What penalty?"

**You will find out when you owe it.**

Above, the doors were opening faster now. August could hear footsteps—many, unhurried, the terrible calm tread of enforcers who had never once needed to run. White robes in the lamplight of the upper gallery. The courier's voice was screaming in August's bones, wordless, just urgency.

And August—who had survived Thornfell by being nobody, who had spent twenty-six years learning exactly how much a person could lose and still walk—did the arithmetic they would remember for the rest of their life.

If they ran with both jars, the enforcers would take both jars back, and Maren would go back on her shelf, and August would go back on theirs, and nothing would ever change except that the court would learn a thief's face. If they ran with one—

No. There was no version of this where they chose. There was only a version where they bargained, because bargaining was the one trade they knew better than carving. Everything negotiable. Everything has a price. Their mother and father had proved the first part; the Salt Court had proved the second; and August Villalobos, who had charged a grieving mother coin they didn't need because charging was safer than caring, was about to learn the third part at the worst possible moment:

Some things you cannot buy. You can only pay for them.

"Wait," they said. "Wait. I want to make a deposit."

The frost stopped spreading.

**State the deposit.**

"Not a jar. Not a soul off your shelves." August knelt, slowly, keeping both jars cradled, until their knee touched the attentive stone. "Me. Half of me. You're already holding my soul—it's right here, it's been yours since before I could walk, you minted it, you know its weight better than I do. So split it. Keep half. Right here, in your vault, as surety. As collateral. As—" they fumbled for the court's own language and hated how easily it came, "—as margin."

**Margin against what obligation?**

"Against the rest of them." August lifted their chin and looked up at the glowing terraces, thousands of jars, thousands of names, the whole mined country of the dead poor. "I'm taking more than two. I don't know how many yet—I won't know until I'm out—but I swear it on the half I'm leaving you: I will come back. Winter gates, sealed passes, enforcers, all of it. I will come back for these souls, every season, every year, for as long as it takes, and you'll hold half of me the whole time so I can't weasel out, because you'll have the piece of me that keeps promises. Sell it if I fail. Mint it. Spend me. But while I live, I work for your prisoners. That's the account. Take it or call your white hands down here and let's see which of us the dead would rather serve."

The vault was quiet for a long time.

Long enough that the footsteps above reached the top stair of the gallery. Long enough that August counted eleven of them by sound, spread across the upper rail, closing on the terrace ramps. Long enough that the courier's voice in their bones stopped screaming and went still, watching, the way the dead watch the living do something irreversible.

Then the warmth returned. It rose up through the stone, through August's kneeling knee, through their spine, gentle and enormous, like a hand the size of the mountain deciding, just this once, to be careful.

**Terms accepted,** the vault said. **Hold still.**

"It will hurt," the courier whispered. "August, love, it will hurt."

"I know," August said. "Everything worth carrying does."

The splitting was not like breaking. August had broken things all their life—bone, wax, trust, hearts, their own reflection in a smuggler's mirror the winter they stopped using their name—and breaking was fast, a clean crack and then the long aftermath. This was slow. This was the vault reaching into them through the place where their soul lived, which turned out to be somewhere behind the breastbone and slightly left of every word they'd ever said, and dividing it the way a carver divides a length of ivory: measuring first, honoring the grain, finding the line where the material wanted to part.

It hurt the way grief hurts. It hurt the way the truth hurts. It hurt like hearing, for the first time, the exact pitch of your own voice and realizing you'd been singing slightly flat your entire life.

August did not scream. They bit down on the sleeve of their merchant's disguise and made a sound like a saw working and stayed kneeling, and the two jars in their arms glowed and glowed, and somewhere above the enforcers paused at the terrace head, uncertain—for the first time in perhaps a century—of what they were walking into.

When it was done, August was lighter.

Not smaller. Lighter. As though half the gravity they'd carried since birth had been set down on a shelf and labeled. They could feel the absence precisely: a coolness behind the sternum, a silence where half the chorus used to be. And they could feel the other half too—still there, still theirs, but *held*, tethered, a kite string running down into the vault's dark, and the strange thing, the thing they would turn over for many sleepless nights afterward, was that the tether did not feel like a chain.

It felt like an anchor feels to a boat. Like the reason you don't drift.

**Balance recorded,** the vault said. **Half of lot 3,091, held in trust. Surety accepted against the liberation of stored accounts. Note well, carver: the trust accrues interest. Each soul you free reduces your debt. Each season you delay increases it. I will be counting.**

"Counting," August repeated, dazed, getting to their feet. Their legs shook. Their hands, around the two jars, were steady as a surgeon's. "You're—you're helping me. Why? You're the court's vault. You're the lock on all of this."

A pause. When the voice came again, it was lower, and August could have sworn—would swear later, to Sefa, to Brannoc, to anyone who asked—that it sounded tired. Ten thousand years of remembering, tired.

**I am not the lock. I am what is locked.**

* * *

They ran.

Veyra's corridor swallowed them just as the first white robe rounded the third terrace, and behind them the vault's song changed key—from alarm to something else, something that sounded almost like cover, a great rolling chord of ten thousand voices rising to fill the galleries and blind every listening ear the enforcers possessed. The dead, hiding the thief. The dead, buying time.

August ran with a jar against each side of their ribs, their own heartbeat knocking on their own soul like a fist on a door, and the courier's voice flew ahead of them through the dark calling turns—*left, left, down, mind the low arch*—and it was going well, it was going almost well, right up until the moment they burst through the archive door into the antechamber and stopped dead, because the antechamber was full of white.

Not one enforcer. Six. Arranged in a crescent, unhurried, salt-white robes unstained, faces smooth as poured plaster. And at their center, stepping forward with his hands open and his old toll-keeper's ring still glinting on one finger, stood a man August knew better than they had ever wanted to.

Brannoc looked at the jars in their arms—at the small blue seal, at the salt-frost label bearing a name he must have known, must have witnessed, must have signed—and sighed like a man picking up a tool he'd hoped never to need again.

"August," he said. "Put them down. Let's talk about what you owe."
