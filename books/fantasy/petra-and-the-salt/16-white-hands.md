## 16. White Hands

The galleries of the Salt Court were built to be admired, and so they had never been built to be escaped.

August learned this in the space of six corridors. Each one was a rib of white stone arched high enough that their footsteps came back to them doubled, tripled, multiplied into the sound of a crowd fleeing where only one person ran. The walls were veined with salt the way marble is veined with grey, and in the light of the everburning lamps—lamps that burned without flame, without heat, without mercy—the veins glittered like frost on a window. Beautiful. Everything down here was beautiful. That was the court's first cruelty and its most effective one: it made theft look like vandalism and made the thief feel, at every turning, like a child smearing mud across a cathedral.

The jar rode against August's chest under their coat, wrapped in the spare shirt they'd carried since Lowmark. It was warm. It should not have been warm. A jar of salt-glass holding a soul ought to weigh nothing more than its glass, but this one pressed against their sternum like a sleeping animal, rising and falling with a breath that was not theirs—or was theirs, half of it, the half they'd left behind in the vault an hour and a lifetime ago.

*They've found the empty shelf,* said the courier's voice, from the small carved whistle at August's throat. Her bones rode in August's pack; her voice lived in the bone nearest August's ear, which happened now to be a finger-joint bead sewn into their collar. *I can hear them shouting. You have perhaps four minutes before they stop being polite.*

"Four minutes is generous," August muttered. "You always were."

*I was punctual,* the dead woman corrected. *It's why they hired me. Left at the next arch. The gallery of ministers bends east; the service stair behind it bends down.*

August bent east. The stair was there, narrow and unlit, cut for hands that carried buckets rather than dignitaries, and August took it two steps at a time with one hand braced on the wall and the other flat over the jar as though they could hold the heartbeat steady by pressure alone. Half a soul made strange arithmetic. They felt lighter—they had left something heavy below—and yet every step cost double, as if the missing half had been the half that believed in going forward. Grief did that too. They knew grief's weight well enough to recognize its counterfeit.

At the bottom of the stair the corridor opened into a long gallery lined with niches, and each niche held a lamp, and beneath each lamp stood a salt-white figure with its head bowed.

August stopped so fast their boots skidded on the polished floor.

For one full second they thought: *the dead, standing, waiting.* Then the nearest figure shifted its weight, and the bowing head lifted, and August saw the eyes—pale, patient, terrible—and understood these were not monuments. These were enforcers. A dozen of them. Standing in the dark of a gallery no honest person would walk through, heads bowed, waiting exactly the way salt waits: without hurry, because dissolution comes to everything eventually and salt has never once been on the losing side of time.

"Well," said August, to the jar, to the whistle-bead, to whatever gods kept ledgers beneath mountains. "That's that, then."

*Not yet,* said the courier. *Count them.*

"I can count."

*Then count the doors.*

There were three doors. Two were the kind that opened onto more gallery—wide, lit, and by the sound drifting faintly from beyond them, occupied. The third was low and iron-banded and set into the wall like an afterthought, and it was the one the white figures had arranged themselves to watch, because enforcers are not subtle in their geometry even when they are silent in their bodies. Whatever lay behind the iron door mattered. Which meant, August reasoned with the cold clarity of a person who has already lost, that it was also the only direction worth going.

"You could tell me to surrender," August said quietly. "You'd be right."

*I'm dead,* the courier said. *Being right stopped mattering to me years ago. Being useful is all I have left. Run for the iron door and don't stop when they move, because they will move slowly. Salt always does.*

The enforcers moved the moment August's boot touched the gallery floor—not quickly, not slowly, but with the horrible inevitability of tide. Twelve white figures unfolded from their niches and converged, and their faces, up close, were worse than blank: they were *finished*, smoothed of expression the way a pebble is smoothed by water, and their hands were white to the wrist as though they had reached elbow-deep into something and never bothered to wash.

August ran.

The first hand caught their sleeve and the wool simply came away, torn free as if the grip had decided the fabric was unworthy of resistance. The second caught their collar and August twisted out of the coat entirely, leaving it—a coat bought in Lowmark, mended eleven times, smelling of wood-shavings and the last three years of a careful invisible life—draped over a white arm like a shed skin. Underneath, the jar thumped against bare skin, warm, insistent, alive.

The iron door was locked. Of course it was locked.

August put their shoulder against it anyway, because the alternative was twelve pairs of patient pale eyes, and then—through the door, faint but unmistakable—came a sound that stopped their heart more thoroughly than any enforcer could have.

Footsteps. Unhurried. Descending.

And humming. A low, tuneless, familiar hum, the hum of a man who had once sat on a mountain pass and mended toll-chains by firelight while August pretended not to watch his hands.

"No," August said aloud.

*Yes,* said the courier, gently. *I'm sorry. I heard him three corridors back. I hoped I was wrong.*

The lock turned from the outside. The door swung inward, and Brannoc filled it—broad as a doorway himself, grey at the temples now, wearing not the patched wool of a toll-keeper but a long coat of undyed leather, and at his belt, where he had once hung the brass weights of his old office, a ring of keys. His eyes found August immediately, the way they always had, the way that had once felt like being seen and now felt like being *read*.

Behind him, in the corridor, stood two more enforcers. Not chasing. Escorting.

"Hello, carver," Brannoc said.

* * *

They put August in a small room with a table and two chairs and a lamp that burned without warmth, and they put Brannoc in the room too, alone, which told August everything about how confident the court was and how little it needed to fear from either of them. The enforcers withdrew. The door did not lock. It didn't need to. The whole mountain was a lock.

Brannoc sat down heavily, the way he sat down at every fire they had ever shared, elbows on knees, hands hanging. Up close August could see the toll-keeper still lived somewhere in the face: the weather-cracks at the eye corners, the scar through the left brow from a chain-whip gone wild. But the rest of him had been re-tailored. Expensive leather. Clean hands. Hands that had never once been clean in the years August knew him.

"The map," Brannoc said. "Where is it?"

"Burned."

"When?"

"In my imagination. Frequently."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth—not quite a smile, not quite its ghost. "You always were a liar in the second sentence and honest in the third."

"And you were honest until the money got involved." August stayed standing. Their legs wanted a chair; their pride refused. "Twenty-six years, Brannoc. Twenty-six years you watched me carve bones on your mountain, ate my bread, slept—" They stopped. Swallowed it. "You witnessed the contract. Your name is in the archive downstairs, signed in your own hand, on the sale of a newborn's soul. Mine. Tell me the ledger's wrong. Tell me there are two Brannocs and I met the better one."

He didn't flinch. That was the worst part. She had expected flinching, denial, rage—anything but the tired steadiness with which he looked back at her, as if she had asked him about the weather on a day he'd already memorized.

"There's one Brannoc," he said. "That's the whole tragedy of it."

"Then say it. Say what you did."

"I witnessed a signature." His voice was level, almost gentle, and gentleness from him was unbearable. "I didn't hold the knife. I didn't write the terms. A family came down the pass in a hard winter with a baby they couldn't feed and a debt they couldn't carry, and the court's minister offered grain enough to see them to spring, and someone had to countersign or the offer died on the table. So I signed. And they fed their other children. And the baby grew up—" he gestured at her, at all of her, the ruined coat and the stolen jar and the twenty-six years of careful nobody-ness "—grew up free on the surface, with a trade, with talent, with a life. What exactly did the signature take from you, August? Show me the wound. I've looked for it for years and I can't find it."

The words landed like well-aimed stones, and that was the thing about Brannoc—he had always known exactly where to throw. August felt each one find its mark and hated him for the accuracy and hated herself more for the flicker, deep down, of *he's right, aren't the children alive, wasn't the winter survived—*

Then the jar against their chest pulsed, warm and indignant, and the courier's voice rose through the bone at their collar, quiet and cutting:

*Ask him what the grain cost. Ask him what the family paid after spring came.*

"What did they pay," August said, "after spring came?"

Brannoc's hands went still on his knees.

"They came back," August continued, and their own voice sounded far away to them, cold and clear as meltwater. "Didn't they. When the second winter came. Because the first loan buys the second, and the second buys the third, and somewhere around the fourth there's nothing left in the house to sell except—" they touched the jar through the shirt, lightly, like touching a bruise "—except the things the court considers currency. How many contracts, Brannoc? How many signatures on your mountain? Is that what the toll became? Not passage—*recruitment*?"

"You don't know what those winters were like." For the first time something cracked in the level voice. "You weren't keeping the pass. You weren't the one who decided which caravan starved. I kept people *alive*, August. That was the work. That was always the work. And the court let me keep doing it as long as I—"

"As long as you fed it."

"—as long as I was useful." He stood, abruptly, and paced to the wall, and stood looking at the veinless white stone as if it might offer him a better argument. "You want me to be sorry. Fine. I'm sorry. I'm sorry the way a man is sorry for a scar—he's glad the wound closed and ashamed of how. But don't stand there with half a soul and a stolen jar and tell me you wouldn't have signed. You *did* sign, August. Downstairs, an hour ago, you struck a bargain with the vault itself. Half of you, held as surety. You think the court invented that arithmetic?" He turned. "You just wrote your name in their ledger. In your own hand. The only difference between us is that I did it to save strangers and you did it to save the dead."

The room went very quiet. The warmthless lamp hissed its flameless hiss.

"That's not the same," August said.

"No," Brannoc agreed, and there was something awful in his gentleness again, something that said he had rehearsed this, that the court had paid him not merely to track but to *persuade*. "It's worse. They'll return your half someday, you know. When the surety matures. When the other jars are paid off, decade by decade, interest accruing. You've given them a hostage they can treat kindly forever. I gave them names. Names get used up." He crossed the room and stopped close, close enough that August could smell road-dust and leather and, underneath, faintly, the pine smoke of a hundred fires on a pass they had both loved. "Come with me. Put the jar back. Veyra will cover the shelf-gap; she owes the court more than you know. Walk out under my escort, go home to Lowmark, and carve your whistles. In thirty years the vault releases you whole and no one is the wiser. That's the deal on the table. It's a good deal. I've spent my whole life learning to recognize one, and this is one."

August looked up at him—at the face they had loved on a cold mountain, at the hands that had mended chains and split wood and once, memorably, held hers over a fevered week in a toll-house while she hallucinated her mother's voice in the wind—and she felt the love and the fury occupy the same space in her chest without cancelling each other out, the way two fires can burn in one room and only make the room hotter.

"You expect me to understand," she said.

"I expect you to *survive*. Understanding is optional."

"No." She shook her head slowly. "You chose survival. I understand that perfectly—it may be the one thing about you I understand completely. You looked at an impossible world and you picked the part of it you could live in, and you've been living in it so long you've mistaken it for the whole world." She stepped back, out of the radius of his smell, his warmth, his history. "But survival isn't the same as living, Brannoc, and it certainly isn't the same as *being someone*. You sold my name before I had one. Do you know what I found in the archives? Not a crime. A *transaction*. Neat columns. My whole existence reduced to a line item, and your signature making it official." Her hand found the whistle-bead at her collar. "The dead woman whose bones I'm carrying—she died getting proof of that transaction to the surface. She died with salt on her lips and my name in her mouth. And you're offering me thirty years of polite captivity as a *good deal*."

"It is a good deal," he said, and his voice finally, finally broke its level line. "It's the best deal anyone's ever gotten out of this mountain. I tore it out of them for you. Don't you understand what that cost—"

"I understand exactly what it cost." August lifted the jar free of their shirt and held it between them, warm and faintly glowing, and watched Brannoc's face change as he understood what it was, what it must be, whose lifetime hummed inside the salt-glass. "It cost me nothing. That's the difference. Everything worth doing costs *you*, and you've stopped paying. That's why you took their coin. Not because you're wicked. Because you're *tired*. And tired men make excellent instruments."

The jar pulsed. And through the bone at her throat, the courier spoke—not to August, but past her, in the flat carrying tone of a woman who had delivered messages for a living and knew how to make one land:

*Toll-keeper. Tell them what you told the minister. Word for word. You remember it—you've been repeating it to yourself for twenty-six years to make it bearable. Say it out loud and let's see if it survives the air.*

Brannoc went white. Whiter than the enforcers. And in the silence that followed, August heard, very distantly, through the walls of the small room, through the ribs of the mountain itself—a sound beginning. Faint. Rhythmic. A thousand voices finding one another in the dark and beginning, note by note, to sing.

The vault was waking. Or the vault was being *sung awake*—and there was only one person in the mountain reckless enough, half-souled enough, desperate enough to be playing the dead through carved bone.

Sefa. Holding the drowned stair. Buying time with noise.

Brannoc heard it too. His head came up like a deer's, and for one unguarded moment August saw the toll-keeper underneath the tracker, the man who had once run toward avalanches because someone had to count who came out the other side.

"That's not possible," he whispered. "Those voices are sealed—"

"They were," August said, and slung the pack onto their shoulder, and picked up the jar. "Sealed things crack, Brannoc. You taught me that. On the pass, the winter we met—you said the ice doesn't break all at once, it breaks at the weak places, and the weak places are always where the water remembers flowing." She walked to the door. He didn't stop her. His enforcers, summoned or dismissed, did not appear. "I'm going to go open the water. You can stand here and survive. It's what you're best at."

She was three steps into the corridor when his voice caught her—not commanding, not pleading, but raw, stripped of every tailored layer, the voice of a man on a mountain calling into wind:

"August. The minister who countersigned with me—the young one, the one who hated it—her name was Veyra. She was the one who argued for your family's grain. Remember that, whichever of us walks out of here."

August didn't turn. But she carried it with her, down the corridor, toward the singing—Veyra's name added to the small, growing weight of things she knew and could not yet afford to feel.

Behind her, in the small white room, Brannoc stood alone with the lamp that gave no warmth, and began, at last, to pay.
