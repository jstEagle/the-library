## 17. The Chorus Rises

The galleries of the Salt Court were built to echo. August had understood that only dimly before—had felt the architecture pressing in on them like a held breath—but now, running with a jar of stolen voices against their chest and half a soul rattling loose inside their ribs, they understood it completely. The court was an instrument. Every vaulted ceiling, every ribbed pillar of white stone, every polished floor was shaped to carry sound the way a throat carries a scream.

And the mountain had been holding its breath for a very long time.

Behind them, the alarms had not stopped. They weren't bells—the Salt Court did not use bells, because bells were honest things that anyone could hear. Instead the alarm was a sound August felt in their teeth: a high, mineral whine, like wet salt drying too fast, like crystal growing. It came from everywhere and nowhere, and beneath it ran footsteps. Many footsteps. The soft, deliberate tread of enforcers whose boots never quite touched the ground.

August ran anyway.

Their body was doing something strange. They had noticed it first at the vault door, when the jar's weight settled into their arms and their own heartbeat stuttered like a dropped stitch. Half a soul, Veyra had said, carried out in flesh. But flesh was not made to hold half of anything. August's edges felt porous. When they passed a niche where a dead miner's name was carved into the wall, they could feel the letters as if they'd been cut into their own skin. When they breathed, the air tasted of other people's memories: a wedding on a green hillside, a fever breaking at dawn, bread pulled from an oven by hands long since rotted to nothing.

*Left,* said the courier's voice, sharp and close, though her bones rode in the satchel at August's hip and her ghost had no reason to be kind. *Not the grand stair—they'll funnel you there. Left, through the tithe gallery.*

"How do you know?"

*Because I helped build this place's lies for six years before they killed me.* A pause, and then, quieter, with something almost like shame: *Because I mapped every corridor I could reach and hid the map in wax seals so no one would ever find it. Go left, carver.*

August went left.

The tithe gallery was a long hall lined with alcoves, and each alcove held a single block of white salt-stone, and each block bore a name. Not names of the living. Names of the sold—the poor, the indebted, the unlucky, harvested from villages August had walked through without ever guessing what the mountains took from them. The blocks hummed faintly as August passed, a sound below hearing, below thought. The jar in their arms answered. It warmed against their sternum like a sleeping animal waking, and the voices inside it stirred—not words yet, but the memory of words, the shape of speech waiting for a mouth.

August skidded around a corner and stopped so hard their boots slipped on salt-polished floor.

Three enforcers stood at the far end of the gallery. White from crown to sole, faces smooth as poured wax, featureless except for eyes that were not eyes but small dark apertures, like the mouths of wells. They did not run. They did not need to. The gallery was long, and they were patient, and behind August—August heard it now, the whisper of white cloth—there were more.

*Carver,* the courier said. *You cannot outrun them. No one outruns them.*

"I know," August whispered back.

Their hands were shaking. That was the terrible thing—they could feel the shaking and could do nothing about it, because their body belonged half to themselves now and half to the fear. But their hands, even shaking, even half-empty of soul, knew their work. Twenty-six years of carving lived in those fingers. Bone remembered what the mind forgot.

August set the jar down between their feet, gently, the way one sets down a child, and drew out the piece they had been working since the drowned stair without fully admitting why: a flute, or nearly a flute, carved from the courier's own shinbone, bored with seven holes because seven was the number of griefs, and unfinished because August had been afraid to finish it. Afraid of what would come out.

Now there was nothing left to be afraid of. Everything had already gone wrong in every way it could.

They finished the last hole with the point of their knife, right there on the gallery floor, chips of bone curling away white as teeth, while the enforcers advanced at their unhurried pace and the alarm whined and the jar hummed against August's ankles like a hive deciding whether to swarm.

"What are you doing?" The courier's voice cracked through the bone in August's hands, through every bone in their body. "That's me. What are you—"

"Playing you," August said. "I'm sorry. I should have asked."

*No one has ever asked,* she said, and her voice broke open like ice on a spring river.

August raised the flute to their lips.

* * *

The first note was not music. It was a name.

It came out of the bone the way water comes out of a split dam—all at once, with weight behind it—and it filled the tithe gallery wall to wall, ceiling to floor, and it was the courier's name, the true one, the one she had carried across three mountain ranges and died with crusted salt on her lips: **Maren Oduya**, spoken in her own voice, in the voice of a woman who had once laughed in a kitchen and argued about the price of wool and loved a daughter enough to die twice for her.

The enforcers stopped.

Every one of them, all along the gallery, stopped mid-stride, and their well-dark eyes turned toward the sound, and for the first time August saw something move in those faces—something like hunger, or like fear, or like both wearing the same mask.

Because here was the thing the Salt Court had built itself upon, the thing no minister spoke aloud and no ledger recorded: souls remember their names. You can sell a soul, mint it, shelve it in salt-glass for a generation, grind it fine to pay a farmer's grain debt. But the name goes with it, buried deep, and a name is a seed, and seeds wait.

And August Villalobos had just planted Maren Oduya's name in a hall walled with ten thousand waiting ones.

The second note was a different name—a miner's, someone called Hesk, who had died coughing in the flooded dark and whose soul had been taxed out of his corpse before his family finished weeping. The third was a girl's name, sung rather than spoken, because some names are songs. The fourth, fifth, sixth—August stopped counting. They played the way they had never dared play in Lowmark, not for coin, not for grieving mothers, not for anyone: with everything, with the whole of their half-soul poured through the bore of the bone, every name they had ever heard rising up their arms and out through their mouth, and the flute taking each one and giving it wings.

The walls began to answer.

It started in the alcoves. The white blocks trembled in their niches, shedding powder like snow, and from inside them came sounds—not voices, not yet, but the pressure of voices, the way a house groans before it speaks. Then the floor. August felt it through the soles of their boots, a deep subterranean thrum, and realized with a lurch that the court's foundations were not stone at all, not entirely. The whole mountain city had been packed and mortared with the compressed dead, centuries of them, the bedrock of the Salt Court was the sold, and every one of them was hearing their name.

*Stop,* said a new voice, and it was not the courier's. It came from everywhere—from the walls, the floor, the air. It was vast and layered, a thousand throats in imperfect unison, and it was the most piteous sound August had ever heard. *Stop. We will break. We will break.*

"No," August said, around the flute, around the tears freezing on their face. "You'll wake. There's a difference."

They played louder.

* * *

The first crack opened in the ceiling of the tithe gallery with a report like a cannon, and white dust sheeted down, and through the crack August saw not sky but another chamber above, and beyond it another, the strata of the court laid bare like the rings of a felled tree. In the dust-haze, the enforcers had begun to move again—but wrong now, stumbling, their waxen faces fissuring along the jaw, and from the cracks in their faces leaked thin threads of light, threads of *voice*, because August understood suddenly, with the clarity that comes only in the middle of catastrophe, what enforcers were.

They were made of the dead too. Packed and pressed and given white shapes, souls hammered into armor and told to walk. And they had heard their names.

One of them fell to its knees. Its mouth-split widened, and from it poured a single word, over and over, a man's word, a father's word: *"Tobin. Tobin. Tobin."* And the light pouring out of it was not dying but *leaving*, streaming upward through the crack in the ceiling toward whatever waited above, and the enforcer-shape slumped empty, and the light rose, and somewhere in the upper chambers of the Salt Court a minister screamed.

Good, thought August, with a ferocity that frightened them. Let them scream.

The jar at their feet was blazing now, warm as a hearthstone, and August snatched it up and ran—not away from the collapse but *with* it, letting the falling dust and the buckling floors cover their flight, following the courier's shouted directions through corridors that were tearing themselves apart. Behind them the chorus grew. It was not orderly anymore; it was an avalanche of sound, ten thousand names colliding and harmonizing and dissonating, and the mountain rang with it like a struck bell, and chunks of the ceiling came down in sheets, and somewhere far off August heard the great doors of the antechamber—the ones they had entered through disguised as a bone-merchant—shear off their hinges.

*The drowned stair,* the courier gasped. *Sefa's holding it—I can feel her, she's there, she's still there—north corridor, carver, RUN—*

"I'm running," August panted, and meant it, and their legs obeyed even as their heart wanted to stay, wanted to stand in the ruin and play until every last name was free, every last thread of light climbed home. But the jar was heavy, and half a soul is a small lamp to steer by, and Veyra's warning rang in their skull: *carried out in flesh or they evaporate*. The freed ones streaming upward through the cracks might find their own way. The ones in the jar needed August.

So August ran, cradling the jar, playing the flute even as they ran—an impossible thing, a desperate thing, breath ragged, notes fracturing—and the music did not care. Broken notes worked as well as whole ones. Grief needs no tuning.

They burst into the north corridor and found it transformed. The walls here were older, rougher, pre-court, natural rock veined with salt glitter, and the whole passage was singing—literally singing, the rock resonating, harmonics standing in the air like pillars of glass. Ahead, maybe two hundred paces, a doorway glowed with grey daylight and falling snow: the drowned stair, Sefa's silhouette braced in it, small and stubborn as a nail.

Between August and the doorway, the corridor was filling with white.

Dozens of enforcers. More than dozens. They had come from every direction, converging, and they were not walking anymore—they were being dragged forward by their own contents, hauled along by the names screaming inside them, and their shapes were failing, splitting, shedding light like torn sacks of grain. It should have been a victory. It looked like a massacre. The dead tearing themselves out of the shapes their jailers had forced them into, and the jailers' shapes collapsing, and the whole corridor a blizzard of released light and falling white dust.

But at the center of it, one enforcer still stood whole.

Taller than the others. Its wax face uncracked. Its well-eyes fixed on August with a focus the others had never possessed, and as August watched, it raised one white hand—not toward August, but toward the flute, toward the bone, and its mouth opened, and what came out was not a name.

"You cannot carry them all," it said, in a voice like salt grinding under a millstone. "The vault remembers what is taken. The vault is waking. And it knows your name, Villalobos."

The jar in August's arms went cold as grave-water.

Behind the enforcer, the corridor's far end shuddered, and from the direction of the vaults—deep, deep below—came a sound August felt in their remaining half of soul: a slow, vast inhale, as if the mountain itself had drawn breath to speak.

August lifted the flute, aimed it at the tall enforcer like a blade, and played the loudest name they had left.
