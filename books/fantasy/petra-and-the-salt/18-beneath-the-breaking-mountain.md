## 18. Beneath the Breaking Mountain

The water came for Sefa the way debts always did: slowly, and then all at once.

She stood on the third landing of the drowned stair with her lantern held high, watching the black water climb the steps she had climbed a hundred times in another life, when the mines still belonged to the smugglers and the smugglers still belonged to no one. The water should have been at her ankles by season's turn. It was at her knees now, and rising, and it was not rain that fed it. Somewhere above and behind her—deep in the mountain's wet dark throat—the Salt Court had opened its channels, and the mountain was being made to swallow.

They know someone is coming. She had known it since the stair began to flood out of season, since the water rose with a purpose no water had. They know, and they are drowning the road behind us so that whatever goes down cannot come back up.

Except something *was* coming back up. Something with half a soul and a jar full of other people's lives, and a courier's ghost for a compass, and Sefa's word holding the door.

"Come on," she whispered to the dark below. "Come *on*, carver."

The stair shuddered. Not water-shudder—stone-shudder, a long grinding groan that traveled up through her boots and into her teeth. Dust sifted down through the lantern light like flour through a sieve. Far off, muffled by rock and fathoms of flooded tunnel, something enormous let go of something else enormous, and the mountain sighed the way old men sigh when they sit down for the last time.

Sefa counted her assets the way her mother had taught her to count coin: what do you have, what can you spend, what will you never get back. One lantern, half full of oil. One smuggler's knife, salt-pitted, honest. One body, thirty-one years used, none of them well. And one promise, made in the dark of the drowned stair to a woman she'd known four days: *I hold the stair. You run.*

She had made promises like that before. That was rather the whole of her trouble.

* * *

The betrayal had a name, and the name was Oren Vask.

Eleven years ago, in these same tunnels, when Sefa had been the fastest guide the salt-runners had and twice as arrogant, there had been a crew of six going down to lift a shipment from a court waystation—a small theft, a routine one, the kind of work that kept Lowmark fed through winter. Oren had been the youngest, all elbows and laughter, forever singing off-key in the flooded galleries where sound carried too far. And the enforcers had come early because someone had told them where to look, and Sefa had known—had *known* with a certainty that lived in her spine even now—that the tip had been sold by the crew's own guide, because only the guide knew the route, and only the guide had been seen speaking with a white-cloaked man at the Widow's Stair three nights before.

Only the guide. Only Sefa.

She had said nothing. That was the shape of it, the true and shameful shape: not that she had sold them, but that she had *let* them believe it might be anyone, had watched Oren taken by the enforcers at the waystation while she slipped out through the sump channel she alone could swim, and had surfaced into clean air and told herself the story she would tell for eleven years—that she hadn't betrayed anyone, that she simply hadn't died, that survival was not the same as sin.

Oren had come back, eventually. They all came back from the low galleries eventually, or their voices did; the mountain kept what it mined. Sefa had heard him once, years later, in a bone whistle some carver had brought through Lowmark—a boy's voice, laughing, singing off-key—and she had paid the carver triple for the whistle and then thrown it into the harbor, because keeping it felt like keeping a debt and throwing it away felt like paying one, and neither was true. Nothing had been true since the sump channel. She had stopped guiding after that. Stopped trusting herself down into the dark, which for a woman born under Thornfell was a kind of slow death, and she had chosen it anyway, the way you choose a cell you can lock from the inside.

And then August Villalobos had walked into the Drowned Anchor with a map sealed in wax and asked—not begged, *asked*—for a guide who knew the flooded mines, and Sefa had heard herself say yes before the better part of her could object.

Now the mountain was collapsing, and the better part of her had nothing left to say.

* * *

The second collapse came closer. This time she felt it in the water first—a pressure wave rolling up the stair, slapping against her thighs hard enough to stagger her—and then the sound arrived, a boom like a giant's door slamming somewhere far below, and the lantern flame bent sideways and nearly died.

Dust. Darkness. The taste of stone in the air, fine and chalky.

Then, threading up through it, thin as wire: singing.

Not Oren this time. A woman's voice, cracked and human, singing a walking song Sefa half-remembered from her grandmother's knee—and beneath it, fainter, a dozen other voices, a hundred, the sound August had described with white lips back at the Ashwife's fire: *the walls are full of them, they're all still in there.* The vault's stolen dead, loosed by whatever August had done down there, rising through the mountain's bones like bubbles through beer.

And threaded among them, unmistakable, a voice Sefa knew: *"Guide! Guide, if you can hear me—"*

"I hear you," Sefa said aloud, uselessly, to the dark. Sound didn't travel down. But she put her free hand flat against the wet wall anyway, palm to stone, the way you'd press your hand to a friend's back. "I'm here. I'm holding it."

*"Stair's yours,"* the voice came again, clearer now, closer, riding the chorus like a boat on a flood. *"How long?"*

"How long can I hold it? Long enough. How long do you need?"

A pause. In the pause, the mountain groaned again, deeper, and somewhere above her a crack propagated through rock with a sound like ice splitting on a lake.

*"Ten minutes,"* August said. *"Maybe twelve."*

"Liar."

*"Eight,"* August admitted, and there was something almost like a laugh in it, ragged at the edges. *"There are four of them behind me. White cloaks. The water's slowing them more than it slows me—I don't mind the wet anymore, apparently half a soul floats—but they'll reach the stair before I clear the upper galleries. You need to go, Sefa. Take the sump channel. You're the only one who can swim it."*

The sump channel. Of course August knew about the sump channel; Sefa had talked about it on the descent, the way guides talk, naming her escapes like a gambler names his cards. The channel that had saved her life eleven years ago while Oren Vask was dragged away singing.

"No," Sefa said.

*"Don't be stupid. I'm not worth—"*

"You shut your mouth and climb." Her voice came out harder than she meant it to, cracking against the stone. "You want to talk about worth? I've got a man's voice in my head that sings off-key, and I put it there. Eleven years I've been paying interest on that, carver, and I am *done* paying. So you climb, and I hold, and in eight minutes we both walk out of this mountain or neither of us does. That's the toll. That's my price. Climb."

Silence, except for the water and the distant chorus and the mountain's slow dying groan.

Then, quietly: *"Climbing."*

* * *

Four minutes. Five. Sefa moved up the stair two landings, then three, keeping the water at her calves instead of her knees, buying distance the way a miser buys bread—one coin at a time, hating every expenditure. The lantern guttered. Above her, dust fell in ropes. The stair itself had begun to list, she realized—a degree, maybe two, the whole drowned staircase leaning eastward like a drunk against a wall, and every smuggler's instinct she owned screamed that stairs which lean are stairs that fall.

Six minutes. The water went cold all at once, shockingly cold, snowmelt cold, and she understood: the court wasn't just flooding the stair. They were flushing it. Somewhere above, gates were open that should have stayed shut until spring, and the mountain's winter reservoirs were emptying themselves down the old mine-works, and in ten minutes this stair would not be a stair but a waterfall.

Seven minutes. She heard them.

Boots in water. More than four pairs now—reinforcements, or the ones August thought were behind her had found a faster road. Lantern-light bloomed in the flooded gallery below, white and sourceless, the enforcers' salt-lamps that burned without flame, and silhouettes waded into view at the bottom landing: tall figures in white cloaks gone grey with wet, faces she couldn't see and didn't need to. They moved without hurry. That was the worst of it. Men who hurry can be outrun. These moved like the tide.

The lead one raised a hand, and the others stopped, and the white lamp tilted upward, and Sefa knew—with the flat, ringing certainty of a bell being struck—that the light had found her.

"Well," she said, to no one, to everyone, to Oren Vask wherever his voice was swimming. "Hello, then."

She did the arithmetic fast, because it was the only kind of sums she'd ever been good at. Eight minutes for August. Two minutes, maybe less, for the enforcers to close the distance up the stair. The sump channel mouth was forty feet above her, a black slot in the wall that she could reach in ninety seconds if she ran and didn't look back. The channel led to the river, the river led to the valley, the valley led to a life—the same life she'd been living for eleven years, the locked cell, the locked door, the slow death chosen carefully.

Below her, faint and getting fainter, the walking song climbed higher. Still three galleries down. Still climbing.

Sefa looked at the sump channel. Then she looked at the water.

Then she took off her boots, tied them around her neck by the laces, and waded back down the stair to meet the tide.

* * *

Later—much later, in a world made entirely of cold and noise—she would try to reconstruct what she'd done and fail, because memory broke apart under pressure the way bone did. Fragments, mostly:

The lowest landing, water to her waist, the white lamps close enough now that she could see the enforcers' faces at last—smooth and young and terribly calm, faces like unfired clay.

Her knife in her hand, and the absurd thought: *salt-pitted, honest, thirty years old, worth maybe four coppers.*

The lead enforcer reaching for her—not striking, *reaching*, the way you reach for merchandise—and her voice saying, clear and steady, words she hadn't planned: "His name was Oren Vask. He sang off-key. Somebody write that down, because nobody ever did."

The enforcer's hand closing on her wrist, cold as the water, colder—

—and then the mountain intervening, because the mountain, drowning, was done taking orders. The gallery ceiling above the lower landing let go all at once, a cathedral's worth of stone, and the flood it displaced came up the stair like the fist of God, and Sefa was lifted, tumbled, slammed, and for a long bright moment she understood exactly how a pebble feels in an avalanche.

She lost the knife. She lost the lantern. She lost, briefly, any conviction that she was still alive.

What she kept—what her hands closed on in the roaring dark, with the stubbornness of a woman who had spent eleven years holding onto the wrong things and finally, finally found the right one—was a rope. An old smuggler's guide-line, frayed but knotted every arm's length, anchored somewhere above, strung along the stair by hands long dead for exactly this purpose. Her people's rope. Her people's stair. The mountain was falling down around her, and the drowned stair held her the way it had held generations of thieves and runners and desperate souls, and Sefa climbed.

* * *

She broke the surface of the upper pool gasping, blind, alive, and a hand caught her collar before she could sink again.

"Got you—got you—breathe, breathe, you absolute *idiot*—"

August. Half-drowned herself, hair plastered flat, the salt-glass jar strapped to her back and glowing faintly through the wet canvas like a lantern full of fireflies, hauling Sefa up onto the last dry ledge with a strength no half-soul should have owned. Behind them both, the stair was gone. Not damaged—*gone*, swallowed, the passage where Sefa had stood seven minutes ago now a churning throat of white water and tumbling stone. The enforcers were nowhere. Whether the flood had taken them or they had simply withdrawn to find another road, Sefa would never know, and decided she could live without knowing.

For a while there was only breathing. Two women on a wet ledge in the dark, gasping like landed fish, the jar humming softly between them.

"You went *down*," August said at last. "I told you to hold the stair. I didn't tell you to drown with it."

"You told me to climb and you lied about the minutes." Sefa coughed out half the pool. "We're even."

"We are not remotely even." August's voice did something complicated—anger fraying into something else, something rawer. "I heard it, you know. Through the water. The dead carry sound down here like nothing else, and I heard you shouting at them. *His name was Oren Vask.*" A pause. "Who was he?"

And there it was. The question Sefa had spent eleven years making sure no one would ever ask, asked now by a half-souled bone-carver on a ledge at the bottom of a breaking mountain, when lying would have been the easiest thing in the world and also, for the first time in eleven years, impossible.

"My crew," Sefa said. "Eleven years ago. I was the guide. The enforcers came early, and I got out through the sump channel, and Oren didn't. I never sold them out—I want that said plain, I never took their coin—but I didn't warn them either, and I didn't stay, and I let them think it might have been anyone. For eleven years I let it be anyone." Her voice shook, and she let it. "I heard him once, in a whistle. Some carver's work. He was still singing."

August was quiet a long moment. Water dripped. The jar hummed its many-voiced hum, and somewhere inside it, Sefa could have sworn, a young man laughed off-key.

"He's in there," August said softly. "Some of them, the ones taken in the low galleries—they ended up in the vault. I heard a dozen Lowmark accents down there. If he's among them, he comes out with the rest. That was the bargain. All of them or none."

Sefa found she couldn't speak. She nodded instead, hard, twice, the way you nod when nodding is all that stands between you and weeping, and August—mercifully, wisely—pretended not to see.

"Can you walk?" August asked finally.

"I can walk. Can you?" Sefa looked her over: the grey face, the blood thinned to pink water running from a scalp wound, the way August's shadow seemed slightly wrong in the jarlight, thinner than the body casting it. "Half a soul. What does it feel like?"

August considered this with the seriousness August gave everything, even bleeding in the dark.

"Like standing in a doorway," she said. "Half of me in the warm room, half of me out in the cold. Like hearing my own name from two directions." A crooked almost-smile. "Like being nobody and somebody at the same time. Come on. The courier says the river gate's two galleries north, and she says the mountain's got maybe an hour of standing left in it, and I have learned not to argue with dead women about deadlines."

Sefa pushed herself upright. Every joint reported in, and every report was bad, and she ignored all of them. Ahead, the passage sloped up toward air that tasted, faintly, impossibly, of pine and snow—surface air, real air, the smell of the world above the world.

"One thing," she said, catching August's sleeve. "When we're out. When the souls are loose and the mountain's settled and you're off doing whatever half-souled saints do next." She steadied her voice. "Take me down again sometime. When it's safe. I want to be the one who guides them out. All of them. I owe the road that much."

August looked at her for a long moment in the jarlight—looked at her the way nobody had looked at her in eleven years, as though she were someone worth looking at—and nodded.

"Together, then," August said. "Now run."

Behind them, with a sound like the end of the world clearing its throat, Mount Cindrel began to bring down its final gallery—and ahead, at the edge of the lantern reach, the river gate stood open, and beside it, impossibly, patiently, wrapped in a white cloak gone grey with cave-dust, a figure was waiting.

It was not an enforcer.

It was Brannoc.
