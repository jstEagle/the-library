## 20. The Long Winter Finishes Falling

The mountain let them go the way a fist lets go of something it has held too long: slowly, joint by joint, with a grinding reluctance that August felt in their teeth.

They climbed for what felt like days. The tunnels behind them had stopped groaning—the chorus had done its work and gone quiet, the way a bell goes quiet, still trembling somewhere below hearing—but the passages ahead were choked with rubble and old ice, and Sefa led them through the dark by touch and memory, calling turns in a voice gone hoarse from smoke and salt. Veyra's small jar rode against August's chest, wrapped in three layers of wool. Their own half-soul rode somewhere deeper than wool could reach, a warmth behind the sternum that was theirs and not theirs, half a hearth-fire burning in a house built for two.

They did not speak much. There was nothing to say that the mountain had not already said to them.

On the third day—or the fourth; time had gone strange beneath Cindrel—they saw light. Not lantern-light. Not the pale glow of salt-glass. Sunlight, thin and gray and cold as a knife laid flat against the eye, falling through a crack in the rock above like water through a failing roof.

Sefa stood beneath it with her face turned up, and for a moment she looked younger than August had ever seen her, all the smuggler's cunning washed out of her, leaving only a woman who had been underground too long.

"Snow," she said. "Smell it?"

August could. It came down through the crack with the light: clean, mineral, empty. The smell of a world with no one in it yet. They breathed it until their lungs ached, and then they kept climbing.

* * *

The Widow's Stair had been treacherous in autumn. In winter it was a rumor of itself—a spine of switchbacks stitched up the mountain's shoulder, buried to the knees in drift, the guide-ropes stiff as frozen sinew. The wind met them at the top of the first pitch and did not stop meeting them after that. It came off the high passes in long white shrieks, carrying snow that was not falling so much as flying, horizontal, stinging, filling every seam of clothing, packing itself into the corners of eyes and mouths until August tasted salt and could not tell whether it came from the storm or from themselves.

They moved roped together, Sefa first, August last. Behind them the entrance they'd used—a smuggler's mouth in the cliffside, half-collapsed now—had sealed itself with sliding scree within an hour of their exit, as if the mountain were closing its lips over a secret. Ahead there was only white, and the memory of a map, and the knowledge—cold and certain as the stone underfoot—that the passes were closed. The court had called winter down early. Weeks ahead of any honest season, the sky had opened its granaries, and now Thornfell lay under a fall that would not finish for months, a fall meant to keep thieves in the ground until spring.

Except the court was cracking. Except its ministers were digging through rubble. Except two figures were climbing out of its reach anyway, into the very weather meant to bury them.

"There," Sefa shouted once, pointing with her whole arm, and August followed her finger to a shape in the white: a cairn, human-built, taller than a man, one of the toll-markers Brannoc must have raised in another life, another winter. They reached it and pressed their backs against the lee side and ate frozen bread and did not speak, because speaking meant opening their mouths to the wind, and the wind took whatever it was offered.

It was there, in the lee of the cairn, that August first heard the jar.

Not Veyra's jar. That one slept, or pretended to. This was the other thing they carried—the thing they had not dared open since the vault, since the galleries, since the chorus rose and the mountain broke. A pouch at their hip, and inside the pouch a stoppered vial of salt-glass, and inside the vial the voices they had scooped up in the chaos when the shelves came down: a dozen souls, maybe more, spilled and gathered in handfuls the way you'd gather spilled grain, without counting, without choosing, because there had been no time to choose.

They hummed. Faintly. Under the wind, under the blood in August's ears, a sound like a wet finger circling the rim of a cup. Like a room full of people humming in their sleep.

*We should have left them,* Sefa had said, that first night in the deep camps, watching August turn the vial in firelight. *Taking your own was the job. Taking theirs is just theft with better reasons.*

And August had said: *Then I'm a thief. I've been called worse this month.*

But climbing into the teeth of the court's winter, with the vial humming against their hip like a hive of small sleeping animals, August was no longer sure the answer had been wit. It might have been truth. And truth, carried long enough, got heavy.

* * *

They sheltered that night—or what passed for night—in a hollow beneath an overhang where the wind went over them instead of through them. Sefa built a fire out of smuggler's tricks: a spirit-lamp, wax shavings, a twist of oilcloth, all of it cupped in stone so the flame showed no higher than a cupped hand. The light turned their faces orange and made the darkness beyond the hollow seem total, a black ocean pressing close on every side, and the snow hissed against the rocks above like sand poured on a drum.

August took out the vial.

Up close, in firelight, the salt-glass was beautiful the way frost on a window is beautiful: fine-grained, faintly luminous, veined with lines that moved if you watched too long. The voices inside had gone quiet now that they were being looked at. August knew that trick. The dead did it in ivory too—went shy, went still, waited to see what the living intended.

"I don't know how to carry you," August said quietly. "I don't know if I'm supposed to."

Sefa looked over. She didn't say anything. She'd stopped saying anything about the vial somewhere on the second day, but she watched now with the particular attention she gave to things she thought might kill someone.

"You were mined," August told the glass. "Dug out of whoever you were and minted into coin. And then a thief knocked your shelf over and put you in her pocket, and now you're here, in a storm, on a mountain, and I don't—" Their voice caught on something. They pushed past it. "I don't know what freedom looks like for you. Whether it's a body you can't have. Whether it's just... not being owned."

The vial hummed. One voice among the many rose a little, the way a hand rises in a crowd.

August knew that voice. Had known it through bone and through salt and through the dark of the vault, through every mile since Lowmark. The courier. The woman who had died on their workshop floor with a name in her mouth and a map in her hands and salt crusted on her lips, who had spent her death insisting, patient as tide, *it belongs to you, it belongs to you*, until August had stopped arguing and started walking.

"Hello," August said.

The hum steadied. Concentrated. Became, almost, a word.

Sefa leaned closer. "What's it saying?"

"It's not saying." August turned the vial so the firelight moved through it. "It's asking. It wants to know how far we've come." A pause. "I think—I think it wants to know if we're high enough."

"High enough for what?"

August looked out past the lip of the hollow, into the storm. The wind had slackened a little, the way mountain wind does in the smallest hours, gathering itself. Above them, invisible, the pass waited—the saddle of the world, the roof of Thornfell, the place where everything fell from. Snow was still coming down out of that darkness in freight-loads, silent between gusts, endless.

"For letting go," August said.

* * *

They climbed through the gray hours before dawn, and near the top the storm found its second wind and tried to take the mountain back.

August remembered little of it afterward in sequence—memory in storms comes home as fragments, as single images nailed up in the dark. Sefa's rope-hand rising and rising through the white. The taste of blood where ice had split a lip. A moment when the slope tilted wrong and the world became a slide and the only thing that mattered was the knife, driven sideways into the crust, holding, holding, while the wind read them its whole long sermon about smallness. And through all of it, against their chest and hip, two warmths: the small jar, and the vial, and their own half-soul answering both like a struck string.

Near midday—or what the light suggested was midday, a bruise-colored brightness with no sun in it—they crested the pass.

There was no triumph in it. The summit was a shallow saddle of wind-scoured ice perhaps twenty paces wide, and beyond it the world dropped away north and west into ranges they knew only from maps and from the stories smugglers told to frighten each other. The storm was thinner here, torn apart by the height, and through its gaps August could see a long way: white peaks marching to the horizon like a congregation, valleys drowned and featureless, the sky low and the color of old pewter. Somewhere down there, weeks away, was Lowmark. A workshop with a shattered door. A life built carefully out of being nobody.

Somewhere down there, too, was spring. Eventually. The court's winter would finish falling, and the snow would let go of the passes, and the roads would open, and the mountain would still be standing—cracked, wounded, furious, but standing—and inside it the ministers would be counting what remained.

August stood in the saddle with the wind pulling at them and understood that this was the place.

"Here?" Sefa asked. She had come up beside them, hood back despite the cold, hair crusted white. She was looking at the vial in August's hands. "Here's far enough?"

"I think it always was." August worked the stopper. It resisted, then gave with a small sigh, like a held breath released. "I just didn't want to do it while we were still under the mountain. While it could still hear."

"What happens to them? When you—"

"I don't know." August lifted the vial. The voices inside had risen, all of them now, a chord, a held note, and among them one voice clearest of all, familiar as grief. "Nobody's ever stolen souls before. Nobody's ever carried them out. We're writing the rite as we go."

"That's not comforting."

"No," August agreed. "It isn't."

They tipped the vial.

* * *

The souls did not pour. They rose.

They came out of the salt-glass the way heat shimmer comes off summer stone—not liquid, not smoke, something between, a brightening in the air that resolved, for a heartbeat each, into shapes. August saw hands. Saw a tilt of head like a person listening. Saw, once, unmistakably, a woman standing in the wind with her arms slightly away from her body, palms open, the posture of someone stepping into rain after a long drought.

The storm took them gently. That was the thing August had not expected—the gentleness. The wind that had spent two days trying to peel the flesh from their bones received the dead like a host receiving guests, bore them up and outward, scattered them across the whiteness in slow bright arcs, and everywhere they passed the snow seemed briefly to remember falling instead of flying, drifting down soft and straight, as if the whole sky had been asked to hold still for a moment out of respect.

And the voices—

The voices sang.

Not words. Not quite. But the hum that had ridden against August's hip for a hundred miles opened out into harmony, into many-voiced sound, and the sound did not fade so much as widen, spreading over the pass, down the valleys, thinning as it went the way a bell thins into silence, and in the middle of it, threaded clear as a needle through cloth, the courier's voice said:

*Thank you.*

August's breath went out of them.

*I never got to say it properly,* the voice said, and it was fading even as it spoke, stretching thin and bright, a note held past all reason. *You carried me farther than anyone ever carried me alive. And you're going to carry her bones home—you promised—and you'll find her daughter, and you'll tell her that her mother was brave right up to the end, braver than the end needed her to be—*

"I will," August said aloud, into the wind, into the widening silence. "I swear it. On my name. Both of them."

A pause. The kind of pause a smile lives in.

*Both of them,* the voice echoed, warm. *That's the right number.*

And then the storm closed over the last of the brightness, and the pass was only a pass again, and the wind was only wind, and August stood in the saddle with an empty vial in their bare hands and felt the cold arrive all at once, everywhere, as if the souls had been the only thing standing between them and winter.

They did not cry. Or rather, they cried the way the mountain cries—internally, structurally, in places no one can see—while their face stayed still and their hands stayed steady, and Sefa put one gloved hand on their shoulder and left it there, saying nothing, because some silences are the largest possible kindness.

After a while August stoppered the empty vial and put it away. Some vessels you keep. Not for what they held—for what they taught you about holding.

* * *

They camped that night in the saddle's lee, and August slept without dreams for the first time since Lowmark, and woke to a sky scrubbed clean and hard and blue, and the world below them glittering to every horizon like a floor of crushed glass.

Half a soul, they thought, watching the light. That's what I brought out. Half of me is still down there in the dark, in a vault, in a bargain, standing surety for strangers. Half of me is collateral.

It should have felt like loss. They turned it over in themselves, carefully, the way they'd turn a carving, looking for the wound in it, and could not find one. What they found instead was stranger: a sense of extension. Of reach. Somewhere beneath the ice their other half stood in the dark among the jars, among the mined and minted and forgotten, and it was *theirs*, and it was *there*, and as long as it was there, August had a reason to come back that no winter could seal shut.

The court thinks it took a hostage, they thought. Let it. Every hostage is also an anchor. Every debt is also a thread, and threads run both directions.

Sefa handed them bread and hot tea from the lamp-warmed tin, and they ate and drank and studied the northern approaches with a carver's patience, marking routes, marking shelters, marking the shape of the journey they would make when the world thawed. Then they took out their knife and a scrap of antler from their pack—there was always a scrap, there had always been a scrap—and began, without quite deciding to, to carve.

The antler wanted to be a whistle. August let it. Small, plain, a traveler's whistle, the kind shepherds carried, the kind mothers gave children going off to market. As the blade moved, August listened the way they always listened, for the voice in the material—and found none, and found that this, too, was a kind of answer. Silence. Choice. A thing not yet claimed by anyone, waiting to be named by the hand that finished it.

When it was done they held it up against the sky, turning it, and the morning light ran along its curves.

"One more trip," Sefa said, watching them. "You're going back. For the rest of them."

"For all of them." August tucked the whistle away. "And for my other half. And I'm not going alone, and I'm not going as nobody, and next time the mountain will know exactly whose feet are on it."

Below them, far to the south, barely visible, a plume of smoke rose from a crack in Cindrel's flank—the mountain breathing, or coughing, or clearing its throat. Watching it, August felt their half-soul shift in its keeping, faint and far, like a sleeper turning over.

Like something answering.

They shouldered their packs and started down, into the long white silence, toward the spring that was waiting for them at the bottom of the world.
