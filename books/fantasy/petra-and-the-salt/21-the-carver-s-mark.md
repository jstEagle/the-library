## 21. The Carver's Mark

The thaw came late to Lowmark that year, and when it came it came all at once, as if the mountain had been holding its breath since midwinter and could bear the silence no longer. Snowmelt roared down the Widow's Stair in silver ropes. The gutters of the border town ran white with mineral light. And in the small workshop above the cooper's yard—new glass in the window, new hinges on the door, a new smell of pine shavings and beeswax where there had once been only bone-dust and fear—August Villalobos sat before a half-finished whistle and did not know what name to put at the bottom of it.

They had been back for six weeks. Long enough for the frostbite scars on their fingers to fade from angry purple to a pale, tender pink. Long enough for the town to stop staring quite so openly at the person who had gone up the mountain in winter and come down in spring, alone, thinner, with a jar of salt-glass that sang at night if you listened too long. Long enough for August to have carved eleven pieces and signed none of them.

The whistle was for a child—not dead this time, thank every quiet god, but born without breath enough to cry. The mother had come in March, wrapped in a man's coat two sizes too big, and set a bundle of swaddling on the bench without a word, and August had unwrapped it and found not a body but a sleeping infant, blue-lipped, breathing in sips.

*Whistle her a wind,* the mother had said. *Something she can borrow until her own comes in.*

So August carved. They worked slowly now, the way they had learned to work in the dark places under Cindrel, where haste was a kind of theft. The bone was elk, old and dense, and into its barrel they cut a spiral like a snail's shell, and into the spiral they breathed—literally breathed, lips to the raw ivory, the way Veyra had taught them in the last hour before the gate, the way no guild-carver would ever admit to doing—so that the instrument would carry not just sound but *self*. A borrowed wind. A little of the maker's own breath, sealed in the grain.

Half a soul made for generous spending. That was the joke August told themselves on bad nights, and some mornings it even worked.

The trouble was the mark. Every carver marked their work—it was law in the guild towns, superstition everywhere else, and simple sense always: an unmarked piece carried no warranty against hungry spirits, no lineage, no protection. For twenty-six years August had left their work blank. Blank was safe. Blank was nobody. A blank whistle could be dropped in a ditch and no enforcer would come asking whose hand had shaped it. The whole architecture of their survival had been built on anonymity, stone by stone, and now they sat holding a finished piece—a good piece, maybe the best thing their hands had ever done—and the space at its base waited like an open door.

*Sign it,* said the voice that lived sometimes in the bones on the shelf, and sometimes, more dangerously, in the marrow behind August's own ears. The courier's voice. Sefa had carried her bones home to her daughter in Harrow Fen in April, and August had kept only the smallest splinter, with permission, the way you keep a lock of hair. *You paid for a name. It cost you half of everything you are. Wear it.*

"It isn't mine," August said aloud, to the empty room, because talking to dead women had stopped feeling strange somewhere around the ninth circle of the mountain. "It's the name they sold me under. It's the price tag."

*It's the name you walked back out with.* A pause, and then, gentler, in the tone she'd used at the vault door: *Petra. Say it once. Just to hear how it sits.*

"Petra." The word came out rough, unpracticed, like a joint that hadn't moved in years. Stone. Of course. Of course her parents—or whoever had held the contract, whoever had stood in that white antechamber with a newborn and a debt—had named her for stone. Rock. The thing that remains. Some clerk with cold hands had written *Petra Villalobos* in the ledger of the Salt Court, and beneath it, the sum: one soul, whole and mint-fresh, sold against a family's ruin.

August turned the whistle in the lamplight. Below the spiral, in the smooth band of bone reserved for the maker's mark, they picked up the finest graver—the one Brannoc had given them a lifetime ago, or three years ago, or however long ago the passes were still passable—and began to cut.

Not initials. Not a glyph. Letters, full and plain, legible from across a room: **P. VILLALOBOS**.

It took less time than they expected. The strangest part was how ordinary it felt, the small biting whisper of steel in ivory, the curling ribbons of pale dust. Twenty-six years of being nobody, undone by four seconds of carving. When it was done, August held the whistle up and looked at the name and felt—nothing dramatic. No thunder. Just a settling, like a house at night when the last fire settles into coals. As though something that had been braced for years had finally been allowed to lean its weight down.

Outside, the first carters of spring were shouting in the lane. Somewhere below, the cooper's dog barked at nothing. The world went on being ordinary, and Petra Villalobos sat in it, named, and found that it fit the way worn boots fit: not beautifully, but truly.

* * *

The sanctuary idea was Sefa's, though Sefa would deny it to her grave, which knowing Sefa might be soon and might not.

She had stayed through the tail of winter in the spare room that wasn't really a spare room, just a corner of the workshop with a cot and a curtain, ostensibly repairing the boat she'd abandoned at the drowned stair and actually drinking August's tea and arguing about everything. It was during one of these arguments—this one about whether smugglers or carvers were more superstitious—that the knock came at the door.

The woman on the step was maybe forty, gray-faced, dressed in clothes that had been fine a year ago and were now just tired. She looked at August, then past them at the interior of the workshop, and her eyes went to the shelf of bones with an expression August knew intimately: hunger and terror in equal measure.

"You're the one," she said. "From the mountain. You're the one who broke it open."

"I didn't break anything open," August said, which was true; the chorus had done that, the mined dead rising through August's carved throat in a resonance that had cracked the court's foundations like river ice. "I made noise. That's all."

"My husband." The woman's hands were shaking. "He worked the Cindrel mines. Eleven years. He came home in the spring—walked home, all this way—but he's—" She faltered. "There's a hole in him. Where he used to be there's a hole, and he sits by the window, and he doesn't—" Her voice cracked. "They said you brought jars down. They said some of them got their souls back."

"Some did," August said carefully. "The ones I carried were released into the snow above Thornfell. If his soul was among them, it went free. But freed souls don't always find their way home. Half of them scatter. Some go back to the court rather than stay loose."

"He followed me," the woman whispered. "He walked the whole road behind me like a shadow. And I thought, when we got here, when he saw the house—he'd come back to himself. He didn't. He just sits."

August looked at the woman for a long moment. Then at the shelf. Then at the splinter of courier-bone wrapped in silk beside the lamp, humming faintly, attentive.

"Bring him tomorrow," they heard themselves say. "At dawn. Before the street wakes up."

The woman wept right there on the step, ugly and grateful, and August stood awkwardly in the doorway holding the doorframe as if it might hold them too.

When she was gone, Sefa emerged from behind the curtain with the expression of someone who has watched a friend walk into deep water. "You can't fix him. You know that. Half-souled is half-souled. The ministers kept the other halves as surety—you said so yourself, you *bargained* so—"

"I know what I bargained."

"Then what are you going to do?"

August sat down at the bench. Picked up the whistle. Set it down again. "I don't know yet," they admitted. "But I know what it's like to sit by a window with a hole in you. I did it for twenty-six years and didn't even know what the hole was." They looked up. "And I know how to make things that carry voices. Maybe a soul doesn't need to come all the way home. Maybe it just needs to be able to call through the wall."

Sefa stared at them. Then she laughed—one short, disbelieving bark—and dragged a stool to the bench and sat down across from August like a woman taking up a post. "Right," she said. "Dawn. We'll need more tea. And you're going to need a bigger shelf."

By midsummer, the shelf had become a wall, and the wall had become the workshop's second trade. Word traveled the way word does among the desperate—faster than any honest news, slower than any lie. They came on foot and on carts, from Lowmark and beyond it, from the mining villages on Cindrel's southern flank and the farm towns along the salt roads: families of the returned, carrying their hollowed loved ones like fragile cargo. A miner who had forgotten his children's names but remembered every verse of a song he couldn't explain why he loved. A girl of nineteen who spoke in a flat monotone except when she slept, when she wept and called for someone named Miri. An old man who sat perfectly still for hours and then, without warning, reached out and touched whatever bone was nearest, with an expression like a man reading a letter.

And August—Petra, though most of them still called them August, and Petra let them, because names were theirs to give now, not anyone else's—took each case and studied it the way they'd once studied contracts in the court archives: patiently, looking for the signature underneath.

The method, such as it was, grew out of failure. The first dozen attempts failed. Carved instruments that should have bridged the gap between the sitting-husbands and their scattered souls produced only silence, or worse, echoes—fragments of strangers' lives bleeding through, which frightened the families more than the emptiness did. It was the old man who taught them, without meaning to. He touched a scrap of elk bone on the shelf and murmured, clear as day, in a voice not his own: *cold down here, and the doors all locked.* And Petra understood.

The souls weren't lost. They were *locked out*—of their bodies, of the world, hovering at the threshold the way the courier's ghost had hovered, unable to cross without a door. And a door, in Petra's trade, was simply a shaped passage. Bone that had been breathed into. Ivory that remembered.

So they built thresholds. Small ones, mostly: a whistle a wife could blow at the window each evening, so that the husband's wandering half could hear the shape of home and drift closer. A ring of beads for the girl who wept for Miri, strung on cord that had been knotted over the graves of Miri's actual bones, so that in sleep she could follow the knot-line down into dreams and visit. Nothing cured. Nothing restored. But the sitting men began to sit nearer their families. The flat-voiced girl woke one morning and said "good morning" with a rise at the end of it, like a question, like a person, and her mother had to leave the room to cry.

Payment was a problem Petra solved the way they solved most problems now: badly at first, then honestly. They refused coin from the poorest and accepted it from those who had it, and posted no prices at all, which scandalized everyone and somehow worked. What they asked instead, quietly, of every family they helped, was a promise: when the winter gates opened again—if they opened, if the court honored its bargain, if the half-held surety was ever released—someone would walk up the mountain and help carry the rest of the dead home. A chain of hands, reaching toward Cindrel. Petra had left half their soul in the vault as ransom for the stolen. They intended, someday, to collect on that ransom. They were assembling, one grateful family at a time, the people who would climb up and help them do it.

Sefa called it an army. Petra called it a debt schedule. Both of them were right.

* * *

On the evening of the autumn equinox—the year turning, the light going thin and gold—the last piece of the season sat finished on the bench, and Petra sat before it with the graver in hand and the lamplight steady, and found that the hesitation was gone.

It was a bell, this time. Not a whistle, not a flute: a small hand-bell of elk bone for the gray-faced woman's husband, who had begun, in recent weeks, to stand at his window at dusk and move his lips as if answering someone. The bell was tuned low, a sound like a distant herd, and inside its crown Petra had breathed the deepest breath they had—half a soul's worth of generosity, spent gladly.

Below the crown, in the smooth band, they cut the mark. No hesitation. Four seconds of steel in ivory.

**P. VILLALOBOS**

Then, because it was equinox and the year was turning and some things deserved ceremony, Petra did something they had never done: they carried the bell to the window, opened the shutters to the cold gold evening, and rang it once, out over the rooftops of Lowmark, toward the mountain.

The note went out thin and clear. And from far away—from everywhere, from the marrow of the world, from the shelf behind them and the ground beneath the town and the dark mass of Cindrel on the horizon—something answered. Not an echo. An answer. A single tone, deep and patient, like a great door beginning, after a very long winter, to open.

The splinter of courier-bone hummed on its silk. *Well done, Petra,* said the dead woman, warm as a hearth. *Now they know your name too.*

Petra smiled, set the bell down, and reached for the next piece of bone.

Because on the doorstep below, plain as a scar in the evening light, lay a line of fresh white salt.
