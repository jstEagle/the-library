## 22. Salt in the Doorway

The first frost of spring had come late and gone early, and by the time the crocuses pushed their pale fists through the mud of Lowmark's common, Petra Villalobos had learned to sleep with her window open.

This was not courage. Or rather, it was a particular kind of courage—the kind that looks like carelessness from the outside. For twenty-six years August Villalobos had slept behind shutters and bolted doors, had carved in workshops with two exits, had kept no ledger, signed no work, owed no one anything that could be traced. Now there was a sign above her door, painted in her own hand: *Villalobos — Bone & Ivory, Carvings Made to Order.* Now there was a ledger on the counter with names in it, real names, written in her own careful script. Now there was a door she unlocked each morning and locked again each night, and everyone in Lowmark knew which door was hers.

She had spent the winter learning what it cost to be findable. Some nights the price still felt ruinous.

But this morning, when she came down the narrow stair with her sleeves rolled and her hair still damp, she stopped on the last step because the light through the workshop's front window was wrong—too bright, too clean—and then she saw why.

A line of salt ran across her doorstep.

Not spilled salt. Not the scatter of a careless delivery boy or the leavings of a fishmonger's cart. A line, drawn straight and deliberate as a scribe's rule, white as bone against the dark stone of the threshold, running the full width of the doorway and stopping precisely at either jamb as though it had been measured. Petra stood very still on the stairs and looked at it, and the half of her soul that lived inside her chest—the warm half, the half that had walked out of the mountain singing quietly to itself—went cold in a way that had nothing to do with the season.

She knew the mark. Everyone who had ever traded below the mountain knew the mark, though few admitted knowing it. The Salt Court did not send letters. It did not knock. It drew a line of salt at your threshold, and the line said only one thing, in the wordless way that all the court's messages were wordless:

*We know where you live.*

Petra descended the last three steps slowly, the way one approaches a sleeping animal, and crouched at the door without touching it. Through the frosted glass of the upper pane the morning light fell in a soft grey wash, and beyond the salt line the street was empty—cobbles shining with meltwater, the baker's chimney two doors down just beginning to smoke, a cat sitting on a wall with the profound indifference of cats. Ordinary. Entirely ordinary. That was the trick of it, she thought. The court never came in thunder. It came in the shape of an ordinary street.

She reached for the latch, hesitated, and then—because she was no longer the person who hesitated at her own front door—lifted it and swung the door wide.

The salt line lay unbroken before her boots. And beside it, set neatly against the left-hand jamb where a milk bottle might wait, sat a small jar.

Salt-glass. She knew it on sight, the way you know the face of someone you loved in a dream—by its wrongness, by the faint interior shimmer that ordinary glass never had, like water remembering it was once snow. It was perhaps the height of her thumb, stoppered with wax that had been pressed smooth by careful fingers, and inside it—

Inside it was *her.*

There was no other way to say it. Petra knelt on her own doorstep in the cold morning air and looked into the jar, and what looked back was not a thing but a presence: a warmth, a weight, a slow pulse like a sleeper's breath seen through a blanket. The jar hummed against her palm when she lifted it—not sound, exactly, but the memory of sound, the way a struck bell keeps humming in the hand long after the ear gives up on it. Half a soul. Her other half. The half she had left in the vault beneath Mount Cindrel as surety for every stolen dead soul she had carried out into the snowstorm and freed.

She had told herself, all winter, that she would never see it again. That was the bargain. Half for the many; the mountain kept its share until the debt of the stolen dead was paid—or until the mountain decided otherwise. She had made her peace with it in the way you make peace with a missing limb: completely, and never.

And now here it sat on her doorstep, returned unasked, unclaimed, without note or signature or price.

Her hands were shaking. She noticed this with a strange detachment, as though observing a carving that had developed a flaw. The court did not give things back. The court did not forgive debts. The court did not leave gifts on doorsteps in the springtime like a neighbor returning a borrowed pan. Whatever this was, it was not kindness.

It might, she thought slowly, turning the jar so the light moved through it, be surrender.

Or it might be bait.

* * *

She did not open it. Not yet. She carried it inside instead, past the workbench where three memorial whistles waited in various stages of completion, past the shelf of finished pieces each bearing her new mark—a small V cut into the base, two strokes meeting like a bird in flight—and set it on the corner of the bench where the morning light could reach it. Then she went back outside with a broom.

She swept the salt line away. All of it, every grain, into the dustpan and out to the gutter, where the meltwater took it in a thin white ribbon toward the drain. Let them know she'd read the message. Let them know she hadn't flinched. There was no answer she could send that would be adequate, so she sent the only one she had: she opened the shutters wide, hung out her sign, and set a small carved wren on the windowsill where the street could see it, priced at almost nothing, because grief should not be expensive and neither should hope.

The first customer of the day came at midmorning: the cooper's wife, wanting a charm for her son who was going down to the coast to seek ship-work. Petra carved while they talked, the knife moving through a scrap of antler with the easy intimacy of long acquaintance, and the woman chattered about tides and wages and whether the coastal towns had good bread, and none of it mattered, and all of it mattered enormously. This was the life August Villalobos had never dared want: an ordinary morning, an ordinary customer, a name spoken aloud in her own shop without fear.

But her eyes kept going to the jar on the bench.

By afternoon she had stopped pretending otherwise. She washed her hands, closed the shop early—there would be gossip; let there be gossip—and sat down across from the little salt-glass vessel with her elbows on the bench and her chin in her hands, the way she used to sit across from difficult carvings, waiting for the material to tell her what it wanted to be.

"You're supposed to stay gone," she said aloud. Her voice sounded strange in the empty shop. "That was the whole point of you staying gone."

The jar pulsed gently, like something turning over in its sleep.

She understood, distantly, what the ministers must have reasoned—if indeed it was the ministers, if indeed there were still ministers to do any reasoning. The chorus had cracked their foundations. The mined dead had risen in resonance through every gallery of the court, and the mountain had groaned like a living thing in pain, and when the dust settled, whatever authority remained below had inherited a vault full of sureties it could no longer pretend were property. Perhaps the new masters—there were always new masters—had decided that a hostage half a soul was worth more returned than kept. Returned, it bought something. Kept, it bought nothing but the memory of a thief who had beaten them.

Or perhaps, said a smaller and less comfortable voice, it was simply a message after all. Not *we know where you live*—that was the salt line, the old grammar. The jar was the new sentence, and its meaning was simpler and more terrible:

*We can reach you. Anywhere. Whenever we choose.*

Petra picked up the jar. It was warm. It had been warm since the moment she touched it on the doorstep, and it was warmer now, and she found she could not make herself put it down.

* * *

The courier's bones had gone home in March.

Petra had carried them herself, wrapped in oilcloth, up the long road east to a village whose name she had promised a dying woman she would learn. The daughter had met her at the gate—a girl of maybe nineteen with her mother's sharp jaw and none of her mother's frostbite—and when Petra unwrapped the bundle and the girl saw the bones, she had not wept. She had put her hand flat on her mother's skull, the way you'd quiet a horse, and said, "You're late," and then she had wept.

They had buried her at the edge of the barley field, under a stone Petra carved herself: no saint's symbols, no guild marks. Just a name, and dates, and beneath them a single line of text the daughter had asked for: *She carried things the whole way.*

On the walk home, alone on the high road with the thaw swelling the streams, Petra had heard—for the first time since the snowstorm—nothing. No voice in the ivory. No dead woman murmuring through the whistle in her pocket. Just wind, and her own breathing, and the enormous ordinary silence of a world that had finally stopped asking things of her.

She had cried then, walking, without breaking stride, the way you cry when you've been holding a door shut for a year and someone finally tells you it's allowed to open.

Now, in the workshop, with the jar humming under her fingers, she thought about that silence and understood it differently. The dead had gone quiet because the dead were free. But the jar on her bench was not dead and not free. It was hers—half of hers—and it had crossed the whole distance from the vault to her doorstep through passes that should have been sealed until summer, through territory the court controlled absolutely, delivered by hands unknown.

Someone had carried it the whole way.

She turned the jar over. The wax seal was unbroken—but as she tilted it against the lamp, she saw what she had missed on the doorstep: marks pressed into the cooling wax, small and deliberate. Not a sigil of office. Not the court's interlocking rings. A letter, or nearly a letter—a stylized shape that might have been a V, or might have been a bird with folded wings, or might have been both at once.

Her own mark. Cut into the seal of the jar containing her own soul.

Petra sat back. The lamp hissed softly. Outside, somewhere down the street, the baker's dog began barking at nothing and gave it up.

"Veyra," she said quietly.

The Minister of Small Souls, who had stayed below. Who had held the ministers' attention in the last collapsing gallery while Petra and Sefa and the jar of voices fled through the dark. Who had asked, at the final gate, with a composure Petra still didn't know how to classify, for one small favor: *carry out the jar I never had the courage to steal myself.* Petra had carried it. She had released it into the storm with all the others, a child's soul, a soul so small it barely weighed against her palm, and she had watched it go up into the snow like a spark reversing.

If anyone below had survived the fall of the old court with both their position and their conscience intact, it was Veyra. If anyone had reason to send a stolen half-soul home wrapped in the mark of the thief who inspired her—it was Veyra. And if the salt line and the jar together meant what Petra increasingly suspected they meant, then the message was not a threat at all.

It was an invitation.

*Come and see what we've built on the ruins,* the jar seemed to hum against her skin. *Come and see whether the dead are safer now. Come and finish what you started, because we cannot finish it without you.*

Or it was a trap. It could still be a trap. Petra had learned enough about power in one winter beneath the mountain to know that traps rarely announced themselves as traps; they arrived shaped like gifts, warm as sleeping animals, humming with everything you'd ever wanted back.

She looked at the jar a long time. Then she laughed—short, surprised, a little ragged—because she had just realized she was doing the thing she'd sworn off forever: weighing safety against purpose as though they were coins of the same metal. August Villalobos would have swept the jar into the gutter with the salt and bolted the door and carved anonymous whistles until the mountains crumbled. August had earned her rest, God knew. But August was a name on a sold contract, and Petra Villalobos was the name she'd chosen, and Petra had left half her soul in the dark on purpose, and purposes, once begun, did not politely conclude themselves because the weather improved.

She rose, fetched her coat, and packed her kit with the economy of long practice: knives, wax, blank bone, the map—she still kept the courier's map, softened now at every fold, more relic than tool. Into her pocket went the jar, warm against her hip like a second heartbeat.

At the door she paused, took out her carving knife, and considered the salt line's ghost on her threshold—the sweep-marks still visible in the damp stone, the court's sentence half-erased. She crouched and, with the tip of her knife, cut a single fresh mark into the stone beside her door where anyone passing with eyes to see it would find it: a V, two strokes meeting like a bird in flight, deep enough to outlast weather.

*Received,* the mark said, in the wordless grammar of thresholds. *And answered.*

Then Petra Villalobos locked her shop, hung the key on its nail inside—she had neighbors now; they would mind the place—and stood in the street looking north, toward the Thornfells, toward the pass called the Widow's Stair, toward the mountain that breathed salt and memory and had held half of her all winter.

The evening light lay along the peaks like a blade laid open. Somewhere under all that stone, a minister was waiting, and a vault was learning to be something other than a prison, and the dead—her dead, the ones she'd freed and the ones still shelved—were watching to see what the woman with half a soul would do next.

She smiled. It was not a gentle smile. It had teeth in it, and history, and a debt to collect.

Petra pulled her collar up against the wind, turned her back on the warm lit windows of the only home she'd ever chosen, and started walking toward the mountain.

Behind her, in the jar against her hip, her other half beat like wings.
