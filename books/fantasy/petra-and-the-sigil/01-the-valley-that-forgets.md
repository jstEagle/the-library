## 1. The Valley That Forgets

There was a particular hour in Hollowmere when the light went thin and honest — late afternoon in autumn, when the sun dropped behind the western ridge and the whole valley seemed to exhale. Lior Iversen had lived here eleven years, and she had learned to love that hour precisely because it asked nothing of her.

She was gluing a spine.

The book on her workbench was a hymnal, older than the town's church and considerably better loved, its boards swollen from some long-ago flood. She worked with a brush no wider than her little finger, laying paste into the gutter with the patience of someone who had nothing else in the world to do and had made peace with it. Mostly.

Outside her window, Hollowmere went about its business. The baker's boy hauled his trays across the square, trailing the smell of brown bread. Two women argued cheerfully about the price of wool outside Tamsin's shop. A dog slept in the exact center of the road because no cart had come through in three days and the dog knew it. It was, by any reasonable measure, a good life. Quiet. Warm. Small enough to hold in both hands.

Lior set down the brush and flexed her right hand.

The scar ran across her palm like a river drawn by a careless child — a pale line that started at the base of her thumb and wandered up toward her wrist before giving up. Eleven years old, that scar. Older than her exile, though she couldn't say how much older, because she couldn't remember getting it. That was the arrangement of things: there were pieces of herself that had been sanded away, and she'd learned not to run her fingers over the gaps too often, the way you learn not to touch a bruise.

It ached now, faintly, the way it always did this time of year. Somewhere beyond the ridges — out in the wide world where weather still meant something — a storm was passing. She could feel it the way sailors feel a change in tide: not as sound or sight but as pressure, a low tugging behind the scar tissue, like a door held shut from the other side.

"None of that," she told her hand, and went back to the hymnal.

Magic died at the ridge line. Everyone in Hollowmere said it the way they said the valley's name, flatly, without wonder, the way you'd say *the mill is downstream* or *it rains in spring*. Except it didn't rain in spring, not properly — mist came up the slopes and dampened the fields, but true rain, the kind that fell hard and loud and washed the world clean, never crossed the ridge. The founders had seen to that a century ago. They'd drawn their great working around the valley like a chalk circle and unmade magic inside it, and whatever storms raged in the world beyond broke against the hills like waves against a seawall.

The children of Hollowmere grew up thinking this was normal. Lior had grown up elsewhere, and so she knew it for what it was: a wound dressed as a blessing. But knowing didn't help. You could not argue with a ridge.

She finished the spine, weighted the hymnal under two flat stones and the brass anvil she used for pressing, and wiped her hands on her apron. Paste, thread, leather scraps, the sweetish smell of wheat starch — these were the borders of her world now, and mostly they were enough.

*Mostly* was the operative word, and she knew it, and she declined to examine it, which was itself a kind of craft she'd practiced for eleven years.

* * *

Marta Pell came in just before closing, as she did every Thursday, carrying a book wrapped in a flour sack.

"You've got to save it," Marta announced, unwrapping it on the counter with the reverence of a woman unveiling a relic. "The mice have been at it."

The book was a ledger — the Pell family farm accounts, going back four generations — and the mice had indeed been at it, along with damp, neglect, and what appeared to be a child's enthusiastic crayon rendering of a horse across the final page.

"This is a record of debts," Lior said, turning pages. "Your grandfather owed the Hendersons nine shillings for a ram."

"And we paid it, thank you very much, in 1888, and I want it *recorded*." Marta planted her elbows on the counter. "My son says throw it out. My son says paper's paper. But that book has every lamb born on our land since before the war my grandmother wouldn't talk about, and if you burn a thing like that, you're burning the lambs."

Lior smiled despite herself. "I'll do what I can. The crayon horse stays, though. It's load-bearing."

"It's my brother's work. He was six."

"Then it definitely stays."

Marta stayed to gossip — about the Hendersons' new roof, about the warden's clerk who'd been seen buying two pairs of boots in one month, about the strange traveler reported on the north road. That last one caught Lior's ear more than she wanted it to.

"A traveler? We don't get many."

"That's what I said! Old Cobb saw her at the waystation. Said she had a foreign accent and asked more questions than a tax assessor. Asked about the valley, and the ridge, and—" Marta lowered her voice with relish, "—whether the stories about the founders were true."

"What did Cobb tell her?"

"Cobb told her the founders built the mills and died rich, which is what Cobb tells everyone, because Cobb believes history is a form of politeness." Marta sniffed. "Anyway. Thursday next for the ledger?"

"Thursday next," Lior agreed, and walked her out, and stood a moment in the doorway after the bell stopped swinging.

A stranger asking questions. Well. Strangers passed through sometimes — peddlers, surveyors, the occasional lost pilgrim who'd been promised a shortcut that didn't exist. They looked at the valley, found it gray and damp and unremarkable, and moved on. Hollowmere had a genius for being forgettable. Lior had come to think of it as the town's one great talent, and she'd borrowed it shamelessly.

Still, she stood in the doorway a moment longer than necessary, watching the road wind up toward the northern pass, where the last light was going copper on the ridge stones.

Her palm ached. The storm beyond the hills was moving, she thought — sliding east, dragging its weight along the top of the world like furniture dragged across a floor above her head.

She closed the door.

* * *

Her rooms above the shop were small and exactly arranged, which is to say they were arranged the way a person arranges things when they are pretending the arrangement is a preference rather than a fortification. One chair by the fire, angled to face the window. One cup, one bowl, one plate — though there were four of each in the cupboard, bought in her first optimistic year, never used. Books everywhere else. Books stacked on the stairs, books in the window seat, a book propping up the short leg of the table, which she'd never fixed because the book happened to be a dreadful treatise on drainage and deserved the demotion.

She ate bread and cheese and an apple that had gone soft on one side, and she read, and the fire ticked, and it was fine. It was all fine. This was the sentence she repeated to herself most nights, the way other people said prayers: *It is fine. You chose this. You are safe.*

Because that was the truth of exile, the part nobody warned you about: after enough years, the cage starts to look like a shape you picked. Safety and imprisonment wear the same coat. She had a trade people respected. She had Marta's Thursdays and Cobb's grudging nods and the baker's boy who saved her the end crusts because he believed — wrongly, sweetly — that she was too poor to buy a whole loaf. She had a life with no sharp edges anywhere.

And she had the ache in her hand, which flared again as she banked the fire, and she sat with it a moment, as she did most nights, letting it hurt.

This was the one dishonesty in her careful life that she permitted herself: she let the scar ache, and she did not ask it to stop. Because the ache was the only proof remaining that she had ever been anything other than a bookbinder in a valley where nothing magical could survive. Somewhere past those ridges, lightning was walking on the mountains, and her body remembered how to answer it, even if her mind had been made to forget.

Eleven years ago she had been sentenced here — or so the tribunal records said, the ones Maren kept locked in the warden's archive, the ones Lior had signed without reading because she'd been nineteen and terrified and half-mad with a fever the trial physicians called "residual storm-sickness." *Uncontrolled evocation,* the verdict read. *Endangerment of life. Sentence: permanent residence, Hollowmere Valley.* She remembered almost none of it. She remembered rain. She remembered screaming, though whether it was hers she couldn't say. She remembered waking on the south road with a scar on her palm and a hole in her memory shaped exactly like the worst night of her life.

The founders had sealed the valley against magic a century before she was born. Her crime had been impossible. Everyone said so — kindly, uncomfortably, the way people discuss a mad relative. *Storm-calling, in Hollowmere? Poor girl. Whatever she saw that night, it wasn't real.*

So she had stopped insisting. That was the trick of surviving in a place that forgot things: eventually you let yourself be forgotten too, and then the forgetting does half your grieving for you.

Lior blew out the lamp. In the dark, the ache in her palm pulsed once, twice, like something knocking politely on a far-off door, and then settled as the storm moved on, over the ridge and away, raining on a world she would never see again.

"Goodnight," she told it, as she always did.

* * *

Thursday brought rain — the valley's version of it, a fine gray sift that never quite committed — and with it, Marta's ledger, and with Marta's ledger, news.

"She's here," Marta said, shaking out her shawl on the doorstep like a woman shaking off scandal. "The traveler. Took a room at the Waystone Inn, of all places, and ordered tea at four in the afternoon like she had nowhere else in the world to be."

"Perhaps she didn't."

"Mm." Marta's eyes narrowed with the pleasure of a woman building a theory. "And here's the thing, Lior — Cobb swears he saw her on the north road Tuesday, heading *out*. And this morning she's back, ordering tea, asking the innkeeper about the shops. About *your* shop, specifically."

Lior's hands went still on the counter. "My shop?"

"Whether the bookbinder took commissions. Whether you were friendly. Whether—" Marta leaned in, dropping to a whisper that carried perfectly across the empty square, "—you were the one who restored the Ellery family Bible, because she'd heard of it."

"Heard of it *where*? Nobody outside the valley has heard of my work. Nobody outside the valley has heard of the valley."

"Well, there's the mystery, isn't there?" Marta straightened up, satisfied, and gathered her mended ledger against her chest. "A stranger who knows things she shouldn't. Mark my words, something's coming. Weather like this always means something's coming."

"It's been 'weather like this' for eleven years, Marta."

"All the more suspicious, then." And off she went, bell jingling, leaving the smell of rain and flour behind her.

Lior stood alone in the shop. Outside, the gray sift fell on the square, soft as ash, and beyond the rooftops the ridges stood with their collars up, keeping the world's wild weather out and the valley's quiet in.

She looked down at her palm. The scar lay pale across it, silent today, dormant, just a line in the skin.

*A stranger who knows things she shouldn't.*

"Don't be ridiculous," Lior said aloud, to the shop, to the hymnals, to the drainage treatise holding up the table leg. "People pass through. People ask questions. It doesn't mean anything."

But she caught herself, that evening, choosing the blue dress for tomorrow instead of the brown one — the blue one, the one she hadn't worn since her second year in Hollowmere, on the grounds that it made her look like someone who expected company.

She hung it on the hook by the door anyway.

Just in case.
