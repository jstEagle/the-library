## 2. A Stranger Orders Tea

The trouble with living in a valley that forgets, Lior had learned over seven years, was that it never forgot her.

Hollowmere forgot everything else with admirable efficiency. It forgot the names of travelers within a season of their leaving. It forgot grudges—mostly—and it forgot weather, in the sense that no one expected any. Rain came to the ridge line and stopped there like a polite guest declining to remove its boots. The sky above the valley stayed a pale, rinsed blue eleven months of the year, and the twelfth month it merely went gray and sulked without producing anything so dramatic as a storm.

Lior had built a life inside that forgetting, brick by careful brick. She rose before dawn because she liked the way the light came gray-gold through the papered windows of her shop. She ground her own wheat paste. She kept her accounts in a neat hand and her opinions to herself, and when the occasional traveler asked what had brought her to Hollowmere—a question that came perhaps twice a year—she said, "The quiet," which was true, and "I've always loved bookbinding," which was also true, and neither answer mentioned storms at all.

Her scar ached this morning.

It did that sometimes, though there was nothing to ache about. No storm had come near the valley in years; whatever weather moved through the world beyond the hills passed them by entirely, as if the founders who'd sealed Hollowmere had also negotiated some private treaty with the clouds. But the old wound on her palm—the one shaped like a sliver of lightning, thin and white and faintly ridged—would wake at odd hours and throb, low and patient, like a dog scratching at a door it knew would not be opened.

She pressed it flat against the workbench and got on with her day.

There was work. There was always work, which was one of the small mercies of her trade: books decayed at exactly the rate human carelessness demanded, and Hollowmere had more old books than sense. The temple sent her hymnals with cracked spines. Farmers brought ledgers swollen from damp. Once a year the warden's office delivered something genuinely ancient, wrapped in oilcloth, with instructions written in Maren's narrow, watchful script—"handle gently, report anything unusual"—and Lior handled it gently and reported nothing, because it never was unusual, only old, and old was safe.

Old things couldn't hurt you. That was practically the founding principle of her entire existence.

* * *

The stranger arrived on a Tuesday, which Lior would remember later, because Tuesdays were market days and market days were the only days Hollowmere could be said to have an event.

She noticed the stranger before she understood she was noticing her, the way you notice a wrong note in a familiar song. A woman stood at the far end of the square, by the well, looking up—not at the stalls or the crowd but at the sky, with the particular stillness of someone checking for something. Her travel coat was good quality and road-worn, her hair dark and cut short in a style no one in Hollowmere wore, and she held a leather satchel the way scholars hold satchels, against the body, protectively.

Then the moment passed. The woman lowered her gaze, joined the drift of market traffic, and became simply another face, and Lior scolded herself for staring and went back to haggling over onions.

"You'll want the ones without the soft spots," the farmer's wife said, following Lior's eyeline. "That's new blood, that is. Came in on yesterday's coach."

"Did she say where from?"

"Didn't ask. Tam asked, though." The woman leaned in, delighted. "Said she was going north. Said she'd heard there was work here for someone with steady hands."

"Steady hands," Lior repeated. "Doing what?"

"That's the thing, isn't it? Nobody knows yet." The onion seller beamed, having extracted every drop of entertainment available. "Small place like ours, we'll all know by Friday."

They all knew by Thursday.

By Thursday afternoon, three separate customers had told Lior about the newcomer, and the accounts had already diverged in the way village accounts do—one said she was a surveyor, one said she was fleeing a marriage, and Old Bess from the dye house said, with the confidence of a woman who had once correctly predicted a hailstorm, that she was "one of those restorers, like yourself, dear, come sniffing at your trade."

Which was why, when the bell above the bookbindery door rang late that afternoon, Lior looked up from her bench with her guard already half-raised.

The stranger stood in the doorway, shaking rain off a coat that had no business being wet.

"I'm sorry," the woman said. "Is it raining out there, or is that just me?"

Lior blinked. "It doesn't rain here."

"So I've been told. Repeatedly. By everyone I've met since the ridge." The woman stepped inside, dripping apologetically onto the clean floorboards. "And yet. Here we are."

"It happens," Lior admitted. "Once or twice a year. A little drizzle slips through. It never amounts to anything."

"A drizzle that doesn't amount to anything." The stranger smiled, and it was a good smile—crooked, quick, the kind that suggested she found the world mildly funny and herself funnier. "You say that like it's a selling point."

"It is a selling point. It's the whole point of the valley."

"Mm." The woman looked around the shop with open, unembarrassed curiosity—at the presses, the stacks of waiting volumes, the long workbench under its window, at Lior herself, flour-paste to the elbows. "I'm looking for the bookbinder. Someone in town told me the best one in Hollowmere worked here, and then someone else told me she was the *only* one in Hollowmere, and I decided not to investigate the discrepancy too closely."

"That's usually the wise course." Lior wiped her hands. "I'm Lior Iversen. You are?"

"Petra." A pause, half a heartbeat, as if the name were being weighed before release. "Petra Voss."

"Well met, Petra Voss. What can I do for you?"

"I'm told you restore old books." Petra set her satchel on the edge of the bench, carefully, asking permission with the gesture before committing to it. "Very old ones, if the rumors are right. And I have—" she hesitated, and for the first time the easy humor dropped away, replaced by something rawer, "—something I need looked at. If you'd take the work."

"Depends on the something."

Petra unbuckled the satchel and drew out a book wrapped in linen, and even before the wrapping came off, Lior felt the familiar tightening in her chest that meant *old*, genuinely old, older than the temple hymnals, older than the warden's annual deliveries. When the linen fell away, her breath caught despite herself.

It was a psalter—or had been, once. The cover was oak boards gone silver-gray with age, the spine broken in two places, and the leather that remained was dyed a deep storm-blue, stamped with a pattern Lior didn't recognize: interlocking lines radiating outward from a single point, like ripples around a struck bell.

"Where did this come from?" she asked, and heard her own voice go careful.

"My grandmother's attic. And before that, I genuinely don't know. She wouldn't say, and now nobody can make her." Petra's mouth twisted. "The pages are holding together, mostly. But the spine's failing, and there's water damage along the lower edge—I think it flooded at some point, decades back. Can it be saved?"

Lior turned the book over in her hands, gentle as handling a sleeping bird. The stitching was a style she'd seen only in the oldest of the warden's deliveries. The water damage was bad but not fatal. The boards could be rebacked. It was, professionally speaking, a straightforward job.

It was, personally speaking, the most interesting object anyone had brought into her shop in seven years, and that fact alone made her want to be careful.

"It can be saved," she said. "It'll take time. Weeks, maybe longer, if I want the rebacking to match the original leather, and I do. The blue dye isn't made anymore—you can't buy it, it would have to be matched by hand."

"Match it by hand, then." Petra said it simply, without haggling, and Lior realized the woman had been prepared to beg. "Whatever it costs. Whatever it takes. Please."

"There's one condition."

Petra went very still. "Name it."

"I work slowly, and I don't take on jobs I can't finish. So while I have it—" Lior tapped the psalter's cover, "—you don't sell it, don't lend it, and don't let anyone else touch it. Not the temple, not the warden's office, nobody. Some people in this valley get ideas about old books."

Something flickered across Petra's face—too fast to name, gone before Lior could decide whether it was surprise or recognition. "Agreed," she said. "No one else touches it."

"Good." Lior wrote out a receipt in her neat hand, gave Petra a copy, and quoted a price that was fair rather than generous, because charging a desperate person full freight sat badly in her stomach. "Come back in two weeks and I'll show you progress. I don't do surprises."

"No surprises," Petra echoed, and smiled again—that crooked, world-amused smile—and Lior felt something in her chest shift half an inch, like a joint remembering it used to move.

* * *

She should have left it there. A professional transaction, cleanly concluded, a stranger passing through. That was the sensible shape of things, and Lior had spent seven years becoming very good at sensible shapes.

But the next morning she caught herself thinking about the ripple pattern on the cover, and wondering whether the dye had been iron-gall based or something rarer, and by noon she had pulled out her reference shelf and by mid-afternoon she was annoyed to discover she wanted the woman to come back purely so she could ask her questions.

Petra came back in four days.

"Not to rush you," she said, standing in the doorway with two cups from the tea house and an expression of elaborate innocence, "but I was walking past."

"This is the only street with shops on it."

"Pure coincidence." She held out a cup. "Chamomile. The tea house man said you took it plain, which he seemed to consider a moral failing."

"The tea house man considers everything a moral failing. It's his charm." Lior took the cup anyway, and hated how easily she took it, and took it regardless.

Petra wandered the shop while she drank, hands clasped behind her back like a visitor in a museum, reading spines without touching. "You've been here a long time," she said. It wasn't quite a question.

"Seven years."

"What was before?"

"Before?" Lior kept her eyes on the psalter, on the delicate work of lifting the broken spine lining. "Other places. This is a valley where people come to be forgotten. It suits some of us."

"And it suited you."

"It suits me." The words came out a shade too firm, and Petra, to her credit, let it lie. They drank tea in companionable silence, and Lior worked, and somewhere in the middle of the second cup she realized she was talking—actually talking, telling a near-stranger about wheat paste ratios and the criminal practice of repairing books with tape—as if the muscle hadn't atrophied at all.

"You love this," Petra said quietly, watching her hands.

"It's just a trade."

"No. It isn't. You lit up like a lamp when you described the rebacking." Petra shrugged, unrepentant. "I like watching people do the thing they actually love. It's rare. Most people are doing the thing they settled for and calling it the same word."

Lior looked down at her scarred palm, curled loosely around the teacup, and turned the hand over so the scar faced the wood. An old habit, automatic as breathing. "What did you settle for, then? Before the attic and the grandmother and the long coach ride north?"

For a moment he thought—she thought—Petra wouldn't answer. The woman's thumb traced the rim of her cup, around and around.

"Dreams, mostly," Petra said at last. "Bad ones, lately. I came here because I needed somewhere quiet enough to hear myself think. Or stop thinking. I haven't decided which."

"Hollowmere's excellent at both."

"So I'm discovering." Petra set down her cup. "Thank you for the tea tolerance. And the book. I'll leave you to it."

She paused at the door, one hand on the frame, and looked back—an ordinary look, a goodbye look, except that it lasted a beat too long and landed somewhere below Lior's ribs.

"For what it's worth," Petra said, "I'm glad the drizzle found your street."

Then she was gone, and the bell settled, and the shop was quiet again, and Lior stood in the middle of it feeling like a woman who has walked out onto ice and heard it hold—for now.

* * *

That night she dreamed of rain.

Not valley drizzle—real rain, the kind she barely remembered, sheeting down in gray curtains, thunder rolling over hills that were not Hollowmere's hills. In the dream she stood with her arms raised and her palm open and burning, and the sky answered her, and she woke with tears on her face and no memory of why.

The scar throbbed. Outside, the stars were out, cold and orderly, and the ridge line stood black against them, keeping the weather out, keeping everything out, exactly as it had for seven uneventful years.

Lior pressed her hand flat against the mattress until the ache dulled, and told herself the dream meant nothing, and almost believed it.

Almost.

Because tomorrow, she realized, lying awake in the dark, was the day the tea house man had promised to introduce the newcomer around properly—and Lior had caught herself, with genuine alarm, planning which dress to wear.
