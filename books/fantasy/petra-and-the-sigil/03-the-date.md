## 3. The Date

Lior spent forty minutes deciding what to wear, which was thirty-nine minutes longer than she had spent on any decision since her exile began.

The problem was that her wardrobe contained exactly two categories of clothing: work clothes stiff with paste and thread-clippings, and the one good dress she'd brought to Hollowmere seven years ago and never once had occasion to wear. The dress was dark green, high-collared, and smelled faintly of the cedar chest it had lived in—smelled, in other words, like a stranger's memory of her. She held it up to the window, then put it back, then took it out again.

"You're being ridiculous," she told her reflection. Her reflection, an honest creature, agreed.

In the end she compromised: the good dress, but with her work apron's absence papered over by a scarf she'd bought years ago from the tinker carts and never worn because it was too fine for glue and too cheerful for hiding. It was the color of storm-light over water. She tried not to read anything into her own choice.

Petra had said *sundown, by the well*. Not asked—said, with the particular confidence of someone who had already decided Lior would come, and whose certainty was so unbothered that arguing with it felt like arguing with weather. Lior had said yes before she'd finished deciding to, and had spent the rest of the day ruining two spines' worth of good work because her hands kept drifting toward thoughts instead of thread.

*It's one evening,* she reminded herself, locking the bookbindery. *One evening, and then you go back to being no one, and she goes back to wherever charming strangers come from.*

The thought should have been comforting. It sat in her chest like a stone instead.

Hollowmere at sundown was at its most deceptively beautiful. The valley caught the last light and held it the way a cupped hand holds water—amber on the slate roofs, gold in the orchard rows, the ridge line to the east going violet and then black as though someone were drawing a curtain across the world. Beyond that curtain, Lior knew, the sky did whatever it liked. Storms built and broke out there all the time. She could sometimes feel them, a distant ache in the scar on her palm, like a tooth remembering it had once been a tooth.

She stood by the well and waited, and hated waiting, and was glad of every second of it.

Petra arrived the way she seemed to do everything: without hurry, as though the evening had been arranged around her schedule personally. She wore a coat the color of walnut ink and carried two lanterns, one lit, one not.

"Two?" Lior said.

"One each." Petra handed her the dark one and struck a match without asking whether Lior wanted light. "I assumed you'd want your own. You strike me as someone who doesn't like being walked home."

Lior blinked. "I've never been walked anywhere."

"I know," Petra said, and lit her own lantern, and smiled like that wasn't the strangest thing anyone had ever said to her. "That's rather my point."

* * *

They went west first, away from the ridge, along the lane where the lamplighters hadn't yet made their rounds and the whole street belonged to them and their two small flames. Hollowmere after dark was a town of sounds: shutters latching, a dog complaining about something philosophical, the millrace muttering its one long sentence under the bridge.

"So," Petra said. "Tell me the thing you're best at that nobody here knows about."

"That's a strange question."

"It's my favorite question. Everyone has one. Most people die without telling anyone."

Lior considered. The obvious answers were all bound up in the life she'd buried—the things her hands could do when the sky was wrong, the way weather used to answer her like a well-trained dog. Those didn't count. Those were dead, or as good as dead, past the ridge line where magic went to be forgotten.

"I can tell a book's history by its smell," she said finally. "Not just the leather and glue. Old books hold what happened to them. A volume that survived a fire smells different on the pages nearest the spine. A book that was wept over—" she shrugged, embarrassed suddenly, "—the damp changes the binding in a way that never quite dries right. I can read a book's biography with my nose."

Petra stopped walking.

"What?"

"I said—"

"No, I heard you." Petra turned to face her fully, lantern raised, and there was that look again—the one from the teahouse, hungry and delighted at once, aimed at Lior like sunlight through a lens. "Do you know how rare that is? That's not a skill people learn. That's a sense. Like perfect pitch, or—"

"It's a bookbinder's trick," Lior said quickly. "Anyone could learn it."

"Could they?" Petra tilted her head. "Have you taught anyone?"

"No one's ever wanted to learn."

"No one's ever *noticed*, you mean." Petra resumed walking, slower now, matching Lior's stride exactly. "You're full of things nobody's noticed, Lior Iversen. It's like walking beside a house where all the windows are curtained and knowing there's someone inside."

Lior's heart performed a maneuver she did not authorize. "How do you know my surname? I don't think I gave it."

"You wrote it in the front of the ledger at the teahouse. You have very deliberate handwriting." A pause. "Also the whole town talks about you. Did you know you're a minor local mystery? *The bookbinder who keeps to herself. The one who came from nowhere.* I got three separate warnings about you before I found your shop, all of them essentially: *she's strange, be careful, she's wonderful.*"

"They said *wonderful*?"

"Old Tam at the mill said it. He said, and I quote, 'She fixed my mother's hymnal for free and never told anyone she did it, which is either sainthood or spying.'" Petra grinned. "I told him I intended to find out which."

"And which is it?"

"Oh, I'm hoping for spying. Sainthood sounds dull at dinner."

Lior laughed—a real laugh, startled out of her, loud enough that the dog down the lane answered with a single approving bark. She pressed her hand to her mouth, half mortified. She couldn't remember the last time she'd laughed outdoors. Possibly ever. Laughter, in her experience, was an indoor activity, conducted quietly, alone, usually at her own expense.

"There it is," Petra said softly.

"There what is?"

"Nothing." But she was smiling at Lior in a way that made the lantern light seem redundant.

* * *

They crossed the footbridge and climbed the low hill behind the shrine—not the eastern ridge, never the eastern ridge, but the gentle western rise where the town kept its apple orchards and its dead. The cemetery was old and kind, all leaning stones and long grass, and from its crest you could see the whole valley laid out below: the rooftops, the millpond holding a coin of moonlight, the smoke from evening fires standing straight up in the windless air.

Petra set her lantern on a flat tombstone without ceremony. "Sorry," she added, to the stone. "Occupied?"

"Who's in there?"

"No idea. Someone patient, hopefully."

They sat on the hillside in the grass, shoulder to shoulder, not quite touching. Below, Hollowmere glowed its handful of lights. Above, the stars came out in the unhurried way they did here—thickly, confidently, as if the valley's darkness were a reputation they trusted.

"Your turn," Lior said. "Best thing about you nobody knows."

Petra was quiet for so long that Lior thought she wouldn't answer. When she did, her voice had changed—lighter surface, darker water beneath.

"I talk in my sleep. Full conversations. Whole speeches." She plucked a grass stem and rolled it between her fingers. "When I was a girl, my sister used to wake me up to take dictation. She swore I said things I couldn't possibly know. Things about places I'd never been."

"What kind of things?"

"Valleys." Petra's fingers stilled on the grass. "Ridges. A town with a millpond shaped like a teardrop." She glanced sideways. "This was years before I'd ever heard of Hollowmere. Before I knew it existed."

The night went very still around Lior. Even the millrace seemed to pause mid-sentence.

"That's a strange coincidence," Lior managed.

"Mm. Or a strange non-coincidence." Petra let the grass stem fall. "I started dreaming about this place when I was nine. I came here because of a dream, Lior. Does that frighten you?"

Every instinct Lior owned—the ones that had kept her alive and invisible for seven years, the ones that flinched from warden's eyes and founder's names—stood up at once and said *yes, say yes, run.* But Petra was looking at her with the lantern light catching the edge of her face, and her hands were folded in her lap like someone braced for a blow, and Lior discovered that the truth had gotten ahead of her mouth.

"A little," she said honestly. "But I've been frightened of stranger things and survived them."

"Such as?"

"Such as—" Lior hesitated. This was the part of the evening where she was supposed to lie, or deflect, or offer the curated version: orphaned young, trained in a trade, came to Hollowmere for the quiet. All true, in the way a skeleton is a person.

Instead she heard herself say: "I wasn't always a bookbinder. Before this, I was—I was someone else. Someone louder. And I did something, or something did itself through me, and I ended up here, and the person I was stayed on the other side of that ridge." She nodded east, where the hills cut off the stars' horizon. "I don't remember much of it. That's the strangest part. It's like a room in my own house that I know is there but can't open."

Petra didn't laugh. Didn't press. She reached over, slowly, giving Lior time to move away—and Lior didn't—and took her hand. Her thumb found the scar on Lior's palm without looking for it, tracing it once, gently, like reading a line of text.

"You feel storms before they come," Petra said. It wasn't a question.

Lior's breath stopped. "How could you possibly—"

"Lucky guess," Petra said, which was such an obvious lie, delivered so serenely, that it circled all the way around to honesty. She didn't explain. She just held Lior's hand, scar and all, as if it were an ordinary hand, as if Lior were an ordinary woman, and the two lies together made something that felt, impossibly, like the truth.

Above the eastern ridge, far beyond the hills, thunder muttered—the deep, rolling voice of a storm that would break itself against the valley's boundary and never arrive. Lior felt it in her palm like a knock at a door. Petra must have felt her tense, because she shifted closer, shoulder settling against shoulder.

"Let it rain out there," Petra murmured. "We're fine in here."

* * *

They talked until the candles in the lanterns burned low. Small things, mostly—Petra's disastrous attempt to learn the fiddle ("my sister hid it in a well"), Lior's feud with the town cat who treated the bookbindery's window display as a personal lounge. But between the small things ran the larger current, unspoken: that neither of them had done this in a long time, whatever *this* was; that both of them had arrived in Hollowmere carrying luggage they didn't name; that sitting together on a hill above a sleeping town felt less like a first evening than a resumption.

When the last lantern guttered out, they walked back. At the corner where the lanes divided—Petra lodging at the inn, Lior's shop two streets on—they stopped, and the stopping had a shape to it that both of them recognized.

"Well," Petra said.

"Well," Lior agreed.

Petra leaned in and kissed her. It was brief and warm and tasted faintly of the tea they'd shared days ago, and when she drew back, the first drops of rain were falling—soft, scattered, tasting of dust and evening.

Lior looked up sharply.

Rain. In Hollowmere. Rain fell outside the valley, beyond the ridge, where the sky was allowed its moods—but here, inside the boundary, the clouds broke apart like crowds politely making way. It had not rained in Hollowmere in seven years. It had not rained, she suspected, in longer than that.

Yet here it was, landing on her cheeks, spotting her scarf, beading in Petra's hair.

"It's raining," Petra said, wonder plain in her voice. "Is that—is that unusual? I can't tell yet what's unusual here."

"It shouldn't be possible," Lior whispered. She turned her face up into it, heart hammering, some instinct older than memory singing a note she couldn't name—joy or alarm or both braided tight together. The drops were warm. They fell straight down, unhurried, as if the sky were being careful.

"Maybe the valley likes you," Petra said lightly. But she was watching Lior's face with that lens-bright attention again, cataloguing, and Lior realized with a lurch that Petra had known about the rain too—or suspected, or hoped—and had chosen this moment, this corner, this kiss, deliberately.

Everything about tonight had been deliberate. The question about secrets. The dream of the teardrop millpond. The thumb on her scar.

And underneath the joy—underneath the terrifying, unfamiliar warmth of being seen, truly seen, for the first time in seven years—something cold and quiet unfolded in Lior's chest, like a page turning.

"Come to the shop tomorrow," she heard herself say. "There's a book I'm restoring. An old one. I want—"

She stopped. She didn't know what she wanted. To show Petra the book she hadn't opened yet. To test something she couldn't name. To stand next to her while the ground did whatever it was about to do.

"I'll be there," Petra said simply, and squeezed her hand once—right across the scar—and walked away through the impossible rain, leaving Lior standing alone under a sky that had no business behaving like this, holding a warmth in her palm and a cold, gathering question in her chest:

*What else does she know?*
