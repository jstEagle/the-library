## 4. Ink Older Than Memory

The donated volume arrived on a Tuesday, which Lior would later think of as the last ordinary day of her life.

It came wrapped in sacking and tied with twine that had gone brittle as old cheese rind, delivered by a farm boy who set it on her counter with the reverence of someone handling something that might bite. "From the Halloway farm," he said. "Old mistress died in spring. Family's clearing the house. Warden said you'd know what to do with it."

Lior did know what to do with most things. That was rather the point of her.

"Did they say what it is?"

"They said it's heavy." He shrugged with the eloquence of the young and departed before she could ask anything else, which suited her fine. She liked working alone. Alone was safe. Alone was the whole architecture of her life, load-bearing and carefully maintained, like the shelf brackets she'd carved herself from valley oak because imported iron rusted strangely in Hollowmere air.

She cut the twine and unwrapped the sacking, and there it was: a book bound in green leather gone dark at the corners, swollen with damp decades, its spine cracked along both hinges. A ledger, by the size of it. Or a family Bible. Or one of those self-important local histories every prosperous farm seemed to produce a century ago, full of engravings of prize pigs and paeans to men whose only distinction was dying rich.

It smelled, when she opened it, of cellar and candle smoke and something else—something faintly electrical, like the air before weather. Which was absurd. Nothing in Hollowmere smelled of weather. Weather happened *beyond*, up past the ridge line where the clouds piled against an invisible wall and broke apart like waves on rocks that weren't there. Down here, rain was a rumor. Storms were stories grandmothers told to frighten children into coming indoors, though there was nothing outdoors to frighten them from but beetles.

Lior had lived eleven years beneath those dammed-up skies. She knew exactly one thing about them, and she carried it in the palm of her left hand: a scar, thin and pale as a fishbone, that ached on the far side of the hills whenever lightning walked. The healers in the low country had said it would fade. It hadn't. Some mornings she woke with it burning like a struck match, and she'd lie in bed and know, without seeing, that somewhere past the ridge the world was doing something bright and violent, and none of it was for her.

She'd made her peace with that. Mostly. On Tuesdays, say, when a stranger with storm-grey eyes ordered tea and looked at her like she was worth looking at, and Lior caught herself thinking dangerous thoughts about *again* and *next time* and other words with futures attached to them.

She turned the first page. Ownership inscription: *Property of E. Halloway, 118 years after the Founding.* So. Local history after all, or something adjacent. The paper was good stock underneath the foxing, and the text block was sound; it was the binding that needed mercy. She could have it restored within the week. There was comfort in that—the finite, solvable problems of books. A cracked spine was not a mystery. It was simply a spine, waiting for thread and patience.

She set it aside for the evening, because the light through the shop window had gone honey-colored, and because Petra was coming at seven.

*Petra.*

Even the name in her own head felt like showing off.

They had met—how long ago now? Nine days? Ten?—over a spilled tin of tea leaves and a conversation that should have been nothing. Petra was passing through, she'd said. Staying at the inn above the teahouse. She'd picked up Lior's mended copy of *The Shepherd's Almanac* from the lending shelf and asked, with genuine curiosity, how the spine had been rebuilt so invisibly, and Lior had heard herself explaining wheat paste and linen thread and the particular treachery of nineteenth-century adhesives, and Petra had listened. Actually listened. Asked follow-up questions. Laughed—at Lior's dry little jokes, the ones that usually landed in silence like coins down a well—and the laugh had done something structural to Lior's chest that she was still refusing to inspect closely.

Tonight they were walking to the lantern festival. Or "walking," anyway. Petra had called it a walk. Lior had spent two days pretending it wasn't anything more than a walk, while simultaneously re-inking her best boots and lying awake composing and discarding sentences like a woman rehearsing testimony.

She closed the shop at six, washed the paste off her hands—carefully around the scar, always carefully—and met Petra under the crooked sign of the Tallow & Thyme.

The evening went the way evenings are not supposed to go, which is perfectly. They walked the lantern route past the millpond, where floating candles doubled themselves in black water. Petra bought roasted chestnuts and burned her fingers and swore with a fluency that made Lior laugh until her ribs hurt. They talked about nothing important—Petra's travels, the strange flat quality of Hollowmere light, whether the miller's second son was courting the ferryman's daughter or merely losing arguments to her—and all the while some quiet animal part of Lior kept saying *this, this, be careful of this,* and Lior kept stepping over it like a root in a path.

By the time they reached the high lane above the tannery, the lanterns were embers behind them and the sky beyond the ridge had gone the deep bruised violet that meant distant weather. Lior felt it before she saw it—a prickle in her palm, the scar waking like a sleeping foot.

"You've gone quiet," Petra said.

"I'm deciding something."

"And?"

And Lior kissed her. There under the empty lane, with the lantern smoke still sweet on the air and the scar singing its far-off song, she took Petra's face in her hands and kissed her, and Petra kissed back like she'd been waiting nine days too, and the world narrowed to the warm point where their mouths met and the vast dumb happiness expanding outward from it.

When they parted, the first drops fell. Fat, warm, sporadic—rain that belonged to the mountains, leaking over the ridge in defiance of everything Lior understood about her prison. It touched her face and steamed faintly away, or she imagined it did.

"It never rains here," Lior said stupidly.

"First time for everything." Petra's smile was crooked and close. "Come on. I'll walk you home."

* *

She didn't sleep. Of course she didn't sleep. She lay in her narrow bed above the shop listening to the impossible rain tick against the shutters, cataloguing the evening like a miser counting coins: the chestnuts, the laugh, the kiss, the rain. Twice she got up to touch her scarred palm to the window glass, half expecting the ache to mean something. It meant only that storms still lived beyond the hills, indifferent to her.

Around midnight she gave up and went down to the workroom, because sleeplessness and unfinished books were old companions, and there was the Halloway volume waiting on her bench with its broken spine and its smell of candle smoke and pre-storm air.

She lit two lamps and began, properly this time, the assessment. She opened the covers gently, working from the center out, checking the sewing stations. The text block was odd—not a ledger's ruled columns, not a Bible's chapters, but dense continuous prose in a small, disciplined hand. She tilted a page toward the lamp.

Not local history. Not memoir either, quite. The opening pages read like a record of visions. *In the hundred-and-eighteenth year after the Founding,* it began, *in the season of the long rains, I was given to see—*

A crank, then. Some Halloway ancestor with a taste for scripture and too much time in winter. Lior almost closed it. But the hand was steady, unfevered, and the prose had the terrible confidence of someone reporting rather than inventing, and besides, restoration meant reading. You couldn't mend a book you refused to know.

She worked as she read, easing pages flat, softening old adhesive with a damp brush. The visionary—E. Halloway, presumably—wrote of the war between the storm-crowned houses and the founders of the valley, a war Lior knew only as the vague moral of school lessons: *the founders sealed Hollowmere from magic to end the bloodshed forever.* Every child learned it. Every child stopped asking about it by ten.

Then the handwriting changed. Grew larger. Slower. As if the writer were taking dictation.

*And I was shown a night in the days to come, when two would walk the lantern road above the tannery, and the sky would weep across the ridge for the first time in a hundred years.*

Lior's brush stopped mid-stroke.

*One of them bears the mark of the Key upon her hand, a scar like a fishbone, given in fire before memory. The other carries the Sigil sleeping in her blood, and knows it not.*

The lamps hissed. Somewhere in the walls, the house settled with a sound like a held breath.

Lior read it again. Then a third time, moving her lips slightly, the way she did with difficult colophons. The words did not rearrange themselves into sense. They sat there, patient as gravestones.

*Two will walk the lantern road.* She had walked the lantern road. Tonight. With—

No. No, this was coincidence dressed in prophecy's clothing. Vague words snagged on any life. Two people walked that road every festival night. Rain crossed ridges sometimes; weather was weather; scars like fishbones were common as freckles among folk who'd grown up around forge-fire and thorn fences. She was a bookbinder with a bad hand and a nice evening behind her, not a figure in a madwoman's century-old vision.

She turned the page because her hands turned it, apparently having opinions independent of her terror.

*On that night the Key shall give the Sigil-bearer a kiss beneath the weeping sky, and the rain shall fall warm, and the Sigil shall stir in its sleep and turn toward the light of it, as a seed turns toward a door crack.*

The workroom was very quiet. The rain had stopped—of course it had; the ridge allowed nothing to stay—but the shutters were still ticking with the last of it, and each tick sounded to Lior like a word being counted off a list.

*The teahouse,* she thought wildly. *If it says the teahouse next. If it names the Tallow & Thyme. If it gets the chestnuts right, if it knows she burned her fingers—*

She flipped forward, hunting, hating herself for hunting, telling herself she was looking for the moment the vision dissolved into ordinary nonsense, the place where the crank showed her seams. Pages of dense prophecy slid past under her thumbs—war, awakening, a Key that must *turn* or *break*, houses rising, founders stirring—and then, near the middle of the quire, a passage set apart by itself, centered on the page like an epitaph:

*And the two shall meet first at a house of tea, where one shall spill the leaves, and the other shall kneel to gather them, and their hands shall meet upon the floor, and the Sigil-bearer shall say: "You have kind hands," and the Key shall feel the ground go strange beneath her.*

Lior sat down. Not gracefully—her knees simply resigned, and she found herself on the stool with no memory of traveling there.

Nine days ago. A spilled tin of chamomile. Petra kneeling, gathering scattered leaves into her palm, looking up, and saying—warmly, idly, in the tone of a person making conversation—"You have kind hands."

Lior looked at her own hands. Kind hands. Scarred left palm. Fishbone scar, given in fire before memory.

There was no version of this in which it was coincidence. Coincidence did not quote your conversations back to you verbatim, a century in advance. Coincidence did not know about the chestnuts. She checked—it was there, three pages on, a throwaway line about *roasted nuts bought at the festival, and fingers burned, and laughter that loosened something long clenched.* The madwoman—or the prophet, the distinction had stopped mattering—had written down Lior's perfect evening like a receipt, itemized, dated, witnessed.

The ground of her life, the careful floor she'd laid board by board for eleven years—small shop, small deceptions, small safe days—went soft as wet paper beneath her.

She became aware, distantly, that she was shaking. She also became aware of something else, something worse than fear, worse even than vertigo: the passage continued. She'd seen it in her flipping, a page or two beyond the kiss, and her eyes had skated over it, but now they returned to it the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth.

*But let the Key take heed,* the old ink said, in that calm dictation-hand, *for the Sigil wakes to war, and the founders have decreed what must follow: the Key is made to turn, and what the Key unlocks, the Key must end.*

End. End *her*. End Petra, whose fingers smelled of chestnut smoke, who laughed at dry jokes like they were gifts, who was at this moment asleep above the Tallow & Thyme believing—believing what? That she'd had a nice evening with a lonely bookbinder. That tomorrow there might be another one.

Lior closed the book. Her hands were steady now, which frightened her more than the shaking had. Steady hands were working hands, and some part of her had already begun treating this as a job: assess the damage, stabilize the text, decide what must be done. Eleven years of practice at surviving quietly, mobilizing without her permission.

Outside, beyond the ridge, the real storm was walking. She felt it in her palm, pulse after pulse, like a heart beating in a locked room on the far side of a wall.

She looked at the closed green cover of the Halloway volume, sitting on her bench under the lamplight, ordinary as bread.

"One of us," she said aloud to the empty workroom, to the dead visionary, to the rain that wasn't falling, "had better be wrong."

The lamp guttered. And in the silence after, Lior realized she already knew, with the certainty of a woman reading her own name in a stranger's ledger, that the book was not finished with her—that what she had skimmed past in her panic was only the beginning, and that somewhere in those remaining pages, written a hundred years before a girl spilled a tin of chamomile, the prophecy went on to say exactly what the Key was, and who.

Her scar ached. She turned the page.
