## 5. What the Page Promises

The teahouse closed at nine. The rain had stopped by then—stopped at the ridge line, exactly as the book said it would—and Lior walked home with the prophecy volume wrapped in brown paper under her arm like something contraband, which, in a way, it was.

She did not open it again that night. She told herself this was restraint. It was actually cowardice wearing restraint's coat, and she knew the difference because she'd spent eleven years learning to tell herself comfortable lies with a straight face.

She lit no lamp. She sat in the dark of her rooms above the bookbindery with her hands folded in her lap like a well-behaved corpse, listening to the wind worry the shutters, and she thought: *I dreamed about her before I met her. I dreamed about rain that never crossed the ridge. I have been dreaming this valley my whole life.*

That was not true. She knew it was not true. But knowing and believing were two different animals, and only one of them slept in her house.

* * *

She was seven years old, standing in a courtroom that smelled of tallow and old stone, and someone was asking her a question.

*Lior. Look at me. What did you do?*

She remembered hands. Her own hands, small, held out in front of her, palms up. And she remembered—this was the part that came back to her now, lying awake, with terrible new clarity—that everyone in the room had been looking at her left palm. Not her face. Her palm.

*I don't remember,* she had said, and meant it, and the judges had exchanged looks over her head the way adults exchange looks when a child says something inconveniently true.

Then a woman in grey had knelt down to her eye level. Kind eyes. Tired eyes. She'd taken Lior's hand gently, turned it over, and pressed her thumb to the center of the small scar there—the one shaped like a branching river, the one Lior had woken up with after the trial and never been able to explain—and said, softly, so softly:

*It's all right. You won't remember any of this either.*

Lior had never thought about that sentence in twenty years. It had simply dissolved into childhood, into the general fog of being small and frightened and handed off to strangers on a cart going east. Now it surfaced whole, word for word, and she lay in the dark with her heart slamming and thought: *what kind of person promises a child she'll forget?*

A kind one, probably. That was the worst part. A kind one, doing a necessary thing.

* * *

Morning came the way mornings do in Hollowmere: grey, unhurried, smelling faintly of bread from the bakery two doors down. Lior went through the motions of opening the shop—unshuttering the windows, setting the kettle on, coaxing the stove—while the brown-paper parcel sat on her workbench radiating the particular silence of an unexploded shell.

"You look terrible," said Odette Brannigan, who owned the bakery and considered herself entitled to opinions on all matters within a hundred yards of her ovens. She deposited a heel of yesterday's loaf on the counter without being asked. "Pale as a ghost's nightgown. Are you eating?"

"I'm eating."

"That's not eating, that's surviving." Odette squinted at her. "Is this about that girl? Petra? Marta saw you two walking by the millpond holding hands like teenagers and now the whole street knows, so you may as well tell me everything."

"Odette."

"I'm just saying, it's nice. It's been years since you looked at anyone like that. Years! You've got a face like a locked drawer, dear, and suddenly it's—" Odette made an expansive gesture that nearly took out a display of ribbon. "Open. Is what I mean."

Lior managed a smile. It felt like lifting something heavy. "Thank you, Odette."

"And eat the bread. You're skin and secrets."

When the bell above the door had stopped jingling and the street had settled back into its morning murmur, Lior stood alone in the shop with the bread going cold and the parcel waiting, and she understood that she could not spend another hour pretending it wasn't there. Avoidance, she'd learned, was like damp in a wall: ignore it long enough and it brought the whole structure down.

She unwrapped the book.

* * *

In daylight it looked less sinister, which somehow made it worse. Sinister things you could fear. This was just a book—a modest octavo, quarter-bound in cracked leather, its sewing intact but its joints starting to fail, the sort of thing that came across her bench twice a month. Someone had loved it once; someone had let it go. Books were like that. People too.

She'd already read the first entry. She made herself read it again anyway, because the first rule of restoration was that you verified your damage before you treated it.

*On the evening of the fourteenth of Harvestmonth, in the year 1123, the Sigil-bearer shall come to Hollowmere. She will take tea at the Widow Quill's house, in the window seat, and she will ask after the bookbinder by name.*

Marta had confirmed it that morning, breathless, delighted: the stranger had asked for Lior by name before they'd ever been introduced. Lior had laughed it off at the time. *Maybe Odette talked.* Odette talked about everything except, apparently, the one thing that mattered.

*She will walk with the bookbinder beneath the lanterns on Cordwainer Lane, and the bookbinder will confess the shape of her exile, and the Sigil-bearer will say: "You were sent away for weathering?" And the bookbinder will laugh, and the rain will hold itself beyond the ridge, and they will kiss where the lamplight ends.*

Every word. Every single word, down to the joke Petra had made—"you were exiled for *weather*?"—down to the exact spot where the last lantern's light gave up and the dark began. Lior had noticed that dark. She'd thought, in the moment, *how convenient, how private*, and kissed a stranger in it.

She turned the page.

Her hands were steady. She noted this with a strange professional detachment, the same detachment she brought to a water-damaged folio: assess the condition, don't panic, panic later. The ink here was different—older, browner, the hand less sure. Whoever wrote the first entry had written it after the fact, or during it, watching. Whoever wrote the second had written it a century ago, guessing.

*And the Sigil shall wake in her.*

There was a diagram beneath the words. Lior had seen sigils before—in the margins of confiscated grimoires that passed through the bindery on their way to Maren's office, always defaced, always burned or scraped. This one was intact, and it was drawn not as an abstract mark but as a map of a body: a figure, arms slightly out, and at the hollow of the throat, a small branching symbol.

Lior knew that shape. She saw it every day of her life. She had traced it absently with her thumb while reading, while thinking, while falling asleep, for twenty years.

It was her scar.

"No," she said aloud, to the empty shop, in the reasonable tone she used on books that were misbehaving. "No, that's—coincidence. Branching patterns are common. Rivers. Lightning. Veins. Any fool drawing a sigil would draw something like—"

But the figure in the diagram had its left hand open, palm out, and the sigil was drawn *there* too, small, at the center of the palm, and the two marks were joined by a thin line of ink running up the wrist like a river finding the sea.

Like a key finding its lock.

* * *

Flashback, again, unwelcome as ever: she is nine, newly arrived, and the other children are playing a game in the lane behind the tannery. They stand in a circle and one of them chants—some nonsense rhyme every child in Hollowmere seems to know, though no adult will admit to teaching it—

*Storm-child, storm-child, locked away,*
*Who holds the key till judgment day?*
*The valley holds it, deep and dark,*
*And burns the hand that bears the mark.*

They're laughing. It's a skipping rhyme. Then one of them—Bren, the tanner's boy, freckled and mean in the way of children who sense weakness—stops laughing and looks at her hand, at the scar she's always half-hidden in her sleeve, and his face goes strange and careful, and he says, "Where'd you get that?"

"I was born with it," Lior lies, because even at nine she understands that some truths are doors best kept shut.

"Nobody's born with marks like that," Bren says. And then, quieter, backing away: "My gran says the Key's still in the valley. My gran says she can hear it humming at night."

The children scatter. Lior stands alone in the lane smelling of tallow and hides, and she does not cry, because she has already learned that crying confirms things.

She had filed it away as cruelty. Children were cruel; valleys were small; scars invited stories. It had never once occurred to her—not in twenty years of careful, quiet, invisible living—that the rhyme might be *documentation*.

* * *

She read on. She wished she hadn't. She read on.

*The Sigil-bearer will wake, and when she wakes, the houses of the storm-crown will know it, though they be dust these hundred years, and their servants will come out of the hills, and the war that was ended badly will begin again worse.*

*Therefore the founders made provision. As the Sigil sleeps in the bearer, so the Key sleeps in the valley, sealed and forgetting, until the hour of need.*

*The Key must end her.*

Four words. Lior read them perhaps forty times. They did not improve with repetition.

*The Key must end her, before the waking is complete, and the manner of the ending is thus: [the rest of the page is water-damaged, illegible, eaten by some long-ago flood] ...and the valley will forget her kindness, and the rain will cross the ridge again, and the founders will sleep sound, having spent the only coin that mattered.*

Below that, in yet another hand—younger, hurried, the ink blotched as if written in weather—someone had added a single line:

*God forgive us. We asked a child to carry this.*

* * *

Lior set the book down very carefully, the way you set down something that might go off, and walked to the window, and stood there.

Outside, Hollowmere went about its business. Odette's chimney smoked. Two men argued cheerfully about a fence post. A dog trotted past with the specific self-importance of a dog on an errand. The ridge line stood green and definite against the sky, and beyond it, she knew without looking, clouds were stacking up the way they always did, and the rain was falling on the far side, and none of it would ever get in, because the valley was sealed, because the valley was a box, because she was the thing in the box.

"It's not me," she told the glass. Her reflection told it right back, unconvinced. "I'm a bookbinder. I burn the toast. I fell asleep during the founding-day lecture three years running. I am not a—I'm not a *key*. Keys unlock things. I've never unlocked anything in my life except Mrs. Halloway's shop when she forgot hers, and that was a paperclip."

The shop declined to agree with her.

Because here was the trouble with denial, and Lior knew this professionally too: a forgery survives scrutiny only if you never look closely. And she had spent the morning looking closely. At the diagram. At the rhyme. At the memory surfacing now in pieces, each piece fitting the others with the horrible snugness of truth: the judges looking at her palm. The woman in grey promising her forgetfulness. The way her scar ached, every time, when storms passed beyond the hills—ached like something recognizing its counterpart through a wall.

And Petra. Petra, who had appeared in town with no history anyone could find, who had asked for Lior by name, who had followed her home along routes the prophecy had chosen a century before either of them was born. Petra, whose collarbone Lior had kissed in the dark past the last lantern, and beneath her lips, in that moment, she had felt—she had thought she imagined it—a warmth. A faint, rising warmth, like a banked fire deciding.

"Oh," Lior said quietly. "Oh, no."

She thought about the damaged page. *The manner of the ending is thus:* and then nothing, a century-old flood having eaten the instructions, which meant that somewhere out there was a task with her name on it and no directions, which meant—

Which meant nothing. Which meant an old book full of ravings. Prophecies were a copper a dozen in the cities; she'd restored a dozen fraudulent ones herself, usually commissioned by people hoping to sell them to richer fools. Ink could be aged. Paper could be faked. A clever forger with a century's lead time could arrange anything.

Except the tea. Except the name. Except the kiss in the exact dark where the lamplight ended.

Lior pressed her scarred palm flat against the cool window glass, and outside, far beyond the ridge, thunder muttered like something turning over in its sleep, and the scar answered—a low, familiar ache, an old dog hearing its name.

She pulled her sleeve down over it. An old habit. The oldest one she had.

"She's coming back today," Lior said to the empty shop, because Petra had promised to return for her repaired satchel strap, and because saying it aloud made it ordinary, made it just a Tuesday, made it anything but what the page said it was. "She's coming back, and I'm going to give her the strap, and we're going to have lunch, and none of this—"

The kettle, forgotten on the stove, began to scream.

Lior didn't move. She stood with her hand hidden in her sleeve and her heart going like a trapped bird, and she thought, with the last clear corner of her mind: *the book knew the rain would stop at the ridge. The book knew the joke. The book knew the kiss. What else does it know? What comes next?*

She turned the page before she could stop herself.

The next entry was dated tomorrow.
