## 6. Petra, Unremarkable and Impossible

The dream always began the same way: rain.

Not the polite, apologetic drizzle of the lowlands where Petra had grown up, but real rain—rain with opinions, rain that arrived like a relative who intended to stay a while. It hammered on slate roofs and ran silver down windowpanes, and beneath it stood a valley folded between two ridgelines like a secret kept in cupped hands.

In the dream she was always looking down at it from the ridge. And in the dream there was always a woman below her, small as an ink dot, walking through a town that smelled of glue and paper and woodsmoke.

Petra had been having this dream for eleven years. She had stopped telling people about it somewhere around year four, when her sister had suggested, with the particular gentleness one reserves for the possibly unwell, that perhaps she should see someone about it.

So when she finally stood on the actual ridge line above Hollowmere—an actual ridge, with actual wind trying to shove her back the way she'd come—and looked down at the actual valley, she did not feel triumphant. She felt, absurdly, like a woman arriving late to her own appointment.

"Well," she said to no one. "You're real."

The valley did not answer, which was fair. Valleys rarely did. But something in her chest—the thing that had been pulling her north for a decade, through three jobs, two broken engagements, and one deeply regrettable stint selling insurance—went quiet and warm, like a hand finally unclenching.

She had told herself a hundred stories about why she'd come. Wanderlust. A change of scenery. The collapse of her career as a restorer of antiquarian maps, which had ended not with scandal but with something worse: boredom so profound she'd caught herself alphabetizing her spice rack by *emotional resonance*. But every story was a coat thrown over the truth, and the truth was simple and slightly humiliating:

She had dreamed of this place since she was nineteen years old. And last spring, the dream had changed.

* * *

The new dream had a woman in it.

Not the distant ink-dot woman from the ridge. This one was close—close enough that Petra could see details, which was how she knew the dreams were not ordinary dreams, because ordinary dreams did not bother with details. Ordinary dreams gave you a blur and let your sleeping mind fill in the gaps. These dreams gave her specifics with the smug precision of a court stenographer.

A bookbindery with a crooked green door. A workbench scarred by decades of blades. A woman with dark hair escaping its braid in a way that suggested the braid had lost an argument. Ink on her fingers—always ink, in the creases of her knuckles, under her nails, a smear along one jawline like a bruise made of midnight.

And on her right palm, when she reached out to take a book from Petra's hands: a scar. Pale, old, running diagonally from the base of her thumb toward her wrist. In the dream, Petra always wanted to ask about it. In the dream, she never got the chance, because the woman always looked up at exactly that moment, and her eyes—

Her eyes were the problem. Her eyes were the reason Petra had spent most of a year's savings on a coach ticket and a room above a bakery in a town she'd found on no map she'd ever trusted.

Because in the dream, when the woman looked up, she looked *lonely*. Not sad—lonely is a different animal entirely, quieter and more patient. She looked like someone who had gotten very good at being alone and had mistaken the skill for contentment, the way you can mistake a cage for a nest if you've lived in it long enough.

And dream-Petra would reach across the workbench, and the dream would end before their hands touched. Every time. Eleven years of dreams, and the last year of them had ended at the same unbearable inch of air.

"I'm not romantic," Petra had informed her reflection, the night before she left. "I'm curious. There's a difference. Romantic is a character flaw; curiosity is practically a virtue."

Her reflection had not believed her either.

* * *

Hollowmere, in person, was smaller than the dream and somehow more itself, the way a face is more itself than any portrait. It sat in its bowl of hills with the wariness of a cat that has been kicked before. The buildings leaned together like old friends sharing gossip. There was a millpond gone soft with reeds, a market square with exactly enough stalls to be called a square, and a teahouse whose sign—a painted kettle mid-pour—had faded until the tea appeared to be pouring into thin air, which Petra found quietly hilarious.

She also noticed what the dream had never mentioned: the silence.

It took her two days to name it. On the third morning, standing in the market buying bread she didn't need just to have a reason to stand there, she realized: there were no storms. Not calm—calm has texture, has potential, has that tight-wire feeling before weather breaks. This was different. The sky over Hollowmere was *empty*, the way a room is empty after the furniture's been sold. Clouds came over the ridges and went flat and gray and purposeless, like travelers who'd forgotten why they set out.

And once, only once, she'd seen it happen. A proper black-bellied cloud had shouldered over the western ridge, dragging its shadow behind it, and the whole street had paused—not dramatically, nothing anyone would admit to—but she'd seen a fruit-seller's hands go still over her apples, seen an old man glance up and then quickly away, the way you glance at a door you've locked and double-locked. The cloud had thinned. Frayed. Given up. Rain fell everywhere else on the hillsides except inside the valley, where the ground stayed stubbornly dry.

*Magic dies here,* Petra had thought, with no idea how she knew the words or why they arrived wearing certainty like a uniform. *Something was locked out.*

She'd stood very still in the market square, bread going cold in her hand, and thought: *I know things I shouldn't know. I have known things I shouldn't know for eleven years. And I am standing in the exact place all of it points to, and I have not once asked myself the obvious question.*

The obvious question being: *what if the dreams aren't a gift?*

What if they were instructions?

* * *

She found the bookbindery on her fourth day, though "found" was generous. She had walked past it twice already, telling herself she was sightseeing, and both times her feet had slowed to the pace of a funeral procession. The crooked green door was exactly right. The smell leaking through its seams—paste and leather and old paper, that sweet dusty breath of books—was exactly right. Through the window she could see a workbench scarred by decades of blades.

And at the workbench, bent over a book with a bone folder in her hand, hair losing its argument with a braid, was the woman.

Petra stood outside for what she would later admit was an embarrassing length of time. Long enough that a passing grandmother gave her a look usually reserved for shoplifters and loitering suitors. Long enough that Petra rehearsed opening lines and discarded all of them: *Lovely weather* (it wasn't; it was that furniture-less non-weather), *Do you repair atlases?* (she owned no atlases), *I've dreamed of you for eleven years* (arresting, in the bad sense).

The problem—the actual, structural, load-bearing problem—was that Petra knew too much. She knew the woman's hands moved left-to-right when she smoothed a page, same as Petra's own. She knew there was a chipped mug on the shelf above the bench that the woman never used, only moved aside. She knew the scar was on the right palm. She knew the loneliness.

None of which she could say. You could not walk up to a stranger and recite her life back to her. That wasn't flirting; that was a haunting.

So instead Petra had pushed open the crooked green door and said the first neutral thing she could find: "Hello. Do you take restoration work?"

Which was true, as it happened. She did need something restored. She had planned ahead for this, in the manner of a spy preparing cover, and had brought with her a water-damaged atlas of the northern coast that she'd bought specifically to be damaged—no, that wasn't quite honest either. She'd bought it cheap because it was already damaged, and then she had told herself the purchase was practical rather than providential, and then she had slept badly, dreaming of ink-dark eyes.

The woman had looked up. And here was the thing no dream had prepared her for: the dreams had made Lior—Iversen, the sign by the door said, *L. Iversen, Bookbinder*—into a figure, a symbol, a woman-shaped answer to a decade of questions. The reality was worse. The reality was a person. Tired around the eyes in a way that sleep wouldn't fix. Wry at the mouth. Guarded the way a house is guarded when the owner has been robbed before and has learned to keep the good things hidden.

The reality was someone Petra could hurt.

That realization had arrived sometime during the second visit, or maybe the third—time had gone strange and syrupy since she'd arrived in Hollowmere—and it had brought with it its ugly twin: *you are lying to her.* Every day she didn't mention the dreams was a day built on omission. Every laugh they shared was mortgaged against a confession Petra kept promising herself she'd make tomorrow. She was doing, she understood dimly, what people do in the first act of tragedies: hoarding secrets because the truth might cost her the happiness she'd only just found, never asking what the truth might cost the other person if it arrived late.

"You're doing it again," said the baker's wife, catching Petra staring out the window of the bakery toward the bookbindery on the evening of the sixth day. She set down a plate of rolls Petra hadn't ordered. "Staring."

"I'm admiring the architecture."

"Mm." The baker's wife had the flat delivery of a woman who had raised four sons and believed nothing anyone said after age six. "The architecture with the ink on her nose."

"There's ink on her nose?"

"Every day. Never notices." The woman collected the plate money and added, without cruelty, "Whatever you're circling, girl, land or fly off. Towns like this don't care for hovering."

* * *

That night, the dream changed again.

For eleven years it had been the ridge, the valley, the workbench, the almost-touch. Tonight Petra stood in the bookbindery—her dream-self, barefoot on floorboards she could almost feel—and watched the woman work by lamplight. And the woman spoke, which she had never done.

"You keep coming back," the dream-Lior said, not looking up. Her voice was lower than Petra had imagined, roughened at the edges like well-handled leather. "Eleven years. Don't think I haven't noticed."

"I—" Dream-Petra's voice worked poorly, the way voices do in dreams. "I didn't mean any harm."

"No one ever does." The woman set down her bone folder and looked up, and her eyes were worse awake, because awake there was a whole person behind them, a whole history, a whole set of reasons for the loneliness. "Here's what I can't work out. Are you following the dream, or is the dream following you? Because those aren't the same thing. One of them means you chose me." She turned her palm up. The scar caught the lamplight, pale as a moon-track on water. "The other means something *sent* you."

"Sent me where?"

"That," said dream-Lior, "is the question you should have asked before you bought the ticket."

The lamp guttered. The walls of the dream-bookbindery peeled away like pages turning, and behind them was not night but something else—a vast dark full of watching, a darkness with attention, a darkness that had been waiting a long time and was very nearly done waiting. Petra felt it notice her the way a fish feels the hook before the pull: a small cold point of contact, intimate and terrible.

And from everywhere and nowhere, in a voice like dry paper folding, something said—or began to say, or perhaps only thought at her, loudly enough to count:

*Soon.*

Petra woke in her room above the bakery with her heart slamming and her hand pressed flat over her collarbone, where the skin burned faintly, tenderly, like the memory of a sunburn she'd never gotten. She lay still in the gray pre-dawn and made herself breathe.

Below, the ovens were waking; she could smell bread beginning. Somewhere down the lane, the bookbindery's crooked green door would open within the hour, and a woman with ink on her nose and a scar on her palm would begin her careful, guarded, half-hidden life, unaware that the stranger she'd laughed with twice was carrying a decade of dreams and a growing dread like stones in each pocket.

*I'll tell her today,* Petra promised herself, as she had promised herself yesterday, and the day before. *Today I'll tell her everything.*

She got up. She dressed. She practiced the words in the small warped mirror—*I have to tell you something strange, please don't look at me like that*—and hated how rehearsed they sounded, and hated more that rehearsal was necessary at all.

Then she went downstairs, into the empty non-weather of Hollowmere, and walked toward the bookbindery, and knocked on the crooked green door, and smiled, and lied.

"Good morning," said Petra, whose dreams had sent her. "I was wondering—if you're not busy—whether you'd like to get dinner sometime?"
