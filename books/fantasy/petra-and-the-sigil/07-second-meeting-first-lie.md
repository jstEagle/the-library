## 7. Second Meeting, First Lie

I did not sleep.

I want to be honest about that up front, because everything I did the next day was built on top of a foundation of no sleep and cold tea, and I would like it noted in the record—my record, the one I keep in my own head—that I at least *tried* to be rational about the whole thing.

I tried. That counts for something.

The problem with trying to be rational at three in the morning is that you have only your own mind for company, and my mind kept returning to the same page like a tongue to a broken tooth. *The stranger orders tea she does not drink. The binder's hands shake. Rain falls on the lane but not past the ridge.* Word for word. Word for word. I had held the book under three different lamps. I had checked the date twice against two calendars, because Hollowmere keeps the founders' calendar in the town hall and an ordinary one in the bakery window, and they disagree by eleven days, and I prayed—I, who had not prayed since exile—for the discrepancy to save me.

It didn't. Either calendar, the evening matched.

So when morning came, gray and ordinary over the rooftops of Lower Row, I did what any sensible woman would do. I opened the shop late, burned the toast, and told myself I would simply never see Petra again.

She was at my counter by half past nine.

* * *

"You're out of the willow-bark tea," she said, "so I brought you some."

She set a small paper packet on the counter between us, next to the awl I'd been pretending to reposition for ten minutes. She looked rested and bright-eyed and entirely unhaunted, her hair pinned up with what appeared to be a pencil, which should have been ridiculous and instead made my chest do something complicated.

"I didn't tell you where I lived," I said. "Or worked. Or anything."

"You said 'the bookbindery off Cooper's Lane, the one with the crooked sign.'" She tilted her head. "There's exactly one bookbindery off Cooper's Lane, and its sign is crooked in a way I can only describe as architectural."

The sign had been crooked since before my exile. It would be crooked after I died. It felt suddenly, absurdly important to defend it.

"The sign is load-bearing," I said.

"Obviously." She smiled, and I watched myself smile back, and somewhere beneath both smiles the prophecy sat in my chest like a swallowed stone. *The binder laughs at nothing. The stranger stays until closing.*

I had laughed at nothing yesterday. Twice.

"Thank you for the tea," I said, and slid the packet toward me, and hated how carefully I moved, as though it might be trapped or spelled or watching. It was tea. It was just tea. Willow-bark, which I drank for headaches, which I had been having all night, which she could not possibly have known—

Unless she could. Unless the dreams went both ways. Unless this whole valley was a stage and we were both reading from the same script without knowing who held the master copy.

"Lior?" Petra said. "You've gone somewhere."

"I'm deciding whether to trust you," I said, before my brain could stop my mouth.

To her credit—and I would spend the rest of this story cataloguing her credits, so let me start early—she didn't flinch. She leaned her elbows on the counter, which violated approximately four of my workshop rules, and said, "And? What's the verdict?"

"Pending," I said. "There's a review process. Forms. A small committee."

"Of one."

"The committee values its independence."

That got a laugh out of her, warm and real, and I felt the stone shift in my chest, because here was the trap laid bare: every true thing she said fit the page. Every joke, every kindness, every crooked-sign observation was another line confirmed. I couldn't talk to her without testing her anymore. I couldn't test her without lying to her. And underneath it all ran the question I couldn't ask: *do you know what you are?*

Do you know what you're here to wake?

* * *

I invented work for her.

This is the part I'm least proud of, so I'll narrate it briskly, like pulling a splinter. There was a stack of damaged volumes from the temple annex—water-damaged hymnals, mostly, their spines gone soft—and I set Petra to pressing pages while I "supervised," which meant I stood close enough to watch her hands and far enough away to feel like a coward about it.

Because here was my theory, hatched somewhere around dawn: if the prophecy had recorded our first meeting word for word, then perhaps it had recorded other things. Details. And if I fed her details—names, dates, objects—and she reacted as though she already knew them, then she wasn't a stranger at all. She was sent. Placed. A key fitted to a lock by someone with a century's head start.

"So," I said, casually, handing her a hymnal with a detached cover. "Where are you staying?"

"The inn on Mill Street. The one with the geese."

"There are no geese on Mill Street."

"There are absolutely geese on Mill Street," she said. "Three of them. They have opinions about me. One hissed at my luggage."

I stopped. Because there *were* geese on Mill Street—the widow Harsany kept them, and they terrorized deliveries, and everyone in Hollowmere had a goose story—but I hadn't mentioned them. Had I? I turned back through the previous evening in my memory, that lantern-lit walk, hunting for the moment I might have said something about geese, and found nothing, and felt the floor tilt a degree.

"And your name," I pressed on, because the committee required documentation. "Petra. Just Petra? No family name?"

"Just Petra." Something flickered across her face—quick, gone before I could name it. "My mother thought surnames were cages. She gave us each one name and told us we could earn the rest."

"That's either very wise or very cruel."

"She was both. Often on the same day." Petra smoothed a warped page flat with the heel of her hand, gentle, practiced. "You're interrogating me, by the way."

"I'm making conversation."

"You're making conversation the way a customs officer makes small talk." She looked up at me, and her eyes were steady and amused and, worst of all, kind. "Ask me whatever you actually want to ask. I'd rather answer the real question badly than the fake one well."

And there it was—the exact shape of the trap, offered to me openly. Because the real question was *did you come here on purpose, do you know what sleeps in you, does the book know your name*, and I could ask none of it without confessing the book, and I could not confess the book without confessing that I'd read our kiss in ink dried a hundred years before either of us was born, and I could not confess *that* without watching the light go out of her face.

So I lied. First lie, clean and small, the way all the worst ones start.

"I wanted to know if you'd stay in Hollowmere long," I said. "For the repair work. It's slow going, and I could use steady hands."

Something in her expression eased—or dimmed; I genuinely couldn't tell, and the not-telling has haunted me since. "Steady hands," she repeated, holding them up, palms out. Ink-stained already, an hour into the job. "I'm yours for as long as you'll have me."

*As long as you'll have me.* I checked it against the page in my memory. Not there. Not there, thank every indifferent god—not there—and I exhaled around the relief and immediately despised myself for the relief, because what kind of person hears *as long as you'll have me* and checks it against a death warrant?

Me. Apparently. I was that kind of person now.

* * *

At midday, Tam Voss came in for his quarterly Bible rebinding and stayed an hour too long, because Tam Voss has never met an hour he didn't want to keep. He's the ferryman's son, sixty if he's a day, built like a barrel with a beard attached, and he regards my shop as his personal parliament.

He squinted at Petra over the counter. "Don't know you."

"Petra. I'm new."

"Nobody's new." He said it comfortably, the way you'd say *the sun comes up*. "Been forty years since anybody was new in Hollowmere. Before my time, even. My da talked about it. Said the last new folk came up the valley road in autumn and left again by spring, quick as weather."

"What happened to them?" Petra asked, pleasant, curious.

Tam shrugged. "Left. Valley doesn't hold strangers. Never has." He tapped the counter with one thick finger, and I felt the tap land somewhere behind my ribs. "Valley holds what it holds, mind. Lets go of what it doesn't."

"Well," Petra said lightly, "I'm very holdable."

I choked on my tea. Tam looked deeply satisfied, as though he'd personally arranged the joke, and took his Bible and his leave, pausing at the door to give me a look I chose not to interpret.

When he was gone, Petra said, "Your friend thinks I won't last the season."

"Tam thinks a lot of things. He thinks the mayor is a ghost. He thinks rain is a government conspiracy."

"Is it?"

"No," I said, and then heard myself, and laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, thin at the edges. Because rain *was*, in a sense, a matter of governance in Hollowmere—it just wasn't *our* government. The ridge took the storms. The founders' seal took the magic. Everything else was weather's leftovers, drizzle and mist, the crumbs off a richer table.

Petra was watching me with that look again—the one that saw too much. "You do that," she said.

"Do what?"

"Go somewhere. Mid-sentence. Like a door opening onto a dark room and shutting again before anyone sees inside." She said it without accusation, almost tenderly. "I'm not going to pry. I just want you to know I notice. People pretend not to notice things about each other, and I think it's lonelier that way."

*I notice,* the page had said. *The stranger notices what the valley forgot.*

I had read that line six times and assumed it referred to the magic, the seal, the dead years. Standing there with flour dust on my apron and a lie dissolving on my tongue, I understood it meant something smaller and much worse. She noticed *me*. The valley had forgotten Lior Iversen—had been *made* to forget her—and this woman had arrived and noticed within a day.

Every true thing tightened the trap. Every kind thing did worse.

* * *

In the afternoon I committed further crimes against my own conscience, and I'll list them quickly: I asked her birthday (she gave it; not on the page); I asked how she learned bookwork (an uncle in a river town, "he smelled of glue and disapproval"); I asked, watching her face in the good afternoon light, whether she dreamed much.

"All the time," she said. "Bad ones, lately. Big ones." She frowned at the hymnal as if it had asked. "Cities burning. Towers with crowns on them. Weather doing things weather shouldn't." A pause. "A woman standing in the middle of it, calm as Sunday."

My hands went still on the press.

"What does she look like, this woman?" I asked. My voice came out level. I have never been prouder of my voice.

"Haven't seen her face." Petra shook her head, rueful. "Isn't that always the way? The dream spends an hour on the towers and skimps on the casting." She glanced up. "Why? Do you dream?"

"Everyone dreams."

"Not like a question, though. You flinched."

"I flinched at the glue. It's fresh." I held up the pot as evidence. She let it go, visibly filing it away, and I understood with cold clarity that she was keeping a ledger too. Everyone in this story was keeping ledgers. Perhaps that was what prophecy actually was: someone's ledger, written down ahead of time, waiting for us to catch up to it.

Near closing, she stretched—ink to the second knuckle, a smear of paste on her jaw she didn't know about—and said, "Same time tomorrow?"

"It's a workshop, not an appointment."

"It's an appointment," she said, "if you say yes."

I said yes. Of course I said yes. The committee voted unanimously and hated itself in the morning.

* * *

After she left, I lit the lamp over the workbench and took out the prophecy book, because I am apparently incapable of learning.

I read the day's entry again. Most of it I had already matched: the tea, the laughter, the questions. But there was a line near the end I had skimmed past in my first horrified reading, and now it rose up off the page like a hand out of dark water.

*On the second day the binder tests her, and the stranger answers true, and knows not why she answers true. And the binder says nothing, and the silence is the first stone of the wall between them.*

I put the book down. I picked it up. I read it again, hoping the words would rearrange themselves into something survivable.

They didn't. The book was patient. Books usually are; it's people who rush.

*Answers true, and knows not why.* So she wasn't acting. She wasn't sent, or if she was sent, she didn't know the errand. The geese, the name, the noticing—all of it true because it was simply *her*, and the page had merely gotten there first. Which meant the prophecy wasn't a script anyone was following.

It was a script we were doomed to perform without rehearsal, and the only person who knew the lines was me.

And the wall—the wall was mine. I was building it right now, brick by brick, with every question I dressed up as small talk and every answer I swallowed. The prophecy didn't demand the wall. It just knew me well enough to predict it.

I sat in the lamplight for a long time, listening to the shop settle, feeling the scar on my palm ache its low dull ache—the ache it always carried, faint as a memory of weather. Then, because the day demanded one more cruelty, I turned the page.

The next entry began: *On the third night, the warden comes to the bindery door...*

And below it, in the same century-old hand, a single word I had not yet earned:

*Maren.*

Someone knocked.

Two soft knocks, apologetic, at half past midnight, when nobody in Hollowmere knocked on anything but their own doors. I blew out the lamp on instinct, heart hammering, the book shut beneath my palm like a caught animal.

Through the glass, in the streetlamp's thin light, I could see the outline of a figure on my step—and the gray streak of hair I would have known anywhere in this valley, or out of it.

Warden Maren stood at my door, and she was not smiling.
