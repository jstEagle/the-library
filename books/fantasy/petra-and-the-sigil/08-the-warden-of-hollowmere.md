## 8. The Warden of Hollowmere

Lior spent three days pretending the prophecy book did not exist.

She was good at pretending. She had built a whole life out of it — the small deceptions of a small woman in a small valley: the way she never looked too long at the ridge line, the way she laughed a half-beat late at jokes so no one would notice she hadn't quite caught them, the way she kept her scarred palm curled inward like a coin she didn't want to spend. For eleven years, pretending had been a craft, and Lior had been its finest artisan.

But this was different. This was not hiding a scar or miscounting change at the market. This was carrying a book that knew her future, and a kiss that had apparently been scheduled a hundred years before either of them was born, and a name — *the Key* — that sat in her chest like a swallowed stone.

So she pretended. She opened the bindery on time. She mended a hymnal for the chapel with thread the color of weak tea. She ate bread. She said *good morning* to Old Tam at the well, who said it back, because he always did, because Hollowmere was a place where every morning was a copy of the morning before.

And on the fourth day, Warden Maren came to her door.

* * *

Maren did not knock the way other people knocked. Other people knocked to announce themselves; Maren knocked to inform you that announcing yourself had become unnecessary. Three raps, evenly spaced, and then the door opening while the echo of the third was still deciding whether to exist.

"Warden." Lior set down her bone folder with the deliberate calm of someone placing a weapon just out of reach. "I wasn't expecting you."

"No one ever is," Maren said. "That's rather the point of me."

She filled the doorway the way winter fills a window — not by force, exactly, but by making everything else seem provisional. She was sixty-something and straight-backed, silver hair pinned with what Lior had long suspected was an actual iron nail, and she wore the warden's gray coat even on days when there was nothing to ward. Especially on those days, probably. A coat like that needed wearing, or it started asking questions.

"I'm restoring a hymnal," Lior said, which was true, and therefore the best lie available.

"I know. You've been restoring it for four days. It's a thin book, Lior."

"It's a beloved book."

"Mm." Maren stepped inside without being asked, the way weather does, and stood in the middle of the shop looking at everything and nothing: the presses, the pots of paste gone skin-tight overnight, the shelf where the donated volumes waited in their patient, dusty rows. Her gaze snagged on the empty space where one book had recently been moved from the *to-do* pile to the drawer beneath the counter. It snagged the way a fishhook snags. "You've been busy."

"There's a queue."

"There's never been a queue in eleven years. I once watched a man wait three weeks for you to rebind his ledger because you said the glue needed to 'rest.' Glue, Lior. Resting." Maren turned. Her eyes were pale gray, the color of the ridge stones, and they were not unkind, which was somehow worse. "Sit down."

"I'd rather stand."

"Of course you would." Maren took the stool herself, which was such an unexpected move that Lior's whole defensive architecture tilted. Wardens did not sit in bookbinderies. Wardens loomed, issued notices, collected the tithe of quiet that Hollowmere paid instead of taxes. They did not perch on a customer's stool with their hands folded like a grandmother about to explain where babies come from.

"You've been agitated," Maren said.

"I've been working."

"You've been working *loudly*. You press too hard when something's eating you. Always have. Your paste is overworked, your stitching is crooked on the last three jobs, and yesterday you apologized to a chair." Maren paused. "The chair did not accept."

Lior folded her arms. The scarred palm pressed against her own ribs, aching faintly, the way it did when storms passed beyond the hills — except there were no storms beyond the hills today, only gray, obedient cloud. "If you're here to fine me for crooked stitching—"

"I'm here because I've been watching you for eleven years, and in all that time you have done exactly two reckless things." Maren held up fingers. "One: you took in a stray cat during the frost year and fed it fish you could not afford, and lied to me about the fish. Two: last week, you let a stranger walk you home in the rain."

The word *rain* went through Lior like a needle through vellum.

"The rain doesn't cross the ridge," Lior said. Too fast. Too flat.

"No," Maren agreed softly. "It doesn't. It hasn't in a hundred years. And yet you stood under it with that girl as though it were the most natural thing in the world, and I thought—" she exhaled through her nose, a sound almost like laughter, if laughter had been wrung dry and hung out to stiffen, "—I thought, *there it is.*"

"There *what* is?"

Maren studied her for a long moment. Outside, Hollowmere went about its copy of itself: cart wheels, the chapel bell, someone arguing cheerfully about turnips. Inside, the silence had texture, like unsanded paper.

"You want the truth," Maren said at last, "and you want it to be small. I can hear you hoping. You're hoping I'm going to say something like *the council frowns on fraternizing with outsiders*, and you'll pay a little fine, and we'll both go back to our copies of the day before." She shook her head slowly. "I can't give you that. I gave up trying to give anyone that about thirty years ago, and it made me very unpopular, and I've made my peace with being unpopular."

"Then give me the truth that isn't small."

Something flickered across Maren's face — regret, maybe, or relief, or the particular exhaustion of a person who has carried a bucket of water uphill for decades and has finally been asked why the well is at the bottom of the hill.

"All right," she said. "But not here. Walls in this town are thinner than your glue, and some of them listen."

* * *

They walked up past the chapel, past the last house with its blue shutters, up the switchback path toward the warden's station — a squat stone building that everyone in Hollowmere politely ignored, the way one politely ignores a mole. The higher they climbed, the quieter the town became behind them, until the only sounds were wind and Maren's boots and Lior's own heartbeat, which had developed opinions.

"You knew Petra was coming," Lior said suddenly. "Before she arrived. Didn't you."

Maren didn't break stride. "I knew someone was coming. There are signs, when the boundary strains. Like a door swelling in wet weather." A pause. "I didn't know it would be *her*. I hoped, frankly, it wouldn't be."

"Hoped? Why?"

"Because hope is how they get you," Maren said, and would say nothing more until they reached the station.

Inside, it smelled of lamp oil and old paper — a smell that stabbed Lior with homesickness for a life she was currently living. Shelves lined the walls, but these were not ledgers of tithes and grievances; these books were older, their spines cracked like riverbeds, their titles stamped in an alphabet Lior half-recognized from the margins of the prophecy book. Her stomach dropped through the floor and kept dropping.

Maren lit two lamps, closed the shutters, and drew a curtain across the inner doorway with the ceremony of a woman closing a coffin.

"Eleven years ago," Maren began, "you were sentenced to exile in Hollowmere. That is what you were told. That is what the records say. That is what *I* was told, when I was appointed warden, along with instructions that seemed strange at the time and seem considerably less strange now."

"Instructions."

"That you were to be left alone. Not watched — *left alone*. No reports required. No restrictions beyond the ordinary. A sentence of exile, administered by nobody, enforced by nobody, in the one place in the world where the crime you committed was impossible to commit twice." Maren's mouth twisted. "Do you see the shape of it yet? Exile is a punishment. What they gave you was a *shelf*. A safe, dark, quiet shelf, where nothing could reach you and you could reach nothing."

Lior's arms had wrapped around herself without permission. "That's not — I remember the trial. I remember the storm. I remember—"

"Do you?" Maren asked gently. "Truly? Close your eyes and show me the courtroom."

And Lior tried, and the memory came — the great hall, the judges' faces, the thunder still ringing in her ears, her mother crying — but when she reached for the edges of it, the edges weren't edges. They were soft. Blurred. Like a page that had been handled too many times, ink worn to suggestion. She remembered the storm-calling the way you remember a story someone told you about yourself.

"What did they do to me?" Her voice came out smaller than she wanted.

"They took the sharp parts off the memory. Enough that you'd stop asking questions. Not enough that you'd stop *aching*." Maren nodded at Lior's right hand, curled against her ribs. "That palm of yours. It aches when storms pass beyond the hills, yes? Even storms a hundred miles out, even storms the valley never sees. Because the seal doesn't stop the world from happening, Lior. It stops the world from happening *here*. You're standing on the far side of a wall, listening to a party you were thrown out of."

"The founders," Lior whispered. Every child in Hollowmere learned the word the way children learn the names of saints — the Nine Founders, who raised the ridge and sealed the valley safe from the war of the storm-crowned houses. Safe. Quiet. Magic-dead, and proud of it.

"The founders sealed magic out of Hollowmere a century ago," Maren said. "Every schoolchild knows it was to end the war. To starve the storm-crowned houses of a battleground." She leaned forward, lamplight pooling in the lines of her face. "Here is what the schoolbooks leave out. A seal like that doesn't run on stone and good intentions. It runs on *hunger*. It starves magic — all magic, everywhere within the boundary — and it feeds the difference into itself. And there was exactly one thing alive that the seal needed most to starve."

The room tilted. Or perhaps Lior did.

"No," she said.

"You were eight years old, and you called lightning out of a clear sky, and the founders did not send you to prison, because you cannot imprison a key. You *shelve* it." Maren's voice was low and steady, the voice of someone reciting facts she had hated for thirty years. "They altered your trial. They altered your memory. They put you on a shelf in the one valley in the world where your nature would go unfed — and they told everyone, including me, including *you*, that it was justice."

Lior heard herself laughing. It was a horrible sound, thin and automatic, like a hinge. "That's — no. I'm a bookbinder. I burn the porridge. I can't—"

"You called a storm at eight years old that the houses fought a *war* over," Maren said. "What do you imagine you'd be now, if anyone had ever fed you?"

The silence after that was enormous. Lior became aware, distantly, that she was shaking. That her scarred palm had gone from ache to burn, as though the hand itself understood the conversation before the rest of her did. Eleven years. Eleven years of careful, small, invisible living — the late laughs, the resting glue, the coin of a scar kept hidden — and none of it had been hers. Not the punishment, not the mercy, not even the forgetting. She had been a book, shelved by people who feared what was written in her.

"They're still watching," she managed. "Aren't they. The founders. Someone is."

"The dead don't watch," Maren said. "But they *wait*. And lately—" she glanced at the shuttered window, at the gray obedient sky, "—lately the waiting has changed pitch. The seal is straining. It strained when the girl crossed the ridge, and it has been straining since, and I do not think it is a coincidence that the strain began the week she kissed you under rain that should not exist."

Lior's breath stopped. "You saw that."

"I see everything in this valley. It's the job." Maren rose, and for a moment her sternness cracked along an old fault line, and what showed underneath was tiredness, and something that might, in a better-lit room, have been kindness. "I am telling you this because you deserve to hear it from a human mouth before you hear it from any other kind. And I am telling you this because you are going to make choices in the next few days, and I would rather you made them knowing the shape of the shelf you've been sitting on."

"What choices?"

Maren looked at her for a long moment. Then she crossed to the oldest shelf, ran her finger along the spines like a woman choosing a weapon, and stopped.

"The founders left instructions," she said quietly. "Sealed orders, for the warden, in the event the Sigil-bearer entered the valley. I have read them. I have known since the day she arrived what those orders require." Her hand rested on a spine black with age. "And I am about to decide whether to obey them."

Outside, far beyond the hills, thunder rolled — a storm the valley would never feel, striking somewhere free.

Lior's palm burned like a struck match.

"*What* do they require?" she asked.

Maren drew the black book from the shelf, and did not answer.
