## 9. Dreams of the Storm-Crown

Petra had always been a generous sleeper. Her mother used to say she could doze through a festival parade, and once had—face-down in a flower bed at the Midsummer Fair, drooling into the marigolds while the whole town danced around her. Sleep came to her easily, warmly, like an old friend who never overstayed.

So it was deeply unfair that the dreams had chosen her.

They started small. A smell of ozone that wasn't in her room. The sensation of standing somewhere high, though her boarding-house bed sat firmly on the ground floor of Mrs. Tallow's establishment, above the bakery, where the only heights were the bread racks. In the dreams she stood on stone that hummed underfoot, and something vast moved behind her eyes—not a thought, not quite, but the *shape* of one, too large to fit.

Then came the crowns.

The first time she saw them, she thought they were hats. That was the sort of person Petra was: she looked at a burning halo of storm-light circling a warlord's brow and thought, *that's a bold hat.* It took three nights of dreaming before she understood what she was looking at, and even then part of her insisted on calling them hats, because if they were hats, then this was just a strange play, and plays ended, and you got to go home afterward.

There was no home to go back to in these dreams.

* * *

On the fourth night, she dreamed the war properly.

She stood on a ridge—the ridge, some part of her knew, though she'd never seen Hollowmere's rim except as a smudge of hills from the valley floor—and below her two armies faced each other across a drowned plain. On one side, towers of white glass, and people before them wearing light like armor. On the other, figures crowned in storm, lightning crawling down their shoulders and pooling in their fists like water finding its level.

She could hear them shouting. Not words—she couldn't make out words—but the *argument* underneath them, old and enormous, the kind of quarrel that has forgotten its beginning and will not stop until everything is finished. It made her teeth ache. It made her chest ache worse, because some traitorous corner of her recognized the argument the way you recognize a song you've hated your whole life but know every note of.

"Those aren't hats," she said aloud, to no one, in the dream. "Those are crowns."

The nearest storm-crowned figure turned its head.

She woke with her heart slamming and the taste of rain in her mouth, though the window showed a dry, starry sky, and beyond it the ridge line that kept all weather politely outside the valley like a doorman who'd been given very firm instructions.

* * *

"You look terrible," said Mrs. Tallow the next morning, setting down a plate of rolls with the tenderness of a woman who expressed affection exclusively through baked goods and criticism. "Terrible in a romantic way, mind. Like a heroine. But still terrible."

"I'm sleeping fine," Petra lied, and reached for a roll.

"Mm." Mrs. Tallow watched her with small bright eyes over the top of her apron. "You were talking in your sleep again. Third night running."

Petra's hand stopped halfway to the butter. "What did I say?"

"That's the thing." Mrs. Tallow wiped her hands slowly, drawing it out, because she was seventy years old and had earned the right to savor a moment. "It wasn't talking so much as... declaiming. Like a speech. You said—" she cleared her throat, and gave a startlingly good impression of Petra's own voice gone low and formal—"'The crown remembers what the head forgets.'"

The kitchen went very quiet. Somewhere upstairs a floorboard creaked; the bakery's first batch ticked in the oven; the world continued being ordinary, which felt suddenly like a courtesy someone was extending to her.

"Well," Petra said. "That's embarrassing."

"Is it?" Mrs. Tallow picked up her tray. "Sounded important to me. Sounded like something a person ought to write down." She paused at the door. "My grandmother used to say this valley keeps things out for a reason. Weather. Magic. Memory. She said the founders didn't build a wall against enemies." The old woman's eyes flicked to Petra, quick and unreadable. "They built it against *answers*. Eat your roll."

Petra ate her roll. It was excellent. She tasted almost none of it.

* * *

That evening she went to see Lior.

She told herself it was for ordinary reasons. She had a book that needed mending—a slim volume of valley folk-songs she'd bought mostly as an excuse—and Lior Iversen was the bookbinder, and visiting a bookbinder about a book was the most defensible activity in the world. Nobody could read anything into it. Nobody could accuse her of simply wanting to stand in the warm paper-and-glue smell of that little shop and watch Lior's hands move, precise and careful, doing the one thing in the world she seemed to trust herself to do.

Lior trusted her hands. Petra had noticed that early. Everything else about the woman was guarded—her smiles arrived a half-second late, like messengers checking for ambush—but her hands were honest. When she worked, she forgot to be careful.

"Your songbook," Lior said when Petra came in, holding it up. "Good news and bad news."

"Oh no."

"The good news is the binding's salvageable. The bad news is someone in 1841 appears to have used page forty as a coaster." She turned the book around to show a brown ring stamped through a stave of music. "Either that or they were drinking something that left rings shaped like accusations."

"What song is it?"

Lior squinted at the ghost of notation beneath the stain. "'The Ballad of the Nine Founders.' Which, given the state of the page, feels thematically appropriate. The founders have been getting stained by other people's messes for a century."

Petra laughed, and meant it, and watched Lior's mouth twitch in answer—that half-second delay again, the smile arriving like it had checked the exits first. Something in her chest pulled tight. She was lying to this woman by omission, by accumulation, by the simple fact of being here at all, and every laugh they shared made the lie heavier and more worth carrying.

"I've been having strange dreams," Petra heard herself say.

The words escaped before she'd vetted them. She watched Lior go still—not dramatically, not obviously, but the way a deer goes still, which is to say completely and with total attention.

"Dreams," Lior repeated.

"Everyone has them. Mine are just..." She turned her hands over, studied her palms as if the explanation might be written there. "Vivid. There's a war in them. Two sides. People wearing crowns made of storms." She laughed, lightly, testing. "Which I realize sounds mad."

"It doesn't sound mad," Lior said quietly.

And there was something in the way she said it—something that wasn't surprise. Petra had spent enough of her life reading faces to know the difference between *I believe you* and *I expected this*, and Lior Iversen's face, for one unguarded instant, was the second thing.

Then it closed, gentle and polite as a shop door.

"Valley folk take dreams seriously," Lior said. "Old habit. When you live somewhere magic can't reach, dreams are the only weather that gets in." She smiled, and began wrapping the mended songbook in brown paper, and the conversation moved on to safer ground—ink prices, the quality of Hollowmere thread, whether Mrs. Tallow's sourdough was genuinely miraculous or merely well-marketed.

But Petra walked home under the dry stars turning one sentence over and over: *it doesn't sound mad.*

Not *you're not mad.* Not *dreams are just dreams.*

*I expected this.*

* * *

The fifth night, the dream put her in the middle of it.

No more ridge. No more safe distance. She stood on the drowned plain between the armies, and the water was ankle-deep and warm as blood, and both sides had stopped fighting to look at her.

That was the worst part. Not the crowns, not the lightning pooling in white-knuckled fists, not the glass towers leaning like held breath. The *looking*. Hundreds of faces turning toward her with the same expression—hope, hunger, recognition—and Petra understood, with the sick clarity dreams grant, that she was not a spectator here. She had never been a spectator. She was the reason they'd come.

"She's waking," said a voice beside her, and she turned and found a figure in storm-crown whose face was—

*Lior's.*

Not quite. That was the horror of it. The face was Lior's face worn slightly wrong, like a coat buttoned askew—older around the eyes, lit from within by blue-white light, and absolutely certain of itself in a way the real Lior never was. This Lior raised one scarred palm toward the sky, and the sky answered. Lightning came down like a curtain coming down, and the whole plain lit white, and Petra heard herself screaming though her mouth never opened—

"Wake up," said the real Lior, shaking her shoulder. "Petra. Hey. Wake up."

Petra surfaced gasping into lamplight. She was in the bookbindery's back room—she'd fallen asleep on the workbench, she realized dimly, she'd come by after closing to return a borrowed awl and Lior had made tea and she'd sat down *for a moment*—and Lior was crouched beside her, real and rumpled and human, her hand warm on Petra's arm.

"You were crying out," Lior said. "Talking, too. You said—" she hesitated. "You said 'not my name. Don't give me their name.'"

Petra's whole body was trembling. "How long was I asleep?"

"An hour, maybe. You looked so peaceful I didn't want to wake you." A pause. "Right up until you started reciting things in a voice that wasn't yours."

"There's a voice?"

"There's a voice." Lior fetched her the tea, cold now, and Petra drank it anyway. Her hands shook around the cup. And it was then, lifting her arms, that she felt it—a pull at her collarbone, a tightness like a sunburn she hadn't earned.

"Lior," she said. "Can you—there's something on my—"

She couldn't reach it properly. Lior set down the teapot, leaned close, and tugged the neck of Petra's shirt aside with two fingers, gentle as handling a first edition.

The silence that followed lasted four full seconds. Petra counted. Counting was the only thing keeping her from screaming.

"What is it?" she asked finally.

"I don't know," Lior said, and her voice had gone carefully flat, the voice of someone standing very still on ice. "It looks like ink. Or a birthmark. Except it wasn't there yesterday, and birthmarks don't..."

"Don't what?"

"Don't *grow*." Lior straightened. In the lamp's glow her face was pale. "It's a spiral. Small. Right at the base of your throat. Like a—"

"Like a sigil," Petra said.

The word came out of her in a voice that wasn't entirely hers either—lower, older, resonant as a struck bell—and the lamp flame bent sideways though no door had opened. Across the room, every sheet of blank paper in Lior's stock rustled at once, like a crowd inhaling.

Lior took a step back. Not away from Petra—Petra would understand that later, would cling to that distinction like a plank in a flood—but away from the thing speaking through the space between them.

Outside, far beyond the ridge line, thunder rolled. It should not have been audible. Nothing beyond the ridge was ever audible.

Both of them heard it anyway.

"We need to talk," Lior said. "Tomorrow. About the dreams. About all of it." Her eyes dropped once more to the mark at Petra's throat, and whatever she saw there made her jaw tighten. "And Petra—whatever happens tomorrow, whatever anyone tells you that you are—"

"Yes?"

"Just don't let them finish the sentence."

* * *

That night Petra did not sleep generously. She barely slept at all, and when she did, the dream was waiting, patient as a creditor. But this time she stood her ground on the drowned plain, ankle-deep in warm water, and shouted at the armies, at the crowns, at the wrong-Lior with her raised scarred hand:

"I'm not yours! Nobody chose me! I chose *me!*"

The armies did not answer. They only turned their faces toward her—all those hundreds of faces—and smiled with the same patient, terrible certainty, the smile of people who have read the last page of a book and are content to wait for everyone else to catch up.

She woke at dawn with the spiral grown another inch, curling now toward her heart like a vine that knew exactly where it was going.

And on her windowsill, though the window had been latched all night, lay a single sheet of paper, blank except for five words in handwriting she almost recognized:

*The Key is already here.*
