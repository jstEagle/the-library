## 10. Confessions in the Bookbindery

Lior had rehearsed the confession eleven times on the walk from the archive to the bookbindery, and by the twelfth attempt she had settled on a version that began with "I found something interesting" and ended, hopefully, before anything terrible happened.

It was a coward's script. She knew it was a coward's script. But her hands were steady as she unlocked the shop, and steadiness was its own kind of lie she'd been telling for nine years.

The bell above the door chimed at half past four—Petra was punctual in a way that still startled Lior, who had spent nearly a decade among valley folk for whom "tomorrow" meant sometime before winter. Petra came in shaking rain off her shoulders, cheeks pink from the cold, carrying a paper bag that smelled of cinnamon.

"I brought bribes," Petra said, holding up the bag. "In case you decide my book is beyond saving."

"Nothing is beyond saving." Lior took the bag anyway. "That's practically the shop's motto. I should paint it over the door."

"You should. Right under 'Dravenmoor Bindery'—" Petra stopped. Her eyes had landed on the worktable, where Lior had left it out. Deliberately. The prophecy book lay open under a linen cloth, and Lior had spent an hour arranging the cloth so that it looked casually thrown rather than carefully staged, which had somehow taken longer than restoring the binding itself.

Petra went very still, the way people do when they sense weather coming.

"Lior," she said slowly, "what is that?"

* * *

Petra had known something was wrong since morning.

She'd woken in her rented room above the chandler's with the taste of iron in her mouth and the dream already fading—the valley again, always the valley now, but this time there had been a table in it, and a book, and Lior standing behind the table with an expression Petra recognized because she'd seen it last night across the teahouse: the look of someone holding a door shut against something strong.

So when she walked into the bindery and saw the linen cloth and Lior's face doing exactly that same thing, Petra felt almost relieved. At least now she could stop pretending she hadn't noticed.

"What is that?" she asked, and watched Lior's hand hover over the cloth like a person deciding whether to pet a strange dog.

"It's a donation," Lior said. "Came in last week. From the Halloran estate—they're clearing out the old house on Tarn Row." A pause. "I've been restoring it."

"And?"

"And." Lior pulled the cloth back.

The book underneath was old—old the way things in Hollowmere were old, which was to say older than any story anyone told about them. Its cover was grey leather gone soft as cloth, and its pages were the color of weak tea. Petra stepped closer despite every instinct screaming at her not to, and read the page Lior had opened to.

She read it twice. Then a third time, because the first two readings had apparently failed to make the words behave.

*"On the evening of the ninth of Frostmoon,"* the page said, in ink that had dried a century before Petra was born, *"the Sigil-bearer shall take tea at the Lamplight House with the Key, and they shall speak of books, and the Key shall laugh at a joke about the chandler's dog, and rain shall fall upon the town though it cannot cross the ridge, and beneath that rain the two shall kiss, and thus shall the wheel begin to turn."*

The ninth of Frostmoon was yesterday.

The Lamplight House was the teahouse where they'd had dinner. The joke about the chandler's dog had been Petra's joke. It hadn't even been a good joke.

"Okay," Petra said, and her voice came out steadier than she deserved. "So either you wrote this and you are a truly committed prankster, or—"

"I didn't write it." Lior's hands were flat on the table, braced. "I checked the ink. I checked the paper. I did everything short of asking it nicely to be fake. It's a hundred years old, Petra. Every word."

"The rain," Petra said. "It rained last night. On the town. And I've been here three weeks and it has never once rained on the town."

"No," Lior said quietly. "It hasn't."

The cinnamon rolls sat between them on the table, going cold, absurdly domestic next to a book that had apparently been taking notes on their lives since before their grandparents met. Petra laughed—a small, cracked sound—and immediately wished she hadn't, because Lior flinched.

"Sorry," Petra said. "Sorry. It's just—I had a whole speech prepared. For what I was going to tell you today. And now it feels very small."

* * *

Lior looked up sharply. "Your speech?"

Petra set down the paper bag. Unfolded it. Refolded it. Lior recognized the gesture—it was what people did with their hands when their mouths weren't ready.

"I didn't come to Hollowmere by accident," Petra said.

The words landed in the quiet shop like a dropped stitch. Lior heard herself say, "All right," in the calm voice she used with clients whose heirloom Bibles had water damage, the voice that said *whatever you tell me, I have seen worse*, even when she hadn't.

"I've been dreaming about this valley since I was nineteen," Petra said. "Eight years. Same dream, more or less. A valley shaped like a cup. Hills all around like cupped hands. A town with one crooked clocktower. And a woman with dark hair and a scar across her palm, standing in a doorway full of paper and glue." She smiled, and it wobbled. "I thought I was losing my mind. I drew the valley so many times my sister hid my pencils. And then two years ago I found a map in a secondhand shop in Corvane—a surveyor's map, sixty years old—and there it was. Cupped hands. Crooked tower. Hollowmere."

"So you came looking for me."

"I came looking for the valley," Petra said. "And then I walked into your shop to ask about a repair, and you looked up, and you had flour on your nose—" 

"That was paste."

"—and the scar on your hand, and I thought, *oh no.* Because I'd hoped, obviously, that I'd find you, that's why I stayed in Corvane six months working up the nerve. But hoping a thing and having it stand in front of you with paste on its face are very different animals." Petra exhaled. "I knew you were in the dream, Lior. Before I ever saw you. That's not nothing. That's not innocent. So yes—I sought you out. I just never figured out how to say it without sounding like either a lunatic or a stalker, and I kept waiting for a good moment, and then we were kissing in the rain and it seemed rude."

Despite everything—the book, the dread sitting on Lior's chest like a cat that had decided to stay—she laughed. It escaped before she could catch it.

"I'm glad my suffering amuses you," Petra said, wounded.

"It doesn't. It's just—" Lior pressed her knuckles to her mouth. "We've both been keeping the same kind of secret. You dreamed me. And I—" She turned the book around so the next page faced Petra. "Read this."

* * *

Petra read.

The handwriting changed on the second page—same century, different hand, as if the book had been written by committee, which somehow made it worse. The new hand was cramped and urgent:

*"The Sigil-bearer shall wake, and her waking shall be as a lamp lit in a house long dark, and the storm-crowned houses shall see it across the mountains, and they shall come. And the founders of the valley, who built the silence to keep the war buried, shall demand the old price: that the Key turn against the bearer, and end her before she ends the peace. For the Key alone can unmake what the bearer becomes; and if the Key refuses, then the valley burns, and the burning will be laid at the Key's door."*

Petra read it aloud, because reading silently suddenly seemed too intimate, and hearing it in her own voice made it real in a way she hated. When she finished, the shop was so quiet she could hear the paste pot settling.

"'The Key,'" she said. "That's you?"

"My palm," Lior said. She held up her right hand. In the lamplight the scar showed pale and old, a thin crescent across the heel of her hand. "I've had it since I was seven. Nobody remembers how. There was an accident, supposedly. Except—" Her jaw worked. "Except I've been reading ahead. There are pages further in. Diagrams. Of a sigil, a mark, and the diagrams show it being *opened*, and every diagram shows a key-shaped scar touching it. Mine matches. Exactly."

Petra looked at the scar. At the book. At Lior, whose composure was magnificent and whose eyes were terrified.

"So the book says I'm going to wake up as some kind of weapon," Petra said slowly, "and you're supposed to kill me before I do."

"Or the valley burns."

"Those are the options? Those are the only two?" Petra heard her own voice rise and made an effort to bring it back down. "A hundred years ago somebody wrote down our dinner date to the joke, and the best future they could offer us is murder?"

"There might be other pages," Lior said. "The book's damaged. Water damage, actually, which is ironic enough that I'd appreciate it if the universe would stop being clever for five minutes. Some leaves are fused together. I might be able to separate them. I might not."

"And if you can't?"

"Then we know how the story starts," Lior said, "and not how it ends."

Petra turned back to the first page. Their evening, rendered in someone else's ink: the tea, the books, the chandler's dog, the impossible rain, the kiss. Whoever had written it had gotten every detail right, and yet—

"They got one thing wrong," Petra said.

Lior looked up.

"The book says 'thus shall the wheel begin to turn.' Like it's machinery. Like we're cogs." Petra touched the page, lightly, the way she'd touch a client's fragile folio. "But I chose to come here. Eight years of dreams, and I still bought the ticket myself. And you—" she nodded at Lior's hand, the scar, whatever history lived inside it—"you didn't choose your part, fine. But you choose what you do next. Books don't get to decide that. Books just sit there." She managed a smile. "Believe me. I've met a lot of books."

"You're giving a pep talk to a century-old manuscript."

"Somebody has to. It's been very smug."

And Lior laughed again—really laughed, helpless, the sound bouncing off the presses and the paper stock—and for one bright moment the shop was just a shop, and they were just two women arguing with a book, and the dread receded like a tide.

Then Petra's collarbone itched.

She'd been feeling it all day—a warmth under the skin, low and persistent, like sunburn from a sun that wasn't out. She'd told herself it was nothing. She reached up now, absently, to scratch it, and her fingers found raised skin where smooth skin had been that morning.

She stopped laughing.

"Lior," she said. "When you restored the cover. Did you notice any marks on the leather? Any impressions?"

"Impressions? No, why—"

"Show me your scar again. The exact shape."

Lior extended her hand, puzzled. Petra took it, turned it palm-up, and laid her own fingers against the crescent—and the warmth at her collarbone flared, sudden and bright as a struck match, and through the window behind them the grey afternoon light stuttered, once, like a lamp about to fail.

Outside, far beyond the ridge, thunder rolled. It had no business rolling there. Nothing crossed the ridge.

Neither of them let go of the other's hand.

"Whatever happens next," Petra said, "we figure it out together. Not the book. Us. Deal?"

"Deal," Lior whispered—and neither of them said the thing hanging unspoken in the air between them, the thing the prophecy had already written and they had not: that *together* might mean one of them holding a knife.

Above the hills, the thunder rolled again, closer than it had any right to be.
