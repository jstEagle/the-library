## 11. The Founders' Ledger

The warden's archive sat beneath the meetinghouse like a stone heart, and like most hearts it was best approached from behind.

Lior had spent two days learning its rhythms. Maren kept the front door locked with a key she wore on a cord at her throat—no help there—but the building was old, and old buildings in Hollowmere had been built by people who assumed magic would always be there to guard them. Why reinforce a cellar wall when no thief in living memory could so much as light a candle with a thought? The founders' confidence in their own ban was, Lior reflected, the single most useful thing they'd ever done for her.

She went in through the coal chute.

It was not dignified. She was thirty-one years old, a respected tradeswoman, and she was wedged shoulder-deep in a chute that smelled of a century of hearth ash, wearing her second-best coat and a lantern strapped to her chest like an absurd brass pendant. A spider had opinions about this and expressed them on her neck. Lior breathed slowly, thought of Petra's laugh—the real one, the startled one, not the one Petra wore like a borrowed coat—and dropped the last three feet onto a floor of packed earth.

* * *

The archive itself was smaller than she'd imagined. She had expected grandeur: iron shelves, chained tomes, the weight of a century of governance. Instead it was a low room of whitewashed stone, dry as a sermon, lined with plain cedar cabinets and one long table scarred by candle wax. Whoever the founders had been, they had not trusted their own legacy to splendor. They had trusted it to tedium.

Lior understood tedium. Tedium was bookbinding. You defeated time not with heroics but with paste and patience.

She set her lantern on the table and began at the nearest cabinet, working methodically. Land grants. Water rights. Minutes of the valley council, decades of them, arguing about drainage ditches with a passion that would have been funny if her hands weren't shaking. She read faster. Somewhere above her, the town moved through its evening—cart wheels, a dog, the bell marking the hour—and every sound made her flinch toward the coal chute.

*Exile,* she reminded herself. *You were exiled. Whatever you find here, it can't be worse than what they already took.*

That was a lie, and some part of her knew it while another part insisted on believing it, because that was how she had survived eleven years in Hollowmere: by carefully curating which lies she let herself keep.

* * *

The third cabinet stuck. She pulled; it groaned; somewhere dust sifted down like patient snow. Inside were ledgers bound in grey leather, unmarked except for dates stamped in gold along the spines—dates going back further than Hollowmere's founding, further than the war, back to the storm-crowned houses themselves.

The oldest ledger opened to a page written in a hand so fine it looked typeset.

*Subject: the child Iversen. Age six. Assessment of aptitude.*

Lior's breath went strange in her chest.

*The child demonstrates the full inheritance. Storm-call confirmed under controlled conditions—see attached testimony of the midwife Halden. Recommendation: immediate relocation to Hollowmere under sentence of exile. Memory intervention to follow. The Key must be raised ignorant of its nature. An informed Key may choose. An ignorant Key can only be used.*

She read it again. Then again, because the words refused to arrange themselves into anything she could live inside.

*Storm-call confirmed.* At six years old. Under *controlled conditions*—which meant watched, prompted, perhaps coaxed, perhaps something less gentle than coaxing. And then: *relocation under sentence of exile*, as if her whole life since had been a filing decision. As if the trial she half-remembered—the shouting, the fear, her mother's face gone white as paper—had been theater. Staged. A play performed once, for an audience of one frightened child, so that the child would carry the guilt of it forever.

Her memory wasn't broken. It had been *edited*. Someone had reached into her mind with careful fingers and torn out the true shape of that day, leaving her a smear of storm and shame to fill the gap.

Lior sat down on the floor without deciding to. The stone was cold through her coat. She was distantly aware that she was laughing—not the good kind, the kind that comes out when the body has run out of more appropriate responses—and she pressed her scarred palm against her mouth until the laugh died against the old ache.

They hadn't punished her. They had *installed* her. Like a lock fitted to a door.

* * *

She should have stopped there. Any sensible person would have taken the ledger and climbed back up the coal chute and done—something. Screamed at Maren. Burned the meetinghouse. But rage is a fuel that burns clean and long, and Lior had eleven years of small deceptions banked behind hers, so she wiped her face with her sleeve and kept reading.

The next entries were annual. *Subject stable. Scar maintained. No anomalous weather events attributable to subject.* Annual reports on her life, filed like crop yields. There was a note from the year she turned fourteen: *Subject shows interest in bookbinding. Approve. Manual trades occupy the hands and quiet the mind.* Her apprenticeship—her beloved trade, the thing she'd built her whole self around—had been *approved*. By them. For the stated purpose of keeping her docile.

There was a note from the year she turned nineteen: *Subject attempted to leave the valley during the autumn fair. Boundary deterrence applied. Subject retains no clear memory of the attempt.* She remembered that autumn. She remembered coming home from the fair with a headache and a feeling like grief with no object, and deciding she must have drunk too much cider. She had blamed herself for wanting to leave. She had called it restlessness. Called it ingratitude.

It had been the boundary burning her back, and then someone reaching in and tidying away even her memory of trying.

"Eleven years," she said aloud, and her voice came out flat and terrible in the little room. "Eleven years of being *managed*."

The final section of the ledger was thinner—a handful of pages, recent, in Maren's own cramped hand. Lior read standing up now, because sitting felt too much like surrender.

*The Sigil-bearer has entered the valley. Confirmed by dream-report and by the subject's behavior. The prophecy holds. Preparations proceed per the founders' instruction.*

And below that, in fresher ink:

*The Key remains ignorant. This is essential. If the Key learns her function before the Sigil wakes, she may refuse the turning, and the founders' design fails. All contact between subject and bearer to be monitored. Do not inform the warden's deputies. Do not inform the subject. Trust no one in this matter but myself.*

*Trust no one but myself.* So Maren stood alone in this, or nearly alone—which meant that everything Lior did next would meet no resistance but one woman's caution. It also meant Maren had known. Had watched Lior bind books and drink tea and fall, clumsily and hopelessly, in love, all while knowing exactly what the ledger said Lior was *for*.

The Key. That word again, threaded through a century of entries. *The Key must be raised ignorant. The Key may unlock or destroy. The Key turns only by consent—or by grief.* Lior stared at that last phrase until the letters blurred. Consent, or grief. Those were the two doors they'd built her life around: either she would choose to end Petra, or losing Petra would break something open in her that could be used.

Either way, the design required her to lose.

Unless she refused both doors. Unless a lock could be picked from the inside.

* * *

She copied what mattered—her hands steadier now, oddly, the way they always went calm in the middle of a difficult repair—onto loose pages from her coat pocket, folding them small. Trial staged. Memory altered. Exile arranged. Key. Consent or grief. Then she put the ledgers back exactly as she'd found them, aligned to the dust shadows on the shelf, because whatever else she was tonight, she was still a craftsman, and craftsmen leave no trace of their work.

At the bottom of the last cabinet, wedged behind a box of sealing wax, she found one more thing: a slim volume, newer than the rest, wrapped in oilcloth. Inside was a single sheet of heavy paper bearing a seal she didn't recognize—a circle crossed by seven lines, like a wheel with spokes of lightning.

Beneath the seal, a list of names. Seven names. Six were struck through, each strike dated across the last hundred years, each name followed by a brief note: *died natural.* *died crossing the ridge.* *died refusing.* *died refusing.* *died refusing.*

The seventh name was written in fresh ink, not yet struck through:

*Iversen, Lior.*

Not a sentence. Not a punishment. A *roster*. She was the seventh person in a century to be fitted to this lock, and the first six had all died—three of them, by the notes, specifically for refusing. The founders had been running this experiment for generations, feeding Keys into the mechanism and striking them off when they broke.

Lior stood very still in the cold room, holding the list of her predecessors, and felt something in her finish changing. Fear had brought her down the coal chute. Fear was climbing back up without her. What remained was quieter and much more dangerous: the particular calm of a woman who has nothing left to be careful about.

They had taken her childhood. They had taken her memory, her mother's truth, her right to leave, her right to want. They had written her into a prophecy before she could write herself into anything. Fine. She had spent eleven years learning how old things came apart—how paste softened, how stitching gave, how even the proudest binding surrendered to patient hands.

She would take their book apart too.

* * *

Climbing out through the coal chute was worse than going in; going down, at least, gravity had been sympathetic. She emerged into the alley behind the meetinghouse smelling of ash and looking, she suspected, like a burglar, which was accurate. The night air was soft. Somewhere beyond the ridge, a storm was passing—she felt it as a faint ache in her palm, the old familiar toothache of exile—and for the first time in eleven years the ache felt less like a wound and more like a reminder. *You are still in there. Whatever they edited, the storm is still yours.*

She walked home the long way, past the darkened teahouse where it had all begun, past the window of her own shop with its neat display of mended books. Mended things. That was what she was, apparently: something broken on purpose and patched over, and everyone in town had admired the repair work without ever asking what the damage had been.

Her lamp was lit when she got home. She stopped dead in the street.

Then she saw the silhouette through the curtain—dark hair, that particular way of sitting with one knee drawn up—and the terror arrived a half-second late, tripping over relief. Petra. In her house. Uninvited, at midnight, glowing softly in Lior's lamplight like something out of the wrong kind of story.

Lior stood in the street with stolen secrets folded against her ribs and ash in her hair, and understood that the next few minutes would decide everything. Because Petra was looking at her through the window now, and Petra's expression was not surprise. It was guilt—the exact same guilt Lior had been carrying for days, the guilt of someone keeping a secret shaped exactly like a confession.

Petra had come to tell her something. And Lior, with a founder's roster in her pocket and six dead names in her head, realized she had maybe one chance to decide whether they told each other the truth—or whether they spent whatever time remained performing for each other, two liars in love, exactly as designed.

She opened the door.

"You're up late," Petra said, and her voice shook, just slightly, on the words. "I—I need to show you something."

"So do I," Lior said, and stepped inside, and closed the door on the night.
