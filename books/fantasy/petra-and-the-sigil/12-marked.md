## 12. Marked

The mark had opinions about weather.

That was the first thing Petra learned, in the three days after the dreams turned from memory into instruction. When clouds gathered beyond the ridge—grey, sullen, forbidden to cross—it warmed against her collarbone like a hand pressed there. Not painfully. Almost companionably. *There you are,* it seemed to say. *I've been waiting for a sky worth talking about.*

She'd named it, privately, the way she named everything: the blot. It had started as a smudge of violet beneath her skin, small as a thumbprint, and she had told herself it was a bruise from sleeping wrong, or an ink stain transferred from her own fingers, because Petra Voss was very good at telling herself things.

She was less good at stopping the whispering.

* * *

It began on the second morning, while she was mending a fence post for Widow Hesk because Lior had asked her to and because Widow Hesk paid in plum jam, which Petra considered a fair exchange for nearly anything.

The post was rotten through. She braced her boot against it, gripped, pulled—and the wood came apart in her hands with a sound like a sigh, fibers splitting along a line so clean it might have been drawn first. She sat down hard in the mud, holding two halves of a fence post that had parted exactly where she wanted them to.

"That's not strength," she said aloud, to the goats, who regarded her with the flat indifference of their kind. "Goats don't count as witnesses."

But the warmth at her collarbone pulsed once, pleased, and somewhere behind her ears—not in them, that was the horrible part, it came from inside the skull like a thought wearing someone else's voice—a voice said: *Yes.*

Petra dropped the fence post and did not touch it again.

* * *

By evening she could hear more of them. Not constantly—they came in tides, like weather, like the ache in Lior's scarred palm must have come all those years before Hollowmere's dead air pressed it quiet. They spoke in a formal, old-fashioned cadence, the voices of people who had spent a century being very sure of themselves.

*The girl is receptive,* one said, dry as ledger paper.

*She is frightened,* said another, softer, almost kind. *Frightened things grip tightest.*

*Then we begin gently.*

They did begin gently. That was the worst discovery of the week: the founders were patient. They didn't shout instructions or seize her hands. They offered. A suggestion here, a preference there—the sense that she ought to walk past the warden's house on her way home, that she should look longer at the boundary stones, that the tea Lior made was too sweet and she should say so, why should she not say so, honesty between—

"No," Petra said, out loud, standing in the middle of the lane with her shopping basket, startling a delivery boy into dropping his apples. "Absolutely not. The tea is perfect."

The voices went quiet, in the manner of people deciding to pretend they hadn't spoken.

She picked up the apples. Two of them were hers now by right of chaos, and she ate one on the walk home out of pure spite.

* * *

The problem, she reflected that night in the little room above the inn, was that lying to Lior required a completely different set of muscles than lying to everyone else.

Petra had lied her whole life, cheerfully and mostly harmlessly. She'd lied to border clerks about her purpose of travel. She'd lied to a customs officer in Corvane about the provenance of a hat. She'd told her mother she liked the stew. Lies were how a person moved through the world without snagging on every sharp edge of it.

But Lior looked at her the way no one ever had—like Petra was a book she intended to read slowly, cover to cover, including the boring parts—and every lie Petra tried to assemble in that gaze fell apart like wet paper.

So she hid instead. Hiding was just lying with better posture.

"You're quiet tonight," Lior said, three days after the fence post. They were in the bookbindery, which had become their place the way certain benches and certain corners become places: not by decision but by accumulation. Lior was working glue into the spine of a hymnal, her scarred palm flexing around the bone folder, and lamplight made her look like a painting of domestic peace that Petra wanted to climb inside.

"Tired," Petra said, which was even true. The whispers kept a schedule now. They woke when she woke. They murmured inventory lists of the valley—gate, well, archive, ridge—as though she were expected to memorize them. "Widow Hesk worked me hard today."

"Plum jam hard?"

"Cruelly plum jam hard." She smiled, and Lior smiled back, and the warmth under Petra's collarbone pulsed with something that felt horribly like approval. *Good,* said the soft voice. *Keep her close. The Key is safest near its lock.*

Lior was the Key. Petra had understood that since the dreams showed her a woman calling lightning down onto a battlefield, a woman with a scar across her palm shaped like a keyhole, and then shown her the same woman hunched over a workbench gluing spines, and Petra had understood with total, sickening clarity that both women were real, and both were Lior, and the founders had built this whole grey valley as a box to keep the lightning in.

And they were using Petra to open it.

"I have a confession," Lior said, setting down the bone folder. Her expression had gone carefully casual, which on Lior meant deeply not-casual. "I found something in the donated collection. An old book. I haven't finished reading it, but—" She stopped. Started again. "There are parts I think you should see. When I'm ready. Which is soon. Possibly tomorrow."

Something in Petra's chest went cold and bright at once. She knew what book it was. She'd known since the dream two nights ago, in which she'd stood in a bindery exactly this one and watched herself read her own future off a page.

"The mysterious donation," Petra said lightly. "Every bookbinder's dream. Was it at least in good condition?"

"Terrible condition. Foxed throughout. Water damage." Lior's mouth quirked. "Someone loved it badly for a hundred years."

*I wrote it,* said one of the voices, unexpectedly, and Petra's teacup rattled against its saucer. She covered it by laughing at nothing.

"Clumsy," she said. "Long day."

Lior watched her a moment too long. Then, mercifully, let it go. "Tomorrow, then. Come early. I'll make the too-sweet tea."

"It's my favorite flaw of yours," Petra said, and meant it, and heard the founders murmur among themselves like committee members taking notes.

* * *

Sleep had become a negotiation.

She lay in the narrow bed listening to rain that never crossed the ridge, drumming patiently on the far side of the hills like a guest kept waiting outside a locked door, and the mark spread another finger's width toward her shoulder, warm as bathwater, and the voices held their evening session.

*The awakening requires proximity to the Key,* said the dry one.

*It requires consent,* said the soft one. *The prophecy is explicit.*

*Prophecies are drafts,* said a third voice, new, harder, and Petra got the impression of a man who had spent his life being obeyed and resented every second he wasn't. *We wrote the draft. We will write the final copy.*

*You wrote a book a hundred years ago and buried it in a bindery,* said the soft one. *Forgive me if I trust your editorial instincts.*

Petra laughed into her pillow, alone, at midnight, at the ghostly sound of immortals bickering like scholars, and realized halfway through the laugh that she was doing it to keep from screaming.

Because underneath the bickering, underneath the inventory lists and the suggestions, she could feel what they actually wanted. It leaked through the mark like water through plaster. They wanted the war again. Not won—refought. There was a difference, and the difference was everything. Winning ended a story; refighting fed it forever. They had been dead a century and they were starving for it, and they had waited all this time not for a hero or a villain but for a hinge. For a door with legs.

For her.

"Find someone else," she whispered.

*There is no one else,* said the soft voice, almost apologetic. *There was only ever going to be you. That is what being chosen means, child. It means the choosing happened long ago, by people you'll never meet, for reasons you'll never be told.*

"Well," Petra said to the ceiling, "that's the most honest thing anyone's said to me since I got here."

The mark pulsed. Outside, the rain kept knocking on a door it couldn't open.

* * *

In the morning she caught herself doing it.

She was washing her face at the basin when her hand rose—her hand, her own hand, she would insist on that detail later, clinging to it—and pressed flat against the mirror glass. And the fogged surface cleared in a circle around her palm, wiped clean by nothing, showing her reflection with terrible clarity: the mark now reached her throat, a violet branch-work like frost grown inward, delicate and spreading.

Behind her reflection, in the mirror, the room was different. Older. Stone walls hung with banners she half-recognized from the dreams, storm-grey and crowned with lightning. Figures stood in it, robed, faceless, arranged like a delegation waiting for an audience.

*You see us,* said the hard voice, satisfied.

Petra snatched her hand back. The mirror went ordinary. Her heart slammed against the mark, and the mark drank the pounding like applause.

She dressed. She braided her hair with steady hands because her hands had always been steady even when nothing else was. She went downstairs and ordered breakfast and ate all of it, deliberately, because whatever was happening to her, she refused to meet it unfed. Then she walked to the bookbindery, rehearsing lies the whole way—*I slept badly, I'm fine, don't worry about the mirror, what mirror*—and arrived to find Lior in the doorway, pale, holding a familiar leather-bound book against her chest like a shield.

"You already know," Lior said. It wasn't a question. Her eyes were red-rimmed and furious and terrified, and Petra felt the founders lean forward inside her skull like an audience settling in.

Here was the moment. Here was the fork. Tell the truth—all of it, the dreams, the fence post, the mirror, the voices, the spreading—and stand in the open with Lior against whatever came. Or lie, one more time, the best-laid lie of her life, keep the mark hidden under her collar and the war hidden under her tongue, protect Lior from the knowledge that the woman she'd kissed under impossible rain was a loaded weapon walking around in a dress.

*Lie,* said the hard voice, silk-soft. *She will try to stop it. She will try to cut it out of you. You know she will. Keep her safe. Keep her ignorant. Ignorance is armor.*

And the truly awful thing, the thing Petra would carry like a stone for days afterward, was that the voice was right. Lior would try. Lior would take a knife to her own collarbone before she let the Sigil wake in Petra's skin, and Petra could not—could not—watch her do that.

So she smiled. She had always been good at smiling.

"Know what?" Petra said. "That your mysterious book is probably a diary some bored founder wrote to annoy posterity? Lior, I came here because of a dream about a valley and a woman with a scarred hand. Whatever's in that book, I promise you my secrets are older than yours."

It was true. Every word of it was true, and none of it was the truth, and Lior searched her face for a long, long moment while the rain knocked on the hills and the mark climbed another inch toward Petra's heart.

"Come inside," Lior said finally. "I'll make the tea."

Petra stepped over the threshold into the smell of glue and paper and the only home she'd ever chosen, and behind her ears, softly, the founders began to hum—a war song, old and patient, in four-part harmony.

*Soon,* they said. And the mark, warm as a held breath, agreed.
