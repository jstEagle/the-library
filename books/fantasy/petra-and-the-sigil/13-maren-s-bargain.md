## 13. Maren's Bargain

Maren came to the bookbindery on a Tuesday, which felt insultingly ordinary. Tuesdays were for mending spines and arguing with the flour merchant about the price of rye. Tuesdays were not for wardens in formal grey, standing in my doorway with her hands folded like a woman attending a funeral she had personally arranged.

"I'll only take a moment of your time," she said.

"You can have all of it if you leave now."

Her mouth did something that was almost a smile. "May I come in?"

I should have said no. I want that recorded somewhere, in some ledger even Maren couldn't burn: *Lior Iversen, who had read a prophecy naming her beloved as a thing to be killed, who had spent four sleepless nights with the book hidden under her floorboards, looked Warden Maren Halloran in the eye and stepped aside to let her in.* But there it is. When the person who holds every card over you asks politely, politeness feels like mercy.

She crossed my shop the way she crossed everything—like an inspector. Her gaze snagged on the press, the stacks of uncut signatures, the jar of bone folders, the little brass weather-vane I kept on the sill because it had been my mother's and it never turned anymore, not here, where no wind worth measuring ever blew.

"Cozy," she said.

"It's a shop."

"It's a life." She turned to face me. "That's rather the point."

My hands found the counter behind me and gripped it. "If this is about the archive—"

"This isn't about the archive." She said it gently, which was worse. "Though we will get to the archive. Sit down, Lior."

I sat. It seemed safer than standing, somehow. Standing felt like a posture from which one might be struck down.

Maren did not sit. She picked up a half-finished binding—a hymnal for the chapel, plain calfskin—and turned it over in her hands with the air of someone appraising a horse.

"Do you know why Hollowmere has no magic?" she asked.

"Because the founders sealed it out. You told me. To starve someone specific." I had repeated it to myself so many times in the dark that it came out flat, worn smooth like a river stone. "You told me my exile was arranged. That my trial was—" My voice caught on the word. "*Staged.*"

"Yes." She set the hymnal down. "And you've spent the days since then doing what, exactly? Packing? Planning? Writing angry letters to people who can't read them?"

"None of your business."

"On the contrary." She pulled out the chair across from me—the customer chair, the chair where old Widow Ashe sat while I talked her through repairs to her late husband's recipe book—and sat down in it, folding her hands. "It became my business the moment Petra Vane walked into your shop."

The name landed in my chest like a dropped anvil. I kept my face still. I'd had practice; I'd spent eleven years keeping my face still.

"I don't know what you mean," I said. "She's a customer. She likes books."

"She likes books," Maren agreed. "And she dreams of storms. And she has begun, in the last fortnight, to glow faintly at the collarbone when she sleeps, which the night watchman's dog finds very upsetting." She watched me. "You didn't know about the mark."

I said nothing, which was its own confession. Something cold slid down through my ribs. *A mark.* The prophecy book had shown a sigil blooming over a heart, drawn in ink gone brown with age, but I had let myself believe—I had *prayed*—that it was metaphor. Ink. Symbol. Anything but a literal flower opening on Petra's skin.

"There it is," Maren said softly. "You do love her. Good. That makes this conversation possible instead of merely cruel."

"Maren—"

"Warden Halloran," she corrected, without heat. "For the next few minutes, I need to be the warden. Afterwards—" she exhaled, and for just a moment the official grey of her seemed to sag, and underneath it was the tired woman who bought cough syrup for her watchmen and once, years ago, had stood up for a frightened exiled girl at a grain riot, "afterwards you can hate me. I'd prefer that, honestly. Hate is simpler than what you're going to feel."

"Try me."

So she tried me.

* * *

What she told me took less time than I expected, because most of it I already knew, or had guessed, or had lain awake constructing in the small hours until it fit together like a well-bound spine. The founders had sealed the valley a century ago—not against magic in general, but against one specific convergence: the Sigil and its Key, meeting, waking, choosing. Prophecy is a stubborn weed, Maren said. You can pave over it, but it finds the cracks. So the founders had paved. They had taken a storm-calling child from one of the old houses, staged her trial, altered her memory, and planted her in the deadest ground they owned, where she would grow up small and careful and alone, her power starving quietly in the dark like a lamp with no oil.

"And it worked," Maren said. "For eleven years it worked. You were supposed to live out your whole life here, Lior. Bind books. Grow roses. Die at eighty, unremarkable, and the Sigil would never wake because its Key never turned. That was the design. A whole human life, spent as a lock."

"A lock," I repeated.

"A key," she said. "Forgive me. Both. You know what I mean."

I did know what she meant. I had known since the archive, since the ledger with my childhood trial written in a founder's neat hand—*memory adjusted, subject stable, monitoring quarterly*. Eleven years of quarterly reports on my life, filed by wardens who smiled at me at market.

"So what changed?" I asked. My voice sounded far away. "Your design worked. Why are you here?"

"Because designs require maintenance, and the maintainers are dead." She spread her hands. "The founders are gone, Lior. Have been for decades. What's left is their machinery—the seal, the wards, the protocols—and wardens like me, following instructions written by people who assumed they'd always be alive to revise them. There is no one left who understands the seal. There is no one left who could undo it safely, or renew it, or amend it. And into that decaying machine walks Petra Vane, carrying a sigil that is already waking, toward the one person in the world whose touch can complete it."

"The prophecy says I'm supposed to kill her."

"The prophecy says a great many things. Most of them are wrong, or were wrong the moment they were written down. But that part—" Maren's jaw tightened. "That part the founders believed. And everything they built assumes it's true. The seal, the boundary, the wards on the ridge line—all of it is calibrated to end the Sigil if the Key turns the wrong way. If you and she meet fully, if the awakening completes—" she stopped herself, and I understood she was editing, sparing me, and hated her for it and loved her for it in the same breath.

"If it completes, what?"

"War," she said simply. "Not the war of a century ago. Worse. The houses outside the valley have spent a hundred years arming for a prophecy they think is theirs. The Sigil waking is the bell that starts it. And the valley—" she looked around my shop, at the hymnal, the weather-vane, the jar of brushes, "the valley burns first. Every soul in it. That's not rhetoric. That's arithmetic. I've read the founders' projections."

The shop was very quiet. Somewhere outside, Mrs. Tulliver was scolding her goat, and the sound was so absurdly, blessedly mundane that I nearly laughed.

"So kill her," I said. "You're the warden. Kill her yourself."

"I can't." For the first time, Maren looked away from me. "The wards won't permit it. The founders built the execution clause around the Key, not the wardens. Only your hand can end the Sigil cleanly. Anyone else who tries—" she shook her head. "Anyone else who tries makes it worse. Faster. We tested the theory once, early on, before your time. A warden drew steel on a partial awakening in the eastern villages. The sigil jumped. Three towns. Two hundred dead in a night. The founders covered it up and wrote stricter protocols." She met my eyes again. "I am not asking you to do murder, Lior. I am telling you that the machinery the founders built gives us exactly two doors, and both of them open onto you."

"What are the doors?"

"One: the Sigil wakes, the war comes, the valley burns, and the storm-crowned houses fight over the ashes for another century. Two: the Key turns the other way. Quietly. Soon. Before the awakening completes." She leaned forward. "Kill Petra Vane, and I will lift your exile. Not ease it—lift it. Your name restored. Your record burned. Your memory returned—all of it, everything they took from you, every year of your childhood, unaltered. You could walk out of this valley a free woman with her whole self intact, and no one in any court or house would ever touch you again. The founders' own authority, such as it remains, guarantees it. I guarantee it."

The offer hung in the air between us, glittering and obscene.

*Freedom. Your name. Yourself.*

Eleven years I had wanted exactly that. I had wanted it with a hunger I barely admitted to myself—wanted my stolen childhood back, wanted to stand outside the valley and feel weather, real weather, wanted to be *Lior Iversen* somewhere other than in my own head. And here it was, offered on a Tuesday, in exchange for the one thing I had found in this exile that made the exile bearable.

"That's not a bargain," I said. My voice cracked on it. "That's a ransom."

"Yes," Maren said. "It is. I'm sorry."

"How long do I have?"

"Three days. After that, the awakening reaches a threshold and the choice stops being yours. The wards will act on their own protocols, and their protocols are—" she paused. "Older and crueller than anything I would choose. If the machinery decides, it decides for everyone."

"Three days," I said.

"Three days." She rose, smoothed her coat, and paused at the door with her hand on the frame. "Lior. One more thing, and then I'll be the tired woman again instead of the warden, and you can throw something at me if you like."

I waited.

"When I was a girl," she said, "my grandmother told me that the founders weren't monsters. They were people who loved the valley so much they were willing to feed it children. And she said the truly dangerous thing about people like that isn't their cruelty. It's that they're *right*, often enough that you start listening." She looked at me for a long moment. "I have spent my whole career trying to be right less often and kind more. I have mostly failed. Whatever you decide in three days—decide it as yourself. Not as their key. Not as their lock. As the woman who binds books and grows roses and argues with the flour merchant. Because if you let them make the decision for you, even by obeying, even by rebelling—they win either way."

Then she was gone, and the door swung shut, and the little brass weather-vane on the sill gave one shudder, though there was no wind.

* * *

I don't remember the rest of Tuesday. I remember fragments: the hymnal sitting unfinished on the bench, its spine gaping like a wound. The flour merchant coming and going, me agreeing to any price he named, him leaving looking worried. The light moving across the floor from morning to evening without my noticing it pass.

I remember standing at the window at dusk, watching the road, waiting for a figure that wasn't coming, because I hadn't sent for her, because I couldn't look at her yet, because every time I imagined her face the prophecy's words lay over it like a watermark: *and the Key shall turn, and the bearer shall be ended, and the valley shall sleep.*

Three days.

I thought about my scarred palm, the ache of it when storms passed beyond the hills. I thought about the ledger in the archive, my childhood written down in someone else's hand. I thought about Maren's grandmother, and people who loved things so much they fed them children, and how the founders' machinery was still turning, still hungry, still expecting to be fed.

And then I thought about rain that never crossed the ridge. About a kiss under a sky too cowed to weep. About Petra laughing at my terrible jokes about glue, Petra holding a finished book like it was a bird she was afraid to startle, Petra saying *tell me about the storms you almost remember* with an expression I had mistaken for idle curiosity and now understood was homesickness—for a home she'd never seen, inside a body she hadn't met yet.

Three days to decide whether the person I loved most in the world was a person or a prophecy.

There was a knock at the door.

I knew the knock. Of course I knew the knock—two quick, one soft, a rhythm she'd invented our second week because she claimed my shop needed a signature tune. My heart did something complicated and painful.

Through the window I saw her on the step, lantern light catching her hair, a paper-wrapped bundle under one arm—pastries, probably, she always brought pastries, she said a bindery ran on sugar and gossip. She was smiling. She had no idea. Or—some cold, clear part of me noted—she had *some* idea, because there was a new wariness in the set of her shoulders, a flicker in her eyes as she glanced up and down the street, and the mark beneath her collarbone—

She raised her hand to knock again.

I opened the door.

"Lior," she said, and held up the bundle like a peace offering, like a shield, like a gift. "I brought almond twists. Also—" her smile faltered, just slightly, just enough, "—also I think we need to talk. There's something I haven't told you."

Behind her, far off beyond the hills, thunder rolled against the boundary of the world, and my scar ached like a second heartbeat.

Three days had just become three days minus one evening.
