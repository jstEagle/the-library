## 14. Three Days

### Lior

The first morning of the last three days of Petra's life, I burned the bread.

I stood in the bookbindery kitchen watching smoke curl off the loaf and thought, with a strange and terrible calm: *I have never burned bread before. Not once in nine years. And now, with everything ending, my hands have forgotten how to be ordinary.*

Petra appeared in the doorway wearing one of my shirts, sleeves rolled to her elbows, hair still damp from washing. She looked at the smoking oven. She looked at me.

"New technique?" she asked.

"It's called *charcoal sourdough*," I said. "Very fashionable. In places that aren't here."

"In places that aren't anywhere," she said gently, and came over, and took the tongs from my hand, and set them down, and held both my hands instead. Her palms were warm. Mine were shaking, which was new too.

We had not spoken of it since Maren left. Three days, she had said. Kill her quietly, and your name comes back to you like a returned letter. I had watched Petra's face while Maren said it — watched her go very still, the way she went still when she was listening to something none of us could hear — and then we had walked home through the evening market together buying onions and thread and a jar of plum preserves, as if the world were a place where those things still mattered.

Maybe they did. Maybe they were the only things that mattered. I honestly didn't know anymore.

"We should do something," Petra said. "Today. Something you've always wanted to do."

"I've always wanted to finish rebinding the Aldous ledger."

"Lior."

"It's a very satisfying ledger," I said. "The spine is a tragedy. It's been a tragedy for six years. There's something beautiful about fixing a tragedy with paste and linen thread."

She laughed — that real laugh, the one that crinkled her whole face — and then the laugh caught on something and stopped, and for a moment we just stood there in the flour-dusted kitchen holding hands over a ruined loaf, two people pretending very hard at morning.

"All right," she said finally. "The ledger. But tonight—" her voice did something complicated— "tonight you're taking me somewhere. Somewhere you love. You keep saying there's a hill behind the tannery where you used to watch storms come in, and you've never once taken me."

"That's because storms don't come in," I said. "That's rather the whole problem with this valley."

"Then show me where they would come in. If they could." She squeezed my hands. "Show me the place where you miss them from."

And because it was day one of three, and because I had already decided — in some wordless chamber of myself, some room I hadn't dared enter yet — that I would burn this whole valley's law to ash before I let Maren touch her, I said yes.

* * *

The ledger took us until mid-afternoon. We worked side by side at the bench, Petra tearing linen strips into even widths while I sewed, and it struck me, somewhere around the third signature, that she had learned my craft without either of us noticing. Her knots were mine. Her tension on the thread was mine. Nine months of mornings had written themselves into her hands.

"You're staring," she said, not looking up.

"I'm assessing your workmanship."

"Mm. And?"

"Passable. For an amateur. Who I'm possibly in love with."

She looked up at that. The afternoon light came through the workroom window the way it always did, gold and slow, full of dust motes and the smell of hide glue, and Petra sat in the middle of it looking at me like I'd handed her something breakable.

"Possibly," she repeated.

"Don't make it weird," I said, and bent back over the sewing frame so she couldn't see what my face was doing.

But she reached across the bench and turned my chin with two fingers, gentle as lifting a page, and made me look at her anyway. "Say it properly," she said. "I want it said properly. Today of all days."

The trouble with Petra — one of the ten thousand troubles of Petra — was that she knew exactly when to ask for things. I put down the needle. I wiped my hands on my apron like a nervous apprentice.

"I love you," I said. "Not possibly. Not conditionally. Not *despite*. I love you the way the valley loves its silence — completely, and to its own ruin, and I don't know how to stop."

Something moved across her face — grief, or guilt, or that listening-stillness again — and then she kissed me, tasting of tea and linen dust, and for a while the ledger waited and the clock in the corner ticked and neither of us counted anything at all.

* * *

### Petra

Here is what I did not tell her, on the first day: that the whispering had started again at dawn.

It lived under my heartbeat now, the founders' murmur — *wake, bear it, wake, the war is owed, wake* — and it got louder every time Lior touched me, which felt like a joke some cruel god had written into the margins of my life. Every kiss fed it. Every morning she handed me tea, the mark on my collarbone warmed like a banked coal, and something old and patient turned over in its sleep beneath my ribs.

So when she said *I love you the way the valley loves its silence*, I kissed her partly because I meant it more than I had ever meant anything, and partly because if I didn't move my mouth soon it might have opened and spoken in a voice that wasn't mine.

I was getting good at knowing the difference. That was the worst part. I could feel the seams of myself now, the places where Petra ended and the Sigil began, and the seams were fraying.

But the day belonged to her. I had decided that. Whatever the next three days were, day one would be hers: burnt bread and ledgers and, when the light went long and amber, the hill behind the tannery.

She brought a blanket and a bottle of elderflower wine she'd clearly been saving for an occasion better than this one, and we climbed through gorse and dry grass to a bald crown of rock overlooking the ridge line — that sawtooth wall where the sky visibly changed, stormclouds stacking up against it like water against glass, forever refused entry.

"Oh," I said quietly. "Oh, Lior."

"I told you."

"You didn't tell me it looked like *that*." The clouds beyond the ridge were enormous, violet-bellied, lit from inside by lightning that never reached us. They had been doing it for a century, I understood. Waiting at the door. "It's like watching someone knock and knock and knock."

"That's exactly what it's like," she said. "Every day of my childhood I came up here and watched, and I used to think — maybe today. Maybe today the wind shifts wrong and it gets through. And then I grew up and understood nothing gets through, ever, because the founders built the lock, and I stopped coming." She paused. "Until recently."

"Why recently?"

She was quiet long enough that I almost repeated the question. Then she said, "Because you arrived, and the scar on my hand started aching again after nine years of quiet, and I wanted to look at the thing that was hurting me and try to understand it." She laughed, short and unhappy. "That sounds mad when I say it out loud."

"It doesn't sound mad," I said, and my chest hurt, because I knew about the scar, I had dreamed about the scar before I ever saw it, a woman on a drowned battlefield pressing her bleeding palm to the sky — but I couldn't say any of that, not yet, not while she was finally handing me pieces of herself. "It sounds like a body telling the truth."

We drank the wine. The sun went down behind the ridge and the stormclouds went gold, then rose, then bruise-dark, and lightning stitched silently along their undersides like a hem being sewn. Lior leaned against my shoulder. Her scarred hand lay open in her lap between us, palm up, and I looked at it and looked at it, and the whispering under my heart said, soft as a lover: *that one. that one opens the door.*

"Tell me something true," I said. "Anything. One true thing per day. That's the rule for the next three days."

"One true thing," she said. She thought about it. "All right. Here's mine: I don't care about my name. Maren offered it back to me like it was the whole world, and I sat there realizing I'd trade it — all of it, the title, the house I barely remember, the family crest — for one more ordinary morning of burning bread with you." Her voice cracked slightly on the last word. "That's my true thing. Your turn."

My turn. My true thing sat in my throat like a stone, and beside it sat another true thing, smaller and warmer:

"I came here for you," I said. "Before I met you. Before I knew your face. I dreamed this valley — the ridge, the teahouse, a woman with a scarred hand — hundreds of times, since I was a child. And when I finally found it on a map I sold everything I owned and traveled four months to get here, and when you handed me that first cup of tea I nearly cried in front of a stranger, because you were *real*, and I had no idea why I'd been dreaming you, and I didn't care." I swallowed. "That's mine. That's the whole ugly wonderful truth of it."

Lior stared at me. The lightning flickered beyond the ridge, soundless.

"Dreams," she said carefully. "Of me. Since childhood."

"Since childhood."

"And you didn't think to mention this *at any point in nine months*?"

"I was going to!" I said. "I was waiting for — for the right moment, and then the right moment kept not being right, and then—" I gestured helplessly at the ridge, at the whole sealed valley, at everything. "Then it got complicated."

Lior was quiet for a long moment. Then she tipped her head back and laughed — really laughed, helpless and a little unhinged, wine-loosened — and said, "We are *extraordinary* idiots, you know that? We are two people who each carried a secret for months, sitting on a hill, and I promise you mine is worse than yours."

"Mine involves prophetic dreams."

"Mine involves a prophecy book with our first date written in it a century before we had it."

I went cold. "...What?"

"Not tonight," she said quickly, and took my hand, and pressed her lips to my knuckles, and I let it go — I shouldn't have, I would spend the next two days not letting it go — because the light was dying and her mouth was warm and the whispering under my heart had gone suddenly, eagerly silent, like something leaning closer to listen.

### Lior

The second day, we did nothing at all, magnificently.

We slept late. We ate breakfast on the workbench among the glue pots. Petra fixed the wobbly leg on the kitchen table with a folded scrap of leather and tremendous ceremony; I read aloud from a water-damaged novel we were restoring, doing all the voices badly. In the afternoon it rained — ordinary rain, valley rain, thin and gray and magicless — and we stood in the doorway of the shop watching it with our arms around each other like a pair of old marrieds, and I thought: *this is the thing Maren cannot understand. This is what she thinks can be traded away for a name.*

Somewhere in the middle of that gray afternoon, Petra went quiet against my shoulder in a way I recognized now. The listening-quiet.

"What are they saying?" I asked.

She stiffened. "Who?"

"Whoever you listen to." I kept my eyes on the rain. "You go still. You've done it since the night Maren came. I'm not going to pretend I haven't noticed, and I'm not going to ask you to explain whatever it is before you're ready. But I'd rather you lied to me less, in the time we have. Even if the truth is terrible. *Especially* if the truth is terrible."

The rain ticked on the awning. When she finally spoke, her voice was small.

"They say *wake*," she whispered. "Old voices. Dead voices, I think — dead founders, though I don't know how I know that. They say the war is owed. They say I'm the payment." Her hand drifted to her collarbone, where — I saw now, and my stomach dropped — a mark showed above her collar like ink blooming through paper. A sigil. A spiral of interlocking lines, fine as engraving, that I was quite certain had not been there a month ago. "They've been saying it since before I came here. Louder every day. And Lior — sometimes lately, when I'm half-asleep, my mouth says things I haven't decided to say yet."

I turned that over. Part of me screamed. Part of me — the part that had read the prophecy book four times now, by candlelight, alone, hating every word — simply nodded, because it fit. Of course it fit. The page had promised this too.

"Okay," I said. "Thank you. That's your true thing for today."

"That wasn't the rule. The rule was one nice true thing—"

"The rule evolved. Circumstances changed." I took her hand — the unscarred one — and laced our fingers. "Here's mine, then, to balance the books: tomorrow night, before Maren's deadline, I'm going to get you out of this valley. I don't know how yet. But I've spent nine years learning every fold of Hollowmere's borders, and there's a stretch of the ridge past the old quarry where the boundary runs thin — I've felt it thin, like a scar of its own. If anyone can get you through, it's me. So tomorrow we pack light, and we leave at dusk, and you're going to be furious with me about the pace I set, and I don't care."

Petra was staring at me. Rain-light moved on her face.

"You've known for two days that they gave you three days," she said slowly, "and you spent today reading bad novels and fixing tables."

"Yes."

"Why?"

"Because," I said, and found the honest answer rising in me like groundwater, "because if these are the last ordinary days either of us gets, I refuse to spend them being hunted. I refuse to let Maren take even the memory of them. Let her have her deadline. These two days were ours. Tomorrow we run."

For a moment she didn't move. Then she pulled me into the shop, out of the rain, and kissed me with the door wide open where the whole street could see, which was unlike her, which was glorious, which felt like a beginning even as I stood there counting hours.

### Petra

The third day dawned clear and cold, and I woke before Lior and lay watching her sleep and tried to memorize her.

This was a discipline I had begun without admitting it: cataloguing. The exact brown of her eyes, darker near the iris. The way her scarred hand curled under her chin even in sleep, guarding itself. The small crease between her brows that appeared when she dreamed — and she dreamed, I could tell, her eyes moving fast beneath the lids, and once, softly, she said a word I couldn't catch, and the mark on my collarbone *answered*, warming, and I lay very still and hated the thing inside me for eavesdropping on the woman I loved.

*Wake,* the voices murmured, drowsy, insistent. *Bear it. The war is owed.*

"Owed to whom?" I whispered into the gray light. "Nobody alive remembers the war. Nobody alive chose it. What kind of debt collects itself from children?"

The voices didn't answer. They never answered questions. Questions were not in their script.

I got up quietly and made breakfast — proper bread this time, kneaded with more force than the dough deserved — and when Lior shuffled out following the smell, hair wild, she stopped in the doorway and looked at the table: bread, honey, the good jam, two cups, and packed beside the door, our bags.

"So it's real," she said. "Today."

"Today." I slid her cup toward her. "Eat first. Run second. Those are the rules."

She sat. She ate. Halfway through the honey she paused, spoon in the air, and said, "If this works — if we get over the ridge — what then? Have you thought about what's out there? Because I haven't seen outside this valley in nine years, and you followed a dream here, and neither of us knows what we're running *toward*."

"I've thought about it," I said. It was true; I'd lain awake half the night mapping it. "There's a port town two weeks east called Vessany. I passed through it on the way here. They say the storm-crowned houses still hold the coast — the heirs, the remnants, whoever survived the peace. It won't be safe. Nothing will be safe, probably, ever again, once the Sigil leaves this valley awake." I looked at my own hands. "But it'll be *unwritten*, Lior. No prophecy. No ledger. No dead founders with opinions. Just two women, one of whom occasionally hears ghosts, figuring out the rest of it as it comes."

Lior reached across the table and took my hand, and turned it over, and studied the palm as if reading something there.

"Unwritten," she said. "Yes. That's the word. That's the whole plan, really — everything else is just walking."

We finished breakfast. We washed the cups, because even at the end of the world there were cups, and dried them, and set them in the rack, and I caught Lior doing it very deliberately, very slowly, and understood she was memorizing the kitchen the way I had memorized her sleeping face, and my throat closed up so tight I couldn't speak for a minute.

Then we shouldered the bags, and she checked the shop door twice, and we stepped out into the last cold gold morning Hollowmere would ever give us, and turned north, toward the quarry, toward the thin place in the world's skin.

Behind us, at the far end of the street, three figures in warden gray detached themselves from a doorway and began, unhurriedly, to follow.

Lior saw them. I felt her see them — felt her hand tighten on mine, felt her breath change — and she didn't stop, didn't speed up, only murmured, "Good. Saves me the trouble of finding them later," and led me on toward the ridge line, where the boundary waited, thin as a scar, and where I would learn within the hour that some doors do not open from the inside — no matter who is holding your hand.
