## 15. The Ridge Line

The plan, such as it was, fit inside a flour sack.

That was the first sign it was a bad plan. The second sign was that I'd made it at three in the morning, standing in the bookbindery in my nightgown, holding a sack of flour like a war council of one. But bad plans have one advantage over good ones: they don't wait for you to feel ready.

Two days left on Maren's clock. One night to get Petra over the ridge.

I had spent the day being ordinary with a precision that would have impressed a jeweler. I bound two ledgers. I drank tea with Widow Corren and let her tell me the story of her hip, all four acts. I smiled at the baker's boy. If anyone watched me—and someone always watched me now—I wanted them to see nothing but a woman whose life was exactly as small as it appeared.

Then the lamps went out along the mill road, one by one, as they did every night when old Tammer made his rounds, and I went to fetch Petra.

She was waiting at the back door of the inn with a traveling cloak and an expression I recognized from my own mirror: terror wearing its best coat.

"You look terrible," she said.

"So do you."

"Good. We match." She glanced past me at the dark street, then back. "Lior. Tell me again this is going to work."

"It's going to work," I said, which was the third lie I'd told her that week, and by now they came easier than truth, which is how you know a lie has settled in properly, like damp in a wall.

* * *

We went up through the orchards, where the trees stood black and patient against the stars, and then into the sheep paths above them, where the ground remembered every footstep you'd ever lied about. Petra climbed well. Better than me, though she pretended not to notice, slowing her pace and blaming her boots.

"Where are we crossing?" she asked, low.

"The shepherd's cut. North ridge. It's where the boundary is thinnest—Maren's wardens patrol the ford and the south pass, but nobody watches the cut because nothing's supposed to be able to cross anywhere." I paused. "In theory."

"And if your theory is wrong?"

"Then we'll know very quickly."

"That's not comforting."

"It wasn't meant to be. Comforting things slow you down."

She laughed—a short, startled sound, quickly smothered—but she reached for my hand as we climbed, and I let her take it. Her fingers were cold. Mine were shaking. Between us we made almost one steady person.

I want to record what that climb felt like, because afterward everything about that night got rewritten by other people, louder people, people with titles. But before the ridge, before the burning, there was just this: two women walking fast through wet grass under a sky full of indifferent stars, holding hands, telling each other jokes neither of us found funny, because the alternative was saying what we were actually thinking, and neither of us could afford that yet.

Petra broke first, of course. She never could hold silence the way I could.

"When we're out," she said, "where do we go? I've thought about it. East, I think. There's a port city—Vessany. They say you can be anyone there. Nobody asks where you came from." Her voice picked up speed, the way hope does when it's running downhill. "You could open a shop. A real one, not a—no offense to Hollowmere, but a real bindery, with customers who bring you books worth saving. And I could—" She stopped herself. Laughed at herself. "Listen to me. Planning. As if planning has ever once worked out for either of us."

"Keep planning," I said. "One of us should."

"What does that mean?"

"It means I'm saving my breath for the climbing."

It wasn't the whole truth. The whole truth was that I couldn't picture past the ridge. My whole adult life fit inside this valley like water fits a bowl, and I didn't know what shape I'd be if you poured me out. But there was no time for honesty, so I gave her the climb instead, and she took it, and we went up.

* * *

The shepherd's cut was a fold in the hills where the land pinched together like a closed hand. In daylight it was unremarkable—scree, gorse, a leaning fence post some optimist had planted decades ago. At night, with the wind coming down off the heights, it felt like standing in a doorway.

I stopped twenty paces from the crest and held out my arm.

"This is it?"

"This is it."

"Where's the boundary? I don't see anything."

"Neither do I. That's rather the point of boundaries." I dug in the flour sack and pulled out what I'd brought: a ball of twine, a stub of candle, and the one thing I'd taken from Maren's archive without asking—a scrap of warding paper, thin as onion skin, that supposedly registered the valley's edge. "Hold this."

She took the paper. It lay flat and dead in her palm.

"Nothing," she said.

"Give it here." I held it myself, walked three steps toward the crest, four. On the fifth step the paper twitched, curled at one corner like a leaf deciding about autumn, and went still. "There. Edge runs right about... here." I marked the spot with the candle stub, set it down unlit. "Now. When you cross, walk straight through. Don't hesitate at the line. Hesitation is how the body talks itself into turning around, and I need your body to be braver than the rest of you."

"Lior." Her voice had gone quiet in the way that made my chest hurt. "What happens to you? After I cross?"

"I come back down. I deal with Maren. I have a name again, a life again, and eventually—when it's safe—I find Vessany, and I stand in your shop, and I pretend to be a customer who needs a book mended, and you pretend you don't recognize me until the door's locked, and then—"

"And then," she agreed.

"We'll manage," I said. "People have done harder things than be happy. Probably. Somewhere."

She kissed me then, quick and fierce, tasting of cold air and the tea she'd drunk too fast, and then she turned to face the ridge, squared her shoulders like a swimmer at a cold shore, and walked.

I have relived those five steps more times than I can count. The set of her shoulders. The way she lifted her chin. The little hitch in her stride as her boot came down on the exact spot where my candle stub sat—the border between the valley and the rest of the world.

And then the light came.

Not lightning. Not fire. Something older and more bureaucratic than either: a seam of pale gold opening in the empty air, following the contour of the hill like a thread pulled taut, and where it touched her it *sang*—a low, grinding note, the sound a door makes when it hasn't been opened in a hundred years and resents being asked.

Petra screamed.

I was moving before the scream finished. I don't remember deciding to move; my body simply resigned from the plan. I crossed the distance in a handful of strides and caught her as the boundary threw her backward—threw her the way a wave throws driftwood, without malice and without mercy—and we went down together into the scree, her weight on top of me, both of us gasping.

The gold seam hung in the air above us, fading, humming its closed-door note. And across Petra's collarbone, where her cloak had fallen open, the mark was glowing.

Not flickering. Not warming. Glowing—steady, deliberate, like a lamp lit behind her skin by someone else's hand.

"No," she whispered. She was staring down at it. "No, no—it's never done that. Lior, it's never—"

"Let me see." I pushed the cloak off her shoulder. The mark had spread since the last time I'd seen it. What had been a coin-sized whorl at her collarbone now branched down toward her heart like frost growing across a window, fine silver lines that moved when I looked away and were somewhere else when I looked back. Where the boundary had struck her, the lines burned brightest, and the skin around them was reddened—not wounded, exactly. *Refused.*

"Talk to me," I said. "Does it hurt?"

"It burns. Not—like sunburn. Like being *read*." She laughed, high and wrong. "Like something opened me up to check whether I was allowed out, and it found what it was looking for, and it said no."

I knew. That was the worst part—I knew before she finished the sentence, because I'd read the ledger, I'd read the founders' neat, patient handwriting, and there had been a line in it I'd hoped was metaphor. *The vessel may not leave the valley while the Sigil sleeps within it. It will wake, or it will be spent. There is no third disposition.*

There was no third disposition.

"Get up," I said. "We try again."

"Lior—"

"The cut narrows further north. Maybe the boundary's weaker where the stone's thinner. We try again."

"Lior, it *threw* me."

"Then we try until it throws us into next week. Get *up*."

* * *

We tried the narrow place. The seam sang, and the mark blazed, and the valley put her back on her side of the line with the gentle, absolute insistence of a tide.

We tried the western shoulder, where a rockfall had torn a raw scar through the hillside. Same note. Same refusal. This time the mark flared bright enough to light the scree around us, and Petra dropped to her knees, and when I reached her she was laughing—that awful, breathless laughter that lives next door to sobbing.

"Do you know what it feels like?" she said. "It feels like the valley is *holding* me. Like a hand around my ribs. Like I'm a book someone shelved and locked the library." She looked up at me, and her eyes were wet and furious and terrified. "Is that what exile felt like for you? All those years? Is this what you lived with?"

I knelt in front of her. "Yours has a lock on the inside. Mine only had walls."

"That's not better."

"No," I admitted. "It isn't."

Wind came down the cut and moved through the gorse, and somewhere below us a nightbird started up its idiotic, beautiful song, entirely unaware that the world had ended twice already tonight. I sat down in the wet grass beside her because my legs had stopped taking instructions, and after a moment she leaned into me, and we stayed like that, two failed escapees, watching the place in the air where the seam had been.

"There's no way out," she said finally. Flat. Testing the sentence to see if it would hold her weight.

"Not while—" I stopped.

"While it's asleep in me." She finished it anyway. She'd earned the finishing. "Say it, Lior. Say the whole thing. You've been carrying pieces of it for days and handing them to me one at a time like I'll drop them. Say all of it."

So I did. The prophecy. The Key. Maren's bargain, the three days, the choice dressed up as mercy. I said it all into the dark, quietly, the way you tell someone a diagnosis, and she listened without interrupting, which was somehow worse than screaming.

When I finished, she was silent a long time. Then:

"You were going to do it." Not accusatory. Just precise, the way she was precise about everything. "Kill me. Quietly. And get your name back."

"Yes."

"And then you weren't."

"After the archive. After I read what they did to me." I turned my scarred palm up between us. The old ache had woken with the storm-season, and it throbbed now in time with the mark on her skin, two instruments tuned to the same broken string. "They built their whole wall to keep me from ever meeting you. A hundred years of engineering, and the one thing they never planned for was that I'd love you anyway. Love isn't in their ledger, Petra. It's the one column they forgot to rule."

She was quiet. Then she took my scarred hand and pressed it, gently, against the mark on her collarbone.

The effect was instant and quiet: the burning eased. The silver lines dimmed from blaze to ember. She sighed like someone setting down a load.

"Well," she said shakily. "That's new."

"What did it feel like?"

"Like the key fitting." She looked at me, and I watched her arrive at the same conclusion I'd been circling for two days, and I watched her refuse it, the way I had refused it, the way any living person refuses it. "No. Whatever you're thinking—no. I am not waking up on purpose. I am not letting them turn me into their weapon just because the alternative is staying in a cage."

"I wasn't going to suggest it."

"You were absolutely going to suggest it."

"...I was going to mention it exists."

"Lior."

"Petra."

We glared at each other in the dark, and then—because grief and fear leave you no dignity whatsoever—we both started laughing, helplessly, horribly, huddled against a hillside that wouldn't let one of us leave and wouldn't let the other one go.

* * *

We came down off the ridge in the grey hour before dawn, when the valley looks most like a painting of itself—soft edges, smoke rising straight from chimneys, everything waiting to be inhabited. Petra walked beside me with her hood up and her hand fisted in mine, and neither of us spoke, because there was nothing left to say that the walk itself wasn't already saying.

At the fork by the mill, she stopped.

"One day," she said. "Left."

I nodded. One day, and then Maren's wardens would come for her, and I would be expected to have done the arithmetic myself.

"Lior." She turned my chin up with two fingers, the way she did when she wanted to make sure I couldn't hide. "Whatever you're planning—you're doing that thing where your face goes very calm and your eyes go very busy. I know that face. I've *read* that face. It's the face you make when you're about to do something reckless and call it practical."

"I don't have a face."

"You have several faces and I've catalogued all of them."

"Go inside, Petra."

She kissed me—slowly this time, thoroughly, memorizing—and then she went, up the lane toward the inn, and I stood in the grey light watching her go, feeling the ache in my palm pulse like a second heartbeat.

Because she was right. I did have a plan. It had arrived sometime during the fourth attempt at the boundary, fully formed and monstrous, while I lay in the scree listening to the boundary sing.

The founders' ledger had said there was no third disposition. Wake, or be spent. But the ledger had been written by people who believed their own rules were the rules of the world—and I had spent eleven years in this valley learning the one thing they'd never learned: that books are written by hands, and hands can be wrong, and pages can be torn out.

The Sigil needed a key. Fine. I was the key.

But keys don't only unlock doors.

They lock them, too.

I turned toward the warden's house, where the archive waited with its iron fittings and its patient, lying ink, and I began to walk.
