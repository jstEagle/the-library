## 16. Awakening

The thing about being cornered in a bookbindery after closing, Petra decided, was that it gave a person far too much time to think about the furniture.

There was the press, for instance—the great iron screw-press Lior used to flatten spines—which would be useless as a weapon and heartbreaking to break. There were the pots of paste, which smelled of flour and clove oil and suddenly seemed like the most precious things in the world. And there was the back door, which was currently blocked by two wardens in grey coats who had the particular stillness of people who had done this before and did not enjoy it.

"Petra Vane," said the taller one. He had a kind face, which somehow made it worse. "Warden Maren requests your presence."

"At this hour?" Petra heard herself say, because apparently her mouth had chosen gallows humor over survival instinct. "I have an appointment with a woman I love very much, and she's due back any minute with bread."

"She'll come to you," the second warden said. "That's rather the point."

Ah. So they knew about Lior. Of course they knew about Lior. Everyone in Hollowmere knew everything about everyone; it was a valley built like a cupped hand, and secrets pooled at the bottom.

Petra's hand drifted to her collarbone without her permission. Beneath her shirt, the mark had been warm all day—not painful, not yet, just *present*, like a hand resting on her shoulder from behind. All week it had been spreading: a filigree of silver lines that crept outward from the hollow of her throat each morning and faded by noon, except that every day it faded a little less. Last night she had woken at three to find it glowing faintly in the dark, and she had lain there watching it pulse in time with her heartbeat, thinking, *this is fine, this is manageable, I will simply never sleep again.*

She hadn't told Lior. That was the sin she kept committing, the one she'd sworn off after Lior showed her the prophecy book with shaking hands: another secret, folded small, tucked into the same pocket where Petra kept the truth about why she'd really come to Hollowmere. She'd followed dreams here. She'd known the shape of this valley before she ever saw it. She'd known the scar on Lior's palm before she'd ever taken that hand across a teahouse table.

And now the mark was whispering to her.

Not metaphorically. Not the way grief or guilt whispers. It whispered in voices—old ones, dry ones, voices like pages turning—and they had opinions. They had *instructions*. *Come to the ridge,* they said, when she walked to market. *Open the boundary,* they said, when she washed dishes. *You were made for this,* they said, when she laughed at something Lior said, and the laughter died in her throat like a bird hitting glass.

"I'll come quietly," Petra told the wardens, "if you tell me one true thing first."

The tall warden hesitated. The short one didn't.

"You're not going to Maren," the short one said. "Maren's orders ended at sundown. We're taking you to the founders' circle above the lake."

"The founders are dead," Petra said. "Everyone says so. A hundred years dead."

"Yes," the tall warden agreed softly, and his kind face finally made sense—it was pity. "That's what everyone says."

* * *

They walked her out through the orchard paths, away from town, where the lamplight thinned and the night got serious. Hollowmere at night was a held breath: black water, blacker hills, the ridge line visible even in darkness as a place where stars simply stopped, cut off clean, like the sky itself had been trimmed to fit inside the ban.

Petra counted her heartbeats. It was a trick she'd learned as a child, when the dreams first started—count until the fear becomes arithmetic, arithmetic becomes boring, boring becomes survivable. She'd gotten to four hundred and eleven when the mark stopped being warm and started being *loud*.

It happened between one step and the next. One moment she was walking; the next, the silver lines beneath her skin lit like a city seen from the air, and every voice that had been whispering spoke at once, and they were no longer whispering.

*Here,* they said. *Here is where we begin again.*

Petra's knees buckled. The tall warden caught her elbow—gently, damn him—and she heard him say, "It's starting. Run if you're going to run," and she wanted to laugh at that, because run where? The Sigil couldn't leave the valley. She'd learned that at the ridge line with Lior's hands burned trying to push her through. The valley wasn't a prison around the Sigil. The valley was a *sheath* around it, and she was the blade.

"No," Petra said aloud, to the voices, to the wardens, to the whole rotten architecture of fate. "No, I don't consent to any of this. I want that on the record. I am a bookbinder's—" she stumbled over the word, chose it anyway, "—a bookbinder's *partner*, I make tea badly, I am nobody's vessel—"

The voices found this funny. She felt their amusement the way you feel a draft under a door.

*You were dreamed into being for this,* said one voice, older than the others, patient as sediment. *Every dream you ever had of this valley was us, calling you home. You came willingly. That was your consent.*

"That was *curiosity*," Petra snarled, and then the world went white.

* * *

Later—if it could be called later; time had gone strange, thick as cooled wax—Petra understood that she was still present, still herself, but pushed to the edges of her own body like a guest pressed against a wall at a party she no longer recognized. She watched her hands rise. They weren't moving the way she moved them; they moved the way water moves, finding levels, settling into shapes.

She stood in the founders' circle above the lake—a ring of standing stones she'd only seen in dreams, moss-eaten, half-fallen, and now blazing with light that came from nowhere and everywhere. The wardens knelt. Other figures had arrived out of the dark—more wardens, and behind them townsfolk she knew by name: the baker's wife, the ferryman, old Tomas who sold apples. Drawn, she realized, the way iron filings are drawn. The whole valley was being pulled toward her like a wound pulling shut.

*Children of the seal,* said her mouth, in a voice that was four voices braided into one, and Petra felt the words leave her throat like swallowed stones coming up. *For a hundred years we have waited in the walls of this valley. You starved our power to cage our enemy's key. You forgot our names. But the Sigil has woken, and the war will be refought, and this time we will finish it.*

*Refought against whom?* Petra screamed from her corner of herself. *Against whom? There's nobody left! The storm houses are dust! You're going to resurrect a war with no soldiers!*

But the founders didn't answer corners-of-people. The founders answered the sky.

Her right hand rose, and lightning answered it—not striking down, but *rising up*, a white thread climbing from her fingers into the low clouds, and the clouds began to turn. Slowly at first, then faster, a wheel of cloud spinning above the lake, and the temperature dropped so fast her breath plumed, and somewhere below in the town, dogs began to howl.

*This is what we were,* the founders said through her, and there was something almost tender in it, which was the worst part. *This is what you took from us. Watch, children. Remember.*

"Stop," Petra said. Her own voice, thin, from far away. "Please. I have a life. It's small. It's *stupidly* small. I burn toast. I lose arguments about punctuation. I love a woman who hides scarves in her workshop because she thinks I don't know she makes them for me." Her voice cracked. "None of that is yours to spend."

*Everything is ours to spend,* said the oldest voice, gently. *You are the coin. We are merely spending.*

And that—that word, *coin*—did something. Because Petra had spent her whole life feeling spent. Dreamed at, pulled at, aimed like an arrow at a target she'd never chosen. Every dream, every omen, every silver line crawling up her collarbone: other people's handwriting on her skin.

Well. She was a Vane, whatever else she was. And Vanes, her grandmother used to say, could find the flaw in anything. A lock, a contract, a story.

A possession.

Because here was the flaw, the hairline crack she could feel with her tongue the way you feel a chipped tooth: the founders needed her *consent*. Lior had figured it out days ago, pacing the bookbindery with the prophecy open, saying *it needs your yes, Petra, the whole text bends around agreement, kill or serve, but always choose.* The founders had dressed coercion up as destiny, but underneath, the machinery still required her to say yes. Which meant her no meant something. Which meant she was not a coin.

She was a locked door, and she was holding the key from the inside.

So Petra Vane did the only thing left to do. She planted herself in the center of her own mind—her toast-burning, argument-losing, scarf-hoarding self—and she *refused*. Not dramatically. Not with lightning. With the flat, dogged stubbornness of a person who has decided that no means no, repeatedly, indefinitely, forever.

*I don't consent,* she thought, and put her whole weight on it. *I don't consent. I don't consent. Ask me again tomorrow. Ask me again in a hundred years. The answer is no.*

The wheel of cloud stuttered.

The founders felt it—she felt them feel it, a shudder of outrage rolling through her bones like thunder looking for somewhere to land. *You cannot hold out,* they said, and for the first time they sounded less like sediment and more like men. *We have waited a century. We can wait inside you a century more. We will wear you smooth.*

"Then you'd better start," Petra said out loud, through her own teeth, her own voice, "because I am *very* smooth already."

Below, at the edge of the lamplight, the crowd parted.

Lior came through it like weather.

Petra saw her and felt something in her chest crack open—relief and terror in equal measure, because Lior was supposed to be safe, Lior was supposed to be *hidden*, Lior was the one person in this valley the founders had spent a hundred years making sure would never stand in a circle like this. And here she was, walking straight toward the blazing stones with her scarred hand bare at her side, and her eyes—

Her eyes were doing something Petra had never seen them do. Something that made the founders, all four braided voices at once, go quiet.

Lior looked at the sky, and the sky looked back.

Far beyond the ridge line, past the ban, past the boundary that had burned them both—clouds were gathering. Clouds that had no business existing in a magic-dead valley. Clouds with lightning in their bellies.

"Oh," Petra breathed, from her corner of herself, watching the woman she loved walk into the founders' light with a storm answering her like a dog answers its name. "Oh, you beautiful, impossible, *exiled* woman. What did you do?"

The first drop of rain crossed the ridge line and fell, hissing, onto the founders' stones.

The voices inside Petra began, very quietly, to scream.
