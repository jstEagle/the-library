## 18. Storm Over Hollowmere

**Lior**

The iron of the archive door was still warm from her blood when Lior stepped out into the square and felt the sky notice her.

It had been eleven years since she had let herself reach. Eleven years of pressing that reaching down into the scar on her palm, folding it smaller and smaller until it fit inside a life of paste and thread and careful, forgettable days. Now she opened her hand and stopped folding.

The valley's ban pressed back at once—a weight like a hand flat against her sternum, the founders' oldest seal, the one that had starved every spell born in Hollowmere for a century. Lior had spent her whole exile believing it was weather. It wasn't weather. It was a lock, and she was the key, and she had just cut herself open on the thing it guarded.

"Sorry," she said aloud, to no one, to everyone, to the dead men who had written her life in a ledger before she could walk. "I'm done being kept."

She reached past the ridge line.

The storm beyond the hills had been waiting—she understood this suddenly, the way you understand a dog has been waiting at a gate—for eleven years, circling, patient, called and recalled by a child who hadn't known what she was doing. It came over the ridge like something breaking loose from a tether, and with it came magic, raw and unruled, flooding down the slopes of Hollowmere the way meltwater floods a dry creek bed. Lior felt it fill the streets. Felt it fill *her*.

The lamplighters' lamps flared blue. The well water stood up in a trembling column. In the bookbindery window across the square, every sheet of blank paper rustled as though waking from sleep.

Above the town, the sky went the color of a bruise.

* * *

**Petra**

Inside herself, Petra was drowning in voices.

They had come gently at first—the way rot comes gently into wood—and now they were everywhere, the dead founders speaking through the Sigil's spreading warmth, through her blood, through her teeth. *Refound the houses,* they said. *Refight the war. The valley forgot its masters; make it remember.*

She was still in there. That was the terrible part. She could feel her own self pressed small against the inside of her skull like someone hiding in a cellar during a raid, listening to soldiers walk overhead. The mark on her collarbone burned with a light she couldn't see but everyone else could; she knew because Maren's wardens had stopped advancing and started praying.

*You are ours,* said the voice that wore her mouth. *You were made for this.*

*I was not made at all,* Petra thought, with what little of her remained. *I was born. There's a difference. Ask my mother.*

The founders laughed. It was the worst sound yet—not cruel, exactly. Worse than cruel. *Amused*, the way old men are amused by a girl who argues.

Then, above the town, the sky split open, and the laughter stopped.

Petra felt it even from inside her own hijacked body: a pressure change, a taste like copper and rain, and underneath it all a signature she knew better than her own heartbeat. She had followed dreams of this woman across half the world. She would know her storm anywhere.

*Lior,* she thought, with everything she had left. *Lior, I'm in here. I'm still in here.*

Whether the storm heard her, she couldn't say. But the lightning that answered struck the clock tower instead of the wardens, which felt, somehow, personal.

* * *

**Lior**

The first thing magic did, upon returning to Hollowmere after a hundred years, was misbehave.

Lior stood in the center of the square with rain beginning to fall upward around her, hair lifting, scar blazing white, and watched a century of suppressed enchantment come home like cats returning to a house whose owners had moved away. The warden's wards unraveled in ribbons of gold smoke. The founders' seals on the church doors cracked like ice on a pond. Somewhere down Tanner's Row, an enchanted broom that had been an ordinary broom for three generations remembered itself and began sweeping with tremendous enthusiasm, chasing old Mr. Aldous out of his own shop.

"Control it," Maren shouted, staggering toward her through the wind, her coat snapping like a flag. "Lior, for pity's sake, *control it*—"

"That's rather the point!" Lior shouted back. "Nobody controls it! Not me, not you, not your precious founders! That's what they were afraid of!"

Lightning walked the ridge line on legs of white fire, and where it touched, the boundary seal—the one that had burned Petra's skin the night before—shattered into motes that drifted down over the valley like slow snow. Lior felt each one break. It felt like pulling splinters out of a wound she'd forgotten she had.

Maren caught her arm. Her face, up close, was not the face of a tyrant. It was the face of a tired woman who had been holding a door shut for thirty years and had just been told the monster behind it might be imaginary.

"They'll kill her," Maren said. "Not the wardens. The *founders*. Through her. You've seen it."

"I've seen it." Thunder rolled, close enough to feel in her teeth. "And I'm going to stop it. So either help me or get out of my weather, Warden."

Something crossed Maren's face—grief, maybe, or relief, or the particular exhaustion of a woman who has been waiting all her life for someone reckless enough to do the obvious thing. She let go of Lior's arm.

"The archive," she said. "The iron you cut yourself on—it's the anchor. The founders speak through it. If you want to break their grip on the girl, you break it *there*."

"I know," Lior said. "I read the ledger."

"You broke into my archive?"

"You gave me three days and a murder. I'm sorry, did you expect me to spend them *honestly*?"

For one absurd moment, in the middle of the end of the world, Maren laughed. Then she turned and ran toward the wardens, shouting orders that were, for the first time in Hollowmere's history, orders to stand down.

Lior turned her face up to the storm.

"Take me up," she said.

* * *

**Petra**

The founders had gotten sloppy, drunk on their own returning power, and sloppiness was a crack, and cracks were how Petra had always gotten into places she shouldn't.

She couldn't move her body. The Sigil walked it now, standing in the ruined square with light pouring off its collarbone while wardens knelt in the mud and wept. But the founders' attention was fixed outward—on the storm, on Lior, on the delicious prospect of a war refought—and so nobody was watching the cellar.

*Here's what I know about you,* Petra told them, gathering herself in the dark of her own mind. *You wrote a prophecy a century ago because you needed the future to be a place you'd already won. You altered a child's memory because you needed her to be a tool. You built a valley-sized cage because you were afraid of weather.*

Nothing answered. Good. Arrogance was also a crack.

*But here's what I know about me.* She pressed against the walls of her prison, feeling for the seam. *I found Lior Iversen across half a continent because I dreamed about her hands. I have been stubborn since birth. My mother used to say I could argue with a locked door until it apologized.*

The seam gave, just slightly. Just enough.

*So consider this an argument, gentlemen. With a locked door.*

She threw everything she was against it—not power, she had none, not magic, she'd never had any—but *selfhood*, blunt and human and furious, the plain animal refusal of a person who declines to be a vessel. And somewhere far above, in the heart of the storm, she felt something answer her. Not the founders. Not the Sigil.

A woman with a scarred hand, holding lightning like a rope thrown to a drowning swimmer.

Petra took hold.

* * *

**Lior**

Flying, it turned out, was less like soaring and more like being argued with by the entire sky at once.

The storm carried her up over the rooftops of Hollowmere, and Lior—who had once been exiled for calling a storm she barely remembered—discovered that she remembered *everything* now. The night of her trial. The lightning answering her grief at her father's funeral. The founders in their grey robes, deciding that a nine-year-old's mourning was a weapon too dangerous to leave intact, and writing her sentence before she woke.

Below her, the town glowed with returned magic, chaotic and alive. Below her, in the square, Petra stood wrapped in borrowed light, and Lior could see—even from this height, even through the rain—that it wasn't Petra standing there. The posture was wrong. The head tilted at an angle no living person would choose.

*Come down,* said the founders, and their voice came from everywhere, from the iron anchor in the archive, from the stones, from the air itself. *Come down, Key, and finish what the page promised. Kill the bearer. End the cycle. Or give us the Sigil freely, and be restored—your name, your title, your city. Everything stolen, returned.*

"And if I refuse?" Lior asked the sky.

*Then we take her from within, and we use her anyway, and you will have accomplished nothing except a very dramatic funeral.*

"Yeah," Lior said. "That's what I figured."

She didn't come down. Instead she did the one thing no prophecy had accounted for, because prophecies are written by people who assume everyone can be bought, and Lior Iversen had spent eleven years learning to live on nothing at all.

She let go of the storm.

Not all of it. Just her grip on it—her claim, her ownership, the last shred of the controlling instinct the founders themselves had taught her by example. The lightning slipped free of her hands and became, for one breathless moment, nobody's. Wild. Unruled. Hers only in the way a friend is yours: by choosing to be.

And the storm, unowned, chose.

* * *

**Petra**

Later—if there was a later—Petra would try to describe what happened next and fail, because language was built by people who had never been pried open from the inside by the combined wills of four dead tyrants and then rescued by weather.

What she could say was this: the lightning did not strike the Sigil. It struck *around* it, again and again, a cage of white fire, and each strike burned away a layer of the founders' grip the way heat burns paint off old wood. She felt them shriek. She felt her own body come back to her one muscle at a time—fingers first, then breath, then the ordinary ache of standing too long in the cold.

And through the noise, cutting through it like a blade through ribbon, she heard Lior's voice. Not shouted. Spoken, close, impossibly close, though Lior hung forty feet above the square in a fist of wind.

"Petra!" Lior called. "Your name! Say your name!"

*Ours,* snarled the founders, rallying, coiling back into her bones. *Her name is ours—*

"It isn't," Petra gasped, and her lips were her own again, and her voice cracked in the middle like a girl's, like anyone's, like a person's. "My name is—" She searched for it, terrified, certain it had been burned away. "My name is Petra Voss. I was born in Corvane Harbor. I hate olives. I learned to pick locks when I was twelve and I never told my mother and I crossed two mountain ranges because I dreamed about a woman mending books in a valley that smelled like rain."

She looked up, through the lightning, and found Lior's eyes.

"My name is Petra," she said, "and I am *not* in your book."

* * *

**Lior**

The founders broke the way old glass breaks—all at once, everywhere, into pieces too small to ever reassemble.

Lior felt it happen from her perch in the storm: the grip on Petra shattering, the voices in the valley's stones going silent mid-word, the great cold presence that had leaned on Hollowmere for a century simply... ceasing, like a held breath finally released. The iron anchor in the archive rang once, a clear bell-note, and went dark forever.

The prophecy book—she would find this later, in the wreckage of the bindery—burned itself to blankness in the same instant, every word of the last hundred years lifting off the pages like ash off a log, leaving nothing but clean paper and a faint smell of rain.

The storm, having no master and needing none, did what storms do. It rained.

Real rain, warm and enormous, falling straight down for the first time in a hundred years, crossing the ridge line without permission, soaking the wardens kneeling in the square, soaking the shattered clock tower, soaking Petra Voss—who swayed, and crumpled, and was caught forty feet above the ground by a woman who had once been exiled for calling weather, and who now descended slowly through the deluge holding the only person in the world who mattered, while the town below came out of its doors to stand in the wet and stare.

"You're bleeding," Petra murmured against her shoulder, dazed, looking at Lior's scarred palm.

"Mm," Lior said. "Occupational hazard of being a key."

"You flew."

"I did some flying."

"There was a lot of lightning."

"I've been meaning to apologize to the clock tower for years, actually. We have history."

Petra laughed—a wrecked, soggy, astonished laugh—and buried her face in Lior's neck, and the storm, gentle now, went on falling around them like applause.

Neither of them saw the last of the founders' light gather itself, thin and desperate, at the edge of the square—neither noticed it slip away into the dark of the broken archive, where the ledger lay open on the floor, its final page no longer quite as blank as it should have been.

* * *

Rain drummed on the bookbindery roof, and inside, among the smell of glue and wet wool, Lior set Petra down on the workbench chair and lit every lamp in the shop, because some things deserved light.

"We're alive," Petra said, as if testing the claim.

"Provisionally."

"The founders—"

"Gone. Broken. I'll want to check the archive in the morning, but—" Lior flexed her bandaged hand, and the scar beneath the linen was quiet for the first time in eleven years. Quiet, and small, and hers. "Whatever's left of them, it isn't enough to matter."

Petra reached out and took her undamaged hand, and for a long moment neither of them spoke, because the storm was saying everything worth saying, and it was saying it beautifully.

Then, from the direction of the archive, faint but unmistakable, came the sound of a quill scratching across parchment.

Both of them went very still.

"...Lior," Petra said slowly, "what was the last thing in the ledger?"

Lior looked at the dark window, at the rain, at the woman she had refused two prophecies to keep.

"Blank pages," she said. "It was supposed to be blank pages."
