## 20. Aftermath of Weather

The first thing Petra learned about the morning after the end of the world was that it smelled like wet wool.

She woke in the bookbindery's back room, under a quilt that smelled of glue and cedar and someone else's lavender soap, and lay still for a long moment, taking inventory. Her body: present. Aching in the way of a body that had been borrowed by something vast and returned without an apology. Her hands: hers, though the knuckles were scraped raw from where she'd clawed at the floorboards while the founders spoke through her mouth. Her collarbone: bare.

The mark was gone. That should have been a relief. Instead she pressed her fingers to the smooth skin there and felt the absence like a missing tooth—her tongue kept going back to check, and kept finding nothing, and every time it found nothing her stomach dropped a little further.

*Am I empty?* she wondered. *Or am I finally just me?*

She didn't know which answer she wanted. That was the trouble.

Downstairs, the shop was a disaster. Shelves toppled, glass from the front window glittering across the floorboards in drifts, and through the broken frame the valley lay open to the sky in a way it hadn't been in a hundred years. Clouds moved over Hollowmere. Actual clouds, low and gray and heavy with unspent rain, drifting where they pleased, snagging on the ridge line and passing over it as if the ridge were nothing at all—as if it had never been a wall, only a hill.

Magic was back. She could feel it the way you feel a change in pressure before weather: a faint ringing in the teeth, a prickle along the arms. Unruled, Lior had called it later. Like a river after its dam breaks—everywhere at once, going nowhere yet, waiting to learn its own shape.

Petra stood in the wreckage of the shop with her arms wrapped around herself and thought: *I did this.*

And then, because grief is never tidy, immediately after: *No. It did this. Through me.*

And then, because she was who she was, a third voice, smaller and more stubborn than either: *Well. Someone ought to sweep up.*

She found the broom behind the counter. She began with the glass.

* * *

Lior came back at midmorning, gray-faced and moving like her joints had been replaced with rusted hinges, and stopped dead in the doorway when she saw Petra sweeping.

"You're cleaning," Lior said.

"There was glass everywhere."

"The founders rose from their graves, possessed my—the woman I—" Lior pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes. "The sky tore open, magic flooded a valley that has been dry for a century, and Maren's wardens are currently standing in the square arguing about whether they still have jobs, and you're *sweeping*."

"Somebody has to," Petra said. "If we leave it, it'll just become a metaphor."

Lior lowered her hands and looked at her for a long moment. There was something raw and careful in her face, the look of a person approaching a frightened animal—or a frightened person—and Petra realized, with a lurch, that she was the one being approached carefully. That Lior didn't know what would be left when you peeled the Sigil off a woman. That Lior was afraid of what she'd find.

"It's me," Petra said. "I'm still here. Mostly. I think the edges are a bit chewed."

"Chewed," Lior repeated.

"Like a book a dog got hold of. The story's all still in there. The cover's just—" She gestured vaguely at all of herself. "Corner-bitten."

Something in Lior's shoulders let go. She crossed the room, stepped over the glass drifts with the automatic grace of someone who had spent years rescuing books from worse, and took the broom out of Petra's hands. Then she hugged her, hard, glue-and-cedar and all, and Petra put her face into Lior's shoulder and shook.

"I keep hearing them," she said, when she could speak. "Not words anymore. Just—the shape of them. Like a song stuck in your head but it's not even a song, it's just the idea of a song." Her fingers curled into Lior's shirt. "They used my mouth, Lior. They said things with my voice. What if some of what's left in me is theirs? What if I can't tell anymore which thoughts are mine?"

"They're yours," Lior said. "I'll help you sort. I'm good at sorting. It's most of my job."

"That's books."

"Books, prophecies, founders. Same principle. You find the forgery by knowing the real thing well enough." She pulled back and looked at Petra steadily. "I know the real thing well enough."

Petra laughed, wetly, and it came out half sob, and Lior held on until it finished becoming the other thing.

* * *

The town, it turned out, did not know quite what to do with them.

Hollowmere had spent a century being a place where nothing happened, and its people had developed a deep institutional expertise in nothing happening. Now everything had happened at once, and the collective response was to carry on as normally as possible while glancing at the sky every eleven seconds.

Petra went to the market on the third day, because they needed bread and because she refused to become a woman who hid, and the conversations she overheard were a masterclass in polite avoidance.

"—heard the old Tamsin girl called lightning down right over the chapel—"

"—wardens say the ridge doesn't burn anymore. Tam Wentworth walked clean over it Tuesday, says his ears popped—"

"—and the Sigil-woman? They say she's living above the bindery now, plain as anything, buying *turnips*—"

That last one was said within arm's reach of her, by Mrs. Aldery, who then turned, saw her, and went the specific shade of pink achieved only by people caught gossiping at close range.

"Turnips are very grounding," Petra said pleasantly. "In a nutritional sense."

Mrs. Aldery made a sound like a kettle deciding against boiling and fled toward the cheese stall.

It wasn't cruelty. Petra understood that. It was fear wearing the mask of curiosity, and underneath both, something almost like awe, and she didn't know how to be any of those things to these people. For months she had been simply the stranger who ordered tea and asked too many questions about bookbinding. Now she was the vessel through which the dead founders of the valley had spoken and the storm had come, and there was no version of small talk that bridged that distance.

Except, apparently, turnips.

Old Hobbe, the root-seller, who was approximately as old as the valley itself and had survived it through a policy of aggressive indifference, handed her a bag of them and said, "You owe me two coppers, same as anyone," and Petra nearly kissed him.

* * *

The dreams didn't stop. That was the worst of it.

She had come to Hollowmere following dreams—a valley between ridges, a woman with a scarred hand—and she had assumed that once the Sigil woke and died and the founders burned, the dreams would stop. Accounts settled. Books closed.

Instead they changed.

Now she dreamed of the war itself—not as the founders had shown it to her, all banners and glory and righteous storm, but as it must actually have been. Mud. Screaming. A village burning that no ballad ever mentioned, full of people whose names nobody wrote down because the storm-crowned houses were busy writing down each other's. She dreamed of the founders—young, then, ambitious, certain—and watched them make choices that seemed reasonable from inside their certainty and monstrous from a century's distance, and she woke with her heart hammering and the taste of ash in her mouth.

And sometimes, in the dreams, she stood on the field herself, wearing the Sigil like a crown of light, and raised her hand, and the dream-people below looked up at her with the faces of everyone in Hollowmere—Mrs. Aldery, Old Hobbe, the warden's young wardens, Lior—

She woke from that one gasping, and Lior was already awake beside her, because Lior slept the way cats do, in shallow watchful shifts, and Lior gathered her in without asking questions and said, "Tell me the worst part."

"The worst part?" Petra managed. "There's a whole menu."

"Pick one. Naming it shrinks it. Bookbinder's trick."

"The worst part is that some of them—the founders, in the beginning—they weren't wrong about everything. They were trying to protect people. From the houses, from the war, from—" She swallowed. "From people like what I became. And I can't hate them cleanly. I want to hate them cleanly. It would be so much easier."

Lior was quiet a moment. "My trial was staged," she said. "My memory was rewritten. My whole life was a locked room built around keeping me away from you. And I *still* catch myself thinking, some mornings, 'well, but the valley was peaceful, wasn't it, a hundred years of peace.' The mind reaches for the easy ledger." She tightened her arms. "We don't have to hate them cleanly. We just have to not become them."

"And if I already did? For ten minutes on that hill, I was exactly what they built me to be."

"For ten minutes." Lior's voice was steady. "And then a woman cut her hand open on iron and told a storm your real name, and you fought your way back out of a century-dead throat to hear it. Ten minutes isn't who you are, Petra. It's what they did with you. Those aren't the same crime."

Petra cried then, quietly, thoroughly, the way you drain a wound, and afterward Lior said, "Also, if you're going to be haunted, do it quieter, some of us have to rebind three hymnals by Friday," and Petra laughed so hard she hiccupped, and the night turned bearable.

* * *

Maren came to the shop on the sixth day.

Petra saw her through the new window glass—Lior had insisted on replacing it immediately; "a broken window is how you get opinions," she'd said—standing in the street in her warden's gray, looking up at the sign, and for a moment Petra thought she might simply stand there forever, a monument to institutional embarrassment. But eventually she came in.

The bell rang. Maren flinched at it, which told Petra a great deal.

"Warden," Lior said coolly, from the workbench, not looking up from her paste brush.

"Not anymore." Maren's voice was flat. "The council met yesterday. Or—the twelve families who are left of the council. They voted. The office is dissolved pending... whatever comes next." She looked around the shop, at the shelves, at the mended window, at Petra standing by the press, and something complicated moved through her face. "I came to say two things."

"Say them quickly," Lior said. "The paste sets."

Maren ignored her. She was looking at Petra. "The first is that I'm sorry. Not for the order—I gave it because I believed the valley would burn otherwise, and I'd give it again, and I know that's not the apology you want, but it's the honest one, and I've decided to spend my last week of authority being honest." She drew a breath. "The second is that I've read the founders' ledger. All of it. Every entry, including the ones about you, and about her." A nod toward Lior. "And there's a page missing. The final page. The record of what happens *after* the Sigil wakes. Either they never wrote it, or—" her jaw worked, "—or someone tore it out, a long time ago, because they didn't like what it said."

"What did it say?" Petra asked.

"I don't know. That's rather the point." Maren set something on the counter: a slim leather folder, sealed with cracked wax. "This is the rest of my copy of the archive index. Where everything is. Including the ledger's binding notes—which mention, in the founder Vess's own hand, that the final page was written on storm-paper. Paper treated so it burns blank unless read by the Key."

The shop went very quiet. Outside, actual rain began to fall—soft, unhurried, crossing the ridge like it owned the place.

"Storm-paper," Lior said slowly. "Read by the Key."

"So the story goes." Maren straightened her gray coat, a reflexive gesture, armor for an office she no longer held. "I'm not asking you to do anything. I'm telling you that somewhere in that archive there may be a page that says what you two are *for*, and that the only person alive who can read it is standing in this room, and that I think—gods help me, I think you deserve to know what was planned for you, even if it's worse than what happened." She paused at the door. "The council will send someone to inventory the archive next week. After that, it gets sealed. Whatever you decide, decide fast."

The bell rang again behind her, gentler this time, almost apologetic.

Petra looked at Lior. Lior looked at Petra. Rain ticked softly against the new glass.

"A page that says what we're for," Petra said.

"Mm." Lior picked her paste brush back up, examined it, set it down again. "I spent twenty years believing my life was written by other people. Then I spent one night proving it wasn't." She looked up, and there was something in her eyes that was equal parts terror and the particular bright stubbornness Petra had come to love more than sleep. "But I'll admit I'm curious what Vess thought she knew."

"She also thought sealing a valley and stealing a child's memory was good governance," Petra said.

"Yes, well." Lior wiped her hands on her apron. "Nobody's all wrong. That's the whole problem with people." She nodded toward the back room, where the archive key—liberated, technically, during the events of the hill—hung on its nail. "Tomorrow?"

"Tomorrow," Petra agreed. And then, because her heart was pounding and honesty had gotten easier lately: "Lior. If the page says something terrible—if it says the Key was meant to die with the Sigil, or that one of us was always meant to—"

"Then it's storm-paper," Lior said, "and storms," she glanced out at the rain falling free and easy over Hollowmere for the first time in a century, "have recently developed opinions of their own."

* * *

That night Petra dreamed again, but differently.

She stood in the ruined village from her dreams, the unnamed one, the one no ballad remembered, and instead of raising her hand she knelt down in the mud and helped a woman pull a child from under a fallen beam. The founders' shapes stood at the edge of the dream, watching, and for the first time they had no words for her at all. One of them—Vess, she somehow knew, Vess with her ink-stained fingers—looked almost, almost like she wanted to kneel down too.

Petra woke before dawn with tears drying on her face and her hand closed around Lior's scarred palm, and outside, the rain went on falling, patient as a blank page, waiting to see what they would write on the day.
