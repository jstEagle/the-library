## 21. Names We Choose

The summons came on a Tuesday, which felt appropriate. Tuesdays were the day the council had always chosen for unpleasant things—tax assessments, road repairs, and apparently the formal restoration of a title I'd spent eleven years learning to live without.

"Lady Liora Vess-Iversen of the Storm-Crown," read the herald, in the town square, before perhaps forty people who had come mostly to see whether I would faint. "By decree of the restored concord, your name is returned to you. Your lands in the eastern hills are returned to you. Your seat upon the council awaits."

I stood there with flour on my sleeve—I'd been baking that morning, badly—and thought about how strange it was to hear my whole name spoken aloud. *Liora.* Petra called me Lior. The town called me Lior. The bookbindery's account ledger, in my own careful hand, said simply *L. Iversen, binder.* Nobody had said *Liora* since the trial, when a magistrate with cold hands had pronounced it like a diagnosis.

"Thank you," I said, because it seemed polite. "I'll need some time to consider."

The herald blinked. Heralds, I suspected, were not accustomed to being thanked and then kept waiting. "Consider?"

"Yes. Whether I want it."

A murmur went through the square. Old Tam Wicker dropped his pipe. Somewhere near the back, a child asked her mother loudly why the storm lady didn't want her storm back, and her mother shushed her with the particular ferocity of a parent who has not yet worked out the answer herself.

I walked home through streets that smelled of woodsmoke and autumn apples, and I did not feel like a lady. I felt like a woman with flour on her sleeve who needed to talk to her partner about paperwork.

* * *

Petra was in the workshop when I got back, up to her elbows in a broken hymnal, glue pot steaming beside her like a small loyal dragon. She'd learned the trade fast—faster than I had any right to expect—and she'd developed opinions about spine linings that could hold a room hostage for an hour.

"You're doing the hinge wrong," I said, hanging up my coat.

"I'm doing the hinge *innovatively.*"

"You're doing it wrong innovatively."

She looked up at me, ink on her cheekbone, that little scar of light still faded but present at her collarbone where the Sigil had lived and left, and something in my chest did its usual quiet rearrangement. A year ago I had been planning to kill this woman. Six months ago we had stood in a storm together and refused to. Now we argued about book hinges. Grief and miracle both, if you let them, become domestic eventually.

"The council offered me everything back today," I said. "Title, lands, seat. The whole inheritance."

Petra set down her bone folder with the care of someone who had learned, over a hard year, that my announcements required full attention. "And?"

"And I don't want it." I sat down on the workbench across from her, which was against at least three of my own shop rules. "I spent eleven years being nobody, Petra, and everyone acted like that was the tragedy. But the tragedy wasn't being nobody. The tragedy was that they took the choice away. They made me small so I'd fit their design." I turned my hands over, looked at the thin white scar across my palm—the key-scar, the archive-iron scar, the scar that had started every terrible thing. "If I take the title now, I'm just letting them decide again. Bigger, this time. I'd be Lady Liora Vess-Iversen, the woman who broke the valley's ban, the hero of the Concord. I'd be somebody *they* wrote."

"And what do you want to be instead?"

I had expected the question to be harder. It wasn't.

"A bookbinder," I said. "In Hollowmere. With bad taste in partners and worse taste in hymnal repairs."

"My hymnal is *fine.*"

"It's held together with hope and too much glue."

"That's also a description of us," Petra said, "and look how well we've turned out." She came around the bench and kissed me, tasting faintly of wheat paste, which should have been unromantic and somehow wasn't. When she pulled back she was smiling, but there was seriousness underneath it, the kind she wore when she was about to say something brave. "Then renounce it properly. Not by ignoring the letter. By standing up in front of everyone and saying it out loud. You spent years being erased by other people's words, Lior. Take one sentence of your own."

"One sentence," I repeated.

"Well. Maybe two. There will be formalities."

There are always formalities.

* * *

The renunciation happened three weeks later, on a bright cold morning, in the same square where the herald had made his announcement. Maren stood at the edge of the crowd—not stripped of office anymore, exactly; she had resigned before the council could finish stripping her, which everyone agreed was very like her—and watched me with an expression I had slowly learned to read as respect wearing a disguise of skepticism.

I had written my speech four times. The first draft was furious. The second was eloquent. The third was eloquent and furious, and Petra had read all three and said gently, "You're writing for the founders again. They're gone. Write for the people actually listening."

So the fourth draft was short.

"I was born Liora Vess-Iversen," I told the square, my voice carrying further than I intended, because some habits of storm-calling never quite leave the blood. "That name was taken from me by men who feared what I might choose. Today they offer it back, wrapped in honors, as if a name were a debt they were forgiving." I held up my scarred palm where the crowd could see it. "But a name isn't given. Not by councils, not by prophecies, not by crowns. It's chosen, daily, in what you do with your hands. And these hands bind books. These hands chose this valley when it wanted nothing from me but silence, and they choose it now, when it wants everything. So I return the title. I keep the name I made: Lior Iversen, of Hollowmere. Bookbinder."

Silence. Then old Tam Wicker began to clap, awkwardly, pipe clenched in his teeth, and the sound spread through the square like weather.

Maren approached afterward while the crowd dispersed. She looked older than she had a year ago—the founders' fall had taken something from her too, some scaffolding she'd leaned on so long she'd forgotten it wasn't hers—but her eyes were the same dry, level gray.

"A good speech," she said. "Short. I notice you've learned restraint."

"I had excellent teachers. Some of them tried to execute my girlfriend."

"Water under the bridge," Maren said, in the tone of a woman who knew perfectly well it was not water and not under anything, and who respected me enough not to pretend otherwise. Then, quieter: "For what it's worth, Iversen—the founders built this valley to prevent a future. You built one instead. History will be confused about which of those counts as governing." She inclined her head, exactly one degree, and left.

I stood in the square a moment longer, empty-handed, lighter than I had ever been, and then I went home to my shop.

* * *

Rebuilding the bookbindery was Petra's idea, though technically the building hadn't gone anywhere. "We're rebuilding it anyway," she insisted, standing in the middle of the workshop with her hands on her hips. "New shelves. New press. New ledger. Everything the old place was, we inherited from a life you were hiding in. Let's make furniture for the life you're choosing."

"We can't afford new shelves."

"We can afford *one* new shelf. Symbolic shelving."

So we rebuilt in increments, the way you rebuild most things. We tore out the back wall where the damp had gotten into the boards and found, behind it, a cavity containing a bird's nest, forty-one buttons, and a child's drawing of a horse done in charcoal, presumably by some previous owner's daughter a century past. Petra insisted we frame the horse. It hangs over the press now, lopsided and magnificent.

The new press arrived in November, secondhand from a binder in Corroway who was retiring, and it took the two of us and Tam Wicker's cart and an hour of creative swearing to get it through the door. That night we ate stew off the crate it came in because we hadn't yet unpacked the table.

And the ledger—we argued about the ledger for a week.

"The old one's fine," I said. "It's got six blank pages left."

"It's got six blank pages and a hundred pages of a life you were enduring. Start clean. New book, new hand, first entry your own."

"You're sentimental about stationery."

"I'm sentimental about *you,* and by extension your stationery. It's how love works."

She won, obviously. She usually does, in matters of the heart and matters of paper.

* * *

On the first of December, with snow starting past the window—real snow now, falling freely over Hollowmere the way rain had begun to that spring, the valley's ban broken and its weather finally belonging to itself—I sat down at the new desk with the new ledger open to its first page, and Petra sat across from me with tea, watching, because she claimed witnessing was also part of the ceremony.

"What do I even write?" I asked. "'Lior Iversen, binder, opens accounts'? It's not exactly poetry."

"Write what's true."

I dipped the pen. Outside, the snow came down without permission or prophecy, and inside, the stove ticked, and the framed charcoal horse presided over everything with its lopsided eyes.

*Lior Iversen & Petra — binders,* I wrote. *Hollowmere. This book contains no prophecies.*

I paused, pen hovering, and then added, because it was also true:

*It contains: one commission from the schoolhouse (readers, twelve). One repair, hymnal (finally finished correctly). One order of marbled endpapers from Corroway. One promise, ongoing.*

"What's the promise?" Petra asked, reading upside down, which she'd gotten scandalously good at.

"Classified. Ledger security."

"Lior."

"Drink your tea, Sigil-bearer."

"*Former* Sigil-bearer," she corrected, and there was no flinch in it anymore, or almost none—a scar where the wound had been, the way my palm had its own. "Retired. Emigrated. Very much a bookbinder now, thank you."

"Prove it. The Marlowe folio needs resewing and I'm not doing it alone."

She groaned, set down her cup, and rolled up her sleeves. And I looked at her—at the ink that seemed permanently resident on her left knuckle now, at the steam rising off two cups, at the blank pages waiting in their hundreds, patient and unhaunted—and I understood that this was the thing the founders had never comprehended, in all their long careful centuries of design.

They thought a life was something that happened to you, and so it must be controlled, predicted, written in advance by whoever held the pen.

But a life was a ledger. You opened it yourself. You wrote it in your own hand, one ordinary true line at a time—and the pages after the last thing anyone predicted were the ones where you finally got to live.

Outside, the snow kept falling, crossing the ridge like it owned the sky.

Inside, we got to work.
