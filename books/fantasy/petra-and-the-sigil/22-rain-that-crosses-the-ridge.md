## 22. Rain That Crosses the Ridge

**Lior**

The rain came on a Tuesday.

Lior knew this because Tuesdays were delivery days, and delivery days meant Tam Wicker arrived at the bookbindery at half past eight with his cart and his opinions, and on this particular Tuesday he arrived soaked to the skin and delighted about it.

"It's crossing the ridge," he said, dripping in her doorway like a happy dog. "Do you see it? It's *crossing the ridge*. Forty years I've lived here and it's never once—"

"I know," Lior said. "I called it."

"You did not call it."

"I absolutely did." She set down the awl she'd been using and turned her scarred palm up, as if presenting evidence. The scar had faded over the year, from angry red to a pale silver line, but it still ached on the far side of weather—except now the ache felt less like a warning and more like an old friend clearing its throat. "Small storm. Warm front off the western hills. I gave it a nudge."

Tam stared at her hand with the expression of a man who had known someone for a decade and only just learned she could juggle knives. "You're supposed to register that with the council."

"The council is three farmers and Petra's aunt, and last week they spent forty minutes arguing about whether the new well should have a roof. I think they'll survive one unregistered drizzle."

"Drizzle." He pointed out the door, where water was hammering the cobblestones hard enough to bounce. "That's not drizzle, that's a *weather event*."

"That's what I said," Lior agreed pleasantly, "and then I made it a drizzle anyway. Would you like tea? You're dripping on my floor."

He was. He took the tea. He stayed an hour longer than he needed to, because half of Hollowmere drifted past the bookbindery's windows that morning, faces tipped up, hands out, letting themselves be rained on for the first time in living memory. Old Widow Sarn stood in the square with her arms spread like a scarecrow until her daughter came to fetch her in. Children who had never seen rain ran shrieking between the puddles as though the sky had turned traitor in the best possible way.

Lior watched them through the glass and felt something in her chest that had no name yet, so she decided not to name it. Naming things was how prophecies got started.

* * *

By afternoon the storm had spent itself into a soft gray mist, the kind that didn't fall so much as hang, and Lior closed the shop early—which was to say, she flipped the sign, left the door unlocked, and went upstairs to find Petra asleep on the settee with an open book sliding off her chest.

This was another thing the year had taught her: Petra slept anywhere. In chairs, in doorways, once standing upright against the doorframe of the archive with her arms crossed, which Lior had initially mistaken for a very committed form of disapproval. The Sigil-bearer, terror of the founders, scourge of prophecy, champion napper.

Lior eased the book from her fingers—a treatise on drainage ditches, of all things; Petra read everything now with the hunger of someone who'd spent years being told what to think—and Petra opened one eye.

"Is it still raining?"

"Misting. It's being shy."

"Tell it I'm proud of it." Petra sat up, hair flattened on one side, and Lior's heart did the small stupid thing it always did, the thing she had stopped fighting sometime around midwinter. "The whole town's been talking about nothing else since dawn. Aunt Hesta says the harvest will come in early. Tam says you owe him a registered storm report."

"Tam can file his report with the well committee."

Petra laughed, and outside, absurdly, the mist thickened for a moment, a fat drop striking the window like punctuation. They both looked at it.

"It does that when I'm happy," Lior admitted. She'd never quite said it out loud before. "Or angry. Mostly happy, lately. The valley's learning my moods. I'm going to be insufferable by autumn."

"Impossible. You're already insufferable."

"Flatterer."

Petra caught her hand—the right one, the scarred one, always the right one, though the gesture had long ago stopped being about the scar—and pressed her thumb along the silver line. A year ago that touch would have sent lightning across three counties. Now it just sent warmth up Lior's arm, which was better, if she was honest. Lightning had never once made her feel safe.

"Walk with me?" Petra said. "Before it stops. I want to stand under actual sky-rain while it lasts. Who knows how long your mood holds."

"My moods are famously durable."

"Your moods once flooded the tannery because someone spoiled the ending of a serial novel you were following."

"That was a *justified* flood, and the tanner apologized."

They went out into the soft gray afternoon together.

* * *

**Petra**

The ridge line looked different in rain.

For a century it had been a wall—you could see it from anywhere in the valley, that pale seam where the world simply stopped, where weather piled up against nothing and slid away. Petra had dreamed of it for years before she ever saw it: a border drawn in absence, magic dying at the crest like breath failing at the lips. She'd followed that dream down out of the mountains and into Hollowmere expecting a prison.

It wasn't a prison anymore. It was just hills. Green, wet, ordinary hills, with sheep on them, and today—with rain falling freely over their shoulders, all the way up, all the way over—the ridge was the least remarkable thing in the world.

Petra found she loved it most for that.

"Lior," she said, as they climbed the shepherd's path, mud sucking at their boots. "Can I tell you something embarrassing?"

"You've told me many embarrassing things. The time you introduced yourself to the miller as 'the Sigil-bearer' by accident. The time you cried at a drainage ditch."

"The ditch was *beautifully engineered*, and this is different." She watched her feet pick their way over a wet stone. "When I first came here—before I met you, before any of it—I used to stand at the edge of town at night and try to make myself turn back. Go home. Whatever home was. Because I knew something was wrong with me, something the dreams wanted, and I thought maybe if I never found what the dreams were pointing at, they'd stop."

"And instead you found me."

"And instead I found you." She smiled despite herself. "Worst possible outcome, according to every dead founder who ever whispered in my ear. Best one, according to me. But here's the embarrassing part: some nights, even now, I miss having a purpose handed to me. Isn't that awful? A year of freedom and part of me still wakes up waiting for instructions."

Lior stopped walking. Rain misted her dark hair flat; her cheeks were pink from the climb; she looked, Petra thought, like a woman entirely unprophesied, which was the most beautiful thing anyone in this valley could look.

"Lior Iversen," Petra said solemnly, "spent eleven years building a life out of small deceptions and book glue because the alternative was being a weapon. If *you're* allowed to miss the shape of a cage now and then, I get to miss mine too."

"That's—" Lior blinked. "That's actually very wise. I was going to say something supportive but less structured."

"I contain multitudes. Also I've been reading."

"You've been reading *drainage manuals*."

"Wisdom is where you find it." Petra held out her hand, and Lior took it, and they crested the ridge together.

Below them, Hollowmere lay in the rain: slate roofs darkened to blue, chimney smoke bent sideways, lamplight already blooming gold in the early dusk. Somewhere down there, Tam Wicker was filing an unauthorized weather report. Somewhere down there, Maren kept a small house at the valley's edge and grew vegetables and did not, as far as anyone knew, scheme—though Petra still checked, sometimes, out of habit, and hated herself a little for the habit, and was working on it.

And somewhere down there was the bookbindery, with its crooked stairs and its smell of leather and paste, and the ledger on the counter that they were writing together. Not a ledger of debts or names or fates. Just a record. What sold. What needed mending. What the weather did, day by day, now that the weather was theirs.

"Do you remember," Petra said, "when we stood here and the boundary burned me back?"

"I remember carrying you downhill while you swore in a language I'm fairly sure you invented."

"The dead founders taught it to me. It means several rude things about wardens." She squeezed Lior's hand. "I keep thinking—I couldn't leave. The Sigil couldn't leave. And now there's no Sigil, or there's only me, and I could walk off this ridge tomorrow and nothing would stop me. Nothing would burn. Nothing would even notice."

"But?"

"But I don't want to." She said it plainly, watching the valley breathe under the rain. "I spent my whole life wanting to arrive somewhere. Turns out I already had. I just hadn't unpacked."

Lior was quiet for a moment. When Petra glanced over, she was doing the thing she did when she was moved and wished not to be—pressing her lips together, examining the horizon as though it owed her money.

"For the record," Lior said finally, "the first time I stood on this ridge after the storm, I tried to call lightning just to prove I could. Nothing happened. I panicked for two days thinking I'd broken myself."

"And then?"

"And then it rained on the tannery again, and I realized power isn't a muscle you flex. It's more like—" she gestured vaguely at the mist, the hills, the sheep regarding them with damp indifference, "—a neighbor. You don't command it. You just live next to it long enough that it starts borrowing your tools."

"That's the least romantic description of magic I've ever heard."

"I'm a bookbinder. We describe things structurally." Lior turned to face her fully, rain beading on her lashes, and whatever she was about to say next, Petra could see it gathering like weather. "Petra. A year ago I chose you over a prophecy, over the founders, over the entire architecture of my own exile. And I'd do it again. But I want you to know that's not why I stay."

"No?"

"No. I stay because you cry at ditches. Because you sleep sitting up like a gargoyle. Because you learned bookbinding badly and insist on doing the difficult volumes anyway." Her voice roughened slightly. "Because you're not the Sigil, and you never were, and neither am I the Key, and the page that said otherwise burned to blank in my hands, and every morning since has been *unwritten*, and I find that I love it. The unwritten part. I love you inside it."

The rain, obligingly, thickened around them—not storming, just falling harder, a curtain of gray-gold light, as though the sky had leaned in to listen.

Petra kissed her. On the ridge line, in the open rain, where for a hundred years no weather had dared to cross and no love had dared to matter. It was, she thought distantly, exactly the kind of scene a prophecy would have loved to claim credit for. Let it try. There was no book left to write it down in.

* * *

**Lior**

They walked home in the blue hour, soaked through and unbothered, and the town was lit and loud with the novelty of wet streets. Someone had built a bonfire in the square—of course someone had; Hollowmere would celebrate anything with a bonfire—and people were dancing badly around it, and Aunt Hesta had commandeered a table and was selling mulled cider at a markup she described as "festive."

They did not go to the bonfire. They had been the center of enough significance to last several lifetimes. Instead they went home, and dried off, and ate bread and cheese and the last of the plum preserves, and then Petra fell asleep on the settee again within the hour, mid-sentence, one hand still resting on the drainage manual like a scholar at rest.

Lior banked the fire and went downstairs to the shop.

She hadn't planned to work. But the lamp was there, and the quiet was there, and the ledger lay open on the counter where they'd left it, and beneath the day's entries—one repair, a family Bible, spine cracked with grief; two sales; weather: rain, free, first—there was space.

Blank space.

A year ago, blankness had terrified her. Blank pages were where prophecies lived, waiting to be filled by other people's hands. She had spent her whole life believing the future was a book someone else had written, and her only choices were to play her part or die trying not to.

Now she sat with the pen hovering, and understood, at last, what the blankness was.

It wasn't empty. It was *hers*.

She wrote, in her careful binder's hand:

*Rain crossed the ridge today. Everyone came out to meet it. Nobody asked what it meant.*

She considered adding more—some grand summation, some closing line—but that was the old instinct talking, the instinct that believed stories needed endings. Stories didn't need endings. Stories needed tomorrow. She capped the ink, closed the ledger, and left the rest of the page alone.

Above her, the floor creaked—Petra, awake, coming down the stairs with her hair wild and her eyes half-shut, drawn by the lamplight the way she always was.

"You're writing in the dark like a ghost," Petra murmured, wrapping her arms around Lior from behind, chin hooking over her shoulder. "What did you write?"

"Weather report."

"Liar."

"Bookbinder," Lior corrected. "There's a difference."

Outside, softly, the rain went on falling—over the ridge, over the hills, over the town, over the two of them standing in a lamplit doorway with an unwritten page behind them and an ordinary, impossible morning ahead.

It showed no sign of stopping. Neither, Lior thought, were they.
