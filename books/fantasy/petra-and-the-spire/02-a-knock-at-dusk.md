## 2. A Knock at Dusk

The storm came up the valley like something with a grudge.

Felix had watched it build all day from the herb terrace — a bruise-colored wall stacking itself against the western passes, swallowing the peaks one by one, and they had done what they always did when the sky turned ugly: brought the goats in early, latched the shutters, banked the fire, and told themselves the same lie they had been telling themselves for six years, which was that nothing in this valley could touch them.

That was the whole point of the valley. That was why Felix had chosen it, or rather, had walked until their legs gave out and found that the walking had chosen for them. Here, past the ridgeline, magic died. A spell spoken in the lowlands arrived at the border of the hollow like a bird striking glass — it simply stopped, folded, fell. Felix had tested it in those first raw months, half-hoping, half-dreading: little cantrips, a light-gathering charm, the small domestic workings Maren had drilled into them until their hands ached. All of it guttered out at the ridge as though snuffed between wet fingers. Even the old habits of thought went strange here, like trying to remember a dream in another language. The valley forgot magic, and in exchange, magic forgot Felix.

It was, they had told themselves, a fair trade.

So when the storm broke over the cottage at dusk — rain like flung gravel, wind worrying the chimney into a low moan — Felix did what any sensible herder did. They sat by the fire with a bowl of barley soup and a mending basket, and they listened to the world rage at walls that had stood through forty years of worse, and they did not think about anything at all.

The knock came at full dark.

Three strikes, uneven, small — more like branches thrown by the wind than knuckles, except that branches do not pause between blows, and branches do not come again when you do not answer. Felix set down the soup. The fire popped. Outside, the rain hammered the turf roof with a sound like a thousand small fists, and beneath it, faint but unmistakable, came a fourth knock. Then a fifth. Then a thin, wavering sound that was either crying or singing or both.

"Go away," Felix said, to no one, knowing already that they were going to open the door. They hated themselves a little for how quickly they knew it. Six years of careful erasure, of being no one to anyone, of a life sanded smooth of every attachment that could be pried loose or burned — and some soft-bellied animal part of them still heard a child's voice in a storm and got up off its stool.

They unbarred the door and the night fell inward.

* * *

The child on the doorstep was so caked in mud that for a moment Felix genuinely could not tell if it was a child at all. It might have been a root-wad, a bog-thing, one of the peat-shapes children dared each other to dig up. Then it blinked, and two eyes looked out of the mud — gray eyes, startlingly pale, the color of the sky over ice — and Felix's stomach dropped through the floorboards, because those eyes were not afraid.

Everything else about the child said *afraid*. She was shaking so hard her teeth chattered. Her lips were blue-gray. Mud had dried in layers on her face and hands, cracked like drought-earth, and beneath the mud she was scraped and thorn-torn from head to foot. She could not have been more than eight or nine years old. She was alone, at dusk, in a killing storm, on the doorstep of a stranger in a valley that appeared on no map Felix had ever seen.

And she was not afraid. She looked up at Felix the way a traveler looks at a milestone — noting it, measuring it, moving on. There was something almost insulting in it.

"You're the one who listens," the child said.

The words were in no language Felix knew, and yet Felix understood them perfectly, the way one understands a blow to the sternum. Perfectly, immediately, without translation, without the small lag of meaning arriving — the words simply *were* understood, already inside Felix's head before the child's mouth finished shaping them. The sound of them was wrong, too: too resonant for such a small chest, each syllable carrying an undertone like a bell struck under water.

Felix's hand found the doorframe and gripped it.

"What did you say?"

The child swayed. Whatever else she was, she was also, plainly, at the end of her strength; Felix watched the fearlessness flicker and gutter now, overtaken at last by the body's blunt arithmetic. Her knees buckled. Felix caught her — mud and cold and bone-thin ribs under a coat that had been fine once, long ago, cut for someone much taller — and the child looked up at them from the crook of their arm and said, in that impossible ringing undertone, "I looked for the listening one. The others stopped hearing. You didn't."

Then her eyes rolled back, and she fainted, and Felix stood in the doorway holding sixty pounds of frozen mud while the storm tried to take the roof off, and made the second-worst decision of their life by carrying her inside.

(The worst decision, Felix would think later, had been made years ago, on a different night, in a different doorway. But that is later.)

* * *

Getting the mud off took an hour and most of the hot water.

The child never woke, not fully. She surfaced in shallow increments whenever Felix peeled a layer of crusted clothing away or touched a wound, mumbling fragments in that resonant tongue — never modern speech, never the valley dialect, never anything Felix could have named — before sinking again. Felix worked in grim silence, cataloguing as they went, because cataloguing was what hedge-witches did instead of panicking: scrapes, mostly. Thorn cuts. One deep gash along the calf, cleaned and stitched with waxed thread. Bruises in patterns that suggested falls rather than blows. No burns. No binding-marks. No brand of any order or guild that Felix recognized — and Felix, despite six years of pretending otherwise, recognized quite a lot of brands.

Underneath everything, the child was just a child. Thin as a winter wren. Calluses on her palms that spoke of stone and rope rather than farm work. And her feet — Felix paused over these a long time. They were hard as hooves, the soles thickened into something halfway to leather, as though she had walked a very great distance over very bad ground. From where? The nearest village was two days' walk down-valley, and no child walked *into* the hollow. Nothing walked into the hollow. The hollow was where things stopped.

Felix dressed her in a spare shift, put her in the box bed by the fire, and sat back on their heels and looked at her and felt the familiar walls of their life pressing in, closer than usual, the way walls press when something has knocked on them.

"No," Felix said aloud, to the cottage, to the storm, to the general arrangement of the universe. "No. I'm not doing this again."

The universe, as usual, declined to negotiate. The child slept on, breathing in slow, even pulls, color creeping back into her lips, and outside the rain kept up its drumming, and Felix sat in their chair with their cold soup and told themselves firmly that in the morning they would feed her, point her down-valley toward Bram's village and whatever passed for authority there, and wash their hands of the entire affair.

It was a good plan. It was a sensible plan. It was exactly the kind of plan the person Felix had spent six years building themselves into would make.

Then the candles bowed.

Felix noticed it first as a change in the light — a softening, a lean, the way a room dims when a cloud crosses the sun. But there was no cloud; there was only storm-dark and firelight, and both flames in the room — the tallow candle on the table and the rushlight by the bed — had bent sideways, together, in the same direction, toward the sleeping child, like flowers turning after a sun that wasn't there. They held that bow for three heartbeats. Four. Five. The flames stretched, elongated, went thin and tall and strangely silent, and the shadows in the room swung around them like compass needles finding north.

Felix did not move. Six years of stillness served them well; fear wanted them bolt upright, wanted them running, and they sat instead with their hands flat on their knees and watched, because watching was the other thing hedge-witches did instead of panicking, and because some cold, buried, unforgivably alive part of them was *fascinated*.

There was no magic in the valley. That was the law of the place, older than any writ, older than memory. Spells died at the ridgeline. Charms guttered. Wards unraveled. Felix had staked six years of survival on that law, had hidden behind it like a wall.

And here was a sleeping child making candle flames kneel.

The flames held their bow for the length of ten slow breaths. Then, gently, without any snap or flare, they straightened, and went on burning as though nothing had happened, and the shadows settled back into their ordinary places, and the storm filled the silence the way water fills a footprint.

In the box bed, the child murmured something in her sleep. Just a fragment — two syllables, maybe three — but the sound of it went through the cottage like a finger drawn around the rim of a glass, and for a moment Felix heard it twice: once with their ears, and once somewhere behind the breastbone, in a register that had no business existing in a body that had renounced such things.

Felix got up, very calmly, and checked the door bar. Then the shutter latches. Then, because ritual is a comfort to the frightened, the loose board under the floor by the hearth, where a bundle of stolen pages had slept wrapped in oilcloth for six years, untouched, unread, unburned — pages Felix had sworn a hundred times to destroy and a hundred times had failed to, because destroying them would have meant admitting what they were.

Felix did not unwrap them now. They just knelt there with their hand flat on the boards, feeling the grain of the wood, feeling their own heartbeat, and listened to the storm, and to the child's breathing, and to the small terrible quiet between the two.

"She's just a child," Felix said to the fire. "Children have nightmares. Nightmares don't mean anything. Wind does strange things to flames."

The fire crackled noncommittally. It was, Felix reflected, a poor liar even for a fire, and it had learned its dishonesty from them.

They took the blanket off their own bed and added it to the child's, and then sat up all night in the chair by the hearth with a knife across their knees, telling themselves it was for defense, knowing perfectly well that they were keeping watch — that some instinct older than their caution had appointed them sentry over this small, mud-caked, impossible stranger, and that the appointment had been made without consulting them at all.

* * *

Dawn came gray and dripping. The storm had blown itself out against the mountains sometime in the small hours, leaving the valley rinsed and steaming, every stone shining, the air scrubbed down to its bones. Felix stepped out into it stiff-necked and red-eyed, fed the goats, milked, let the hens out, and performed each chore with the deliberate slowness of someone postponing a conversation.

When they finally went back inside, the child was awake.

She was sitting up in the box bed with the blankets pooled around her, and she had found the wooden comb on the shelf beside her and was working it through her hair with the patient thoroughness of someone who has clearly done this in worse conditions than these. Her hair, freed of mud, was a plain brown, cropped short and unevenly, in the style of someone who had cut it herself with a knife. Up close, in daylight, she was even thinner than she'd seemed — wrists like bundle-twine, collarbones sharp enough to hang washing on — and there was a smudge of last night's mud still in the crease of her ear that she had missed.

She looked at Felix. Felix looked at her. The fire ticked.

"You stayed," the child said. Again that double hearing — the ears catching sounds like bells rung underwater, the mind receiving plain sense. "I dreamed you'd be gone. Dreams are usually right."

"I live here," Felix said. Their voice came out rougher than intended. "Where do *you* live? Where did you walk from? Who gave you that coat? Who taught you—" They stopped, reined themselves in. Questions were a net with too many holes; ask them all at once and everything slipped through. They started over, slowly, the way one speaks to a spooked animal. "What's your name?"

The child considered this. Not the way children usually consider — shyly, or slyly — but with a grave, weighing attention, as though the question were a stone she was testing for a bridge.

"Petra," she said at last. And then, with the air of someone adding a necessary footnote: "That's the short version."

"The short version," Felix repeated.

"Names are long where I'm from." She said it simply, without mystery-mongering, which somehow made it worse. Her eyes — those pale, unafraid, ice-sky eyes — moved around the cottage with frank curiosity: the herbs hanging from the rafters, the goat-milk strainer, the mending basket, the knife still lying across the chair where Felix had forgotten it. She looked at the knife without alarm, catalogued it, dismissed it. "You have a garden that talks to you."

"It doesn't talk to me."

"It hums. When you touch it." She said this the way another child might say *your shirt is untucked*. "You can't hear it?"

Felix could not hear it. Felix had not heard anything hum in six years, by design, by geography, by the mercy of a valley that forgot. They crossed to the window and looked out at the herb terrace, at the rows of sage and feverfew and valerian beaded with rain, utterly ordinary, utterly mute, and felt the ground of their life shift by a degree so slight that no one watching would have noticed — but they felt it, the way one feels a house settle.

"Are you hungry?" they asked, because it was the only question they trusted themselves to answer honestly.

"Yes," said Petra, with the first ordinary childish thing Felix had seen her do: she perked up, entirely, instantly, like a plant given water.

* * *

She ate four eggs, half a loaf, a bowl of curds, and the heel of the cheese Felix had been saving, in that order, with a focus that precluded conversation. Felix watched her eat and revised their estimate of how far she had walked upward, considerably. Nobody ate like that who hadn't earned it. Between mouthfuls she studied Felix with the same frank attention, and Felix, unused to being studied, found themselves narrating their own actions like a fool — *I'm just checking the kettle, I'm just feeding the fire* — until they caught themselves and stopped.

When the food was gone, Petra pushed the bowl away, sighed with deep satisfaction, and said, "You smell like someone who used to know words."

Felix's hands went still on the table.

"I don't know what you mean."

"You do." No malice in it. No accusation either. Just that unbearable plainness, that traveler-looking-at-a-milestone gaze. "But it's all right. This place makes people quiet. That's why I could find you. Everyone else had stopped listening, but you—" she touched her own sternum, mirroring, unconsciously or not, the gesture Felix had made last night at the floorboards, "—you still had a door in you. Rusty. But a door."

Outside, a goat bleated. Inside, Felix discovered that their heart was going fast and hard, and that six years of practiced calm were worth approximately nothing against a nine-year-old with soup on her chin.

"Petra." Felix made their voice flat, practical, safe. "Last night. Before you slept. What you said to me — *the one who listens*. And the words you say in your sleep. What language is that?"

For the first time since the doorstep, something moved behind the child's eyes that was not hunger and not fearlessness. It took Felix a moment to name it, and when they did, their stomach clenched: it was loneliness. Old loneliness, worn smooth, the kind that stops looking like sadness and starts looking like weather.

"It's not a language," Petra said. "It's the language. The first one. The one everything answered to, before everyone stopped answering." She picked at a thread on the blanket. "I know four hundred and eleven words. I'm supposed to deliver all of them. But nobody out here knows how to receive."

She looked up, and the morning light caught her pale eyes, and she smiled — a small, tired, devastating smile — and said the phrase. The one from her sleep, the one that had bent the candles. Two syllables, three, spoken softly, in daylight, with no power behind them that Felix could detect.

The fire went out.

Not guttered — *stopped*, mid-flame, a cone of smoke rising straight and unwavering from dead coals, and the kettle's steam hung motionless above the spout for one full, impossible second before the world remembered itself and the fire coughed back to life and the steam scattered and the cottage was just a cottage again, smelling of woodsmoke and goat and eggs.

Felix sat very still.

"That one," Petra said apologetically, "isn't a question. I should have saved it. The questions are safer."

Felix rose, walked to the shelf, and took down the oilcloth bundle from behind the loose board — six years of *no*, six years of *never again*, six years of a life built on the promise that they would never need these pages, never look at them, never hear that resonance again — and set it on the table between the soup bowls and the cheese rind with hands that were not entirely steady.

Petra looked at the bundle. Then at Felix. Her pale eyes widened very slightly, and she leaned forward, and said, in the tone of someone greeting a long-lost relative:

"Oh. You *do* have some."

Outside, far down the valley, past the village, past the ridgeline where spells went to die, something answered her — so faintly that Felix would spend days convincing themselves they had imagined it: a single note, deep under the wind, like a bell struck at the bottom of the world.

Petra's head came up. She went absolutely still, the way animals go still, and her eyes shone.

"It heard me," she whispered. "It's been waiting so long."
