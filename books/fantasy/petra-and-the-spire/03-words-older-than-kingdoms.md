## 3. Words Older Than Kingdoms

The morning after the storm, Felix woke to the smell of wet ash and the memory of a word.

That was the trouble with a mind trained as theirs had been—trained, then starved, then buried alive under seven years of goat dung and chamomile—it did not forget. It only waited. All night the child's syllables had turned over in Felix's skull like river stones, smooth and cold and older than anything the river had ever carried, and when Felix finally opened their eyes to the gray light seeping through the shutters, the first thought was not *who is this child* or *what do I do with her* but simply: *I know that cadence.*

They lay still beneath the wool blanket and listened. In the loft above, Petra slept on. The house was quiet except for the tick of the hearthstone cooling and the small industrious sounds of mice in the wall, who had learned long ago that Felix was no threat to anyone.

*I know that cadence.*

Felix rose carefully, dressed in the dark, and stood at the foot of the ladder that led to the loft, one hand on the rail, looking up into the dimness where a small shape lay curled in the hay like something washed ashore. The candle stub on the mantel—the one Petra's dreams had bent last night, flames bowing toward her like courtiers toward a passing queen—sat unlit now, and Felix found they were reluctant to light it.

Instead they went out into the wet morning to do what they always did when the world became unbearable: they fed the goats, and then they went to the chest at the back of the pantry, moved the dried yarrow and the false bottom, and took out the pages they had stolen eleven years ago and sworn never to read again.

* * *

There were nine of them. Nine leaves of parchment, brittle at the edges, water-stained, copied in Maren's narrow slanted hand during the years before the falling-out—before the night Felix had walked out of the tower with nothing but a satchel and a wound that had never quite closed. Maren had called them *exercises*. She had said, in her dry way, *if you're going to snoop through my library, at least transcribe accurately*, which was her manner of admitting she'd caught them and choosing not to care.

Felix had never asked her what the exercises were *for*. That was the cowardice at the center of everything, the rotten beam their whole life here was built upon: they had seen the shape of the thing and declined to look at its face. The pages were full of words in no alphabet Felix had ever been taught—characters like lightning strikes frozen mid-fall, like bird tracks in snow, like the cracks in old glaze—and beside each one, in Maren's hand, phonetic renderings, breath-marks, notes on stress and duration. And in the margins, occasionally, a single modern-tongue gloss, always hedged, always qualified:

*approx. "to open" — but not as a door opens; as a wound opens*

*no true equivalent — closest is "to remember forward"*

*DO NOT SPEAK ALOUD (see p. 41 of the sealed commentary)*

Felix had been nineteen and furious and in love with the idea of power the way only the powerless can be, and they had copied these pages because they were forbidden, and they had fled before ever understanding them, and they had spent eleven years telling themselves the story that mattered was the quarrel, the betrayal, the door slammed behind them. Not the pages. Never the pages.

Now they spread the nine leaves across the kitchen table in the gray light, weighted at the corners with the salt dish, a spoon, a river stone, and a jar of honey, and waited for the child to wake.

She woke hungry, of course. Everything about her was hungry—her cheeks still too sharp beneath the storm-washed grime, her wrists like kindling—but she came down the ladder with none of the wariness Felix expected from a starving lost child. She surveyed the kitchen, the table, the nine strange pages laid out like a tarot spread, and her eyes went wide and fixed, and she made a sound low in her throat that was not surprise exactly. Recognition, maybe. Or homesickness.

"You know these," Felix said. Their voice came out rougher than intended. They cleared their throat. "These marks. You've seen them before."

Petra looked up at them. Her eyes were an unsettling color—not quite brown, not quite amber, but the color of lamplight through honey, and there was something in them that did not move the way a child's eyes should move. Too still. Too patient. As if someone very old were looking out through a very new face.

Then she pointed one grimy finger at the third page—at a cluster of characters like a lightning strike frozen mid-fall—and spoke.

The sound filled the kitchen the way water fills a cup. Not loud. Felix had heard hedge-cant and chapel-latin and the market-babble of three kingdoms' worth of traders, and none of it prepared them for this: each syllable landed complete, rounded, *finished*, as though language before this one had all been sketches and this was the first drawing done in ink. The fire in the hearth, banked to embers, flared once, gently, like a sleeper turning over.

Felix's hands were shaking. They pressed them flat against the tabletop and made themselves look at the page, at Maren's phonetic rendering beneath the characters, and matched the sounds—the breath-mark here, the stress there, the long falling vowel—

It matched. Syllable for syllable, stress for stress, the child's speech sat down inside Maren's transcription like a key into a lock that had been waiting eleven years in a drawer.

"That's—" Felix stopped. Started again. Made themselves be the scholar, because the scholar was a safe place to stand, a room with walls, and everything else in them was screaming. "That's the exercise on the third leaf. The one Maren glossed as—to remember forward." They looked at Petra. "Do you know what it means? What you just said?"

Petra tilted her head. And answered—in the same tongue, at length, a whole flowing sentence of it, her hands moving too, shaping things in the air, and Felix understood none of it and understood all of it, the way you understand music without knowing the score. Then she stopped, and seemed to grasp the failure between them, and her face did something complicated—young and old at once, frustrated and forgiving—and she tried again in the market tongue, halting, accented, words picked up who-knew-where:

"Old. Before." She frowned, hunting. "Before the—before putting-to-sleep." Her hands made a shape in the air, two palms pressing down, pressing down, pressing down. "Words from before the sleeping."

"The sealing," Felix whispered. "You're saying your language comes from before the sealing."

And the scholar in them leaned forward, hungry, delighted, taking notes even now, some detached corner of the mind crying *do you understand what this IS, this is the First Tongue, this is the speech the old commentaries say the Spire itself dreamed in, this is worth more than every library in the capital*—while underneath that bright scholarly voice, deeper, in the cellar of them, another voice said quietly: *and the Order of the Ashen Hand burns unlicensed speakers, and this child speaks the oldest licensed-and-forbidden speech there is, and she appeared at your door in a storm, and nothing, nothing, nothing comes to a valley that forgets by accident.*

* * *

They spent the day at it, and it was the best day Felix had had in eleven years, and they hated themselves for it.

That was the treachery of fascination: it felt exactly like joy. Felix sat at the table with the nine leaves and a fresh sheet of paper, cross-referencing, testing, and Petra sat across from them eating bread and honey with the steady concentration of someone who had known real hunger, and every hour yielded more. The character like bird-tracks meant *to listen*—not with ears; the gloss Maren had left said *with the place behind the ears*, whatever that meant—and when Petra said it, Felix felt it as a faint pressure, a fingertip touched lightly to the inside of the skull. The character like cracked glaze meant *to hold*—but again not as hands hold; the marginal note, in Maren's smaller, tenser script, said *as the earth holds a seed it has not yet decided about*.

"Where did you learn these?" Felix asked, for perhaps the tenth time, in perhaps the tenth way. "Who taught you? Your mother? Your village? There are no villages that speak this. There are no *people*—"

Petra considered the question with the seriousness she brought to everything, chewing, swallowing, wiping her mouth with the back of her hand.

"Beneath," she said at last. "Down where it hums." She pointed at the floor, then, after thought, at the distant eastern ridge, beyond which—though she could not know it, though no map showed it—lay the mountains, and the crater, and the Spire. "The voices raised me. They gave me words the way you give bread." She broke a piece off the loaf as if to demonstrate, held it out. "But they could not come out. So I came out."

"Came out carrying what?"

She looked at Felix for a long moment. And then she set down the bread, and folded her hands in her lap like a small solemn official, and said the phrase.

Felix would come to know it intimately in the weeks ahead—would hear it in sleep and waking, would transcribe it in three scripts and dream in its cadences—but this first full hearing never left them. Four phrases, really, a sentence in four movements, and Petra delivered it not as speech but as *office*, the way a herald delivers a proclamation, the way a priest delivers a rite, with her spine straight and her strange honey-colored eyes fixed on something past Felix, past the wall, past the world:

The first movement fell like a stone into deep water—*to witness*, Felix wrote later, trembling, *the first word means to witness, to see fully, without turning away*. The second rose like heat off summer stone, and the hearth embers brightened though no one fed them. The third was soft, almost tender, a mother-word, a lullaby-word, and the candle on the mantel—they had lit it by then, they had to have light for the work—bowed toward the child like a flower toward sun. And the fourth—

The fourth was quiet. That was the terrible part. After all that building, the fourth phrase was barely a breath, and it landed in the kitchen like the last grain of sand that tips the scale, and every hair on Felix's arms stood up, and outside, the goats—stupid, safe, mundane goats—began to scream.

Not bleat. *Scream.* Both of them, hurling themselves against the fence, eyes rolling white, and somewhere down the valley a dog took up the cry, and then another, and the sound rolled away down the valley like a stone skipping across still water, farm to farm, fading, and then silence, the particular ringing silence that follows panic.

Petra sat with her hands folded, watching Felix with those too-still eyes, and finished, in her careful market tongue:

"I am to say it to someone. Someone who still listens." A pause. "I have been saying it a long time."

"How long?" Felix's voice was a ruin.

"Since before the storm." She counted on her fingers, lost track, frowned. "A long time. No one hears. People hear the words after me"—she tapped her own chest—"but not the words *in* me. You—" and here her face did the young-old thing again, and she pointed one finger at Felix's sternum, with what felt unnervingly like accuracy, "—you heard a little. Last night. In your sleep. You said a piece of it back."

Felix had not known that. Had not known they talked in their sleep, or what they might say in it. The cellar-voice in them said: *you dreamed in it. You dreamed in the First Tongue. Eleven years buried and one night under this roof and it's in your dreams already, growing, like something planted.*

"What does it mean?" Felix asked. "The whole thing. The four parts. What are you saying, when you say it?"

Petra looked genuinely puzzled, as if the question had never occurred to her, as if asking what the phrase *meant* were like asking what bread *tasted* of to someone who had only ever been the loaf.

"It is not a saying," she said. "It is a knocking." She rapped her knuckles on the table, twice, softly. "From inside."

* * *

Night came early, the way it did in autumn, and Felix did not sleep.

They sat at the table with the nine leaves and their notes and a candle burned down to a thumb's width, and they told themselves they were being rigorous, methodical, scholarly—and the lie was so thin they could see the candle flame right through it. Because here was what the scholar's eye had found, the thing they kept circling and refusing to land on:

Maren's marginalia changed tone across the nine leaves. On the first leaf, the notes were curious, open, a teacher's delight. By the fourth, they were shorter, clipped. By the seventh, Maren had begun writing in the margins not glosses but *warnings*, and by the ninth leaf the handwriting had gone small and hurried, and there was a single line at the bottom, pressed hard enough into the parchment to emboss the wood of the table beneath, in a phrase Felix had read a hundred times over eleven years without ever once understanding until tonight:

*If one comes speaking it whole, do not answer. Do not answer in kind. Whatever you do, DO NOT ANSWER.*

Below that, scratched out so violently the parchment had thinned nearly to lace, another line survived only in fragments:

*...thirteen... ...lives... ...the valley must forget...*

Felix sat with the candle guttering and read it again, and again, and the cellar-voice was no longer quiet, was standing at the bottom of the stairs now, saying plainly what the scholar had been hired to talk over:

*A child comes out of a storm to a valley that forgets, speaking the language of before the sealing, repeating a message like a knock from inside a locked room. And your mentor—who helped build the lock, whose handwriting shakes on the ninth leaf—left you a warning written in fear: do not answer.*

And Felix, who had spent eleven years perfecting the art of not answering—not Maren, not the past, not the sealed commentary, not the question of what they'd run from—looked up through the dark at the loft where the child slept, and saw, in the blackness, the faint glow of the banked hearth bending, gently, patiently, toward her, like a listener leaning in.

The candle went out. Not blown. *Bowed out*, the flame folding toward the loft like a courtier, like a flower, like a kneeling thing.

In the dark, Felix heard the child turn over in her sleep, and murmur—softly, faithfully, alarmingly—the first movement of the phrase.

And far off, past the ridge, past the passes, so faint it might have been wind, might have been blood in the ears, might have been the valley itself shifting in a long uneasy sleep—something knocked back.
