## 4. The Mentor's Silence

The letter took Felix three days to write and eleven days to decide to send.

It began, in those first drafts, as an apology. *Maren, I am sorry. I was young, and afraid, and I did not understand what you were asking of me.* It ended, in the final draft, with none of that. What remained was a single page of careful, clerkish handwriting — the hand Felix had been taught at Maren's own desk, ink-brown and precise — containing only a question.

*I have heard words spoken that should not exist anymore. Words from before the sealing. If you know what they mean, tell me. If you do not, tell me that instead. Either answer is better than silence.*

Felix read it over by lamplight on the third night, sitting at the kitchen table while Petra slept in the loft above, and hated every line of it. Not because it was badly written. Because it was honest. Honesty, in Felix's experience, was a door that swung both ways: once you opened it toward someone, you could not stop them walking through it back toward you. And Maren Halloran — no relation, though the shared surname had once made them laugh — had spent twenty years with every right to walk through any door she pleased and find Felix behind it.

*You abandoned her,* said the small voice that lived under Felix's ribs, the one that sounded suspiciously like Maren herself. *You let her think you were dead rather than face what she asked of you. And now you want a favor.*

*I don't want a favor,* Felix told the voice. *I want to keep a child alive.*

The voice had no answer for that, which was its own kind of concession.

* * *

The valley had one post, and the post was a man named Hobb, who drove a cart down the southern pass twice a season when the weather allowed and carried letters out in a oilcloth satchel that smelled of tallow and onions. There was no post coming *in*, not really. Letters arrived for the valley perhaps four times a year, addressed mostly to the miller, the elder, and Odile — though Odile's letters, Felix suspected, were fewer than they appeared, since half the village believed Odile could not read and Odile had never troubled to correct them.

Felix walked the letter down to Hobb's yard on a gray morning with frost still silvering the fence rails, and stood there holding it so long that Hobb finally leaned out of his barn and said, "You planning to eat that or send it?"

"Send it."

"Then give it here before the dew gets it." Hobb wiped his hands on his apron and took the letter, turning it over. "No name on the outside."

"It's better without one."

"Mm." Hobb squinted at him with the mild, terrible shrewdness of a man who had driven the same road for forty years and missed nothing on it. "Going far?"

"To the address inside."

"That's not what I asked." He tucked the letter into the satchel anyway, between a wheel of cheese and a bundle of invoices, and patted it once as if settling a hen onto eggs. "Letters go where they go. But a man ought to know if he's mailing something or mailing himself."

"I'm mailing a question," Felix said.

Hobb looked at him a moment longer, then shrugged and went back into the barn, and Felix walked home feeling lighter and more frightened than he had in years, the way one feels after setting a bone.

* * *

Waiting, Felix discovered, was a craft he had never learned. He had learned herb-lore and goat-tending and the particular patience of watching weather; he had learned, in another life, the discipline of cantrips and the long memorized ladders of theory that Maren had drilled into him before dawn. But waiting — waiting with a question folded into the world somewhere, traveling south along bad roads toward a woman who might burn it unread — that was new, and it sat in his chest like a swallowed stone.

He filled the days the way he always did. He mended the south fence where a doe had leaned on it. He dug and divided the comfrey bed. He taught Petra, without quite admitting he was teaching her, the names of things: *this is yarrow, this is sorrel, this is a wren, listen — that's its song.* She learned the way water learns a channel, effortlessly and completely, and repeated the common tongue back to him in a small clear voice that grew less halting each day. Her own language she kept for herself, murmured under her breath at dusk like a rosary, and Felix did not ask her to translate it again. He was waiting for someone else to do that work. He was waiting for permission, maybe. Waiting for Maren to tell him what he already half-knew, so that the knowing would belong to someone else too.

At night he took down the box.

It lived under the floorboard beneath the loom, wrapped in waxed linen, and it held everything Felix had stolen from the tower the night they ran: three books, a bundle of notes in Maren's hand, a cracked lens that hummed faintly if held to the ear, and — loose among them, kept apart because keeping it apart felt safer — six pages torn from a larger manuscript, written in an alphabet that predated every alphabet Felix knew. The pages were old enough that the vellum had gone the color of weak tea. The script marched across them in columns, dense and deliberate, and in the margins, in Maren's younger handwriting, were translations. Some of them. Fragments. Maren had never finished the work; Felix had never understood why until now.

*...and the word for the opening of the eye is also the word for the closing of the grave...*

*...it does not sleep as we sleep. Sleep is a mercy. What was done to it has no name in our tongues, which is why we must use its own...*

*...asked properly, it answers. Asked wrongly, it answers anyway.*

Felix read these lines so many times they wore grooves in him. And now, with Petra's voice in his memory — that single phrase she repeated, over and over, the one that had made the candle flames bow the first night — he began, terribly, to hear it in the pages. Not the words themselves; the *shape* of them. The cadence. The way her phrase rose at its center and fell away at the end like something exhaling.

On the ninth day after sending the letter, he laid the six pages on the table beside a slate and copied out the phrase as best he could render it in common letters, knowing even as he wrote that he was transcribing music into arithmetic, catching maybe a tenth of it. Then he sat and looked at it.

*Vesh-ka-orum. Vesh-ka-orum.* That was how his ear caught it, anyway — two breaths and a held note. He said it aloud, quietly, testing.

Nothing happened. Of course nothing happened. This was the valley; nothing happened here; that was the whole point of the place. But the lamp flame leaned toward him anyway, just slightly, just once, like a dog lifting its head at a familiar footstep, and Felix put out the lamp with his fingers and sat in the dark for a long time with his heart going hard.

Above him, through the floorboards, he heard Petra turn over in her sleep and murmur — and the murmur was the same phrase, the same two breaths and a held note, spoken perfectly, easily, the way children speak the names of their mothers.

* * *

Eighteen days after Hobb's cart went south, the raven came.

Felix saw it first as a smudge against the pale sky above the ridge, and thought distantly that it was flying wrong — ravens rode the wind here the way everything rode the wind here, dumbly, beautifully, untouched by anything but air. This one flew as if it had somewhere to be. It crossed the ridgeline without hesitation, which nothing with wings should have been able to do so cleanly, so *purposely*, and spiraled down over the cottage garden, and landed on the fence post beside the gate with the officious dignity of a tax collector.

It was a big bird, black as wet slate, with eyes that caught the light wrong — amber where they should have been dark, and too steady. Around one leg was a band of dull metal worked with a sigil Felix recognized the way you recognize a scar in a mirror: a circle broken by a single vertical stroke. Maren's mark. The mark she'd pressed into wax on every letter she'd ever sent him, back when he still received letters.

His mouth went dry. "You're hers," he said to the bird.

The raven regarded him with what he would have sworn, in a weaker moment, was impatience.

He fetched it bacon rind, because his hands needed something to do, and it ignored the bacon entirely, which settled the matter: no ordinary raven refused bacon. It simply watched him with its amber eyes and waited, with the terrible patience of a created thing, until he approached close enough — and then it stepped onto his offered wrist with a weight heavier than any bird had a right to be, and held out its leg.

The message tube was bone, capped in pewter. Inside was a scrap of parchment, burnt at one edge, curled like a dead leaf.

Felix unrolled it with fingers that did not entirely feel like his own.

Half the page was gone. What remained was the bottom-left corner of a letter, and Maren's handwriting — older than he remembered it, shakier, the confident brown strokes of his apprenticeship now thinned and grayed, a hand that had begun to tremble — marching up to the charred edge and stopping mid-sentence:

*—do not let her say it aloud again. Whatever she has told you, whatever she asks, the words are not a prayer. They are a counting. Every time the First Tongue is spoken truly, something on the other side hears it, and keeps tally, and the tally is not in our favor. I sealed my part of it with my name and I have regretted—*

And there the fire had taken the rest.

Felix stood in the garden with the scrap trembling in his fingers. The wind moved the bean rows. Somewhere down the lane a dog barked twice and gave up. The raven shifted its weight on his wrist, patient as stone.

*They are not a prayer. They are a counting.*

Petra said the phrase every evening. Had said it every evening since the night she arrived. Sometimes in her sleep. Sometimes, he realized with a cold sliding sensation in his stomach, when she thought he wasn't listening — standing at the window at dusk, facing the ridge, facing north, toward the Spire she had come from beneath, saying it softly, steadily, like a child calling home across a wide dark field.

*Something on the other side hears it, and keeps tally.*

"And the reply?" Felix said aloud, and his voice cracked on the second word. "She sent a letter. Where's the rest of it?"

The raven turned its head and looked at him, and in its amber eye there was — he would carry this image the rest of his life — there was a reflection that did not match the garden. A flicker of orange light. Firelight, or lamplight, or something burning somewhere very far from this quiet green valley. And then the bird opened its beak, and what came out was not a caw.

"*Ashen,*" it said, in a voice like dry paper tearing. "*The hand is ash-en. Ask her noth-ing. She can-not—*"

The voice stopped as if cut with shears. The raven shuddered, head to tail, feathers rippling, and then it was only a bird again — confused, ordinary, blinking dark eyes at the bacon rind as if seeing it for the first time. It hopped down off his wrist, ate the bacon in three efficient stabs, and flew away south without looking back, crossing the ridgeline and vanishing as if the sky had closed behind it.

Felix stood alone in the garden for a long time.

*Ask her nothing. She cannot—*

Cannot what? Cannot answer? Cannot hear? Cannot *speak*? The sentence broke exactly where a sentence breaks when the writer is interrupted — and Maren had not been interrupted by fire alone, Felix knew that in his bones, because Maren burned her own letters herself, always had, a habit from the old paranoid years; she would have fed the page to the flame deliberately, deliberately, and sent only the corner that mattered most—

Or someone else had burned it for her.

He went inside. Petra was at the table, drawing with a charred stick on the back of an old invoice: her endless circles, her towers, her small figures with raised arms. She looked up when he came in, and smiled — that quick, unguarded smile that had already, in three weeks, rewired something fundamental in his chest — and then her smile faded, because she was a child who had survived whatever she had survived, and children like that read faces the way other people read weather.

"Felix," she said carefully, in her new common tongue. "Bad?"

"No," he said, and sat down across from her, and put the burnt scrap on the table between them, and watched her look at it. "Petra. The words you say. At the window. Every evening."

Her chin came up slightly. Guarded. Ready.

"What are they *for?*"

She was quiet a long moment. Outside, the light was failing; the lamp between them guttered and steadied. When she answered, she answered in the First Tongue, softly, and pointed north, through the wall, through the ridge, toward the thing she had been sent out from. And then she said one word of common speech, the only word she seemed to have brought with her ready-made, the word she had said the first storm-night on his doorstep with mud to her knees and no fear in her at all:

"*Listening.*"

"They're listening," Felix said. His mouth was very dry. "Or you are."

She considered this with the grave attention she gave all questions, then shook her head, then nodded, then placed her small hand flat on the table between them, over the burnt scrap, over Maren's broken sentence, and said — patiently, as if explaining rain to someone who had never been outside —

"*Both.*"

That night Felix did not sleep. He sat at the table with the six stolen pages and the burnt corner of Maren's letter arranged in front of him like an altar, and he understood, finally, with the clarity that comes only at the worst hours, that the past had not stayed buried at all. It had been waiting. It had sent a child ahead of it, the way armies send scouts, the way storms send wind.

*Every time the First Tongue is spoken truly, something on the other side hears it, and keeps tally.*

Three weeks of evenings. Three weeks of tallies.

And somewhere south, in a tower full of regrets, Maren Halloran had tried to warn him and managed only half a warning before the fire took the rest — and Felix did not believe, could not believe, that the fire had been Maren's own.

He got up at the gray edge of dawn, took down the travel pack from its peg for the first time in seven years, and began, quietly, to count what he owned.
