## 5. Riders on the Ridgeline

*The following account was set down by my own hand, but it is not mine. It is Petra's, told to me over three evenings in the spring after everything ended, in her halting trade-speech and my stumbling attempts at hers, and where her words failed us both she drew pictures in the ash of the hearth. I have tried to translate faithfully — not only what happened, but how it felt to be small and hunted and knowing more than any child should. Where I have added anything of my own, I have marked it with a bracket, so that no one will ever again mistake my understanding for hers.*

*— F.V., from the preface to the second telling*

* * *

Petra had learned the sound of riders before she had learned most words.

Not the horses themselves — horses were only horses, big breathing things that smelled of grass and iron — but the particular way they moved when someone sat on them who did not love them. Kind riders let a horse wander. Kind riders stopped when the horse wanted to stop, and leaned forward along its neck, and spoke to it in low voices as if apologizing for the weight. The gray-cloaked ones never apologized. They sat like stones sewn into their saddles, and their horses walked the way prisoners walk, each step placed exactly where it was told, and the sound of many hooves moving in that exact way carried farther than thunder.

She heard them on the third morning after the storm, while she was crouched at the tree line above the valley with a handful of stolen oats cupped against her chest, watching a village wake below.

There were nine of them. She counted twice, because counting mattered; the woman beneath the Spire — the one whose voice had been warm and enormous and sad, the one Petra still could not say aloud without her chest hurting — had taught her that numbers were the first thing hunters lost track of, and the last thing the hunted should ever lose. Nine riders cresting the ridgeline in single file, gray cloaks pulled up against the wind, and where the first rider's hand rested on his knee there was a glove of dull black metal shaped like a closed hand with too many fingers.

Petra went still the way small animals go still: completely, instantly, every part of her agreeing at once.

Below her, the valley lay green and oblivious in the morning light. Smoke rose from chimneys. Goats bleated somewhere out of sight. A man — she knew him now, the goat-keeper, the one who had taken her in, the one whose name she was still learning to hold in her mouth the way you hold water in cupped hands — was somewhere down there among the herb rows, probably arguing with a weed or muttering at the sky. Felix. That was the name. *Felix.* It sounded like a word that had been dropped and picked back up again.

The riders reached the top of the ridge and stopped.

And then something happened that Petra had been waiting for without knowing she was waiting for it.

The lead rider raised one gauntleted fist, and light bloomed around it — a sickly amber color, like old honey held up to a dying fire — and spread outward in a ring, feeling its way across the ground the way a blind man's fingers feel across a wall. Petra had seen this before. In the city under the mountain, in the corridors of gray stone, the cloaked ones had done this at every doorway, every crossing, every place where the world might hide something from them. A seeking-light. A question asked in magic: *are you here? are you here? are you here?*

The ring of amber light rolled down off the ridgeline, across the scree, toward the valley grass.

And died.

It didn't fade. It didn't thin. It simply stopped, all at once, like a candle pinched between wet fingers, and where it had been there was nothing at all — just morning, just grass, just wind moving the long stems in slow silver waves. The lead rider's fist stayed raised. His horse shifted under him, uneasy, and he wrenched its reins sideways hard enough that it cried out, and even from the tree line Petra heard the anger in the sound he made, short and bitten-off, the sound of a man whose tool has failed him in front of others.

He tried again. The amber light bloomed, rolled, touched the valley's edge —

— and was gone, swallowed whole.

One by one the other riders made their own lights, and one by one the valley ate them. Petra watched with her oats forgotten in her cupped hands and her breath held so long her ribs ached, and she understood what she was seeing, though she understood it the way children understand things: completely and wrongly all at once. She thought the valley was hiding them. She thought the quiet place loved them, the way the dark beneath the Spire had loved her, wrapping itself around whatever was small enough to need hiding.

*[I have not corrected this in the telling, because I am not sure either of us knows yet whether she was wrong.]*

The riders conferred. Their voices came down the slope thin and flattened by distance, but Petra's ears were good — better than good; she had spent her whole life listening through stone — and she caught the pieces that mattered.

"— dead air, Captain. Nothing answers."

"Then we go in on foot and ask with our mouths, like beggars." The captain's voice was dry as old rope. "Spread along the ridge. Anyone comes or goes, we mark them. Especially—" a pause, and when he spoke again his voice had changed, gone careful, the way men's voices go careful around things they fear, "—especially anything small. Anything young. You know the description."

A murmur along the line. One rider, younger than the rest, said something Petra couldn't catch, and the captain answered sharply:

"Because the Hand does not send nine riders and two hundred miles of road after nothing. Because the Ashen Father himself signed the writ. And because if the child is here, and we come home without her, none of us will live long enough to regret it. Now ride your lines."

They split. Four went east along the ridgeline, four west, and the captain alone rode down as far as the boundary would let him — right to the invisible line where the valley began to forget — and sat his horse there for a long moment, looking down into the green.

Petra looked at him and felt something she had no word for yet. Not fear exactly. Fear was cold and fast and lived in the legs. This was slower, heavier, settled somewhere behind her sternum like a swallowed stone. Recognition, maybe. She knew that posture. She had seen it on the faces of the gray-cloaked ones in the corridors beneath the mountain, leaning close to the sealed doors, pressing their seeking-lights against the stone, wanting in. Always wanting in. Never asking.

The captain turned his horse and rode back up the slope, and the amber light of his last attempt guttered out behind him like a courtesy.

* * *

For three days the riders worked the ridgeline, and Petra watched them the way she had once watched spiders work their webs.

She had a system. She had built it without noticing she was building it, the way she had learned everything — by needing to. Mornings, she kept to the north slopes where the pines grew thick and the needles muffled her steps. Midday, when the sun flattened shadows and made eyes lazy, she moved. Evening, she returned to the hollow behind the fallen oak where she slept, a place she had chosen not for comfort but because it had two ways out and both were downhill, and downhill was always faster.

She watched the riders change watch at dawn and dusk. She watched them make camp on the ridge itself, never descending past the line where their magic died, and she noticed — filed away, the way she filed everything — that they would not cross it. Not one of them. Not even to relieve themselves. They sent their questions in on wings of amber light and got silence back, and they treated that silence the way sensible people treat deep water.

She watched them stop a woodcutter on the western path, two riders flanking him, and speak to him for a long time. The woodcutter shook his head a great deal. When they let him go he walked fast, not running, the careful not-running of a man who knows running is a confession, and that night Petra saw lamplight in his window burn very late.

She watched them pay a shepherd boy a coin for nothing, and the boy bite the coin, and the rider laugh — a wrong laugh, a door-shaped laugh with nothing behind it.

And on the second evening she watched the captain stand alone at the highest point of the ridge, facing the valley, and take off his gauntlet, and press his bare palm flat against the air — against the border itself, the invisible skin of the quiet place — and hold it there until his arm shook.

She was close enough, hidden in the bracken forty feet below, to hear what he said. He said it softly, almost tenderly, the way you speak to an enemy you respect:

"I know you're keeping something."

Then he put his glove back on, and mounted, and rode away, and Petra lay in the bracken with her heart going like a trapped bird and understood, with terrible clarity, that the valley's forgetting was not a wall. It was a door. And doors could be argued with, and worn down, and eventually opened.

That night she dreamed of the Spire. She dreamed of the vast warm dark beneath it, and the voice that had raised her, and the words it had pressed into her like a seal pressed into wax — words she carried everywhere and could not read, a letter written on the inside of her ribs. In the dream the voice was singing, and the song had a crack in it, and through the crack she could see the riders' amber light, searching, searching, patient as rot.

She woke with tears frozen on her face and did not know which part of the dream had made them.

* * *

On the fourth day, the riders found a way to be patient, and patience was worse than hunting.

They stopped riding the lines. They made a proper camp on the shoulder of the ridge, visible from the valley — deliberately visible, Petra realized, a message written in smoke and canvas: *we are here, we are not leaving*. Wagons arrived on the second day with supplies, and then a tent, a tall gray pavilion with a banner hanging from its peak: a black hand, open, fingers spread, on a field of ash-white.

Open. Not closed. Petra stared at the banner for a long time from her perch in the hawthorn thicket, working at it the way she worked at all puzzles, turning it over and over. Beneath the mountain, the cloaked ones had worn the closed hand. Fist = taking = seizing = *mine*. But this hand was open, fingers spread, and it took her half a day of chewing on it to arrive at the answer, and when she arrived she wished she hadn't:

An open hand is for reaching.

They weren't trying to seize the valley. They were waiting for the valley to come to them. Or — smaller, softer, easier — for one small thing to wander out of it, the way a rabbit wanders from cover, the way a child wanders after a butterfly, the way children do, because children are made of wandering.

So Petra did not wander.

She became a creature of routine so rigid it would have shamed a monk. Dawn: water from the stream at the bend where the alders grew, approached from downwind, drunk lying flat. Morning: observation from the hawthorn. Midday: movement. Evening: back to the hollow, eating whatever she had gathered — roots, mostly, and once the eggs from a ground-nest she found and hated herself for robbing and ate anyway, all four, raw, because hunger is honest and morality is a luxury of the full.

She did not go near the cottage. That was the hardest part, harder than the hunger, harder than the cold nights. Every day she told herself: today I will go down, today I will knock on that door again, today I will show him the words, the words I'm carrying, the words I was sent to deliver, *find someone who still listens*, and every day she climbed to the hawthorn instead and watched the gray tents on the ridge and understood that the words in her ribs were also a scent, a trail, a thread leading straight from the Spire to her, and anyone who touched the thread could be pulled along it.

She would not lead them to the goat-keeper. She would not lead them to the valley. She would sit in the thorns and eat roots and wait for the riders to give up, because everything gave up eventually; the dark beneath the mountain had taught her that too, taught her that even the deepest hungers grow tired of knocking.

But on the fifth evening, two things happened that broke her system.

The first: the captain rode down to the boundary line again, alone, at dusk, and this time he did not press his hand against the air. This time he unrolled a strip of parchment on a board across his saddle, weighted its corners, and began to write — slowly, carefully, glancing up after every few words at the valley below him, the way a portrait painter glances at his subject.

He was drawing the valley. Mapping it. And Petra, squinting through the thorns in the failing light, saw that the parchment already bore marks — little inked symbols scattered across an outline of the ridgeline — and that some of the symbols were fresh, their ink still shining.

He was marking where the seeking-lights had died. Measuring the shape of the silence. And a shape, once measured, could be searched systematically. Corner by corner. Hollow by hollow. Thorn thicket by thorn thicket.

The second thing happened behind her.

She heard it before she understood it — a soft shifting of earth, the particular compression of soil under a careful foot — and she was moving before the understanding arrived, rolling left out of the bracken as a net of woven shadow came down where she had been lying. It wasn't rope. It was darkness given weave and weight, and it hissed as it hit the ground, and where it touched the bracken the stems went gray and brittle, aged decades in a heartbeat.

A figure stood at the edge of the thicket. Gray cloak, hood back, and beneath the hood a face that was wrong in a way Petra's instincts screamed about before her mind caught up: too smooth, too symmetrical, a face like something remembered rather than seen. One of the Order's bound ones — the shadow-walkers, the ones beneath the mountain had whispered about in the last days, *they have learned to spend people, little messenger, do not let them touch you.*

The bound one tilted its head. Its mouth smiled without its eyes changing at all.

"Small thing," it said, in a voice like pages turning. "The Captain said the valley keeps something small."

Petra ran.

Downhill, always downhill, through the thorns letting them tear her, because torn skin heals and caught children don't. Behind her the shadow-net lifted and flowed after her like a living thing, and ahead of her the slope fell away toward the valley floor, toward the streams and the cottages and the lamp already lit in the window of a certain crooked cottage where a goat-keeper was surely worrying about a missing girl who had vanished five days ago without a word—

—and Petra made her choice in the space between one stride and the next, the kind of choice that isn't really a choice at all, that the body makes while the mind is still catching up, the kind of choice that is just love wearing the clothes of instinct.

She ran *toward* the cottage. Toward the lamp. Toward Felix.

Behind her the shadow-thing gained, silent as falling dust, and ahead of her the valley's border rushed up to meet her, and she crossed it — she felt it pass over her like cool water, like a blessing, like a hand briefly laid on her head — and heard, faint and furious, the pursuing darkness shriek to a halt at the line as if it had struck glass.

It stood there at the valley's edge. It could not follow. It looked at her across the invisible skin of the quiet place with its page-turning smile, and it raised one smooth white hand, and it pointed — not at her.

Past her. Down the slope. At the cottage with its one lit window.

Then it turned and flowed away up the hill, and Petra stood in the deep grass with her heart slamming and her arms bleeding and the first stars coming out, and she understood that she had just done the one thing she had sworn for five days not to do.

She had led them to a door. And now they knew which door to watch.

She walked down through the dark toward the lamplight, slow now, dragging, suddenly so tired her bones rang with it, rehearsing in her head all the ways she might explain — and finding that in the language of this valley she had almost no words at all, and that the truest explanation was the one she could not give: *I am a letter, and the people who wrote me want me back, and everyone who reads me is in danger.*

The cottage door opened before she reached it. Lamplight spilled gold across the grass, and Felix stood in it — sleepless, worried, furious, relieved, all of it fighting for space on their face — and Petra looked up at them and did the only honest thing left.

She opened her mouth and spoke the phrase. The one from beneath the Spire. The one that was prayer and alarm and letter all at once, the words carved into her ribs before she had a name, and she watched them land on Felix's face like stones thrown into still water, watched recognition move through them in slow ripples — recognition, and beneath it, rising fast, something that looked terribly like fear.

*[I know those words, her face said. God help me, I know those words.]*

And far above, on the dark ridgeline, the lamps of the gray camp burned steadily on, patient as the open hand on their banner, waiting for morning.
