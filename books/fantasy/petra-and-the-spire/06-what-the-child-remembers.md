## 6. What the Child Remembers

The rain had passed by morning, leaving the valley rinsed and gleaming, every stone washed to a deeper color of itself. Felix woke before dawn out of old habit—the goats did not care for sleeping in—and found the child still curled in the chair by the hearth, wrapped in the good wool blanket, her breathing slow and even.

The candle on the table had burned down to a stub overnight. Felix remembered lighting it, remembered the flames bowing toward the girl like courtiers toward a queen, remembered deciding not to think about it. In daylight, with the storm gone and the ordinary sounds of the valley rising—water dripping from the eaves, a wren arguing somewhere in the hawthorn—it was easier to pretend nothing had happened. Easier, but not honest. Felix had built this whole life on the careful practice of not being honest with themselves, and they knew the architecture of that lie well enough to feel it creak.

She was still there. That was the thing that would not be pretended away. A mud-caked child had walked out of a storm and spoken words that belonged in stolen pages and dead languages, and she was asleep in Felix's chair, and the world had not ended, and the goats needed milking.

Felix milked the goats. Fed the hens. Cut bread and fried it in butter because fried bread made everything seem survivable. By the time the smell had filled the cottage, Petra was awake, sitting up in the chair with the blanket pooled around her like a small gray mountain, watching Felix with those unblinking dark eyes.

"Fried bread," Felix said, in the valley tongue, and held out the plate.

She took it. She ate with the focused, unhurried intensity of someone who had known real hunger—not the sharp hunger of a missed meal but the long, grinding kind that teaches patience—and when she finished she looked at the plate, then at Felix, and said something in that language that should not exist.

"I don't understand you," Felix said. "You know that, yes? I don't understand a word."

Petra considered this. Then she pointed at herself, and at the floor, and made a gesture like something falling—a small, precise motion, fingers tumbling through the air—and said another phrase, shorter this time, almost gentle.

"No," Felix said. "You're not going anywhere until we've eaten and you've told me—" They stopped. Telling required language. Felix rubbed their face with both hands. "Right. Fine."

* * *

It took three days.

Not because Petra was unwilling—she seemed, if anything, relieved to be asked—but because communication between them had to be built from nothing, plank by plank, like a bridge over a gorge. Felix tried the market tongue first; Petra's face went carefully blank, which Felix learned meant she didn't know it. Then the high speech of letters and contracts, which she also didn't know. Then, half as a joke, an old shepherd's counting-song Felix's grandmother had sung, nonsense syllables mostly, and Petra's head tilted like a bird's, and she repeated one of the syllables back with perfect pitch, and they were off—or rather, they were nowhere, but they were nowhere together, which was a start.

Drawings did most of the work.

Felix had charcoal from the hearth and the backs of old herb-invoices, and it turned out Petra could draw—not well, in any trained sense, but with a startling directness, as though her hand were simply reporting what her eyes had seen without editorializing. Her first picture was of the cottage. Every detail correct: the crooked chimney, the herb boxes, even the notch in the doorframe where Felix had tested a knife's edge years ago. She had been in the house less than a day. Felix looked at that drawing for a long time.

"You see everything," they said quietly. "Don't you?"

Petra watched them watch the drawing, and something in her expression suggested she understood the tone if not the words—that she knew she had given them something strange, and was waiting to see what they would do with it.

The second drawing came that evening. Felix had cleared the table, set out the charcoal and paper again, more as a game than anything, and Petra bent over the page with her tongue between her teeth and drew a tower.

Not just any tower. It rose from the center of the page in long vertical strokes, impossibly tall, its top extending past the edge of the paper as though the paper itself were too small to hold it. Around its base she drew a ring of broken ground—jagged lines, radiating outward like cracks in ice—and scattered across the cracks she pressed her thumb, again and again, making dozens of small dark ovals.

"Bones," Felix said, before they could stop themselves. "Those are bones."

Petra looked up. She didn't nod, exactly. She touched the tower, then touched her own chest, then touched the tower again, and said a word—one word, low and resonant, a word that seemed to make the air in the room sit up straighter.

Felix knew that word.

They had known it for twenty years, since the nights they'd sat cross-legged on Maren's tower floor with stolen pages spread around them, pages copied from books Maren kept locked away, books whose titles Maren never spoke aloud. The First Tongue. The speech from before the sealing, the language that had commanded the Spire itself, preserved only in fragments because everyone who had spoken it fluently was either dead or had helped kill it. Maren had taught Felix perhaps forty words of it, carefully, the way one handles shards of glass—*for understanding only*, Maren had said, *never for use*. And then Felix had run away, and left the pages behind, and spent two decades trying to let the words decay out of memory.

They hadn't decayed. The word Petra had just spoken sat in Felix's chest like a swallowed coal.

"Spire," Felix translated aloud, uselessly, in the valley tongue. "You're saying... Spire."

Petra nodded this time. Firmly. Then she took up the charcoal again and turned the paper over.

* * *

What she drew over the next three days, in fragments, in pieces Felix slowly assembled like a man piecing together a shattered mirror, was this:

A tower, always the tower, drawn taller each time, until Felix began giving her larger sheets of paper so she wouldn't have to break its line at the page's edge. Beneath the tower, roots—not metaphorical roots but literal ones, drawn as descending chambers, level under level under level, going down further than the tower went up. She filled these chambers with small careful details: stairs carved from black stone that reflected light like water. Doors without handles that opened at a touch. Rooms where the walls glowed faintly, sourcelessly, the way deep water holds light after sunset.

And people. Or figures—she drew them featureless, smooth-faced, which unsettled Felix more than faces would have. They moved through the underground rooms. They stood beside a smaller figure, a child-sized figure, in what was unmistakably a bed. They brought the child food. They taught the child—there was a sequence of drawings showing the small figure and the smooth-faced ones sitting opposite each other, mouths open, sound rendered as fine spiraling lines rising between them.

"They raised you," Felix said slowly. "Underground. Under the Spire." Their mouth had gone dry somewhere around the fourth chamber. "Who were they? What were they?"

Petra couldn't answer that. But she answered other things. Yes and no were easy—nod and shake, and beyond that she had a repertoire of gestures so expressive that Felix wondered if the smooth-faced teachers below had communicated largely without words themselves. When Felix pointed at the featureless figures and mimed sleep, Petra shook her head. When Felix mimed death—hands folding, head drooping—Petra hesitated, then made a third gesture: hands opening slowly, like something releasing its grip.

Gone, then. Not dead. Gone. Felix thought about that distinction longer than was comfortable.

On the second evening, Petra drew the vault.

She drew it differently from everything else—slower, pressing harder, the charcoal nearly tearing the paper. It was a door, or the idea of a door, vast and seamless, set into a wall of the same black reflective stone. Before it she drew the small child-figure, alone. Above the door she drew spiraling lines—the sound-lines she used for speech—but these spirals were different: denser, layered, drawn over each other until the space above the door was a solid mass of darkness.

Then she put down the charcoal, placed her palm flat against the drawing of the door, and looked at Felix with an expression that Felix would spend many sleepless nights afterward trying to name. It was not fear. It was something older than fear. It was the look of someone describing a wound they had been born with.

"She sleeps," Felix whispered. "The thing behind the door. You lived beneath a sleeping god."

Petra's chin lifted slightly. Corrected him, somehow, without a word. Felix searched Maren's stolen lessons, dredging up fragments, testing shapes of meaning against the shape of her certainty. Not sleeping, then. Not quite. Something else. Something Felix didn't have a word for in any language, and suspected the First Tongue did.

* * *

On the third day, the rain returned, softer now, a gray whisper against the shutters, and Petra drew the last drawing.

She had been restless all morning—going to the window, looking out at the ridgeline where the valley's rim cut the sky, coming back, standing over her stack of drawings, rearranging them. Felix recognized restlessness; it was the body's way of rehearsing a departure. The thought arrived cold and unwelcome: *she's going to leave.* Three days of bread and charcoal and she would walk back out into the world, and Felix would return to goats and herbs and the practiced art of forgetting, and no one would ever know—

But instead of leaving, Petra sat down at the table and drew one final picture.

It was simple. Almost crude compared to her others. A road—just two wavering lines—running from a small square house (the cottage, unmistakably) out past the valley's edge, up and up, to a single vertical stroke at the top of the page. The tower. Between the house and the tower, on the road, she drew two figures: one large, one small.

Then she tapped the small figure. Herself. And then she tapped the large figure, and looked up at Felix, and waited.

"No," Felix said.

Petra tilted her head.

"No," Felix said again, more firmly, and heard how much the firmness cost them, how much it sounded like fear wearing authority's clothes. "Whatever you're asking—I'm not taking you anywhere. I don't leave the valley. No one leaves the valley, not really, not once they've—" They stopped. Pressed their lips together. Started again, slower. "This is a quiet place. That's the whole point of it. Things end at the ridgeline. Spells die at the ridgeline. Whatever you are, whatever followed you here, it can't follow you out if I stay, and it can't find me if I stay, and I have stayed for twenty years, and I am very, very good at staying."

Petra listened to all of this with the same patient attention she gave everything, as though Felix's voice were weather, worth observing but not negotiable. Then she picked up the charcoal one more time.

Below the drawing of the road, in careful strokes, she wrote a line of text. Not the valley script. Not the high speech. The characters were angular and dense and beautiful, and Felix's stomach dropped through the floorboards, because they knew these characters too—they had traced them by candlelight at nineteen years old while Maren slept one floor above, telling themselves it was scholarship, telling themselves it was harmless.

One sentence. Petra tapped it, then tapped her own chest, then pointed out the window—east, toward the ridgeline, toward everything beyond—and then, finally, she pointed at Felix.

"Read it," she seemed to say. "You can read it. I've seen you look at my words like a man reading his own handwriting in a dream. Read it."

Felix read it.

The translation assembled itself in their mind with terrible slowness, character by character, each one unlocking a fragment of grammar Maren had drilled into them and they had sworn they'd forgotten:

*Find the one who still listens.*

"That's your instruction," Felix said. Their voice came out strange, flattened, as though heard from underwater. "That's why you came here. Someone sent you out—those faceless ones, whoever they were—they sent you up and out with one task. Find someone who still listens." They laughed, and the laugh came out wrong too, cracked down the middle. "Child, you've made a mistake. This is the listening valley. Nothing listens here. That's what it's *for*."

But even as they said it, Felix's eyes went to the window. To the ridgeline. To the place where, three days ago, during the storm, the candle flames had bowed toward a sleeping child—and where, this morning, Felix had noticed something they had been carefully not noticing: the wild thyme on the south slope, blooming. Out of season. Weeks early. The first flowers Felix had seen inside the valley in twenty years that had not been planted by human hands.

Magic was leaking in. Or leaking out. Or the border was thinning, and the child who spoke the First Tongue was standing at the center of it like a stone dropped into still water.

Petra watched Felix's face do all of this—watched the denial crack, watched the fear come through it—and she did not smile, and she did not gloat. She reached across the table and turned her stack of drawings around so they faced Felix: the tower, the roots, the glowing rooms, the smooth-faced guardians, the vast sealed door, the road running east.

Then she folded her hands and waited, the way one waits for a kettle, or a verdict.

"The one who still listens," Felix repeated, barely above a whisper. "And you think that's me."

Outside, the rain thickened. And far off, at the edge of hearing—at the very limit of what a human ear could claim to catch—something on the ridgeline hummed.

Felix had lived in this valley for twenty years, and the ridgeline had never hummed before.
