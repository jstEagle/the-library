## 7. The Hedge-Witch's Ledger

Felix had never thought of themselves as a thief.

They had stolen before, in the small ways everyone does—apples from an orchard at the valley's edge where the trees grew wild and unclaimed, a handful of coppers from a purse left too long on a market stall. But those were the thefts of appetite and accident, forgivable things. What Felix planned for the night of the waning moon was something else entirely, and they knew it, and they went anyway.

The trouble had begun three days earlier, when Petra drew the flower.

She had been sitting by the hearth with one of Felix's old charcoal stubs, drawing as she always did when words failed her—which was often, since her tongue belonged to no kingdom Felix knew and her patience for pointing and guessing wore thin faster than Felix's did. Most of her drawings were simple: goats, the crooked chimney of the cottage, the storm from the night she'd arrived rendered in furious diagonal strokes. But that evening she'd drawn a flower, tall-stemmed, many-petaled, and beneath it a line of her strange script, and when Felix asked her what it was, she pointed out the window toward the ridge and said, in her clear, unhurried voice, *the word for it*, or something very like.

Felix had gone cold all over.

Because Felix knew that flower. Everyone in the valley knew it, though most pretended not to. It grew only along the ridgeline, in the thin soil where the world's magic died against the valley's borders—a pale, ghostly thing called the stillbloom, which folk said marked the graves of spells. It bloomed in high summer and only there, and it had never once been seen below the tree line in all the years Felix had lived in the valley.

And yet that morning, gathering herbs on the south slope, Felix had found one. A single stillbloom, open and pale as bone, growing forty paces inside the border that should have killed anything of its kind.

The border was leaking.

* * *

Felix sat up late that night, after Petra slept, turning the problem over like a stone too heavy to lift. The valley's deadness was the whole architecture of their life. They had chosen this place precisely because magic could not follow them here; they had built their safety on that silence the way other people build houses on foundations. If the silence was failing—if the seal, whatever ancient working held the Spire beyond the ridge, was wearing through—then everything Felix had fled from was coming back, and bringing its teeth with it.

And Petra. Petra, who spoke words older than kingdoms, whose dreams bent candle flames, who had appeared at Felix's door out of a storm with mud to her knees and no story she could tell that Felix could understand. The travelers at market asking about *the quiet place*. The raven with its burnt scrap of parchment and Maren's half-warning. The pieces were arranging themselves into a shape Felix didn't want to look at directly.

There was exactly one person in the valley who might know more, and Felix had spent six years avoiding her.

Odile Vasse had come to the valley before Felix did, which was saying something, because the valley collected people the way driftwood collects on a quiet shore—the burned-out, the hiding, the ones who wanted to be forgotten. She kept a cottage on the far side of the mill stream, half-swallowed by brambles, and she was the finest herbalist anyone had ever seen, and she was also, unmistakably, a witch. Not a hedge-witch of the harmless kind—not someone who hung charms and brewed teas and called it tradition. Odile's craft had edges. Children were told not to take shortcuts past her fence. And every few years, some newcomer would ask why she'd been exiled from the village proper, and the answer would come back the same way it always did: *she tried to work magic here, and it doesn't work here, and she wouldn't stop trying.*

Felix had met her twice. Once at market, where they'd exchanged the flat, wary courtesies of two people who each recognized something in the other and preferred not to name it. Once on the mill path, when Odile had looked at Felix's hands—ink-stained, herb-bruised, the hands of someone who'd once done finer work than goat-tending—and said, "You count your steps when you walk past the ridge. I've watched you do it. Only people who've lived with magic count their steps away from it." Then she'd walked on, leaving Felix standing in the road feeling flayed.

If anyone in the valley had studied the border—if anyone had dates, measurements, notes, the slow arithmetic of a dying seal—it would be her.

Which meant Felix had to break into her house.

They told themselves stories about it while they waited for the moon to wane. That they would only borrow the ledger, not steal it. That they would leave payment. That necessity made a kind of right, or at least a kind of excuse. The stories didn't sit well. Felix had run from an apprenticeship rather than make compromises of exactly this shape—they remembered that now, remembered it with a clarity that felt like punishment—and here they were, six years later, oiling the hinges of their own conscience so it wouldn't squeak.

But then Petra woke in the night again, crying out in the First Tongue, and both candles in the room bowed toward her bed like courtiers, and Felix lay in the dark listening to her breathe and thought: *I don't care what I have to become. I'm not letting anything reach her.*

That was the lie thieves tell, of course—that it's for someone else. Felix knew that too. But knowing a lie doesn't stop you from needing it.

* * *

The night was overcast, which suited. Felix left Petra sleeping with the fire banked and the door barred, and crossed the fields with a shuttered lantern they didn't light, moving by memory and starlight through gaps in the cloud. The mill stream ran loud with autumn rain. Felix forded it above the wheel, boots filling with cold water, and came up through the brambles on the far side with scratches down both forearms like the land itself objecting to their errand.

Odile's cottage was smaller than it looked by day, hunched under its roof of moss-heavy thatch. No smoke from the chimney. A single window glowed faintly amber—herbs drying, perhaps, or a night-lamp left burning out of habit. Old women kept lamps burning. Old witches especially.

Felix stood in the wet grass for a long moment, listening. Frogs. Wind in the bracken. Nothing else.

"You can still turn back," they murmured to themselves, and then, because they'd known even as they said it that they wouldn't, they went to the door.

It wasn't locked. That stopped Felix cold, hand on the latch. Six years of caution, and the door of the valley's most feared woman stood unlatched? Either Odile trusted the valley's fear to guard her better than any bolt—or she expected visitors.

Felix chose not to think about the second possibility. That, too, was becoming a habit.

Inside, the air was thick with scent: dried yarrow and wormwood, camphor, something resinous and unfamiliar, and underneath it all the mineral smell of stone dust, as if the walls themselves were slowly becoming geology. Shelves climbed to the rafters, crammed with jars labeled in a cramped, slanting hand. Bundles of herbs hung from every beam. A kettle sat cold on the hearth. And everywhere—on the table, the chairs, the floor beside the bed—paper. Notes, sketches, charts, pages covered edge to edge in figures.

Felix lifted the lantern-shutter a finger's width and let out a thread of light.

The nearest page was a map of the valley, drawn with a surveyor's precision, and pinned to it—literally pinned, with a thorn—was a sprig of dried stillbloom. Around the flower, Odile had written in her slanted hand:

*Bloomed 14th of Harvestmonth, south slope, forty paces within the line. First recorded instance below the tree line in my residence here. The border is not holding. The border has not been holding for years. We have all been standing in a house whose roof we refused to look at.*

Felix's mouth went dry. They set the page down carefully, as if it might wake, and turned to the table.

There, square and swollen with years of entries, bound in cracked green leather, lay the ledger.

It was bigger than Felix had imagined—a ledger in truth, a record kept across decades, its spine broken soft with handling. Felix opened it with fingers that trembled slightly and told themselves it was the cold.

The early pages were herbal: remedies, dosages, notes on patients whose names Felix recognized as grandparents of people they now knew. But somewhere around the middle—Felix could find the exact page afterward, always, in memory—the entries changed character. The handwriting tightened. The subject shifted.

*Third year in the valley. Tested the border at the western notch today, as I do each season. Carried a charged charm across the line. It died at the same stone as always—the boundary is precise to less than a pace, which means it is maintained, which means it is not natural. Dead zones are accidents. This is a wall, and walls have builders.*

Felix turned pages faster.

*Ninth year. The wall requires maintenance. I am nearly certain now. Each season the charm dies a hand's breadth further out than the last. Negligible, if you measure in seasons. Alarming, if you measure in decades. At current rate of decay, the border will fail entirely within—*

A number followed, and Felix's eye snagged on it and refused to move. Forty years. Perhaps fifty. Odile had written it decades ago. By her own arithmetic, the valley should have had time—time enough for Felix to live out their whole careful, cowardly life inside the silence and die safely forgotten.

But the later entries told a different story.

*Year unknown—my numbering has slipped, I confess it. The decay is accelerating. This season the charm failed a full fathom further in. Something is spending the seal. Or feeding on it. Or the seal was woven by hands less patient than its makers believed. I have begun keeping the stillbloom records separately. See appendix.*

Felix flipped to the back of the ledger, and there it was: pages of columns, dates and locations, each entry recording a stillbloom sighting below the tree line. One, twelve years ago, near the hot spring—dismissed as seed carried by birds. Another, seven years ago. Then three in a single decade. Then, in the last five years, the entries crowded together, the columns filling, the locations marching steadily inward from the ridge like a tide chart of something rising.

And on the final written page, dated barely a month past, a single line:

*A child has come to the valley. I have seen her at the stream with the Villalobos one. She hums when she thinks no one listens, and where she hums, the water forgets which way to flow. I do not know what she is. I know what she arrives with. The border began to fail in earnest the same season she appeared. Coincidence is a word cowards use for patterns they are afraid to finish.*

Felix stood very still in the amber lamplight, the ledger open in their hands, and felt the floor tilt beneath everything they believed.

Petra hadn't brought the decay. Had she? The entries went back decades; the rot predated her by years. But she hastened it—Odile was certain of that, and Felix's own skin was beginning to agree, some animal part of them whispering that the child who slept in their cottage pulled at the world the way the moon pulls at the sea. Every day she stayed, the roof came down a little faster. Every day she stayed, the riders on the ridgeline—the ones the market gossip had spoken of, gray-cloaked, asking after quiet places—came a little closer to understanding why their compasses spun.

And if the seal was failing anyway, decaying on its own schedule, woven by hands that had built in an expiration date—

*Woven,* Felix thought, and the word arrived with a weight that made them grip the table's edge. *Seals aren't woven from nothing.* Maren's burnt warning scraped at the back of their mind: half a sentence, half a confession. *What did you help build, old woman? What did it cost, and who paid?*

They needed the rest. They needed names, dates, the mechanics of the thing. They needed—

Light bloomed behind them.

Not lamplight. Candlelight, sudden and total, and with it a voice like dry leaves and vinegar, close enough that Felix felt breath on the back of their neck:

"Well," said Odile Vasse. "Either you're the clumsiest thief the valley's ever produced, reading your plunder by my own lamp—or you're exactly the person I've been waiting twenty years for."

Felix turned, slowly, the ledger still clutched to their chest like a shield, and found the old witch standing in the doorway to her bedroom wrapped in a shawl, a candle raised in one knotted hand and an expression on her face that was not anger, not surprise, but something far worse: recognition.

"Sit down," Odile said. "You'll want tea for this. And put my ledger down gently, child. It's the only honest thing either of us owns."
