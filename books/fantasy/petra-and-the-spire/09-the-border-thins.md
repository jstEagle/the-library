## 9. The Border Thins

Felix had walked to the ridge a hundred times in the years since they'd come to the valley, and every one of those walks had ended the same way: with the world going quiet.

It was not silence, exactly. Birds still sang on the high slopes, wind still moved through the pines, and the goats—when Felix brought them, which was rare, because goats were idiots about edges—still bleated their idiot grievances. But beneath all of it lay something else, something like the hush inside a church or the held breath before a confession. Magic did not die at the ridgeline so much as it was *refused*. A spell cast downhill simply unraveled, thread by thread, until nothing remained but the caster's own foolish face. Felix had tested it early and often in those first paranoid months: charms, wards, a cantrip for warming tea that Maren had taught them when they were nine years old and still believed learning magic was the same as being loved. All of it came apart at the border like sugar in rain.

That was why they had chosen the valley. That was why they stayed.

So when Felix crested the sheep-track below the western ridge on a gray morning in late autumn and smelled *thunder* where there was no storm, they knew, before they saw anything at all, that something had gone wrong with the world's careful forgetting.

* * *

They had left Petra asleep under three blankets with strict instructions not to open the door for anyone, which the child had acknowledged with the grave solemnity of a soldier accepting orders she fully intended to interpret however she pleased. Three days had passed since Odile's ledger, since the dates marching down its pages like a countdown no one had wanted to read. Three days of Felix turning the numbers over in their mind the way a tongue worries a broken tooth.

The decay was accelerating. Odile's earliest entries—the ones in faded iron-gall ink, decades old—spoke of the border in terms of centuries. *The hedge holds. The hedge is patient.* Then, somewhere around fifteen years ago, the intervals between her measurements began to shrink. Ten years. Five. One. And in the last pages, written in a hand gone hurried and cramped, the entries came monthly, then weekly, and the final line, dated eleven days ago, read only:

*It is not failing. It is being eaten.*

Felix had copied those numbers out by candlelight, checked them twice against their own memory of the valley's seasons, and felt the floor tilt gently beneath everything they had built. Because fifteen years ago was when Felix had arrived. And the acceleration had begun, as near as Odile's figures could show, within a season of their crossing the ridge.

They had told themselves it was coincidence. They had been telling themselves that for three days, and the story had worn thin as an old glove.

Now, standing on the sheep-track with the smell of ozone and green things in their nose, Felix stopped pretending.

The wildflower grew from a crack in the granite six paces below the true crest of the ridge—a place where nothing should bloom, where nothing *had* bloomed in all the years Felix had tramped this path, because the border's dead zone ran wider than any sensible root would dare. It was a wood anemone. Felix knew flowers; a hedge-witch's apprentice learned botany before they ever learned spells, because herbs were honest in a way magic never was. Wood anemones bloomed in April. It was November. The flower stood open and white and impossibly delicate against the black rock, its petals shivering in a wind that carried warmth instead of frost, and around its base the lichen had turned a green so vivid it looked painted.

Felix crouched beside it for a long time without touching it.

Magic leaking through the border would do this—would push life into shapes and seasons it had no business wearing, the way water forced through a crack in a dam comes out strange and violent. The valley's protection worked by unmaking enchantment, but enchantment and living growth were not so different at the root, and if the hedge was thinning, then the land just beyond it would begin to dream in colors it had forgotten. Out-of-season bloom. Warmth in November. Felix had read about such things in Maren's library, back when reading about them was safe precisely because they could never happen here.

Here, now, happening.

They took out the compass.

It had belonged to Maren—stolen, along with the stolen pages, on the night Felix ran—and it was a good instrument, brass-cased, its needle balanced on a ruby bearing, the kind of thing that made even non-mages feel briefly competent. In the valley it behaved itself perfectly, because a compass was not magic; it was merely obedient to the world. Felix had used it a hundred times on foggy mornings.

The needle spun.

Not wavering. Not hesitating between north and some local disturbance, the way it might near iron ore or a lightning-struck tree. It spun smoothly, steadily, around and around like a thing dancing to music only it could hear, and as Felix watched, the glass over the dial fogged from the inside, then cleared, then fogged again, breathing.

"Right," Felix said aloud, because saying things aloud made them real, and real things could be fought, and they shut the compass case with fingers that were not quite steady.

They looked west, past the ridge, toward the low hills and the long road and the world where magic lived. The sky out there was the same gray as the sky above the valley. Nothing moved on the road. Everything looked exactly as it always had.

Everything looked exactly like a wall that has begun, very quietly, to lean.

* * *

Felix did not go home by the direct path. Habit—paranoia dressed up as habit—took them the long way, down through the alder stand, across the stream at the stepping stones, past the abandoned charcoal-burner's hut with its collapsed roof. They were halfway down the slope, thinking hard and looking at nothing, when they realized the forest had gone quiet.

Not the valley-quiet. Not the deep, protective hush of the border. This was the quiet of animals holding still, and Felix froze with one foot on a moss-slick stone and listened the way Maren had taught them to listen, back before everything, back when the lessons were gifts and not debts.

A shape detached itself from the bole of an alder twenty feet away and became a child.

Petra stood with her arms wrapped around herself, barefoot—barefoot, in November, and her feet weren't even blue—mud to her knees, hair full of twigs, watching Felix with that unnerving steadiness she had, the look of someone much older wearing a small girl like an ill-fitting coat.

"I told you to stay in the cottage," Felix said.

"You said don't open the door." Petra shrugged. "I went out the window."

"The window is worse."

"The window is smaller," Petra agreed, as though this settled the matter. "I followed your smell."

"My *smell*?"

"Goats. Smoke. The bitter plant you chew." She said it without embarrassment, the way she said everything, and Felix decided not to pursue the question of what else the child could smell, because the answer would probably be upsetting. "You went to the top. To the edge." Her chin lifted toward the ridge, invisible now behind the trees. "You went to ask it something."

"I didn't ask it anything. I looked at it."

"That's asking," Petra said. "Looking is asking with your eyes."

Felix closed their eyes briefly and asked whatever gods governed stubborn children for patience. "We're going home. You're going to eat breakfast. We're going to talk about windows."

But Petra hadn't moved. She was looking past Felix, up the slope, and her expression had changed—had gone from its usual watchful calm to something Felix had never seen on her face before, something that made the hairs rise along their arms.

She was afraid.

"Petra."

"It's louder today," she said quietly. "The edge. Yesterday it hummed like a sleeping person breathes. Today—" She pressed her hand flat against her own sternum, and Felix remembered the drawings, the vault beneath the Spire, the phrase she repeated like a prayer or an alarm. "Today it's like someone breathing fast. Like someone having a bad dream. I heard it from the garden. That's why I climbed."

"You heard the border. From the garden. Half a mile away."

"I hear lots of things." She finally looked back at Felix, and her fear resolved into something harder, something that looked, uncomfortably, like resolve. "It's getting thinner because of me. Isn't it. That's what you've been thinking for three days. You think I'm eating it."

Felix said nothing, which was answer enough.

Petra nodded slowly, absorbing it, and Felix braced for tears or anger or the terrible adult calm again—but what came was neither. She reached down, picked up a pinecone from the stream bank, and held it out in her cupped palms like an offering.

"Show me," she said.

"Show you what?"

"What happens. When I touch the edge-things. If I'm eating it, I want to see how. I want to know what I am." Her voice didn't waver. Her hands, Felix noticed, were trembling very slightly around the pinecone. "Nobody ever showed me what I am. They just put me outside the door and told me to walk."

And there it was—the whole terrible shape of her, laid open in one sentence. Felix thought of Odile's ledger, of the dates, of *it is being eaten*, and of the alternative, which was leaving a seven-year-old alone in a cottage to work out her own monstrosity in the dark. They thought, unwelcome and unwilled, of Maren's hands guiding theirs through a first cantrip, the warmth of approval, the sense of being *made for something*—and how that same sense had curdled, years later, into the reason they'd run.

"Give me the pinecone," Felix said.

* * *

They went back up together. Felix told themselves it was to gather more evidence, to be thorough, to approach the problem scientifically. They told themselves a great many things on that climb, and believed almost none of them, because the truth was simpler and colder: if the border was thinning, and if Petra was hastening it, then Felix needed to know the shape and speed of the danger, and there was exactly one way to measure it, and the way had small bare feet and was already ahead of them on the path, scrambling over rocks with the sureness of a goat.

At the wildflower, Felix made Petra stop ten paces off.

"Do you feel anything?"

"No. Should I?"

"Not yet. Walk forward slowly. Stop when I say."

Petra advanced one step. Two. Three. At five paces from the flower, she stopped on her own, head tilting, listening to something. "There," she said. "Like standing near an oven."

"Keep going. Slowly."

Seven paces. Eight. At eight, the flower changed.

It happened without violence, without light or sound—simply, horribly, the way ice becomes water. The anemone's white petals darkened, curled, and fell away all at once, and the stem beneath them thickened and split its own skin, and where a spring flower had stood in November there was now a woody stalk crowned with a bud of dense, waxy, wrong-seasoned green that Felix did not recognize at all. Around it, the lichen's impossible brightness drained to gray. The warm pocket of air folded shut like a fan.

Petra stood over it, fists clenched, shoulders hunched, waiting to be told she was a monster.

"Huh," Felix said.

Petra looked up. "Huh?"

"It didn't die. It *changed*. It got more—solid. More ordinary." Felix crouched, examining the transformed stalk without touching it. Their mind was moving fast now, the old apprenticeship gears engaging after years of rust, and they had to admit, privately, that some part of them was glad to be using them again, and hated that part for its gladness. "If you were eating the border, this would be the other direction. Things would get *stranger* near you, not tamer. Wilder. More magical, not less."

"But the ledger says—"

"The ledger says the border is failing. I know what the ledger says." Felix sat back on their heels. "But maybe we've been reading it wrong. Maybe the border isn't being eaten. Maybe it's being *pulled*—like a knot working loose because something on the other side is drawing the thread." They looked at Petra. "Or maybe something on *this* side is pulling it. Something that doesn't belong here. Something the valley wants to spit back out."

Petra considered this with her head tipped sideways. "Me."

"Maybe you. Or maybe not you yourself—maybe whatever sent you. Whatever made you. The Spire's seal was woven to keep magic *in* the world and *out* of this valley both, if Maren's old notes are anything to go by. If a piece of the Spire walked across that border—" Felix stopped, because the next step of the thought arrived all at once, cold and complete: *then the border wouldn't fail gradually. It would fail around her, specifically, like water finding the hole shaped exactly like her.* "Hold out your hand."

Petra held out her hand.

Felix drew the compass from their coat, opened it, and set it in the child's palm. The needle spun, smooth and steady, the same mad dance as before—but slower here, eight paces downslope, and as Felix watched, the spin began to *tighten*, the circles shrinking, the needle's arc narrowing, until it was trembling in a tight frantic oscillation that pointed, always, always, back up the ridge. Toward the world. Toward the Spire, far beyond the hills, beyond knowing.

Toward home, if you were a fragment of something sealed away.

"It points at you," Felix said softly. "Even here. Even through the dead ground. It's trying to point at you and the Spire at once, because it can't tell the difference anymore."

Petra looked down at the compass in her hand. Her face did something complicated—fear and longing and a grief too large for her features, all passing over her like weather. When she spoke, her voice was very small.

"Is that why it's waking up? Because it misses me?"

"I don't know." Felix took the compass back, closed it, and found their own voice had gone rough. "But I know someone who might. Someone who helped build that border, who owes me a letter that's weeks late, and who is going to explain to me exactly what my running away cost the world." They stood, knees cracking, and looked east—not toward the ridge and the dying hedge, but down-valley, toward the village, the market, the smoke of ordinary chimneys. "Because here's what I've worked out, Petra, walking down that hill. This valley was never a home. It was a hiding place. And hiding places only work while the walls hold. Ours are coming down whether we stay or go, and when they come down, everyone in this valley is standing next to us when the Order arrives."

Petra was quiet for a moment. Then, practical as ever: "So we run."

"So we run. Eventually. Carefully. With food and a plan and not out windows." Felix started down the track, and the child fell in beside them, matching their stride with the tireless ease of the young. "But first I have to write a letter that will actually be answered, and I know only one address worth sending it to."

Above them, unseen behind the trees, the ridge stood silent against the gray sky, its ancient hedge worn thin as old cloth—and somewhere far beyond it, something vast and patient felt, perhaps, the thread go slack, and began, very gently, to pull.
