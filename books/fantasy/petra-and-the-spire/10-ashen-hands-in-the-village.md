## 10. Ashen Hands in the Village

Bram Halloway had been village elder for nineteen years, and in that time he had learned that most trouble announced itself the way weather did—by smell, by silence, by the particular quality of light before a storm. The morning the gray riders came down from the pass, it was the birds he noticed first. The starlings that usually mobbed his eaves at dawn were gone, and the air over the market square had the held-breath stillness of a room where someone has just said something unforgivable.

He was setting out his scales on the trestle table outside the tithe barn—he still liked to weigh the grain disputes himself, though Tam Cooper had offered twice to do it, because Bram trusted his own hands more than any man's arithmetic—when the first of them rode into the square.

There were six. They came at an easy walk, unhurried, their horses shod so quietly that the only sound was the creak of leather and the soft crunch of hooves on packed earth. Their cloaks were the gray of wood ash after rain, hoods back, faces open and pleasant. That was the first wrong thing. Soldiers who meant no harm arrived dusty and loud, complaining about the beer or the price of oats. These men looked like guests arriving slightly early for a dinner they had already decided to ruin.

The one in front reined in by the well and removed his glove finger by finger, as if the morning were warm instead of sharp with autumn. He had a long, kind face, the sort of face you'd trust with your purse and your daughter both, and when he smiled at Bram it reached all the way to his eyes.

"Good morrow," he said. His voice carried without effort, the way a bell carries. "I'm looking for the elder of this village."

Bram wiped his palms on his apron, though they weren't dirty. "You've found him. Bram Halloway. Welcome to Hollowmere, such as it is."

"Hollowmere." The man tasted the name like wine. "A quiet place, I'm told."

"We do our best."

"And quiet is valuable." He dismounted, handing his reins to one of the others without looking, and crossed the square with his hand extended. His grip was dry and exact, neither too firm nor too soft, calibrated like everything else about him. "Corvane. I serve the Order of the Ashen Hand. Perhaps you've heard of us."

Bram had heard of them the way you hear of wolves—stories, mostly, and none of them from anyone who'd met one twice. "Can't say we get much news up here," he said.

"No," Corvane agreed pleasantly. "That's rather the point of places like this, isn't it?"

* * *

They took over the common room of the Drowned Goose, which was the only building in Hollowmere large enough to hold six riders and their arrogance, and by noon the whole village knew something was happening, because Sela Prewitt had seen the Order's banner unrolled—a black hand on gray silk, fingers spread—and had told her sister, who told the miller, who told everyone within the hour.

Bram sat across from Corvane at the Goose's long table while the innkeeper's boy brought ale nobody drank. Corvane had spread a map of the valley on the scarred oak, weighted at the corners with coins, and it was a good map, better than any Bram had ever seen of his own home. It showed footpaths Bram had walked since boyhood. It showed the old shepherd's hut above the falls that hadn't been used in thirty years. Somebody had drawn this map recently, and carefully, and from close by.

"You'll forgive the intrusion," Corvane said. "We're searching for a child. A girl, perhaps eight or nine years old, traveling alone or with a companion. She may be hurt, or frightened, or simply lost. She speaks—" he paused, choosing the word with visible care, "—an unusual language. Old words. Words most people wouldn't recognize." He smiled again. "Some might call it gibberish. We know better."

"What manner of child is this?" Bram asked. "That the Order rides six strong into my valley?"

"The kind that matters." Corvane turned a coin between his fingers—a silver piece, heavy, minted in some southern capital Bram had never seen. "She's not in trouble. Quite the opposite. There are people who wish to help her. People with resources." He set the coin on the table between them, where it caught the light from the window. "For information leading to her safe recovery, the Order pays generously. For silence—" the smile thinned by a degree, no more, "—well. Silence is also a choice, Master Halloway. All choices have consequences. I'm sure a man of your standing understands that better than most."

Bram understood. That was the worst of it. He understood exactly what was being offered and exactly what was being threatened, and both were wrapped in the same soft voice and the same reasonable face, and there was nothing in the room he could point to and call a knife.

"I'll ask around," he said.

"Of course you will." Corvane rose, satisfied, and pushed the coin an inch closer. "Keep that. Consider it a retainer against your honesty. And Master Halloway—if she passes through, if you so much as dream of her, send word to the Goose. We'll be here some days. We're patient men." He gathered his map, folding it along creases worn soft with use. "Patience is the whole of our trade, really. Everything else is detail."

* * *

By evening the village had divided itself the way villages do under pressure—not into factions, but into silences. At the well, at the bake oven, in doorways, people spoke in lowered voices and stopped speaking altogether when children came near. Nobody had seen the girl. Nobody admitted to having seen anything at all, which was its own kind of answer, and Bram watched his neighbors perform ignorance with varying degrees of skill and thought: *we have all become liars in a single afternoon, and none of us chose it.*

He walked home past the tithe barn, past the shrine to the local saint whose name half the village had forgotten, and let himself think about what he wasn't thinking about.

Because here was the thing Bram Halloway could not say aloud, could barely say to himself: three years ago, the winter his wife had taken sick and the physic's fees had eaten through their savings like rot through a beam, money had appeared. Not charity—he'd have refused charity, would have drowned first. It had come as payment, a loan arranged through Odile the herbalist, who lived at the valley's edge and whom decent folk didn't visit, with terms never spoken and interest never named. And Odile, when he'd tried once to thank her, had looked at him with those flat gray eyes and said, "Don't thank me, Bram. Just remember, someday, that debts are how the world keeps score."

He owed Odile. And Odile, everyone knew, traded with Felix Villalobos, the hedge-witch up the western slope—the runaway apprentice, the one who kept goats and grew herbs and asked nothing of anyone. And if a strange child had come to the valley, mud-caked and speaking old words, then the odds were good as bread rising that the child had gone to the one person in Hollowmere who would take her in without questions.

Which meant Bram knew—or could guess with terrible accuracy—where the Order's coin should be spent. And saying so would buy him safety, maybe prosperity, and cost him whatever remained of the man his wife had married.

He stood in his own doorway a long time, watching the light die on the western ridge, and did not go to the Drowned Goose, and hated himself a little for noticing how easy it had been not to go, and how much easier it would be tomorrow.

* * *

Two mornings later, Bram was at the market early, laying out apples, when he saw Felix Villalobos come down the slope path.

He'd known Felix since they were a gangly youth trailing their mentor Maren through these same hills, back when the world seemed simpler, or at least differently complicated. Felix kept to themselves now. Bought lamp oil, salt, thread, the occasional bolt of cloth, paid in small honest coin, spoke little, left quickly. The village tolerated them the way you tolerate weather—without warmth, without malice, because there was no alternative.

But today Felix was buying differently.

Bram watched, weighing apples for Widow Marsh and pretending not to watch, as Felix moved through the market with a list in their posture. Hard cheese, a whole wheel of it. Smoked ham, two slabs. Dried fruit by the sack. A new waterskin, the big kind pilgrims carried. Boots—Felix tried on boots at Cobbler Anselm's stall, which they had never once done in all the years Bram had known them, and paid without haggling, which was so unlike Felix that Anselm himself blinked after them.

Travel provisions. Enough for two. Maybe three.

And there—there was the thing that settled cold in Bram's stomach, heavier than any debt. Because Felix bought dried meat and hard cheese and boots, yes, but Felix also stopped at the herb-seller's stall and bought valerian root and willow bark and a jar of honey, and those were not travel goods. Those were the purchases of someone caring for a sick person, or a frightened one, or a child who cried at night.

Widow Marsh took her apples and shuffled off, and Bram stood alone at his trestle a moment too long, and when he looked up, Felix was looking back at him.

Across the square, over the heads of buyers and sellers, the hedge-witch's eyes met the elder's, and Bram saw in them a question he couldn't answer and a fear he recognized as his own. Felix knew. Of course Felix knew—the Order's riders were camped at the Goose, their horses watered at the public trough, their gray cloaks visible from the ridge. Felix had come down into the lion's mouth anyway, because a body needed feeding whether lions watched or not, and because courage and necessity look identical from the outside.

Felix gave him the smallest nod. Not a plea. Not quite a warning. Something more like acknowledgment—*you see me; I see you seeing me; what you do with that is yours.*

Then Felix shouldered the provisions and walked back up the slope path, unhurried, and the gray riders drinking ale on the Goose's porch watched them go with the mild interest of men watching weather, and did nothing at all.

* * *

That night, Corvane sent for him.

Bram found the Order man alone in the common room, the fire burned low, the map spread again on the table—though now Bram saw it bore new markings, small precise circles inked around the outlying farms, the shepherd's paths, the western slope.

"You've been asking around," Corvane said. It wasn't a question. "What have your people told you?"

"That nobody's seen any child."

"Mm." Corvane poured two cups of wine and slid one across. Bram didn't touch it. "You know what interests me about valleys like this, Master Halloway? Everyone lies, but everyone lies badly. A village is a net, and truth catches in it like fish. Someone has fed an extra mouth this month. Someone's flour runs short. Someone buys cheese and ham and boots for a journey they've never once planned in all their careful little years." He turned the cup slowly on the table. "I don't need you to tell me where she is. I only need you to confirm what I already suspect. Confirm it, and the debt your household carries to a certain herbalist—yes, we know about that too, we know a great deal—could find itself... forgiven. By generous hands. Refuse, and I'm afraid the same hands might remember it differently."

The fire popped. Somewhere upstairs, a floorboard creaked under an innkeeper's careful weight.

Bram thought of his wife's grave on the hillside. Thought of the winter money, and Odile's flat gray eyes, and the phrase she'd given him: *debts are how the world keeps score.* Thought of Felix's nod across the market square, and of a mud-caked child he had never seen, who had done nothing to him or anyone, and who spoke words older than kingdoms and was hunted for it by men with patient smiles.

He thought, finally, of what his wife would have said, and heard it plainly, in her voice, the way the dead stay useful: *"Bram Halloway, you can be afraid and decent at the same time. It's harder, but it's the only way worth doing either."*

"There's no child in this valley," Bram said. "That's what my people tell me, and I believe them, and I'm the elder, and my word is the word you'll carry out of Hollowmere."

Corvane studied him for a long moment, and then smiled the kind smile all the way to the eyes, which was somehow worse than any other expression he'd worn. "Loyalty," he said softly. "How quaint. How expensive." He rose, buttoning his gray coat. "We ride at dawn, Master Halloway. But orders being orders, we'll leave two of our number behind. For the child's protection, you understand. In case she turns up needing help." He paused at the door. "Do sleep well. Fear dressed as prudence is still fear, and fear is such a talkative thing, given time."

After he'd gone, Bram sat alone by the dying fire with the untouched wine and the unpaid-for silence, and understood that he had not saved anyone. He had only spent himself, a coin at a time, buying hours.

Up the western slope, past the darkened fields, a light still burned in the window of a certain cottage. Two figures crossed it, briefly—one tall, one small—and then the curtain drew, and the light steadied, and held.

Bram drained the wine at last, because it seemed cowardly not to, and went home to lie awake beside the memory of his wife, listening to the valley breathe around him, counting the hours until dawn—and wondering, with a dread he could not shake, what the two remaining riders would do when the cottage light finally went out, and whether the sound of hoofbeats on the slope path would wake him, or whether he would sleep through it, complicit and comfortable, like a man dreaming safely in a house already on fire.
