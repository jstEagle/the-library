## 11. Run Before Moonrise

The knock came at the hour when even the goats had stopped complaining, and Felix knew before opening the door that nothing good traveled after dark in a valley that forgot.

Odile stood on the step with rain streaming off her hood and her eyes lit like a struck flint. She was seventy if she was a day, bent as a blackthorn branch, and she had not come to Felix's door in the six years Felix had lived within her grudging orbit. She did not wait to be invited.

"They're at the south pass," she said, wringing water from her cloak in a single vicious twist. "Three of them tonight, asking after a child of the old speech. By morning there'll be a dozen, and by tomorrow night they'll have every road out of this valley sewn shut like a wound." She thrust a bundle into Felix's arms—dried meat, a twist of willow bark, a small clay pot still warm from her hearth. "You have until moonrise."

"Odile—"

"Don't." Her voice cracked like a whip, but her hand, when it closed on Felix's wrist, was trembling. "I read your ledger theft, girl. Boy. Whatever you're calling yourself this decade. I know what you found in my pages, and I know you didn't find the last entry, because I hadn't written it yet." She leaned close, rain dripping between them, and her breath smelled of yarrow and woodsmoke. "The border's thinning. It thinned faster the week she arrived. Whatever that child is, she's a lamp in a dark room, and the moths are coming from three kingdoms away. Take her out before the light draws them all."

Felix's mouth had gone dry. "Out where? The passes—"

"The shepherd's track, north ridge. It's not a pass; it's a goat's mistake that goats have been making for two hundred years. The Order won't think of it until it's too late, because Order men don't talk to goats." Something flickered across her face then—not quite softness, but its distant cousin. "Felix. I exiled myself to this valley for reasons I've never told anyone, and I'll die here with them, and that's my choice. But that child—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "That child hums in her sleep. Did you know that? I heard her through your window last week, walking home. The whole hillside hummed back."

Felix had known. Felix had lain awake listening to it, telling themselves it was wind.

"Go," Odile said. "And don't look back at my cottage. If they burn it, let them think there was nothing in it worth saving."

She was gone into the rain before Felix could find a word—not thanks, not apology, though both sat in their throat like swallowed stones.

* * *

Petra was awake, of course. She was always awake when the world went wrong; some animal part of her seemed to taste trouble on the air an hour before it arrived. She sat on the pallet by the hearth with her knees drawn up, watching Felix pack with the unblinking attention she gave to everything, as if memorizing the world in case it were taken from her.

"We're leaving," Felix said. "Now. Tonight."

Petra nodded. No questions, no tears. That frightened Felix more than anything—the ease of it, the way the child gathered her one possession (a smooth gray stone she carried everywhere) and pulled on her boots without being told twice. Children who fled easily were children who had fled before.

Felix banked the fire, took down the satchel they'd kept packed for six years without ever admitting why—a hedge-witch's habit, Maren had called it once, half fond and half sorrowful: *you build doors into every room you live in*—and blew out the candle.

Outside, the storm had spent itself into a fine cold drizzle. The valley lay black under a lid of cloud, and somewhere far to the south, so faint it might have been imagination, torchlight moved along the ridgeline like a slow procession of fireflies that had learned cruelty.

Petra slipped her hand into Felix's without asking. Her fingers were small and cold and absolutely certain.

They went up.

* * *

The forest behind the croft was old growth, the kind the valley's forgetting had preserved precisely because no one remembered to cut it. Beech and ash closed overhead into a roof that dripped and dripped, and beneath it the dark was total, the kind of dark that pressed against the eyes like wool. Felix had walked these woods a hundred times by day and perhaps ten times by night, and never once without fear. Fear of roots, fear of drops, fear of the particular silence of a place where no owl hunted and no fox screamed because even the animals' small magics died at the ridgeline, and something in their bones knew it.

But tonight the silence broke.

It began as a sound Felix felt before hearing—a vibration in the palm where Petra's hand held theirs, a hum like a struck tuning fork buried in flesh. Then it rose into audibility: the child was singing. Not a song, exactly. There was no melody he could have written down, no rhythm a drummer could have kept. It was language reduced to its purest state, syllables flowing one into another like water over stones, and each syllable landed somewhere in Felix's chest and rang.

"What is that?" Felix whispered, and immediately felt foolish, because Petra had explained before—or tried to—and the explanations dissolved like sugar in rain. *Old words. First words. The words the Spire dreamed before anyone taught it to speak.*

But this time Petra answered differently. She squeezed Felix's hand and said, in her careful, accented modern tongue: "It's so the dark knows we're people."

Felix stopped walking.

Because it was true. That was the terrible, beautiful truth of it. Where Petra walked, the darkness changed quality—not lighter, exactly, but *occupied*. The trees did not lean away. The spaces between the trunks felt less like absence and more like room. Twice Felix caught movement at the edge of hearing—a deer, probably, or a badger—and instead of the flat dead silence of the valley's interior, there was the ordinary rustle of living things going about their living, drawn, perhaps, to the sound the way Odile's hillside had hummed back.

The valley forgot. But around Petra, for a few paces in every direction, the world remembered itself.

Felix walked on with a lump in their throat the size of an apple, and did not trust themselves to speak for a long time.

* * *

The trouble came at the stream crossing, an hour past midnight, when the clouds finally tore open and spilled moonlight silver as knife-blades across the water.

Felix saw it first as a wrongness in the reflection: lights on the eastern ridge where no lights should be. Torches—no, colder than torches. A pale, steady glow, evenly spaced, moving with military precision along the skyline. And beneath the glow, faint but unmistakable now that the border had thinned enough to carry it, a sound Felix had hoped never to hear again outside memory: the chanted cadence of Order working-magic, voices braided together to weave a net across the pass.

"They're sealing it," Felix breathed. "They're sealing the *valley*."

It was the oldest trick in the Order's book, older than the Ashen Hand itself: don't chase the quarry, close the box. A sealing-work laid across a valley's exits would do here what magic could not ordinarily do—it would make the deadness permanent, airtight, a tomb with a view. Anyone inside would be trapped until the Order came door to door, smiling, offering coin for information about a child of the old speech.

Petra tugged Felix's sleeve. She was looking not at the lights but at the sky above them, her head tilted, listening.

"They're afraid," she said.

"They're an army, little one."

"No." Her brow furrowed with the effort of translation, of dragging meaning between languages like hauling a net full of stones. "Under the working. Under the chant. They sing loud so they don't have to hear it." She looked up at Felix, and her eyes in the moonlight were old, old, old. "The Spire is louder tonight. Because I'm closer to the edge. They can feel it through their boots and they won't say so."

Felix thought of the gray-cloaked riders who had faltered at the treeline weeks ago, horses refusing the ridge, men dismounting to walk and walking badly, like swimmers out of their depth. Afraid. They had been afraid then, too.

Good, said a small ugly voice in Felix's chest. Be afraid. And underneath it, a larger voice that sounded uncomfortably like Maren: *Fear makes men cruel, and cruelty makes them thorough.*

"North ridge," Felix said aloud. "The goat track. We climb while they're busy singing at the south pass."

* * *

The climb nearly killed them both.

The shepherd's track was less a path than a suggestion the mountain occasionally honored—a shelf of scree and sheep-cropped grass angling up through black crags, slick with drizzle, crumbling at the edges into drops that the darkness mercifully hid. Felix went first, testing each hold, passing Petra's weight up hand over hand at the worst places, the child climbing with a sure-footedness that made Felix's heart seize. Of course she could climb. Whatever vault had raised her beneath the Spire, it had apparently not raised her gently.

Halfway up, the chanting from the south pass changed key.

Felix felt it as pressure in the ears, a popping like descent from altitude, and knew—even without training, even with six years of deliberate forgetting—that the seal had closed. Somewhere behind and below, the valley had just become a locked room. Every exit except this one, this goat's mistake, this crumbling shelf of mountain, was now wrapped in working-magic thick enough to stop a thought.

And then, worse: a new light. North. High on the shoulder of the ridge ahead of them, a single pale point bloomed against the stars, steady as a lamp in a window.

A watcher. Already placed. Already waiting.

Felix froze, one hand fisted in Petra's collar, pressing her back against the cold rock. Below them the drop fell away into cloud. Above them, the light. Between them and it, forty yards of open slope with nowhere to hide.

"Off the path," Felix breathed. "There's a hollow—I camped here my first winter—"

But Petra wasn't looking at the light. She was looking at Felix, and her face wore an expression Felix had never seen on it: apology.

"It sees us," she said simply. "It's a seeing-thing. Words in a bottle. It already saw." She slid her hand free of Felix's grip and stepped forward onto the open slope, into the wash of that pale light, and began, softly, to speak.

Not humming this time. Speaking. The First Tongue at full voice for the first time since the cottage, and the night *broke open* around the sound.

Felix had no words for what happened next, only impressions that would return for years in dreams: the light on the ridge guttering like a candle in a draft, bending, bowing—*the way the candle flames bowed when she dreams*, Felix thought wildly—and then going out. Not extinguished. *Answered.* The seeing-thing had been asked a question in the oldest language there was, and whatever answer Petra gave it, it chose to close its eye.

Silence rushed back in like water into a footprint.

Petra turned around. In the starlight her face was very pale and very young again, and she was shaking, fine tremors running through her small frame, and when Felix reached her she collapsed against their chest as if her strings had been cut.

"That one cost," she whispered. "Words spend. Nobody ever tells you words spend."

Felix held her and looked up at the empty place where the watcher had been, and understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, two things at once.

First: the Order would know. A seeing-thing silenced was a message in itself. By dawn, every hunter Serath commanded would know exactly which crack in the mountains the child had slipped through.

Second: they were going to make it off the ridge. Tonight, at least. Because a seven-year-old had just spent something irreplaceable to buy them the dark, and the dark, for once, had listened.

"Come on," Felix said, lifting her onto their hip, her head lolling heavy against their shoulder, her breathing gone thin and exhausted. "Sleep if you can. I'll carry you."

And high above the tree line, as the first gray hint of dawn touched the peaks ahead, Petra stirred just enough to murmur one more phrase against Felix's neck—not the First Tongue this time, but plain village speech, learned in a stolen autumn, worn soft as river stone:

"You smell safe," she said, and slept.

Felix climbed on into the breaking light, toward the passes, toward the world beyond the valley where magic waited like a dog behind a gate—and did not let themselves think about how much it would hurt when the gate opened.

Below and behind, faint across the miles, the sealed valley began, very quietly, to hum.
