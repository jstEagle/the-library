## 12. The Road Out of the World

The valley let them go the way a drowning man lets go of driftwood—clumsily, gratefully, with no thought for what came after.

Felix felt it happen at the ridgeline. One moment they were climbing through the last stand of pines, Petra's small hand gripping theirs, the moonless dark pressing close; the next, they crossed some invisible threshold in the air and the world *changed*. Not around them. In them.

It began as heat. A prickling along the forearms, deep beneath the skin, like blood remembering an old wound. Then the prickling became a hum, and the hum became a roar, and Felix staggered against a boulder with a sound that was half gasp and half sob.

"Felix?" Petra's voice, small and sharp with fear. "What's wrong? What's happening?"

"I don't—" Felix's hands were shaking. They held them up to what little light the stars allowed, and saw that they were glowing. Faintly. Unsteadily. Like coals someone had breathed on after years in the ash. Threads of pale gold light crawled beneath the skin of their fingers, up the blue roads of their veins, pooling at the wrists. "I don't know."

But they did know. That was the terrible thing. They knew exactly what it was.

Magic. The real thing, the living thing, the thing they had spent seven years pretending didn't exist—the thing that died at the valley's edge like a moth against glass. Out here, beyond the border, magic hadn't forgotten Felix Villalobos. It had been waiting. And now it came home to them all at once, all of it, every neglected lesson, every abandoned exercise, every hour Maren had drilled into their stubborn apprentice hands before Felix had thrown it all away and run.

The light flared. Pain lanced up both arms, white-hot, precise as needles. Felix cried out and dropped to one knee on the frost-brittle heather.

"Make it stop," they heard themselves whisper. "Make it stop, make it stop—"

"It won't," said a voice from behind them. "Not by asking."

* * *

Odile had refused to come with them. She'd stood in her doorway with her ledger clutched to her chest and told Felix, flatly: "I'm too old for roads, and this valley is my patient. Someone has to watch it die slowly instead of quickly." But she had pressed a small bundle into Felix's hands at the door—a stoppered clay flask, a twist of dried herbs, and three words of advice:

"Ground yourself. The first hour past the border is the worst. Your power's been starving in there same as you, and starved things eat fast when they're fed." She'd looked at Felix with those flat gray eyes that missed nothing. "You'll want to grab everything at once. Don't. Take it like water after drought—one swallow at a time, or it'll drown you."

Felix had not listened well enough. Starved things eat fast.

They knelt in the heather with their arms burning and their teeth clenched, and tried to remember the first lessons. Grounding. Maren's voice, seven years old and older than that: *You are not a vessel, Felix. You are a riverbed. Let it move through you.* Their breathing came ragged and shallow. The light under their skin pulsed with each heartbeat, brightening, dimming, brightening again, out of rhythm, out of control.

"Riverbed," they muttered through gritted teeth. "Riverbed, riverbed—"

"What is a riverbed?"

Petra had crouched beside them. Up close, in the starlight, her face was grave and attentive, the way she looked at things she intended to keep forever. Mud still streaked her cheek from the night's flight. Her eyes—those strange, old eyes that made Felix deeply uneasy when they caught the light wrong—were fixed on the glow crawling under Felix's skin with something that was not fear.

Not fascination, either. Something more like recognition. Like watching a dog remember how to bark.

"It's—" Felix laughed, breathless, bitter. "It's nothing. It's a thing my teacher used to say. You let the power move through you instead of holding it, because if you hold it—" The flare came again, sudden as a struck match, and Felix hissed and curled forward over their hands. "Because if you hold it, it holds you back."

Petra considered this with the seriousness of a scholar and the practicality of a child who has slept in ditches.

"You're holding it," she said.

"Yes."

"Why?"

"Because I'm afraid of it!" The words tore out of Felix before they could stop them, raw and ugly in the quiet dark. "Because I ran away from it! Because I spent seven years in a valley where none of this could touch me, and I *liked* it, I loved it, I loved being empty—" They stopped. Breathed. The glow guttered. "I loved being nobody."

Petra tilted her head. "You're not nobody," she said, with the simple, devastating certainty of a child stating that the sky is up. "You're the person who opened the door."

And she put her hand—small, cold from the night air, mud-streaked—directly onto Felix's burning forearm.

The world went white.

* * *

Later—minutes later, or hours; time had gone soft and unreliable—Felix found themselves sitting against the boulder with their head between their knees, breathing like a man hauled up from deep water. The pain had receded to a deep, thrumming ache, the kind that follows a fever broken. The glow under their skin had settled into something quieter. Still present. Still alive. But no longer screaming.

Petra sat cross-legged in front of them, watching with her chin propped on her fists.

"What did you do?" Felix rasped.

"I don't know." No guilt in it, no apology. Just fact. "Your fire was going sideways. So I told it which way was down." She frowned, hunting for words. "In the old speech there's a word for when a river remembers its bed. I said that. To your arms."

Felix stared at her. Seven years in the valley had taught them to translate wonder into manageable pieces, but this piece refused to be managed. A child, speaking the First Tongue—the language that had raised the Spire, sealed it, sunk kingdoms—and using it to do what? To soothe a hedge-witch's fraying nerves. To tell wild magic *which way was down*.

Maren would burn a year of sleep just to hear her say it.

The thought arrived uninvited and brought its usual cargo: guilt, sharp and familiar, the weight Felix had carried so long they sometimes mistook it for a spine. *You are not a vessel. You are a riverbed.* Maren's voice, always Maren's voice, even now, even here, even with the Order's riders somewhere behind them in the dark and Maren herself a tower of silence and unanswered letters.

"You're doing the face again," Petra observed.

"What face?"

"The one where you look far away and sad. You do it when you think about the tower lady." She said it without accusation. Simply, the way children say true things. "The letters that never came."

"The raven came," Felix said automatically. "With the burnt scrap. Half a warning."

"That wasn't a letter. Letters have words in them." Petra picked up a pebble and turned it in her fingers. "That was someone else's hand writing over hers. Or hers writing scared." She looked up, and her eyes caught the starlight and held it wrong, the way they always did. "Words sound different when the mouth is afraid. Even written ones."

Felix wanted to ask how she knew that. They were afraid of the answer. They were afraid of most answers now; the valley had trained it into them, this flinching from truth, this love of erasure. Odile's ledger with its dates of decay. The seal woven from lives. The phrase Petra repeated in her sleep like a prayer or an alarm. Every answer was a door, and behind every door was something Felix had spent seven years hiding from.

But they were past the border now. The dead-magic valley was behind them, and so was the luxury of not knowing.

"We should keep moving," Felix said, and got up, and found that their legs mostly worked.

* * *

They walked through what remained of the night, following a drover's track that wound down from the ridge into foothills silvered with frost. Magic moved through Felix the whole way—not the roaring flood of the crossing anymore, but a steady current, intimate and insistent, like a second pulse. It was agony and it was ecstasy and Felix hated how much they loved it.

Every few hundred yards, something new would surface. A cantrip Maren had taught them in the second year—*light, small and steady, cup it like water*—rose up complete in their memory, and when Felix instinctively flinched away from it, the knowledge *stayed*, humming, refusing to be forgotten. An old exercise in reading the weather through pressure on the skin. The muscle-memory of warding-signs their fingers had once traced a thousand times. All of it coming back. All of it *waiting*.

Once, passing a frozen stream, Felix's hand brushed a low-hanging willow branch and the branch shivered, unfroze, budded—three green leaves in the dead of winter—and Felix snatched their hand back as if burned.

"Did you see that?" they whispered. "Tell me you saw that."

Petra nodded solemnly. "The tree remembered spring." A pause. "You reminded it."

"I haven't practiced in seven years. I *ran* from it. This shouldn't—I should be useless, I should be starting over like a child, why is it all still *there*—"

"Because it's yours." Petra said it the way she said everything, plainly, terribly, like a stone dropped down a well. "Things that are yours don't leave just because you shut the door on them. They wait in the house."

Felix walked faster after that.

* * *

Dawn found them in a hollow between hills, where a shepherd's ruin offered walls waist-high and a windbreak of thorn. Felix built a small fire with trembling hands—not with magic, deliberately not, though the temptation sang in their fingertips—and set out Odile's hard bread and the last of the cheese. Petra ate like a wolf cub, quick and silent, eyes flicking to the treeline between bites. Old habits. Hunted habits. Felix's chest ached looking at her.

Then Petra stopped chewing.

She was staring at Felix's hands.

Felix looked down. Without deciding to—without noticing, the way one doesn't notice a habit returning—their fingers had begun tracing warding-signs in the air above the fire. Small ones. Idle ones. The little spiral that meant *warmth held here*. The three-pointed knot that meant *nothing harmful passes*. Seven years of nothing, and their hands had started praying in their sleep, so to speak, the litany rising up through the muscle and bone because the mind had finally stopped forbidding it.

The fire burned steadier where the signs passed. The light bent gently toward them, like plants toward sun.

"Oh," Felix said.

"You're doing it wrong," Petra said.

Felix blinked. "What?"

"The knot." She pointed at the fading three-pointed sign with her bread crust. "It's crooked. It's supposed to go—" and she traced a shape in the air, quick and sure, a shape that was almost the same and utterly, fundamentally different, and where her finger passed the air itself seemed to *lean in*, to listen, the way the candle flames had bowed when she dreamed—

The fire leapt. Not dangerously. Joyfully. Like a dog hearing its name.

Felix stared at their own hands, then at the fire, then at her, and something inside them cracked open along an old fault line. Because here was the truth they'd been dodging since the ridgeline, since the valley, since the day they'd run from Maren's tower with their ears ringing and their heart full of righteous, cowardly certainty: they had told themselves they left magic behind. They had built a life on the story that Felix Villalobos was finished with power, done with it, cleansed of it, better off empty.

And their hands had never believed a word of it.

"You're angry," Petra said.

"No." Yes. "No, I'm not—" Felix pressed the heels of their hands against their eyes. The glow showed pink through the eyelids. "I'm angry at me. There's a difference. There's supposed to be a difference."

"Why?"

"Because—" Felix dropped their hands and gestured helplessly at the fire, at the frost-bright hills, at the child sitting across from them with crumbs on her chin and centuries in her eyes. "Because I spent seven years telling myself I didn't want this. And it was a lie. It was a lie the whole time, and I *knew* it was a lie, and the lie was easier than the truth, and now the truth is in my hands again and it hurts worse than ever because—" Their voice broke. They hated that. "Because I love it. I love it, Petra. It's like a fever breaking. It's like being handed back a piece of myself I buried alive, and I hate that I love it, because the last time I loved it, people got hurt. People I—"

*Maren paid alone,* whispered the oldest guilt, the one Felix never let finish its sentence.

Petra watched them fall apart with no expression at all, which from her was its own kind of kindness. When Felix had run out of words, she reached across the fire, took one of Felix's glowing, shaking hands, and turned it palm-up in both of hers, examining it with a craftsman's attention.

"Your fire is very loud," she said at last. "But it's a good fire."

Felix made a sound that was mostly laugh and mostly sob.

"And you're bad at lying," she added. "Even to yourself." She released the hand. "Especially to yourself."

And then—Felix would remember this moment for the rest of their life, would turn it over and over in the dark years afterward like a coin worn smooth—Petra smiled. Not her rare, brief, watchful almost-smiles. A real one. Wide and crooked and entirely a child's, lighting her strange old face from underneath until she looked exactly her age for the first time since the storm-door night. And she *laughed*.

It was a small sound, rusty, startled out of her like a bird from cover—as if laughter were another language she was relearning, another tongue half-remembered. She laughed at Felix's stricken, glowing, self-loathing face, at the crooked warding-knots still drifting above the fire, at the tree that had bloomed in winter, at the sheer ridiculousness of a grown runaway witch sitting in a shepherd's ruin having a crisis while a seven-year-old corrected their sigils.

Felix stared. The ache in their chest changed shape. Some of the loathing drained out of it, not gone, but diluted, cut with something warmer.

"Well," they managed. "Glad I could amuse you."

"You're funny," Petra said, wiping her eyes. "When you're miserable. You get very dramatic." She demonstrated: clutched her own hands, rolled her eyes upward, intoned, "*Make it stop, make it stop*"—and dissolved into giggles again.

"I did not sound like that."

"You sounded exactly like that."

Felix found, to their considerable surprise, that they were smiling. Smiling, with the Order's riders somewhere behind them and a burnt warning scrap in their pack and a child of impossible speech depending on them. Smiling, with stolen magic singing under their skin like a returned exile.

Odile would have had something dry to say about that. *Comfort is the first casualty of honesty,* maybe. Or just her flat gray stare, which said everything.

"All right," Felix said. "All right. Eat your breakfast, you little horror. We have a long road and I have—" They flexed their fingers. The glow answered, obedient now, or nearly. Terrifyingly nearly. "I have a lot of practice to undo. Seven years of pretending to be a riverbed with no river."

Petra licked cheese from her thumb and regarded them seriously. "The river was always there," she said. "You just built a house on top of it." A pause. "Badly."

"*Eat your breakfast.*"

She grinned and obeyed—or pretended to, sneaking glances at Felix's hands between bites, and once, when she thought Felix wasn't looking, tracing a small careful shape in the air over her own knee. Felix pretended not to see. There would be time for questions. Time for lessons, maybe—her teaching them, gods help them both. Time for the road ahead, wherever it went: to Maren's tower, probably, because the burnt warning meant danger and danger meant Maren, and guilt was a compass that always pointed the same direction.

For now there was frost melting off the thorn bushes as the sun cleared the hill. For now there was a fire that leaned toward them like a friend. For now there was a child laughing, really laughing, and magic running warm in a runaway's hands, painful and intoxicating and theirs.

Felix banked the fire with a proper sign—straight, not crooked—and stood, and shouldered the pack, and looked north along the drover's track toward the hills where Maren's tower stood against the sky like a needle.

That was when they saw it: far off, at the limit of sight, a smudge of movement on the morning road. Riders. Gray-cloaked, moving with the unhurried confidence of hunters who already know where the quarry must drink.

Petra followed their gaze. Her laughter faded, but she did not look afraid. She looked—resigned. Old.

"They found the river too," she said quietly.

Felix took her hand. The magic rose in their palm, unasked, ready, hungry.

"Not yet, they haven't," they said. "Walk fast. Don't run. Running says we're guilty."

And they went down off the hill together, two runaways and a returning tide, toward the road out of the world—and whatever waited on it.
