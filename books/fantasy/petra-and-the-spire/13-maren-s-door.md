## 13. Maren's Door

The tower stood where Felix remembered it standing, and that was the first cruelty of the journey.

Everything else had changed in the years since Felix had fled down this same mountain path with a stolen satchel and a heart full of righteous, childish fury. The pine forest had crept closer to the road. The river had shifted its course, leaving a gravel scar through the meadow like an old wound. Even the village at the valley's mouth had grown a second chimney or two, a new barn, a fence where no fence had been.

But Maren's tower rose from its knoll exactly as it had always risen—crooked-topped, slate-roofed, wrapped to the third story in ivy that was not ivy at all but something older and greener and patient. It looked as though it had been waiting for Felix specifically, which was, of course, precisely what it had been doing.

Felix stopped at the edge of the tree line with Petra's small hand swallowed inside their own and let themselves feel the whole weight of it: the fear, the shame, the strange vertigo of returning to the one place they had sworn never to see again.

"Is that it?" Petra asked. She had learned enough of the common tongue by now to ask simple questions, though her accent turned every word into something half-song. "That's where your teacher lives?"

"That's where my *former* teacher lives," Felix said. "If she still does. If she doesn't turn us away. If she doesn't—" They stopped. There was no point cataloguing all the ways this could go wrong; the list was longer than the road behind them.

Petra looked up at them with those eyes that were too old for her face, eyes that had aged another decade in the space of a single word spoken against the Order's binding-spells. "You're afraid of her."

"I'm afraid *for* her," Felix said, and found, to their surprise, that it was true—or true enough. "There's a difference. Mostly."

They had crossed three days of hard country to get here. The salt flats lay behind them, white and blinding, and Maren had walked them leaning on Felix's arm, growing lighter with every mile, as though the world itself were slowly reclaiming whatever she had spent. She had refused to speak about it. She had refused to speak about most things, in the way of people who have been carrying a secret so long it has grown into their skeleton.

Now she stood beside Felix, gray-faced, her breath coming shallow, and studied the tower with an expression Felix could not read at all.

"It hasn't warded itself off," she said quietly. "It's warded itself *in*. Do you feel that?"

Felix did feel it, now that they were close enough. The air around the knoll had a texture to it, a tautness, like the skin of a drum. Magic pooled here—not dead, as it was in the valley they'd left, but held, compressed, pressed down under layers upon layers of working laid over decades. Maren had not merely protected her home. She had buried it alive.

"She knew we might come," Felix said.

"Maren has always known everything," Maren said dryly. "It's her worst quality." She coughed then, a wet, rattling sound that made Felix's stomach clench, and waved off their reaching hands. "Go on, then. Knock. I'll wait here and enjoy the suspense."

"You'll do no such thing. You can barely stand."

"And you can barely breathe, looking at that door. One of us should be sitting down for this, and it isn't going to be me out of spite." But when Felix started forward, Petra's hand tightening in theirs, Maren followed anyway, stubborn as a stone in a boot.

* * *

The door was black oak, iron-banded, unremarkable except for the fact that Felix knew there was nothing behind it that was unremarkable at all. The knocker was a ring of cold bronze shaped like a coiled serpent eating its own tail. Felix had polished that serpent a thousand times as an apprentice, on mornings when they'd earned Maren's displeasure, and their palms remembered the chill of it before their mind did.

They raised their hand and stopped.

"What do I even say?" they murmured. "Eleven years, and I walk up and knock? 'Sorry I abandoned you, sorry I let you seal yourself into a dying bargain alone, oh, and by the way, here's the living key to the Spire, hope that's not inconvenient'?"

Petra squeezed their fingers. "She taught you to listen," she said. "So listen first."

And Felix—who had spent months learning to hear the child, who had thought themselves a fair listener by now—did as they were told. They closed their eyes and opened that inward ear Maren had trained into them, the one that heard past sound into intention, past intention into the shape of things.

What they heard made their blood run cold.

Behind the door, the wards were not sleeping. They were *straining*. Layer after layer of protection, woven thick as felt, and beneath all of it something else—a hum, faint and rhythmic, like a heart beating inside a locked chest. And threaded through the hum, almost too quiet to catch, a voice. Not words. Not yet. Just pressure. Just attention.

The tower knew they were there. The tower had known since the tree line.

And it was asking a question.

Felix opened their eyes. "It wants something," they whispered. "Before it lets anyone in. Some kind of... proof. A password, maybe, or—"

Maren had gone very still. "A name," she said. "It will want a name. Mine is already written into it a hundred times over, but if it's asking again after all these years, then it isn't asking for mine." Her gaze slid, slow and reluctant, to Petra. "Child. What did you say to break the Order's bindings? On the flats. What word?"

Petra's chin lifted slightly—the stubborn tilt Felix had come to know as well as their own heartbeat. "I don't give that word away," she said. "Not to doors. Not to anyone who asks twice."

"This door won't ask twice," Maren said. "This door will simply never open, and we will die on this knoll, and I will die extremely annoyed about it."

Felix crouched to Petra's level. Up close, in the flat gray light, he could see how thin she'd gotten since the flats, how the brightness in her came and went like a candle in draft. "No one's making you," they said. "Whatever you choose, we find another way. We always have."

Petra considered the door for a long moment. Then she stepped forward, out of Felix's grip, and stood before the black oak the way she sometimes stood before the sea of grass beyond the valley—small, straight-backed, utterly unafraid of things much larger than herself.

She spoke.

It was only three syllables. Felix had heard her say them before, or fragments of them, in sleep and in play, and had thought they understood what they were hearing. But spoken here, against Maren's wards, against eleven years of layered intent, the First Tongue did not sound like language. It sounded like the moment before a storm breaks. It sounded like a key being cut to fit a lock that had waited a very long time.

The hum behind the door stopped.

Then, softly, with a sound like a long-held breath released, every ward on the tower let go at once. Felix felt it pass over their skin like warm water—the compression lifting, the straining ceasing, the whole buried weight of Maren's protections settling into stillness. The ivy-that-was-not-ivy shivered along the walls. Somewhere above, a shutter creaked open an inch, as if the tower itself were peering down to look.

The door swung inward without a touch.

And Maren Voss, last of the thirteen, the woman who had sealed the Spire with her own life and her own silence, stood in the doorway—and went white as bone.

* * *

For a moment nobody moved.

Maren's face did something complicated and terrible: shock passing through recognition, recognition passing through grief, grief arriving somewhere near the border of horror and setting up camp. Her hand found the doorframe and gripped it, and Felix watched the knuckles whiten, watched the great composure of her—the composure that had survived a war and a sealing and eleven years of Felix's absence—crack straight down the middle.

"*Where did you learn that word,*" she said. It was not quite a question. It was a demand wearing a question's clothes, and her voice shook on the last syllable.

"Maren—" Felix began.

"Inside. Now. All of you, inside, before the whole mountainside hears what she just said." She stepped back from the threshold, and her eyes never left Petra, tracking her the way one tracks a lit fuse. "Quickly."

They crossed into the tower's entry hall, and memory hit Felix like a physical blow. Nothing had changed here either. The same flagstones, worn concave by centuries of feet. The same shelves climbing the curved walls, crammed with jars and folios and things that shifted uneasily when observed. The same smell—dried yarrow, lamp oil, beeswax, and underneath it all the faint mineral tang of old magic, like rain on hot stone. Felix had run from this smell. They had told themselves they hated it. Standing in it now, chest tight, they understood they had hated it because they had loved it too much, and love had been the one thing they couldn't afford to owe anyone.

Maren shut the door. Her hand lingered on the wood a heartbeat longer than necessary, and Felix saw her murmur something—three syllables, the same three, reversed somehow, a lock instead of a key—and felt the wards settle back into place around them, gentler now, like a blanket drawn up rather than a fist closing.

Then she turned, and all that terrible composure came back, and she looked at Felix as if they were a stranger.

"Eleven years," she said. "Eleven years, and the first time I see your face again, you bring *that* to my door."

"I didn't bring it. She brought herself. She found me." Felix heard their own voice go high and defensive and hated it. "Maren, please. She's starving and hunted and the Order of the Ashen Hand has riders on our trail, and I didn't know where else—"

"The Order." Something flickered across Maren's face—too fast to name. "Serath's hounds. How close?"

"A day behind us. Maybe less. They sealed the passes behind us; we went over the scree instead." Felix hesitated. "Maren, they're not just hunting her. Odile thinks—I think—they want to use her. To open the Spire."

"I know what they want." Maren crossed the hall in quick strides, moving through the cluttered space with the blind certainty of long habit, and began pulling wards from the shelves—chalk-white stones, bundles of ash twigs, a stoppered vial that glowed faintly red. Her hands were steady. Her voice was not. "Sit down, both of you. Eat something. There's bread in the crock, it's two days old, I don't care. We have perhaps until nightfall before this tower stops being safe, and I am not having this conversation while standing."

"We haven't had any conversation yet."

"No," Maren said. "We haven't." And there it was again, under the briskness: the crack, the wound, the thing she was holding shut with both hands. She set the vial down harder than she needed to. "You ran from me, Felix. You ran from me without a word, without a letter, without so much as a stone turned on the path to show me which way you'd gone, and I woke one morning to an empty bed and an empty satchel hook and eleven years of wondering whether you were dead in a ditch somewhere. So forgive me if I need a moment to arrange my face before we discuss what you've brought to my doorstep."

The words landed like thrown stones. Felix stood in the middle of the hall and took them, because they had earned every one.

"I wrote to you," they said quietly. "From the valley. About her. About the words she speaks. The raven came back with burnt parchment and half a warning. Was that you?"

Maren went still, mid-motion, a bundle of ash twigs in her fist. For a long moment she said nothing at all.

"No," she said finally. "That was not me. I never received any letter." She set the twigs down, slowly. "Which means someone read it. Which means someone is watching the routes between here and your valley, and has been for some time, and I would very much like to know what else they've seen." She rubbed her eyes with thumb and forefinger, and just for a moment she looked every year of her age and several she hadn't reached yet. "Later. All of it, later. Right now—"

She turned to Petra.

The child had not sat. She stood near the cold hearth, mud still dried on her hem from three days of road, watching Maren with that unnerving, patient attention she gave to anything that frightened her. Now, meeting the old woman's gaze, she tilted her head.

"You're the one from the Spire's stories," Petra said.

The silence that followed was absolute. Even the fire in the lamp seemed to lean in.

"What stories?" Maren's voice had dropped to almost nothing.

"Thirteen voices made a promise," Petra said, in the careful cadence of someone reciting something learned by heart, something repeated ten thousand times in the dark. "Twelve kept it. One kept it longest. The Spire remembers her name. It says it to itself, sometimes, when it gets lonely." She paused. "It says it wrong, though. Names sound different when you say them to yourself."

Maren sat down. It was not graceful; her knees simply decided the matter for her, and she caught the edge of the worktable and lowered herself onto the stool beneath it, and for a moment she looked less like the formidable mage who had raised Felix and more like a very old woman receiving news of a death.

"It knows my name," she whispered. "After all this time. After everything I paid to make sure it would never—" She stopped. Pressed her lips together. When she spoke again, her voice was level by sheer force of will. "Child. What is your name? Your true name, the one you were given under the earth."

"Petra."

"That is a name someone gave you *after*. I'm asking for the first one. The one the voices used."

Petra's eyes flicked to Felix—quick, uncertain, seeking permission or courage or both. Felix nodded, once, and hated themselves a little for it.

"They didn't use names," Petra said softly. "They used the word. The one I said at your door. That's what I am called, down there. That's what I was sent up to carry." She lifted her chin. "Find someone who still listens. That was the whole instruction. That was all of it."

Maren looked at her for a long, long moment. Then she looked at Felix, and Felix saw the exact instant the old woman arrived at the same terrible conclusion Felix had been circling for weeks—the conclusion Odile's ledger had hinted at, the conclusion the thinning border had whispered, the conclusion that had chased them across the salt flats like a wolf at their heels.

"That word," Maren said. "Do you know what it means, Felix? In the First Tongue?"

"No. I hoped you would."

"It means *the door is opening*." Maren's hands were flat on the table now, steadying herself against it. "It is the first word of the unsealing rite. It is the word the Spire has been waiting eleven years—no. Longer. Centuries—to hear spoken aloud by a living throat. And a child walked up to my door today and said it like a greeting." She laughed, short and hollow, with no humor in it anywhere. "Of all the doors in all the world, she comes to mine. Either that is the greatest fortune of my life, or the cruelest joke the Spire has ever played, and I have never yet managed to tell the difference between the two."

Outside, far down the mountain, a bird startled up from the pines in a clatter of wings—too many wings, or none at all. Maren's head snapped toward the window, and when she turned back her face had hardened into something Felix recognized from old stories about the sealing: the face of a woman preparing to pay again.

"Get your cloak," she said. "Both of you. We're leaving within the hour—and Felix, while you pack, you are going to tell me everything. Every word she's spoken, every dream, every date in Odile's ledger. Because I built that seal with my life, child, and if it's failing—"

She stopped. Her hand had drifted, without her seeming to notice, to her own chest—over her heart, where something beneath the skin pulsed faintly, out of rhythm, like an echo answering a call it could not refuse.

"—then it is taking me with it," Maren finished quietly. "And I would very much like to know how much time we have left to stop it."
