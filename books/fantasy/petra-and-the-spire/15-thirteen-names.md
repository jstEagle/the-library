## 15. Thirteen Names

The fire in Maren's tower had not been lit in years, and it burned the way old things burn—reluctantly at first, then all at once, as if ashamed of its hesitation. Felix crouched before it, feeding it split birch from the stack by the hearth, and tried not to look at Maren, who sat in the chair opposite with her hands folded in her lap like a woman waiting for a verdict.

Petra had fallen asleep an hour after they arrived, curled on the window seat beneath Felix's own travel cloak, her breathing slow and even. Even asleep she hummed, very faintly, that language without edges. The candle flames on the mantel leaned toward her like flowers toward sun.

Maren had not stopped looking at the child since the door opened. She looked at her now the way one looks at a wound one has spent decades pretending is healed.

"You said thirteen," Felix said at last. They kept their voice low, though there was no one to wake but Petra, and Petra slept through storms. "You said the seal was woven from thirteen lives. Whose?"

Maren's hands tightened in her lap. In the firelight Felix could see how old she had become—not merely older than memory made her, but *worn*, the way a stair is worn, the way a coin is worn by passing through too many hands. When Felix had fled this tower eleven years ago, Maren had seemed carved from the same stone as the mountains. Now she seemed like something the mountains had let go of.

"You understand," Maren said, "that once I speak these names aloud, you cannot unhear them. You cannot go back to being someone who doesn't know."

"I haven't been that person for some time."

"No." A ghost of a smile, there and gone. "No, I suppose you haven't. You never could leave a locked box alone, even as a boy. Especially as a boy." She corrected herself gently: "Even as whatever you were. Are. Forgive me. I knew you before you knew yourself; the old words stick."

Felix fed another branch to the fire and said nothing. They had learned, in the valley, the discipline of silence—the hedge-witch's patience, letting a thing come to the surface in its own time, like a root loosened from soil. But beneath the patience their heart was hammering, because they understood what Maren was doing. She was gathering herself the way one gathers oneself before a confession. And Felix had already heard half of one tonight.

Maren rose, crossed to the great oak cabinet against the eastern wall—the one Felix had been forbidden to open as an apprentice, forbidden so many times and with such weight that the prohibition itself had become a kind of door—and drew from it a bundle wrapped in oilcloth, tied with cord gone gray with age. She set it on the table between them but did not untie it.

"Thirteen mages," she said. "The strongest of our generation, or close enough. We called ourselves the Concordance, which tells you how young we were—young enough to think a grand name would make us grand. Serath. Ilva. Corvane. Odile's sister, Ysolde—that's why Odile hides in your valley, if you ever wondered; grief keeps its own borders. Tammas Bright. The twins, Hesper and Hallow. Old Wexl, who everyone said was too old for it and who insisted he was exactly old enough. Merrin of the Fenlands. Ashka. Dov. Ilva again—" no, Felix thought, she's listing, she's stalling, "—and me."

She counted them off on her fingers as she spoke, and Felix watched her fingers move and understood, with a cold settling in their stomach, that this was not the first time she had performed this counting. This was a rite she had rehearsed alone for thirty years, in the dark, in this tower, with no one to say it to.

"Ten are dead," Maren said. "Not died. Were *spent*. The seal required lives freely given—not taken, never taken, the Spire knows the difference the way a lock knows a key cut from the wrong metal—and each of us gave what we were. Not all at once. That's the part no one understands, the part I couldn't tell you when you asked me, all those years ago, why I wouldn't let you help." Her voice caught on the last words, and she steadied it with visible effort. "A seal woven from thirteen deaths would have lasted a century, perhaps. A seal woven from thirteen *lives*—lives still lived, still burning, poured out slowly across decades—that lasts as long as we do. We are the wick, Felix. The valley's forgetting is the wax. And the Spire sleeps inside the flame."

The fire popped. On the window seat, Petra turned over in her sleep and murmured something in a language that predated kingdoms, and both of them flinched.

"So when a sealer dies," Felix said slowly, working it through, feeling the shape of it like a bone set wrong, "the seal thins."

"When a sealer dies, the seal must be rewoven around the absence. The others carry more. Ten of us carried the weight of thirteen for years, and then nine, and then eight—" Maren lifted her left hand, and in the firelight Felix saw, for the first time, what they had taken for age or arthritis: the fingers were translucent at the tips, faintly luminous, as though the light behind the world shone through her where her flesh had been spent. "I am the eighth name still living. Can you see why I wrote to you so rarely? Why I couldn't answer your letter?" She laughed, short and bitter. "Your raven arrived three days after you'd already fled the valley. I sent what I could—a warning, half-finished, because I was mid-working, holding the border together with my teeth while some fool of an Order acolyte probed it from the north. You got ash and half a sentence. I'm sorry. It's the most honest thing I've given anyone in thirty years."

Felix stood. They hadn't meant to stand. Their legs had decided on their own, and now they stood at the hearth with their back to the fire, looking down at this woman who had raised them, trained them, lied to them, and burned herself slowly alive to keep a promise the world had forgotten existed.

"The two missing," Felix said. "You said ten dead. Thirteen names. Who's missing?"

Maren was quiet a long moment. Outside, wind moved through the high pines with a sound like the sea, like the sea Felix had never seen, like every story about distant water.

"One is Ilva," she said finally. "Ilva of the White Hands. She walked away from us twenty-two years ago—walked out of the last renewal working, in front of all of us, and said, 'I will not feed this thing one more year of myself.' None of us stopped her. None of us *could* stop her; she'd earned the right to refuse if anyone had. We told ourselves she'd died in the mountains. It was kinder than the truth, which is that she was right and we knew it." Maren's jaw tightened. "Find her, if you can. If she still lives, she may be the only person alive who remembers the bargain as it was actually made—as opposed to how we retold it to ourselves until it sounded noble."

"And the third."

"Felix—"

"The third, Maren. You've counted twelve. There were thirteen. You didn't teach me arithmetic badly enough for me to miss that."

Maren closed her eyes. When she opened them, she looked, for one unguarded moment, not like a mage or a mentor or a dying wick but like a girl who had once made a terrible choice beside someone she loved, and had spent her whole life since deciding whether that love or that choice defined her.

"Serath," she said.

The name landed in the room like a dropped glass.

* * *

Felix had heard the name before, of course. Everyone had. Serath the Shield-Hand, Serath the Unyielding, the hero of the Spire War whose statues stood in three provincial capitals and whose face was stamped on the silver marks the Order of the Ashen Hand paid its informants with. Children played at being Serath the way children elsewhere played at being kings. He was the man who had sealed the great evil away. He was the reason the world was safe.

"He leads them," Felix said. It wasn't a question. They had seen the riders on the ridgeline; they had read the warrant-language in the Order's broadsheets; they had watched Bram's face when the gray-cloaked agents came asking after a child of the old speech. But hearing it connected—*he* was one of *them*, the thirteen, the wicks, the ones who gave themselves—was like watching two rivers turn out to spring from the same wound in the mountain.

"He leads them," Maren confirmed. "He has led them for nineteen years. And Felix, you must understand—he did not fall. That's the lie everyone tells, that power corrupts gradually, that good men slide. Serath leapt. He chose. He chose the moment he understood what the seal actually cost, and he decided he would rather own the price than pay it forever."

She untied the oilcloth bundle at last. Inside lay a book—not a grimoire, nothing so dramatic, but a plain ledger bound in gray leather, its corners soft with handling. Maren opened it to a page near the middle, and Felix saw columns of figures in thirteen different hands, dates going back thirty years, amounts recorded with the dry precision of a merchant tracking grain.

"What is this?"

"Our tithes. What each of us pours into the seal, measured each season. Look at the last decade." She turned pages forward, and Felix saw it: the entries thinning, faltering, gaps widening as name after name went dark—and then, beginning eight years ago, a new column, in a hand Felix didn't know, recording *withdrawals*. Drawings from the seal. Sums flowing out instead of in.

"Someone's been taking from it," Felix said. "Draining it."

"Serath found a way to reverse the flow. The seal is a channel; a channel runs both ways if you're ruthless enough to dig the second bed. Every season he draws more. Power enough to fuel the Order's workings, his wards, his armies' enchantments, his own—" Maren hesitated over the word, as if it were hot iron— "his own *ascension*. He means to become what the Spire is. He told us so, once, at the last renewal any of us attended. He stood in the circle where we had all bled and said, 'Why should the mountain keep what we mined?' And Ilva laughed at him, and Corvane struck him, and he left, and within five years he had an order of knights wearing his sigil and a doctrine that sounded like protection."

Felix looked at the ledger, at the patient arithmetic of sacrifice and theft, and felt something cold assemble itself in their chest. "And the child. Where does Petra fit in his design?"

Maren glanced toward the window seat. The girl slept on, small and mud-stained and humming, utterly unaware that the most powerful man in the world had spent months hunting her.

"She's the key—or she's the voice, I don't yet know which, and neither does he, and that's the only mercy in any of this. The seal answers to the First Tongue. It always has; that's the tongue the Spire speaks, the tongue the bargain was spoken in. A seal woven in the First Tongue can be renewed in the First Tongue—or unmade in it. Serath can't speak it. No living soul can; it died with the last generation born under the old sky. Except—" and here Maren's voice did something Felix had never heard it do, something small and afraid— "except apparently it didn't die. Except it sent itself out of the dark wearing a child's body, walking down a mountain into a storm, to find someone who still listens."

"That phrase she repeats." Felix's mouth was dry. "The one she says like a prayer. You recognized it."

"I recognized it because I helped write it." Maren pressed her palms flat against the table, as if holding it down in a gale. "It's the first clause of the bargain. The opening line of the sealing. 'That which waits shall wait willingly.' Thirty years ago, thirteen of us stood at the foot of the Spire and spoke those words together, and the Spire answered, and the answering nearly killed us all. If she carries that clause in her mouth—if she delivers it back to the heart of the seal—"

"It renews."

"It renews. Or it opens. Depending on who asks, and how, and what they intend. Which is why Serath wants her alive and untouched. Which is why he'll burn provinces to get her. He doesn't want to destroy the Spire, Felix. He never wanted it destroyed. He wants to finish the bargain—on his terms, with himself as the beneficiary. He wants to walk into that heart and take what we spent our lives refusing to take."

The fire had burned low. Felix fed it the last of the birch and watched the flames climb, and thought about the valley behind them, the quiet place where spells died at the ridgeline, the sanctuary that was really a wound dressed as a harbor. Thought about Maren's translucent fingertips. Thought about a child sent out of a vault of loneliness with one instruction: *find someone who still listens.*

"Why didn't you tell me?" The question came out quieter than Felix intended, worn smooth by the night. "Eleven years ago. When I found the stolen pages, when I asked you what the First Tongue was—you told me to burn them. You told me some doors only open outward. You let me run."

"Because you were nineteen, and furious, and I loved you, and if you had known—if you had known what I was spending myself on, what the valley's peace was costing—you would have stayed. You would have offered yourself. You'd have demanded a place in the weave, and I would have had to refuse you, or worse, accept." Maren met their eyes, and there was no armor anywhere in her face. "I had already buried ten friends inside a spell. I would not bury you too. So I let you hate me instead. It was the cheaper price. I've regretted it every day since, and I'd pay it again tomorrow."

Felix turned away, because there was nothing in their face they wanted witnessed either. On the mantel, the candles bowed toward the sleeping child. Somewhere far to the north, past the passes the Order was even now sealing, a man who had once been a hero sat in a citadel built on drained sacrifice, reading reports about a mud-stained girl and a runaway hedge-witch, and deciding how much fire it would take.

"Maren," Felix said. "If Serath gets her—"

"Then the age of bargaining ends, and the age of ownership begins. And the land itself will scream, because the Spire was never a treasure, Felix. It was a wound in the world that agreed to behave. Take its consent away and you will learn what it is when it stops behaving." Maren closed the ledger. Her luminous fingertips left faint prints on the gray leather, glowing, fading. "We need Ilva. We need the truth of the original terms before anyone speaks anything at the foot of that mountain. And we need to move soon, because Serath's riders reached the valley's edge yesterday, and the border that protects your goats and your herbs is thinning faster than any natural decay can explain."

Felix looked at the window seat. At the child. At the candle flames leaning toward her like a congregation toward its god.

"She's hastening it," Felix said. "Being here. Being near the seal. Isn't she."

Maren didn't answer. She didn't have to. The silence said it plainly: the safe harbor was closing, the hunters were circling, and the only road left ran toward the very thing everyone had spent thirty years agreeing to fear.

Above the pines, past the ridgeline, the first gray light of a bad dawn was gathering—and with it, distantly, from the direction of the village, the sound of horns.
