## 16. Lessons at Midnight

The tower had a way of making time feel optional.

Felix noticed it on the third morning, or what they assumed was the third morning—the light through the eastern slit-window came at the same slanted angle it had come the day before, and the day before that, and Felix could not have sworn to how many meals they'd eaten or how many times Petra had fallen asleep mid-sentence with her cheek against the worn oak of the table. Maren's wards did more than keep people out. They kept *hours* out too, blurring everything into a single long amber afternoon, like flies caught in honey. It should have been maddening. Instead, Felix found themselves grateful for it, because every hour that passed without a knock at the door, without ash-gray cloaks on the road below, was an hour stolen back from the thing that was coming for them.

They both knew it was coming. Neither of them said so.

Instead, there were lessons.

It had started, as most of their disasters and small mercies did, with bread. Felix had been trying to teach Petra to knead—she handled dough the way she handled everything, with an unnerving directness, as though the flour might confess something if she stared at it hard enough—and she had asked, in her careful, foreign-voiced way, *"What is this called? The pushing?"*

"Kneading," Felix had said. "K-N—" and then stopped, because she was looking at them with that expression, the one that meant a word had landed somewhere deep and she intended to keep it.

From there it had grown the way ivy grows: quietly, overnight, until it was everywhere. Every morning now began with words. Felix would write them in charcoal on the slate propped against the hearth—*water, bread, door, storm, afraid*—and Petra would copy them in her strange, precise hand, each letter formed like it was being set in stone. In exchange, she taught Felix scraps of hers. Not the great working-speech, not yet; she guarded that the way a bird guards its nest, offering only twigs. But small things. A word for the color of dusk. A word that meant *the moment just before rain*. A word with no modern equivalent at all, which she used only when looking at Felix, and which Felix had privately decided not to ask about, because some translations were better left undone.

Tonight the tower was loud with wind. A true storm had found them past the wards—Maren's protections bent weather around the stone but couldn't erase it entirely—and rain lashed the high windows while the fire cracked and spat, and Petra sat cross-legged on the rug drawing in the ashes with a stick of charcoal twice the size of her fist.

"Say it again," Felix said. "Slowly."

Petra drew a breath and spoke, and the candle flames bowed toward her—not dramatically, not the way they'd bowed that first night in the valley cottage, but gently, the way grass leans when a child runs past. The word she said was short. Three syllables, maybe four; Felix still couldn't always count them right, because the First Tongue had a habit of folding sounds inside other sounds, like hands closing over coins.

"What does it mean?"

"It means—" Petra frowned, pressing her lips together, hunting for the shape of it in Felix's language. "It means when you are cold, and someone puts a blanket on you. But also the blanket. But also—" She gestured vaguely at the fire, the rain, the whole warm room. "Also this."

"There's no word for that here."

"I know." She said it without pride, the way you'd note that a neighbor's house had no well. "Your words are very... separate from each other. Mine are holding hands."

Felix laughed before they could stop themselves—a real laugh, startled out of them, the first in longer than they could remember. Petra looked up sharply, suspicious, then pleased, then suspicious again, as if laughter were another word she needed to learn the grammar of.

"Again," she commanded, pointing the charcoal at them. "Do the laugh again."

"No."

"Do it."

"You can't order me around, I'm your teacher."

"You are my teacher of *your* tongue. I am your teacher of mine. We are equal." She said it with such gravity that Felix laughed again, and this time Petra laughed too—a bright, unpracticed sound, like something breaking carefully—and the candles stood up straight, and outside the storm went on hammering at a world that wanted them dead, and none of it, for one whole minute, mattered at all.

* * *

The lessons changed shape as the nights went on.

Felix learned that Petra's mind worked in pictures first and words second. When Felix wrote *afraid* on the slate, Petra didn't repeat it; she drew instead—a small figure under a large dark shape, hatched over with furious lines—and then pointed at the figure, then at herself, then waited. Only after Felix nodded did she take up the charcoal and write *afraid* beneath it, letter by letter, as if pinning the feeling down so it couldn't move.

She was learning faster than any student Felix had ever seen, and Felix had been a teacher's disaster once, which made them, they supposed, an expert on students. Words stuck to her like burrs. By the fifth night she could ask for water, complain about the food (which she did constantly, and with growing sophistication), and name every object in the tower's main room except the astrolabe, which she refused to call anything but *the lying star*, and Felix eventually gave up arguing.

In return, Felix's own tongue grew stranger. They began to hear the valley-language of their childhood differently—heard the gaps in it, the places where meaning had been sanded off over centuries. Petra's fragments filled those gaps like water filling footprints. And Felix noticed, with a scholar's unease and a hedge-witch's dread, that when Petra spoke even these small words, the tower answered. Not the wards—those were Maren's, and they held—but the *stone itself*. Dust motes drifted toward her voice. The fire leaned. Once, when she hummed a fragment of some old song while washing at the basin, the water in the basin went perfectly, impossibly still, a coin of silver, and stayed that way until she stopped.

Felix didn't tell her. Some knowledge was a weight, and Petra carried enough.

But they did write it down, in the little journal they kept hidden under a floorboard—*her voice moves matter. small things only. she doesn't seem to know she's doing it.* And below that, because honesty had become a habit they couldn't break: *I don't know what I'm preparing her for. I don't know what I'm preparing myself for.*

* * *

On the seventh night—or the ninth; the tower blurred them together—Petra couldn't sleep.

Felix found her in the window alcove, knees drawn up, watching the storm-wracked dark beyond the glass. Lightning walked the distant hills in slow, silent strides. She didn't turn when Felix sat beside her, but she leaned, slightly, deliberately, until her shoulder rested against Felix's arm. It was the first time she had initiated touch. Felix sat very still, the way you do when a wild thing chooses to land near you.

"The gray men," Petra said at last. Her English had grown fluent enough for the words to come easily, which somehow made them worse. "You dream about them. You think I don't see. Your face does the *afraid* picture even when you're awake."

"My face does no such thing."

"It does. Right here." She touched the space between her own eyebrows. "It makes a small fold. Like paper."

Felix exhaled. Below them, far off, a light moved along the valley road—too steady for a lantern carried by a farmer, too slow for anything innocent. It vanished behind the tree line. Neither of them mentioned it.

"When I was small," Petra said, "before the voices raised me, I lived in a place with no names. Everything was there, but nothing was called. I ate fruit I could not have told you the name of. I slept in a house I could not have told you the name of. And I remember thinking—even then, even small—that it was like being a word that nobody had written down yet." She turned her face up toward Felix. In the lightning-flash her eyes looked older than her body by decades. "Then the voices gave me my instruction, and sent me out, and the first person who wrote me down was you. You said 'Petra.' You gave me the name I wear." A pause. "So whatever happens to me next—I was written by you first. Remember that. Whatever the gray men say I am."

Felix's throat closed. They thought of all the things they could say—that they'd protect her, that they'd run forever, that names given freely couldn't be taken back. All of it true and all of it insufficient. So instead they said, "I'll teach you to write your own name tomorrow. Properly. Both tongues."

"I already know how," Petra said. "I've been practicing in the ashes when you sleep."

And she showed him—showed them—traced in the cold hearth where the fire had died: *Petra*, in careful modern letters, and beneath it, in the folded, interlocking script of the First Tongue, the same name, glowing faintly, faintly, like a coal remembered rather than seen.

The candles bowed. Outside, the moving light on the valley road had stopped.

Felix watched it stop, and felt the warmth of the room pull tight around them like a blanket drawn up against a cold that had finally found the door.
