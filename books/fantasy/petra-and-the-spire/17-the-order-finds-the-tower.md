## 17. The Order Finds the Tower

Petra woke before the birds did.

She had learned the tower's rhythms in the weeks since they had come to it: the groan of Maren's stairs at dawn when the old woman climbed to her workroom; the tick of the water-clock in the hall; Felix's footsteps, soft and uneven, on their way down from the loft where Petra slept. The tower was a living thing in its own way, breathing through its stones, and Petra had learned its breath the way she had once learned the breath of the vault beneath the Spire—by lying still and listening with her whole body.

But this morning the tower's breath was wrong.

The wards hummed. She had not known they hummed until now, because she had never heard them strained. It was a sound below sound, a thread of tension running through the walls like a wire pulled too tight, and it woke her the way a hand on the shoulder wakes a sleeper—not gently, but with meaning.

She sat up in the dark of the loft. The window showed a sky the color of wet slate, dawn still only a rumor behind the eastern hills. Frost furred the edges of the glass. Somewhere below, Felix was already stirring; she could hear the small domestic noises of them lighting the stove, setting the kettle, moving through a morning that did not yet know it was the last ordinary morning.

Petra pressed her palm flat against the floorboards.

*They are coming.*

She did not know how she knew. That was the trouble with what she was—knowledge arrived in her the way weather arrived over mountains, without asking permission, without explanation. But she knew it the way she knew her own name, which was to say: not entirely, and not comfortably. There were men in gray coming up the mountain road, and they moved the way water moves when it has found a crack in a dam—patiently, inevitably, filling every low place.

She dressed quickly, pulling on the wool stockings Maren had knitted and the coat that had belonged to some earlier inhabitant of the tower, too big in the shoulders, smelling faintly of cedar and old smoke. On the stairs she passed the row of Maren's preserved specimens in their jars—a salamander coiled in amber fluid, a sprig of silverleaf that never wilted—and for the first time she thought they looked less like curiosities and more like prisoners.

Felix stood at the hearth, feeding the fire. They looked up when she came in, and something in her face must have told them, because their hands went still and the kindling dropped from their fingers.

"What is it?" Felix asked. "Petra. What is it?"

"They're coming." Her voice came out small in the stone room. "Up the road. Before light."

Felix crossed the room in three strides and pulled back the shutter on the western window—the one that faced the switchback trail below the tower. The glass was frosted at the corners; Felix breathed on it and wiped a circle clear with their sleeve.

For a long moment there was nothing. Just the gray slope, the black pines, the thin ribbon of the road where last autumn's leaves drifted like dirty snow.

Then the trees moved. Not with wind—with purpose. Shapes detaching from shapes, gray cloaks against gray trunks, six of them, then more behind, climbing without torches, without voices, in a silence that was itself a kind of spell. Petra felt it even from here: a hush laid over them like a blanket over a birdcage, muffling everything they were.

"Ashen Hand," Felix said. Their voice had gone flat and strange, the voice of someone reading their own death warrant aloud to check the spelling. "Strike-team. That's a strike-team formation. Six forward, support behind, a speaker at the center to—" They stopped. Swallowed. "Maren. *MAREN!*"

They were already running for the stairs, taking them two at a time, shouting the old woman's name into the dark above. Petra followed, her bare feet silent on the cold stone while Felix's boots rang like alarm bells.

Maren's workroom door was open. The old woman stood at her table with her back to them, and she was already working—her hands moving over a spread of chalk lines and iron filings and a bowl of water that trembled though nothing touched it. Her white hair was loose down her back. She had not slept either, Petra realized. She had been awake all night, listening to her own wards the way a farmer listens to a storm.

"I know," Maren said, before Felix could speak again. "I've known since moonset. They've been walking since midnight—they stopped at the tree line to wait for the light." She turned, and her face was terrible in its calm, the calm of a woman who had done arithmetic she did not like and gotten the same answer three times. "Six ward-breakers, two binders, one speaker. Nine. And Serath's mark on their leader's collar, which means he wants the child alive and does not much care about the rest of us."

"Can you hold them?" Felix asked. "The wards—"

"The wards are old and I am older." Maren smiled, and it was the saddest thing Petra had ever seen. "They'll hold for a quarter hour after sunrise. Perhaps twenty minutes if I feed them everything I have left in me, which I intend to do."

"Then we run," Felix said. "Out the cellar, the old mine tunnel, Odile said—"

"The tunnel collapsed forty years ago, child. I let Odile believe otherwise because it kept her from digging in it." Maren turned back to her table and began rolling up her sleeves, revealing forearms mapped with old scars and faded ink—sigils burned into the skin so long ago they had become part of her. "There is no running. There is only the door, and what we say when they knock."

Petra stood in the doorway and felt the word rise in her before she understood she was going to say it.

"No."

Both of them looked at her.

"You keep saying *we*," Petra said. "You and Felix. You keep saying what *you* will do. But they are not coming for you." She heard her own voice shake and hated it, and kept talking anyway, because the words were true and true words deserve to be said even by shaking voices. "They are coming for me. For what I can say. So it should be my door too."

Maren looked at her for a long moment. Something moved behind the old woman's eyes—grief, or recognition, or both wearing each other's clothes.

"She's right," Felix said quietly. "Maren. She's right, and you know it."

"I know it," Maren said. "Knowing it doesn't make it bearable." She crossed the room and knelt, slowly, joints cracking, until she was eye-level with Petra. Up close her face was a landscape of wrinkles, and her eyes were pale as winter water. "Listen to me, child of the Spire. What I am about to do will buy us minutes, no more. When those men come through my door, they will come with binding-spells woven so tight that a grown mage couldn't wriggle free in a year. Do you understand? They will not fight us. They will *tie* us."

"Yes."

"But you—" Maren took Petra's hands in hers. Her fingers were cold and dry as paper. "You speak the tongue underneath their spells. The tongue their whole art is built on top of, like a house built on older foundations. If the foundation speaks, the house listens." Her grip tightened. "But hear the cost of it, because no one told me the cost until I had already paid it, and I will not do that to you. The First Tongue is not a tool. It is a debt. Every word you speak in it, you pay for out of yourself. Out of your years. Your strength. Your—" she hesitated— "*self*. Do you understand me? It spends you."

Petra thought of the vault beneath the Spire. Of the voices that had raised her, growing quieter each year, thinner each year, spending themselves syllable by syllable until sending her out into the world had been less a choice than a last coin clutched in a dying hand.

"I understand," she said. "Better than you think."

Maren held her gaze a moment longer, searching for the lie, and finding none. Then she rose, and the moment for gentleness was over.

* * *

Dawn came up the color of an old bruise.

Petra stood at the tower's high window with Felix beside her and watched the gray shapes resolve into men. Nine of them, fanning out across the frozen yard below in a formation so practiced it looked choreographed. At their center walked a tall figure whose cloak bore the ash-gray hand sigil stitched in silver thread, and whose face—

Whose face Petra could not see, and was glad of it.

The leader raised one gloved hand. The others stopped. And then, in unison, nine voices began to speak.

It was not the First Tongue. It was its descendant, its diluted grandchild—the modern craft-tongue, all structure and no soul, words cut into shapes that could be stacked and mortared into spells. Petra felt the binding-spell go up around the tower like a net thrown over a hawk: threads of force lacing together above the roof, weaving a cage of quiet, designed not to break anything but to hold everything—hold the doors shut, hold the air still, hold the people inside like flies in amber.

Below her, Maren stood alone in the yard.

The old woman had gone out to meet them, because of course she had; that was the kind of person Maren was, the kind who put her body between the wolf and the sheepfold and called it strategy. Petra watched her raise her arms, watched the frost on the ground leap upward in glittering spirals as the tower's last stored power poured through her, watched the first wave of Ashen Hand mages stagger as the wards flared—

—and began, thread by thread, to fail.

It happened faster than Maren had promised. Petra understood why a moment later: the nine were not attacking the wards. They were *unraveling* them, speaking the craft-tongue in careful counterpoint, picking apart Maren's weaving the way children pick apart a sweater, one pulled thread at a time. It was patient work. It was horrible to watch. Maren threw everything she had into the weave and the weave came apart anyway, gracefully, almost politely, and Petra saw the moment the old woman felt it—saw her shoulders drop half an inch, saw her head bow.

The leader stepped forward. Even at this distance his voice carried, amplified by craft, smooth as oiled wood:

"Maren of the Western Reach. By authority of the Order of the Ashen Hand, you are commanded to surrender the vessel known as Petra, and submit yourself to tribunal for crimes against the Sealed Accord. Resist, and you will be bound. Harm any brother of the Order, and you will be bound *harder*. These are generous terms. The Order remembers its friends."

His friends, Petra thought. He means his friends. He means the twelve others who wove the seal, the ones who split like a dropped plate, and he stands there calling murder generosity.

Beside her, Felix made a sound low in their throat—not quite a word, not quite a growl. Their hands were curled against the windowsill, and Petra saw, with a strange detached clarity, that they were trembling. Not with fear. With the effort of *not doing something*. Whatever training Felix had fled from all those years ago was in their hands right now, begging to be used, and they were holding it down like a dog that wanted to bite.

"We can't let them take her," Felix whispered. It wasn't clear whether they meant Maren or Petra. It didn't matter. It was both.

The last ward-thread snapped with a sound like a harp string breaking. Below, Maren sank to one knee in the frost, spent, and the nine closed around her like gray water closing over a stone.

And Petra stopped watching.

* * *

Later—much later, in another life, in whatever life came after this one—she would try to explain what it felt like, and she would never quite manage it. Words failed in both directions. The First Tongue could not be described in the modern tongue, and the feeling of speaking it could not be described in any tongue at all, because it was not like lifting something heavy or running somewhere fast or even screaming. It was like being *poured out*.

She went down the tower stairs two at a time and out through the kitchen door into the freezing yard, and she heard Felix shouting behind her—*Petra, no, PETRA*—and she did not stop, because stopping was a thing the old Petra would have done, the girl who waited to be told, and the girl who waited to be told was exactly what the Order had been built to catch.

The frost crunched under her feet. Nine faces turned toward her. Maren, kneeling, lifted her head and mouthed one word—*no*—and Petra met the old woman's eyes and shook her head once, gently, the way you'd refuse a second helping at supper.

The binding-spells were already reaching for her. She felt them as cold threads against her skin, seeking her wrists, her throat, her jaw—because of course they sought the jaw first; a captured speaker is a silenced speaker; the Order's whole architecture assumed that mouths could be locked like doors.

Petra opened her mouth, and spoke.

Not loudly. That was the strangest part, remembered afterward—that she did not shout. The First Tongue did not need volume. It needed only to be *true*, and she was the truest thing in the yard.

"*Veth-kal-irum.*"

One word. Three syllables. A word whose shape meant, roughly, in the way that water is roughly wet: *unbinding; the loosening of that which was fastened by force.*

The world rang.

Not the air—the world. The frost leapt off the grass in a single silver breath. The binding-spells, nine of them interlaced into one great net, did not shatter so much as *remember themselves*—remembered that they were made of stolen language, built on borrowed foundations, and the foundations spoke, and the whole elegant structure came apart like a sandcastle meeting the tide. Petra felt it go: nine mages' worth of craft unraveling in the space of a heartbeat, whipping back along its own threads, and the nine gray-cloaked figures staggered as their own bindings closed briefly, harmlessly, humiliatingly around their wrists and throats and released.

Someone cried out. Someone else fell. The tall leader alone stayed upright, and even he swayed, and even he put a hand to his throat as if checking that his voice still belonged to him.

And Petra—

Petra paid.

She felt it leave her. Not pain, exactly. Pain would have been simpler. It was more like... subtraction. As if a page had been taken from the book of her, cleanly, professionally, and she could feel the gap where it had been without being able to read what had been written there. Warmth drained out of her fingertips. The colors of the dawn went slightly grayer, slightly further away, as if seen through one more pane of glass than before.

And when she raised her hands to her face, they looked—older. Not aged the way a picture ages. Aged the way a candle ages, burned down. The knuckles sharper. The veins more visible. Small hands becoming old hands, and she was eleven years old, or she had been, and something in her eyes—

She knew without seeing. She could feel it, the way you feel a missing tooth. Something behind her eyes had been spent. Some brightness, some *youth*, drawn up out of her like water drawn from a well, and gone into the word, and gone into the world, and not coming back.

That was the cost. That was what Maren had tried to tell her. The First Tongue does not spend mana or strength or any of the currencies the modern craft traded in. It spends *you*. Every word is a withdrawal from an account that cannot be refilled. And she had just bought nine mages' humiliation with a piece of her own childhood, and the terrible thing, the thing she would carry secretly forever, was that in that first raw moment she could not tell whether it had been worth it.

"Petra!"

Felix reached her first, dropping to their knees in the frost, catching her by the shoulders. Their face was white. "Your eyes—Petra, your *eyes*—"

"It's fine," she said, and heard how thin her voice had gone. "It's fine. I'm fine."

"You're not. Look at me. *Look at me.*" Felix tilted her chin up, and their expression when they saw whatever was there—she watched it happen, watched their face change, watched horror arrive and be mastered and arrive again. "They're—there's gray in them. Around the edges. Like frost on a window. Oh gods, Petra, what did you *do*?"

"What I came here to do," she said. "What I was sent to do. One of the things, anyway." She managed a smile, and felt it sit crookedly on her older mouth. "You always wanted to know what I was for. Now you've seen a piece of it."

Behind them, Maren got slowly to her feet. The nine Ashen Hand mages were regrouping at the edge of the yard, shaken, furious, re-weaving—but slower now, warier, because they had felt what she was, truly felt it, and fear makes even fanatics pause. The tall leader was speaking rapidly to his people in a low voice, and though Petra could not hear the words, she could read the shape of them in his posture: *report back. This changes things. We need more than nine. We need Serath himself.*

Which meant they would withdraw. Which meant minutes, perhaps an hour. Which meant—

"Run," Maren said. The old woman's voice cracked across the yard like a whip, and it was not a request. "All three of us, *now*, while they lick their wounds. Salt flats, Felix. The salt flats and the mountains beyond. We are past the time for hiding and past the time for cleverness. All that's left is distance." She was already limping toward the tower's rear gate, already tearing strips from her own hem to bandage a gash on her forearm that Petra hadn't seen her take. "Move, children. Grieve later. There's a whole lifetime of grieving ahead of us if we live, and not one minute of it if we don't."

Felix gathered Petra up—one arm under her knees, one behind her shoulders, lifting her as if she weighed nothing, as if she were made of paper instead of spent language—and ran.

Over Felix's shoulder, Petra watched the tower shrink behind them. Watched the gray-cloaked figures at its edge, not pursuing, not yet. Watched the tall leader raise his hand—not in threat, but in salute, or something shaped like one—and mouth words she was too far away to hear but too much herself to misunderstand:

*Soon.*

She settled her head against Felix's chest and listened to their heart hammering, and thought about the piece of herself she had left in that frost-covered yard, and decided—quietly, privately, in the place where she kept the things she wasn't ready to say aloud—that she would spend every last page of herself before she let them take her home in chains.

The salt flats lay east. The sun was rising.

They ran toward both.
