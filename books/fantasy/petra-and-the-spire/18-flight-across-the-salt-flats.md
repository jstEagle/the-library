## 18. Flight Across the Salt Flats

The mountains had been a rumor for two days before they became a fact.

Felix saw them first as a smudge of blue-gray on the horizon, so faint they might have been weather, might have been a trick of the light on all that white. But each hour the smudge sharpened, and by the second dawn it was unmistakable: a wall of peaks rising out of the earth's edge like the teeth of something buried, and above them—though Felix told no one they'd noticed—a thin column of haze that did not move with the wind.

The Spire's breath, maybe. Or its dreaming. It was hard to say which was worse.

They crossed the flats in the gray hour before sunrise and again in the last light after sunset, because Odile had told them once, in one of her rare moments of unguarded honesty, that salt under full sun could blind a person in a day, and a blind traveler on open ground was a dead one. So they slept through the worst of the heat beneath a lean-to of oiled canvas stretched between Maren's staff and Petra's small shoulders, and they walked when the world went soft and silver, and Felix counted the hours not by the sun but by how much weaker Maren seemed to grow.

It made no sense. That was the thing Felix kept returning to, turning over like a stone looking for what lived underneath. Maren had been old, yes—older than she'd ever admitted, older than the tower suggested—but she had been *strong*. In the gorge she had held a pass against eleven hunters with a working so vast Felix still dreamed about it: the whole cliffside shrugging its shoulders, stone remembering it was once mountain. She had walked away from that with dust in her hair and blood at her ear and her spine straight as a plumb line.

Now she leaned on her staff the way an old woman leans on a fence rail, not for balance but for company, because standing upright alone had become a negotiation.

"Maren." Felix fell into step beside her as the stars gave way to a bruised pink dawn. "Eat something."

"I've eaten."

"You've nibbled. There's a difference, and I learned it from you, so don't argue with your own teaching."

Maren's mouth twitched—the ghost of the dry smile that had once made Felix feel, in the tower's cold years, like there was warmth somewhere in the world worth walking toward. She took the strip of dried goat from Felix's hand and chewed it slowly, obediently, and Felix hated how easy it was to make her obey now, how little it took.

"It isn't hunger," Maren said at last. She said it quietly, in the tone she used for things she'd already decided not to discuss further, which meant of course that Felix was going to discuss them. "Stop watching me like a hawk watches a field mouse. I'm fine."

"You're lying."

"I'm *managing*." She swallowed. Looked east, toward the mountains, toward the haze. Her eyes did something complicated—longing and dread braided together so tightly Felix couldn't tell which strand was which. "There's a difference in that too."

* * *

By midmorning, sheltering from the light, Felix understood what Maren would not say.

Petra found it first. The child had a way of finding things—noticing the small wrongnesses that adults trained themselves past—and she came crawling out from under the canvas with her brow furrowed, holding her hand out flat, palm up, the way she did when she wanted to show rather than tell.

"Look," she said, in the modern tongue she was learning word by word, each one handled like a bird that might startle. "Maren's shadow."

Felix looked. The sun stood low and hot behind the eastern ridges, and everything on the flats threw long shadows westward: the canvas lean-to, the packs, Felix's own crooked silhouette. Maren sat just outside the shade, and her shadow lay beside her on the salt like a spilled inkwell.

Except it was *fading*. Not thin, not stretched—faded, the way a dye fades through washings, the black gone gray at the edges, going translucent near her feet until it seemed less a shadow than a memory of one. And where the shadow should have been darkest, at its heart, Felix could see the salt crystals beneath it. Could see them *through* it.

"Petra." Felix kept their voice level, the way you keep a hand level when a horse is spooked. "Go sit with Maren. Ask her to tell you about the tower cats. All of their names. She'll pretend to be annoyed."

Petra looked up at Felix with those too-old eyes—eyes that had aged a season in a single morning when she spoke the shattering word against the Order's binding-spells—and for a moment Felix thought she would refuse, would ask the question Felix didn't want asked. Then she nodded, solemn as a small priestess, and crawled back under the canvas, and a moment later Felix heard Maren's reluctant voice begin: *"Well. There was Bristle, who was nobody's cat..."*

Felix crouched where the shadow had been and pressed two fingers to the salt.

Magic, when Felix had been young and stupid and hungry for it, had felt like drinking. Like cold water down a dry throat. What they understood now—what the valley's dead air had taught them across seven quiet years—was that magic was never really taken. It was *borrowed*. Every spell drew from somewhere: the land, the speaker, the woven reserves of a life. And every borrowing left a debt.

The seal had been woven from thirteen lives. Maren had confessed it in the tower's cold kitchen with her hands flat on the table as if steadying herself against a gale: thirteen mages who had given themselves, wholly or in part, to bind the Spire's power into sleep. And now they were walking toward the Spire, day by day, mile by mile, and Maren was fading like ink in rain.

The seal was calling back its investment.

Felix sat back on their heels in the blinding light and let that truth settle into them like a swallowed stone. Somewhere behind them lay the valley, the passes the Order had sealed, Wren bleeding in an undercity they might never see again. Somewhere ahead lay the Spire, humming audibly now even across forty miles of salt, a sound more felt than heard, a pressure behind the teeth. And between here and there walked a dying woman who refused to name her dying, guarding a child who might be the key to everything, accompanied by a runaway apprentice who had spent seven years learning how to be no one and was discovering, mile by terrible mile, that no one was exactly who they'd always been.

"Grief later," Felix said aloud, to no one, because saying it made it a plan instead of a feeling. "Walk now. Grieve later."

* * *

They walked.

The salt flats were not silent, though travelers' tales called them so. They creaked, at night, as the crust settled and shifted—a sound like vast slow footsteps, like the earth turning over in its sleep. They hissed when wind dragged fine crystals across the surface in long whispering sheets. And once, in the deepest hour of the third night, they *sang*: a single high harmonic note that rose from nowhere and everywhere, held for ten heartbeats, and died.

Petra sat bolt upright in the dark, eyes enormous.

"You heard it," Felix said. Not a question.

"The Spire." Her voice was hushed, reverent, frightened. "It's talking in its sleep."

"What did it say?"

She was quiet a long time. Under the canvas, Maren's breathing had gone shallow and rapid, and Felix could hear her fighting for each one, and pretended not to, because pretending was the only kindness left that she would accept.

"It said—" Petra's hands moved as she searched for the modern words, shaping them clumsily in the air. "*Alone.* It said alone. But not—" she frowned, frustrated, "—not sad-alone. *Waiting*-alone. Like when you put bread by the window and watch the door."

Felix's chest hurt. "Like waiting for someone to come home."

"Yes." Petra nodded fiercely. "Home."

They walked on. Felix taught Petra new words as they went—*ridge*, *thirst*, *endure*—and Petra taught Felix scraps of hers in return, syllables that tasted of frost and deep water, and neither of them mentioned the fading shadow, and both of them watched it.

* * *

On the fourth day the salt began to fail.

It happened gradually, then all at once: the flawless white crust giving way to patches of gray grit, then to cracked mud baked into crazy paving, then to actual soil, brown and stubborn, with weeds crowding the cracks. Life creeping back into the world the closer they drew to the Spire, as if its dreaming leaked outward and fed whatever ground received it. Felix knelt and touched a weed—some tough aster, blooming months out of season, its petals an impossible violet—and felt, faintly, under their fingertips, the hum.

Their hands woke too.

That was the other thing no one had warned them about, or perhaps someone had and Felix hadn't listened; they'd stopped listening to teachers long ago. Beyond the valley's border, magic returned violently, painfully, like blood rushing into a limb that had slept for years. It prickled in Felix's palms. It buzzed behind their sternum. Neglected training flickered alive without invitation—sparks jumping between their fingers when they were afraid, the air warming around them when they were angry, small unwanted workings blooming like bruises. On the flats it had been manageable, a distant tinnitus. Here, forty miles from the Spire, it was a shout.

And Maren—

Maren was dying by inches, and the closer they came, the faster the inches went.

She stopped hiding it on the fifth night. They had made camp in the lee of a broken ridge, the first real stone since the flats began, and Felix built a fire from scrub-cedar while Petra counted out the last of the dried fruit with the fierce precision of a child who had known hunger and would not be surprised by it twice. When Felix brought Maren her share, she took it, and looked at it, and set it down untouched.

"Felix." Her voice was very steady. Steady the way ice is steady. "Sit down. We need to talk about the mountains."

"We'll talk when you've eaten."

"*Felix.*"

The sharpness of it startled them both. Maren closed her eyes, and when she opened them again they were wet, and Felix realized with a lurch that in all the years of apprenticeship—in all the years of exile after—they had never once seen Maren cry.

"I know what's happening to me," Maren said. "I've known since the tower. Since before the tower, if I'm honest, though knowing a thing and admitting it are different countries, and I've been living in the first one." She laid her hand on the salt-crusted ground, palm down, and Felix saw that her fingers trembled. "I am part of the seal. Thirteen lives, woven into thirteen anchors, and mine is one of them. The seal has been fraying for years—that's why the valley's border thinned, why Odile's ledger showed the decay, why the Order could finally reach us at all. And now we're walking straight toward the wound. Every mile, the Spire pulls a little harder at what it's owed. I can feel it reeling me in like a fisherman who's been patient a very long time."

"Then we stop," Felix said. "We go around. We find another way to—"

"To what? To leave the seal broken and Serath holding the knife?" Maren shook her head, and the motion cost her; Felix watched her pay for it. "There is no around, child. There is only through. There was only ever through—I just hoped I'd have more road than this."

"How much?"

Maren smiled, and it was the worst thing Felix had ever seen, because it was gentle. "Enough. Probably enough."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only kind I have left." She picked up the dried fruit at last, deliberately, and ate a piece, and made herself swallow, and Felix understood that this too was a gift—an effort made so that Felix could stop begging. "Listen to me. Whatever happens at the Spire, you have a job, and it isn't saving me. Your job is her." Her eyes went to the fire, where Petra sat cross-legged, humming under her breath, and where—Felix saw it clearly now, had seen it building for days—the flames bent toward her slightly, leaning in like flowers toward sun. Listening. "Everything else is weather."

"Maren—"

"Promise me nothing," Maren said quickly, sharply. "Don't you dare promise me anything. Promises are currency in that place, and the Spire keeps accounts better than any banker ever born. Just—" and here her voice cracked, finally, one clean break like ice on a pond, "—just be kinder than I was. To her. To yourself. To the thing in the mountain, if it comes to that. I bargained with it out of fear, and fear makes cruel terms. You've always asked better questions than I did. Ask them there."

Felix sat very still. The fire popped. Out in the dark, the flats creaked their vast slow creak, and beneath it, faint but unmistakable, the hum of the Spire rose like a held breath.

"I'm not ready to lose you twice," Felix said.

Maren reached over and, with the last of her strength or perhaps simply her stubbornness, tucked a strand of hair behind Felix's ear, the way she had when Felix was nineteen and furious and packing to leave.

"You won't lose me," she said. "You'll lose my body, maybe, if it comes to that. But I've been pouring myself into that seal for sixty years. Whatever's left of me afterward will be standing in the mountains, keeping the sky on. Try to be proud of that instead of sad. If you can manage it." A pause. The ghost-smile again. "You never could manage pride. It was your worst flaw and your best feature."

* * *

They climbed.

The foothills came up to meet them on the sixth day, gray-green slopes stitched with scree and stunted pine, and the air turned cool and thin and smelled of stone and snowmelt and something else—something electric, mineral, ancient, like the smell of a struck flint magnified a thousand times. The hum was audible now without touching the ground. It lived in the rocks. It lived in their own bones; Felix could feel their teeth buzzing faintly when they clenched them.

Maren rode the pack-goat they'd bought from the last farmstead, because walking had become beyond her, and she permitted this arrangement with a grimace that contained, somewhere deep inside it, gratitude. Petra walked ahead, tireless, drawn uphill like water finding its level, and where she stepped the pines leaned, very slightly, in her direction.

On the evening of the seventh day they crested a shoulder of rock and saw it.

The crater opened below them like a bowl of dusk, miles wide, ringed with cliffs of glassed sand—the scars of some battle so old that history itself had been melted smooth. Rivers of obsidian ran down from the rim in frozen black falls. And at the center, rising from a plain of fused glass that mirrored the early stars, stood the Spire.

It was taller than any tower Felix had ever imagined, and wrong in the way that dreams are wrong: its proportions refused to settle in the eye, so that looking at it too long produced a vertigo, a sense that it was somehow both nearer and farther than it appeared. It was black, and it was silver, and it was neither; it drank the starlight and gave back something older. And the sound of it—gods, the *sound* of it—filled the whole crater, filled the whole sky, a chord with no beginning, a note that had been sounding since before music was invented.

Petra made a small sound. Felix turned, alarmed.

The child was crying—but smiling, both at once, tears running freely down her face as she stared at the Spire with an expression Felix had no word for except *recognition*.

"It's so lonely," she whispered. "All this time. It's been so lonely, and it's right *there*."

Behind them, Maren slid from the goat's back. Felix caught her before she hit the stone.

She weighed almost nothing. That was the horror of it—she weighed almost nothing, as if the Spire's pull had already drawn most of her elsewhere, as if she were becoming a rumor of herself. Her eyes found Felix's, and they were clear, terribly clear, and calm.

"There it is," Maren murmured. "Sixty years, and there it is." Her hand rose, trembling, and pointed—not at the Spire but at the trail descending into the crater, the switchbacking path of glass and ash. "Not far now. And Felix—" her grip tightened on their wrist with sudden ferocious strength, the strength of a final warning, "—look. At the trailhead. Do you see them?"

Felix followed her finger.

Far below, tiny with distance, torchlight moved along the crater's rim. Not scattered light—organized light. Columns of it. Torches in ranks, descending the far switchbacks in disciplined silence, a river of flame flowing down toward the Spire's base.

The Order had not been chasing them.

The Order had simply taken the longer road, and arrived first.

"They're already inside the crater," Maren breathed. "Serath's army. He's going to open the heart tonight." She laughed, softly, horribly, a laugh with blood in it. "We walked seven days across the end of the world, and we're a half-day late."

Petra wiped her face. When she turned to Felix, the tears were gone, and what remained in their place was something that had been growing in her all this long road, quietly, the way the weeds grew in the salt: something patient, and waiting, and no longer afraid.

"No," she said. "We're not late." She took Felix's hand, and her small fingers were warm, and humming. "We're right on time. We're the ones it's been waiting for."

Below them, the torch-river flowed on, and the Spire sang its endless note into the dark, and Maren's weight grew lighter in Felix's arms with every passing heartbeat, like something being gently, patiently, called home.
