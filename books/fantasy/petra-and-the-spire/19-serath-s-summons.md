## 19. Serath's Summons

The citadel of the Order of the Ashen Hand had been a monastery once, and it still smelled faintly of incense and old stone, though the prayers had long since been replaced with ledgers. Serath liked that about it. A building that remembered what it used to be was a useful thing to work inside; it reminded him daily that transformation was not corruption, no matter what the sentimental said. The monastery had become an instrument. So had he.

He stood at the high window of his study on the morning he composed the summons, watching frost crawl across the glass in patterns that were almost, but not quite, geometric. Below, the training yards were white with early snow, and the sound of drill-blades rose up to him like the ticking of some enormous patient clock. Two hundred initiates. Four hundred sworn hands. Archives three floors deep beneath his feet, and in the deepest vault of all, a sealed cistern of power that he had been drinking from, drop by careful drop, for eleven years.

None of them knew. None of them needed to know. That was the whole art of leadership, as Serath understood it: you carried the weight so that others could walk upright.

The letter lay open on his desk before him, its parchment pale as a peeled egg. It was not ordinary paper. He had ground the ink himself at midnight — ash from a burned oath-scroll, suspended in oil pressed from grave-lilies, thickened with a bead of his own blood, which was the least of what he had given the Order over the years and the only part anyone would have thought to mourn. An enchanted letter of this grade did not simply deliver words. It delivered *weight*. When Felix Villalobos broke the seal — and Serath had no doubt whatsoever that Villalobos would break it, because hedge-witches were constitutionally incapable of leaving a sealed thing alone — the words would arrive not as ink on a page but as a voice in the room, low and reasonable, unmistakably his. He wanted that. There were things better spoken than written, and threats were only one of them. Truths were another.

He dipped the pen.

*To the runaway apprentice Felix Villalobos,* he wrote, and paused, because the opening mattered. Not *to the thief*. Not *to the harbinger*. Those were true, or near enough, but truth deployed carelessly was a blunt instrument, and Serath had not survived this long by swinging blunt instruments. He wrote instead:

*To Felix Villalobos, formerly apprentice to Maren of the Tower, keeper of the western passes.*

Courtesy first. Always courtesy. Let the man — woman? Serath had never been certain, and had never cared enough to press the question — let them feel addressed rather than hunted. Fear made people stupid, and stupid people ran in straight lines. He needed Villalobos thinking clearly, because clear thinking would lead them, inevitably, to the conclusion he had already prepared for them.

*You do not know me, though I knew your master when she was young and foolish in ways you cannot imagine.* A small lie braided into a large truth; Maren had indeed been young and foolish, and Serath had loved her for it, in the way one loves a blade before it has been honed. *I write to you not as an enemy but as the last of a company that included her, and I write because time has run out for the luxury of silence.*

He set down the pen and walked to the window again. This was the discipline of composition: you could not write a letter like this in one sitting. You had to let each paragraph cool, test it against your own throat, ask whether it would sound like mercy or menace to a frightened person reading it by candlelight in a valley that forgot. The answer, ideally, was both. Mercy and menace were the same message viewed from different ends of the corridor, and the corridor was the future, and Serath had spent thirty years staring down it without blinking.

Through the frost-ferns on the glass he could see the eastern mountains, where the Spire slept under its shroud of bargain and bone. On clear days like this one he could almost feel it — a pressure behind the sternum, a hum below hearing, the sensation of standing near a door that someone had nailed shut from both sides. Eleven years of sipping from the cistern had given him appetites he did not care to name and senses he could not switch off. He could feel the seal thinning now. Not failing — not yet — but thinning, the way ice thins over a well in late spring: invisibly, gradually, and then all at once.

And he could feel the child. That was the strangest gift the cistern had given him, and the one he trusted least. Somewhere west and south, past the dead-magic border of the forgetting valley, there was a small bright wrongness moving through the world, a note struck on an instrument that should not exist. Petra, the scouts called her. The child of the old speech. Serath had read every report twice, then a third time alone in the vault with his hand pressed flat against the cistern's cold stone, listening to what sloshed beneath, and he had felt the note resonate. The seal recognized her. Which meant the Spire recognized her. Which meant everything depended on who reached her first, and what they asked her to say.

He returned to the desk.

*Here is what I know, and here is what you must weigh.*

*The seal upon the Spire is dying. Not weakening — dying. It was woven from thirteen lives, and ten of those lives are spent. The valley you hide in is not a sanctuary; it is a bandage over a wound, and the wound has begun to weep around the edges. You may have seen it yourself. Flowers out of season. Needles that will not hold their point. Dreams that leak. If you have not yet seen it, you will.*

This was the dangerous paragraph, the one that required all his craft, because it was entirely true and therefore could not be defended against with ordinary cynicism. Villalobos would check. Hedge-witches always checked. They would walk to the ridgeline and find exactly what Serath described, and they would come back to the letter shaken, and shaken people grasped at the hand that offered steadiness. He made sure the next paragraphs offered a hand.

*I do not want the child harmed. Whatever the Order's rank and file believe, whatever the zealots among my own council whisper, I have no interest in breaking the Spire open like a piñata and letting the age of wonders spill out over a world that has forgotten how to survive wonders. What I intend is renewal. A new seal, woven properly, with consent and preparation and time — not the desperate botch my company managed in our youth, which cost us ten of thirteen and bought the world forty years of borrowed quiet. Forty years nearly spent.*

Another pause. Another turn at the window. Snow was beginning now, fat unhurried flakes, and somewhere below an initiate shouted a count and two hundred voices answered. Serath watched them, these children of the ash, and felt the old familiar tenderness that he had learned to mistake for nothing at all. He had recruited half of them personally. He had sat with their mothers in kitchens and promised, honestly, that the Order would give their daughters purpose and their sons discipline and that neither would ever be asked to die for a lie. He had kept that promise so far. He intended to keep it forever. It was remarkable, he reflected, how much evil a man could accomplish while keeping every promise he made — provided he was careful about which promises he offered.

*Now the terms, plainly, because I despise riddles and I suspect you inherited that from Maren.*

*Surrender the child to my envoys, unharmed and willing, and the valley is spared. Every soul in it. I will withdraw the riders from the passes, strike the village from our lists, and leave your goats and your herb-beds and your small gray life exactly as you found them. You have my oath on it, and my oath is the one currency in this world I have never debased. Ask Maren, if you can get her to speak of me, which I doubt.*

*Refuse, and understand what refusal means. The seal does not fail gently. When it goes, it goes all at once, and everything the valley has been forgetting comes home at speed — every buried spell, every drowned working, forty years of dammed magic through a broken dam, with a child of the First Tongue standing in the flood channel. The valley dies. Not because I will it. Because arithmetic wills it, and physics older than kingdoms, and the bargain my company struck in blood and haste. I am offering you the only exit from that outcome. There is no third door. Believe me; I have looked for one for thirty years, and looking is how I came to lead the Order, and I found nothing but wall.*

He read it back. It was good. It was very good, if he said so himself, and he permitted himself the rare indulgence of saying so, alone, to the frost and the falling snow. Every sentence was load-bearing. Every claim was either true or unfalsifiable. The threat was real — the seal genuinely would kill the valley when it broke, and Serath had run the calculations himself more times than he could count, because he was honest with himself about consequences even when he was dishonest about everything else — and the mercy was real too, or real-shaped, which in practice amounted to the same thing. Surrender the child and live. It was a reasonable offer. Any reasonable person would take it.

That was the beauty of it. That was the whole architecture of his life. Reasonableness was the finest armor ever forged, because no one thought to strike at it.

What the letter did not say — what no paragraph could carry without collapsing the entire structure — was what renewal actually required. Thirteen lives. Again. Fresh ones, freely given or otherwise, though Serath had already begun assembling the list, and the list was longer than thirteen, because a prudent architect built in redundancies. And at the center of the new weave, holding the keystone position that Maren's generation had filled with their own hearts' blood, Serath intended to place something better than a mortal life. He intended to place the child herself — or rather, the fragment of the Spire that wore the child like a coat. Seal the Spire with a piece of the Spire. Close the lock with its own key. Eternal, self-sustaining, requiring no further harvests, no more kitchen-table promises to weeping mothers, no more slow sip-sip-sipping from a cistern that could never, ever be enough.

And at the center of *that*, unspoken even in the vault of his own skull except on the bad nights, was the simplest truth of all: that whoever wove such a seal would stand outside it. Would hold the threads. Would be, in every way that mattered, the steward of all the magic in the world — the hand on the valve, the voice at the gate, the one to whom petitioners came kneeling. Serath had watched sorcerers squander power and kings squander thrones and mages squander miracles, and he had concluded, with the humility that was itself a kind of pride, that the world's problem had never been power. Its problem was that power kept ending up in the hands of people who wanted things. He wanted nothing. He wanted only order, and continuity, and the permanent, benevolent, unanswerable management of the impossible. That this management would be his was not ambition. It was triage.

The snow thickened outside. He sealed the letter with wax the color of dried blood and pressed into it the sigil of the Ashen Hand — the open palm, the falling flakes of gray — and then, after a moment's thought, turned the sigil slightly, so that the palm seemed less raised in blessing than extended in offer. Small things. The small things were where letters won or lost.

He summoned the courier at midday: a lean, silent woman named Vetch who had once been a falconer and whose birds flew through weather that grounded anything with a heartbeat. The raven took the letter-tube from Vetch's gauntlet with the delicacy of a surgeon and stood on the windowsill a moment, head cocked, one black eye reflecting the study, the fire, the man.

"West," Serath told it. "Past the ridge where the wind forgets. Find the one who smells of goat and feverfew." He hesitated, then added, quieter, "Do not land near the child. Circle high. Some things see farther than feathers."

The raven went out into the snow like a thrown knife.

Serath remained at the window a long while after it vanished, hands clasped behind his back, watching the mountains. The hum behind his sternum had changed pitch in the night — he had woken at the third bell with his teeth aching and the taste of lightning in his mouth, and he had known, before fully waking, what it meant. The border was leaking. The child was closer to the edge of the valley than the reports suggested, or the valley itself was thinner than any report could capture, and either way the clock he had been winding for thirty years had begun to run down audibly.

There would be a reply, or there would not. If there was a reply, it would be defiance dressed as negotiation — Villalobos would haggle, stall, demand guarantees, buy time the way the poor buy bread: grudgingly, in crumbs. Serath would grant every concession requested and mean none of them, and the correspondence would continue until the riders were in the village square, at which point the correspondence would become moot. If there was no reply, then Villalobos had already chosen flight, and flight had a shape, and the shape pointed toward the tower of Maren of the western passes like iron filings pointing north.

Which was, Serath admitted to the empty room, almost elegant. The runaway would run to the mentor. The mentor would confess. The confession would crack open everything Maren had spent forty years burying, including the location of the vault-door and the names of the dead, and Serath's hunters need only follow the grief to find the truth. He had not even needed to plan it. People were rivers; you did not chase them, you merely dug the channel and waited.

He poured a measure of plum brandy he did not drink — he had not drunk in eleven years, not since the cistern, since alcohol interfered with the tasting — and held the glass to the firelight, watching the color shift.

"Ask me," he murmured to the mountains, to the child somewhere beyond them, to the nailed-shut door humming under the snow. "Go on. Ask me anything. I will answer honestly, and you will believe me, and that will be the end of you."

Behind his sternum, the hum deepened by a half-tone, and far to the west, though he could not have known it, a smugglers' fire was being built against the dark, and three shadows bent over a scrap of burnt parchment, and the ice over the well sang one note thinner.

Serath smiled at the frost, set down the untouched brandy, and rang for his secretary.

"Ready the second rider," he said. "And wake the Council. We are done waiting on letters."
