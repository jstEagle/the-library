## 20. The Council of Doubt

The smugglers' fire burned low and blue, fed on peat cut from the flats where nothing honest grew. It gave off more smoke than light, which was the point—Wren had chosen the hollow for them, a fold in the earth behind a shelf of wind-scoured stone, where the glow would read as nothing more than marsh-gas to any watcher on the heights. They had crossed out of the salt flats at dusk, Maren leaning on Felix's arm the whole way, her weight increasing by the mile as though the land itself were slowly reclaiming what she owed it.

Now Maren slept—if it could be called sleep. She lay curled beneath Felix's travel cloak near the fire's edge, and even in rest her brow was furrowed, her lips moving around words that had no sound. The closer they came to the mountains, the thinner she seemed, as if someone were drawing her out through a straw, slow and patient and merciless. Felix had watched her hands that morning: they trembled until she closed them into fists, and then they trembled anyway, inside the fists, where no one was meant to see.

Petra sat cross-legged on the far side of the flames, turning a smooth grey stone over and over in her small fingers. She had not spoken since they made camp. That was unlike her. In the tower, in the forest, even on the flats where every step cost effort, Petra had been a low constant hum of talk—questions, observations, fragments of the First Tongue offered up like gifts she didn't know were dangerous. Silence, from Petra, was a weather change. Felix had learned to read it the way sailors read the sea.

The stone in her hand, Felix realized with a lurch, was the shard. Ilva's shard—the fragment of the original seal-stone, wrapped these past days in oilcloth at the bottom of Felix's pack. Sometime during the day's march, Petra had taken it. Or perhaps it had come to her; Felix no longer knew where the boundary between those two things lay when it came to the child.

"Petra." Felix kept their voice soft, pitched under the wind. "You should sleep."

"I'm not tired." She said it without looking up. The firelight moved across her face, and Felix saw again what the First Tongue's use had done to her in the gorge—that terrible moment when her word had shattered the Order's binding-spells and left her eyes looking decades older than the rest of her. The age had faded since, mostly. Mostly. But in certain lights, in the blue hour before true dark, Felix still caught it: a depth in her gaze that belonged to something much older than eight years old.

"Maren says the seal must be renewed," Petra said, still turning the stone. "She told me today while you were scouting the pass. She thinks I wasn't listening. She always thinks I'm not listening."

Felix's stomach tightened. "What did she say exactly?"

"That it has to be done again. Before Serath breaks it or it breaks on its own. That thirteen held it once and thirteen can hold it again." Petra finally looked up, and her eyes in the firelight were very clear and very young and very old all at once. "She didn't say what thirteen means."

* * *

Felix fed another clod of peat to the fire and watched it hiss and smolder. Around them the salt flats gave way to the first stony shoulders of the mountains, and the wind here carried a different voice—not the flat whisper of the lowlands but a keening, almost melodic, that Felix had begun to suspect was not wind at all. The Spire's hum. Audible now, Wren had confirmed grimly, for fifty miles in every direction. The whole world could hear the thing waking, and the whole world would soon have to decide what to do about it.

Maren stirred. Her eyes opened—not gradually, the way ordinary sleepers wake, but all at once, sharp and aware, the way she had opened them for forty years in a tower full of things that watched. She looked at the fire. She looked at Petra. She looked at Felix, and something in her expression suggested she already knew what the night's business would be, because Maren always knew, and had always known, and knowing things she refused to say aloud was half of what had broken them apart the first time.

"You heard us," Maren said to Petra. Not a question.

"I hear everything lately." Petra set the shard down between herself and the fire, deliberately, the way one lays a card face-up. "It's getting louder. All of it."

Maren pushed herself upright against her pack, and Felix moved to help her and was waved off—a small motion, proud and pained, and Felix withdrew their hand and hated how familiar the gesture was, how many years ago they had last been waved off like that, standing in this same woman's doorway with everything they owned in one bag.

"Then we should talk," Maren said. "All three of us. Because tomorrow we climb, and after tomorrow there may not be time for talking."

* * *

The fire had settled into a steady orange burn by the time Maren finished laying out her case, and Felix had to admit—as they had admitted privately, grudgingly, a dozen times on the road—that the old mage argued beautifully. That had always been Maren's gift. Not the working of spells, though her craft was deep enough, but the working of reasons: the patient assembly of logic into a shape you couldn't argue with, so that agreeing with her felt less like surrender and more like seeing clearly for the first time.

"The seal is failing," Maren said. Her hands, folded in her lap, had gone nearly translucent in the firelight; Felix tried not to look at them. "I wove it. I know its threads better than anyone alive, and I am telling you it is unraveling. Every year the border thins a little more. Every year the valley forgets a little less. You've seen it, Felix—you tested the ridge yourself, before we ever left. A wildflower blooming out of season. A compass spinning like a drunk. That was two years of decay. What will ten years look like? Twenty?"

"And when it fails completely?" Felix asked, though they knew the answer; Maren had drilled it into them as an apprentice, back when the lessons were theoretical and the stakes were history rather than blood.

"When it fails completely, everything the Spire holds comes out at once. Unshaped. Unmediated. Raw creation pouring through a crack in the world." Maren's voice did not rise; it never needed to. "Serath wants that chaos—he thinks he can ride it, that he can tear loose the Spire's heart and wear its power like a crown. He'll kill thousands trying. But even if he died on the threshold, even if the Order vanished tomorrow, the seal would still be failing. The problem isn't Serath. Serath is just the sharpest edge of a blade that's falling regardless. The only question is whether it falls controlled or uncontrolled."

"So we renew it." Felix said it flatly, tasting the words.

"We renew it. Thirteen mages, freely giving, as before. I know the rite. I built half of it. With the Spire awake and listening, the weaving might even hold longer this time—centuries instead of a lifetime of centuries." Maren leaned forward, and the firelight caught the wet shine in her eyes that she would deny to her grave. "Felix. I have spent my whole life holding this door shut with my body. Do you think I say this lightly? I am asking for thirteen people to die so that millions can live. There is no arithmetic that makes that clean. But there is arithmetic that makes it *necessary*, and I have done that arithmetic every day for sixty years, and it has never once come out differently."

The wind keened above the hollow. Somewhere far off, a night-bird cried—or something that mimicked one.

"And the thirteen," Felix said quietly. "Where do they come from, Maren? Volunteers? Do you imagine there's a queue forming?"

"They give freely or the seal won't take. That's the nature of the working—it must be given, never taken. I'd ask. I'd go to the schools, the orders, anyone with the strength and the—" Maren stopped. Felix watched her stop, watched the thought arrive behind her eyes and get caught there like a bird against glass.

"You'd ask," Felix repeated. "And if thirteen don't step forward? If twelve do? Does the seal take twelve and hold badly? Ten? Do we weave it thin and hope?"

"Felix—"

"Because I've done my own arithmetic." Felix's voice stayed level, which surprised them; they had expected it to shake. "Ilva told us what the seal keeps. Literally, she said. It keeps its promises literally. And I keep thinking about what the first promise actually was. Thirteen mages walked into that working, Maren, and the valley forgot them, and the world forgot them, and their names are carved nowhere except your memory and a dying woman's hospice bed. That's what 'freely given' bought them. Erasure. Forever. So when you say you'll *ask*—ask what, exactly? Ask thirteen people to vanish so the rest of us can keep pretending magic is a locked room?"

"It bought the world peace," Maren said, and now her voice did shake, barely, a hairline fracture running through sixty years of certainty. "It bought children who grew up without dragons in their skies. It bought—"

"It bought a lie that's killing a valley," Felix said. "And it bought you sixty years alone in a tower, paying for a bargain you made with twelve other people, waiting to find out whether the debt would ever be called in. Look at your hands, Maren."

Silence. The fire popped. Petra had gone very still, her eyes moving between them like a spectator at a match where both players were family.

Maren looked down at her own hands. For a long moment she said nothing at all, and Felix understood, with a sick settling feeling, that they had wounded her—not with cruelty but with accuracy, which was worse—and that the wound was one Maren had been carrying open for six decades and had simply learned to walk on.

"The valley," Maren said at last, very quietly, "was the price of the peace. I have never pretended otherwise. I have only ever asked: is the price worth paying? And every day, for sixty years, I have answered yes. Can you answer differently, Felix? Truly? Knowing what's loose out there?" She gestured upward, toward the keening dark, toward the mountain and the thing inside it. "You felt your own magic come back the day we crossed the border. Tell me it felt safe. Tell me it felt tame."

Felix said nothing, because they couldn't. The memory rose unbidden: the road out of the valley, the wild surge of power flooding back into hands that had starved for years, painful and intoxicating, awe and self-loathing tangled together like roots. No. It had not felt safe. It had felt like being handed a lit lantern in a house full of straw.

* * *

It was Petra who broke the silence, and she broke it the way she did most things—without announcement, without force, simply by beginning to speak as though the silence had been waiting for her.

"She's wrong," Petra said.

Both of them turned. The child had picked the shard up again and was holding it flat on her palm, and Felix saw now that it was faintly warm—it had been warm since the gorge, since her word had cracked the Order's bindings, since the stone had begun, in some sense, to recognize her.

"Not Maren," Petra clarified, with the scrupulous fairness of the young. "Well. Maren too. But the Spire. Everyone keeps saying it's asleep. Asleep, sealed, dreaming, waiting to break out like it's a prisoner or a monster." She frowned at the shard, at the dim light gathering in its grey heart. "It's not asleep. It's not a prisoner either, exactly. It's—"

She stopped. Started again. Felix had learned to let her start again; the First Tongue had no words for some human things, and the human tongue had no words for some Spire things, and Petra lived in the gap between, translating constantly, spending herself a little each time.

"When I was little—I mean when I was little-little, before the vault sent me out—there were voices. The ones who raised me. They weren't people. I know that now. They were pieces of it, or echoes, or... thoughts it was having. And they were sad. Not angry. Sad the way you're sad when you're in a room and everyone who comes to the door only wants something." Her fingers curled slightly around the shard. "Kings wanted its power. Mages wanted its secrets. The thirteen wanted it gone. Nobody ever just—came in and sat down. Nobody ever asked how it was."

"Petra," Maren began gently, "what you felt was its loneliness. I don't doubt it. Loneliness is real, even in a thing like that. But loneliness doesn't make it safe—"

"It's not asleep," Petra said again, louder now, and the fire flared—not dramatically, not with any working Felix could sense, just a soft rising of the flames, like a sleeper drawing breath. "It's *waiting*. Waiting to be asked properly. That's what the voices told me before they sent me out. Find someone who still listens. Not someone strong. Not someone clever. Someone who *listens*. Because it can't open itself, and it can't explain itself, and every time it tries to speak, the world hears a command instead of a question." She looked up at them both, and her eyes were wet and furious and eight years old. "You want to sew it shut again. He wants to rip its heart out. Has anyone—*anyone*—in the whole history of everything—just asked it what it wants?"

The wind keened. The fire settled. And Felix felt the question go through them like a needle through cloth, catching on every thread.

Because Felix had heard it speak. In the Gate of Names, with their own name burning on their tongue, they had felt the Spire remember every promise ever made to it—and underneath the vastness of that remembering, there had been something else, something they had filed away as imagination, as exhaustion, as the mind's defense against the incomprehensible. A quality in the attention that fixed on them. Not hunger. Not wrath.

*Attention.* The quality of someone listening through a wall for footsteps that never stop outside their door.

"Ilva said it keeps its promises literally," Felix said slowly. "Maybe that's not malice. Maybe that's just—precision. The way you'd be precise if every conversation you'd ever had ended with someone taking a piece of you."

"Felix." Maren's voice had gone careful, the care a healer uses approaching a wound. "I understand what the child is saying. I have *always* understood it—that is precisely why I sealed it. Yes, it is lonely. Yes, perhaps it suffers. But it is also a power beyond anything we can govern, and lonely powers do not stay gentle. Give it a door and it will learn to want. Give it witnesses and it will learn to demand them. What Petra describes as a question, I promise you, becomes a need, and needs become commands, and commands become—"

"You don't know that," Petra said. "You *guessed* that. Sixty years ago. And then you made thirteen people die so you'd never have to find out if your guess was wrong."

The words landed in the hollow like dropped stones. Maren went white—not the pallor of her wasting, something else, something struck. And Felix, watching her, felt the strange vertigo of hearing their own argument in a smaller mouth: because Felix had run from the apprenticeship rather than be woven into a new seal, had let Maren pay alone, had carried the shame of it for years—and here was Petra, doing with honesty what Felix had only managed with cowardice. Naming the cost. Refusing to look away.

"Child," Maren said at last, and her voice was very old and very tired, "when you have watched a thing like that unmake a kingdom—"

"I haven't," Petra said. "Neither have you. You watched it refuse to be owned. Those aren't the same thing." She looked down at the shard, and her voice dropped, and the fury went out of it, leaving something much harder to bear. "I think I'm a piece of it. A piece of its voice, walking around in a girl. And if that's true, then when you sealed it, you sealed me too. And when he tears it open, he tears me too. So I'm not choosing sides. I'm the thing both sides are fighting over, and nobody's asked *me* either."

No one spoke. Above the hollow, the keening wind rose and fell, rose and fell, and Felix thought—not for the first time—that it sounded less like wind every day, and more like breathing.

* * *

It was Maren who ended it, because Maren could not abide an unresolved chord; she had been that way through forty years of Felix's childhood, tying off every argument, every lesson, every grief, with a neat bow of finality.

"We climb at first light," she said. "Nothing we say tonight changes that. Serath is coming whether we speak to the Spire or sew it shut, and Ilva's shard knows the way to its heart, and we are the only three people in the world standing between him and it." She eased herself back down onto her bedroll, movements stiff, and closed her eyes as though the conversation were a drawer she could slide shut. "Sleep. Both of you. Whatever the truth is, it will keep until morning. Truths are patient. People are not."

But Felix did not sleep. They sat by the dying fire long after Maren's breathing slowed into that thin, laborious rhythm that frightened them more each night, and they watched Petra, who also did not sleep, sitting upright with the shard in her lap, humming—so softly it was almost subsonic—the phrase she had carried out of the vault like a coal cupped in bare hands.

And in the space between one note and the next, Felix heard it change.

Not the melody. The meaning. The phrase she had repeated since the first storm-tossed night at their door—prayer or alarm, they had never determined which—shifted subtly under her breath, the way a sentence shifts when it stops being rehearsed and starts being meant. Felix's grasp of the First Tongue was a beggar's handful of coins beside Petra's treasury, but they had lived inside that phrase for months, and they knew its shape the way you know a scar.

It was no longer *find someone who still listens.*

It had become, note by note, night by night, mile by mile: *someone is listening. Someone is coming. Hold on.*

A message changing as it traveled. A letter rewriting itself in the carrying.

Felix looked east, toward the black mass of the mountains, toward the crater of glass and old battlefields where the Spire stood humming its one long note into the dark—and felt, with absolute and terrible clarity, that somewhere inside that note, something had noticed them noticing.

The shard in Petra's lap glowed, briefly, like a held breath.

And far below them, at the mouth of the pass they would climb at dawn, torchlight kindled—many torches, moving in formation, patient as arithmetic.

The Order had found the mountain road.
