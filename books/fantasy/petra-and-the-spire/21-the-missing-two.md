## 21. The Missing Two

The hospice clung to the mountainside the way old ivy clings to a wall it has long since outgrown—half-swallowing it, half-holding it up. Felix saw it first as a smudge of smoke against the gray shoulder of the pass, then as a scatter of low stone buildings with turf roofs going green and silver in the wind, and finally, as the switchback trail delivered them closer, as a place that smelled of woodsmoke, wet wool, and something else beneath: a sweetness like crushed stems, the smell of herbs burned past their usefulness.

They had been walking for nine days.

Nine days of Maren's map, copied out in her cramped left-slanting hand before they left the tower—the tower where the wards had been breached once already and would be again, Maren said, if Serath wanted them badly enough. Nine days of Petra riding on Felix's back when the trail steepened, her arms loose around their neck, humming fragments of the First Tongue under her breath so quietly that sometimes Felix wasn't sure whether they were hearing it or remembering it. Nine days of watching the high country change around them: pines giving way to wind-bent birch, birch giving way to nothing but lichen and stone and the small stubborn flowers that grew in the cracks of the world.

And nine days of following the names.

Maren had given them thirteen names on that last night at the tower, spoken slowly, each one handled like something with an edge. Ten dead—she had marked them with a simple stroke, no ceremony, though her voice had caught on two of them. One traitor: Serath, who had been a hero once, whose name was still carved somewhere on some monument to the sealing, and who now led the Order of the Ashen Hand with the patience of a man draining a well he intended to own. And two missing. Vanished, in the years after the seal was woven. Not fled, Maren insisted—not those two. *Gone quiet*, she had said. *There's a difference.*

Ilva Stone-Hand. And a man named Corvess, about whom Maren would say almost nothing except that he had loved the Spire too much and hated himself for it, and that if Felix found him, they should run.

Ilva, then. Ilva first.

*She'll be in the high places*, Maren had said. *She always said the air was thinner where the truth lived. If she's alive—and I don't know how she could be, after what we did—she'll be somewhere people go to die.*

People went to the hospice of Saint Vessa-of-the-Snows to die. That much was clear from the moment Felix passed under its low lintel, ducking because the doorframe had been built by and for smaller, wearier bodies than theirs. Inside was warmth and dimness and the hush of a place where everyone spoke softly, not from reverence but from exhaustion. A broad room with a hearth at one end, rows of cots along the walls, and perhaps twenty souls laid out upon them in various stages of leaving. Some slept. Some stared at the rafters. One old man near the fire was telling a story to no one, his voice worn smooth as river stone, and a young sister in undyed wool sat beside him listening anyway, which told Felix something kind about the place.

A sister with iron-gray hair and flour on her forearms met them in the entryway, already speaking before Felix could open their mouth.

"Pilgrims take the left bench. The sick are fed at dusk. We have no coin to give and ask none."

"We're not—" Felix stopped. Started again. "We're looking for someone. An old woman. Ilva."

Something moved behind the sister's eyes—too quick to name, gone before it settled. "No one of that name here."

"She'd be very old," Felix said. "Older than old. She might not use the name anymore." They hesitated, then did the thing they had decided on the trail, three days ago, staring into a fire while Petra slept against their shoulder. They said, carefully, in the flat careful way one handles a live coal: "She was there. At the sealing. She would know what the word *witnessed* means."

The silence that followed had weight. Out in the main room, the old man's story trailed off; even the fire seemed to lean in.

The sister looked past Felix, then—at Petra, standing half-hidden against the doorframe, mud still on her boots from the last river crossing, watching everything with those eyes that were too calm for a child's face. Petra met her gaze without flinching. And then, softly, she hummed three notes.

Felix felt them in their teeth. The sister's hand went to her collarbone, pressing down as if something under her skin had knocked.

"Come with me," she said. "Not through the common room. She doesn't like to be looked at."

* * *

They went out again into the cold, along a path cut into the slope behind the hospice, to a hut set apart from the others—set apart the way lepers were once set apart, Felix thought, or the way dangerous things were. It was smaller than the rest, older, its turf roof grown through with a single gnarled juniper that had clearly been there longer than the building. Smoke rose from it thin and straight in the windless cold.

"She came to us eleven years ago," the sister said as they walked. "Carried down from the high pass by a shepherd who swore he found her sitting in the snow, waiting, with no tracks around her. Neither coming nor going. Just *there*. She was already old then. She is—" the sister paused, recalculating, "—she should be dead several times over by any accounting I know. She takes no food but a spoonful of honey. She sleeps maybe an hour a night. And every year, on the same day, she walks out to the edge of the cliff and stands there until dawn, and comes back, and says nothing about it."

"What day?" Felix asked, though they already knew. They could feel it coming off the woman like heat off a stone.

"The longest night," the sister said. "The night the sky over the far mountains glows, if you stand just right. She calls it her watch." The sister stopped at the hut's door and turned, and up close Felix could see that her hands were trembling, very slightly, and that she was gripping them together to hide it. "I've nursed the dying for thirty years. I know what keeps a body alive past its season. It's either love or unfinished business, and Ilva has never once spoken of love." She knocked—a strange, deliberate pattern, knuckle-knuckle-palm—and pushed the door open. "Go in. And whatever she gives you, take it. She doesn't offer twice."

The inside of the hut was one room, dark, smelling of honey and juniper and old smoke. A bed against the far wall. A chair. A window no bigger than a book, framing a slice of the eastern peaks, white against white. And in the bed, propped on pillows that had long ago surrendered their shape, lay a woman made mostly of angles and sinew, her hands folded on the blanket like two pieces of driftwood, her face so seamed and weathered that it took a moment to find the eyes—which were, when Felix found them, startlingly awake. Startlingly *present*. The eyes of someone who had never once looked away from anything.

"Well," Ilva said. Her voice was dry as seed husks, but it carried. "Maren sent me a hedge-witch and a—"

She stopped. Her gaze had found Petra, and whatever she had been about to say died in her throat. For a long moment the only sound was the wind worrying the juniper on the roof, and Petra looking back at the dying woman with her head slightly tilted, the way she looked at things that interested her, and Ilva looking at Petra as if she were looking at a grave.

"*That*," Ilva said at last, very quietly, "is why you're here."

"Yes," Felix said.

"Then close the door. What I have to say isn't for the mountain."

* * *

Felix closed the door. Petra curled herself onto the floor near the bed, cross-legged, hands in her lap, patient as a cat outside a mousehole, and Ilva watched her for a while with an expression Felix couldn't read—grief and recognition and something almost like terror braided together—before turning to Felix at last.

"Maren's letter never reached me, did it. Years ago. A hedge-witch asking about the First Tongue." It wasn't a question. "She wrote to all of us who were left. Testing whether anyone remembered enough to answer. Corvess answered. That was the last anyone heard of him."

"You knew about the letter?"

"I knew about everything, child. That was my part in it. I was the one who kept the records. Thirteen names, thirteen lives, thirteen promises—and I wrote down every word of the bargain, because someone had to, because a promise that isn't written down gets *edited* by memory until it means whatever the survivor wants it to mean." Her mouth twisted. "Serath understood that better than any of us. He's been editing for forty years."

Felix lowered themselves onto the stool beside the bed. Up close, Ilva's skin had the translucence of paper held to a flame; Felix could see the pulse in her throat, slow and irregular, a clock winding down. "Maren said you vanished. Gone quiet, she called it."

"I did vanish. I walked into these mountains and I asked the hospice to let me die and they were too kind to manage it." A dry sound that might have been a laugh. "Do you know what the sealing cost, hedge-witch? Not in the way Maren tells it—she tells it clean, she always told things clean, it's her great flaw. I mean the true ledger. Thirteen mages gave their lives into the weave. But a life given into a working doesn't end. It *continues*. Spread thin. Ourselves poured out along the seal like oil along a thread, holding it lit." She raised one driftwood hand, and Felix saw now what the failing light had hidden: the fingers were fused at the second knuckle, webbed with scar tissue hard and pale as quartz, as if the flesh had tried to become stone. "This is what remains in the body when most of you is elsewhere. I am a wick, hedge-witch. I have been burning at both ends for forty years, and lately—" her eyes flicked toward the little window, toward the east, "—lately the flame is guttering. Because the seal is failing. Because someone has been drinking from it."

"Serath."

"Serath." She said the name without hatred, which frightened Felix more than hatred would have. "He was the best of us, once. Do you understand? When we climbed to the Spire's gate, he was the one who volunteered first. He believed—truly believed—that magic was a fire that must either be banked or burn the house down, and that he was the only one steady-handed enough to hold the poker. Forty years of tending a seal alone will teach a man otherwise. Or it will teach him that the fire belongs to him. He learned the second lesson." She coughed, a small terrible sound, and when she spoke again her voice had dropped. "But that's not why you're here, and I don't have the hours for sermons. Give me your hand."

Felix hesitated. Then they gave it.

Ilva's grip was stronger than it had any right to be—stone-strong, literally, the fused fingers closing around Felix's wrist with the pressure of geology. And she pulled, gently, until Felix leaned close, close enough to see the fine map of veins at her temple, close enough to hear the words come not from her voice but from somewhere deeper, the way Petra's First Tongue came from somewhere deeper.

"I'm going to give you two things," she said. "A gift and a warning, and you will not thank me for either. First—the gift."

With her free hand she worked at the collar of her rough shift, and drew out a cord, and on the cord hung a shard of stone the size of a thumbnail. It was unremarkable at first glance—gray, rough-edged, the kind of rock chip that litters every mountain path—but as it swung free of her clothing it caught the dim light wrong. Light went *into* it and didn't quite come back out. It drank the room. And Felix felt it before they understood it: a pull, faint and directional, like the tug of a current felt from the bank, pointing east. Pointing up. Pointing home.

"This is from the gate itself," Ilva said. "Broken off the night we sealed it. Each of us carried a piece afterward—thirteen shards, thirteen anchors, so that no one of us could ever unmake the whole. Mine is the last that survives. The others were buried with their bearers, or lost, or—" her jaw tightened, "—collected. Serath has been gathering them. Did you know that? Every shard he finds, he can draw more freely from the seal. He's spent decades buying back, piece by piece, the key we broke apart to keep from existing." She pressed the cord into Felix's palm and folded their fingers over it, and the stone was warm, warmer than her hand, warm as a living thing. "It wants to go back. It has always wanted to go back. That's the gift and the trap in one, so listen now, and listen *well*, because this is the warning:

"The Spire keeps its promises. Literally. Exactly. Without mercy and without interpretation. That is the nature of the thing—it cannot lie, and it cannot bend, and it cannot forgive a careless word. When we sealed it, we swore an oath, all thirteen of us, and every clause of that oath has been fulfilled to the letter across forty years, including the clauses we didn't notice we were swearing. Maren promised to forget, and so she forgot, and it has hollowed her like a gourd. I promised to keep the record, and so I have kept it, and I could not die until the record passed to other hands—you are those hands now, and blessed be, I can feel the knot loosening already. And Serath promised—" she paused, and her eyes went distant, remembering, and what she remembered made her mouth go bitter, "—Serath promised to *guard the power*. All of it. Forever. Against all comers. Including us. Including himself. He has spent forty years finding the loophole, hedge-witch, and I believe he has found it: if the seal breaks and the power passes to him *freely given*, then he has guarded it, hasn't he? Guarded it straight into his own pocket. The Spire will honor that. The Spire honors everything. That's the horror of it."

Her grip tightened. The quartz-hard fingers bit.

"So when you reach it—and you will reach it, the shard will see to that—watch every word that leaves your mouth. Watch every word the child speaks. No vows. No bargains. No casual 'I swear' or 'as I hope to be saved.' It will take you at your word. It took me at mine." She released Felix's wrist and sank back against the pillows, and for the first time she looked truly old, truly finished, a lamp with nothing left in it but the last inch of oil. "We thought we were saving the world. We were writing a contract in blood with a thing that does not understand metaphor. Whatever happens at that gate—be precise. Be humble. And above all, be *kind*, because kindness is the one language it was never taught, and the one thing it has been starving for."

Silence. The wind. Somewhere below, the hospice bell rang the hour, soft and bronze.

Petra stirred. She had been perfectly still through all of it, but now she rose, crossed the small room, and stood at the bedside looking down at Ilva with an expression Felix had never seen on her before—something old and sorrowful and gentle, far too large for her small face. And she reached out and touched the fused, stony fingers resting on the blanket.

"It can stop hurting," Petra said. In the modern tongue, haltingly, the words chosen with care. "If someone asks it properly."

Ilva stared at her. And then the dying woman began, very quietly, to weep—not with grief, Felix realized, watching her, but with relief, the relief of a sentinel who has heard, at long last, footsteps on the road behind her: someone coming to take the watch.

"Then go," Ilva whispered. "Go east. Go now, while I still hold enough of myself to keep the pass quiet behind you. And hedge-witch—" her eyes found Felix's one last time, burning low and fierce, "—when you speak to it, remember whose names are written in the weave. Speak ours kindly. We were trying, God help us, we were *trying*."

* * *

They buried her at dawn, or rather—they stood witness while the sisters of the hospice did, wrapping her in undyed wool and carrying her to a shelf-crypt cut into the mountainside, facing east, facing the far peaks where on the longest night the sky glowed faintly over the place where the Spire stood. The sisters sang. Petra hummed along in a language older than their song, and the notes fit inside it like a key fits a lock, and more than one sister wiped her eyes and didn't know why.

It happened as they turned to leave. Felix felt it rather than saw it—a loosening, a release, like a rope going slack. Ilva's body, laid out on the stone shelf, changed. The fused fingers softened. The quartz-pale scarring faded, hour by hour, as they watched, until the hands that remained were simply an old woman's hands, ordinary and human and finally, at long last, entirely her own.

The seal had let her go. The record had passed to other hands.

Felix stood in the cold morning with the shard warm in their pocket, pulling gently eastward, and understood that they were carrying a piece of the gate itself, a compass needle aimed at the heart of everything, and that Ilva's warning rang in them like a struck bell: *it keeps its promises literally; watch every word.*

Behind them, Petra tugged Felix's sleeve. She was looking up the trail, toward the high pass, toward the east—and her face, usually so composed, was troubled.

"Felix," she said. "The Spire knows we're coming." She swallowed. "It started saying my name last night. While I slept."
