## 22. What Petra Is

The hospice room still smelled of Ilva's dying—lavender and old linen and something underneath, like rain on hot stone—and Petra could not stop thinking about the shard.

It sat in Felix's pocket now. She had felt it the whole way down the mountain, a small cold weight that was not a weight at all but a *pulling*, gentle as a hand on her sleeve. The stone wanted to go home. She knew this the way she knew the words in her mouth before she spoke them: not learned, but remembered.

And knowing it made her afraid in a way the riders never had.

* * *

They made camp that night in the lee of a fallen pine, high enough on the mountainside that the valley below was a smear of silver mist and the Spire was not yet visible but *present*, the way a headache is present before the pain arrives. Maren slept by the fire with her hands curled under her chin like a child's, and her breathing had a whistle in it that hadn't been there a week ago. Felix sat up sharpening a knife they did not need to sharpen, the blade whispering against the whetstone over and over, and watched Petra across the flames.

She knew that look. She had been learning to read faces the way she read weather, and Felix's face tonight said: *I am going to ask you something I have been carrying for three days.*

"Petra." Their voice was soft. Too soft. "When Ilva gave you the shard—you held it first. Before me."

"Yes."

"What did it feel like?"

The fire popped. Somewhere below, a night bird called once and thought better of it.

"Warm," Petra said. It was true, as far as it went. "Like a hand."

"And what did it *say*?"

There it was. Not *what did it feel like* but *what did it say*, because Felix had finally understood, in the way slow careful people understand things all at once, that everything around Petra said things. That the world spoke to her in a language older than kingdoms, and that she answered it, and that neither of these facts had ever been explained.

Petra looked at her hands. They were small hands, chapped from the road, with dirt in the creases that no amount of washing fully lifted. Ordinary hands. A child's hands.

"It didn't say anything," she said. "It sang. But it sang the way—" She stopped. Started again. "Felix, when you hum, do you know where the song comes from?"

"I suppose my mother. Or somewhere. Why?"

"Because I don't know where mine comes from." Her voice came out smaller than she intended. "That's the answer you're actually asking for, isn't it? You've been circling it since Ilva's house. Since the gorge. You want to know what I am."

Felix set down the knife. The whetstone clicked against a stone beside them, deliberately loud, a small ceremony of putting something away. "I want to know what *you* know," they said. "That's different. And if you're not ready—"

"I'm ready." She wasn't. But she had been carrying it since the vault, since the long dark corridor and the voices that raised her, and carrying it alone had begun to feel like being buried alive in a very polite sort of way. "I think—I think I've been ready since before I met you. I just kept hoping someone would tell me I was wrong."

Maren stirred in her sleep. Neither of them moved until her breathing settled back into its thin whistle.

* * *

"You have to understand," Petra began, and then stopped, because that was wrong; that was how adults began stories, laying down rules first. She tried again. "I remember the corridor."

"The one beneath the Spire."

"Not beneath. *Inside.* Though inside and beneath might be the same thing there." She hugged her knees. The firelight made the shadows of her fingers long and strange against her shins. "It was warm. Not fire-warm. Warm like—the moment before you wake up, when the dream is still holding you? Everything there was like that. Soft edges. No hunger. I didn't have a name for a long time, because names are for things that are separate from other things, and I wasn't separate from anything. I was—" She searched for the word and found, annoyingly, that the modern tongue did not hold it well. She said it instead in the First Tongue, three syllables like water finding its level, and watched Felix flinch the way they always flinched when she used it casually, as if she'd opened a door onto a drop.

"What does that mean?" Felix asked quietly.

"*Part-of-and-listening-to.* It's one word there. Here it would take ten and still be wrong." She picked up a twig and drew in the ash, without quite deciding to: a circle, and then lines radiating out from it, and then—after a hesitation—a small figure standing at the end of one line, far from the center. "The voices raised me. That's what I told you. But I lied a little, or told it badly, because it makes it sound like there were many voices, people-voices, talking over each other. There weren't. There was one voice. Very large. Very tired. It spoke in many directions at once so it seemed like many, but it was one, and it was alone, and it had been alone for—" She shook her head. "A long time. Longer than your kingdoms. Longer than the forgetting."

"The seal," Felix said. "The bargain. Thirteen lives."

"That's part of why it was alone. But only part." The twig trembled slightly in her grip. This was the edge of it now, the place where the ground dropped away, and she had stood at this edge every night since the hospice, looking down. "Felix, do you know what a messenger is?"

"A messenger carries a message. From someone else. To someone else."

"Yes. And do you know what a fragment is?"

The fire cracked. Felix did not answer, which was an answer.

"A fragment is a piece broken off of something," Petra said. "It isn't carrying anything. It *is* the thing, just—smaller. Just able to fit through doors." She looked up. Her eyes were burning and she let them burn; she was done hiding behind fearless, done performing the child who understood more than she showed. "Ilva asked me, in the hospice, when Felix was fetching water. She asked me what I was sent to deliver. And I said a question. *Will you be witnessed?* And she looked at me with those dying eyes and she said, 'Child, who sent you?' And I started to say the Spire, the way I always say it, the way I've been saying it since the tree line and the riders and the storm at your door. And then I couldn't. Because when I reached for the memory of being sent—of standing at a threshold and hearing an instruction—it wasn't there. There was no threshold. There was no instruction given *to* me." Her voice thinned to almost nothing. "There was only the loneliness. So large it bent. And then there was me, walking out of it, already walking, already saying the question under my breath like a prayer or an alarm. As if the loneliness had folded itself small enough to fit through the world. As if—"

"As if the messenger and the message were the same thing," Felix said.

"As if the voice sent itself." Petra pressed her forehead against her knees. Saying it aloud did not make it lighter. Saying it aloud made it real in the specific terrible way that spoken things are real, carved into air that does not forget. "I'm not from the Spire, Felix. I think I *am* the Spire. A piece of it. Its voice, the actual voice, the one that spoke the First Tongue before there were tongues—given flesh, given feet, given hunger, because it could not bear its solitude anymore and a voice cannot walk. So it broke off a piece of itself and sent it out into the world to ask one question, and the piece it broke off was—" She gestured at herself, at the chapped hands and the mud-stained hem and the whole small improbable fact of her body. "Me."

* * *

Silence took the camp. The fire ate down to embers. Far above, through the black lattice of branches, stars wheeled with the slow indifference of things that had watched the Spire be built and sealed and bargained with and had never once been asked their opinion.

Felix came around the fire and sat beside her, close enough that their shoulders touched. They did not speak for a while. When they did, their voice had the roughness in it that meant feeling held carefully, like a bird with a broken wing.

"When I ran from Maren," they said, "years ago—I told you it was because I was afraid. That was true. But it wasn't the whole truth. The whole truth is that they offered me a place in something. A purpose. A role that would have defined me forever. And I refused it, and I have spent every year since telling myself that refusing made me free." They turned the knife over in their hands, unlit blade catching ember-light. "But Petra, listen to me. Listen carefully, because this is the important part. Refusing a purpose someone else builds for you—that can be freedom. But you haven't been *given* a purpose. You've been *told* you have one, by everyone who's ever looked at you. Serath looks at you and sees a key. Maren looked at you and saw a threat to thirteen graves. Even Ilva, kind as she was, even she asked 'what were you sent to deliver' and not 'what do you want.' Everyone has been reading you like a document." They set the knife down in the ash, next to her drawing, deliberately burying the blade's outline. "So here is my question, and it's the only one that matters, and you don't have to answer it tonight or tomorrow or ever. Not: what are you? But: *who*? What does Petra want? Not the voice. Not the fragment. The girl who laughs at me when my magic misfires. The girl who hums to make the dark less empty. Does she exist?"

Petra stared at him—at them, at Felix, at this person who had taken her in out of reluctant compassion and stayed out of something fiercer—and felt something shift in her chest, some load-bearing grief giving way.

"I don't know," she whispered.

"Good," Felix said. "That's the most honest answer anyone's given on this whole wretched mountain."

"But Felix—if I'm a fragment, then when we reach the Spire, when it asks—" Her hand went unconsciously to her sternum, where the pulling lived. "What if going home means being taken back? What if the question gets delivered and I get—unmade? Folded back in? What if there was never a Petra at all, only a shape the loneliness wore so it could go outside?"

"Then we'll be having a very firm conversation with a very large rock," Felix said, and though their voice shook, they put their arm around her shoulders, and the shaking stopped being hers. "You are not a message, Petra. Messages don't get scared. Messages don't lie awake worrying about whether they're real. Whatever you are, you're the thing that's afraid right now, and fear belongs to people, not parcels."

She wanted to believe it the way you want to drink when your throat is full of dust. She believed maybe half of it, which was more than she'd had yesterday.

Above the tree line, far off toward the spine of the world, the air itself gave a low, resonant hum—the Spire, audible now even at this distance, calling in a voice that was, she understood suddenly and completely, her own voice heard from the outside. It sounded lonely. It sounded vast. It sounded like her name, if she had ever had one before Felix gave her one.

And below them, down the dark slope of the mountain, torches were moving. Many torches, in formation, patient as a tide.

Felix saw them a heartbeat after she did. Their hand closed on her shoulder—not gently this time.

"Petra," they breathed. "How fast can you run?"

She was already on her feet.
