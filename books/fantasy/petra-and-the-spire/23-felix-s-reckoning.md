## 23. Felix's Reckoning

Petra slept at last in the corner of the hospice room, curled against the wall as if she could make herself smaller than her own confession. The dying fire threw her shadow long across the floorboards, and Felix sat watching it and could not stop seeing what she had shown them: a fragment of something vast, poured into a child's shape, sent walking into a world that would either use her or break her.

*You may not be a messenger,* she had said. *I may be the voice itself.*

And Felix had held her while she shook, and said all the right things—*you are still you, you are still a girl who likes honey on bread and hums when she's frightened*—and meant every word. But underneath the comfort, underneath the steady hand on her back, another voice had been speaking. A colder one. An older one.

*You have no right to comfort her.*

Because Felix knew what it was to be offered a place in a bargain and to refuse it. Knew what it was to let someone else pay.

The fire ticked. Somewhere below, Ilva's nurse moved through the dark with a lamp, her footsteps soft as falling ash. Felix did not sleep. Felix took out the seal-shard wrapped in wool at the bottom of their pack, and unwrapped it, and looked at it for a long time without touching it.

It was no bigger than a knucklebone, that shard. Gray-green stone shot through with veins of something that caught light wrong—not reflecting it so much as remembering it. Maren had carried its twin for thirty years. Thirteen sealers, thirteen stones, thirteen lives woven into a lattice around the Spire's heart. Ten dead now, their stones gone dark or scattered. Two missing—Ilva found, Serath lost to his own ambition. And one.

One who had stood in a tower room on a night of high wind, twenty-two years old and furious with fear, and said *no*.

* * *

Felix had never told anyone the true shape of that night. They had built a story instead, the way a mason builds a wall—one stone at a time, mortared with repetition until even the builder half-believed it. *I ran because I couldn't bear the sealing. I ran because the cost was too high. I ran because I was young.* All true, in the way that a map is true. None of it the territory.

Here was the territory:

Maren had come to them in the autumn of their final year, when the research was nearly done and the thirteen names were being chosen, and she had not asked Felix to help with the working. She had asked them to be part of it. To stand in the circle. To give their life—not their death, she had been careful about that distinction, careful in the way that made it worse—their *life*, their living breath and years and self, woven into the seal alongside hers. Not to die for the world but to *become* part of its keeping. Thirteen voices holding one note forever.

She had wept when she asked. Felix had never seen Maren weep before and never saw it again after.

And Felix—who had spent three years learning that magic was a language, that power was a conversation, that nothing given freely stayed free—had looked at the circle diagram spread across the tower table, at the thirteen small hollows where thirteen lives would sit like stones in a ring, and had felt not horror but *calculation*. That was the shame of it, the thing they had never once said aloud, not to themselves in the dark watches, not to the goats, not to the valley that forgot. Not *I cannot do this*. But *why must it be me? Why thirteen? Why this design? Is there not another way?* Questions dressed up as conscience. Cowardice wearing the robes of philosophy.

*I need time,* Felix had said.

Maren had nodded, and said, *Take it,* and something in her face had already known.

There were eleven days between that question and the sealing. Eleven days in which Felix did not go back to the tower. Eleven days in which they told themselves they were searching the archives for an alternative design, and did search, furiously, desperately—and found fragments, possibilities, roads not taken that might have led somewhere or nowhere. And on the twelfth day, having found nothing conclusive, having proven nothing except that certainty is a luxury of people who don't have to decide, Felix had packed a bag and left in the night.

Not because the cost was too high.

Because they had wanted someone else to find a cheaper way first, and no one had, and they could not bear to be the one who paid full price.

Maren had sealed the Spire with twelve. Twelve lives where thirteen were meant to sit. And the seal had held—for a while—but Felix had learned since, from Odile's ledger, from Ilva's dying whispers, from the slow decay of dates written in a dead woman's careful hand, what a seal does when it is underfed. It eats its keepers faster. It leans harder on the valley's forgetting. It thins at the borders and lets wild magic bleed through out of season.

Every year of the valley's slow dying had been measured against the life Felix had withheld.

They had never done the arithmetic before. They did it now, sitting in a dying hospice fire with a shard of the seal in their lap, and the arithmetic was simple and total and it did not kill them, which was somehow the worst part. Shame that kills is over quickly. This kind just lived in the chest, year after year, like a tenant who pays nothing and breaks nothing and simply will not leave.

* * *

"You're doing the thing," Petra said from her corner.

Felix started. "What thing?"

"The quiet-while-loud thing." She sat up, hair tangled, eyes too old in her small face. "Your face goes still but your hands talk." She pointed. "You've been wringing that cloth for an hour."

Felix looked down. They were indeed wringing the wool wrap around the shard, twisting it as though they could strangle the truth out of it. They made their hands stop.

"Go back to sleep, sprout."

"I'm not tired." She came and sat beside them, uninvited, the way she did everything—cross-legged, close, radiating the fierce warmth of a coal. For a while she watched the fire. Then, in the tone of a child who has decided the adults are failing at something: "You heard what I said. About being the Spire's voice. You're scared of me now."

"No." And then, because lying to her had become impossible somewhere on the salt flats: "No. I'm not scared of you. I'm—" Felix turned the shard over in its wrapping, not looking at her. "I'm recognizing something."

"Tell me."

"It's not a nice story."

"None of my stories are nice stories." She leaned her head against Felix's arm, a weight of perhaps forty pounds, and waited with the terrible patience of children and mountains.

So Felix told it. Not the map—the territory. The tower room, the offer, the eleven days, the running. The refusal that had never once been spoken aloud and therefore had never stopped echoing. And then, because Petra deserved the whole of it, the arithmetic: twelve where there should have been thirteen, and what a starving seal eats.

When they finished, the fire had burned down to a red eye. Petra was quiet for a long moment.

"You didn't say no," she said at last.

"What?"

"You think you refused. But you didn't say no. You just... didn't say yes. You left the question open and ran away from it." She frowned at the coals, working it through the way she worked through everything, with her whole body, her brow furrowed. "That's different. Saying no is a door closing. You left a door open and then hated yourself for standing in the doorway."

Felix stared at her. Twenty years of guilt, and a child had picked it apart in a sentence by the simple method of taking words literally—which, Felix reflected, was probably why the First Tongue worked and human speech mostly didn't.

"That's—" They laughed, or something near it, wet and cracked. "That's exactly right, and I hate it."

"Why?"

"Because if I'd said no—if I'd stood in that room and said *no, Maren, I won't, and here is why*—then at least it would have been a choice. A real one. She could have argued. We could have fought. Maybe she'd have found a thirteenth. Maybe she'd have redesigned the whole working rather than lose me. I'll never know, because I never gave her the chance. I let her believe—" Felix's throat closed. They pushed through it. "I let her believe I needed time, and then I stole myself away like a thief in my own story. And she went into that circle knowing one seat was empty, and she wove the seal anyway, and she has been paying interest on my absence for twenty years."

Petra considered this. "Ilva said the Spire keeps promises literally."

"Yes."

"Do you think it counts the ones you almost make?" Her voice dropped. "Because I think it does. I think that's why the seal is thin. Not just missing a person. Missing an answer."

The words went into Felix like a splinter going into quick—small, precise, impossibly deep. *Missing an answer.* Twenty years of silence, and the silence itself had been a weight on the lattice, a hollow place where a yes or a no should have stood. The Spire kept its promises literally. Perhaps it also kept its refusals literally. Perhaps somewhere in the deep geometry of the seal there was a gap shaped exactly like Felix Villalobos's cowardice, and everything that leaked through the valley's border leaked through *them*.

"Oh," Felix said. Very quietly. It was not a word so much as a structural failure.

Petra patted their knee, twice, the way one comforts a large and bewildered animal.

* * *

Dawn came gray through the hospice shutters. Below, they could hear the nurse moving, and the wet rattle of Ilva's breathing getting shallower, and Felix went down alone while Petra slept again, properly this time, her dreams making the candle flames bow even here beyond the valley, even with the seal thinning—as if the Spire reached for her even now, or she for it.

Ilva was awake. Barely. Her eyes had gone the color of watered milk, but they fixed on Felix with the last of her attention gathered like coins from the bottom of a purse.

"You came back," she rasped.

"I have questions."

"Dying people love questions. Makes us feel the answers still matter." A smile with too little behind it. "Ask."

Felix knelt by the bed. "If a sealer refused—if one of the thirteen walked away before the working—what happens to their place in the seal?"

Ilva's breath caught. For a moment Felix thought it was the death rattle coming early. Then her hand moved, slow as a glacier, and closed around Felix's wrist with shocking strength.

"So it was you." No surprise in it. Only a vast, tired sorrow. "Twelve of us in the circle, and Maren wouldn't say why the thirteenth seat stood empty, and Serath crowed about it—said the design was *purified*, said less flesh meant more clarity, and Maren let him say it." Her grip tightened. "We always thought she'd lost someone to death. Not to a doorway."

"I need to know what it did. What my leaving *did*."

Ilva released the wrist. Her hand fell back to the blanket, and she looked at the ceiling as though reading something written there in a script only sealers could see.

"A seal is a promise," she said. "Thirteen promises, braided. When one strand is missing, the braid doesn't fail—it *leans*. It puts its weight on the strands that remain. Maren bore most of it; her stone anchored the keystone. That's why she's fading faster than any of us ever did. That's why the valley forgets deeper every year—the seal draws its forgetting from her, and her from the land, round and round, a wheel turning on one spoke." Her milk-pale eyes came back to Felix. "But an empty seat isn't just a missing strand, child. It's an open question. The Spire is a creature of literal things. It felt the absence of an answer the way a tooth feels a gap. And things leak through gaps."

"The border. The wildflower. The compass."

"All of it. Every leak in your valley runs along the seam of what you didn't say." She coughed, long and terrible, and when it passed she lay smaller than before. "You want absolution. I can hear it. Dying people smell it on the living." Her mouth twisted. "I won't give it. Absolution is cheap and the seal is expensive. But I'll give you something better."

"What?"

"A use." She fumbled beneath her pillow and pressed something into Felix's palm—the second shard, warm as a living thing, humming faintly against the first in Felix's pocket like two tuning forks finding each other. "Serath drained his stone years ago—I felt it go dark across half the world. He thinks the seal is a bank he's been robbing. He thinks when it breaks, the power falls to whoever's holding the bag." Her laugh was a dry leaf of a sound. "He never understood. Power doesn't fall. It *asks*. It has been asking for twenty years, and the only one who ever answered honestly was a child who wasn't even born when we made our bargain."

Felix closed their hand around the shard. "What do I do with it?"

"Finish the answer." Ilva's voice was going now, fraying at the edges like rope. "Not the promise. The *answer*. Go to the Spire and say what you should have said in that tower room. Say it out loud, in whatever tongue it hears. Yes or no—but say it, and mean it, and let the braid close." Her eyes drifted shut. "Better questions than we asked. Better answers than we gave..."

She did not finish. Whether she slept or slipped further toward the dark, Felix could not tell, and the nurse gently steered them from the room.

* * *

Outside, the morning was cold and clean, the peaks above the hospice burning white with early sun. Felix stood in the yard and held both shards now, and felt them pull faintly eastward, toward the glass crater and the rising hum, two compass needles agreeing at last.

Petra came out rubbing sleep from her eyes and stood beside them, following their gaze to the mountains.

"We're going there," she said. Not a question.

"Yes."

"To answer it. The thing you didn't answer." She had heard, then—or dreamed it; with her it was hard to say which. She slipped her hand into Felix's free one, small and certain. "Good. Because I don't want to be a key, and I don't want to be a voice. I want to be Petra. And I think—" she squeezed Felix's fingers, hard, "—I think if you say your true word, maybe I get to say mine."

Felix looked down at her, this fragment of a lonely god wearing scuffed boots and honey-sticky fingers, and felt the twenty-year tenant in their chest finally begin, at long last, to pack its things.

"Yes," Felix said. "Together, then. Your word and mine."

Far to the east, so distant it might have been imagination, the Spire's hum changed pitch—like something vast turning over in its sleep to listen.

And behind them, down in the valley of the pass, dust rose where no wind blew: riders, many riders, moving fast.
