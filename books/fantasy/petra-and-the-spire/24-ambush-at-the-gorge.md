## 24. Ambush at the Gorge

**FELIX**

The gorge announced itself long before they saw it—a thinning of the wind, a change in the way the salt-crusted earth rang under their boots. Felix had been dreading it since the flats began: the only cut through the mountains that didn't require climbing, which meant the only road an army could use, which meant the one place Serath's hunters would be waiting.

They reached its mouth at dusk. The walls rose sheer and rust-red on either side, close enough in places that Felix could have touched both with outstretched arms. A dry riverbed stitched the floor, its stones pale as old teeth.

"It's a throat," Maren said quietly. She had been walking worse all day—stopping more often, leaning on her staff until the wood creaked—and now she stood looking up at the narrow dark ahead with an expression Felix hadn't seen since they were young. Not fear. Arithmetic. "And we are going to walk down its gullet because there is no other way."

"There's the western scramble," Felix said. "Petra could make it. I might."

"You would break your ankle by moonrise and then we'd all die slower." Maren's mouth twitched, the ghost of her old dry humor. "No. We go through. And I go first."

"Maren—"

"Felix." She said their name the way she used to when correcting a sigil—one soft syllable, absolute. "I have been dying since the flats. You've seen it. Whatever the seal is calling back, it's calling *me* back with it, and every hour I spend pretending otherwise is an hour Petra spends walking beside something that frightens her." Her eyes flicked to the girl, who was crouched at the gorge's mouth, tracing letters in the dust with one finger. "Let me be useful while I still am."

Felix wanted to argue. They had spent twenty years perfecting the art of arguing with Maren Halloran in their head; they'd simply never gotten good at doing it aloud. What came out instead was smaller, and truer:

"I'm not ready to lose you twice."

Maren looked at them for a long moment. The last light caught the silver in her hair and turned it briefly to fire.

"Then don't look," she said, and walked into the gorge.

* * *

**PETRA**

The gorge hummed.

Not like the Spire hummed—the Spire's song lived under everything, patient as a heartbeat—but a thinner sound, a stone-sound, the echo of water that had left centuries ago. Petra put her palm against the wall as they walked and felt the mountain thinking slow red thoughts.

Behind her, Felix kept touching her shoulder every few minutes. Checking. Felix did that constantly now, ever since the tower, ever since her eyes had gone old for a day after she spoke the word that broke the binding-spells. Petra didn't mind. It was nice, being checked on. In the vault beneath the Spire, no one had ever worried whether she was tired. They had only worried whether she remembered her instruction.

*Find someone who still listens.*

She had found them. Two someones. One of them was dying, though Petra wasn't supposed to know that. She knew anyway. She could hear it—not in words, but in the way Maren's footsteps had begun to skip, like a song losing notes.

"Stay close," Felix murmured, for perhaps the hundredth time.

"I am close," Petra said. "I'm always close. You keep saying it like I'm going to evaporate."

Felix made a sound that was almost a laugh. "You might, at that. Don't say things like evaporate."

"Why not?"

"Because in this family, things come true."

Petra filed that away—*family*, Felix had said it without noticing, and she held the word carefully, the way you hold a moth—and kept walking. The light was failing. The walls leaned closer. And somewhere above them, so faint that only someone who spoke to stones could hear it, boots shifted on rock.

Petra stopped.

"Felix," she whispered. "There are people in the walls."

* * *

**FELIX**

Felix froze mid-stride. Ahead, Maren had stopped too—of course she had; she'd been listening longer than either of them—and her hand rose slowly, palm flat: *hold*.

The gorge had gone very quiet. Too quiet. The wind that had threaded through it all evening died between one breath and the next, and Felix understood, with the cold certainty of old training, what that meant. Wind didn't stop in a gorge. Wind was *stopped*.

"Wards," Maren said softly. "Sealed both ends. They've been waiting since before noon."

"How many?"

"Enough." Maren turned, and in the deepening gloom her face was unreadable, but her voice was steady as bedrock. "Listen to me now, because I will only say this once. There is a side channel forty paces ahead, where the river once cut through the north wall. It climbs. It's brutal, and it's exposed, but it comes out above the rim, behind them."

"And you'll—" Felix started.

"I'll hold the pass." Maren lifted her staff, and light gathered along it—not the clean white light of her teaching years but something older, deeper, the color of the seal itself, amber shot through with black. Felix felt their stomach drop. That working was drawing from somewhere. From somewhere *in* her. "It's what I'm for, in the end. Thirteen lives wove the seal. Mine has been unwinding for weeks. Better it should buy three miles than nothing at all."

"No." Petra's voice cracked across the gorge like flint on steel. She planted herself in front of Maren, small and furious, her eyes enormous in the dark. "No. We go together. We climb together. You promised—you said in the tower you'd teach me the modern words for *rain*. You haven't finished."

Something moved in Maren's face then—something that broke and reformed, quick as water. She knelt, staff and all, until her eyes were level with the child's.

"Petra of the Spire," she said. "Do you know what I did, long ago? I helped build a cage around something lonely, and I told myself it was mercy. I asked all the wrong questions, and thirteen people paid my tuition." She took Petra's hand and pressed something into it—small, warm, humming faintly. The shard. Ilva's shard. "So here is your last lesson from me, and it is not about rain. When you get where you're going—ask better questions than I did."

Above them, the darkness coughed.

"Down!" Felix shouted, and the world exploded.

* * *

**PETRA**

The first boulder fell like a dropped sky.

Petra felt Felix seize her collar and haul, felt the air where her head had been turn to thunder, and then they were running—running down the riverbed with the whole gorge screaming, stone after stone leaping from the walls as if the mountain itself were shaking off birds. Light bloomed overhead: cold white lantern-glow, dozens of them, and figures in gray on the rims, cloaks snapping, hands weaving.

The Ashen Hand. Dozens of them. Maybe more.

A bolt of something hissed past her ear and struck the wall, and where it struck the stone went grey and silent—dead, magically dead, a wound in the world's hearing. Petra cried out without meaning to. Everything alive made noise to her; watching a place go quiet was like watching someone's eyes go out.

"They're unmaking the ground!" Felix yelled. "They want to strip your footing—keep moving!"

But the far end of the gorge was already sealed. Petra could see it now through the dust: a wall of woven shadow, gray-cloaked figures ranked before it, and at their center a tall shape whose presence she knew the way you know a word you've never been allowed to say. Cold. Certain. Hungry in a tidy way.

Serath's hunters. Maybe Serath himself.

And behind them, growing fainter with every heartbeat, Maren's voice rose into a language Petra knew better than any other—the First Tongue, spoken by a woman who had learned it secondhand and was spending it like her last coin, because it was.

* * *

**FELIX**

The working Maren raised was beautiful. That was the horror of it. Felix had seen hedge-craft, battle-craft, the Order's brutal institutional sorcery, and none of it prepared them for the sight of a dying woman pouring herself into the gorge like wine into a cup.

Light flooded the pass. The falling rocks stopped mid-air—hung there, hundreds of them, a suspended avalanche glittering in amber—and then Maren *turned* them, all at once, a shepherd turning a flock, and sent them back up the walls at the gray-cloaked archers. Screams. Lanterns tumbling. The ambush breaking apart like a fist opening.

"THE CHANNEL!" Maren's voice, raw now, fraying at the edges. "GO, YOU STUBBORN CHILD, GO!"

Felix grabbed Petra and ran. Forty paces. Thirty. The side channel yawned in the north wall, a black crack full of handholds and old water-smoothed stone. Felix shoved Petra toward it—

—and the world went sideways.

A hunter had reached the gorge floor. One of them, fast, impossibly fast, cloak burned away to show armor beneath, and he moved like the magic-dead bolts he threw: silent, erasing. He was between them and the channel. He was raising his hand, and his fingers were already grey to the wrist, and Felix did the thing they had sworn for twenty years never to do again.

They reached for their training.

It came like blood from a reopened wound. The neglected craft in their hands woke screaming—heat, pressure, the taste of copper and lightning—and Felix hurled it all at the hunter in a single ragged arc. The man staggered. His grey fingers smoked. He did not fall.

He smiled.

* * *

**PETRA**

The hunter was going to kill Felix.

Petra knew it the way she knew rain was coming, the way she knew the Spire's moods: not seeing, *hearing*. The man's intent had a sound, low and final, a door closing. And Felix was on one knee, drained, their borrowed fire guttering, and the channel was ten steps away and might as well have been ten miles.

Petra opened her mouth and gave the hunter a word.

Not the shattering word—that had cost her a day of her own life, had aged her eyes in a mirror until Felix wept. This one was smaller. Older. A word from before commands, from before bargains, from the very bottom of the First Tongue where the language was still just the world talking to itself. She spoke it at the stone beneath the hunter's feet, politely, the way you greet an elder.

*Move.*

The stone moved.

It was not dramatic. That was what made it terrible. The rock simply shifted—an inch, two, the way continents shift—and the hunter's balance went, and his grey hand slapped the dead zone of his own making for support, and the dead stone offered none, and he fell backward into the dark of the river channel with a sound Petra would try very hard, later, never to remember.

She stood shaking. Felix stared at her. Above them both, the amber light was flickering.

"MAREN!" Felix's voice tore upward. "MAREN, WE'RE IN, COME ON, COME *ON*—"

* * *

**FELIX**

For one moment, through the dust and the dying light, Felix saw her clearly.

Maren stood at the center of the pass with her arms raised and her hair loose and streaming as if in water, and she was brighter than anything Felix had ever seen—brighter than the lanterns, brighter than the moonrise beginning over the rim, bright the way a candle is bright in its last inch. The suspended rocks wheeled around her in a slow galaxy. Below her, the hunters regrouping at the sealed end faltered, pressed back by the sheer weight of her attention.

She looked at Felix. Even at that distance, even through the roar, Felix felt the look land like a hand on the shoulder—young again, twenty years gone, standing in a tower study with ink on their fingers, certain they were throwing their life away.

Her lips moved. No sound carried. But Felix had spent a childhood learning to read those lips across crowded lecture halls, and they read it now:

*Ask better questions than I did.*

Then she brought her arms down, and the gorge came with them.

The whole southern wall let go. Not collapsed—*released*, every stone she had held, every stone she had turned, given back to gravity in a single deliberate act, and the pass filled with a roar like the world ending, and the amber light went out all at once, like a held breath finally let go.

Felix did not remember deciding to run. They were simply in the channel, hauling Petra up the broken stair-step of the old riverbed, the girl clutching the shard to her chest, both of them climbing while the mountain buried the road behind them and everyone on it.

At the top, on the rim, in the cold clean wind, Felix turned and looked back.

Where the pass had been there was now a wall of rubble fifty feet high, pale in the moonlight, utterly silent. No lanterns. No gray cloaks. No amber glow.

No Maren.

Petra made a small sound beside them—not a cry, something younger than a cry—and Felix pulled her in and held on, and the wind coming down off the mountains smelled of salt and stone and nothing else at all.

* * *

**PETRA**

They sat on the rim until the moon was high. Felix didn't speak. Petra didn't either. Crying, she had learned in her short life outside, was something people needed room to do, and Felix needed a whole country's worth of room right now.

But after a long time, Petra felt it again—the hum. Faint, far off, past the mountains, past everything: the Spire, singing its patient note. And threaded inside it now, impossibly, a new voice. Small. Dry. Wry, even, if a voice could be wry.

She looked down at the shard in her hand. It was warm. Warmer than it had ever been.

"Felix," she whispered.

Felix didn't answer. Their eyes were closed, their face wet, their hands empty in their lap.

Petra looked at the shard again, and at the buried pass, and at the mountains beyond, where somewhere in the dark the Spire waited with its question no one had thought to ask. And she understood, with the strange calm the First Tongue always gave her, that Maren had not finished leaving. That a thirteenth of something had gone into this stone, and the stone knew the way home.

She closed her fingers around it and stood up.

"Felix," she said again, gently. "The stone wants to go home. And I think... I think Maren wants to come with us."
