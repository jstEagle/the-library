## 25. Grief and the Shard

The rockfall had not finished settling when Felix began to dig.

They dug with their hands at first, then with a broken branch, then with their hands again when the branch snapped. The gorge floor was a chaos of shattered stone and dust that hung in the air like a held breath, and every stone they moved seemed to whisper the same accusation: *too slow, too slow, you were always too slow.* Maren had stood in the pass above them and the mountain had come down and Felix had not even had time to shout her name.

Petra knelt nearby, small and still, her hands folded in her lap the way she did when she was trying very hard to be brave. She did not cry. Felix suspected she had never been taught how.

By the time the light failed they had uncovered an arm. Just an arm — gray-cloaked, dusted white with pulverized granite, the hand half-curled as though it had been reaching for something when the world ended. Felix knew that hand. They had held it once, years ago, across a table scattered with stolen pages, while Maren explained why some doors must stay shut. They had let go of it. That was the whole of their history: holding on, letting go, holding on, letting go, until the last time there was nothing left to hold.

Felix sat back on their heels and looked at the arm and could not make themselves dig any further.

*Ask better questions than I did.*

Her last words. Not *run.* Not *I forgive you.* Not even *take care of the child.* Better questions. As if the whole of her life — the sealing, the silence, the thirteen names, the valley built on forgetting — could be compressed into homework assigned to a student who had already failed every previous lesson.

"I'm sorry," Felix said aloud. The words fell into the dust and died there, like everything else in this place. "I should have come back sooner. I should have—"

What? Stayed? Taken the thirteenth place beside her in the weave and spent two lifetimes as mortar between stones? They had refused that. They had run to a valley where magic couldn't follow and called it survival, called it wisdom, called it anything but what it was.

Petra's cold little hand found theirs in the dark.

"She's not under the rocks," Petra said quietly. "Not all of her."

Felix turned. The child's face was pale in the last gray light, her eyes — those eyes that had aged a decade in a single word of power — fixed on the rubble with an expression Felix had learned to fear and trust in equal measure: the look of someone listening to something no one else could hear.

"The mountain took her body," Petra said. "But she gave the rest away first. You saw. She poured herself into the working." A pause. "She's in the pass now. In the stones. In the way the wind moves through them." Her brow furrowed, searching for words the modern tongue barely had room for. "It's like... like when bread is baked. The flour isn't gone. It's just become something else."

"That's not comfort," Felix said hoarsely. "That's just a different way of saying she's dead."

"No." Petra shook her head with terrible certainty. "It's a way of saying she went where she chose to go. Nobody made her stand in the pass. She decided." Her grip tightened on Felix's fingers. "You have to stop deciding things for her. You've been doing it since before I met you."

Felix wanted to be angry. There was no room for it. The child was right, and being right, she was crueler than any blade.

They buried what they could find of Maren Halloran as night came down. No words, because Felix could not find any that weren't lies or apologies, and Petra hummed — low, almost inaudible, three notes of the First Tongue that made the dust settle gently instead of blowing, that made the darkness feel less like a mouth closing around them. When it was done, the cairn looked like every other pile of stone in the gorge, which felt right. Maren had spent her life being mistaken for ordinary things.

Then Felix climbed back up to the ledge where they'd sheltered, took out the seal-shard, and nearly threw it into the dark.

* * *

It had been Ilva's dying gift, back in the mountain hospice that smelled of juniper and waiting: a jagged fragment of pale stone no bigger than a knuckle, warm as a living thing, wrapped in a scrap of cloth embroidered with thirteen interlocking circles. *The Spire keeps its promises literally,* Ilva had rasped. *Remember that. Every bargain ever struck with it is still being kept, somewhere, by someone who doesn't know they're keeping it.* And then she had pressed the shard into Felix's palm and closed their fingers over it with hands like paper, and said, *Take it home when you understand what home means.*

Felix understood now. Home meant a crater of glass at the roof of the world. Home meant the thing that had eaten thirteen lives and called it conversation. Home meant everything they had spent eleven chapters — eleven years — running from.

And Maren was dead because of it. Because Felix had carried this stone through gorges and salt flats and the Order's checkpoints, and Serath's hunters had followed its warmth like wolves following blood, and Maren had stood in a narrow place between the mountains and paid the toll so the other two could pass.

Felix stood at the edge of the ledge with the shard raised behind their ear, arm cocked, every muscle in their body screaming to let go. Below, the gorge dropped away into perfect blackness. Somewhere down there was a river they couldn't hear. If the shard went into that water, it would tumble and roll and bury itself in silt, and no one would ever find it again, and maybe — maybe — the hunters would lose the trail, and maybe Petra would be safe, and maybe Felix could take her somewhere green and quiet and raise her on goats and herbs and forgetting, the way they themselves had been raised on it, the way they had once believed was the only honest life—

"Stop."

Petra's voice. Not loud. It didn't need to be. It had that quality the First Tongue lent her sometimes, the quality of a word spoken before the world agreed to exist.

Felix's arm trembled. They did not throw. They did not lower it either.

"It wants to go home," Petra said.

"It wants a lot of things," Felix snarled, and heard their own voice crack down the middle. "It wanted Ilva to die alone in a hospice. It wanted Maren to spend fifty years pretending to be a hedge-witch's crank aunt. It wanted me to—" They stopped. Swallowed. Started over, quieter. "Everything that touches it gets used up, Petra. Everything. And you're standing right next to me, and you're made of the same stuff it is, and I am *not* carrying you one step closer to that crater just because a rock has opinions about geography."

Petra considered this with the grave patience she brought to everything, as though Felix were a difficult passage in a text she was determined to read properly.

"You're shaking," she observed.

"Yes."

"Because you're angry."

"Yes."

"Because you're sad."

"*Yes.*"

"And because your arm knows something your mouth won't say yet." She stepped closer, out onto the ledge, close enough that Felix could see the starlight caught in her eyes. "Throw it, then. If you truly believe it only takes." She spread her small hands. "But listen first. Just once. Listen the way you taught me to listen to the goats, when they're frightened and you can't tell why. Listen with your whole chest."

Felix wanted to tell her she was being ridiculous. Instead — because grief had hollowed them out and left room for nothing but obedience — they listened.

At first there was nothing. Wind in the gorge. The distant complaint of settling stone. Their own heartbeat, too fast, and Petra's beside it, slow and steady as a second pulse the world had loaned them both.

Then, beneath all of it, faint as a memory of warmth: a hum. Not sound exactly. More like the sensation of standing near a struck bell after the ringing stops — the air still shaped by the note, still remembering it. The shard was doing that against their palm. And threaded through the hum, so faint Felix might have invented it, was something almost like a direction. Not north or south. Something older than directions. A leaning. A longing.

Like a compass needle, if compasses pointed toward wounds.

"Oh," Felix said. The word came out very small.

"Do you feel it?" Petra whispered.

"It's pulling." Felix lowered their arm slowly, staring at the shard as though it had grown a face. "Not dragging. Pulling. Like — like a dog at the end of a long lead. Like it knows where it belongs and it's been polite about it this whole time." They laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, wet and ragged. "Ilva said *take it home.* I thought she meant deliver it. I thought it was another errand, another burden. But it wasn't a burden, was it. It was a thread."

"Ariadne," Petra said, and then frowned, because the word had arrived from somewhere neither of them had put it. "Sorry. I don't know why I said that. Things come up sometimes. Words. Stories." She touched her own sternum, the way she did when the Spire's song stirred in her voice. "Someone followed a thread out of a labyrinth. The thread was given by someone who loved him."

Felix stared at her. Of all the nights for the child to produce classical allusions from whatever vast, lonely library lived behind her eyes.

"Maren," they said slowly, "gave us the hospice. She sent us to Ilva. She knew — she must have known Ilva had the shard, that Ilva would give it to us. Which means the thread runs through her. Through all of them. Thirteen people, thirteen choices, and every one of them left a mark on this stone, and the marks are still—" They stopped, overwhelmed. "It remembers them. The way the gate remembered my name. It keeps everything. Every promise. Every person."

"And Maren is in it," Petra said softly. "A little. The part she gave away in the pass. So if we follow the thread—" She didn't finish. She didn't need to.

If they followed the thread, they were walking into the Spire's heart, into Serath's waiting army, into the exact jaws of everything Felix had sworn an hour ago to protect her from. But if they threw the shard away, they were throwing away Maren too — the part of her that persisted, the part that leaned toward home like a plant toward light — and they were throwing away the only map to the one place where any of this could end.

Grief, Felix thought, was just love with nowhere to go. And here, suddenly, unbearably, was somewhere for it to go.

* * *

They made camp in a hollow beneath the ledge, a fire no larger than two cupped hands, because large fires drew eyes and eyes were the Order's favorite currency. Felix boiled snow for tea they didn't drink. Petra ate the last of the hard cheese without complaint, in the methodical way of a child who had known real hunger and never intended to perform gratitude for food again.

Felix sat with the shard in their open palm and studied it as though seeing it for the first time.

Up close, in firelight, it wasn't really stone at all. There were layers in it, strata fine as paper, and in each layer — when Felix tilted it just so — something moved. Not figures. Impressions. The ghost of a gesture. Once, unmistakably, a flash of silver-gray hair falling across a familiar lined face, there and gone in the space of a heartbeat, like a reflection in moving water.

Felix's breath stopped.

"What is it?" Petra asked.

"She's in here." Felix's voice came out strange, flattened with awe. "Not — not her, not a soul, I'm not going to pretend I know what happens after. But a shape of her. An impression. The stone was part of the seal and the seal was made of them, and it kept—" They turned the fragment over, and there it was again: a curve of cheekbone, the particular set of Maren's jaw when she was about to say something devastatingly practical. "It kept its promises. Ilva told us. Every promise ever made to it is still being kept. And Maren promised it her life, fifty years ago, and the keeping hasn't finished yet."

Petra crawled close and looked, and her breath fogged the firelight. "There are others," she murmured. "Look. Here — a man laughing. Here — someone with rings on every finger. Here—" She paused at a layer near the shard's heart, and her voice changed. "This one is empty. A shape with nothing in it. Like a room after everyone's gone."

"Serath," Felix said. The name tasted like ash. "He drained his out. Years ago, according to the archives. He's been drinking the seal like wine." They closed their fist around the shard, careful even in anger, and found that the anger had changed texture overnight. Yesterday it had been a wild thing, all teeth. Tonight it had a direction. Tonight it was a tool. "He thinks he's emptying it. He thinks when the last drop is gone he'll simply step in and take the whole thing for himself."

"But the stone remembers what he was," Petra said. "Before. When he made his promise. It's still keeping that promise too, somewhere, even though he's forgotten it." She looked up at Felix, and in the firelight her aged-young eyes held something that was almost pity — pity aimed past Felix, at a man none of them had seen since the salt flats. "That's what will undo him. Not fighting him. Remembering him better than he remembers himself."

Felix looked at this child — this impossible, borrowed, self-made child — and felt the grief in their chest shift position, the way a wound shifts when it begins, at last, to knit.

"We can't fight an army," they said. "We can't outrun one anymore either; the passes are sealed and Wren is hurt and the salt flats are behind us. So we do the one thing they can't anticipate." They opened their hand and let the firelight fill the shard's pale layers. "We follow the thread. Straight through the Order's capital, straight up the crater, straight to the heart. We don't sneak around the story, Petra. We walk into the middle of it and we finish it."

"The stone wants to go home," Petra agreed solemnly. Then, after a moment, with the small crooked smile that was hers alone and belonged to no vault and no spire: "So do you. You just needed it to go first."

Above the gorge, clouds tore open and let down a spill of stars, and for a moment the whole broken landscape lay silver and silent, like a page waiting to be written on.

Felix slept that night with the shard tucked against their sternum, and dreamed — for the first time since the rockfall — not of falling stones, but of a tower door opening, and a dry voice saying, *Well. Ask, then,* and meaning it kindly.

They woke before dawn with the answer already forming, and the shard warm as a held hand, and the thread pulling them onward — down out of the mountains, toward the lights of the city that served the Order, toward the last road home.
