## 26. The City That Serves the Order

The road to Corvast was paved with pilgrims.

Felix had not expected that. They had imagined the Order's capital as a fortress-city, grim and gated, approached by empty roads lined with warning stones. Instead, the last three days of their journey had been spent shuffling in a slow river of the devout—farmers with sunburnt necks, merchants' wives carrying caged doves for offering, whole families from the eastern provinces walking behind carts heaped with salt and candle-wax. Everyone, it seemed, wanted to be seen kneeling before the Ashen Hand. Everyone wanted to be on the right side of the power that was coming.

"They're not afraid," Petra said, walking at Felix's elbow with her hood up and her hands buried in the sleeves of her borrowed pilgrim's robe. She had learned to make herself small in crowds; she did it now without being told. "They should be afraid."

"Fear needs somewhere to go," Felix murmured. "These people have nowhere. So they've turned it into worship."

Maren walked a little ahead of them, leaning on a staff she no longer strictly needed but refused to abandon. The closer they drew to the Spire, the more she faded—her color graying, her steps shorter, as though some ledger somewhere were calling in its debts line by line. She had stopped pretending otherwise around the salt flats. Now she simply endured it, jaw set, eyes forward, and if Felix caught her wincing when she thought no one watched, they were kind enough to look away.

"Keep your voices down," Maren said now, without turning. "From here to the gates, we are three pilgrims from the western valleys, come to give thanks for a good harvest. We are nobody. We have never been anybody."

"You were somebody once," Petra said. "Your name is carved on a mountain."

"And that is precisely why I am nobody now," Maren said dryly. "Dead women cast long shadows, child. Best to walk beside them quietly."

The walls of Corvast rose out of the morning haze like the spine of some buried god—black granite quarried, Felix knew from stolen pages read by candlelight twenty years ago, from the same ridge-stone that ringed their valley. There was a bitter symmetry in that. The Order had built its capital out of the bones of dead magic, and now it sat there gleaming, drinking in the awe of ten thousand pilgrims who came each season to touch the walls and whisper prayers to mages who had once been healers.

Once. That word again. It kept surfacing in Felix's mind like a drowned body refusing to stay down.

* * *

The gates took half a day.

Pilgrims funneled through in lines that wound back along the road, past braziers burning gray flame—the Order's signature, ash-fire, cold and smokeless—and past gray-cloaked wardens who moved down the queues with ledgers and long needles of black iron. At first Felix thought the needles were weapons. Then they saw one used: a warden pressed the point briefly against a farmer's wrist, and the needle stayed dull and dark. He passed through. The woman behind him flinched from the touch; the needle bloomed with a faint blue shimmer, like frost catching light.

"Speaker," the warden said, not unkindly, which somehow made it worse. "Step aside, please."

What followed was brief and bureaucratic and made Felix's stomach turn over. The woman was questioned. Her children cried. She was given a choice, spoken softly enough that only those nearby could hear: registration and licensing—a brand on the wrist, a lifetime of reporting, of permissions asked and granted or denied—or the sanctuary process, which everyone in the queue understood meant the gray tower at the city's heart, from which no one returned to talk about it.

She chose the brand. Of course she chose the brand. They all chose the brand.

"Testing for what?" Petra whispered, watching the needle pass down the line toward them.

"Resonance," Felix breathed. "Whether you carry any spark. Whether the land answers when you—" They stopped. Their mouth had gone dry. "Petra. When they touch you with that thing—"

"I know." Her voice was very small inside the hood. "I've been thinking about it since yesterday."

There was no way around the needles. There was no gate for the untested. Felix ran the options again, the ones they'd been turning over for two days, and arrived where they always arrived: nowhere. So they did the only thing left—they trusted the strangest fact about the girl they'd been running with for weeks.

When the warden reached them, Felix went first. The needle touched their wrist and stayed dark. Twenty years of suppression, of living where magic couldn't follow, had scoured them nearly clean; whatever embers still lived in them were banked too deep for black iron to find. Maren passed next—the needle flickered, barely, a ghost of blue, and the warden frowned at it, tapped it against his palm, and flickered it again. "Old scar tissue," Maren rasped, in the cracked voice of a woman half-dead, which required no acting at all. "Burned it out of myself years ago, praise the Hand." The warden's expression suggested he'd heard stranger penances. He waved her through.

Then Petra.

Felix's heart hammered so hard they were sure the whole queue could hear it. The warden took the girl's thin wrist in his gloved hand and touched the needle to the pulse-point, and Felix watched his face with the attention of a person reading scripture in the dark.

Nothing happened.

The needle stayed black as a dead star. The warden grunted, released her, and moved on—and Petra walked through the gates of Corvast untouched, because whatever she was, she was not a speaker. She was the thing the speakers spoke *to*. The language lived in her the way rivers live in stone; there was nothing in her blood for the iron to find, no borrowed spark, only the source itself wearing a child's shape, and the Order's instruments had never been built to detect the thing they worshipped.

Later, in the shadow of an inn's stairwell, Petra looked at her own wrist as though it belonged to someone else.

"It didn't feel anything," she said. "I felt *it*. It was like a fly landing."

"Don't say that where anyone can hear," Felix whispered, and pulled her hood lower, and hated how much the fear in their own voice sounded like the fear in every other voice in that city.

* * *

Corvast inside the walls was beautiful, and that was the most horrifying thing about it.

Felix had expected squalor and jackboots, a city clenched like a fist. Instead they found fountains and flower-sellers, bakeries venting warm air into streets washed clean each morning, gray banners hung with white doves stitched on them—the dove of the founding covenant, the bird that had carried the thirteen mages' oaths to the Spire, though Felix doubted one pilgrim in a hundred knew that anymore. Children chased each other through the plazas. Old men played dice outside a shrine to the First Circle. Everything was orderly, prosperous, calm.

And everywhere, woven into the calm like gray thread through bright cloth, the Order. Wardens on every corner. Ash-braziers at every intersection, their cold flames burning day and night. Notices nailed to every post in crisp printed letters:

*Magic is a trust, not a birthright.*
*Report unlicensed speech. Protect your neighbors.*
*The Hand holds, so the world holds.*

"They believe it," Maren said quietly. They stood on a bridge over one of the canals, ostensibly admiring the water, actually waiting for a procession to clear the square beyond. "That's what I keep trying to make you understand, Felix. Look at them. They aren't wicked. Serath started with people exactly like this—scared farmers, hedge-mages whose workings had gone wrong and burned a village, parents who'd lost children to wild enchantment. He offered them order. Safety. A world where power answered to law instead of appetite." She exhaled slowly. "I helped him write the first charter. I believed every word of it."

"And then?"

"And then we learned that safety is a hunger, too. And it eats the same things ambition does." She nodded across the canal, toward the great gray mass rising above the rooftops at the city's center: the Bastion, the Order's citadel, its spires crowned with ash-fires that never went out. "Come. The square. You should see it. You came here to see clearly, and clarity is bought ugly in this city."

Felix didn't understand until they reached the Square of the Covenant.

* * *

The executions were held at midday, between the market hours and the evening bells, so that no one would have to choose between them.

A scaffold had been raised beneath the statue of the Thirteen—the founders themselves, rendered in white marble, arms linked, gazing nobly toward a horizon that no longer existed. Below their serene stone faces, six people knelt in a row, wrists bound, gray hoods over their heads. A herald read the charges in a ringing voice trained for exactly this: unlicensed working, sedition, trafficking in forbidden texts, harboring a speaker. The crowd stood shoulder to shoulder in the winter sun, and they were quiet—not hostile, not cheering, just quiet, the way people are quiet at funerals and harvest-tithes. That was the worst part. This was routine. This was Tuesday.

"The third one," Maren murmured. "Look at her hands."

Felix looked. Beneath the hood's edge, the third prisoner's fingers were stained green to the second knuckle—an herbalist's stain, a gardener's stain, the mark of a life spent coaxing things to grow. Someone's grandmother, probably. Someone who had sung to her beansprouts the way folk had sung to bean sprouts since before there were kingdoms, and called it nothing, because it *was* nothing, it was just love with dirt on its hands.

"She isn't a mage," Felix said. Their voice came out strange and flat. "She's a gardener."

"In this city, the distinction is administrative." Maren's face was carved from something older than marble. "Watch."

The Grand Warden presiding raised his hand, and Felix felt it before they understood what they were feeling—a pressure, a wrongness, like the moment before a storm breaks, except inverted, drained, a *pulling*. The warden spoke a word of binding, clean and formal and licensed, and the six prisoners sagged as something was drawn out of them. Felix saw it happen. Saw it with the part of themselves they had spent twenty years starving: the threads of each prisoner's small native magic, the little warmth each soul carries whether it works spells or not, drawn up and out and funneled into the ash-braziers at the corners of the scaffold, where the gray flames leapt and brightened.

The crowd sighed. Some of them bowed their heads reverently. A child near Felix tugged her mother's sleeve and asked why the fire got bigger, and the mother said, *because justice feeds it, darling,* and Felix had to close their eyes.

When they opened them, the prisoners were slumped, emptied, gray-faced—and still alive, which Felix realized was the mercy being advertised. The Order didn't kill unlicensed speakers. It *harvested* them, lightly, repeatedly, a tithe of the soul, and sent them home to tell their neighbors how painless it had been. The two at the end of the row—the ones charged with sedition, with *harboring*—were different. For those, the herald announced, the full forfeit had been decreed. Those two did not stand up afterward.

Petra's hand found Felix's. Her grip was crushing. She had gone absolutely rigid, and when Felix glanced down at her, her lips were moving—soundlessly, frantically—shaping syllables of the First Tongue over and over like a prayer, or a dam holding.

"*Don't*," Felix breathed into her ear. "*Not here. Not one word.*"

"It's asking me," she whispered back, barely audible, her eyes fixed on the scaffold with an expression Felix had never seen on her before—ancient and appalled. "All of it. The whole city. Everything they've taken—it's all pooled here, under the square, and it's *screaming*, Felix. It knows me. It wants—"

"Petra." Felix turned her gently by the shoulders, away from the scaffold, cupping her hooded face in both hands. Behind them the herald's voice rang out the closing benediction and the crowd began to disperse, chattering about supper. "Listen to me. Whatever it wants—you are not its answer today. Today you're a tired pilgrim girl who ate bad shellfish and needs to lie down. Can you be that? Can you hold it until we're behind a closed door?"

Her breathing slowed, degree by degree. She nodded once.

They walked away from the Square of the Covenant at a pilgrim's pace, unhurried, heads bowed, while inside Felix something coiled tighter and tighter and finally hardened into a single cold bright point, like a drop of water becoming ice.

They had come to Corvast afraid. Fear had gotten them through the gates, through the queues, through the square with their heads bowed. But standing in the shadow of the marble Thirteen, listening to a child describe the sound of stolen souls screaming under the cobblestones, Felix found the fear transmuting into something far more useful.

Anger. Not the hot kind that makes mistakes. The cold kind that makes plans.

*Serath began as a protector,* they thought, looking up at the serene marble faces of mages who had once been heroes, and who now presided over gardens and grandmothers being milked of their souls to feed gray fires. *He told himself every step was necessary. And every step was smaller than the last one, until there were no steps left, only a slope.*

*This is what the seal buys,* they thought. *This is what 'safety' looks like when it grows up.*

They had spent years believing the valley's forgetting was a mercy. Standing in Corvast, they finally understood what the forgetting was *for*—not protection from magic, but protection of a monopoly. The Order didn't fear the Spire waking. The Order feared the Spire waking *free*, answering to no license, no ledger, no gray-clad hand. Every execution in that square was a brick in the wall Serath was building around the heart of the world, and he meant to be the only door.

Well. Felix had crossed a dead valley, survived salt flats and gorge and grief, traded their name to a gate of stone. They could manage one more wall.

* * *

They lodged that night at the Sign of the Tired Dove, a pilgrims' house near the tanners' quarter where the beds were cheap, the questions were few, and the walls were thick enough that Maren judged them safe to speak behind—if they spoke softly, and only after she had chalked three small nulling sigils in the corners of the room, workings so old and so minor that even the Order's iron needles would have shrugged at them.

Petra slept almost at once, curled small, exhausted by the effort of holding herself silent through the square. In her sleep her lips still moved sometimes, shaping fragments of the old language, and the candle flame leaned toward her like a listener.

Felix sat by the window with the shutter cracked, looking out over the rooftops toward the Bastion, where the ash-fires burned against the stars.

"We can't cross the city openly," Maren said from her chair, low. "The Spire road is walled, gated, and patrolled by the Third Circle themselves. Pilgrims don't go there anymore—haven't for a decade. Serath closed the approach after the last... incident." Her mouth twisted on the word. "We need another way. Down, not through."

"Down?"

"Every old city has two bodies, Felix. The one it shows the sun, and the one it grew over. Corvast's undercity runs beneath the archives, the aqueducts, the foundations of the Bastion itself. Smugglers use it. Deserters use it. People the Order has broken use it." She paused, and Felix heard in the pause the weight of what she was about to say, and braced. "There is a man down there they call Wren. Gutter-mage. Ex-warden. If anyone can move three ghosts beneath Serath's floorboards, it's him. And if anyone can get us into the archives on the way—"

"You want to see the drain records," Felix said. "You want proof of what he's been taking from the seal."

"I want you to see it. I've believed it for months. Belief isn't enough anymore. Not for what's coming." Maren's eyes, in the candlelight, were the color of old ice. "Tomorrow night. There's a shrine in the tanners' quarter, defunct, dedicated to a saint the Order struck from the rolls. Leave an offering of three copper birds on the sill, and wait."

Felix looked out at the city one last time—at the gray banners stirring in the dark, the cold brazier-fires, the vast sleeping weight of a capital that served the Order because it had forgotten there was ever anything else to serve—and felt the cold bright point of their anger settle into resolve.

Below the cobblestones, something screamed. Above them, marble heroes smiled.

"Three copper birds," Felix said. "And then we go down."
