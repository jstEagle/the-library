## 27. An Ally in Low Places

Wren had learned to read a city by its smells before he'd learned to read at all.

The capital of the Order of the Ashen Hand—Vhalcrest, though nobody who lived below called it that—smelled different depending on which level you were breathing. Up top, where the white towers scraped the clouds and the pilgrim roads ran clean as bleached bone, it was incense and cut stone and the faint char of execution pyres. Mid-city was bread and horse and tallow. But down here, in the undercity where the gutters ran and the Order's writ went thin as onion skin, it was wet slate and lamp-oil and old smoke baked into brick so deep that no rain would ever wash it out. Down here it smelled like the world before the Order decided to own it.

Wren crouched on a ledge above the Drowned Market with a bowl of eel broth going cold in his hands, and he listened to his city breathe.

He'd been a gutter-mage for eleven years now, ever since the night he'd walked out of the Ashen Citadel with his oath still wet on his tongue and his hands still shaking from what they'd made him do to the unlicensed speaker in Cellar Nine. Eleven years of small magics done quietly: warming sick children's rooms, finding lost things, easing the pain of dying women who couldn't afford a temple blessing. Nothing that would light up the Order's detection-wards. Nothing worth burning a man for. He'd gotten good at being invisible, at being *small*, and he'd told himself for eleven years that small was safe.

Then three days ago, a girl had walked past him in the fish-stalls, humming.

* * *

It hadn't been much. Four notes, maybe five, looping over itself like water finding its way downhill. Any other child's tune, he'd have thought. But Wren had spent six years in the Citadel's archive annex as an apprentice archivist before they'd promoted him to fieldwork, and he had copied out fragments of dead languages until his eyes bled, and one of those languages—the oldest one, the forbidden one, the one whose surviving pages were chained to iron lecterns and readable only by rank of Speaker-Second or higher—had a sound to it. A shape. The First Tongue wasn't just words on vellum. It was a music that predated music, and when it was spoken aloud, even in fragments, even by accident, it did something to the air. It made the air *pay attention*.

The girl's humming had made the air pay attention. The fish-seller's lantern had flared green for half a heartbeat. The puddle beneath her bare feet had gone still as glass, holding her reflection perfectly while every other reflection in the market shivered.

And then she'd passed on, mud to the knees, holding the hand of a tall figure in a hooded traveling cloak, and Wren had stood there with his broth cooling and his heart hammering like a fist on a door.

Because he knew what the Order paid for speakers of the First Tongue. He'd seen the bounty notices. He'd seen, once, from across a courtyard, what they did with them after.

* * *

Now he watched them come down into the Drowned Market, and his stomach turned over slow and cold.

They weren't trying to hide. That was the thing that frightened him most. Three of them, gray cloaks pushed back off their shoulders so the ash-gray hand sigil showed plain on their breasts, boots ringing on the wet stone like hammers on a bell. In the upper city they moved soft, in twos, asking questions in low voices. Down here they didn't bother. Down here everyone was already guilty of something; the only question was whether your guilt was useful.

The tallest one stopped at Old Sefa's stall, where Sefa sold river-mussels and lies in equal measure, and laid a coin purse down heavy enough that Wren could hear the clink from thirty feet up.

"Child of the old speech," the gray-cloak said, loud enough for the whole market. "Small. Dark-haired. Travels with an adult, foreign accent. There is more where this came from for whoever points us true."

Sefa's face did what all the faces in the undercity did when the Order came collecting: it closed like a shutter. "Haven't seen any children but my own grandsons, and they don't talk old anything. They barely talk at all."

A ripple of careful laughter through the nearby stalls. Nobody looked at anybody. Everybody looked at everybody sideways. This was the arithmetic of the undercity performed nightly: how much is silence worth, how much is coin worth, which neighbor will break first. Wren had watched that arithmetic end lives. He'd been on the wrong side of it once himself, back when he still believed the Order's sums were honest.

The gray-cloaks moved on, deeper into the market, and Wren set down his untouched broth and began, quietly, to follow the other thread.

* * *

Finding them again took him two days, and it nearly cost him.

They were lodging—not lodging, hiding—in the hollow beneath the Broken Aqueduct, where the old imperial channel crossed the Sump Ward and the space between its stone legs had been walled off generations ago by squatters and never reclaimed because nothing down there was worth reclaiming. Wren knew it because he'd warmed a feverish smuggler's child there two winters back. He approached the way he approached everything now: slow, open-handed, announcing himself with a knock on stone rather than a spell.

The tall one met him at the gap in the wall with a knife held low and competent, and behind the tall one's shoulder Wren saw the girl—small, dark-haired, mud-caked, watching him with eyes that were far too old for her face—and felt the air around her do that paying-attention thing again, faint as a struck tuning fork.

"I'm not here to sell you," Wren said, keeping his voice flat and his hands wide. "If I were, I'd have taken the purse from the gray-cloaks yesterday and saved myself the walk."

"They wouldn't have paid you," the tall one said. Their voice was rough, genderless in the hood-shadow, accented from somewhere north. "They'd have taken whatever you knew and then taken you, because you're a mage. Unlicensed." A pause, and Wren felt the word land on him like a hand on his collar. "I can see it. You're doing something right now to keep me from seeing how tired you are."

Wren blinked. He *was* doing that—a cantrip so small and so habitual he'd forgotten it was a spell at all, a little glamour that smoothed the face. He let it drop, and felt his exhaustion arrive like weather.

"Felix," the tall one said, after a moment, and didn't offer a hand. "This is Petra. You have two minutes."

"I need less than that." Wren crouched, putting himself below the girl's eye line, the way you did with children and with frightened animals, and he spoke to her directly because everything about her posture said she hated being spoken around. "Four days ago, in the fish market, you were humming. Round tune, four notes, comes back on itself."

Petra said nothing. But her chin lifted a fraction.

"When you hummed it, the lanterns burned green and the water went still." He kept his voice gentle, matter-of-fact, the way you described weather. "That's the First Tongue. I know it because I used to be an archivist for the people hunting you, before I understood what they were, and I copied enough of their stolen pages to know the sound of it if it ever crawled out of a book and walked past me wearing shoes."

Silence. Water dripped somewhere in the dark of the aqueduct, patient as a clock.

"You're the deserter," Felix said at last. "There was a notice about you. Three years ago. Wren of the Ninth Annex, fled during the Tallow Street burnings."

"The Tallow Street burnings," Wren repeated. "Yes. That's one name for what happened there. Here's mine: we burned eleven people for speaking prayers older than the Order, and I carried the torch for number seven, and she looked at me while she burned and she wasn't screaming, she was *singing*, and I have not slept properly since." He heard his own voice crack on the last of it and didn't bother to smooth it over. Honesty was the only currency he had left. "So. That's what I am. Now let me tell you what I'm offering, because I know you won't take charity and neither would I."

Felix's knife-hand hadn't moved. "Go on."

"There's no road out of Vhalcrest anymore. I've checked. The Order sealed the pilgrim gates two days ago—they're calling it a plague quarantine, which is a lie so lazy it's almost insulting. Every gate, every bridge, every ferry has a detection-ward strung across it tuned to find wild magic and old speech. You walk through one of those with her"—he nodded at Petra—"and every bell in the Citadel rings at once. The only way out of this city that isn't watched is underneath it. The undercity runs on top of the old city, and the old city runs on top of the *older* city, and some of those deepest ways predate the Order entirely. Predate the Spire's sealing, maybe. I've mapped pieces of them. Enough pieces."

"And the price," Felix said. Not a question. Everyone down here had a price; it was practically a greeting.

"The truth." Wren stood, knees cracking, and looked from Felix to the girl and back. "I've spent three years hearing rumors. That the Spire isn't sealed, it's sleeping. That the seal is failing. That the Order's own Speaker-General has been bleeding it for years to keep his magic fat. That there are people—children—who come out from under it speaking the First Tongue like it's their mother's lullaby." He nodded at Petra. "She's one of those rumors standing in front of me. So here's my price, and it's not coin. You get passage through the deep ways—all the way out, under the walls, to the salt flats side. In exchange, when we're clear, you tell me what happens at the Spire. All of it. What she is. What the seal actually is. What the Order is really doing up in that citadel besides burning singers. I want the truth with the skin still on it. No cleaned version. If it's ugly, I want it ugly."

"Why?" It was Petra who asked. Her first words, and her voice was young and strange and carried that faint harmonic underneath it, like a bell rung inside a larger bell. "Why does the truth matter to you?"

Wren opened his mouth to give the easy answer—*because I deserve to know what I helped build*—and found it wouldn't come out. He stood there in the dripping dark with the question hanging, and finally gave the true one instead.

"Because I burned a woman alive for singing, and I've been telling myself ever since that it must have mattered somehow. That the Order must have had reasons. That the seal must be worth it." His hands had started shaking; he watched them do it without shame. "If the truth is that it was all rot from the beginning—if the Spire never needed caging, if the people in the gray cloaks are just thieves with better robes—then I need to know that, because a man can live with being a monster for a good reason. What I can't live with is being a monster for a lie."

* * *

They argued about it for an hour, in low voices, in the dark behind the wall, and Wren sat outside in the cold and let them, because trust that was given too fast was worth exactly what it cost.

He used the time to check his caches. Two vials of ward-burn, a spool of null-thread, a bone whistle that could call rats by the thousand if it came to that—it had come to that twice, and both times had been memorable. He ate half a heel of black bread. He listened to the city above: the patrol-boots on the aqueduct road, regular as a heartbeat; the criers reading the quarantine proclamation in voices trained to sound sorry about it; and once, distantly, from the direction of the Citadel, a sound he'd hoped never to hear again—the long bronze note of the Summoning Bell, tolling out a convocation of the full Order. Something was happening up there. Something soon.

When Felix finally emerged, the hood was back and the knife was away, and that was answer enough before any words came.

"We'll take your passage," Felix said. "And your price. But not after we're clear. On the road. Tonight, while we walk. I'm not carrying it another day than I have to, and—" a pause, and something moved behind the hood that might have been a grimace, "—and the child says I should tell you now anyway. She says secrets get heavy and heavy things attract attention. She says a lot of things like that. I've stopped asking how she knows."

From the dark of the hollow, Petra's voice floated out, mild as milk: "The Spire taught me to listen. Listening is mostly learning what weight sounds like."

Wren laughed before he could stop himself—a short, rusty sound, the first real laugh in longer than he cared to count. "Your child is either a prophet or a menace."

"She's both," Felix said. "Mostly she's hungry. Can you feed us? We'll pay in stories."

* * *

They went down into the deep ways at moonset, when even the patrols grew bored and the lamps in the Sump Ward guttered low.

Wren led them through a door hidden behind a shrine to a saint the Order had expunged from its calendars two centuries ago—down stairs worn to shallow curves by feet older than any kingdom Felix could name, along a cistern gallery where the water sang in the dark, into corridors where the stones themselves were worked with symbols that made Felix stop and stare and made Petra reach out and touch them like a child greeting familiar faces.

"These are First Tongue," Felix whispered, running fingers over a carved spiral. "Or near enough. These passages are *old*."

"Older than the Order," Wren said. "Maybe older than the sealing. Nobody knows who built them. The Order pretends they don't exist, which is the sincerest form of respect." He glanced back at Petra, walking between them with her hand trailing along the ancient carvings, and lowered his voice. "You said tonight. On the road. I'm holding you to it."

"I know." Felix was quiet for three steps, five, ten. When they spoke again, the roughness in their voice had changed quality—this was the sound of someone opening a door they'd kept barred for years. "Then here is where it starts. The valley I lived in—the quiet place, the place where spells die at the ridgeline—that valley isn't natural. It's the seal. Or it's the seal's shadow, cast onto the world. And the seal isn't stone or wards or anything the Order teaches. It was woven from thirteen mages' lives. Given freely, or so I was told. And one of those thirteen raised me, and lied to me for twenty years, and died three weeks ago holding a mountain pass so we could reach the Spire and ask it—"

"Ask it what?" Wren breathed.

Felix looked back at him, and in the witchlight of Wren's little floating flame, their eyes were wet and steady and terribly tired.

"To be witnessed," Felix said. "Not worshipped. Not caged. Just—heard. That's the whole secret. Thirteen lives and a murdered valley and a burned woman singing on a pyre, and underneath all of it, the entire rotten architecture of the Order, sits a lonely thing that only wanted someone to listen." A breath that shook slightly on the way out. "And the Order's Speaker-General has been drinking its blood for years. We have records. We have proof. And we mean to stand in front of the whole rotten edifice and speak the question aloud before Serath can tear the heart out of the world."

Behind them, Petra hummed—four notes, round, returning on themselves—and the old stones of the deep ways lit softly, gently, all along the corridor ahead, like a house welcoming someone home.

Wren stopped walking. He stared at the light, and at the child who had made it, and felt the last load-bearing lie of his life shift and settle and come down.

"Well," he said, when he could speak. "Then you'd better tell me the rest. All of it. With the skin on."

And far above them, muffled through fathoms of stone, the Summoning Bell began to toll again—faster now, urgent, calling the gray cloaks home to war.
