## 28. Beneath the Citadel

The undercity smelled of wet stone and old smoke, of tallow and rust and the particular sourness of a city's secrets left too long in the dark. Felix had imagined, on the road from the salt flats, that beneath a citadel they would find emptiness—cellars, cisterns, perhaps the bones of some older town swallowed by the Order's ambition. Instead they found a world.

Wren led them down through a hatch behind a dyer's shop, past stairs that spiraled into darkness worn smooth by centuries of feet. He carried no torch. That had been the first strange thing Felix noticed about him, back in the gutter-market where he'd recognized Petra's humming for what it was: he moved through dark places as though the dark owed him courtesy. A gutter-mage's trick, he'd said, when Felix asked. Small magics for small spaces. The kind of magic nobody bothers to license, because nobody bothers to see it.

"Rules," Wren said now, his voice pitched low under the drip of water somewhere ahead. "Rule one: we don't speak above a whisper once we pass the cisterns. Sound travels strange down here—the archives above are built to *listen*. Rule two: if I say *ash*, you drop whatever you're holding and you run for the nearest ladder. Rule three—" he paused at a junction where three tunnels yawned like throats, and touched the wall with two fingers, reading something in the stone that Felix could not see, "—rule three is that nobody touches anything with writing on it until I've looked at it first."

"Why?" Petra asked.

She had been quiet all day, quieter than usual, and Felix had learned to be afraid of her quiet. Since the salt flats, since Maren fell, she had taken to walking half a step behind them instead of beside them, as though she were learning again how to be small. But tonight, in the dark, something in her voice had steadied. Perhaps the Spire's song in her blood liked being underground. Perhaps it recognized where it was going.

"Because everything written down here was written by people who wanted it found," Wren said, "or by people who wanted it *never* found, and the second kind bites." He turned left. "The Order didn't build this. They built *on top* of it. These tunnels predate the Ashen Hand by four hundred years. Back then this city worshipped the Spire openly—pilgrims came up the mountain roads every midsummer to hear it hum. When the seal went in and the humming stopped, the pilgrims stopped coming, and the Order moved into the empty houses and called themselves protectors." His mouth twisted. "My grandmother used to say the Order is just a church that forgot its god. Now I know she was being generous."

They walked. The tunnels narrowed, widened, narrowed again. Once they passed through a chamber where the ceiling opened into a shaft of true darkness, and Felix felt—distinctly, unmistakably—a pressure against their skin, like walking into deep water. Magic. Not dead, not sealed, but *pooled*, the way cold air pools in cellars. Four hundred years of the Order's workings, seeping down through the floors above like grease through paper.

Petra stopped in the middle of that chamber and tilted her head, listening to something.

"What is it?" Felix whispered.

"Echoes," she said. "The words they said up there. They sink." She frowned, and added, in a smaller voice: "Some of them are Maren's."

Felix's chest tightened. They did not ask how she knew. Some part of them already understood: the First Tongue remembered its speakers, and Maren had spoken it once, long ago, kneeling in a circle of thirteen with her life open in her hands. Of course her words had sunk. Words like hers would sink through stone and keep sinking forever.

They passed the cisterns in silence, as instructed—vast brick vaults where black water lay perfectly still, mirroring nothing, and Felix had the crawling sense of being watched by the water itself—and then Wren stopped before a wall that looked like every other wall, except that a hairline crack ran from floor to ceiling, and through the crack came air that tasted of dust and lamp oil and paper.

"The archive undercroft," Wren breathed. "Directly beneath the Hall of Records. There's a grate in the floor up there; sound comes down like rain. This is where I heard things, back when I still wore the gray cloak. Things I wasn't supposed to hear." He looked at Felix, and in the gloom his face was all shadows and one bright eye. "You wanted proof. It's up those stairs. Third door on the left is the Registry of Seals—no, don't look at me like that, I know exactly where it is, I filed papers there for six years. Serath's private annex is attached to it. Sealed with a working keyed to his blood. Which is why we're not going through the door."

Felix stared at him. "Then how—"

Wren grinned, and it was not a comforting grin. "The Order built their hall on top of a pilgrim tunnel because they never look *down*. Nobody looks down. That's the whole religion of power, friend: everyone keeps their eyes on the throne." He drew a thin iron bar from his coat and knelt at the base of the wall, working it into the crack. "There's a crawl-space behind here. Old flue, maybe. Runs up inside the walls into the annex itself. It'll be tight, it'll be filthy, and if there are wards woven into the masonry—which there will be—they'll be tuned to the doors and windows, because Serath trusts his own architecture and architecture always forgets the space between the stones."

"And if he doesn't trust it?"

"Then we're dead, and honestly, given the alternative—" Wren shrugged. "I've been dead since the day I deserted. Everything since is a bonus."

* * *

The flue was worse than tight. Felix went first, because Petra refused to go second and Wren refused to let her go first, and so they compromised with Felix in front, elbows scraping stone, breath loud in their own ears, inching upward through a channel barely wider than their shoulders. Dust filled their mouth. Somewhere below, faintly, they could hear Wren talking to Petra—low, steady, an endless murmured commentary about nothing, about the quality of the mortar, about a girl he'd known in the gutters who could fit through a window bars couldn't stop—keeping her calm the way you keep a lantern lit in wind.

And Felix climbed, and thought about Maren.

Three days dead. Three days, and Felix still caught themselves turning to speak to her—to ask which way, to ask whether the wards ahead were hers or older, to ask the thousand small questions an apprentice asks without thinking. Grief, Felix was discovering, was not one wound but a thousand tiny habits with nowhere to go. Every habit had been Maren's. Every silence between sentences had been shaped by her silences. And underneath the grief, coiled and patient, sat the other thing—the thing Felix had carried since the gorge, since Maren's last words: *Ask better questions than I did.*

What questions? Felix had turned it over every night since, lying awake on frozen ground while Petra slept against their side. What question had Maren asked badly, thirty years ago, kneeling in that circle? *How do we stop it?* Perhaps. And the Spire, which kept its promises literally, had answered: *you stop it by stopping.* Thirteen lives. A valley made to forget. A child made to carry messages no one should carry. Maren had asked how to end the danger, and had never thought to ask what the danger *wanted*, or whether it was a danger at all.

The flue bent. Felix squeezed around the bend, and light appeared above—not much, a seam of amber glow leaking through a grille overhead—and beneath the glow, scratched into the flue wall by some long-dead hand, someone had written a single word in old script. Felix knew enough letters to read it.

*Listen.*

Their skin prickled. They pressed their palm flat against the word for a moment—foolish, probably, but the stone felt warm, or they needed it to feel warm—and climbed on.

The grille gave way to Wren's bar after ten minutes of careful prying, and Felix slid out into darkness, onto carpet. Actual carpet, thick and silent, in a room that smelled of ink and sealing-wax and, faintly, of something else—something mineral and cold, like the air off a glacier. Like the salt flats. Like the crater at the Spire's foot.

Serath's annex.

Wren emerged behind them, then lifted Petra through, and for a moment all three stood still in the dark, listening. Above them, muffled by the floor, footsteps crossed and recrossed. A bell rang somewhere, twice. Voices rose and fell in the cadence of routine administration—an empire digesting its ordinary evening.

"Registry's through that door," Wren whispered, pointing. "But the annex first. Look around. Whatever he keeps close, he keeps *here*."

The room resolved slowly as Felix's eyes adjusted. A desk of black wood, immaculate. Shelves of ledgers, spined in gray leather, each stamped with the ash-gray hand. A map of the known world on the far wall, pinned with colored markers—and Felix's breath caught, because the markers clustered in a pattern they recognized. Around the valley. Around the passes. Around the hospice where Ilva lay dying. He had been hunting the sealers' remnants for years. He had been *tidying*.

And on the desk, centered as carefully as a relic on an altar, lay a single object under a glass dome: a shard of pale stone, veined with dull gold, no bigger than a knife-handle.

Felix knew it instantly. Their hand went to their own pocket, where Ilva's shard sat wrapped in wool, and felt it *answer*—a faint warmth, a pull, like a compass needle trembling toward its twin.

"That's seal-stone," Petra whispered. She had gone very still, her eyes reflecting the amber light strangely. "It's crying."

"It's what?" Wren hissed.

"It's crying. Can't you hear it?" Her face crumpled with something between pity and fury. "He cut it out of the seal and it's been screaming ever since and nobody *listens*—"

"Fen," Wren said sharply, using the false name they traveled under. "The ledgers. We have minutes, not hours. Find me dates."

Felix tore their eyes from the dome. The ledgers. Yes. They pulled the first from the shelf and opened it—and understood within a page that they were looking at something no archivist was meant to file away neatly. These were Serath's private records, kept in his own cipher, but ciphers are only language wearing a mask, and Felix had spent years decoding hedge-recipes and herb-lore written in deliberately garbled hands. Within minutes they had the shape of it. Within ten, they had the truth.

The entries began eleven years ago. *Extraction, minor. Subject stable.* Then, a year later: *Extraction, moderate. Seal integrity: nominal.* Then more often. Twice a year. Then quarterly. Each entry recorded a drawing-down of power from the seal—from the weave of thirteen lives that held the Spire—and each recorded, in columns of neat figures, where that power had gone. Not to the Order's defenses. Not to the border wards. To *him*. To workings of ascension, of preservation, of binding-of-vitality. Serath had been drinking from the seal for eleven years, a spoonful at a time, and the figures told the story plainly: each extraction, the seal's integrity dropped. Each extraction, the numbers of *incidents* rose—wild surges, unlicensed manifestations, villages touched by raw magic at the edges of the world. The very chaos the Order claimed to fight, the very chaos that justified its iron rule, was being manufactured by the man who ruled it.

Felix read on, and the fury came slow and then all at once, rising like water in a closed room. Because there was more. The last pages were different—newer, the ink darker, the handwriting tighter. *Final extraction scheduled.* *Subject: the vessel.* *Upon completion, the bargain lapses and the full reserve transfers to the bearer.* The vessel. The word sat there, clinical and obscene. *Vessel.*

They know what she is. They've known all along.

"Felix." Wren's voice, very quiet. "Whatever you've found—you need to see the date on the last page."

Felix turned the ledger. The final entry was dated nine days hence. *Convocation of the Ashen Hand. Full convocation, all chapters. The Ascension.*

Nine days. And across the valley, across the mountains, every soul in the place that had sheltered Felix for seven years—Bram with his debts, Odile with her ledger, the whole drowsy village that had never done anything worse than value its own invisibility—would stand inside a failing seal when it broke uncontrolled, unless—

Unless someone reached the Spire first. Unless someone asked better questions.

"Felix," Petra said again, and her voice had changed, and Felix turned and saw her standing rigid before the glass dome, both fists pressed against it, tears running silently down her face. "Make it stop. Please. Make it stop crying."

And Felix—furious, terrified, certain for the first time since the gorge—crossed the room, lifted the dome, and took the shard.

It screamed against their palm, a painless agony, a grief with no body, and beside it in their pocket Ilva's shard sang back, and Felix held them together and felt the whole broken seal tremble like a struck bell. *We're coming,* they thought, not knowing if the stone could hear, not knowing if wanting it counted for anything. *Hold on. We're coming home.*

Behind them, above, a bell began to ring—not twice this time, but over and over, urgent, spreading.

Wren was already at the flue. "*Ash*," he breathed. "Move. *Now.*"

* * *

They went down through the dark the way water goes down, fast and silent, Wren leading, Felix clutching the shard, Petra's hand locked in theirs. Behind and above them the citadel woke—doors slamming, boots on stone, the great machine of the Order grinding into motion—and Felix understood, with a clarity that felt almost like peace, that the alarm might not be for them at all. Perhaps no one yet knew they'd come. Perhaps the bell rang for some other failure, some other night.

It didn't matter. Nine days. The plan crystallized in Felix's mind with every rung of the ladder, every turn of the tunnel, hard and bright as frost: reach the Spire before the Convocation. Stand in the heart of it with a child who spoke the oldest language in the world. And ask—not command, not bargain, not seal—*ask*.

In the dark, Petra's fingers tightened around theirs.

"I know what he's going to do," she whispered. "And I know how to stop him. But Felix—it has to be *both* of us. The words won't work from one mouth."

Felix looked down at her, at the tear-tracks drying on her small fierce face, and felt the weight of it settle onto their shoulders like a mantle: teacher and student, witness and voice, the last two people in the world who might speak to the Spire as equals.

"Then teach me," Felix said. "Everything. Starting now."

Above them, far away, the bells went on ringing, and beneath their feet, faint and patient, the stone began—very softly—to hum.
