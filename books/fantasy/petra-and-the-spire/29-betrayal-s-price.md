## 29. Betrayal's Price

Wren had spent eleven years learning every way a body could disappear in the undercity of Vhal Corum, and he had learned them the hard way: with broken fingers, with nights in the drainage channels where the water ran warm and wrong, with the particular arithmetic of knowing exactly how much coin it took to make a gate-guard look elsewhere. He knew the smell of the undercity at every hour — the tallow-and-iron reek of the forges below the Citadel, the sweet rot of the mushroom farms, the cold mineral breath that rose from the old aqueducts where no one had walked since the Order sealed them. He knew which shadows were safe and which belonged to the Ashen Hand's gutter-runners.

What he did not know — what he had never once known in eleven years of careful, cowardly survival — was what it felt like to turn *toward* the thing hunting him instead of away from it.

He was learning now. It felt like swallowing a hot coal.

* * *

They had come down from the aqueduct an hour past midnight, Felix leading with the seal-shard cupped in one palm like a coal of its own, Petra riding on Wren's shoulders because her legs had given out two miles back and she had been too proud to say so until she simply stopped walking. Maren was dead. The gorge was behind them. And somewhere above, in the marble throat of the Citadel, Serath was tearing the last threads off the oldest bargain in the world.

The plan had been simple, the way all of Wren's plans were simple: down through the cistern district, along the Sump Road, out through the ash-gates before the fourth bell. Three hours. Maybe four with the child's pace. Then open country, then the glass crater, then whatever waited there.

Simple plans died loudly. This one died at the mouth of Tanner's Cut, where the passage narrowed between two walls of old brick sweating brine, when Wren heard the sound he had been dreading since they'd entered the undercity: boot-heels. Many. In step. Coming from both directions.

He put his hand flat against Petra's chest before she could take another step, and she went still with the eerie, total stillness he had come to think of as her native language — a stillness older than childhood, older than fear.

"Patrols," he breathed. "Two of them. Closing the cut."

Felix's face did something complicated in the dark — grief and fury and calculation all fighting for the same ground. "Is there another way?"

"There's always another way." Wren's mouth was dry. "That's the problem. Every other way is longer, and longer means the bell rings, and the bell ringing means the whole city knows a gutter-mage is walking three strangers toward the Spire."

The boots came closer. Torchlight bled around the far corner, orange and rhythmic, painting the wet bricks in stripes.

And here was the moment. Here it was, arriving on schedule, wearing its familiar face. Wren knew this moment intimately; it had visited him a hundred times in a hundred smaller shapes. The night the Order swept the Lantern Row and he had watched them drag off the cobbler's daughter who had taught him his first cantrip, and he had pulled his hood lower and said nothing. The morning his partner Tam had been caught forging license-stamps and Wren had denied knowing him so smoothly, so *quickly*, that the interrogator had actually smiled. Eleven years of moments, each one a small door closing, each one leaving him standing in a slightly smaller room.

He looked at Petra. She was looking at him. Not afraid — that was the terrible part. She was looking at him the way you look at weather, or at a stone about to fall, with the patience of someone who has already seen how everything ends and is only waiting for the world to catch up.

"You could," she said quietly, in the modern tongue, with the odd formal precision she used when she was being careful with words, "give us to them."

"I know," Wren said.

"They would pay you. Or forgive you. Or both." Her head tilted. "You have been thinking about it since the gorge. I hear it in your feet."

"My *feet*?"

"People's feet tell me things. Yours have been pointing at the exits all night."

Felix made a small wounded sound. Wren held up a hand. "Let her finish. She's not wrong."

"She's a child—"

"She's the most honest creature I've ever met, and if I'm going to do the worst thing of my life I'd rather do it facing someone who saw it coming." He turned back to Petra. "Say the rest."

She considered him. Torchlight crept closer up the wall, striping her face gold and shadow, gold and shadow.

"The Spire told me once — when I was very small, before I understood I was small — that everyone it ever bargained with wanted something first and became something after. It said people are doors that swing both ways, and most of them never learn they get to choose which way to face." She reached up and touched his jaw, one small grimy hand, matter-of-fact as a physician. "You are choosing right now. I can feel it. Like heat off a forge."

The torchlight was thirty paces away. Twenty-five. He could hear the patrol captain murmuring the sweep-cant — *clear left, hold right, net the mouth* — the liturgy of men who had done this a thousand times and would do it a thousand more.

Wren thought about Tam. He thought about the cobbler's daughter, whose name he had made himself forget and whose name came back to him now, unbidden, whole: *Sefa*. He thought about the eleven years of smaller rooms.

"Right," he said. His voice cracked on it, and he let it crack. "Right. Both ways. Fine."

* * *

There was a thing gutter-mages did not talk about, because talking about it made it real: the escape-burn. Every mage who worked the undercity kept one working folded inside their own body, sewn into the meat of them the way smugglers sewed gems into their thighs — a single spell, held ready, that could be torn open in desperation. Most kept a veil. Some kept a blast. Wren had kept, for eleven years, the one thing he swore he would never spend: the *Wayhome*, a working of his own invention, half hedge-craft and half heresy, that unraveled the space behind him into a corridor of unlit nowhere — a passage through the city's blind nerves that no ward could follow and no tracker could read. It was his masterpiece. It was his insurance. It was the reason he had survived a decade the Order should have eaten him alive.

It was also, he had realized sometime in the last mile, the only thing in the world that could do what needed doing now.

Because here was the arithmetic nobody else in the cut could see: the patrols weren't hunting *them*, not yet. They were sweeping. A sweep found what it found. But if the patrol caught sight of four fugitives and gave chase, the alarm would go up proper — bells, gates sealed, the whole marble machine of Vhal Corum grinding awake — and then nothing short of a miracle would get Felix and Petra to the ash-gates before dawn.

Unless the chase went somewhere that led nowhere.

Unless the prey vanished down a road that didn't exist.

"Listen fast," Wren said, pulling Felix and Petra both into the alcove behind the old tanning vat, where the dark was thickest. "When I move, you count to sixty and follow my voice. Don't follow my light — I won't have any. Follow my voice, and keep your hand on the wall, left side. There's a channel behind the third arch that runs up to the ash-gate sump. Odile's map says it comes out under the leper wall."

"Odile's map—" Felix started.

"—is forty years old and half of it's lies, yes, but the half that isn't will save your life tonight." He was already unbuckling his satchel, pressing it into Felix's arms: the chalk, the salt, the forged pilgrim papers, the little tin of salve. Everything he owned except the working in his chest. "I'm going to give the patrols something to chase. Me. Loud, visible, running the wrong way — down toward the Sump Road, away from your route. They'll commit. They always commit; captains want captures, not reports."

"And you?" Felix's eyes had gone wide in the dark. "Wren. How do you get *out*?"

"That's the beautiful part." He grinned, and hated how much the grin cost him, and grinned anyway, because some habits were load-bearing. "I don't run the wrong way at all. I run the wrong way just long enough for them to see me, and then I burn the Wayhome and drop into the nowhere-corridor, and I come up behind the leper wall like a bad memory. We all walk out the ash-gates together by the fifth bell, and I buy you breakfast with the coin I find in your satchel."

It was even mostly true. What he did not say — what he kept folded flat and quiet beneath the grin, the way he'd kept every true thing for eleven years — was that the Wayhome had never been burned twice in one night. That it had never been burned at full length. That a working lived in the body the way a fire lives in a beam, and tearing it open tore the beam, and there were mages in the gutters who had spent their masterworks and never cast so much as a light-spell again. Empty vessels. The gutter called them *hollowed*. He had seen one once, an old woman who sat by the fish-market selling fortunes she couldn't read anymore, staring at her own hands like they belonged to a stranger.

He had sworn he would never be her.

Petra was still watching him. Of course she was.

"You're lying," she said. Not accusing. Just noting, the way she noted rain.

"I'm simplifying," Wren said. "It's a professional skill."

"It's the same thing with different shoes on." Her small hand found his sleeve. "Whatever you're not saying. Say it, or don't go."

For one obscene moment, Wren almost laughed — this child, this impossible child sent up out of the earth with a dead language in her mouth, holding him to a standard of honesty that the entire Order of the Ashen Hand couldn't enforce. Then the laugh died, because the torchlight swung wide at the mouth of the cut, and a voice called out — bored, hard, official:

"Who walks Tanner's Cut after curfew? Step forward and be seen!"

No more time. There was never any more time; that was the joke the whole world ran on.

Wren gripped Petra's shoulder once, hard, memorizing it. He leaned close to Felix and pressed something into their hand — his lucky piece, a bent copper token stamped with a fish, worthless and irreplaceable.

"Sump Road," he whispered. "Sixty count. My voice. Left wall. If I'm not at the leper wall by dawn—" he made himself smile, made it reach his eyes, made it the best lie of his life, "—then eat breakfast without me and tell the Spire its messenger travels with excellent company."

Then he stepped out of the alcove, into the torchlight, and shouted at the top of his lungs, in the cracked street-yell of a cornered thief:

"Catch me then, you marble bastards!"

And ran.

* * *

They took the bait the way he'd known they would — hungry, immediate, boots hammering, the captain's voice cracking into the pursuit-whistle whose three rising notes meant *prey sighted, commit, commit, commit*. Wren ran the Sump Road with his heart slamming and his lungs full of brine-smell and glory, letting them see him, letting them want him, counting in his head. Forty. Fifty. Sixty.

Behind him — faint, fading, *following the voice, following the left wall* — he heard Felix's footsteps turn away, heard the small quick pad of Petra's, and something in his chest unclenched that had been clenched for eleven years.

*Go on,* he thought. *Go on, you brilliant stubborn people.*

Seventy. Eighty. The patrol was close enough now that he could hear individual breathing, close enough that a crossbow bolt spanged off the brick a hand's width from his ear and filled the air with white dust. Time.

Wren reached into his chest, into the place where the Wayhome lay coiled like a sleeping eel, and — for the first time in eleven years, deliberately, with both hands, the way you open a vein — he tore it open.

The world behind him unfolded.

It did not look like magic. It looked like the city forgetting itself: brick becoming suggestion, suggestion becoming dark, dark becoming a corridor of pure *unlit* that smelled of deep water and older stone, running away beneath Vhal Corum like the underside of a thought. He threw himself into it. The working roared through him, glorious and enormous, spending itself in a flood — and he fed it everything, more than it needed, more than was wise, because the corridor had to be long enough to lose them utterly, long enough that no tracker, no hound, no scrying-ward in the Citadel's arsenal could find the seam where he'd gone in.

He heard, distantly, from the world above: shouts. Confusion. The pursuit-whistle stuttering, dying. Men standing in a dead-end cut staring at walls that had never had a door in them and never would again.

Then the working finished, and the silence after it was the loudest thing Wren had ever heard.

* * *

He came up out of the nowhere-corridor behind the leper wall in a sprawl of mud and triumph, laughing, actually laughing, alone in the dark with the ash-gates two streets away and dawn still an hour off—

—and tried to stand, and could not.

The pain arrived late, the way pain does after great noise, walking in politely through the wreckage. It began in his chest and spread outward along paths he recognized with sickening clarity: the channels, the meridians, the secret roads his magic traveled. Except the roads were — empty. Not blocked. *Empty.* He reached for the smallest working he knew, the little warmth-charm he'd cast ten thousand times, the magical equivalent of scratching his nose.

Nothing.

He reached again. Nothing — not silence, worse than silence, a *shape* of nothing, a hollow where something had always been. He lay in the mud behind the leper wall with his fists pressed against the stones and shook, and understood, with the terrible completeness of a man reading his own death warrant, exactly what he had done. He had fed the Wayhome everything. Every reserve, every fold, every hidden seam of power he had hoarded through eleven years of survival. He had bought four strangers a head start with the only treasure he had ever owned.

He was hollowed. He was the old woman by the fish-market. He was a man with a stranger's hands.

"No," he said aloud, to no one, to the wall, to the absent gods of the undercity. "No, no, no — I did the *right thing*, you absolute bastards, doesn't the story know how this goes—"

But stories, he was learning — watching Felix grieve Maren, watching Petra carry a language that spent her a little more each time she spoke it — stories had never once pretended the right thing was free.

He got up. It took three tries. He got up because the ash-gates were two streets away and Felix was expecting a voice at the leper wall, and because a man who finally chose the right thing did not get to lie down in the mud five minutes afterward just because the right thing had teeth. He walked. He leaned on the wall. Somewhere in the middle of the second street his legs stopped entirely and he crawled a little, dignity having left the negotiation some miles back, and then the wall ended and there was the gate-sump, and there — gray in the pre-dawn, mud-caked, terrified, *alive* — were Felix and Petra, hauling themselves up out of the channel.

"Wren!" Felix scrambled toward him, caught him as his knees quit for good. "Gods — you're bleeding, what happened, where's your—"

"Spent it," Wren said. His voice sounded far away to himself. He was grinning again; he could feel it, ghastly and genuine at once. "All of it. Every drop. Best burn of my life, honestly, they'll be writing confused reports about that corridor for a decade." He fumbled at Felix's sleeve. "Did it work? Tell me it worked. Tell me I didn't give the best trick I ever built to a locked gate."

Felix's face was doing the complicated thing again, but underneath the complication was an answer, plain as bread. "We're through the sump. The gate-wardens haven't been paid to look at anyone leaving before sixth bell. We're outside the walls, Wren. We're out."

"Out," Wren repeated. The word landed strangely. Eleven years inside those walls, eleven years of smaller rooms, and now the rooms were all open and he was too empty to enjoy the view.

Petra knelt in front of him. She took his face in both hands, the way you'd hold something breakable, and studied him with those old, old eyes — and then she did something he would carry however long he carried anything. She hummed. Two notes of the First Tongue, soft, wordless, a sound like the bottom of a well remembering sunlight. It wasn't magic; he was beyond feeling magic, probably forever. But it was *kindness*, structured and deliberate, offered in the oldest language there was, and it went into the hollow places and did not fill them but made them bearable.

"You chose the door," she said. "Both ways. You faced outward." A pause, and then, gravely, formally, as though conferring a title: "The Spire remembers everyone who ever gave it something freely. Now it will remember you."

"Yeah, well." His eyes were stinging; he blamed the sump-water. "Tell it I expect interest."

Felix got his arm over their shoulder and hauled him upright, and the three of them stood swaying together behind the leper wall while the sky went from black to iron to the first thin smear of rose over the eastern mountains — mountains now, real ones, the Spire's direction, the road ahead.

"We need to move before the bell," Felix said. "Can you walk?"

"I can walk," Wren said. "I can't cast, I can't fight, I can't so much as light a candle, and I am going to be *insufferable* about needing help, fair warning." He tested his legs. They held, mostly. "But I know the mountain roads better than any living soul, and apparently I've decided to be the sort of man who finishes what he starts. So."

He looked east, at the faint distant gleam where the glass crater caught the coming sun, and felt the hollow in his chest shift in the wind like an open door.

"One condition," he added. "When we get there — when you talk to that great lonely thing in the rock — somebody asks it, very politely, whether it keeps *everything* it's given." He managed the grin one more time, and this time it didn't cost him a thing. "Because I want a refund."

Petra's hand found his. Small, grimy, certain.

"It heard you," she said. "It's already listening."

And far to the east, so faint it might have been wind, might have been blood in his ears, might have been hope wearing a rumor's clothes — Wren heard the Spire hum.
