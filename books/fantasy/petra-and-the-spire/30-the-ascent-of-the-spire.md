## 30. The Ascent of the Spire

The salt flats ended the way all things end in this country: abruptly, and with a scar.

Felix felt it before they saw it—a change in the ground beneath their boots, a faint tremor that was not an earthquake but a pulse, slow and enormous, like the heartbeat of something buried too deep to wake fully. The cracked white crust gave way to gray ash, then to glass. Not the glass of craftsmen or of lightning-struck sand, but vast sheets of it, fused and rippled, stretching to the horizon in colors that had no business existing: bruise-purple, old-blood red, a green so pale it seemed remembered rather than seen.

"The battlefields," Maren had called them, back when there had been three of them and enough breath for explanations. She had walked this way once, long ago, with twelve companions and a purpose she still would not fully name. Now her footsteps dragged behind Felix like a stone tied to an ankle, and every mile closer to the mountains, she grew quieter, grayer, as though the air itself were drawing something out of her through the skin.

She had stopped speaking entirely two days ago. She walked. That was all. Sometimes Felix looked back and marveled that she was upright at all.

And ahead—Gods help them all—ahead rose the Spire.

* * *

Felix had imagined it a hundred times since childhood, from stolen pages and Maren's rare, reluctant silences. They had pictured a tower, because the word *spire* demanded one: stone and buttress, banners, windows. Something built.

It was nothing built.

It rose from the center of the crater like a thorn from a wound—a single column of what might have been black glass or compressed night, wider at its base than a village, narrowing as it climbed until its tip was lost not in cloud but in a distortion, a shimmer, as though the sky itself bent politely around it. It did not reflect the light. It *held* it, drinking the sun into its depths and giving back only a dim, internal glow, the way coals hold fire after the flames have gone.

And it hummed.

Felix had heard the hum first as a feeling, a vibration in the teeth, in the breastbone, in the fluid of the eyes. Then, over the last day, it had become audible—an actual sound, low and continuous, a note too deep to be sung by any throat and yet unmistakably a note, sustained without wavering, as if the world itself had drawn one long breath at the beginning of time and never let it out.

Petra heard it differently. Felix knew this because Petra had stopped sleeping.

* * *

They made camp that last night in the lee of a ridge of slag, where the glass had shattered into shards the size of shields and piled against each other like the bones of some colossal beast. There was no wood to burn; there had been no wood here for centuries, perhaps. Felix built a fire anyway out of habit and desperation, using the dried dung they'd gathered on the flats' edge, and the flame burned strange—too tall, too still, leaning toward the Spire as if pulled by a thread.

Maren slept, if it could be called sleep. She lay curled on a bedroll with her cloak pulled to her chin, and her breathing came slow and shallow, and when Felix pressed two fingers to her wrist they found a pulse that stuttered like a candle in wind. Her skin had gone translucent at the temples, at the hollow of her throat. Felix could see, faintly, the blue map of veins beneath—and threaded through the veins, a glimmer. A faint gold light moving with her blood.

The seal was calling back its investment. Odile's ledger had said as much, in its dry, terrible arithmetic: *the border decays; the borrowed returns.* Thirteen lives woven into a working, and now, as the weave unraveled, the working was gathering up its threads. Maren was being reeled home like a fish on a line, and there was nothing in any book Felix had ever stolen that could cut the line.

"Stop looking at me like that."

Felix started. Maren's eyes were open, half-lidded, fixed on them across the dying fire.

"I wasn't—"

"You were. You've been wearing that face since the salt flats. Pity looks bad on you, child. You never learned to arrange it properly." Her voice was thin but the dryness in it was intact, and Felix clung to that dryness like a handhold on a cliff.

"I'm not pitying you," Felix said. "I'm furious at you. There's a difference."

"There is." Maren almost smiled. "Fury implies I might still be saved. Go on, then. Be furious. It suits you better."

But before Felix could answer—before they could say any of the ten thousand things crowding their throat, *you should have told me, you should have let me share it, I was a coward and you let me be one*—a sound interrupted them.

A sound from Petra.

She was sitting at the edge of the camp, knees drawn up, facing the Spire. And she was singing.

Not the humming Felix had grown used to on the road—that absent melody, half First Tongue, half a child's nonsense, the sound of her thinking. This was deliberate. This was structured. The phrases rose and fell in patterns Felix had begun, over these long weeks, to recognize: call and answer, question and question, a conversation conducted entirely alone. And as she sang, she *glowed.*

That was the only word. It had been happening for days, growing worse with every mile, and Felix had run out of ways to pretend otherwise. Petra's skin held light now the way paper holds a candle behind it. Her veins showed gold, the same gold that moved in Maren's blood, but where Maren's flickered and guttered, Petra's shone steady, brightening with each phrase of the song, until the girl seemed less like a child sitting in firelight and more like a lantern someone had forgotten to cover.

And she was fading. That was the horror of it, the thing Felix could not make anyone else understand because there was no one else awake to understand it. Brighter and fainter, both at once. As though she were burning away even as she blazed—as though the Spire's call were pulling her apart thread by thread, unraveling her into pure signal, pure song, until there would be nothing left of the mud-caked child who had once stood dripping on a doorstep demanding bread.

Felix crossed the camp and sat down beside her. The glass crunched. Petra did not stop singing, but she leaned, very slightly, until her shoulder rested against Felix's arm. The contact was cold. When had she become cold?

"Petra." Felix kept their voice low, gentle, the voice they used for frightened goats and frightened self. "You can stop now. It will still be there tomorrow."

"I know," she said, between phrases. Her voice, when she spoke the modern tongue, sounded distant, as if arriving from far upstream. "It's not asking me to sing. I'm asking it to wait."

Felix went very still. "Wait for what?"

"For you." She turned her head and looked at Felix, and her eyes were wrong—not blind, not empty, but *full*, lit from within, pupils gone wide and dark as wells. "It keeps asking who is coming. It remembers your name already. You gave it at the gate, remember? Names are doors. You opened yourself to it, and now it wants to know why you're walking so slowly."

"I'm walking slowly," Felix said, and their voice cracked on it, "because I'm afraid."

"I know." Petra said it without judgment, the way she said everything—the way she said *the sky is blue* and *the goat is dead* and *I was made from a loneliness too large for one voice*. "It says fear is a kind of listening. It says most people never listen to anything, not even themselves. It likes that about you."

"It talks about me?"

"It talks about everyone. No one answers." She resumed her song then, softly, and Felix sat beside her in the strange light and understood, with a clarity that felt like a knife going in, that they were losing her. Mile by mile, phrase by phrase. The Spire was not dragging her; she was *going*, willingly, the way water goes downhill, the way a word goes to the mouth that needs to speak it. And every step Felix took toward the thing they had come to save, they also took toward the moment they would have to let her go.

Love against inevitability. They had thought, once, that inevitability meant the Order, or Serath, or the seal. They had been wrong. Inevitability was this: a child made of someone else's loneliness, walking home to the wound that made her.

Felix put their arm around her cold shoulders and stayed there until the fire died and the stars wheeled and the hum filled the whole world like a second sky.

* * *

Dawn came gray and sourceless, the sun a pale smear behind the Spire's distortion. They broke camp without speaking. Maren needed Felix's shoulder to walk; she weighed almost nothing now, which was worse than if she'd weighed too much. Felix had carried goats, sacks of grain, Odile's entire stock of contraband valerian. They had never carried anything so light or so heavy as a dying woman who refused to be carried properly.

"You could stay," Felix said, for the hundredth time. "We could make a shelter here. I could go on alone, speak to it, come back—"

"And leave you alone with it?" Maren's laugh was a dry rustle. "Child, I sealed that thing once. If anyone is going to face what my work has cost, it should be me. Besides—" she paused to breathe, and the pause stretched long enough that Felix's heart clenched— "besides, I want to see it. One more time. Before it sees me."

They walked. The glass fields gave way to a slope of fine black sand, then to ridges of slag that rose like frozen waves, and always the hum grew louder, richer, until Felix could hear layers within it—overtones, harmonics, and beneath them all, faintly, something that was almost a voice. Almost words. Their neglected training stirred in their hands, that painful intoxication they'd felt since crossing the valley's border, magic waking in muscles long atrophied, and they kept it leashed with effort, because here, this close, magic felt less like a tool and more like a current in floodwater.

By midday they reached the rim of the inner crater, and stopped.

The battlefield spread below them: a plain of glass and bone-white sand perhaps two miles wide, littered with the remains of the war no history book had survived to describe. Weapons fused into the ground. Banners reduced to threads of color embedded in the glass like insects in amber. And skeletons—some human, some very much not—arranged in radiating lines, all pointing away from the Spire, as if the force that killed them had come outward from its base in a single annihilating breath.

At the center, the Spire stood. And around its base, Felix saw now, the glass had melted and reformed into smooth concentric rings, like ripples frozen mid-spread, and the rings *glowed*, faintly, in time with the hum. Pulse. Pulse. Pulse.

"Oh," Maren whispered. "Oh, it's *worse* than I remembered." She said it with something like awe. "It used to sleep quietly. It used to dream. Now it's—" she swallowed— "now it's counting the days."

"Maren." Felix gripped her arm. "What happens when we reach it? Tell me plainly. No more bargains, no more omissions. Plainly."

She looked at Felix for a long moment. Behind her, Petra stood at the crater's rim with the wind pulling at her hair, and her outline shimmered like heat-haze, and neither of them mentioned it because mentioning it would make it real.

"Plainly," Maren said at last. "Then hear this. The seal was never a wall. It was a promise—a bargain, spoken in thirteen voices, that the Spire would wait and the world would forget, each holding up its half. Serath has spent years stealing the voices out of that bargain, one by one, feeding himself on them. What remains is not enough to keep the promise alive and not enough to let it die cleanly. So the Spire hangs between, awake and unkept, and everything it touches begins to fray." She gestured with a trembling hand—at the glass, at the bones, at Petra's shining, dissolving silhouette. "When we arrive, one of three things will happen. Either the bargain is renewed—which costs thirteen lives, and we are three. Or the bargain is broken—which floods the world with raw magic and kills everything that cannot bear it. Or—" her voice caught, and Felix realized, with a lurch, that Maren Halloran was afraid, truly afraid, perhaps for the first time in her long and terrible life— "or someone finds a fourth thing. A question no one has ever thought to ask it."

"Petra thinks there's a fourth thing," Felix said quietly. "She said it isn't asleep. It's waiting to be asked properly."

Maren closed her eyes. "Then may every god we ever failed forgive us both," she said, "and let us hope your child asks better questions than I did."

* * *

They descended into the crater as the sun began to fail.

The sand underfoot was warm—not sun-warm, but warm from within, blood-warm, and it shifted strangely, seeming to flow uphill toward the Spire in quantities too small to see. The hum was a physical pressure now, filling the skull, and Felix found themselves matching their breathing to it without meaning to, inhale and exhale timed to that vast patient pulse.

Halfway across the plain, Petra stopped walking.

She stood in the trackless sand with her arms slightly away from her body, palms up, and the light in her had grown so bright that she cast shadows—two of them, three, none of them quite matching her shape. When she spoke, her voice came doubled, a child's voice and beneath it something vast and resonant, the way a bell contains the strike.

"It knows we're here," she said—or the two of them said, together. "It has been waiting so long. It wants—" and here the doubling wavered, fractured, and for a moment it was only Petra again, small and scared and blazing, looking back at Felix with her own eyes— "Felix. Felix, it's so *lonely*. I didn't know. I'm part of it and I didn't know how lonely—"

"I know," Felix said, and crossed the distance between them, and took her hands, and her hands were light now more than flesh, warmth without weight. "Then we'll do what we came to do. We'll listen. Both of us. All three of us." They looked past her to Maren, who had sunk to her knees in the sand, gold light streaming visibly now through the seams of her, thread by thread being drawn home. "All of us. Whatever it costs."

"That's the trouble with promises," Maren murmured from the ground, smiling up at the Spire with tears cutting tracks through the dust on her face. "It keeps them literally."

Ahead of them, at the base of the Spire, the glass parted.

Not broke—parted, smoothly, silently, folding open like a flower the size of a cathedral gate, revealing a passage that glowed from within with a light like dawn behind fog. And rising out of that opening, drifting upward through the humming air, came a sound that stopped Felix's heart and restarted it sideways:

Voices. Thirteen voices, layered and ancient and worn thin as burial cloth, speaking in unison a single word in the First Tongue—a word Felix had learned from Petra's midnight lessons, a word whose meaning she had translated with a child's terrifying simplicity.

*Come.*

Petra's fingers tightened around Felix's. Her light flared—and guttered—and flared again, and for one horrible instant she was nearly transparent, nearly gone, a girl-shaped brightness holding on by will alone.

"Felix," she whispered, in her own voice, only her own voice, small and mortal and afraid. "Hold on to me. Whatever happens in there. Don't let go until I remember which one I am."

The gate waited. The hum swelled. Somewhere behind them, far across the broken lands, Felix could almost feel Serath coming, an army's worth of hunger closing like a fist.

Felix stepped forward, hand locked in Petra's fading light, and walked into the Spire.
