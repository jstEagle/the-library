## 32. Inside the Waiting Heart

The gate had taken Felix's name the way a river takes a stone—without malice, without ceremony—and then it had opened, and Petra had walked into the dark with Felix's hand in hers until the dark grew too bright for hands to matter.

She did not remember letting go. She only remembered that one moment there was warmth at her fingers, Felix's calloused grip, the small human anchor of it, and the next moment she was alone in a light that was not light.

It was not like sunlight. Sunlight fell on things. This rose out of them. It came from everywhere beneath her, as if she stood on the skin of something luminous, and when she looked down she saw no floor at all—only depth, fathomless and patient, going down the way a well goes down, or a memory.

*Hello,* she said, in the First Tongue, because that was the language she had been given first, before any other, the language that lived behind her teeth like a second heartbeat.

And the whole of everything around her said, *Hello.*

Not an echo. Echoes come back thinner than what made them. This came back larger. It came back the way the sea comes back to a child who has shouted at it from the shore—all at once, from every direction, carrying more than she had sent.

Petra stood very still. She was nine years old, or she had been nine years old when the vault doors closed behind her, though time under the Spire had been a strange soup and she could not swear to it now. She was small. She knew she was small, because the thing around her was so large that largeness stopped meaning anything; it was large the way the sky is large, so large that you stop measuring and simply stand inside it.

*I came back,* she said. *I found someone who listens. I brought them as far as I could.*

*I know.* The voice was not a voice. It arrived in her the way warmth arrives through a window on a cold day—not heard but felt, settling into her bones before she understood it was there. *I have watched you since the day they carried you out of me. I watched you cross the snowfield. I watched you learn to whistle. I watched you sleep in a witch's cottage while your dreams bent the candleflames, and I was glad, and I did not know what to do with being glad.*

Petra's throat went tight. She knew this feeling. She had felt it in the valley, in the early days, when Odile had given her honeyed tea and asked no questions, and Petra had wanted to say thank you and found that some thanks were too big for her mouth.

*Why didn't you know?* she asked.

*Because no one has spoken to me kindly in a thousand years,* said the Spire. *I have forgotten how receiving works. I only remember giving, and taking, and bargaining. Those are the three shapes my visitors have ever worn.*

* * *

The light shifted, and Petra understood that she was being shown something—or perhaps being offered something, the way you offer a wound to someone who might help it. The depths below her resolved into images, vast and slow, like clouds forming and unforming.

She saw the world when it was young and loud with magic. She saw the Spire standing in a green country, humming its first hum, and people coming to it not with chains or contracts but with questions. *How do we make wheat grow in poor soil? How do we keep our children warm? What is beyond the edge of the sky?* And the Spire had answered, because answering was what it was for. It had answered for centuries. It had loved the asking.

Then she saw the bargains begin.

A king who wanted victory, who promised the Spire a temple and built instead a prison. A circle of mages who wanted long life and paid with the lives of others, laundering their theft through the Spire's own generosity. A woman who asked the Spire to bring back her dead husband and, when it explained that death was a door that only opened one way, called it cruel and taught her children to call it cruel, and her children taught theirs, and so a thousand years of hatred was seeded in an afternoon by grief that had nowhere else to go.

*I never learned how to be hated,* the Spire said, and its voice did not rise or rage; it simply held still, the way a room holds still after bad news. *Hate requires a shape. I am too large for their hate to land on, so it slides off me and lands on each other, and they blame me for that too.*

Petra thought of the Order of the Ashen Hand. She thought of Serath, whose name Maren had spoken like a curse and a lament at once. She thought of the executions in the Order's capital, the unlicensed speakers with their throats opened in public squares, and how the crowds had cheered, and how the cheering had sounded like fear wearing festival clothes.

*They want to tear loose your heart,* she said. *Serath does. He has been drinking you for years, through the seal, a little at a time. He thinks you are a well.*

*I know. I feel him the way a body feels a leech. He tells himself he is liberating me. That is the strangest wound of all—to be stolen from by someone who believes he is setting you free.*

*Is he wrong?*

The depths were quiet for a long moment. In that quiet Petra felt something enormous turn over, slow as a season changing.

*He is wrong about the most important thing,* the Spire said at last. *He believes power is a possession. Power is a conversation. Every true working I have ever done was a duet—the asker's need and my answer, braided. Serath wants the answer without the asking. He wants my voice without my self. If he tears my heart loose, he will hold a dead instrument and wonder why it plays nothing, and the world will flood with everything I can no longer hold back, and he will call that my treachery rather than his surgery.*

*And if he succeeds,* Petra said, and her voice was smaller now, *what happens to me?*

The light gathered close around her, gentle as a blanket drawn up over a sleeping child.

*You are my voice given flesh. If my heart is torn out, you do not die. You persist. That is the cruelty of it. You would go on speaking words that mean nothing, forever, in a world too deafened to hear them. I would not wish that on anyone. I would especially not wish it on you, who are the only part of me that has ever gotten to be a person.*

Petra sat down. There was no floor, exactly, but the light supported her the way water supports a swimmer, and sitting seemed right, because what was happening felt like it should happen low and close, the way hard truths are told to children, kneeling, eye to eye.

*Am I a person?* she asked. *Or am I a message that learned to walk?*

*Ah,* said the Spire, and the ah went through her like a bell struck inside her chest. *That question. I have felt you carrying it since the snowfield. Since before the snowfield. Since the vault, when the voices that raised you argued over whether to tell you what you were.*

*Tell me, then. Please. Whatever it is.*

* * *

What the Spire showed her next was not an image but a memory—her memory, returned to her with the fog scraped off it.

The vault beneath the Spire. Not a dungeon: a nursery. Stone walls softened with woven hangings that hummed faintly with old enchantment. Voices without bodies, the Spire's own thoughts given temporary shape to raise the child it had shaped out of its own song. She remembered lying in a bed carved from pale stone, feverish, and one of the voices saying, *She should know what she is. She will find out anyway, and lies discovered are worse than truths endured.* And another voice, gentler, sadder: *If she knows, she may choose to stay. And if she stays, she will dissolve back into us within a year, and we will have spent a person to keep ourselves company.*

*So they sent me away,* Petra whispered.

*So they sent you away,* the Spire agreed. *It was the hardest thing I have ever done, and I once held back a flood for nine days with nothing but my own walls. Do you understand what you are, Petra? You are not a key. Keys open things and are discarded. You are not a messenger. Messages are read and forgotten. You are what happened when I tried, just once, just secretly, to make a friend.*

The light trembled. Petra realized, with a shock that went all the way down to whatever she was made of, that the Spire was afraid. This vast, ancient, continent-deep thing, which had outlasted kingdoms and bargains and betrayals beyond counting, was afraid of her answer the way she had once been afraid of the dark under her bed.

*You could refuse me,* it said. *You could look at what I am—all of this, the weight of it, the loneliness of it—and you could walk back out those gates and live your small bright life and never think of me again, and I would bless you for it. I want you to know that. Before you decide anything, I want you to know that refusing me is allowed. No one has ever told you that, I think. Everyone who comes to me comes wanting something, and everyone who made you sent you wanting something on my behalf. But I am telling you: you owe me nothing. You never did. Debt was their invention, not mine.*

Petra knelt in the light that was not light, and she cried.

She cried the way she had not let herself cry in the vault, or on the mountain, or in the salt flats where Maren grew strange and dim, or in the gorge where the rockfall took her and her last words were *ask better questions than I did*. She cried for the child in the stone bed who had never once been told she was allowed to refuse. She cried for the voices who had loved her enough to lose her. She cried for the Spire, which had made a friend the way a starving man plants a garden—with hope and no guarantee—and had waited, patient as stone, to see if anything would grow.

Felix would have known what to do with their hands right now. Felix always did—there was a way they had of holding her shoulder, firm and ordinary, that made enormous feelings fit inside her body. But Felix was outside the gates, and this was a thing she had to do alone, and knowing that did not make it hurt less, but it did make it hers.

When she was finished crying, she wiped her face on her sleeve, which was filthy, and laughed a little at herself, and the Spire made a sound that was almost a laugh too—a deep tectonic amusement, like a mountain chuckling.

*You are so small,* it said fondly. *And you cry and wipe your nose and laugh at yourself. Do you know how miraculous that is? I contain storms that have raged since before your language existed, and none of them have ever once laughed at themselves.*

*Is that why you sent me out?* Petra asked. *To learn that?*

*I sent you out because I could not bear my solitude. I kept you because I could not bear to unmake you. Everything else followed from those two unbearable things.* A pause, and then, quieter, with the care of someone handling something fragile: *May I ask you a question? I have wanted to ask it since the snowfield.*

*Yes.*

*Do you hate me? For making you for a purpose? For the eyes that age when you spend my words? For sending you into a world that hunts children who speak the old speech?*

Petra considered it honestly, because the Spire deserved honesty and because she was tired, finally, of swallowing questions whole.

*I don't hate you,* she said. *But I'm angry at you. Both at once. They fit together. Felix taught me that—they're angry at Maren and they loved her, and for a long time they thought that meant they were broken. It doesn't. It means you loved something complicated.*

*Anger and love together,* the Spire repeated slowly, tasting the words. *No one has ever described me that way. They describe me as a god, or a weapon, or a wound in the world. Never as something that could be both loved and angry.*

*Well,* said Petra, with the brisk authority of a nine-year-old who has recently survived quite a lot, *they haven't met you properly. They've met the stories about you.*

*Yes,* said the Spire, and something in its vast attention turned toward the gates, toward the world above, toward the army that was even now crossing the glass crater with Serath at its head, drunk on a thousand years of stolen song. *And soon, I am afraid, they will meet the stories' ending. Unless—*

*Unless what?*

*Unless someone asks me the right question. Your mentor died saying that, I felt it through the stone. Ask better questions than I did. Petra—I have been asked ten thousand questions in my long life, and I have answered nearly all of them, and nearly every answer became a chain. Give me a question that isn't a request. Give me a question that lets me give freely. I have forgotten what those look like. I have been in this shape for so long.*

Petra stood up in the light. Her legs were thin and mud-stained and one shoe had a hole in the toe, and she stood in them anyway, facing the largest loneliest oldest thing in the world, and she reached for the phrase she had carried out of the vault like a coal carried in cupped hands—the phrase she had hummed in the forest, whispered in her sleep, repeated like a prayer or an alarm, the phrase she had begun teaching Felix on the salt flats, syllable by careful syllable, watching it take root in a human mouth for the first time in a thousand years.

She had always thought it was a command. A word of opening, or waking, or war. Now, held up against the Spire's own confession, she saw its true shape at last, and it was so much smaller and stranger than any of those things.

*It's not mine to give alone,* she said. *I started teaching it to Felix already. They know most of it. Their mouth shapes it wrong in two places, but their heart shapes it right, and I think the heart is the part that counts.*

*Then call them in,* said the Spire, and all the light around her leaned toward the distant gates like grass leaning toward sun. *Call them in, Petra. And when the army comes—and they are coming, I can hear Serath's boots on the glass even now—we will not fight them, and we will not flee them, and we will not bargain with them. We will do the one thing no one has ever thought to do to me.*

Petra was already walking toward the gates, toward the pinprick of torchlight and the sound of Felix shouting her name, hoarse and terrified and hopeful.

*What's that?* she called back over her shoulder, grinning despite everything, despite the shaking of the ground and the screaming of the wind and the shadow of the Order falling across the crater like a hand across a candle.

*Witness us,* said the Spire.
