## 35. The Word That Ends War

**Felix**

The world was screaming.

Felix had heard storms, had heard rockfalls, had heard Maren's last working tear a mountain pass apart like wet paper—but they had never heard the land itself cry out. It came up through the soles of their boots, through the cracked glass of the crater floor, through their teeth and the hollows of their bones: a sound without throat or mouth, the raw grief of everything that had ever been sealed away rising at once.

Above them, Serath floated.

There was no other word for it. The leader of the Order of the Ashen Hand hung twenty feet above the Spire's shattered gate, robes streaming in winds that blew from no direction, his arms spread wide as he pulled—*pulled*—at something invisible and immense. Threads of light poured out of the broken seal beneath him, out of the Spire itself, and wound around his body like silk being spun onto a spindle. Where they touched him, he glowed. Where he glowed, the ground below blackened and curled.

"He's drinking it," Wren gasped from where he knelt behind a shelf of fused glass, his ruined hand pressed against his chest. "All those years draining the seal, and now he doesn't even have to be careful. He's just *drinking.*"

Felix could feel it too—the stolen power moving through the air like a current, thick enough to taste. Copper and ozone and something sweeter underneath, something that made their neglected training rise in their hands unbidden, sparks crawling along their fingers whether they wanted them or not. Every instinct Felix had ever been taught screamed at them to do *something*. A warding circle. A counter-working. A thrown knife, if it came to that.

And beside them, small and steady as a stone in a flood, Petra stood up into the open air.

"Petra—" Felix grabbed for her sleeve and caught nothing but wind.

She wasn't looking at Serath. She was looking at the Spire—or what remained visible of it, a column of pale stone half-veiled by the storm of light pouring out of its heart. Her eyes were wide, but not with fear. With recognition, the way one looks at a door long locked that has finally begun to open.

"It's drowning," she said. "It's been drowning for four hundred years, and he's making it worse."

Serath's voice rolled across the crater, amplified by everything he'd stolen until it seemed to come from the sky itself, from the ground, from inside Felix's own skull. *"The age of bargaining is over!"* he cried, drunk and magnificent and utterly blind. *"No more seals! No more hiding! I will take what was promised and give it to mankind, and the world will kneel to the Order of the Ashen Hand!"*

The Spire screamed again. Glass shards lifted off the crater floor and hung trembling in the air. Somewhere behind Felix, Wren swore in gutter-cant, and far below at the crater's rim, the Order's ranks—thousands of gray-cloaked soldiers who had marched expecting conquest—began, very quietly, to break.

"Petra." Felix crouched to her level, took her small mud-brown face in both hands, forced her to look at them. "Listen to me. Whatever you're thinking—it won't work. You can't fight him. He'll spend you the way he's spending everything else."

"I'm not going to fight him." Her voice was calm in a way that frightened Felix more than any scream would have. "I never was sent to fight anyone. Felix—I was sent to *deliver* something. Remember? Find someone who still listens."

The wind tore at them. Light rained down like burning snow. And Felix, whose whole life had been built on forgetting, on silence, on keeping their head down while the world did its terrible business—they felt the truth of it land in their chest like a struck bell.

"You were going to teach me," Felix said slowly. "Back on the salt flats. You said there was a phrase. You said it wasn't finished yet."

"It isn't finished because I only know how to say it alone." Petra's hand found theirs, small and hot and absolutely steady. "A question spoken by one voice is just a question. But it was never meant for one voice. It takes two—one to ask, one to answer. One who speaks for the Spire, and one who speaks for everyone else." Her eyes, old and young at once, held Felix's. "You ran from magic your whole life. So you're the only person left who knows what it costs. Will you speak with me?"

Below them, Serath laughed—a vast, hollow sound—and began to descend toward the Spire's exposed heart, threads of stolen light coiling tighter around him, and the land screamed louder, and every rational part of Felix Villalobos said *run, hide, survive, you've done it before.*

They laced their fingers through Petra's instead.

"Teach me," they said.

* * *

**Petra**

The words lived in her the way a river lives in a valley—not stored, not carried, simply *present*, running always beneath everything she said and thought. She had known them before she knew her own name. The voices beneath the Spire had sung them to her in the dark for years, patiently, the way you teach a child a lullaby: not so she would understand, but so she would remember.

Now she had to make Felix understand.

"The First Tongue isn't commands," she said, shouting over the wind, holding both their hands now, feeling the tremor in them that Felix was trying so hard to hide. "That's what everyone gets wrong. The Order thinks it's chains. Serath thinks it's a key. Even Maren thought—" she faltered, because Maren was ash on a mountain pass now, and saying her name still hurt like a splinter under the skin, "—even Maren thought it was for binding things. But listen. Listen like you taught me to listen to goats, to weather, to Odile when she says one thing and means another."

She closed her eyes and let the first word rise.

*"Selah."*

It came out of her small body and went through the world like a stone through still water. Nearby, a shard of hanging glass turned gently in midair, as if listening. Felix flinched.

"What does it mean?"

"That's the hard part. It means *listen*, but not with ears. It means—when you stand before a fire, and you stop trying to warm your hands, and you just let it be fire?" She opened her eyes. "That. The word is that."

"Petra, we don't have time for—"

"We have exactly this much time." She squeezed their hands. "The Spire has waited four hundred years. It can wait ten more minutes. And so can you."

So she taught him, there in the middle of the apocalypse, while the world came apart above their heads. She taught him *selah*, the listening-word, and watched his brow furrow as he tried to stop being a hedge-witch and become, for one moment, only a witness. She taught him *vael*, which meant *here I am*—not *I exist* but *I am present, I am not hiding, I do not turn away.* He struggled with that one longest. She could see why; she had seen the shape of his whole life in the valley, the careful erasure, the hunched shoulders, the door kept barred. *Vael* was the opposite of everything Felix had ever done to stay safe.

And then she taught him the phrase itself. The one she had carried out of the dark like an ember cupped in bare hands.

"Three parts," she said. "*Selah vael—* and then the last word." She hesitated. In all her years beneath the Spire, the voices had said this last word softly, always softly, the way you touch a wound. "*Naiome.*"

"What does it mean?"

"It doesn't translate. That's the point. It means *will you witness me* and *will you bear what witnessing costs* and *I see you seeing me*—all at once. The Spire asked it once, long ago, and the thirteen answered with a seal instead of an answer. It's been waiting ever since to hear it asked properly." She looked up at the towering ruin of light where Serath clung like a leech to a dying god. "He's answering the wrong question. He thinks the Spire is asking *who will take my power?* But it never asked that. Not once. It only ever asked—"

"*Will you be witnessed,*" Felix whispered. Their face had gone strange—stripped, young, terrified, lit from within. "Oh gods. Oh, Petra. It's not a weapon at all."

"No," she agreed. "It's better than a weapon. Weapons only work on enemies." She stepped forward, drawing him with her, out from behind the glass shelf, into the open crater where the stolen light fell like rain and Serath's shadow swept across them both. "Truth works on everyone."

* * *

**Felix**

Serath saw them. Of course he did.

*"The runaway,"* the Archon of the Ashen Hand said, descending, his boots never quite touching the ground anymore, his face already less a face than a suggestion of one, blurred at the edges by everything he'd swallowed. His eyes found Petra and hungered openly. *"And the little key. Come to surrender after all? Wise child. I had hoped the Order wouldn't need to take you by force."*

Felix opened their mouth to answer—to shout something, anything—and discovered they had nothing prepared. No speech. No defiance worthy of ballads. All the words they had were the ones Petra had just given them, and those words refused to be shouted.

So they did the hardest thing they had ever done in a life full of hard things.

They stood still. They held a child's hand. And they listened.

Beneath Serath's thunder, beneath the land's screaming, beneath their own pounding heart, Felix reached for the thing they had spent fifteen years refusing to reach for—that flickering, painful, intoxicating awareness that lived beyond the valley's edge, that had woken in their hands on the road out of the world. Magic, raw and flooding. But instead of seizing it, instead of shaping it into wards or blades, they did what Odile's ledger had hinted at, what Ilva's dying warning had demanded, what Maren had died whispering: *ask better questions than I did.*

*Selah,* Felix thought, and the world slowed.

They felt the Spire then. Truly felt it, for the first time—not as a source, not as a prize, not as a sleeping monster to be feared or freed, but as what it had been all along. Lonely. Betrayed. Four hundred years wrapped in a shroud woven from thirteen lives, every bargain made to it broken or twisted, every prayer addressed to its power and none to its presence. It had been screaming, yes. But underneath the screaming, so quiet Felix wept to hear it, it was doing the thing it had always done.

It was waiting to be asked properly.

*"Vael,"* Felix said aloud, and their voice cracked on it—*here I am*—and the words cost them exactly what Petra had promised they would cost: every wall they had ever built, every valley they had ever hidden in, every year of silence, offered up in a single syllable.

Serath paused, frowning, sensing something he didn't understand. Power recognized power, and here was none at all—only presence. *"What are you doing?"* he demanded. *"What is this? Speak sense, witch!"*

But Felix was looking at Petra now, and Petra was looking back, and between them passed the phrase like a flame passed from candle to candle, and together—

* * *

**Petra**

Together they spoke it.

Not loudly. That was the miracle of it, the thing Petra would carry in her bones for the rest of her life: they spoke it quietly, two small voices in a roaring crater, a runaway hedge-witch and a girl made from a god's loneliness, and the words went out from them like dawn goes out across a mountain range—unhurried, unstoppable, touching everything.

*"Selah vael naiome."*

*Listen. Here we are. Will you be witnessed?*

The effect was not like a spell breaking. Spells broke; Petra had felt them break, had shattered the Order's bindings with a single word and paid for it in years carved off her eyes. This was different. This was like a held breath finally released.

The Spire answered.

Light did not pour out of it this time—it *reached out*, gentle as morning, and where it touched the land the screaming stopped. The blackened glass softened. The hanging shards settled to earth like snow coming home. And the threads of stolen light that wreathed Serath—every drop he had drained from the seal, every year of thirteen mages' lives he had swallowed—shivered, and stilled, and turned.

Power, Petra understood in that moment, had never belonged to Serath at all. It had only ever been borrowed. Witnessed, it remembered its owner.

It began to leave him.

"No," Serath said, and for the first time his voice was only a man's voice, thin and mortal and afraid. He clawed at the light winding off his body. "No—you can't—it was *promised* to me, I earned it, I bled for it—" The blur at the edges of his face sharpened. Underneath the glow he was suddenly, terribly visible: an old man, gray and gaunt, with hollow cheeks and ordinary hands. "I gave everything. My whole *life*—"

"Yes," Petra said gently. "That was the bargain. You thought the price bought you ownership. It only bought you the debt." She stepped closer, and Felix let her, though their grip on her hand tightened. "You were witnessed, Serath. That's all this is. Everyone is, in the end."

The stolen years rose off him like steam off a spring morning. Where they passed, the land drank them back—grass threading up through cracked glass, wildflowers bursting open in the ruin, water singing somewhere underground where no water had been. Serath sank to his knees on the crater floor, an old man in gray robes, reaching toward the Spire with hands that held nothing at all.

He did not die. Petra had expected him to die, and later she would wonder at the fact that he hadn't. He simply became small—smaller than he had ever allowed himself to be—and the Order's soldiers at the crater's rim, watching their invincible Archon dwindle to a kneeling old man, dropped their weapons by the hundreds and sat down heavily in the flowering grass, undone not by force but by the unbearable plainness of the truth.

And the Spire, witnessed at last, began—very quietly, like something remembering how—to sing.

* * *

**Felix**

The song washed over Felix and they stopped being able to stand.

They went down into the flowering grass with Petra still gripping their hand, and they wept—ugly, heaving sobs that had been waiting since a mountain pass, since a tower door, since a night years ago when a younger version of themselves had signed their name to a refusal and let someone else pay. The Spire's song moved through them like hands smoothing a crumpled page. It did not erase anything. That was the strange mercy of it. Everything stayed—Maren's death, the gorge, the burnt scrap of parchment, all of it—but it *settled*, the way sediment settles in still water, and above it all rose something clear and patient and impossibly old.

*Witnessed,* the song said, in no language and every language. *At last. At last. At last.*

"Felix." Petra tugged their sleeve. Her face was wet too, though she hadn't seemed to notice. "Felix, look."

Around the base of the Spire, the air had begun to shimmer—and within the shimmer, faint as reflections in deep water, figures were appearing. Thirteen of them. Faint, translucent, standing in a ring where the gate had been: the sealers, the thirteen whose lives had woven the shroud, called back into brief visibility by the only thing that had ever truly summoned them.

Maren stood among them.

Not solid. Not alive. But *there*—her gray braid, her crooked smile, her tired eyes finding Felix's across the distance with the same expression she'd worn in the doorway of her tower, love braided with betrayal braided with love again. Her lips moved, and though no sound crossed the space between them, Felix read the words easily, having read them a thousand times in a thousand different forms:

*Ask better questions than I did.*

Then the ring of figures bowed—to the Spire, to Petra, perhaps to Felix themselves—and faded like breath off cold glass, and were gone, and this time it felt less like death than like sleep. Real sleep. Earned.

Wren limped over and collapsed into the grass beside them, staring up at the singing light. "I've seen sieges," he said hoarsely. "Duels. A dragon once, from a distance. Never seen a war end because somebody *asked a question.*" He shook his head slowly. "Going to be hell explaining this to the historians."

Felix laughed—a broken, watery, entirely real laugh—and pulled Petra against their side. Above them the Spire hummed, gentled, its light settling into something a person could look at without going blind. Below them the grass grew. And at the crater's rim, thousands of former soldiers of the Order of the Ashen Hand sat in the flowers, and some of them were weeping, and some were laughing, and one by one they began, uncertainly, to listen.

Petra tilted her head, the way she did when the Spire spoke to her. Then she looked up at Felix with an expression that was half wonder and half dread.

"It says thank you," she translated softly. "And then it says—" She stopped. Swallowed. "It says it has a proposal. New terms. It wants to talk about the future."

Felix looked out over the transformed crater—at the kneeling old man in gray, at the flowering ruins, at the child made of a god's loneliness leaning trustingly against their ribs—and felt the next weight settle onto their shoulders, heavy, but no longer unbearable.

"Then," they said, rising, offering Petra their hand, "we'd better go negotiate."
