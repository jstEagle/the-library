## 36. A New Bargain

The silence after Serath's unmaking was not like other silences.

Felix had known the hush of a valley where magic died at the ridgeline—knew it the way one knows a scar, intimately, by touch. They had lived inside that silence for years, had built a life in it the way a hermit crab builds a shell from borrowed emptiness. But this was different. This was the silence of a held breath. The whole broken plain around the Spire's crater seemed to lean inward, every stone and shard of glass and scattered soldier waiting for something to happen next.

The Spire stood above them, humming.

Felix could feel it now through the soles of their boots, through their teeth, through the place behind the sternum where old grief lived. It was not the menacing thrum they had felt on the ascent, when each step toward the crater had made Petra grow brighter and fainter at once, as if she were being unpicked thread by thread. This hum had changed register. It was lower. Warmer. It sounded, absurdly, like relief.

*Will you be witnessed?*

The words still hung in the air between them and the ruin of Serath, though Serath himself was gone—unmade so thoroughly that there was not even ash to scatter, only a scorch-mark on the glass in the shape of a man who had reached for too much. The question they had spoken with Petra, the phrase she had carried out of the dark beneath the world, was dissolving slowly into the Spire's song, note by note, like sugar into warm water.

Petra swayed.

Felix caught her before she fell. She weighed almost nothing—or she weighed everything; it was hard to tell anymore. Since they had entered the gate of names, since Felix had spoken their own name freely given and watched the stone accept it, the girl had been flickering at the edges. Not fading exactly. *Loosening.* As though the shape of her had been tied off with knots, and those knots were being undone one by one, and no one—not Felix, not Maren's ghost, not the Spire itself—knew what would be left when the last knot slipped free.

"Petra." Their voice came out rougher than they intended. "Stay with me."

"I'm here." Her voice was strange too—layered, doubled, as if two throats spoke in imperfect unison. One voice was a child's, hoarse with exhaustion. The other was older than kingdoms, patient as geology. "We're both here. That's rather the point."

Around them, the remnants of the Order's army were fleeing down the crater's slopes in ragged streams. Some had dropped their weapons. Some wept openly, or laughed, or simply sat down in the glass and stared at the sky, which had begun—Felix noticed it only now—to change color. Not storm-gray. Not the bruised violet of wild magic flooding loose. Something gentler. A green-gold light was seeping up from the horizon all around them, as though dawn had decided, after long deliberation, to arrive from every direction at once.

Wren limped toward them across the glass, his burned hand cradled against his chest, his face gray with pain and something that might have been awe. Behind him, the land itself was stirring. Where the raw flood of magic had torn through during the Breaking, things were growing—impossibly, instantly, but gently: moss spreading over the scorched fields like a blanket drawn over a sleeping child, pale flowers opening in the cracks of the crater wall, a single tree unfurling from nothing where Serath's citadel banner had fallen.

"It's talking," Wren said. His voice cracked. "I can hear it. Everyone can hear it. Half the deserters back there are on their knees because they've never heard anything speak to them without wanting something first."

"That's not quite true," said the doubled voice against Felix's shoulder. Petra lifted her head. Her eyes were doing something Felix had no name for—the irises shifting like oil on water, bright and deep at once. "It wanted to be heard. Wanting isn't shameful. It just never knew how to ask without frightening everyone."

* * *

They made camp that night in the lee of the Spire itself, which should have been madness and instead felt like sheltering beneath a mountain's shoulder. The surviving soldiers—those who had not fled—kept a wary distance, and Wren, who had a deserter's genius for reading crowds, went among them with his ruined hand held up like a banner of honesty, talking quietly, turning fear into curiosity one conversation at a time. Felix did not ask how he did it. Some magics were not spelled.

Felix built a fire from driftwood that had no business existing in a crater of glass, and roasted the last of the hardtack, and tried very hard not to look at the empty bedroll where Maren's should have been. Grief was going to have its turn. Felix knew that. But grief would have to wait its turn behind necessity, the way it always had, the way it had waited through the whole long flight from the valley, through the salt flats, through the gorge where the rockfall had taken Maren mid-word, her last instruction hanging in the air like an unfinished sentence:

*Ask better questions than I did.*

So Felix asked one.

"Why me?" they said to the fire, and then, feeling foolish, amended it. "Why us? Why a runaway apprentice and a child and a gutter-mage with a burned hand? You had thirteen of the most powerful mages of an age. You had Serath, gods help you. Why did you send a fragment of yourself out into the world to find—" they gestured vaguely at themselves, at the mud-stained hem of their coat, at the whole unremarkable sum of their life— "*this?*"

The fire crackled. Somewhere downslope, Wren laughed at something, a real laugh, startled out of him.

And the Spire answered.

Not in Petra's doubled voice—that came later, when she woke. It answered the way it had answered at the gate of names: directly, wordlessly, pressing meaning into Felix's mind like a seal pressed into wax. And what it pressed there was not grandeur. It was loneliness. Thirteen centuries of it, compressed into a single instant of understanding.

Felix saw the sealing from the inside. Saw the bargain struck in desperation—*we will bind you, and you will sleep, and the world will be safe from your power*—and understood, finally, what Maren had confessed on the road: that the Spire had agreed. Had *wanted* to agree. Because the alternative, offered by frightened people with frightened faces, had been war, and the Spire had already seen enough of war to fill the crater they camped in. It had accepted its imprisonment the way a dog accepts a muzzle from hands that tremble: patiently, sadly, trusting that the trembling meant love rather than cruelty.

Then it had waited. And waited. And the sealers died one by one, and their successors forgot the bargain and remembered only the binding, and the Order grew fat on drained power and called itself protector, and the Spire learned that its trust had been spent like coin by people who never intended to repay it.

So it had done the only thing left. It had taken a thread of its own song, woven it into flesh, and sent it walking into the world with a single instruction.

*Find someone who still listens.*

Felix sat with that for a long time. The fire burned down. Above them the Spire hummed its low, changed note, and the green-gold light breathed on the horizon like something learning to sleep.

"You could have chosen anyone," Felix said aloud, when they trusted their voice. "Anyone who listened. There must have been others."

There had been others. He felt that truth arrive like a second seal pressed into wax—a memory of small moments scattered across years: a shepherd on a far hillside who sang to his sheep in a language he didn't know he knew; a dying woman in a mountain hospice named Ilva who had kept a shard of the seal-stone under her pillow and talked to it every night; a hedge-witch's runaway apprentice, years ago, standing at the edge of a dead-magic valley, who had paused at the ridgeline and looked back at the world they were leaving and thought, *I wish I could hear it again.*

The Spire had heard that thought. The Spire heard everything. It had simply never, until Petra, had anywhere to put what it heard.

"Oh," Felix said. And then, because Maren had told them to ask better questions, they asked the hardest one: "What do you want? Truly. Not survival. Not worship. What do you actually want?"

The answer came slowly, carefully, the way a wounded animal approaches a offered hand.

To be part of the world again. Not above it. Not beneath it. In it. To be listened to the way the wind is listened to—not obeyed, not feared, simply heard. To keep promises instead of having promises kept about it. To give its gift—because that is what magic had always been, a gift, before anyone thought to seize it—to those who chose to receive it, and to withhold it from those who chose otherwise, and to be trusted with the difference.

No more seals. No more sacrifices. No more bargains struck in fear and honored in forgetting.

Magic, held in trust by those who listen.

Felix looked at the fire for a long moment. Then they began, quietly, to laugh—not from joy, or not only joy, but from the peculiar ache of recognition. "That's it? That's the terrible ambition that required thirteen lives to imprison?" They wiped their eyes. "You want what I wanted. What I ran to the valley looking for. A place where nobody makes you prove you deserve to exist."

The hum shifted. If a mountain could be embarrassed, Felix thought, this was the sound of it.

* * *

Petra woke near midnight, wholly herself for perhaps a minute, and asked for water, and drank it, and looked at the stars with an expression Felix had learned to read as *calculating wonder*. Then the doubled quality returned to her voice, softer now, and she turned her head against her rolled cloak and said:

"It wants to talk about me."

Felix set down the water skin. "I know."

"Are you going to let it?"

"No," Felix said. "It's going to talk *with* you. There's a difference. We've spent the whole journey learning it." They moved to sit beside her, close enough that she could lean against their shoulder if she chose, which she did, the way she had been doing more and more often lately—tentatively at first, as though testing whether leaning was allowed, and then with the full weight of a child who had decided that some things were safe.

For a while neither of them spoke. The fire settled. Down the slope, Wren had fallen asleep sitting upright against a boulder, his bandaged hand resting on his knee like something he was learning to make peace with.

"It's offering," Petra said at last. "A choice."

"I know that too."

"Do you?" She tilted her head back to look at Felix, and in the firelight her eyes had stopped their oil-slick shifting and settled into something ordinary and brown and young, and Felix's chest tightened with how much they wanted that ordinariness to remain. "Because I don't think you know what the choice is. Neither does it, really. It's guessing. It's been alone so long it's forgotten how choices work."

"Then tell me."

Petra pulled her knees up and wrapped her arms around them—the posture she took when she was about to say something difficult, Felix had learned, the posture of a girl bracing against her own knowledge.

"If I stay as I am—if I stay its voice—then the song stays whole. Every word of the First Tongue lives in me, complete, forever. I could teach it. I could speak for it whenever the world forgets how to listen, and the world will forget, because that's what worlds do." She shrugged, a small, tired motion. "I'd be a bridge. Bridges are useful. Bridges don't get to be anything else."

"And the other?"

"The other is—" She stopped. Started again. "If I become wholly myself, the song comes apart in me. Most of it goes back to the Spire, where it came from. But some of it—" her hand drifted to her throat, to the place where her voice lived— "some of it stays. A thread. Enough that I'll always be able to hear it, and it'll always be able to hear me. Enough that I could still teach, someday, if I learn the rest the slow way. Like a person learns. Badly, and out of order, and taking forever."

She was quiet for a moment.

"But I wouldn't know anymore. Not for certain. Whether I was made or born. Whether the voices under the Spire raised me or dreamed me. Whether 'Petra' was ever a name or just a shape the song wore." Her voice wavered, steadied. "It says that's the price. Certainty. It can't give me both. Nothing can give anyone both—it checked. It checked very hard."

Felix looked at her—at this mud-caked, fearless, impossible child who had knocked on their door in a storm and spoken words older than kingdoms—and felt the full weight of what was being asked settle onto their shoulders. Not asked of Petra. Asked of *them*, in the oldest way burdens are asked: by love.

Because Felix knew about certainty. They had traded theirs away once, years ago, at the edge of a valley. They had known exactly what they were—an apprentice, a coward, a person who let someone else pay their debt—and that certainty had been a stone they carried everywhere, familiar as a heartbeat. And it had taken a child, a ledger, a mentor's death, and the end of the world to teach them that certainty was not the same as truth. That sometimes the thing you knew about yourself was simply the lie you'd gotten comfortable telling.

"You don't have to decide tonight," Felix said.

"Yes I do." Petra's chin lifted. "The longer I stay doubled, the harder it gets to come apart clean. It's like—" she searched for the word— "like bread rising. You can't unbake it."

That startled a laugh out of Felix, brief and wet-eyed. "Where do you learn these things?"

"The Spire knows a lot about bread. It watched people invent it." She smiled, small and crooked, and then the smile faded into seriousness. "But I want—I need—one thing before I choose. From you."

"Anything."

"Don't tell me what to pick." Her eyes were fierce and frightened and absolutely clear. "Everyone my whole life has known what I'm for. The voices under the Spire. The Order. Even it, maybe—even it has been hoping I'd stay, because staying is easier for it. You're the only one who ever just—" she faltered— "who ever taught me modern words for things like *porridge* and *Tuesday*. Who treated me like I was a person who happened to have a strange language, instead of a language that happened to have a person."

Felix's throat closed. They had to look at the fire for a moment before they could speak.

"Then here's what I'll tell you instead," they managed. "Not what to pick. Just—what I know. Which is that I spent six years being certain of what I was, and it nearly killed me, and the only reason I'm alive is that a child kicked my door open and made me uncertain again." They took her hand. Her fingers were small and calloused and cold, and Felix held them the way you hold something you are terrified of breaking and more terrified of losing. "Certainty feels like safety. But I've lived on both sides of it now, and I'm telling you: the uncertainty is where the living happens. That's all I know. That's the whole of my wisdom, and Maren would laugh herself sick at how long it took me to earn it."

Petra considered this with the gravity she gave to everything, chewing on it visibly.

"So you're saying," she said slowly, "that even if I choose wrong, choosing is how I find out it wasn't wrong."

"I'm saying there might not *be* a wrong. Only different prices."

"Hm." She leaned her head back against Felix's shoulder. "Maren said something like that once. About questions."

"Maren said a lot of things. We mostly weren't listening." Felix swallowed. "We're listening now. That has to count for something."

Above them, the Spire's hum deepened by a single, almost imperceptible interval—and Felix realized, with a jolt, that it had been listening to every word. Of course it had. It heard everything. It had simply never had anyone explain to it that hearing and hovering were different things, that a listener's job was to wait, that love did not lean over the chooser's shoulder.

The hum withdrew, politely, to a respectful distance. Even its loneliness, Felix thought, was capable of learning manners, given time and company.

Petra closed her eyes. Her breathing slowed. And in the space between one breath and the next, Felix felt the decision move through her—not as sound, not as speech, but as a kind of settling, the way a house settles on its foundations at night, creaking into the shape it will hold for a hundred years.

When she opened her eyes, they were brown. Only brown. Ordinary, human, a child's eyes, with a thread of gold buried so deep in the iris that you would only see it if you knew to look—and Felix knew to look, and looked, and found it, and wept without making any sound about it at all.

"I choose me," Petra said. Her voice was only her own now, hoarse and small and entirely mortal. "But it gets visitation rights."

From the Spire came a sound that Felix would spend the rest of their life failing to describe: not a word, not a chord, but something like a vast and ancient creature exhaling after thirteen hundred years of holding its breath—and then, unmistakably, impossibly, something very like laughter, rolling down the crater walls and out across the waking world.

Far below, in the village at the valley's heart, Odile would later swear that every candle in her cottage flamed green for exactly three seconds, and that her ledger, lying open on the table, turned its own final page.

But that was later. For now there was only the fire, and the child asleep against Felix's shoulder, breathing in a rhythm that matched—precisely, tenderly, at a respectful distance—the hum of the Spire keeping watch over them both.

Felix did not sleep. They sat up until the false dawn came from every direction at once, holding the weight of what had been chosen, and thinking about the long road home, and the valley that would have to learn to breathe, and the villagers who had built their lives on invisibility and would not thank anyone for teaching them openness.

Thinking, most of all, about the empty bedroll, and the promise they had made at a graveside they hadn't dug yet.

At first light, Wren stirred against his boulder, winced, and squinted up at the green-gold sky.

"So," he rasped. "What now? We can't stay here. Half the Order's still loyal, the other half is leaderless and armed, and somebody's going to have to explain to the world what happened before the world decides it happened *to* it." He paused. "Also I'm starving, and unless the Spire's offering breakfast, we've got a problem."

Petra opened one eye.

"It says," she announced, in her new single voice, with the ghost of a grin, "that the flowers growing on the south slope are edible, and that it's very sorry about your hand, and that it thinks—we should go home. All of us. It wants to see the valley."

Felix looked east, toward the ridgeline they had once fled across, toward the place where spells used to die.

"Well," they said, and stood, and offered Petra their hand. "Then we'd better teach it the way."
