## 37. Petra's Choice

The Spire's heart was not what she had expected.

Petra had imagined—without ever letting the thought take shape—a room of some kind, a chamber at the center of the tower where something vast and patient waited like a spider in its web. She had imagined eyes, perhaps, or a voice that came from everywhere at once. What she found instead was light without source and warmth without fire, and a presence so large that she could only perceive it in pieces, the way you can only see one stretch of ocean from any single shore.

*You came,* it said, and the words were not words but a settling, like snowfall, like dust finding the floor of an empty house.

*I was sent,* she answered, and felt the truth of it tremble through her. *You sent me.*

*I sent a piece of myself. You are not that piece anymore.* The presence turned over her, around her, gentle as hands she had never had. *You have been eating bread. Learning to be cold. Learning to want things. Do you know how long I have wanted to know what bread tastes like?*

She laughed—or made the shape of laughing—and the sound rang strangely in the hollow of the tower's heart. Somewhere behind her, far away now, Felix stood at the Gate of Names with their hand pressed flat against the seal-stone, refusing to come further. *This part is yours,* Felix had said. *I'll be right here when you're done.* And Wren was somewhere below, cursing at the stairs, and the world outside was still ringing with the unmaking of Serath, whose name no one would speak again except in warnings.

But here, inside, there was only her and the thing that had dreamed her into being.

*Ask me,* the Spire said. *Whatever you have carried down all those miles of road. Ask me, and I will answer truly. It is the least I owe you.*

Petra stood in the light and let herself feel the question she had been carrying since the hospice, since Ilva's dying whisper, since the night under the tree line when she had watched gray riders fail at the ridge and understood, in the wordless way of children, that she was the reason they had come.

*Am I your daughter?* she asked. *Or am I your hand? Did you make someone, or did you make a key with legs?*

The silence that followed was long enough for her to count her own heartbeats. Forty of them. Fifty. The Spire did not breathe, but she did, and the waiting felt like drowning in warm water.

*Both,* it said at last. *And neither. And I am sorry, because that is a coward's answer, and you deserve better than a coward's answer.*

*Try again.*

*When the seal closed around me—when thirteen lives wove themselves into a lock—I could not think whole thoughts. Every idea I had was cut into pieces before it finished forming. So I broke off the smallest piece of myself I could spare and wrapped it in flesh and pushed it out through a crack in my own prison, and I told it: find someone who still listens. I did not know if what walked out of me would be a messenger or a child. I did not know there was a difference anymore. That is what solitude does. That is what they did to me, thinking they were saving the world.* A pause, and then, quieter, in the tone of someone confessing: *And then you learned to be hungry. And I watched you eat bread, and I understood that I had made a mistake I could never undo. Because you were not my hand anymore. You were a girl who liked bread. And girls who like bread cannot be sent back into the dark to be a voice again. Not fairly. Not kindly. Not by anything that deserves the name of mind.*

Petra sat down. There was no floor exactly, but the light gathered beneath her like a mother's cupped palms, and she sat, and pulled her knees up, the way she used to in the vault beneath everything, listening to the voices that raised her.

*Felix says I get to choose,* she said.

*Felix is right. And Felix is also terrified that you will choose wrong, because Felix has spent their whole life believing that choosing is how people get hurt.* The warmth shifted, almost amused. *They are wrong about that, mostly. But do not tell them yet. They need a little longer to grow into being right.*

*If I choose to stay—*

*Then you become the Spire's voice entire, and the world gets a god again, and gods are always, always disappointed in what people do with them. I know this from experience.* The vastness of it contracted, drew close, became almost small—almost the size of a person sitting across from another person. *If you choose to leave, then I remain as I am: heard, witnessed, bound by new terms, held in trust by those who listen. I will be lonelier than I have ever been. But loneliness with hope in it is a different animal than loneliness without. I have lived both. I recommend the first.*

*That isn't fair,* Petra said, and her voice cracked on it. *You're making me choose between you and everyone else. That's what Maren did. That's what Serath tried to do. Everyone keeps making the world a choice between two terrible things.*

*Yes.* No apology in it this time, only grief worn smooth as river stone. *Because that is what bargains do when they are broken badly enough. But hear me, little piece of me, little girl who likes bread: I am not asking you to abandon me. There is a third door. There has always been a third door, and I could not open it myself because I am too large to fit through it. Only something small can. Only something that has eaten bread and been cold and laughed at a hedge-witch falling over their own magic in the road.*

Petra looked up. *What third door?*

*Leave—but carry a thread of me with you. Not my power. Not my commands. My song. One thread of it, woven into your voice, so that wherever you go, whatever you say, even when you are only singing nonsense to the goats, I will hear a little of the world through you. And you will hear a little of me. Not enough to command. Not enough to be commanded. Enough to visit. Enough that neither of us is ever entirely alone again.* The presence hesitated, and Petra realized, with a shock that went through her like cold water, that the Spire was afraid. *It will cost you something. Everything costs. The thread will take the place of your certainty. You will never again know, absolutely and forever, what you are or where you began. The memory will soften every time you reach for it, like a dream at dawn. Some people would call that a small price. I will not lie to you: it is not small. To not know yourself is its own kind of exile.*

Petra thought about the vault beneath the world. She thought about the voices that had raised her—kind voices, patient voices, voices that had never once told her what she was, only what she must do. She had assumed they withheld it to protect her. Now she wondered if they had withheld it because even they did not know, because she had never been one thing, because she had been born already split between purpose and personhood, and no one had known which half would win.

She thought about Felix teaching her the modern tongue by candlelight, sounding out words like *spoon* and *tomorrow*, both of them laughing until the candles guttered. She thought about Odile's sharp tongue and Wren's crooked grin and Bram's frightened, decent silence. She thought about the valley breathing again, green and strange and gentle, magic moving through it like water through dry ground.

She did not want to be certain. Certainty belonged to the vault, to the Order, to Serath with his stolen years and his perfect, rotting conviction. Certainty was what people clutched when they were too afraid to hold anything alive.

*I don't want to know what I am,* she said slowly, testing each word the way she had once tested the First Tongue against binding-spells. *I want to find out. Those aren't the same thing. Knowing is a wall. Finding out is a road.*

The Spire was quiet for a long moment. When it spoke again, the vastness of it had gone soft, the way a great animal goes soft when it finally stops bracing for the blow.

*Oh,* it said. *Oh, that is better than anything I could have taught you. Say it again.*

*I want to find out.*

*Then we have a bargain—the last bargain, and the first good one. No seals. No sacrifices. No names given in blood. Just a thread, freely carried, and a song, freely sung.* The light gathered itself, drew inward, began to condense the way mist condenses into rain. *Hold still, little one. This will feel like being unwound and rewound, and it will hurt the way growing hurts, and afterward you will be smaller than you have ever been and more yourself than you have ever been. Are you ready?*

Petra thought of Felix waiting at the gate. Of the goats she'd left in Odile's care, who were probably eating Odile's herb garden out of spite. Of tomorrow, a word she had learned only months ago and already loved beyond reason.

*Yes,* she said. *Do it.*

* * *

It felt like a long exhale.

Not pain, exactly—or pain, but pain the way a knot coming loose hurts, the ache of tension releasing after years of holding. Something in the center of her chest unwound, thread by golden thread, and she felt each coil leave her and go home, pouring back into the vastness around her, and with every thread that left she grew lighter and smaller and more sharply herself, the way a sculpture emerges from stone by losing everything that isn't the sculpture.

She saw things as the threads left. Fragments of the Spire's memory, offered like gifts at a parting: the world young and loud with magic; the first bargains, struck in good faith; the slow rot of them; the thirteen coming up the mountain with sorrow on their faces and iron in their hearts; Maren—younger, unbent, weeping as she spoke her own life into the weave. She saw centuries of silence. She saw herself, from the outside: a small figure walking out of a crack in the world, mud to her knees, fearless and starving, knocking on a hedge-witch's door while a storm tried to wash the valley off the map.

Then the last thread remained, and the Spire held it a moment, the way you hold a hand a moment longer than necessary at a parting.

*This one stays with you,* it said. *Guard it well. Sing sometimes. Even badly. Especially badly—it makes the hearing sweeter.*

And then it let go, and Petra fell—not downward, but inward, into her own body, into her own single small life, and landed on the stone floor of the Spire's heart with a jolt that knocked the breath out of her.

She was just a girl. Knees scraped. Hair full of dust. Heart hammering. Stomach empty and complaining. She was nobody's messenger, nobody's key, nobody's fragment. She was Petra, and the name fit her like a coat that had finally been tailored, and she sat on the cold stone and cried—not from grief, though there was grief in it, wide and clean as a washed sky, but from the sheer strangeness of being only one thing in only one place at only one time.

When she could breathe again, she sang.

It wasn't much. A scrap of melody she'd hummed on the salt flats, a few notes Felix had whistled once while cooking, stitched together with nothing. But underneath it, threaded through it, faint as starlight, ran something older—the Spire's song, one strand of it, humming in harmony with her small human voice. She could feel it listening. She could feel it glad.

The light around her dimmed gently, folding itself away like bedding put up for summer. The heart of the Spire, no longer needing to manifest for a conversation, settled into its long work of simply existing—heard now, witnessed, held in trust. She could feel the new terms of it all around her, not a cage and not a chain but something like a treaty written in living stone: *no more seals, no more sacrifices, magic held by those who listen.* Somewhere in the walls, the old bargains were dissolving, thirteen debts forgiven one by one, and she thought she felt Maren's debt loosen last of all, sighing out of the stone like a breath finally released.

"Petra?"

Felix's voice, echoing up the stairwell, tight with worry. Then footsteps, fast and graceless, and Felix burst through the inner gate with their hands full of half-formed defensive magic and their face full of terror, and stopped dead at the sight of her sitting cross-legged on the floor, dusty and tear-streaked and grinning.

"You're—" Felix's magic guttered out. "You're smaller."

"I'm exactly the right size." Petra stood up. Her legs shook. She didn't care. "It let me go, Felix. All of it. Except—" She touched her own throat, feeling the thread hum there, faint and constant as a second pulse. "Except one song. It gave me one song to keep."

Felix crossed the floor in three strides and knelt down and pulled her into their arms, and Petra buried her face in their shoulder and breathed in woodsmoke and goat and herbs, the smell of the valley, the smell of home-not-yet-returned-to. Felix was shaking. She hadn't expected that. Hedge-witches, in her experience, were supposed to be made of sterner stuff.

"I couldn't hear you," Felix said into her hair. "The whole time. There was just—light, and silence, and I kept thinking about Serath, and about Maren, and about every bargain that ever went wrong, and I thought—"

"I know." She pulled back and looked at them—at the tired eyes, the ink-stained fingers, the face of the person who had opened their door to a mud-caked stranger in a storm and never managed to close it again. "But it went right, Felix. For once, it went right. Nobody died for it. Nobody was sealed for it. We just—we asked properly. Like I said at the smugglers' fire. Remember? You said I was being naive."

"You were being naive," Felix said, and their voice broke halfway through, and they laughed wetly at the same time. "Don't ever stop."

From below, drifting up the stairwell, came Wren's voice, hoarse and aggrieved: "Are you two going to stand around having feelings all day, or is someone going to help me climb these cursed stairs? Some of us took a wound for this expedition!"

Petra laughed, and the laugh carried the thread of the Spire's song in it, and she felt the whole tower shiver with pleasure, like a dog hearing its name.

She took Felix's hand. Together they started down, out of the heart, past the Gate of Names—which glowed softly now, its demand satisfied, its memory of promises rewritten—and out onto the mountainside, where the air smelled of rain coming and the world below lay green and waiting.

Only one thing remained, and Petra felt it settle over her as they descended, bittersweet as the last note of a song: she could no longer remember the vault. She reached for it and found softness, blur, the dream-fading the Spire had promised. The faces of the voices that raised her were already dissolving into warmth without features. By tomorrow they would be gone entirely, and she would never be able to prove where she came from, and no one—not Felix, not Odile, not any scholar yet unborn—would ever quite believe the story of the girl who climbed out of the Spire.

She found she could live with that. She found, in fact, that she preferred it. A girl with an unverifiable origin was a girl free to invent herself.

Halfway down the mountain, Felix stopped and squinted eastward, toward the lowlands, toward the Order's broken capital and the roads beyond.

"There's still the valley," Felix said. "Odile's holding it together, but the villagers—they'll need to see us come back. They'll need to see that the magic in their fields isn't a curse. And there's Maren's grave to—" Their voice caught. "There's a lot of road yet."

Petra squeezed their hand and felt the thread in her throat hum its small, glad note.

"Then we'd better start walking," she said. "Tomorrow's waiting, and I've only just learned what that word means."

Below them, far off, the valley caught the morning light—and something in it, some green and growing thing, seemed to catch the note of her voice and answer back.
