## 40. The Last Page of the Ledger

The path to Maren's grave had grown over in a year, which Felix took as a kindness. Grass did not grieve; it simply grew, indifferent and green, and there was something in that indifference that made the walking easier. The tower behind them stood empty now, its wards dissolved at Maren's own instruction—written into her will in a hand so precise it looked typeset: *Let nothing of mine keep anything out. Or in.*

Felix carried the ledger under one arm, wrapped in oilcloth against the autumn damp. It was not the hedge-witch's ledger they had once stolen from Odile's cottage—that one had been returned, with an apology and a jar of honey, and Odile had laughed for a full minute before accepting both. This was Maren's ledger, the true one, recovered from a locked drawer beneath the tower's cold hearth. Its cover was cracked leather gone soft as old bread, and it smelled of cedar smoke and ink and, faintly, of the years Felix had spent pretending it did not exist.

Petra walked ahead, as she always did on this path, kicking through drifts of copper leaves with the particular ferocity of a child who had learned that joy was not given but taken. She was taller now—not much, but enough that Felix noticed it in small betrayals: the way her sleeves sat shorter than they had in spring, the way her voice had dropped half a tone when she sang. And she still sang. That was the thing that undid Felix most reliably. The thread of the Spire's song lived in her voice like a splinter of light, and when she hummed on the walk up from the village, the wildflowers along the trail turned their faces toward her as if she were a slow-moving sun.

"Slow down," Felix called. "The dead aren't going anywhere."

"That's the whole point of them," Petra said, without turning around.

Twelve years old, and she spoke about death the way other children spoke about weather. Felix had stopped trying to correct it. There was no correcting a girl who had been raised by voices beneath a mountain, who had watched a man unmade by his own stolen power, who had spoken a question into the heart of raw magic and been answered. You could only walk beside her, and hope, and occasionally be astonished.

She reached the grave first and went quiet, which was her way of being respectful—the stillness of someone who understood that some silences were load-bearing.

Maren's grave was a cairn, because Maren had asked for a cairn. *No marker with my name on it,* she had written. *I spent enough years being a name people feared. Let me be stones.* But Odile, sharp-tongued Odile, who had appointed herself the valley's teacher and its conscience in the same breath, had ignored the instruction in the manner of all good students: she had carved the name anyway, small, on the underside of the capstone, where only someone kneeling would ever find it. *MAREN OF THE THIRTEEN*, and beneath it, in smaller letters, *who stayed.*

Felix knelt. Their knees knew this ground now. They had come here every month since the thaw, and each time the ritual was the same: clear the leaves, set the offerings—a sprig of rosemary, because Maren had hated lilies; a stub of beeswax candle, unlit, because lighting one felt too much like performance—and then open the ledger to the last written page, and read.

* * *

The names had not changed. Felix had checked, the first few times, half-expecting them to shift the way names sometimes shifted in dreams, but stone was stone and ink was ink, and the thirteen remained the thirteen.

They read aloud, as they always did, because Maren had believed—had told Felix once, in the brief thaw before the end, in the tower kitchen while Petra slept upstairs—that names were the smallest spells there were, and the most durable. *Say them out loud or they curdle,* she'd said. *A name kept silent goes sour in the mouth of the one keeping it. I should know. I kept yours for eleven years, and look what it did to me.*

So Felix said them.

"Ilva of the Hospice," they began, and the wind moved through the pines above the cairn like a listener leaning closer. "Who held the eastern stone and gave her last year to a dying woman's hands."

"Ilva," Petra repeated softly. She had never met Ilva, but she had met the shard, and the shard remembered.

"Tomas of the Fenlands. Who argued longest against the sealing, and wept while he wove."

"Brenna Ashfoot. Who left a daughter she never named in her letters, though everyone knew the daughter's name was Wren."

Wren himself had laughed until he cried when he'd read that line, down in the Listening School's drafty first classroom, chalk dust on his fingers. *All those years I thought she abandoned me,* he'd said, *and she was writing letters to a name she couldn't say. Gods. Mothers.* He taught the gutter-mage classes now—practical craft, ward-work, the honest magic of small protections—and he had hung Brenna's only surviving letter above his desk where every student could see it.

"Halvard. Who sealed his own voice into the western anchor, so that he could never again speak a spell, nor a lie, nor a goodbye."

Felix's throat tightened on Halvard, as it always did. Some of these names were abstractions. Halvard was a specific horror: a man who had chosen muteness as his portion of the price, and whose silence had lasted, as far as anyone could determine, exactly as long as the seal—which meant that somewhere in the world, if he yet lived, a voice had returned to him a year ago, after decades of quiet, and Felix did not know what he had said first. They hoped it was a goodbye. They hoped someone had been there to hear it.

"Sera Vane. Dov of the High Passes. Merrin Clove. Anselm. The twins, Rill and Roan, who wove as one thread and died within an hour of each other, ten years apart." A pause, the wind filling it. "Yeva of the Salt Flats. Corvin. And Serath." Felix said that name flatly, without heat, the way you'd set down a hot iron. "Who wanted to finish the bargain. Who is not mourned here, but is counted, because counting is not forgiving."

Petra had insisted on that. *You can't leave him off,* she'd said, the week after the Breaking, when Felix had drafted the list and struck Serath's name through. *He was one of the thirteen. Leaving him off is how he won.* So Serath stayed, inked in full, and Felix had found, over the months, that saying his name without flinching was its own small victory—the last of his power over them bleeding away word by word.

Thirteen names. Thirteen lives woven into a seal that had never been a prison at all, but a bargain struck in terror by people who loved the world too clumsily to ask it what it wanted. The Spire had not been defeated. It had been *silenced*—bound by sacrifice upon sacrifice, fed on forgetting, its loneliness compounded century by century until it had done the only desperate thing left to it: it had sent a piece of itself out into the world wearing the shape of a child, with one instruction.

*Find someone who still listens.*

Felix looked at Petra, kneeling beside them in the wet grass, her dark eyes fixed on the cairn with an expression far too old for her face, and felt the familiar ache-and-awe that had become, over the past year, simply the texture of loving her.

"And the ones who refused," Felix said.

This line was new. It had not been in the ledger when they'd found it. Felix had added it themselves, three months ago, in ink mixed with ash from the hearth of the empty tower, and the first time they'd read it aloud they had barely managed the words. It was easier now. Everything was easier now, which was not the same as easy.

"The ones who refused," they said again, steadier. "Who were offered places in the seal and would not take them. Who paid other prices—exile, silence, the long self-imprisonment of a valley that forgets. Who ran, and hid, and tended goats, and told themselves it was enough." Their voice caught, and they let it catch. Maren had taught them that too, in the end: *A caught voice is an honest one. Smooth it over and you've lied twice.* "And who came back. Eventually. Late. But back."

Petra's hand found theirs in the grass. Small, warm, calloused at the fingertips from chalk and climbing. She didn't squeeze. She just held on, the way she'd held on the night she'd appeared mud-caked at their door during a storm, starving and fearless and speaking words no living tongue should know. Some things about her had changed utterly. Her grip had not.

* * *

They stayed a while after the reading, as the light went long and gold across the valley below. From up here Felix could see nearly all of it: the terraced fields where the villagers had learned, slowly and with much grumbling, to farm alongside things that glowed; the new orchard where the trees bloomed out of season now, cheerfully, having discovered that no one would punish them for it; the rooftops of the village itself, where Bram the elder had nailed a notice to the meeting-house door last spring that Felix still thought about more often than they admitted:

*THIS VALLEY IS NO LONGER QUIET. IF YOU CANNOT LIVE WITH BEING HEARD, THE ROAD OUT REMAINS OPEN. — B.*

Some had taken the road. Most had stayed. Old habits of invisibility died hard—Felix saw it in the way some villagers still flinched when a hedge-light drifted past their windows, in the whispered arguments at market about whether the Listening School was a blessing or an infection—but flinching was not fleeing, and staying was its own kind of courage. Odile said so, loudly, at least once a week, usually while bullying a roomful of students into admitting what they actually wanted.

Below the village, at the valley's mouth, the old border was a memory. Where the ridge had once killed every spell that crossed it, the air now hummed—faintly, patiently—with the Spire's distant song, carried on winds that no longer forgot what they touched. Wildflowers bloomed in drifts along the ridgeline in every season, riotous and unashamed. Travelers came now instead of asking strange questions about the quiet place. They came asking how to listen.

It had cost everything to make this. Felix catalogued the cost the way they once catalogued herbs: Maren, taken by rockfall in a gorge, her last words a gift—*Ask better questions than I did.* Ilva, dead in a hospice bed with a shard of seal-stone pressed into Felix's palm. Wren's burned escape route and the wound that had cost him half his craft, though he claimed the remaining half was the better half. The Order of the Ashen Hand, broken open, its archives exposed, its purpose inverted—from seizing power to stewarding it, staffed now by former deserters and reformed zealots who woke each morning and chose, again, not to become what Serath had become.

And the cost that was not a loss at all, though it had felt like one at the time: Felix's own hard-won belonging, surrendered freely at the Gate of Names. *My name, freely given,* they had said, and the gate had accepted, and for one terrible moment Felix had believed the old bargain's logic would hold—that a name given was a life owed. But the Spire had kept its promise literally, as Ilva had warned, and its promise had never been to take. It had promised to *remember*. And so Felix's name lived now in the Spire's memory alongside every promise ever made to it, and what they had feared would be a payment had turned out to be an enrollment.

Belonging, it turned out, was not a wall you hid behind. It was a name spoken aloud among witnesses.

"Felix." Petra tugged their sleeve. "The page."

Felix blinked. "What page?"

"Your page." She was looking at the ledger, open on Felix's knee to the last written leaf—the one with the fourteen lines. Below *and the ones who refused*, the rest of the page lay blank. It had lain blank for three months, because Felix had known, with the certainty of a person avoiding a thing, what belonged there, and had not been able to write it.

"You said you'd do it today," Petra said. Not accusing. Just certain, in the way she was certain about everything that mattered. "Autumn equinox. You said."

"I said a lot of things."

"You said you'd write your name without flinching." She tilted her head, and the late light caught the faint silver thread in her irises—the residue of the Spire's song, the mark of the voice she had chosen to keep. "You've been practicing the not-flinching part for a year. I've watched. You're very good at it now. Mostly."

Felix huffed a laugh that was half a surrender. Trust a child raised by a lonely mountain to have no patience whatsoever for avoidance.

They took out the pen—they had brought it, of course they had brought it, they had been bringing it every month for three months like a coward carrying a sword they wouldn't draw—and uncapped the ink. The wind picked up, and Felix shielded the page with their body, an old scribe's habit from apprenticeship days that their hands had never forgotten even when everything else had.

The blank space waited.

Here was the truth of it, the truth Felix had circled for a year and finally landed on somewhere around midsummer, sitting on Odile's porch watching Petra teach a class of farm children to hum the First Tongue's gentlest word: that they had spent their whole life believing their name was a debt. First the name of a runaway apprentice, a failure, a coward who fled the offer of a place in the seal and let Maren pay alone. Then the name of a stranger in a valley that erased names, which had seemed like mercy and had actually been another kind of hiding. Every identity they had ever worn had been a way of not signing the ledger of their own life.

But the Spire remembered every promise. And somewhere in that vast, wounded, patient memory was a record of the day Felix Villalobos had stood at a gate of glass and old battlefields and given their name not as payment but as presence—*I am here; witness me*—and had been answered not with a bill but with a welcome.

A name was not a debt. A name was a door.

Felix wrote.

The pen moved steadily. No tremor. The letters came out clean and upright, neither ornamental nor ashamed: **FELIX VILLALOBOS**, and beneath it, because honesty demanded the whole account, in smaller script: *who refused, and ran, and listened, and came back.*

No flinch. They checked—actually checked, the way you'd check a healing wound—and found none. Just the small warm ache of a thing set right.

"There," Petra said softly. "Told you."

"You told me nothing. You ambushed me."

"Same thing." She leaned her head briefly against Felix's shoulder, a gesture she permitted herself perhaps twice a year, and just as quickly pulled away to pretend it hadn't happened, because she was twelve and dignity was survival. But she was smiling. Felix caught it sideways and hoarded it.

* * *

They walked down as the sun touched the ridge, and the valley lit up gold and rose around them, humming its low patient note. Halfway to the tree line, Petra stopped abruptly, head cocked, listening to something Felix could not hear—or could only barely hear, a pressure beneath the wind, a change in the song's long drone.

"What is it?" Felix asked.

Petra was quiet for a long moment. The wildflowers around her feet had all turned toward her, hundreds of small faces, attentive.

"She's singing something new," Petra said at last. Her voice had gone careful, the way it did when the thread of the Spire's song rose loud enough to matter. "Not the waiting-song. Not the listening-song. Something—" She frowned, working it out, translating from a language older than kingdoms into words a twelve-year-old could carry. "Something like a question. She's asking us a question, Felix. All of us. Everyone who can hear."

Felix felt it then, faintly, through the soles of their boots: the hum shifting key, rising, insistent, almost playful. The sound of something vast that had waited centuries in silence discovering, at last, the pleasure of conversation—and finding it had rather a lot to say.

"Well," Felix said, and slung the ledger—fourteen names, one blank page now filled, the whole crooked honest account of it—more securely under their arm. "Then I suppose we'd better teach everyone to answer."

Petra grinned, took their hand, and together they walked down into the singing valley, where the lights were coming on in the windows, one by one, like names being spoken.
