## 1. The Taster's Tongue

There are three rules to surviving as a food-taster in the Thornvale, and I wrote all of them myself, which is the only kind of authority I've ever held.

One: never let them see you enjoy the food. Enjoyment invites conversation, and conversation invites noticing, and being noticed is how tasters die—either quickly, by poison meant for someone else's palate, or slowly, by becoming interesting enough to matter.

Two: always taste the wine first. Wine is where cowards hide their work.

Three: never, under any circumstances, let anyone learn what your tongue can actually do.

I was thinking about rule three that morning while Lord Hessel's cook watched me from across the kitchen with the particular anxiety of a man whose neck and mine were attached to the same invisible rope. The morning's first dish sat before me: a pheasant pie, golden-crusted, smelling of thyme and marrow and the small dishonesties of a man who'd skimped on the saffron and hoped no one would count the threads.

I took my spoonful. Held it on my tongue the way you hold a stranger's secret—carefully, without swallowing too fast.

Bitterness at the back edge. Not henbane—not anything, really. Just the liver of an older bird, doing what older livers do. I let the silence stretch two heartbeats longer than comfort required, because a taster who clears food instantly looks careless, and a taster who hesitates dramatically looks like she wants a raise.

Then I nodded, once, and set down the spoon.

"Clean," I said.

The cook exhaled like a man surfacing. Somewhere above us, Lord Hessel would eat his pheasant pie and think nothing of the woman in the grey apron who had made it possible, because thinking nothing of me was precisely the service I provided. Not the tasting—the nothing. Twenty-two years old, and I had built myself into the shape of a piece of furniture. Useful, unremarkable, impossible to miss in the sense that no one ever looks directly at a chair.

I want to be clear about something: I'm good at this. Not merely competent. Good. My tongue can find wormwood in a barrel of honey and nightshade bruised into the skin of an apple by a thumb pressed there for the length of a prayer. I can taste the difference between water drawn at dawn and water drawn at dusk, between salt from the eastern pans and salt smuggled up from the coast, between a man's fear and a man's guilt if either has touched his hands before he handled my bread.

The guild called it a gift. The guild trained it into me from the age of six, when they took my measurements the way farriers measure horses for shoes, and fed me drops of this and grains of that over a fire I was told would make me strong. It did make me strong. It also made me a thing with exactly one function, and I had spent my whole adult life pretending that was enough.

It was enough. I want that on the record. The Thornvale is a small valley ringed with old stones that keep out everything the rest of the world suffers from—plague, war, and magic, though the last one mostly in theory, since none of us had ever seen any. What we had instead was ordinary life in generous quantities: markets, feuds, weddings, winters. A person could disappear into it beautifully. I had disappeared so thoroughly that sometimes I surprised myself in mirrors.

So yes. Enough. That was the word I'd chosen, and I'd chosen it young, and I intended to die by it peacefully at eighty with my tongue intact and my name unremembered.

The universe, as I would learn, keeps its own records.

* * *

Market day came around the way market days do—all noise and elbows and a fishmonger shouting about the freshness of things that had clearly given up some time ago. I went for onions and stayed for the crowd, because crowds are the one place a furniture-person gets to be genuinely invisible. Nobody watches a face in a sea of faces.

That's how I happened to be close to the gate when the horse came through without a rider.

It was a courier's mare, lathered, one eye rolling white, reins snapped short as if someone had cut them in a hurry. It clattered past the stalls and I remember thinking, distantly, *someone's having a bad day*, and then the crowd at the gate opened the way crowds do when something is wrong, and I saw him.

A man on his knees just inside the wardline stones. Young, or young-looking, in the way of people who have been aged fast by hard travel. Courier's leathers, road-dusted, one hand pressed flat against his side as if holding shut a door.

And from between his fingers, where blood should have been, came shadow.

Not dark blood. Not a wound gone bad. Shadow—actual darkness, thick as tar, sliding out of him and pooling on the cobbles and *moving*, curling at the edges like smoke deciding which way the wind goes. The sunlight near him looked wrong, dimmed, as though the day itself had leaned away.

The market went quiet in that peculiar rippling way, silence spreading outward from him like a dropped stone in reverse. Someone screamed. Someone else did the sensible thing, which was to leave.

I should have left too. Rule four, which I am adding retroactively: when the world shows you something that shouldn't exist, walk away and let it be someone else's problem. Invisibility is a discipline. You don't break it for strangers.

But he saw me. Of everyone in that shrinking circle of gawkers, his eyes found mine—the one woman standing perfectly still—and there was something in his face that wasn't fear. Recognition, maybe. Or relief. Both are worse.

"You," he said. His voice sounded like gravel settling. "You're one of hers."

"I'm nobody's," I said, which was true, and also the last fully true thing I would say for a long time.

He laughed, and it turned into coughing, and more shadow came up with it, spattering the stones. He crawled toward me—one knee-drag, two—and the crowd gave us both a wide berth now, the way people part for madmen and plague, and I stood there like an idiot because my feet had decided, without consulting me, that we were staying.

"Listen." He grabbed my wrist. His grip was cold in a way that had nothing to do with weather. "They'll come asking. Enforcers. Grey cloaks, iron pins. Tell them nothing. Tell them *less* than nothing." He pressed something into my hands—hard, waxy, warm from his body. A scroll case, sealed with a blob of black wax stamped with a sigil I didn't recognize: a crown made of thorns.

"The king is already dead," he said.

"That's—" I glanced around for help, for sanity, for literally anyone. "The king is in the capital. Everyone knows—"

"The man wearing him isn't." His eyes were going glassy, but his grip tightened. "Map. Queen's map. To the heart under the palace. She hid it. She sent me. Find—" A shudder took him whole. "Find the door before they do. Before it roots."

"What roots? *What* roots?"

But he was looking past me now, at something over my shoulder, and whatever he saw made his face go smooth and calm in a way that frightened me more than the shadow had. "You taste clean," he whispered. "That's why. That's why it has to be you."

Then the light went out of him all at once, like a lamp pinched, and he sagged against me, and the shadow pooling beneath him began—slowly, horribly—to sink into the cobblestones as if the ground were drinking it.

* * *

They tell you grief and panic feel different. They lie. They're the same animal wearing different coats, and I wore both for the rest of that day.

The wardens came within the hour, because a dead man bleeding darkness at the market gate is not the sort of thing a valley ignores. They asked questions in the gentle, terrible way wardens have. Did I know him? No. Had he said anything? Words, certainly; words mean little. Where were you standing? Here. Doing what? Standing.

I lied well. I've had practice lying by omission my entire life, and omission is just a lie with good posture. But I kept touching the scroll case through my apron pocket—cold now, colder than the morning justified, cold in a way that felt less like temperature and more like *opinion*—and every time I touched it, I was sure the wardens could hear it.

By evening the market had decided what it had seen: a sick man, a strange death, fever maybe, bad blood from outside the valley. People prefer explanations they can put away after dinner. I envied them enormously.

I went home. I lit my lamp. I sat with the scroll case on my table for a long time, the way you sit with a letter you're afraid to open.

Here is what I knew, in the honest ledger of my own head:

Item: a dying man had called me *one of hers*, and then *clean*, and both words had landed somewhere under my ribs and stayed there.

Item: the seal on the wax was a crown of thorns, and the Thornvale's oldest stories say the high kings of the capital wore crowns grown, not forged, back when magic still touched this land—before the valley's founders raised their stones and locked all that out.

Item: the case was getting colder, and my fingers kept returning to it anyway, traitorous and curious.

Item, the largest item: I did not want to open it. Opening it was a door, and doors are the one thing a person built of invisibility cannot afford.

I opened it.

Inside was a map—not parchment, something finer, silk-fine and pale as skin—and the moment my fingertips touched it, the lamp guttered. Not wind. There was no draft. The flame simply bent away from the map, the way a dog bends away from a whip.

And the map was *cold*. Cold poured up my arms and settled behind my sternum like a swallowed stone, and every hair on my body rose, and somewhere very far away—or very close; distance had stopped meaning much—I felt something turn its attention toward me. Slowly. The way a great beast turns, or a great root grows: without hurry, because nothing in the world can outrun it.

On the map, in a fine feminine hand, lines traced the streets of the capital and then kept going—down, beneath the palace, into passages that no honest architect would draw. At the center, marked with the same thorn-crown sigil, two words:

*The heart remembers.*

I sat in my small, safe, magicless house in my small, safe, magicless valley, holding a dead man's errand in my cold hands, and I understood—with the same certainty my tongue used for poisons—that the wards around the Thornvale had already noticed this object. That it did not belong here. That neither, anymore, did I.

Outside, far off toward the wardline, a bell began to ring. Not the market bell. The deep one, the one they rang only when the stones themselves needed tending.

They had come asking after a courier. And I had until dawn.
