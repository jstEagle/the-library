## 2. What the Valley Cannot Hold

The map burned cold.

I want to be precise about that, because everyone assumes magic burns hot—candles and firelight and all the storybook trimmings—but what sat folded against my ribs under three layers of wool was cold the way a well is cold. The way a grave is cold. It didn't warm to my skin no matter how long I carried it, and by the second morning I had stopped pretending it might.

I'd hidden it in the flour crock first. Then under the loose flagstone by my bed, which is where every fool in every story hides things, which is exactly why nobody found it there. Finally I sewed it into the lining of my tasting coat, because if the enforcers came for me they would search my kitchen and my cot and my person, but no one in the history of the Thornvale had ever wanted to steal a food-taster's coat. It smelled of onions and old tallow. It was, in its way, the safest place in the valley.

The courier's words hadn't gotten any less mad with repetition. *The king is already dead.* I turned them over at night like a coin I suspected of being clipped. Kings died all the time, of course—that was practically their trade—but this king, King Aldric, was young, and the Thornvale's whole quiet arrangement with the crown rested on him staying alive and staying distant. We sent our taxes upriver. He sent his enforcers when he remembered we existed. Between those two courtesies, we lived our small lives and called it peace.

And I tasted for Lord Merrick of Hollowbridge, who ate like a man settling a grudge against his own digestion, and I went home each evening to a cottage that smelled of rosemary, and I told myself the stranger at the market gate had been dying and dying men say mad things.

Except for the blood. Dying men bleed red. This one had bled shadow—a slow black seep that drank the lantern light around it—and I had knelt in it, and it had not stained my skirts so much as *unstained* them, leaving pale ghost-marks where the dye had simply given up. I still had those marks. I kept checking.

On the third morning I took the map out behind the smokehouse and unfolded it, just a corner, just to look.

It wasn't ink. The lines were drawn in something silvered and faintly alive, and where my thumb crossed the river marked on it, the paper shivered like a horse bothered by flies. And beneath the map, pressed into the wax seal that had once held it shut, was a mark I recognized without knowing why: a thorn circled by a ring. The same mark, I would learn much later, that the queen of the realm had worn on her signet. But that morning I only knew that looking at it made my teeth ache, the way biting tin foil does, and that the cold coming off the paper was not metaphorical. Frost feathered across the smokehouse wall where the map leaned.

That was when I understood—or began to, the understanding arriving in pieces like a dropped plate—the thing I should have understood at the market gate.

Magic couldn't enter the Thornvale. Everyone knew that. It was the valley's founding boast, carved over the gatehouse: *Here the world's wonders end.* What nobody said out loud, because nobody had ever needed to, was that the arrangement worked both ways. Nothing touched by magic could stay either. The wards didn't keep the world out. They kept us *in*—kept the valley clean, kept it sealed, kept it a jar with the lid screwed down tight.

And I was carrying contraband. I was carrying a thing the valley intended to spit back out.

* * *

They came on the fourth day. Crown enforcers, six of them, grey-cloaked, riding through the market gate with the particular unhurried confidence of men who have never once been told no. Their horses' hooves struck sparks off the cobbles, which should have been my first warning—iron shod that way doesn't spark on stone—but I was elbow-deep in Lord Merrick's breakfast trout and heard about it secondhand from the fishmonger's boy, breathless, wide-eyed: *they're asking after a courier, Bram, they're asking door to door.*

I did what I always do, which is nothing visible. I finished the trout. I plated it. I stood behind Lord Merrick's chair while he demolished it and complained that the sorrel sauce had lost its edge, and I agreed with him, because agreeing with Lord Merrick was eleven years of my life distilled into a single professional skill. My hands did not shake. I have excellent hands. It is the one part of the job they train you for properly: steady hands, a blank face, and a tongue that will tell you the difference between hemlock and wild carrot before your heart has even finished deciding to pound.

But underneath the blank face I was doing arithmetic. Six enforcers. A courier who had come through the wardline—which he shouldn't have been able to do, which meant either the wards were failing or he'd had help—and died at my feet with a map in his fists and a dead king on his lips. They would ask who found him. The whole market had seen me kneel. There was no version of this where I stayed invisible, and invisibility had been my life's work, my art, my religion. I had built myself into the wallpaper of the Thornvale, and now the wallpaper was being peeled back to look for what was behind it.

I went to Old Tam.

Everyone goes to Old Tam eventually. He had been the valley's hedge-healer since before I was born, the man you visited for fevers and broken bones and unwanted pregnancies and, once, memorably, a curse that turned out to be new boots worn too long. He had taught me half of what I knew about plants and most of what I knew about poisons, back when I was apprenticed and hungry for anything that felt like power. He was eighty, bent as a question mark, and he had buried every person he'd ever loved, which gave him a certain economy of speech.

He listened while I told him everything—the courier, the shadow-blood, the map, the frost on the smokehouse wall. He didn't interrupt. When I finished he poured two cups of nettle tea and pushed one across the table, and I noticed his hand wasn't steady, and Tam's hands were always steady.

"You should burn it," he said.

"I tried. It won't take flame. And I don't think—" I wrapped my hands around the cup. "Tam, I don't think it wants to be burned. I think it wants to be *carried*."

"Things usually do." He looked at me for a long moment with his milky old eyes. "You know what you are, girl?"

"A taster."

"That's what they told you." He said it lightly, but something underneath it wasn't light at all, and before I could ask—and I would spend a great deal of the road ahead wishing I had asked—the knock came at his door.

Not a knock, really. A fist. Three blows like nails going into a coffin lid.

I was in the back room before the second blow landed, because Tam had jerked his chin toward it without turning his head, an old man's sleight of hand performed over teacups, and I went because some part of me had already known I would. Through the gap between the cupboard and the wall I watched him open the door to the grey cloaks, watched them fill his little room like smoke filling a chimney, watched the tall one unfold a scrap of parchment with a sketch on it.

A face. My face, drawn badly, which somehow made it worse.

"Seen this woman?" the tall one asked. "She may have come into possession of crown property. Stolen property."

"She comes for herbs sometimes," Tam said. "Arthritis. Bad knees." A pause you could have balanced a coin on. "Haven't seen her since the week before last."

The lie was perfect. Calm, specific, boring. Eleven years I'd spent learning to lie with a straight face, and here was the master giving a free lesson with his life as tuition. Because they knew. Of course they knew—the market had eyes, the enforcers had ways, and a sketch means someone described me, means someone talked. Tam lied anyway, beautifully, pointlessly, and I stood in his back room smelling dried yarrow and learning the difference between courage and stubbornness, which is that courage knows the price in advance.

They took him anyway. Not for lying—they never even proved the lie. They took him because taking someone makes the next person talk, and because an old hedge-witch with no family was the cheapest lesson available. I heard it from the back room: the scuffle, brief and businesslike; the tall one saying something about *obstruction* and *the crown's patience*; and then Tam's voice, clear and unafraid and pitched deliberately loud, the way a man speaks when he knows the walls have ears and wants them to carry:

"Kitchen's stocked for winter! Don't let the feverfew go to seed!"

Which meant nothing. Which meant everything. Which meant, translated from Tam: *run, girl, and don't look back, and mind the feverfew.*

They marched him out past my hiding place, and I looked at him through the gap, and he looked at the cupboard, and neither of us saw the other, and that was the last time I ever saw Old Tam alive. They hanged him at the crossroads at dusk for obstruction of crown inquiry, after a trial that lasted about as long as it takes to boil an egg. I know because I watched from the ridge above the crossroads, too far away to do anything but memorize it, which is what I'm good at. Memorizing. Tasting. Keeping things down.

* * *

I left at dawn.

Not because I was brave. Let the record show I left at dawn because I was cornered, and grief does funny arithmetic in a person, and somewhere between the hanging and the first grey light I stopped calculating how to stay safe and started calculating how to make them pay, and those are not the same calculation but they wear each other's coats.

I packed like a taster, which is to say like a poisoner: honey of poppy, dried foxglove, oleander leaf, a stoppered vial of distilled nightshade that Tam had made me swear never to use on myself. I took the coat with the map in its lining. I took his feverfew, because he'd told me to mind it, and I am nothing if not obedient to the dead.

The wardline ran along the northern ridge, a seam in the world you couldn't see but could feel—a pressure in the ears, a taste like copper pennies at the back of the throat. Every child in the valley learned to feel it the way fish learn water. Crossing it was unthinkable. Crossing it was exile. The valley would close behind you like water behind a stone, and whatever waited outside would have you, body and blood, forever.

I stood at the seam with the sun not yet up and the frost smoking off the bracken, and I was afraid. Properly afraid, teeth-chattering, bladder-clenching afraid, none of this literary trembling. And I thought about Tam's face at the cupboard, and the courier's hand pressing the map into mine with the last strength he had, and a king I'd never met who was supposedly already dead, and I thought: *someone has to taste this.*

Because that's the job, really. That's all it ever was. Someone stands between the powerful and the poisoned dish, and swallows first, and finds out.

I stepped through the wardline.

It went through me like cold water through cloth, like a bell rung inside my skull, and the taste of copper flooded my mouth and then broke apart into something else—something vast and green and utterly unfamiliar, the taste of a world with magic in it, thick and wild and terrifying, like drinking a forest.

Behind me, the Thornvale sealed itself shut. Ahead, the road ran north toward the capital, toward a dead-alive king and the thing wearing his crown.

Somewhere on that road, I would later learn, trouble was already waiting for me in the form of an apple.

But that came later. That morning there was only the walking, and the cold map against my ribs, and the strange discovery that exile, once chosen, feels less like falling than like finally exhaling.
