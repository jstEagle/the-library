## 3. A Road of Small Poisons

The first thing the Thornvale ever taught me was that everything is a matter of dose. Honey is a poison if you eat enough of it. Nightshade is a medicine if you don't. And a life can be perfectly survivable right up until it isn't.

I crossed the wardline at dawn with my mentor's blood still under my fingernails and a map in my coat that burned cold as a river stone held all winter. I did not look back at the valley. Looking back felt like something a person in a story would do, and I had no intention of becoming a story. I intended to become a rumor, then a memory, then nothing at all.

The map had other plans.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about leaving a valley where magic cannot enter: the world outside has a taste.

I noticed it by midday, walking the old king's road through country I'd only ever seen from the high pastures. The air tasted faintly sweet, like overripe pears left too long in a warm room. Not rot exactly. Something older than rot. Something patient.

In the Thornvale, air tastes of nothing but itself. That's the whole point of the place — a valley scrubbed clean, warded down to its bedrock, a sanctuary for people who wanted magic to stay on the far side of the mountains the way you want wasps to stay out of your kitchen. I grew up believing the outside world was simply more of the same, just bigger and less tidy.

It is not. The outside world hums. And now that I was listening with the only sense I've ever trusted, I could hear it humming in my mouth.

I stopped at a stream to fill my flask and caught it: a thread of bitterness under the cold water, green and metallic, like chewing a willow bark and a copper coin together. Upstream, somewhere above the bend, something was bleeding into the water. I crouched there a long moment, tasting the current the way other people listen for footsteps.

I didn't drink. I filled the flask anyway, because an empty flask is just a bottle, but a full one is evidence.

That was the moment I understood — really understood, not the way you understand a thing from a book — that my tongue was not a servant's tool. It was a lantern. And I had spent twenty-two years using it to light other people's dinners.

* * *

That night, in a palace far to the east, a king dreamed he was drowning in his own garden.

He dreamed in roots. He dreamed of soil pressing up through marble floors, of roses blooming black along the throne room walls, of his wife's voice saying *Aldric, wake up, Aldric, it's wearing you* — and then her voice changing, thickening, becoming the crown's voice, saying *she is dead, she has always been dead, sleep, sleep, the realm needs you asleep.*

In the dream he tried to answer her. What came out instead was the smell of iron and loam, and somewhere very far away, in a body that still technically belonged to him, a man who tasted of ash opened his eyes and asked for wine he would never be allowed to drink.

* * *

The ambush, when it came, announced itself three ways, and I am proud to say I caught two of them.

First: the birdsong stopped. Not gradually, the way birdsong usually dies at dusk, but all at once, like a door closing on a room full of singers. Second: the air changed. That pear-sweetness sharpened into something acrid, chemical, a bite at the back of the throat I recognized from the taster's hall — hemlock's cousin, or something bred from it, aerosolized somehow, hanging in the tree shade ahead of me like weather.

Third — the one I missed — was the man behind me.

"Evening," he said, which it was not; it was barely noon. "You'll want to breathe shallow."

"I've been breathing shallow since the bird thing," I said. "Who are you?"

"The last friendly face you're going to meet on this road." He stepped around me into view: big, gray-bearded, dressed like a carter but standing like a soldier. Behind him, two shapes detached themselves from the treeline, moving with the loose-jointed wrongness of people whose bodies were being operated by someone else. Their collars gaped, and in the hollows of their throats I saw scars — thin, branching, dark, like thorns pressed into skin and left to heal badly.

Thorn-scars. I filed that away with the cold-burning map and the sweet-rot air, in the growing drawer of things I did not understand and refused to be frightened of yet.

"You're the courier's contact," the big man said. "The girl from the valley. Give me the map and I'll let you walk back the way you came."

"The courier's dead," I said. "Bled shadow. So either you killed him, or whatever you work for did, and either way I'm not handing anything to you."

"Brave," he said, without admiration. "The apple, then."

One of the thorn-scarred men held out an apple. Red, glossy, perfect, the kind of fruit that doesn't grow this far north without help. My mouth watered before my brain caught up, and that was the tell. Real hunger doesn't arrive ahead of the food. That apple was *calling* to me, the way a lamp calls to a moth, and underneath the sweetness of its scent ran a note I knew intimately — bitter almond, faint as a whisper in another room.

Cyanide wears perfume. I'd tasted it eleven times in my career, always in time, always in someone else's kitchen, always for someone else's sake.

"No," I said.

"It's just an apple."

"It's an apple dipped in something that makes you want it, laced with enough bitter almond to stop a horse, and I'd bet my boots the seeds are dosed too, in case anyone survives the flesh." I looked at him. "I taste for a living. Or I did. You picked the wrong girl to feed."

Something flickered across his face — surprise, then, unbelievably, respect. "Nobody's called the compote before," he said. "Not once in six years."

"Then your poisoner's gotten lazy."

"My poisoner," he said, "is a *plant*."

And that was when the thorn-scarred men moved, fast and silent, and I learned that knowing a poison exists and being able to do something about it are two entirely different professions.

* * *

I survived the next ninety seconds through a combination of cowardice, geography, and hemlock.

The airborne stuff hung thickest under the trees, so I ran for open ground, holding my breath until my vision sparked. One of the scarred men grabbed my arm and I bit him — hard, on the wrist, through the skin — and whatever was in his blood tasted like the stream water, that willow-and-copper bitterness, concentrated. He flinched as if I'd burned him. Interesting. File that away too.

Then a rock the size of a loaf hit the second man in the temple, and a woman's voice said, conversationally, "Downwind, both of you, honestly, amateur hour," and the whole scene rearranged itself.

She was small, sharp-faced, maybe fifty, with ink-stained fingers and the posture of someone who had been thrown out of better rooms than this. She carried a satchel and a short iron rod, and she used the rod on the first scarred man's neck with a precision that suggested she'd done it many times and enjoyed it moderately.

The big carter-soldier took one look at her and swore with feeling. "Wren. They said you were dead."

"They say that about everyone useful." She looked at me, and her eyes went straight to my coat, to the shape of the map against my ribs, and narrowed. "You're carrying something sealed in white wax with a thorn pressed into it. Don't ask how I know. Show me."

"Why?"

"Because I'm the only person on this road who isn't trying to kill you, and because I spent nine years as physician to the queen who made that seal, and she's dead, and I have questions that keep me up at night." Her voice didn't shake. Her hands did, very slightly, and I trusted the hands more.

The big man cleared his throat. "This is a bad idea."

"Most good ones are," Wren said. "Go home, Torvald. Tell them the girl died at the ford. It's nearly true; the girl she was won't survive the week."

He hesitated, looked at me with something almost like pity, and left. The scarred men lay where they'd fallen, breathing slow, dreaming whatever thorned things dreamed.

Wren turned back to me. "Well? The seal."

I took out the map. When the wax caught the light, she made a sound like a person being told a diagnosis they'd already guessed.

"Where did you get this?"

"A dying courier. He said—" I heard myself say it aloud for the first time, and it sounded worse than it had in my head, which I hadn't thought possible. "He said the king is already dead."

"He's not," Wren said quietly. "I'd know. I've been trying to reach him for a year." She rolled the map closed and handed it back with a care that bordered on reverence. "The queen hid this. She must have known what the crown would do to her records, her letters, everyone who loved her. She sent it *out*, past the wards, into the one place it couldn't be found." Her eyes came up to mine. "Into the valley. To someone clean."

"I'm not clean, I'm a cook's daughter with a good palate."

"Mm." Wren smiled, and it wasn't a kind smile; it was the smile of someone doing arithmetic. "Your palate just dismantled a compote that's killed four trained agents. Come with me to the capital, taster. I can't get within a mile of the palace — they know my face. But kitchens hire. Kitchens forget. And you, apparently, are the most dangerous tongue in the northern provinces."

I should have said no. Every instinct I'd honed for two decades — stay invisible, stay safe, stay small — said no, said turn around, said the valley is gone but you can build a smaller valley anywhere, a cellar, a corner, a life.

But the map burned cold against my ribs, and the air tasted of pear-rot and patience, and somewhere east a man who tasted of ash was asking for wine.

"All right," I said. "Passage, food, and answers. In that order."

"Answers last," Wren agreed. "They always come last. That's how you know they're real."

We walked east, toward the capital, toward the smell of iron and loam. Behind us the poisoned apples gleamed in the grass like a warning no one had written down yet.

And far away, in a garden that grew only inside his skull, the king dreamed of a girl with a lantern for a tongue, and for the first time in a year, the roots loosened their grip just enough to let him wonder why.
