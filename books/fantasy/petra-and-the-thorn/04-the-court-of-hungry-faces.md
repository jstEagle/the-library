## 4. The Court of Hungry Faces

I had forgotten how the capital smells.

Not the filth—filth I remembered, and filth is honest. It was the sweetness underneath it that turned my stomach on our first morning through the gates: honeyed smoke from a hundred braziers, candied fruit, perfumed oil burning in the temple lamps. The city had been dosed like a difficult patient, sweetened to hide the taste of something bitter underneath. A physician's trick. I knew every one of its variations, because I had once used them myself.

"Smile," I murmured to Bram as we passed under the Gate of Bells. "You're my cousin's widow from the coast, come to seek service. Grieving but hopeful."

"I am grieving," she said. "Merrin's dead."

"Then you're perfectly cast. Just aim it at your feet instead of at everyone who looks at you."

She aimed it at her feet. It was, I reflected not for the first time since the Thornvale road, an unnerving education watching Bram Crane learn to perform. In the valley she had been invisible by instinct—a woman who could stand in a room and be furniture. Here, walking into the teeth of the thing that had killed her mentor, she had discovered ambition the way other people discover religion: suddenly, completely, and with a frightening willingness to suffer for it.

The bells overhead rang noon, and the sound came out wrong. I stopped in the middle of the street to listen, which earned me a shove from a porter and a glare from Bram.

"What?" she said.

"When I left this city," I said, "the noon bells were bronze. That's iron." I let the crowd carry us onward before I could say anything more foolish. Iron rings flat. Iron rings the way a struck cage rings. But everyone around us went about their business, haggling over eels and ribbon, and no one else so much as glanced up.

*They've lived with it long enough to stop hearing it,* I thought. *That's the first symptom. Not the fever. The not-noticing.*

* * *

I took her through the palace district by way of the servants' lanes, which was where I'd done most of my living anyway, back when I still had a title and both my hands unburned. And it was there, in the broad cobbled yard behind the kitchens, waiting for the bread ovens to cool, that I watched the court go by—and understood, finally, what we had walked into.

Nobles don't use servants' yards. But these did. They strolled through like it was a garden, silk hems dragging through flour dust, and they smiled at the scullery girls, and the girls smiled back, and nobody's smile ever ended.

"Look at their collars," Bram said quietly.

I had already seen them. At first I'd taken them for fashion—the high lace collars were fashionable this season, I supposed—but fashion doesn't sit identically on forty different throats. Underneath the lace, at the soft place where neck meets shoulder, each of them wore the same mark: a small pale starburst, like a scar from a burn, or the imprint of something pressed against the skin for a very long time.

Thorn-scars. I'd read the queen's marginalia often enough to know the word when I saw its evidence. *Rooting sites,* she had written, in a hand that got shakier toward the end of her notebooks. *It plants itself where the blood runs shallow.*

"It's not just him," Bram said. Her voice was steady. Her hands weren't; she'd tucked them into her sleeves, which was her version of screaming. "Wren. It's not just the king."

"No."

"The whole court."

"The whole court," I agreed, "and likely half the city, and the city half the realm, all the way down until you reach a valley where none of it can follow." I nodded at a young lordling crossing the yard, beautiful as a painted saint, smiling at a kitchen boy with his whole face while his eyes tracked nothing at all. "That's Lord Aldous Vane. He used to gamble. Badly, loudly, wonderfully. Now he smiles at scullery boys in a flour yard. Something is wearing these people the way a glove wears a hand, and it started with the crown and spread outward like damp through plaster."

Bram watched the lordling go. "And no one runs?"

"Where would they run? Out past the walls, into the wilds, to some magicless hole in the hills?" I heard my own voice go dry and bitter and didn't trouble to fix it. "Most people would rather be gently eaten than be free and frightened. Besides—" I gestured at the yard, the ovens, the endless industry of feeding a palace, "—everyone here is fed. Everyone is safe. Everyone is *smiling*. You can't raise a rebellion against comfort, Bram. Believe me. I've tried to talk patients out of comfortable poisons, and I lost more often than I won."

A servant girl drifted past us then, carrying a tray of little honeyed cakes, and she smiled at Bram—a long, warm, unhurried smile, held two beats past courtesy, three, four—and Bram smiled back with the flawless blankness of a woman who had spent her life being overlooked, and the girl moved on.

Neither of us spoke until she was gone.

"They smile too long," Bram said.

"Yes."

"How long have they been doing that?"

"A year," I said. "Give or take."

* * *

Everyone will tell you the queen died a year ago. No one will tell you how.

I learned this within an hour of setting foot inside the palace proper, because I made it my business to ask, and asking is a skill I retained even after they took my title. I asked a chamberlain, who told me the queen had died of a wasting fever and then changed the subject to the price of candles. I asked a laundress, who told me the queen had died in her sleep and then looked over her shoulder as though the linens might be listening. I asked an old gate sergeant I'd known since he was a boy stealing apples from my physic garden, and he gripped my arm hard enough to bruise and said, "Master Wren, if you're wise you'll ask nothing in this place," and then he smiled at me, long and warm and unhurried, and let me go, and I stood in the gateway feeling like a man who has found a hair in his soup and realized the hair is his own.

The official story, stitched together from a dozen evasions, ran thus: Queen Isolde had been frail. She had kept to her rooms. She had loved gardens. She had died in autumn, quietly, and the king had grieved terribly, and the realm had moved on, and wasn't it a lovely morning.

What no one mentioned: that the queen's physic garden had been razed to the gravel. That her apartments remained sealed, dust on the door handles, while every other room in the palace was scrubbed daily by smiling hands. That the royal physicians' rolls showed six names struck through in the last year, mine among them, and that the remaining physicians had all taken lodgings *inside* the palace now, and did not leave it, and smiled.

"She was mapping it," Bram said that night, in the narrow room above a chandler's shop that I'd rented with the last of my disgraced-physician savings. She sat cross-legged on the floor with the courier's map open between us, her fingers hovering over the inked corridors without touching them. "Your queen. The map isn't a treasure chart. It's a diagnosis. Look—" she traced a passage from the servants' stair to the heart of the palace, where the cartographer's ink darkened almost to black, "—it gets darker toward the center. Toward him. She drew the disease."

"And then she died of it," I said. "Or was cured of it. With this court, the two are indistinguishable."

Bram was quiet a moment. Firelight moved on her face, and I saw her do the thing she did—tuck the fear down somewhere below her ribs and sit on it. "Then I need to get to the center," she said. "To the king's table."

"You need to get to the king's table," I repeated. "Which is guarded by men who don't blink, fed by cooks who smile too long, and tasted by a guild that bred you like a prize sow and never told you. Yes. Splendid plan. Let me fetch my hat."

"You have a better one?"

I did not have a better one. What I had was twenty years of court medicine, a face half the old household still vaguely remembered, and a talent for being underestimated, which is the only fortune that never fails a man. So the next morning I put on my least impressive coat, combed my burned hand into obscurity beneath a sleeve, and presented myself and my "grieving cousin from the coast" to the mistress of the palace kitchens—a vast, brisk woman named Dame Ottelie whose smile, thank every merciful power, was merely professional.

"Taster, is she?" Dame Ottelie looked Bram up and down the way a horse-trader looks at a yearling. "Small. Valley-born?"

"Clean stock," I said, and hated myself for the phrase, and used it anyway, because it was the coin this court traded in. "No vices, no lovers, no family to speak of. Trained palate. She can name eleven nightshade derivatives blindfolded."

"Can you, girl?"

"Fourteen," Bram said.

Dame Ottelie's eyebrows rose a fraction. "Cocky. Good. Cocky tasters live longer than humble ones—they check twice." She made a mark in her ledger. "King's table needs a third chair filled since Master Corvin took ill. You'll taste everything before it passes the screen. Everything. You'll report anything odd to no one but me. And girl—" she leaned close, and for one moment her briskness cracked and something old and tired looked out of her eyes, "—whatever you see up there, however strange the king seems, you taste your portion first and you keep chewing. Understand? Keep chewing. The ones who stop chewing get noticed."

"I understand," Bram said.

And that was that. By evening she had a grey apron, a pallet behind the spice room, and a place in the third chair at the tasting screen, thirty feet from the throne where the King of the Thornvale sat nightly amid his smiling court, eating nothing himself, while the black crown drank whatever light was left in his eyes.

* * *

I write this part reluctantly, because it shames me.

On Bram's first night of service, I waited outside the kitchen yard to walk her home, and while I waited I watched the court file out of the great hall after supper, and I caught myself smiling at them. Long. Warm. Unhurried. Two beats past courtesy. Three.

I bit the inside of my cheek until I tasted blood, and the smile died, and I stood in the cold shaking. Nothing had touched me. Nothing had rooted in me—I checked, that night and every night since, at the shallow places, the throat, the wrist, the soft skin at the collarbone. There was nothing there. It was only that the court smiles the way water is wet; stand in it long enough and you forget you're dry.

That is what I want set down plainly, whoever reads this: the corruption is not a serpent coiled in one man's crown. It is the weather. It is the bells rung in iron, the cakes laced with something sweet, the whole city breathing in time. We had come hunting a monster, and instead we had moved into its bloodstream.

When Bram came out of the yard, grey-faced under her new apron, I expected her to say she'd seen the king. She hadn't; the screen was too far, the hall too dim. But she took my arm—she had never taken my arm before—and walked half a block in silence before she spoke.

"Wren. The food tonight. All of it, every dish, the whole king's table." Her voice was very level, the way it went when she was naming a poison. "It tasted perfect. Every single dish. Perfectly balanced, perfectly seasoned, better than anything I've ever tasted in my life."

"That's a complaint?"

"It's a confession," she said. "Nothing tastes perfect, Wren. Perfection means someone adjusted every flavor to exactly what the eater most wants. Someone in that kitchen knows what every person at that table desires best—and is feeding it to them." She looked back toward the palace, where the iron bells were ringing the hour, flat as a struck cage. "It's not poisoning them. It's *feeding* them. And tomorrow I have to sit in the third chair and eat it and smile."

She said the last word like a woman naming the disease she'd been diagnosed with.

We walked on. Behind us the palace glowed against the dark like a hearth, warm and golden and full of hungry faces—and somewhere at its blackened heart, behind sealed doors and raked gravel, the rooms where a queen had drawn her own death in ink waited for someone brave enough, or stupid enough, or bred enough, to open them.

Three days later, Dame Ottelie summoned Bram from the spice room with a look on her face I had never seen her wear—not brisk, not smiling, but afraid.

"The king," she said, "has asked for the new taster. By name."
