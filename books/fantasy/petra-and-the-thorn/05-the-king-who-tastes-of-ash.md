## 5. The King Who Tastes of Ash

They came for me at the hour when the kitchens smell most like forgiveness—bread out of the oven, onions gone sweet in butter—and I was elbow-deep in a vat of plum preserves, deciding whether the cook had tried to poison anyone or simply hated us all.

"Bram Crane." The voice belonged to a steward with a face like folded linen. "His Majesty requires a taster at his private table."

"His Majesty has tasters," I said. "I've seen them. They have a whole bench."

"Their Majesties' bench," he said, with the particular emphasis court people use when they want you to notice what they've removed from a sentence, "is being reorganized."

Wren, chopping herbs three feet away, did not look up. But his knife slowed, just slightly, the way a man's breathing slows when he's listening with his whole spine. He'd been telling me for two weeks that the king never dined publicly anymore. That the king's meals went up on covered trays and came down untouched, or worse, touched. That the last private taster had been promoted to a position no one could name and then stopped appearing anywhere at all.

So this was either an honor or an ending, and in my experience the two traveled together like old friends.

"I'll need to wash the plums off," I said.

"You'll need to hurry," he said.

* * *

The king's private dining room sat at the top of a spiral stair so narrow I had to turn my shoulders sideways, which felt less like architecture and more like a throat. Two guards flanked the door at the top. Neither met my eyes. Both had thin red lines at their collars, half-healed, like something had tried to grow out of them and been persuaded otherwise.

Inside: one table, one chair, one candelabrum, and one king.

I had expected—because everyone expects—an old man. A ruin propped up in ermine. What I got instead was a young man, maybe thirty, dark-haired, sharp-boned, sitting very straight as though straightness were the only thing holding him together. His hands were folded on the table with the deliberate calm of someone who has practiced not shaking.

The crown sat on his head, and it was wrong in a way my tongue understood before my mind did.

It was black iron worked into thorns, each point curling inward toward his skull, and it was not decorative. It was not jewelry. I know poison when I see it, and that crown was poison given permanence—a slow one, patient, the kind you don't taste until it's already made itself at home. It sat against his temples the way a leech sits against skin.

"Your Majesty." I bowed the way Wren had drilled into me. Low enough for respect, shallow enough to keep my eyes working.

"Taste." His voice was quiet and cracked down the middle, like a plate with a hairline fracture that still holds its food. "That's why you're here. Taste."

The first dish arrived by dumbwaiter—a pheasant in cherry sauce, gilded at the edges the way palace food always is, dressed for a job it would never do. I took my spoonful, held it on my tongue, and ran the litany Wren taught me. Bitter at the back? No. Numbness at the tip? No. That faint metallic whisper of arsenic, the almond ghost of cyanide, the green rot of hemlock?

No. Just pheasant, over-salted, undercooked at the thigh. Whoever cooked it hated the king in the ordinary human way.

"It's safe," I said. "Though the thigh is a crime."

Something happened to his face. It took me a moment to recognize it, because I'd seen it so rarely in others and almost never in mirrors: he almost laughed. The laugh died somewhere in transit, but its ghost stayed around his mouth.

"A taster with opinions."

"A taster who's bored," I said. "Boredom makes me honest."

"Eat, then. All of it. If you're going to die for me tonight, you shouldn't do it hungry."

* * *

We ate through seven dishes that way. He watched every swallow I took with an attention that should have been flattering and was instead terrible—the look of a man checking whether the rope will hold. Between courses he spoke, in fragments, when the lucidity was on him.

"You're new," he said, over a soup I pronounced merely disappointing. "The guild sends new ones now. They used to send their best. Now they send whoever won't be missed."

"That's a grim assessment of your own table."

"It's an accurate one." His hand drifted up, touched the edge of the crown, and jerked back as if the iron had bitten him. Maybe it had. "Three tasters before you this year. One wept. One prayed. One asked me for a pardon in advance, which I confess I respected."

"And what did you tell them?"

"To run." He looked at the candle flame instead of me. "None of them ran. Courage is a disease too, I think. Very contagious in this building."

The eighth course was honey cakes, and halfway through tasting them I felt it happen—the moment his lucidity slipped. It was like watching a man go under water. His eyes lost their focus, then their color, going pale as river ice, and his posture changed. The straightness became something else. Rigidity. Puppet-string posture. And the temperature in the room dropped, or I imagined it dropped, the way a cellar drops when you open a door to deeper stone.

When he spoke again, his voice had gravel in it that hadn't been there before.

"She sent you."

My spoon stopped halfway to my mouth. Every instinct I owned—the ones that had kept me alive through twenty years of other people's dinners—screamed at me to say *who*, to play stupid, to be furniture. Furniture survives palaces.

But I'd carried that map against my ribs for a month. I'd bled for it, nearly. And there was something in his ruined voice that wasn't a threat. It was hunger. The specific hunger of a man who has been alone in a locked room for a very long time.

"The courier," I said. "He gave me a map. Sealed in wax. He said—" I hesitated. "He said the king is already dead."

The thing behind Aldric's eyes flickered, furious, and then Aldric surfaced through it like a swimmer breaking water, gasping, gripping the table hard enough to whiten his knuckles.

"He was right," the king said. "He was right, and she knew it before any of us. Gods, Elira." He said the name the way you say a prayer you've stopped believing will be answered. "She made that map. She drew it herself, in the last month, when her hands were already failing. Did it look like her work? Little birds in the margins?"

"There were birds," I said quietly. "In the corners. I thought they were decoration."

"They were directions." He laughed, and it was a horrible sound, joy and grief fighting over one throat. "Everything she did was a direction. Marry me—direction. Build the wards—direction. Die—" His breath caught. "Die, that was a direction too."

"Your Majesty—"

"Don't call me that." He was leaning forward now, and up close I could see what the candlelight had hidden: the gray in him. Not age-gray. Absence-gray. Like a house where the family left years ago but someone still keeps the fires lit. "Listen to me, taster. Whatever your name is."

"Bram."

"Bram." He tested it like a coin between his teeth. "Between dishes, while your mouth is still clean, I'm going to ask you something, and I need you to hear all of it before the fear arrives. Because the fear will arrive. It always does."

I waited. My heart was doing something unprofessional.

"This crown eats," he said. "That's what it's for. It was made to protect the realm—to take everything dangerous and hold it, and it holds it the way a dam holds a river, except dams don't get thirsty. It drinks. It started with my anger, my grief, my doubt—all the things a king isn't allowed. Then it took my friends' faces. Then music. Then the taste of food—I haven't tasted anything in two years, Bram, I only watch other people eat. Then it took her." His voice didn't break. It went flat, which is worse. "It drank my love for her until there was nothing left, and I had to watch myself stop loving her in real time, and I couldn't even mourn her properly because mourning was next on the menu."

The candles guttered. Neither of us moved.

"So here is what I'm asking." He fixed those ice-pale eyes on mine, and underneath the gray there was still a man, young and terrified and ferociously clear for this one borrowed moment. "There will come a night—it's coming soon, I can feel it rooting deeper—when the crown finishes with me and starts wearing me outright. When that happens, whatever is left on this throne will not be Aldric. It will be hungry, and clever, and it will have my hands and my armies. You seem like a woman who finds doors where other people find walls. So find one. And when you find it, don't look back, and don't"—his grip tightened on the table—"don't try to save me. Let me die. That's the mercy, Bram. That's the only mercy left in this room, and I am begging you for it across a dinner table like a beggar, because I cannot even kneel anymore. The crown doesn't let me kneel. It says kings don't kneel."

The ninth course arrived. Roast quail, glazed, absurdly beautiful. I tasted it because my hands needed something to do and because if I didn't keep performing the ritual I was going to shatter along some seam I couldn't afford to shatter along.

Safe. Everything was safe. Everything had been safe all evening, and that was the worst part—that the poison wasn't in the food, it was in the chair, in the iron, in the slow gray drinking of a man who wanted permission to stop.

"I'm not good at letting things die," I heard myself say. "I taste poison for a living. My entire life is refusing to let things kill other things. It's the only skill I have."

"It's not a skill I'm asking for."

"No," I said. "You're asking me to leave a man alone in a locked room with a leech on his head, because leaving is easier than staying. I've done easy my whole life, Your Majesty. I was invisible for twenty years and called it safety." I set down the spoon. "Ask me something harder."

For a moment he just stared at me, and the crown's thorns seemed to lean in, listening, and the temperature dropped again—but this time Aldric fought back up through the cold, and what surfaced on his face wasn't hope exactly. Hope is confident. This was more fragile than that. This was a man seeing a door he'd stopped believing in.

"You shouldn't have said that," he whispered.

"Why not?"

"Because it heard you."

The candles went out—not blown out, *drained*, light pulled from them like marrow from bone—and in the sudden dark I heard the crown settle against his skull with a sound like roots splitting stone, and Aldric's voice came back changed, layered, many-throated:

"*Leave the taster. She stays.*"

And somewhere below us, faint through the floor, I heard a door open, and running feet, and Wren's voice calling my name.
