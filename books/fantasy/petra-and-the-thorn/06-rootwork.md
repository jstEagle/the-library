## 6. Rootwork

I had been a court physician for eleven years, and in all that time I had learned one iron law of the palace: the truth never lives where it is supposed to. It hides in the ledgers of the kitchen, in the margins of hymnals, in the private correspondence of dead queens. So when I went looking for what killed Queen Isolde—or rather, what she had died *of*, which are not always the same thing—I did not go to the royal archive, with its velvet chairs and its archivists who smile too long. I went to her old apartments, which had been sealed since her death and which the crown, in its arrogance, had never bothered to search.

The crown does not read paper. The crown reads people. Paper is beneath its notice, and that, thank every indifferent god, is its blind spot.

I told Bram I was going to check on a patient. She was in the undercellars of the Almoner's Row tenement we'd holed up in, doing something unspeakable to a pot of thorn-leaf extract, so she didn't ask questions. This was one of the many things I liked about her: she understood that some lies are load-bearing.

The queen's apartments were above the east gallery, behind a door locked with a mechanism older than the dynasty. I picked it in forty minutes, which would have embarrassed me at thirty and merely annoyed me now. Inside, dust lay over everything like a second set of sheets. A dressing table. A half-finished embroidery—wrens, ironically, though I choose to believe she did not know my name. And a writing desk with a false bottom, because dead queens who die mysteriously always have false bottoms; it is practically heraldic.

Inside the false bottom: papers. Dozens of them, in a hand I recognized from the map Bram carried—the same slanted, urgent script, as if the writer were always afraid of being interrupted mid-sentence. Which, given how her story ended, she may well have been.

I sat down on the floor of a dead woman's bedroom and began to read.

* * *

Here is what I knew before that night, so you understand the shape of what I learned.

I knew the Thorn-Crown was old. Older than Aldric's grandfather, older than the wardline around the Thornvale, old enough that its origin had passed out of history and into liturgy. The priests said it was a blessing. The soldiers said it was a weapon. The servants said nothing at all, because servants know better than to say anything about the thing their king wears on his head.

I knew the king had changed. Slowly, then suddenly—the way rot works. Ten years ago he'd been sharp, funny, occasionally insufferable in the way intelligent men are permitted to be. Five years ago he'd begun forgetting things. Two years ago he stopped sleeping. Last year his wife died, and after that he was only lucid in fragments, like a man surfacing from deep water long enough to scream.

And I knew why I had been disgraced. That part I will admit freely, since no one else is here to hear it: I told the Lord Protector that the king's illness looked like poisoning, and I named the poison, and I suggested we look for the poisoner somewhere closer to the throne than the kitchens. Three days later I was stripped of my office and lucky to keep my tongue. The crown protects itself. I simply hadn't yet understood how literally.

So. The papers.

The earliest ones were letters between Isolde and someone she called only "R., of the Guild." The Tasters' Guild, I assumed, though the guild's public face is a certification office—a place that stamps wax seals on men who sip soup for a living. The letters were not about soup.

*"You confirm the third generation holds,"* R. wrote. *"Clean blood, clean bone. The valley stock takes the doses without scarring. Begin the fourth pairing."*

Pairing. As in horses. As in hounds.

I am not a sentimental man. I have held dying children and told their mothers comforting arithmetic; sentiment left me years ago, or I sent it away, I'm honestly not sure which of us left first. But I read those letters three times before my hands would stop shaking enough to turn the page, and I want you to understand that the shaking was not grief. It was recognition. Because I remembered the tasters I'd known at court. Quiet ones. Watchful ones. Almost all of them from the valley families, almost all of them orphaned young, almost all of them with that particular stillness—people who had been made small on purpose, the way you prune a tree to control what it becomes.

We called it temperament. We called it discipline. We called it a gift for the work.

It was breeding. Eleven generations of it, if the ledger tucked beneath the letters was honest, and ledgers are honest the way knives are honest: they cut whoever opens them. Names, birth records, dose schedules. Not poisons—no, the early entries were mundane, arsenic and foxglove and the usual alphabet of the trade—but further down, in Isolde's own hand, the entries changed. *Thorn-leaf, distilled. Root-ash. Crown-tincture.* Substances that should not have names, dosed into infants in drops, into children in spoonfuls, building tolerance the way you build any tolerance: by nearly killing the patient, repeatedly, on schedule.

They weren't training tasters. They were growing vessels. People whose bodies could hold the crown's magic the way a glove holds a hand—and, more importantly, could refuse to hold it. Immunity, but not just to poison. To *binding*. The crown roots in flesh; these children were grown in soil where it could not take.

And the last entry, dated nineteen years ago, was a single line:

*"B.C. born. Viable. The line ends with her—one way or the other."*

Bram Crane. Nineteen years old. Born in the Thornvale. Raised in silence, fed on small poisons, kept invisible, kept safe, kept *spare*—and every kindness anyone ever showed her, every accident of her survival, every talent she thought was hers, had been scheduled by strangers two generations dead.

She thought she had built an ordinary life. She had been *assigned* one.

* * *

There was more. There is always more; cruelty is a thorough administrator.

Isolde's later papers laid out the crown's true history, and I will give it to you as plainly as she gave it to me, because she wrote plainly, the way people do when they expect to be murdered for it.

The crown was made four hundred years ago to end the Sundering Wars. Its makers bound into it a protective intelligence—a guardian, they called it, though Isolde preferred *warden*—charged with defending the realm against magical harm. And it did. It ended the wars. It burned the sorcerer-kings' armies out of the sky. It was magnificent, and it worked perfectly, and that was the problem, because nobody had taught it when to stop.

A guardian with no judgment guards everything. A shield that cannot lower smothers what it covers. Over centuries the warden's protection tightened—from the realm, to the capital, to the court, to the king himself—until protecting Aldric meant consuming him. His memories went first, because memories can be disloyal. Then his loves, because love reaches outside the walls. The queen tried to stop it. The queen, I now suspect, was the one who built the vessel program into something that might actually end it—not to preserve the crown, but to break it. Her last letter to R. says exactly that:

*"If the warden cannot be reasoned with, it must be unmade. The vessel must take the binding into herself and shatter it from within. Tell her nothing until she must know. A planned life is bearable; a planned death is not. Let her have her years."*

Let her have her years. Generous of her. Like giving a lamb the run of the meadow right up until the festival.

I sat there among the papers with the candle guttering and felt something I hadn't felt since my disgrace, and it took me a moment to name it, because it wasn't anger, exactly. It was shame. I had served this court for eleven years. I had tasted the king's medicines, signed his charts, watched him disappear by degrees, and I had blamed poison, madness, grief—anything but the glittering thing on his head, because looking directly at the answer would have required me to admit that the whole glittering structure I belonged to was a cage, and that I was a bird who had made peace with the bars.

The crown protects the realm the way a cage protects a bird. Isolde wrote that sentence herself, near the end, and I copied it out onto my cuff in case I lost the papers, because some truths need to be worn against the skin.

* * *

Bram found me at dawn. Of course she did. She has the instincts of a cat and the sleep schedule of a worse cat.

"You've been gone all night," she said, standing in the doorway of my room with two cups of something that smelled like it could strip varnish. "Your patient die?"

"My patient," I said, "has been dying for four hundred years."

I should have eased into it. I had rehearsed easing into it, all the way home through the grey streets—start with the history, give her the wars, let the cage metaphor do its gentle work. But she stood there holding the cups, tired and wary and trusting me more than she has any rational reason to, and the rehearsed version died in my throat.

"Sit down," I said instead.

She didn't. She never does. "Wren. What did you find?"

I spread the papers on the table between us. Her eyes went to the handwriting first—she knows that hand better than I do; it's inked into the map she carries like a scar—and I watched her understand, before I said a word, that the dead woman who drew her map had also drawn her.

Then I told her. All of it. The guild, the pairings, the ledgers with the dose schedules written beside the birthdays. The thorn-leaf extracts she'd been swallowing since infancy. The word *vessel*. The last line—*the line ends with her, one way or the other*—which I read aloud because hiding it would have been a second betrayal, and she deserved to be lied to by professionals only.

She was very quiet for a long time. Down in the street, a cart rattled past carrying bread to the palace, and neither of us moved.

"So," she said finally, and her voice was flat and steady in the way I'd learn to recognize as her most dangerous register. "My whole life. The valley. The tasting. Even—" she stopped, and I saw her arrive at it, the thing underneath all the other things. "Even the way I am. Careful. Invisible. That wasn't me surviving. That was the *plan* working."

"Yes," I said, because she had earned the ugly word.

"And the map. The courier. Was that the plan too? Did they send him? Am I even—" Her jaw tightened. "Am I choosing any of this?"

"I don't know," I said. "That's the worst of it. Maybe the courier was Isolde's last arrangement. Maybe he was a dying man's panic. Maybe both. The papers don't say, and the only person who could tell us wore shadow for blood and died at a market gate."

She looked at her hands. Taster's hands, steady hands, hands that had been calibrated like instruments since before she could walk. When she spoke again, it was barely above a whisper.

"They grew me for this. Like a tool. Like a *dose*."

"Yes."

And here is the thing I will remember about Bram Crane until whatever passes for my ending: she nodded. Once. She took the news that her entire self had been authored by strangers, and she nodded, and then she pulled out the chair and sat down across from me and said, "Show me the rest. All of it. If I'm somebody's plan, I want to read the whole document."

Not broken. Not running. *Reading.* I have known generals who folded faster.

But as I gathered the pages—the ritual texts, the counter-ritual Isolde had drafted and never finished, the diagrams of the binding's architecture—I noticed something I had missed in the night's reading, and my stomach dropped through the floor.

The final letter from R. of the Guild, dated eleven months ago. After the queen's death. Written in a living hand.

*"The vessel has left the valley. She carries the map. Proceed to Phase Four. The crown must be fed before solstice—either the vessel breaks it, or the vessel completes it. Either outcome serves."*

Either outcome serves. Either outcome *serves*.

Someone out there wanted the crown shattered—and was equally content if it finished eating the king. Someone had let the courier through the wards. Someone was watching us right now, and had been since the market gate.

"Bram," I said slowly, "we need to talk about who else has been reading your story."

Outside, far off toward the palace, a bell began to ring—and it was not the hour bell.
