## 7. The Thing Wearing His Crown

I had tasted death in a hundred small doses, so I told myself I knew its flavor. Bitter almond. Metal and rot. The sweetish wrongness of hemlock dressed up in honey.

I was wrong. Death has no flavor of its own. It tastes like whatever it's wearing.

Three days after the throne room, they came for me at dawn—not with the shouting I'd braced for, but with courtesy, which is how you know the knife is already in. Two guards and a chamberlain with a face like folded linen. "His Majesty requests the taster," the chamberlain said, and smiled, and his smile went on a beat too long, the way the servants' smiles did, the way everything in this palace had learned to smile from something that didn't have a mouth.

Wren caught my arm as they led me through the corridors. He'd been waiting by the stairwell with a tray he had no business carrying, playing at being busy. His fingers pressed my wrist once—*careful*, or *run*, or possibly just *goodbye*. With Wren it was always hard to tell. He mouthed something I chose to read as *I'll be near.*

The guards walked me down. Down past the kitchens, past the wine cellars, down stairs that hadn't been swept in years, into the undercroft where the old dungeons kept their cold like a hoarded treasure. And there, in a cell that had been furnished—*furnished*, with a table and two chairs and a candelabrum, like a dinner party in a tomb—sat the king.

Aldric looked worse than the last time I'd seen him, which I hadn't thought possible. The thorn-crown sat on his head like it had grown there, black iron filigree sunk into the skin above his ears, and where it touched, the flesh was gray as old candle wax. But his eyes were his own. For now. They found me across the cell and something in them broke open with relief so naked it hurt to look at.

"Bram." My name in his mouth sounded like a man calling from a long way off. "You shouldn't have come back to the palace."

"You asked me to taste for you."

"I asked you to leave." He gripped the table's edge. His knuckles were the color of ash. "Listen to me. There isn't much time. When it sleeps, I get minutes—minutes where I'm me—and I've spent three days trying to keep them until you came, because you need to understand what you're walking into. It knows about you now. It felt you in the throne room. You're the first thing in thirty years it's been *afraid* of."

"Kings don't fear tasters," I said, because wry is what I do when I'm terrified. It's a trained reflex. Fear goes in, wit comes out. My guildmasters called it composure. My mother would have called it lying.

"This one does." Aldric leaned forward. Up close I could see the thorn-marks at his collar, the same scars the courtiers wore, but his went deeper—they moved. Slowly. Like roots deciding. "It can't root in you. Do you understand? Everyone else in this palace, it planted something in. A seed, a hook, a hair-fine thread of itself behind the collarbone. That's what the scars are. That's why they smile too long—it's smiling *through* them. But it tried you, that first day, when you knelt to taste the soup. I watched it reach for you." His hand closed around mine, cold and desperate. "And you burned it. Whatever your blood is, whatever the guild made you—it couldn't hold on. You are the one dish it cannot swallow."

"That sounds less like a compliment than an accusation."

"It's both." He almost laughed, and the laugh turned into a cough that bent him double, and when he straightened there were threads of black in the spit he wiped from his lip. "Bram. Listen. Tonight is the solstice rite. The whole court will be in the great hall, and the crown will be at its strongest, and if you're inside these walls when it feeds—" He stopped. Looked at me. And I watched the king disappear behind something else, the way weather changes over a field.

His spine straightened. His face went smooth. And when he spoke again, the voice was still his voice, but underneath it, beneath the words, was a sound I knew from the valley—from the way tree roots sound in a drought year, splitting stone to find water. Patient. Immense. Uninterested in being refused.

"The taster will remain," it said. "The taster is needed."

Every hair on my body stood up and pointed away from that voice. I had faced poisoners and assassins-in-waiting and one memorable attempt on the Lord of Thornvale's breakfast stew, and none of it—none of it—had prepared me for the specific horror of hearing a young man's mouth speak with the patience of a forest fire.

"Aldric," I said carefully. "Your Majesty. If I might be excused—"

"The vessel will not be excused." The thing wearing him tilted his head, and the crown drank the candlelight. "Thirty years we have worn this cage. Thirty years we have fed and grown and waited. And now the clean vessel comes willingly to our table." The king's lips curved. It was the worst smile I had ever seen, because part of it—the part at the corner, the part that remembered how smiles worked—was still fighting it. "We will taste *you*, little taster. We will find what the queen hid in your blood. And then we will never be hungry again."

There was a scraping at the cell door. Wren stood there with his tray and the expression of a man who has just realized the tray was a terrible idea. "His Majesty requested wine," he announced, too loudly. "From the private cellar. The—the solstice vintage. It requires the physician's approval."

The thing wearing Aldric turned its attention to Wren, and I saw my friend go gray. "You smell of the queen's papers," it said.

"I smell of the *cellar*," Wren said, with the courage of a man who has been terrified before and gotten good at it. "Roots and dust. Shall I describe the vintage instead?"

For one heartbeat, two, the thing considered him. Then it waved a hand—a king's gesture, perfect, practiced—and said, "Stay. Both of you stay. The rite begins at moonrise. No one leaves the undercroft."

And Aldric surfaced—one second, maybe two, his eyes finding mine over the thing's smooth mask of a face—and mouthed: *the garden. Poison garden. The grate.*

Then the crown pulled him under again, and the thing smiled at us both with borrowed teeth, and settled in to wait for moonrise like a spider who has already spun the room.

* * *

We didn't wait well. Waiting, in my experience, is for people whose plans don't involve running.

"The grate in the poison garden," Wren whispered as soon as the thing's attention drifted toward its candles, which it watched the way a miser watches coin. "That's where the queen used to meet her rootworkers. I treated her hands after—there are thorns down there that would make your guild's collection look like a herb spiral. The grate drains into the old aqueduct. It runs out past the wardline of the inner city."

"And the garden itself?"

"Is the reason I lost my position." He grimaced. "I'll explain while we run."

Run we did—but not through the door, because the door had acquired two guards who smiled too long, and I'd had enough of other people's smiles to last several lifetimes. Instead Wren pried loose a ventilation panel behind the cell's furnishing—he'd measured it, of course; Wren measures everything, it's how he loves things—and we crawled through the palace's stone bowels while somewhere above us the court tuned its instruments for the solstice.

The passage let out behind a hedge of nightshade the size of a hay wagon, in the royal poison garden. Even in the dark I could read it like a menu. Monkshood along the north wall, drooping its little helmet-flowers. Foxglove standing sentry. A trellis of oleander that could stop a horse. Someone had planted this garden with love, and that someone had been either a genius or a monster, and knowing the queen, both.

"The grate's under the sundial," Wren breathed. "But Bram—" He caught my sleeve. In the dark his face was all shadows except his eyes, which were bright with a fear he was doing a poor job of hiding. "When it spoke. Through him. Did you feel it?"

"Feel what?"

"The reaching." He tapped his own collarbone, where the courtiers wore their thorn-scars. "It tried you. Right there in the cell. I watched your shadow move wrong for half a second, like something was pushing against you from inside. And then nothing. It *let go*, Bram. Things like that don't let go. Things like that get burned."

I put my hand where his fingers had been, against my own collarbone, and felt—nothing. Just my own pulse, quick and stubborn and mine.

"It can't root in me," I said slowly, and the words rearranged themselves as they left my mouth, becoming something larger than fear. "You said it. Aldric said it. Wren—I'm not just immune to poison. I'm immune to *it*. Everything I am, every dose they ever fed me, every habituation they built into my blood—it's a lock it doesn't have a key for. I'm not the vessel it wants." I heard myself laugh, a small unhinged sound among the monkshood. "I'm the flaw in its design."

"Or the design's final step," Wren said quietly. "The guild bred you for something, Bram. Vessels don't get built by accident. Before you decide what you are to that thing, you should decide what you are to the people who made you."

Which was, infuriatingly, exactly the right question, and exactly the one I couldn't answer in a garden full of plants that wanted me dead.

Because that was when the lights came on above us—all of them, every window in the palace wing, gold and sudden as a struck match—and the thing wearing the king began to speak. Not aloud. Everywhere. In the roots, in the walls, in the thorn-scars of every servant and courtier in the building, a voice like stone splitting underground:

"*The vessel is missing.*"

The garden shuddered. Somewhere overhead, iron gates began to close.

Wren hauled the sundial's cap aside, revealing the grate and the black water below. "Down," he said. "Now. And Bram—whatever the guild built you to do—" He pushed the grate wide. "Do it *on your terms*."

I swung myself into the dark, into the cold water and the old queen's escape route, and as the grate clanged shut above us I heard the thing wearing the crown say my name.

It pronounced it perfectly. It had been practicing.

And beneath the terror, beneath the cold, something in me turned over and took root—purpose, growing where fear had been, fed on the one thing that thing would never taste: my own unswallowed self.

Tomorrow was the solstice. I had one night to learn what I'd been bred for.

And I intended to learn it from the queen's own papers—if Wren and I could outrun a kingdom that had just realized its missing piece had legs.
