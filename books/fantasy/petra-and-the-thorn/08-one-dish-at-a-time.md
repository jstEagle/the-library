## 8. One Dish at a Time

The first dose nearly killed me.

I want to be honest about that, because everything else I'm about to tell you will sound like heroism, and I'd rather you know it started with me on my hands and knees in a cellar, vomiting into a bucket Wren had thoughtfully labeled — in her neat physician's hand — *specimen*.

"That's the thorn-plant," she said, crouched beside me with the clinical calm I was beginning to hate. "Distilled from a cutting of the crown itself. One drop in a cup of water."

"It tastes," I managed, "like being buried alive."

"Good. That means it's working."

I have spent my whole life learning what things taste like. Bitter almonds, obviously. The sweet rot of belladonna honey. Arsenic's faint metallic ghost under a lord's favorite pheasant. My tongue is a ledger, every entry written in fear, and I had believed there was nothing left it couldn't read.

This was not poison. This was the opposite of poison — or rather, it was poison the way the crown is protection: technically true, catastrophically misapplied. It tasted like roots. Like cold soil closing over your face. Like something patient deciding you belonged to it.

"Again tomorrow?" I asked the bucket.

"Again tomorrow," said Wren.

* * *

We lived beneath a tannery in the Sumpward district, which meant the air above us smelled like a crime against God and no one ever came looking. Wren called it operational security. I called it the reason my eyes watered constantly, though between doses I honestly couldn't tell anymore what was tears and what was chemistry.

The routine was this: wake before dawn. Dose. Survive the dose. Sleep it off. Wake again. Study the queen's papers while my hands shook — her ciphered notes on binding-structures, on how the crown rooted itself into a wearer's loves and drank them dry, on the one flaw in its design. Me.

*Clean vessel,* the guild records called my bloodline. Bred for three generations, dosed against every toxin known to man so that when the day came, magic would find nothing already living in us to grip. A garden kept deliberately empty so the thorn would have nowhere to grow — except, if we chose it, everywhere at once.

I had spent twenty-two years thinking my talent made me invisible. Useful, safe, beneath notice. It turned out I wasn't invisible at all. I was *cultivated*. There is a difference, and the difference is that someone planned you.

"You're doing the face," Wren said on the fourth morning, watching me stare at the vial.

"What face?"

"The one where you're tasting your own life and finding arsenic in it."

"I'm fine."

"You vomited into my specimen bucket six times yesterday."

"And I'll note," I said, "that I only hit the bucket five times. That's progress. That's immunity." I held out my hand for the drop. "More today."

Wren didn't move. She has a way of looking at you like she's reading symptoms off your soul, and I suppose as a disgraced court physician, maybe she is. "Bram. The plan works because you build tolerance slowly. If you rush it—"

"If I rush it, I might survive the solstice rite instead of just surviving until then. Yes. I've read the queen's notes too." I flexed my fingers. They were steady. Mostly. "Wren, I tasted for Lord Havelock for nine years. Do you know what I learned? Poison doesn't care about your courage. It cares about your preparation. So I prepare."

She gave me the drop.

That day, for the first time, when the taste of roots came, I tasted past it. Underneath the burial-cold there was something else — a thread of sweetness, almost, like sap. Like something green remembering it had once been alive.

"Huh," I said.

"What?"

"I think the crown used to be a tree."

Wren went very still, the way she does when a diagnosis turns terrible. "The queen's notes say it was iron."

"The iron is a cage," I said, surprising myself. "Something's inside it. Something that was growing long before anyone forged a crown." I looked at the vial. "And I think it's lonely."

* * *

On the ninth day, Aldric surfaced.

I knew it before Wren told me — felt it as a sudden silence in my blood, the way you notice a headache leaving. For nine days the thorn-extract had carried an undertone of him: fury, mostly, and underneath the fury a terror so vast it made my teeth ache. Then, that morning, nothing but a strange, exhausted clarity, like a room after a storm.

"He fought it down," Wren said, spreading the message on our worktable. "One of the kitchen girls — ours, don't worry — brought this down through the cistern route. He wrote it himself. He says the crown sleeps shallowly after these episodes. Hours, perhaps a day."

The message was short. The handwriting of a man writing against time:

*To the taster. If you are reading this, you did not run, which was foolish, and I find I am glad, which is worse. Do not come to the throne room before the solstice. Whatever you are planning — and the crown suspects you, which means I suspect you, which means part of me hopes — do not do it alone. The thing in the crown knows your name now. It said it while wearing my mouth, and I am sorry. You should know it apologized in my voice. That is the kind of thing it does.*

*I remember my wife sent you the map. I remember loving her. I am telling you this because the crown eats memories and I want at least one of mine delivered somewhere it can't reach.*

*The kitchens still make honeycakes on rest days. I could not tell you why that matters. It simply seemed important that someone know.*

I read it three times. Wren watched me read it three times.

"'Part of me hopes,'" she repeated. "He's addressing you directly. Not the vessel. Not the guild's clean blood. You."

"He's addressing the person who's going to get him killed," I said, and folded the letter very carefully, the way you fold something you intend to keep.

"Bram."

"Don't." I busied myself with the vials. "Don't say anything kind right now. I'm holding a schedule together with spite and honeycake references, and kindness is a solvent."

She laughed — actually laughed, first time since the dungeon — and let it go. But that night I caught her adding something to my evening dose, and when I raised an eyebrow she said, mildly, "Valerian. For sleep." And I chose to believe her, because the alternative was admitting that the king's handwriting had made my chest hurt, and I didn't have time for that either.

* * *

By the twelfth day I could take four drops.

By the fifteenth, seven, and the visions started — not nightmares exactly, more like overheard conversations. Roots talking in the dark. I learned things from them, which frightened me more than the pain had. I learned the crown had been worn by eleven kings before Aldric. I learned it remembered all of them the way soil remembers bodies. And I learned, in a whisper so old the words had half rotted, what it wanted Bram Crane for.

Not a host. Hosts wear out.

A *rootstock*. Something to graft itself onto when the iron finally failed — and the iron was failing; even the cage was dying — so it could walk out of the crown and into the world wearing a body that poison couldn't touch and wards couldn't see.

Me. I wasn't the flaw in its design. I was the *next design*.

I told Wren. She sat with that for a long moment, turning her mortar pestle in slow circles without grinding anything.

"So the guild didn't breed you to break the crown," she said finally.

"They bred me to be its next pot." I smiled, and I know it was a bad smile because Wren flinched. "Well. Good news is, I've been building an immunity."

"To the binding, yes. But Bram — if it grafts onto you, tolerance won't matter. It won't be attacking you. It'll be *becoming* you."

"Then I'll have to break it faster than it can become me." I stood, joints aching, and looked at the queen's counter-ritual spread across the wall — Wren had copied it out in chalk, all its circles and severances, the place at the center marked *the unbound hand*. "How long do you need below, during the rite?"

"Four minutes. Maybe five, if the circles hold."

"Then keep me alive for five minutes," I said, "and I'll keep the crown busy for six."

* * *

The night before the last training day, I found the vial.

It was tucked into my kit among the empty dose-bottles, small and dark green glass, stoppered with wax pressed with a signet I recognized now from the queen's papers — the royal seal, but struck crooked, hurried, by a hand that wasn't quite his own and was fighting to be. Inside, a single drop of something that shimmered wrong. Not thorn-extract. Thinner. Brighter. It tasted — I uncorked it just enough to touch my tongue, because of course I did, I am who I am — it tasted like honeycake. Like a rest-day kitchen. Like a memory someone had distilled and defended from the thing in his head, at cost I didn't want to calculate.

There was no note. Just the vial, and under it, scratched into the wax with something sharp:

*For after. In case there is an after. If there isn't — drink it anyway. I'd rather the last thing you tasted of me be this than ash.*

I sat down on my cot in the tannery cellar and held it and did not cry, mostly, because crying wastes salt and salt preserves things, and I intended to preserve quite a lot.

"Wren," I said, when I could speak evenly. "Tomorrow. Before the solstice. Teach me the fifth circle."

"There is no fifth circle."

"There is now. I'm going to give it one." I corked the vial and put it over my heart, where a root would have to go through it to reach me. "Tell the kitchen girl to stand ready. Tomorrow I taste for the king."
