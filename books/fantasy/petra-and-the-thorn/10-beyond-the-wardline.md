## 10. Beyond the Wardline

The Thornvale would not have me back.

I learned this the way I learned most things—by tasting it and finding out too late what was in it. Wren had warned me, of course, in her flat physician's way, the way she'd tell a patient their leg would need to come off: *the backlash marked you, Bram. Magic doesn't forget who broke its cage.* But warnings are one thing and wardlines are another, and so on a gray morning in early spring I stood at the edge of my home valley with Aldric's hand in mine and felt the wards push back against my skin like water against an oiled feather.

Not pain. Worse than pain. *Rejection.* The valley that had made me—the poison kitchens, the taster's guild, the narrow house where I'd learned to be invisible—looked at the thing I'd become and declined to know me.

"Try again," Aldric said quietly.

"I did try again. It tried harder." I pulled my hand back from the shimmer in the air, the faint heat-haze distortion that any Thornvale child could see and no outsider ever could. "It's like knocking on your own door and having the house pretend you're a debt collector."

"You could wait. Wren thinks the mark might fade."

"Wren also thought the queen's counter-ritual wouldn't crack the palace foundation, and we both remember how that went." I looked at the valley a long moment. Mist sat in it like milk in a bowl. Somewhere down there was Master Corvin's grave—I'd paid for the stone from a distance, through a merchant who owed me—and somewhere down there was the kitchen where I'd spent twenty years being nobody on purpose.

Here is what I had expected to feel: grief. Here is what I actually felt: something smaller and stranger, like a tooth that finally stops aching. You don't miss the ache. You just keep touching the spot to make sure.

"I spent my whole life learning not to be seen," I said. "Turns out I'm very good at it. The valley can't even see me anymore."

Aldric didn't say anything comforting, which is one of the thousand reasons I love him. He just stood there in his plain traveling coat—he'd refused the royal colors for the journey, said they made him look like a flagpole with opinions—and waited until I was ready to turn around.

The realm mended slowly. That was the word everyone used, *mending*, as if the year of the thorn-crown had been a torn sleeve rather than a wound in the world. Fields that had gone gray came back green, but patchy, like a beard on a young man. Rivers ran clear but shy of their banks. And the king—Aldric, Aldric again, not the thing with the voice like roots splitting stone—rode out among his people and remembered what had worn him, and told them so, because he had decided that a realm heals faster from honesty than from comfort.

"They'll want you to stay," I said as we walked back toward the road. "They'll want their mending king."

"They'll get him. Four days a month, Wren's terms." He almost smiled. "She's drawn up a schedule. There's a chart. She laminated it somehow, I don't ask."

"And the other days?"

"The other days," he said, "I'm told there's a woman who used to taste poison for a living and now can't go home, and I intend to find out if she's as insufferable as she sounds."

* * *

We planted the crown in autumn.

Wren had kept the pieces—of course she had; the woman archives her own dreams—in a lead-lined box in the undercity, wrapped in cloth that had once been altar linen. Nine shards of black iron thorn, each one still cold enough to burn bare skin, each one humming faintly when I came near, the way a struck bell hums after the sound has left it.

"It should be destroyed properly," Wren said. "Melted. Ground to powder. Buried under a mountain with appropriate ceremony."

"Where's the nearest mountain?"

"Three weeks' ride, and the ceremony would require clergy, and the clergy are still arguing about whether you're a saint or an abomination."

"What's the current tally?"

"Saints are ahead by two votes. One of them defected last week."

So we chose the garden instead. There was a courtyard in the oldest wing of the palace—a wing closed since the queen died, since before, maybe—where nothing grew. Not poor soil; *nothing*. Gardeners had tried for three generations. Roses died in a week. Weeds refused it as beneath them. The ground was pale and packed hard as a baker's loaf, and when I knelt and pressed my palm flat against it, I understood why.

"There's a root under here," I said. "An old one. From the first binding. The crown drank this place dry years ago and never gave it back."

Aldric crouched beside me. He'd been doing that more lately—kneeling in dirt, talking to gardeners, sitting on the floor of orphanages because someone told him kings shouldn't and he'd decided to do it anyway out of spite. "Can you tell what it wants?"

"It's a root. It wants what everything wants. To be fed and left alone." I turned my hand over. The mark the backlash had left ran across my palm and up two fingers, a fine silver tracery like frost on glass, and when I let a little of what lived in it flow outward, the ground warmed under my touch. Not much. A suggestion of warmth. A rumor.

We dug together. It took most of the day, because the earth fought us—it had been fighting everything for sixty years and saw no reason to stop—and because Aldric insisted on doing it properly, with a spade, like a man planting an apple tree instead of interring the instrument of his decade-long possession. Nine shards went into the hole. They stopped humming the moment they touched soil, all at once, like birds going quiet.

Then we covered them, and I did the thing I'd been practicing alone in my rooms at night, the thing that still scared me more than the crown ever had: I gave.

Magic, I mean. My own—the mark's, whatever had grown into me when the binding shattered and had nowhere else to go. I'd been hoarding it like a miser, dosing myself with it the way I'd once dosed myself with arsenic, small amounts, building tolerance. Now I opened my hands over the fresh-turned earth and let it run out of me like water from a cut wineskin.

Nothing happened.

Of course nothing happened. Gardens don't bloom in October because a woman cries over them. Wren says magic isn't a wish-granting engine, it's a *pressure*, and pressure takes seasons to become anything.

But in spring—this spring, the one we stood in now, at the wardline, before we rode back—one green shoot had broken the pale soil of the dead garden. Just one. Thin as thread, stubborn as sin, growing directly out of the spot where the largest shard lay buried.

Wren took a cutting for study. Aldric posted a guard—not to protect it, he said, but so that someone would be there to witness it every day, because "a miracle nobody counts is just weather." And I went out every morning before the palace woke and sat with it, the way I'd once sat alone in kitchens, except that now, increasingly, I wasn't alone. He started coming. Neither of us mentioned it. Some things you ruin by naming, and some things you save the same way.

* * *

The night before we rode north to the wardline, Aldric found me on the garden wall with my boots off, which is where I do my best thinking and all of my worst decisions.

"The council drew up papers today," he said.

"Abdication papers?"

"Papers." He sat down next to me. The wall was old enough that its stones had gone soft-edged, and we fit into its hollows like we'd been set there years ago. "They want me to formalize the regency arrangement. Make Wren's four-days-a-month permanent. Name a steward for the rest. It's generous, actually. They've realized a king who remembers being eaten makes a better relic than a ruler, and relics are easier to manage."

"Will you sign?"

"I told them I'd consider it." He was quiet a moment. "Bram. When we ride to the valley tomorrow—when the wards push you away again—what will you do?"

"Stand there feeling like a debt collector, probably. Then leave."

"And after that? Where does a woman go when she's been exiled from everywhere twice?"

I looked at my silver-marked hand. "Everywhere, I suppose. That's the terrifying part. I spent twenty years perfecting smallness. One valley, one kitchen, one lord's table. Now the whole map is—" I gestured vaguely at the sky, which seemed to cover it. "I don't know how to want that much."

"Yes, you do," he said. "You wanted me. That's enormous. You managed it while complaining constantly."

"I did *not* complain constantly."

"You called the crown 'a hat with ambitions.' You called the solstice rite 'the worst dinner party in history.' You described falling in love with me as 'an unfortunate development in an already difficult season.'"

"All accurate."

"All complaints."

I laughed, and the laugh turned into something else halfway through, the way it sometimes did lately, and he put his arm around me and let it happen without fixing it, which is the whole art of loving someone, as far as I can tell. Nobody teaches you that in the poison kitchens.

"Come with me," I said, when I could speak again. "Wherever the map goes. Come with—"

"I've already signed," he said.

I stared at him.

"This morning. Before the council session. While they were still drafting the other papers." He shrugged, that infuriating shrug, the one that says *obviously* about things that are not obvious at all. "You asked me once, in the throne room, with the roots in my mouth and the crown drinking everything I loved—you asked if I could hear you. I could. You were the only thing I could hear. Do you understand what that means? Everything else the crown took, and it couldn't take you, because you weren't part of what I loved *before*. You were what I chose *during*. Choice is the one thing it never had."

"That's a very pretty speech for a man who once described himself as 'a hat rack for a hostile hat.'"

"I've been workshopping it."

**Aldric**

The wardline looks different when you were born outside it. To Bram it is a wall, a wound, a door slammed in her face. To me it is barely anything—a heat-shimmer, a suggestion, the air remembering itself. I have crossed it four times now and it has never once noticed me.

That's the cruelty of it, I think. The valley keeps out what it cannot hold, and it cannot hold her, and it holds me so carelessly it doesn't bother. She gave everything to break the cage and the cage's answer is *not you, never you again*. If our positions were reversed I think I would burn it down. She stands before it with her chin up and her jaw set and her hand in mine, and after a long moment she laughs—short, bitter, real—and says, "Well. It always did have terrible manners."

We walk the perimeter instead of crossing. She shows me the gaps in the mist where the market gate stands, the roofline of the guildhouse, a chimney she claims belongs to the house where she grew up learning to die slowly by spoonfuls. Her voice stays level the whole time. Only her hand betrays her, gripping mine hard enough to leave marks, and I let it, gladly, because I have been held by worse things and survived, and I would rather be bruised by her than crowned by anything.

At the northernmost point, where the mist thins to nothing and the road runs free toward mountains neither of us owes anything to, she stops.

"I expected to be alone," she says. It comes out flat, factual, a taster's report. *This dish contains loneliness, mild, notes of iron.*

"You were wrong."

"I'm often wrong. I once identified nightshade as belladonna."

"They're the same plant, Bram."

"See? Wrong constantly."

I take her marked hand—the silver tracery catches the morning light, frost-bright, beautiful, hers—and I step backward across no line at all, into the unclaimed country, pulling her gently with me. Behind us the valley closes its mist like a book it has decided not to finish. Ahead of us the road runs on past the edge of every map either of us was ever given.

She looks back once. Then she looks forward, and does not look back again, and we walk.

Behind us, in a dead garden in a closed wing of a mending palace, in soil that refused everything for sixty years, the green shoot puts out its second leaf.
