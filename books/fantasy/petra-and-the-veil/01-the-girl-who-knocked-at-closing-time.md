## 1. The Girl Who Knocked at Closing Time

There are three rules to selling charms in a city that has outlawed magic, and I have broken all of them at least twice.

Rule one: never sell to anyone who asks directly what a charm does. Rule two: never keep the good stock where you can find it yourself — memory is the first thing they search for, and mine is famously unreliable after the third glass of plum wine. Rule three, the one my grandmother stitched into the lining of my first apron in thread I was told was ordinary gray cotton: *never open the door after the lamps go down.*

I want it on record that when the knock came, the lamps were still lit. Barely. One of them. The wick had drowned itself in its own oil and I'd been too lazy to relight it, which by my accounting meant the door was legally closed and whatever happened next was not my fault.

The knock came again. Three raps, spaced like someone counting them out on their fingers because they had never knocked on a door before and had only read about it.

"Closed," I said, in the tone shopkeepers reserve for drunks, debt collectors, and Warden inspectors, three categories with considerable overlap.

The knock came a third time, softer now, almost apologetic, and I thought — and I will defend this thought to my grave — that any fool persistent enough to knock three times at closing either wanted something badly or was about to give me something worse. In Corvane, those are usually the same thing.

I opened the door.

* * *

Market night on Tallow Row smells like frying onions, wet wool, and the particular desperation of people trying to sell you something before the Wardens' sweep carts come rattling through. My shop sits at the crooked end of the row, wedged between a coffin-maker who does brisk business and a fortune-teller who does none, which tells you everything about this city's sense of humor. Corvane banned magic forty years ago — before my time, though not by much — and ever since, we've all agreed to pretend that luck is just luck, that the little knotted cords and pressed-flower tokens and bottles of river water sold openly as "souvenirs" are exactly what they appear to be.

Nothing. They appear to be nothing. That's the whole trick of surviving here: everything appears to be nothing.

The girl standing on my step appeared to be nothing, too, at first glance. Small — eight years old, maybe nine, hard to tell with children who've been walking longer than they've been eating. Barefoot, in a city where the cobblestones go cold enough in autumn to crack an egg. A dress that had once been fine, judging by the embroidery gone gray at the hem, now worn the way clothes get worn when they're the only clothes. Her hair was dark and cut short with something blunt, and she looked up at me with the flat, patient expression of a cat waiting for you to move the furniture.

"Well," I said. "You're not buying anything."

She opened her mouth and said something.

Now. I speak four languages well, two badly, and one exclusively for arguing with fishmongers. What came out of this child's mouth was none of them. It sounded like rain deciding to be music — syllables that folded into each other, vowels that arrived from somewhere behind the words. And here is the part I have spent the whole night since failing to explain to myself: I understood her.

Not the way you understand a language you've studied. The way you understand your own name being called from another room. Somewhere under my ribs, some door I didn't know I had swung open, and the words walked straight through it.

*The lamp is dying,* she said. *May I come in where it isn't?*

"You may not," I said, in Corvani, because answering in kind felt like agreeing to something. "Where are your shoes?"

She looked down at her feet as if noticing them for the first time, then back up, unbothered. *Shoes are for people going somewhere else.*

"That's a philosophy, not an answer."

*I know where I'm going.* She tilted her head. *You do too. You've just forgotten the word for it.*

I should have shut the door. Any sensible smuggler of harmless nothing-charms would have shut the door, dropped the bar, and told herself in the morning she'd dreamed the whole thing. Instead I heard myself say, "One minute," and stepped back inside to relight the drowned lamp, which is the sort of thing you do when your hands need employment while your judgment catches up.

When I turned around, she was already inside.

I never heard the door. I never saw her cross the threshold. She was simply standing among my shelves with her hands clasped behind her back like a duchess inspecting a garden, examining my stock with an attention that made me deeply uncomfortable, because most of that stock was supposed to look like junk.

"That one's a bottle cap," I said, pointing. "That one's a button. This whole shelf is disappointment, actually, if you're shopping for anything else."

She wasn't listening. She had stopped in front of the case by the window — the case I keep locked, the case containing the charms even I call dead. Every charm has a life in it, a little warmth of working, and every charm spends. These were the spent ones, the burned-out husks I kept partly for parts and partly because throwing them away felt like burying people. There was a knot-cord there that had once found lost things. A pressed blue flower that had held a fever off a child in the Flood Year. A small tin bird that had done something I don't talk about, even to myself, especially to myself.

The girl reached toward the case. "Don't touch—" I started, and then she touched it anyway, one finger, through the glass, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal.

The tin bird sang.

It had been dead eleven years. It had been dead when I inherited it, dead as a doornail, dead as — as something, some word that rose in me and dissolved before I could catch it. But it sang, one thin bright note that went through the shop like a needle through cloth, pulling a thread, and every dead charm on that shelf lit at once. The knot-cord glowed gold at its center. The pressed flower flushed blue as a vein. For three heartbeats my case of failures blazed like a festival, and then it guttered out, and the girl took her finger off the glass, and the silence afterward was louder than the singing.

We looked at each other.

"Right," I said, when I could speak. "You're coming in properly after all. Sit down. Don't touch anything else. Do you eat bread?"

*I eat,* she said, with dignity, and sat on my stool like she'd owned it for years.

* * *

Here is what I knew, watching her demolish half a loaf and a jar of plum preserves: her name was Neth. I didn't learn it; I simply knew it, the way you know the smell of rain before it arrives. She communicated in that folding, raining language, and I answered in Corvani, and somehow the conversation worked, which should have been impossible and was instead merely horrifying.

She would not say where she came from. When I asked, she looked at me with such old, old sadness that I changed the subject to preserves. She would not say how she found my shop. When I asked that, she pointed one sticky finger at my chest, at the exact spot where my sternum sits, and said: *You're the doorkeeper's granddaughter.*

"I'm a shopkeeper," I said. "My grandmother sold buttons."

*Your grandmother sold doors,* Neth said, and ate more preserves, and let me sit there with my heart hammering like a debtor at the gate.

Because here is the thing. Here is the thing I have circled all night like a moth around a lamp I suspect will kill me. Sable Thackeray did not sell buttons. I have always known that and not known it, the way you know a room has a wall without ever having looked at it. My grandmother left me this shop, this stock, this ledger with its lock I've never picked, and a lifetime of instructions shaped like superstitions: never open the door after dark, never count the till aloud, never, ever say the name of the country we came from.

Except I couldn't have named that country if you paid me. I've tried. There's a shape in my memory where it should be — a shape like a country, green at the edges, with a word carved over its door — and when I reach for the word, my mind slides off it the way a thumb slides off wet glass. I assumed for thirty years that this was ordinary forgetting. Everyone forgets things. Everyone's childhood goes soft at the edges.

But a barefoot child just spoke the language of that soft-edged place in my own shop, and I understood every word, and no one taught me.

The Veil, said a voice in my head that sounded unpleasantly like my grandmother. The word arrived whole, unbidden, smelling of her kitchen and her gray thread. *The Veil is thinning, Liora. I told you it would hold your lifetime. I never promised it would hold yours comfortably.*

"Liora," I said aloud, and the girl looked up.

*Yes,* she said, as if I'd finally caught up. *Liora Thackeray. Keeper's blood.* She set down the preserves, wiped her hands on her ruined dress with great formality, and fixed me with eyes that had watched something terrible and carried it a very long way barefoot. Then she said the sentence that ended my ordinary life, in her raining language, and I understood it perfectly:

*They are coming for me. The ones who unmake remembering. I knocked on every door on this street. Yours is the only one that heard.*

Outside, far down Tallow Row, a bell began to ring — the sweep bell, the Wardens calling their nightly tally, telling every shop on the row to show its face and its license and its nothing. The drowned lamp I'd relit flickered. The tin bird, silent again in its case, seemed to lean toward the sound of the bell the way a man leans toward a window.

Neth slid off the stool without a whisper and stood behind me, small and grave, one hand finding the hem of my apron.

And I — breaker of all three rules, keeper of a shop full of nothing, granddaughter of a woman who sold doors — reached up and pinched out the lamp myself, and stood in the dark, listening to the boots come closer, wondering how quickly a person can learn to lie to a Warden.

I was about to find out.
