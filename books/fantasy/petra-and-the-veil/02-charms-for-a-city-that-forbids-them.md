## 2. Charms for a City That Forbids Them

The girl slept in the back room, curled among the moth-winged bundles of dried lavender like something the shop had grown overnight. I had moved her there at dawn, after a night of not sleeping, and she had gone without protest — which worried me more than screaming would have. Screaming children want things. Silent children have already learned that wanting gets you nothing.

I named her Neth because it was the only word of hers I could reliably pronounce.

That was the problem, you understand. Not that she spoke. That I understood her.

Corvane forbids magic the way other cities forbid open flame in a timber district: sensibly, thoroughly, and with regular inspections. The Wardens came through twice a year with their brass resonance forks and their bored expressions, and charm-sellers like me survived by selling things that were almost charms — luck-knots tied from ordinary red thread, "blessed" salts that were just salt, love philtres that were honey and ginger and confidence. My grandmother Sable had built the shop on exactly that principle: sell them what they want to believe, never what's true.

For thirty years I had been a fraud, and a good one. And then a barefoot child had walked into my shop at closing time, said four words in a language that has no business existing, and touched a dead luck-charm until it bloomed warm as a bird's egg in her palm.

So on the morning after, while Neth slept, I did what any sensible woman would do. I went looking for proof that I was dreaming.

* * *

Sable's ledger lived in the floor safe beneath the counter, behind a false bottom I'd found when I was nine and had never once opened since, because opening it felt like reading someone's diary and also like asking a question I wasn't sure I wanted answered. It was bound in grey leather gone soft as ear skin, locked with a clasp that had no keyhole — only a recessed circle, worn smooth, the size of a thumbprint.

I pressed my thumb into it. Nothing. Of course nothing. Magic was illegal, and dead grandmothers were famously uncooperative.

But the girl's touch had woken a dead charm. And whatever else I was, I was a shopkeeper, and shopkeepers know that stock responds to handling.

I carried the ledger to the back room, knelt beside the sleeping girl, and set the clasp against her small brown hand.

The lock sighed open like an old man settling into a chair.

Neth didn't wake. But the lavender bundles stirred, all of them at once, though there was no draft, and somewhere under the floorboards the shop made a sound I can only describe as clearing its throat.

"Right," I told the empty room. "Absolutely fine. Nothing alarming here."

The ledger's pages were written in two hands. The first was Sable's — sharp, slanted, economical, the handwriting of a woman who billed by the word. The second hand appeared only rarely, in the margins, in ink that had faded to the color of old bruises. And the bulk of the text was in the language the girl spoke. Old Vessani, the margins called it, when they deigned to write in Corvani at all.

I read for three hours. I understood perhaps a third of it, which frightened me, because I should have understood none of it. Words rose off the page already translated, the way a tune you've never heard can still feel familiar. *Veil. Threshold. Thirteenth turn.* A country called Vessany, which I had certainly never heard of, except that some traitor part of me kept nodding along, going *yes, yes, the terraced orchards, the bell-roads, obviously.*

There was a seal stamped on the inside cover in wax the color of dried blood: a tower wrapped in ivy, crowned with a star. When I looked up from it, Neth was awake and watching me, and she reached out and touched the seal, and then touched her own collarbone, beneath which — I hadn't noticed before — hung a cord, and on the cord a small lead token stamped with the same tower-and-star.

She said something long and urgent. I caught maybe half of it, the way you catch half of a conversation through a wall. *Heir. House. Hunted.* Or possibly I invented those words out of my own rising panic. Children are dramatic. Children lie. Children are also, occasionally, exactly what they claim to be, which is infinitely worse.

"You're royalty," I said flatly, in Corvani, testing it. "Of a country that doesn't exist."

She didn't understand the words but she understood the tone, and she nodded anyway, solemn as a funeral, and put her hand over the token like a knight swearing an oath.

I sat down hard on a crate of unsold luck-knots.

My grandmother had traded away an entire country. That much the ledger made plain even through the fog of translation — pages of terms, of boundaries drawn in language instead of ink, of a price paid in *name* and *memory*, and Sable's signature at the bottom written so large and so violently that the pen had torn the paper. Whatever she'd bought with it, she'd hated paying.

And now the country was leaking. Through a child. Through my own skull, apparently, which understood a dead language it had no right to know. The Veil around Vessany was thinning, and thinning things have a way of tearing.

I was still sitting on the crate doing the arithmetic of catastrophe when the shop bell rang.

* * *

Warden inspectors travel in pairs, always, so that neither can be tempted or corrupted without witness. This one traveled alone, which should have been my first warning.

He was young, pink-cheeked, with the earnest look of a man who had joined the Wardens out of genuine belief that magic was dangerous, and who had never yet met anything dangerous enough to test that belief. His resonance fork hung at his belt in its leather case, untouched.

"Routine sweep," he said. "Merchant Lior Thackeray?"

"Charm-seller," I corrected, because correcting Warden clerks is one of the few pleasures left to us. "Thackeray, yes. Come in, Inspector. Mind the lavender."

He walked the aisles with his hands clasped behind his back, picking up luck-knots and setting them down, tapping jars of salt, holding a love-philtre to the light with the expression of a man appraising vinegar. Behind him, through the curtain to the back room, I could hear Neth moving. A soft footstep. The whisper of fabric.

My heart was performing gymnastics I hadn't authorized.

"Quiet day," the inspector observed.

"Market night last night. Today everyone sleeps it off." True. All of it true. I was discovering that truth is an excellent vehicle for lying, the way a smuggler uses an honest cart.

"Mm." He drifted toward the counter, toward the floor safe, and I experienced a moment of pure animal clarity in which I understood exactly how a hare feels. "Your grandmother's establishment, I believe. Sable Thackeray. She was... noted."

"Noted?"

"The file is thick." He smiled to show this was banter. Files being thick about Sable was unsurprising; she'd spent forty years selling not-quite-magic to a city that punished real magic, and the Wardens had never once caught her, which either meant she was innocent or the best criminal Corvane ever produced. "You keep her records?"

"Some. She was thorough about taxes. Less so about sentiment."

From the back room: a sneeze. Small. Catastrophic.

The inspector turned his head.

"Cats," I said.

"You keep cats?"

"I keep one cat. He keeps himself. He's a bad employee." I was aware that I was talking too much. I talked more. "He's forbidden from the stock room, he ignores this completely—"

The inspector lifted the curtain.

Neth stood in the middle of the back room, barefoot, clutching a bundle of lavender like a bouquet, looking up at him with the enormous calm eyes of a child who has already been hunted and has decided fear is a luxury.

"She's mine," I heard myself say. "My sister's girl. Sister died in the spring fever; the girl's mute, poor thing, and I'm the only family left. She helps with the drying. Lavender, mostly. She's quick at it."

Every word of it was a lie except the lavender.

The inspector crouched. "Hello," he said kindly. Neth stared at him. He produced, from his coat, a stick of barley sugar — Wardens carry them for questioning children, a fact I found newly horrifying — and offered it. She took it. She even smiled, a small careful smile, and my heart broke a little, watching her perform innocence like a professional.

He straightened. "Mute, you said."

"Since the fever. Doctors say the voice may come back. We're hopeful." Hopeful. I was becoming a poet of falsehood.

He nodded, wrote something in his little book — I watched the pen move and could not read it upside down and would have sold a year of my life to do so — and snapped it shut. At the door he paused.

"The city's been strange lately," he said. "Resonance readings. Nothing confirmed. If you notice anything... unusual, Merchant Thackeray, you'll report it."

"Immediately," I said. "I'm very law-abiding. It's practically a character flaw."

He laughed, and left, and the bell rang behind him, and I stood in my shop with my heart hammering and realized that my hands were shaking so badly I had to grip the counter to stay upright.

In the doorway to the back room, Neth held up the barley sugar and offered me half.

* * *

That evening I locked the door, turned the sign, and sat across from the girl at the workbench with the ledger open between us and a pot of tea neither of us drank.

"Here's how it is," I said, knowing she couldn't follow the words but hoping she could follow the tone, which was the tone of a woman drawing a line in the sand and then immediately standing on the wrong side of it. "I am not brave. I am not heroic. I run a shop that sells lies to people who want to believe them, and I have done it safely for thirty years, and the smart thing — the *sane* thing — is to take you to the Wardens' hall tomorrow and let them sort you out."

Neth watched me with those grave dark eyes. Then she reached across the workbench, took my hand — the same hand that had held the ledger, the same blood that had opened Sable's lock — and pressed it flat against the lead token at her throat.

The tower-and-star seal burned cold under my palm, and every word of Old Vessani I'd ever absorbed rose up in me at once like water finding its level, and I understood, finally, what my grandmother had actually left me. Not the shop. Not the fraud. The *door*. And doors, I was beginning to suspect, don't care whether you're brave. They only care whether they open.

"All right," I said, to her, to the ledger, to the ghost of a woman whose signature had torn the paper. "All right. But we do this quietly, and we do it properly, and if anyone asks, you're my mute niece and I'm a boring woman who sells salt."

Neth smiled — the real one this time, wide and gap-toothed — and said something rapid and ringing in the language of a dead country, and though I caught only fragments, I caught enough:

Something about a thirteenth turn. Something about time running out.

Outside, over the rooftops of Corvane, the moon was already climbing, fat and pale, beginning its next turn.
