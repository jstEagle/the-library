## 4. The Warden Who Remembers

The trouble with forgetting a country is that nobody tells you what you've forgotten. They just hand you the absence and call it peace.

I'd been a Warden for eleven years, and in eleven years I had learned exactly three things worth learning: that magic rots whatever it touches, that the Veil holds because we hold it, and that Captain Vey does not dream. The third was a lie I told myself every morning over cold coffee, because the alternative was admitting that some nights I woke with salt on my lips and a word in my mouth — *vessarath* — that meant nothing, that was nothing, that I always swallowed before it could become anything at all.

The morning of the charm shop inspection, I woke with it again. And this time I wrote it down.

*Vessarath.* It sat on my slate like a small dark bird that had flown in through a window I didn't know I owned. My junior officer, Pell, glanced at it over breakfast and asked if it was a suspect's name.

"It's nothing," I said, and wiped it away, and the slate kept the ghost of it anyway, the way slates do.

* * *

Corvane on market night is a city pretending very hard to be ordinary. Lanterns lit with oil instead of witchfire, bakers selling bread that rises because of yeast and nothing else, children's toys that move only when pushed. We patrol it the way you pat down a blanket over a sleeping child — gently, thoroughly, and with the constant knowledge that something underneath is breathing.

The Thackeray charm shop had come up on the quarterly sweep list, which meant nothing. It came up every quarter. Lior Thackeray sold luck-knots and door-blessings and other trifles legal enough to bore a magistrate, and every sweep I'd ever run through her shop had turned up exactly what a shop like hers should turn up: dust, inventory, and a shopkeeper who answered questions like she was reciting them from memory.

That should have been the first thing that bothered me. Honest merchants are nervous. Guilty ones are rehearsed.

"Captain Vey." She unlocked the door before I knocked, which was either courtesy or surveillance. "Back so soon."

"The city misses you."

"I'm flattered."

Pell went to the till; I took the floor. That was our routine, and routines are how you catch liars — not by catching them out, but by watching them fail to be surprised. Thackeray wasn't surprised. She also wasn't quite rehearsed tonight. There was a tightness around her mouth, a way she stood between me and the back room without standing between me and the back room, and I have spent eleven years learning the difference.

"Quiet week?" I asked, lifting a jar of salt-charms.

"Quiet as graves."

"You say that like it's a good thing."

"In my experience," she said, "graves are the quietest honest thing there is."

I let that sit. Pell counted coins behind me, the small music of copper on wood, and I moved along the shelves past love-lures and traveler's wards and a row of little clay houses glazed blue, the kind you put on a sill to keep ill weather out. One of them had been recently touched. The dust was disturbed in a handprint shape, small — child-small — and I stood there looking at it while my heart did something I refused to name.

Because here is the thing about the Veil that they don't put in the training manuals: it doesn't just erase. It *aches*. There are nights on patrol when I pass a doorway and feel certain — absolutely certain, with no evidence whatsoever — that I have walked through it before, in another life, going home. There is a smell, woodsmoke and river water, that makes me stop mid-stride like a dog scenting a country. And when I asked my instructors about it, years ago, they smiled the kind smile you give to a child asking where dreams go, and said: *homesickness for somewhere you've never been is common among Wardens. It means the Veil is working.*

I believed them for nine of my eleven years.

* * *

It was the coin that nearly ended everything, and it was such a small thing. Coins usually are.

Pell had finished the till and called it clean, and he was right — it was clean, scrupulously clean, too clean, every stack squared and sorted. But as I passed it on my way out, my eye caught a disc of metal at the bottom of the drawer, wedged in the seam where the wood had warped, and I reached in and pried it loose with one fingernail.

It was old. Older than Corvane's minting, older than any coinage I knew, and it was wrong in a way I couldn't catalogue — the metal had a faint warmth, as if it remembered being held. On its face: a tower, and above the tower a moon with thirteen rays.

My hand started shaking before my mind caught up.

Thirteen rays. I knew that moon. Not from any textbook, not from any Warden briefing — the Veil doesn't permit itself to be briefed about — but from somewhere underneath all of that, from the flooded cellar of myself where *vessarath* lived. I knew that moon the way you know your own mother's face: instantly, totally, and with no ability to explain how.

There had been a song. I could almost hear it — a counting song, a child's song, thirteen verses, and I had known all thirteen once, and the first line rose in my throat now like water rising in a locked room —

"*One for the gate and two for the—*"

"Captain?"

Pell. Standing in the doorway with his ledger under his arm, young and earnest and loyal, waiting for me to tell him what we'd found.

I looked at the coin. I looked at Thackeray, who had gone very still by her counter, watching me with the expression of a woman watching a fuse burn toward her life. And I understood, in one long cold drop of clarity, that everything depended on the next four seconds — on whether Captain Vey, eleven years of service, pillar of the sweep rotation, did her duty.

"Nothing," I said.

The word came out steady. I'll give myself that much. It came out steady and it was the hardest thing I have ever said, harder than my vows, harder than my father's funeral oration, and I closed my fist around the coin and felt its warmth against my palm like a small animal, like something alive, like something *mine*.

"Sweep's clean," I told Pell. "Note it and let's move. Two more shops before midnight."

He nodded and went out into the street. Thackeray exhaled — barely, a thread of breath — and I made myself look at her, really look, and saw that she knew. Of course she knew. She'd watched me find it and she'd watched me lie, and neither of us said a word about it, because saying it aloud would make it real, and real things require decisions, and neither of us was ready.

"Your till has a warped seam," I said instead. "You might want to see to it."

"I'll get someone on it," she said. "Captain."

"Shopkeeper."

And I walked out into the market noise with a country cupped in my fist.

* * *

I didn't sleep that night. I sat at my desk in the garrison with the lamp turned low and the coin in front of me, and I tried to do the arithmetic of my own life.

Eleven years. Sweeps and seizures, burnings and bindings, the slow patient work of keeping Corvane safe from the thing that had ruined the world before the Veil — that was the story, the whole story, the story I'd been telling since I was nineteen and desperate enough to need one. Magic rots. The Veil holds because we hold it. And underneath the story, all this time, a cellar full of floodwater: a word, a smell, a moon with thirteen rays, a song missing its second line.

*Two for the what?*

I set the coin on its edge and spun it, and it wobbled and fell, and I noticed then — finally, stupidly — that it had writing around the rim. Old script, angular, flowing, nothing like Corvane's blocky trade-tongue. I couldn't read it. But my eyes moved across it the way eyes move across your own name on a page, snagging, recognizing, and halfway along the rim my breath stopped, because there — in the middle of all that beautiful unreadable script — were two words I could read perfectly.

They weren't in any alphabet I knew. That's the part I can't explain, the part I've decided to stop trying to explain. The letters were foreign and the words were mine, and they said:

*vessarath ilen*

Homecoming. Or coming-home. Or possibly *the road home*, depending on a grammar I never learned and somehow never forgot.

I sat there until the lamp burned down to a thumb's width of oil, holding a coin that was contraband beyond category, evidence of a crime I couldn't name committed by a woman who had lied to me beautifully, and I thought about what happens next. Because it does happen next. That's the cruelty of finding a crack in yourself: you can't unfind it. I could report the coin. I could bury it. I could spend the rest of my career listening for the second line of a song I was never supposed to remember, jumping at woodsmoke, waking with salt on my lips.

Or I could go back to that shop, and ask the shopkeeper the question I'd carried home in my fist.

I put the coin in my breast pocket, over the heart, which felt dramatic and also correct. Then I pulled the sweep roster toward me to see when Thackeray's district fell to me again, and found, already inked in the clerk's neat hand, an order that hadn't been there that morning:

*By directive of the High Warden's office: Thackeray, L., premises of, elevated to priority inspection. Full inventory audit. Repeat visits authorized. — V.C.I.*

Someone else had noticed her too. Someone above me, with better reasons or worse ones, was already circling that little shop with its blue clay houses and its warped till, and whatever was hidden there — a girl, a ledger, a door, I didn't know yet, I only knew it was a *something* — was running out of time.

So, apparently, was I.

I blew out the lamp. In the dark, the coin against my chest gave off its faint stubborn warmth, and from the flooded cellar of myself, unbidden, unswallowed for the first time in eleven years, the word came up whole into the light of my open mouth.

"Vessarath," I said, to no one, to the dark, to a country that officially did not exist.

And somewhere under the city, under the Veil, under everything — I swear I heard it answer.
