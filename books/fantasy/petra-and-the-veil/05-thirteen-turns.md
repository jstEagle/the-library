## 5. Thirteen Turns

The cartographer's shop smelled of ink and old paper and, faintly, of a river I had never seen but somehow missed.

I found it the way Neth had insisted we find it: by walking with my eyes half-closed and letting the girl tug my sleeve at every corner. She couldn't speak Corvani — or wouldn't; there was a difference, and I was beginning to suspect it mattered — but she could speak to me in that other tongue, the one that lived behind my ribs like a second heartbeat. *Left here,* she'd said, in Vessani. *The man who draws lost things.* And so I went left, and down an alley the city planners had clearly given up on, to a door painted the grey of a sky deciding whether to rain.

The sign above it read HALLORAN & SONS, PURVEYORS OF FINE MAPS.

There were no sons. There was one old woman with ink-stained fingers and eyes the color of shallow water over sand, and when she opened the door before I knocked, she looked at me for a long moment and then said, in flawless Old Vessani:

"You're late."

"I didn't know I was expected," I managed.

"Nobody ever does." She stepped aside. "Come in before the street remembers you're standing there. It's bad manners, letting a city memorize your face."

Her name was Halloran, though she admitted, once the door was barred and the shutters drawn, that she hadn't been born with it. Her born name was a Vessani one, and when she said it aloud — softly, like setting down something fragile — Neth made a sound I had never heard from her, small and cracked, and pressed both hands over her mouth.

I understood then that the child hadn't heard her own language spoken by anyone but me since whatever day she'd crossed into Corvane. And I understood, too, why Halloran kept her true name locked away like contraband. In this country, it was exactly that.

* * *

Halloran's shop was a lie told in paper. The front room held honest maps of honest places: Corvane's provinces, its trade roads, its coastline drawn with loving precision. But the back room — reached through a curtain of oiled canvas — held the others. Maps of places that weren't supposed to exist. Not forbidden maps, which Corvane had plenty of, but impossible ones. Coastlines that matched no sea. Mountain ranges no expedition had ever confirmed. A country shaped, when I tilted my head, like a sleeping animal curled against a border that had nothing on the other side of it but blank parchment.

Vessany.

"They come to me in dreams," Halloran said, following my gaze. "Have done for forty years. I wake up and my hands draw what my head can't hold. Then I look at them in daylight and think: madwoman, drawing countries out of spite." She tapped the sleeping-animal coastline. "Then you walked in speaking the language those mountains are shaped like, and I've spent the last hour reconsidering my whole life."

Neth had drifted to the table where the Vessany map lay, and she stood over it the way people stand over graves. Then she did something that stopped my breath: she reached out and corrected it. One fingertip, tracing a river's course a finger-width south of where Halloran had dreamed it.

"That's right," Halloran whispered. "That's — gods. That's right. I always thought the delta looked wrong."

"She would know," I said. "It's her country."

And then, because there was no gentle way to carry it, I asked the question I'd walked across half of Corvane to ask. "You dream of Vessany. Do you dream of the Veil? Of when it ends?"

Halloran went still in the particular way of people who have been waiting decades for a question they dread.

"Sit down, keeper's girl," she said. "This isn't news you want standing up."

* * *

She laid it out the way cartographers do — precisely, without mercy, because mercy is a distortion and distortions kill travelers.

"The Veil isn't a wall. Walls hold. The Veil is more like..." she searched, "...a tide. It goes out and it comes in, and each time it comes in, it comes in weaker, because your grandmother bought it with a finite thing and finite things spend down. Every turn of the moon since the bargain, the Veil has thinned a little. Leaked a little. That leak is why I dream. Why you understand a dead language. Why—" she nodded at Neth, "—she could walk through it at all, and why a dead charm bloomed under her hands."

"So it's failing," I said. "Good. Let it fail."

"It's failing *slowly*, girl, and that's the cruelty of it. A Veil that fails slowly doesn't free anyone. It just starves everyone inside it. You've seen what the leak does to the outside — dreams, ghosts of language. Inside?" She shook her head. "Inside, the country is running out of itself. Memory first. Then shape. Then—"

"Neth," I said, because I am not quick but I am not stupid either. "What happens to Neth?"

Halloran looked at the child with an expression I have decided never to describe to anyone, because describing it would make it real.

"The heir is the seal," she said quietly. "The bargain was struck through the royal line. As long as a Vessani heir lives, the Veil holds — thinning, holding, thinning. When the moon completes its thirteenth turn from the night she arrived on your doorstep, the Veil won't fade. It will *close*. Every threshold, permanently. Sealed like a letter no one will ever open." She met my eyes. "And everything sealed inside dies with it. Including her."

The lamp flame leaned in a draft that wasn't there. Somewhere above us, Corvane went about its evening, buying bread, closing shutters, forgetting.

"How long?" My voice came out flat, which surprised me. I'd expected it to shake. "Thirteen turns. How many are gone?"

Halloran turned to the window, pulled back the shutter a crack, and looked at the moon hanging over the rooftops like a coin someone had bitten and found wanting.

"Four," she said. "Nine left."

* * *

I don't remember walking home. I remember Neth's hand in mine, small and dry and gripping hard, and I remember doing arithmetic the whole way like a woman counting coins she already knew wouldn't cover the debt.

Nine turns. Two hundred-odd days. To find a gate I'd only just learned existed beneath my own floor, learn how to open it, gather every scattered Vessani exile in a country that had made their existence illegal, evade the Wardens who had already visited my shop once and would visit again — and do it all before the sky finished counting.

When we got back to the shop, I lit every lamp I owned, sat down at my grandmother's ledger, and did the only thing I could think of.

I started making a list.

Not of charms. Not of stock. A list of names. Because Halloran had said something else, in that back room full of impossible maps, as I was leaving — something I'd carried home like a stone in my shoe:

*"You're not the first keeper's blood to come asking. There were others, over the years. Dreamers. People who hummed songs they couldn't place. They find each other, keeper's girl. The leak pulls us together like water finding water. If you mean to move a country, start with the ones who already ache toward it."*

So: a list. The clock-mender on Tanner's Row who whistled a lullaby in a language his neighbors assumed was nonsense. The midwife in Lowbridge whose grandmother left her a ring engraved with a sleeping animal. The three brothers who ran the ferry at Weir's Crossing and swore, when drunk, that they could smell a river that wasn't there. Names I'd collected over years without knowing I was collecting them, the way you collect shrapnel without noticing until it starts to pull.

I wrote until the lamps burned low. Neth fell asleep against my shoulder somewhere past midnight, and I let her, and I wrote her name at the top of the list, and underlined it twice.

* * *

They started coming within the week.

I never sent invitations. I told no one anything. But the list names began appearing at my door at closing time, one and two at a time, wearing the sheepish expressions of people arriving at a party they aren't sure they were invited to.

The clock-mender came first, a narrow man named Osk who stood in my doorway turning his cap in his hands and said, "I don't know why I'm here. I've never been here. But I dreamed about your shutters, and in the dream they were blue, and mine aren't, and yours are, and I thought — well. Here I am."

I let him in. What else does a keeper do?

The midwife came with the ring and a basket of bread and the unshakable calm of a woman who has delivered enough babies to know that some things arrive when they arrive and screaming doesn't help. The ferry brothers came together and filled my back room entirely, arguing in whispers about whether the river they smelled was the same river on Halloran's map (it was; I checked; they were insufferable about being right).

By the eighth night there were eleven of them crowded between my shelves of outlawed charms, drinking tea because I owned nothing stronger I dared serve, and I stood at the counter looking at them and felt something I didn't have a word for.

They were strangers. Most of them I'd never exchanged ten words with before this week. And yet they arranged themselves around Neth the way iron filings arrange around a lodestone — not because anyone instructed them, not because they understood what she was, but because some part of them, the part that hummed the lullaby and smelled the river, recognized her. Their country's living seal, sitting cross-legged on my counter eating bread, watching them all with grave dark eyes.

"We should introduce ourselves properly," the midwife said. "In the old way. So we know each other when it matters."

And eleven adults, in a charm shop in a country that forbade everything they were, stood and gave their born names — their true names, the contraband ones, the ones that would cost them everything if a Warden heard — aloud, one after another, like lighting candles in a windless room.

Osk. Maren. The brothers Bren, Tally, and Joss. And seven more, each name falling into the quiet like a stone into a well, each one a small act of treason performed without hesitation, because I had asked without asking, because the list had called them, because water finds water.

When they finished, they all looked at me. Waiting.

"Oh no," I said. "No. I'm not — I'm the shopkeeper. I'm the one with the floor and the tea. I'm not—"

"You're the keeper's blood," Maren said, in the tone she probably used on laboring mothers. "You have the ledger. You have the child. And you're the one who made the list." She smiled, tired and certain. "Leadership isn't a title, Lior Thackeray. It's whoever starts moving first while everyone else is still deciding. You started moving first."

I looked at Neth. Neth looked back at me and said something in Vessani, soft, and I understood it without trying, the way you understand your own heartbeat:

*They were always coming. You were just the door.*

Which was, I reflected later, exactly the sort of thing doors get told right before someone expects them to open.

* * *

I closed the shop early the next evening — earlier than I ever had, earlier than was safe — and took the ledger down into the cellar, because there was a question I'd been circling for nine days like a bird refusing to land, and I could not lead anyone anywhere while it circled me.

The gate was there. I could feel it now that I knew to feel: a hush in the stones, a held breath in the dark behind the shelf where grandmother's strongest charms had always been stored. Sable had hidden a country's last door under her granddaughter's stockroom and never said a word.

I put my palm flat against the wall and spoke to the silence, in Vessani, the first full sentence I'd ever composed in it:

*Nine turns. Tell me how to open you.*

The hush shifted. And beneath my hand, in the dark, something answered — not in words, but in a slow, cold pulse, like a heartbeat under stone. Like a door, hearing its name.

Behind me, on the cellar stairs, a floorboard creaked.

I turned, heart hammering, and found Neth standing in the lamplight with her finger pressed to her lips — and beyond her, at the top of the stairs, framed against the shop door I had sworn I'd locked, a figure in a Warden's grey coat, motionless, looking down at us both.
