## 6. The Market of Borrowed Names

*(Neth speaks; Lior translates. The words below are hers, carried across the gap between languages the way water is carried in cupped hands — some always lost.)*

The market did not exist until you agreed to enter it.

That is what the man at the rope told us, and I understood him before Lior finished translating, which made her go still and look at me the way she does when I frighten her without meaning to. He spoke Corvani, the flat gray tongue of this city where magic is a crime and remembering is apparently one too. But underneath his words ran another current, older, and that current was mine.

*Welcome home,* the current said. *Mind the toll.*

Lior paid him in coin. He took it, weighed it on his palm, and shook his head. "Coin buys you the rope," he said. "The market takes its own."

I am nine years old, or I was when Vessany was taken from the world, and I have been nine for a very long time inside the Veil, where time pools like spilled honey. So perhaps I should not say what I said next. Perhaps a child should not know how to bargain. But I was born in a palace that no longer exists, to a house whose seal I carry under my skin like a swallowed star, and palaces teach you early that everything has a price and most prices are lies.

"What does it take?" I asked, in my own tongue, and let Lior pour me into his language.

He smiled with all his teeth and none of his warmth. "Memories," he said. "True ones. Of the country behind the Veil. The market eats well these days."

* * *

You should understand what the smugglers' market is, because no one explained it to me either and I would have liked someone to.

It lives in the hollow under the Saltbridge, where the canal water moves too slow to reflect anything honestly. Lanterns hung on fishhooks. Stalls made of overturned boats. And everywhere, everywhere, people selling pieces of themselves.

Not flesh. Worse. A woman at the first stall sold the smell of bread her grandmother used to bake, and I watched the smell leave her — watched her face go smooth as an egg where the memory had lived, watched her smile at a customer with nothing behind it but the shape of smiling. A boy my own age sold the sound of his mother singing, and after, he kept opening his mouth like a door that no longer led anywhere.

They were all Vessani. That is the joke the market tells and no one laughs at. The Veil erased our country, but it could not erase the people who remembered it, so the people learned to sell the remembering instead. To strangers. To collectors. To things wearing coats that wanted to taste a country they had helped to kill.

And here is the part that made Lior grab my hand too hard: every payment hollowed the seller. Not metaphorically. I could see it. The memories went out of them like light going out of windows, one room at a time, until what stood behind the stall was a person-shaped building with nobody home on the upper floors.

"They don't have to sell," Lior murmured, translating mostly for herself.

"Yes they do," I said. "Passage costs truth. And we are the last ones who have any."

* * *

Our passage was a barge, and the bargeman was called Ondrel, and he had eyes the color of canal water, which is to say the color of things drowned.

"Four souls to the threshold at Greywater Bend," he said. "Price is four truths. One each." He looked at our little company — Lior with her shopkeeper's hands and her liar's new heart; Tammas the cartographer who had already sold so much of himself that he walked slightly sideways, like a book missing pages; me. "Pay now. Pay true. The market knows a false memory. It curdles."

Tammas went first, because Tammas had decided somewhere along the way that he was our guide and guides go first into rivers.

He sold the harvest festival. All of it. The lantern-boats on the Vessa, the candied quince, the year the twins fell in the shallows and came up laughing. I knew the festival — everyone in Vessany knew the festival — and so I felt it leave him. It went out of the air like heat off a stone. And Tammas stood there afterward, blinking, and asked, quite politely, why his mouth tasted like quince.

"That's how it works," Ondrel said, not unkindly. "You keep the ghost of the thing. Just not the thing."

"You could have warned me it was *that* festival," Tammas said, wry even while hollowed, which I think is the bravest thing I saw anyone do that night.

"It's always that festival," Ondrel said. "It's always whatever you loved most. The market has a sweet tooth."

Then it was my turn, and I will tell you what I almost sold, because keeping secrets is how countries die quietly.

I almost sold my mother's voice. She sang the dawn-song of the royal house every morning of my life before the Veil, and I thought: I am small, I am the heir, I must stay whole, I will give away one morning-song and keep the rest of her. I opened my mouth to sell it.

And Lior put her hand over my mouth.

"No," she said. Then, in my language, badly, the way she is learning it back-to-front like someone walking downstairs in the dark: "Not you. You've given enough just by existing. Take mine instead."

Ondrel raised an eyebrow. "You're not Vessani, charm-seller."

"I'm worse," Lior said. "I'm the granddaughter of the woman who sold your country. I have Vessany in me secondhand — through her ledger, through her stories, through every curse she left in that shop. It counts. Make it count."

* * *

Here is what I know about Lior's mother, gathered the way dust gathers, from small things: a lullaby hummed over stock-taking. A way of folding cloth that Lior does without knowing she does it. A grief she keeps in the same drawer as her scissors.

She stood in front of the bargeman for a long moment, choosing. I watched her choose, and I want you to understand that choosing is the whole of courage; everything else is just the noise courage makes afterward.

"The harbor at Vessa-city," Lior said finally. "My mother took me there. In the last summer, before—" Her voice caught on the word *before*, which in her language is a very short word for such a long fall. "There were white sails. Hundreds. She held me up so I could see over the rail, and she said, *remember this, little one, remember it exactly, because someday they'll tell you it never happened.*"

She paused.

"She was right," Lior said. "Everyone told me it never happened. For twenty years. I started to believe them. This is the only proof I have left that my mother didn't lie to me."

Ondrel's face did something complicated. "That'll do," he said softly, and reached out —

— and I felt it happen. I felt it the way you feel weather change. Something true went out of Lior Thackeray, and the air where it had been was colder.

She swayed. I caught her arm. She looked down at me and smiled, and the smile was whole, but behind her eyes one window had gone dark, and I knew which window. Somewhere in her, a woman holding a child above a harbor rail simply wasn't anymore. Lior would remember that her mother had existed. She would not remember being held up to see the sails. She had sold the proof and kept the wound.

"Well," she said, wry as ever, steady as ever, lying as ever, "now I know how Tammas feels. Quince?"

"Quince," Tammas confirmed solemnly.

Ondrel jerked his chin toward the barge. "Get aboard. Greywater Bend by the dark of the moon. And charm-seller—" he looked at her with those drowned eyes, and for a moment the market-man fell away and something older looked out, something that remembered being a country, "—the heir pays nothing tonight. House rules. Old ones."

"I don't understand," Lior began.

"You will," Ondrel said. "The seal she carries remembers who kept faith with it. Even here. Even now."

* * *

We sailed out from under the Saltbridge, and the market folded itself shut behind us like a fan, and I sat at the barge rail watching Lior.

She was doing inventory. I recognized it because I do it too — counting what remains after a loss, touching each thing twice. Her lips moved. Names, probably. Charms. Customers. Small safe things from the small safe life she had already burned behind her without admitting it.

"Lior," I said.

She looked at me. In the lantern-light her face was tired in a way that had nothing to do with sleep.

"In Vessany," I said, "when someone gives a truth for another person, we have a word for it. *Vessari.* It means — it means the debt can't be paid back, only passed forward. You don't owe me. You owe the country. You pay it by getting me home."

Lior laughed — a wet, cracked laugh, quickly repaired. "Your language has a word for everything."

"My language has a word for *everything that matters*," I corrected. "Yours has words for the rest."

She pulled her coat around my shoulders against the river cold, and we rode toward Greywater Bend through the sleeping city, past walls where Warden lanterns burned their gray, orderly light.

And that was when Tammas, at the tiller, went rigid.

"Lanterns," he said. "Behind us. Four boats. Gray lanterns."

Wardens.

Ondrel swore in three languages and cut the lamp. We slid into darkness, into the reeds, into the cold breath of the canal, while behind us the gray lights grew — patient, methodical, closing like a hand.

And standing in the reed-shadow, I felt the seal under my skin wake and turn toward the nearest boat like a compass needle finding north.

Someone on that boat was Vessani.

Someone on that boat was *remembering*.
