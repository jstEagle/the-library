## 7. Ilsa's Choice

The orders came at dawn, and they were signed by a man whose handwriting I had never trusted.

*By authority of the Wardens of Corvane: detain one Lior Thackeray, charm-merchant, premises on Candlewick Lane, for suspicion of trafficking in outlawed magic. Search and seal the property. Report to Commander Doss by nightfall.*

Commander Doss. A man who alphabetized his informants and smiled with exactly as many teeth as the situation required. He'd written my name at the bottom of the warrant — *execute with all diligence, Capt. Vey* — and beneath it, almost as an afterthought, *no irregularities.*

No irregularities. As if he knew there might be some. As if he suspected that Captain Ilsa Vey, eleven years decorated, eleven years obedient, had been standing in that charm shop three weeks ago holding a coin she couldn't name and letting a crime walk out of her hands like a bird released from a snare.

I sat at my desk in the Wardenhouse and read the warrant four times. Around me the morning shift clattered into motion — boots on stone, the rattle of iron cuffs being counted out, someone complaining about the rain. Ordinary sounds. The sounds of a city that worked because everyone agreed not to look too closely at what held it up.

Eleven years ago they'd found me at the border of nowhere, a girl of fourteen with no papers, no name anyone could verify, and no memories older than a fortnight. The Wardens hadn't asked where I came from. That was the kindness of them, or the cruelty — hard to say which. They'd given me a name, a uniform, a purpose, and a small brass token stamped with the sunburst of Corvane, and told me the past was a country best left unvisited.

I had believed them. Gods, how completely I had believed them.

I folded the warrant into my coat, over my heart, where paper goes when you intend to keep it close — to obey it, or to burn it, and haven't yet decided which.

* * *

Candlewick Lane smelled of wet wool and frying onions, the way it always did before market opened. I went alone, which was itself an irregularity; a search-and-seal of a licensed premises required two Wardens minimum, and Doss would know I'd come solo. Let him know. There was a part of me — a part I'd been feeding scraps for three weeks now, behind the locked door of my own skull — that wanted him to know. That wanted him to ask me why, so I could finally hear myself answer.

Lior Thackeray opened the door before I knocked.

She looked worse than the last time. Grey under the eyes, hair escaping its braid, a smudge of soot along one jaw as though she'd been crawling through her own chimney. Behind her, the shop was dim, every lamp hooded, and the air had a taste to it — ozone and old paper and something sweeter underneath, something that made my back teeth ache.

"Captain Vey." Her voice was steady. Her hands were not; I saw them tremble until she pressed them flat against her apron. "Come about the licensing renewal? It's not due for another month."

"You know why I'm here."

"I suspect I do." She stepped aside. "Then again, I've suspected a great many things lately that turned out to be wrong. Come in, Captain. Mind the third floorboard. It bites."

It was, I thought absurdly, the politest arrest I had ever conducted. She made tea while I walked the shop. She answered my questions with the precise honesty of a woman who has decided lying is no longer worth the effort but isn't ready to hand over the whole truth either. Yes, she dealt in charms. Charms of a superstitious nature, luck-blessings, mood-stones, nothing functional. No, she had never heard of any girl matching Neth's description. (A lie. A clean, deliberate lie, delivered while pouring, and I let it stand, and hated myself for letting it stand, and hated myself more for the relief.)

"The cellar," I said finally.

Something moved behind her eyes. "The cellar's flooded."

"Then I'll want to see how well it floods."

She led me down the stairs with the gait of a woman walking to her own gallows, and I confess I took a certain grim comfort in it. This was familiar ground. This was the work. A suspect, a search, a sealed door. The world arranged in categories I understood.

Except the cellar wasn't flooded.

And the door at the far end of it — set into the stone where no door should be, where last month there had been nothing but shelving and salt-pork barrels — was open a hand's width, and light was coming through the gap.

Not lamplight. Not the grey Corvani dawn filtering through the street-grate above. This light was gold and green together, the color of late afternoon in a place I had never been, and it fell across the cellar floor in a long warm stripe, and my knees nearly went.

Because I knew that light.

I didn't know it. That was the whole architecture of my life — I didn't know anything, I was a woman built on a foundation of fog. But my body knew it. My body reacted the way a body reacts to its mother's voice in a crowd: all at once, below thought, below permission. My throat closed. My eyes filled. I stood in a smuggler's cellar in Corvane with tears running down my face at a stripe of sunlight, and Captain Vey, eleven years decorated, could do absolutely nothing about it.

"Captain." Lior's voice, very quiet. "You should know what this is before you decide anything."

"Don't tell me." I heard my own voice crack and despised it. "Don't— if you tell me, then I have to choose, and I'm not—"

"You already chose," she said gently. "Three weeks ago. The coin in your till. You think I don't know? You think I didn't watch you hold it like it was the first true thing anyone ever put in your hands?"

"It's contraband," I said stupidly. "Foreign currency. I should have impounded it."

"It's a Vessani half-crown," she said. "Minted the year before the Veil. And you wept over it in my shop and told me it reminded you of a word you couldn't remember."

I hadn't told her that. I was certain I hadn't told her that. But grief makes liars of memory, and perhaps I had, in the moment between the coin and the leaving, said something true without meaning to.

She pushed the door wider.

* * *

I will try to describe what lay beyond, knowing I will fail, because the failure is the point. The Veil did not erase Vessany. It erased our ability to perceive it. What stood beyond that door was a country wearing its absence like a shroud — hills the color of embers under a low golden sky, terraced fields gone wild with forty years of no hands, a road winding down toward a town whose rooftops caught the light like the scales of a sleeping fish. And everywhere, everywhere, words. Carved into gateposts, painted on walls, stitched into banners hanging rotted from poles: words in a script I could not read, and each one of them landed in my chest like a hand pressing on a bruise.

I stood on the threshold and wept at words I could not read.

That is the sentence I will carry to whatever grave receives me. Not the arrests, not the eleven years, not the moment I burned my orders. *I stood on the threshold and wept at words I could not read.* Because somewhere under the fog, under the brass token and the borrowed name, some portion of me could read them — could feel their meaning the way you feel warmth through a wall — and that portion was screaming.

One banner hung nearest the gate, half-fallen, its letters broad and black. I traced them with my fingers through the cold threshold-air, and my mouth shaped sounds I had never learned, and the shape of them was: *home.*

Or near enough. Or maybe only what a hollow thing imagines home to be. I have no way to check. That is the wound of it, the wound that will not close: I cannot verify my own longing. I am homesick for a country I cannot prove exists, and the proof is standing right in front of me, and I still can't read it.

Behind me, Lior Thackeray said nothing. She had the wisdom, or the mercy, to let a person drown quietly.

When I could speak, I said: "There are others. In the city. People who feel the way I feel — who hum songs they don't know, who dream in a language they've never heard. I've interviewed dozens of them over the years. Disturbed persons, we called them. Veil-sickness." I laughed, and it came out broken. "We had a *diagnosis* for homesickness."

"We did," she said. "My grandmother invented it."

"And now?"

"Now the Veil is failing. Now there's a girl downstairs who speaks the old tongue, and a moon on its thirteenth turn, and a door that won't stay shut much longer no matter what anyone wants." She paused. "Now there's a choice, Captain. For all of us. And I won't pretend it's an easy one, because it cost me—" she stopped, recalibrated. "It costs. Whatever you choose, it costs."

I took out the warrant.

Doss's handwriting looked smaller than it had at dawn. *Execute with all diligence. No irregularities.* I thought about the night shift, about the cells under the Wardenhouse, about the smooth machinery of a city that had filed my whole life under *recovered, unclaimed, repurposed.* I thought about the diagnosis. *Veil-sickness.* We had named the disease and never once asked what it was a sickness *for.*

I struck a match against the cellar wall.

The warrant caught, curled, went to ash in the space of ten breaths. I dropped it on the flagstones and watched the last corner glow and die. Eleven years of obedience, reduced to a smell like burnt sugar.

"There," I said. My voice sounded strange to me — lighter, and terrified. "I've done it. I've committed treason in a charm-shop cellar. My mother would be proud, if I knew who my mother was."

"She's here," Lior said softly. "Somewhere in there. Or her grave is. Half the remnant came out of Vessany's villages, Captain. If you were found at the border at fourteen—"

"Don't." I held up a hand. "Please. Don't finish that sentence yet."

Because here is the truth, the thread-thin truth I am writing down so I don't lose it: I burned the orders, but I did not step through the door. I stood on the Corvani side of the threshold with ash on my boots and wept at words I couldn't read, and when Lior asked me — kindly, without pressure — whether I was coming, I said:

"Not yet."

Not yet. Not because I doubt. Doubt would be cleaner. But because there are thirty Wardens in that building who hum songs they don't know, who dream in colors that don't exist in Corvane, who carry a homesickness with no address. Because someone has to stand in the gap while the rest of them find their way to this cellar. Because if I walk through tonight, I abandon them to Doss's smile and his alphabetized informants, and I cannot build a home on that foundation. I won't.

So I gave her what I had. The patrol rotations. The names of the sympathetic. The location of Doss's files on the "Veil-sick," a list of sixty-one souls who were about to become a list of refugees. I gave her everything except myself, and she took it all with both hands and thanked me as though I'd given her the moon.

"The gate opens within days," she said at the top of the stairs. "Thirteenth turn. When it does, Captain — when the Veil comes down and the country comes back — there will be a place for you on whichever side of it you're standing. That's a promise. First law of the new Vessany, if I have anything to say about it: nobody gets erased twice."

I walked back to the Wardenhouse in the rain, warrant-less, treasonous, and unbearably awake. At the door I turned once and looked east, toward Candlewick Lane, toward a door I could no longer pretend I hadn't seen.

Tomorrow I would face Doss with no report and no excuse. Tomorrow the machinery would turn on me. But tonight — tonight I let myself say the word I'd traced off that rotten banner, the word my mouth knew and my mind refused:

"*Vessany.*"

Just once. Quietly. Into the rain.

And somewhere across the city, I swear the air tasted different after — sweeter, closer, like a country leaning in to hear whether I'd say it again.
