## 8. The Gate Beneath the Shop

There is a particular kind of silence that lives under a charm shop at midnight, and I had spent thirty years pretending it was just the quiet of a cellar full of sleeping stock. Bottles settling. Charms cooling. The ordinary creak of an ordinary building.

It was not that. It had never been that. It was the silence of something holding its breath.

I stood on the third stair from the bottom with a lantern in one hand and my grandmother's ledger in the other, and for the first time in my life I let myself actually look at the wall at the back of the cellar instead of past it.

The bricks were wrong. They had always been wrong — laid in a pattern no Corvani mason used, mortared with something faintly blue when the light hit it sideways, warm to the touch in winter and cool in summer, which any sensible builder would tell you was impossible. I had stacked crates against that wall since I was nineteen years old. I had hung drying herbs over it. I had, in a fit of youthful rebellion, nailed a shelf to it, and the nail had come out the next morning lying neatly on the floor like a returned letter.

"Right," I said aloud, because saying things aloud makes them real, and because Neth was watching me with those enormous dark eyes and I refused to look like a woman having second thoughts in front of a nine-year-old. "Let's wake it up."

* * *

Here is what the ledger said, in Sable's cramped hand, on the page she had clearly written last and bound first:

*The gate opens to keeper's blood and heir's voice together. Not before. Not after. Not alone. I made sure of that, because I did not trust myself to be reasonable about it, and I do not trust you either, little bird.*

Little bird. Thirty years dead and she was still finding new ways to be insufferable.

We had spent two days preparing, which in practical terms meant that Tam Voss — exile cartographer, professional pessimist, and the only man I knew who could draw a map of a country that officially did not exist — had moved into my spare room and begun issuing instructions nobody asked for. It meant that eleven Vessani exiles, gathered one by one from the corners of Corvane where they'd hidden their accents like contraband, now crowded my cellar smelling of river water and nerves. It meant that Ilsa Vey's note — delivered by a street child, unsigned, four words long: *They know. Hurry. Please.* — sat folded in my pocket like a coal.

And it meant that Neth had practiced.

That was the part that undid me. A mute girl who had not spoken since the night she arrived, who communicated in touches and expressions and a sign-language of her own invention, had spent two days learning to say six words of Old Vessani out loud. *Open the door, I am home.* She'd stand in front of the mirror in my upstairs room mouthing them first, then whispering them, her voice coming back rusty and small, like a hinge that hadn't turned in years. Every time she got through all six, she'd look at me for approval, and every time I gave it, and neither of us mentioned that we were both terrified.

Because here was the other thing the ledger said, further down, in handwriting that had gone shaky:

*When the gate opens, everything the Veil swallowed comes back at once. Magic will pour into Corvane like water into a cracked bowl. There is no opening it gently. Forgive me, little bird. I could never find a gentle way.*

* * *

Midnight. The moon hung outside the high cellar window, fat and three-quarters gone, seven turns into its thirteenth lap. Tam had done the arithmetic twice and gotten the same terrible answer both times: five turns left, maybe less if the moon was feeling ambitious.

The exiles stood in a half-circle behind me. An old woman named Merrit who'd been a weaver before weaving became illegal for people with her bloodline. Two brothers who ran a fish stall and spoke Vessani only in their sleep. A boy of maybe sixteen called Fen who had followed us from the night-market and refused, with the magnificent stubbornness of his age, to be sent home. Eleven souls. The entire surviving remnant of a country, and they were standing in a charm-shop cellar between barrels of moth-repellent tincture.

Neth stood beside me at the wall. She had washed her feet — she still refused shoes, but she'd conceded to washing — and she wore the seal of her house on a cord around her neck, the little silver sun-and-thorn that had started all of this.

"Keeper's blood," I said. "Heir's voice."

"Say it, don't sing it," Tam murmured. "Sable's notes are clear. This isn't a spell, it's a key turning. You don't hum at a lock."

"You've never seen me hum at a lock," I said. "It works surprisingly often."

Nobody laughed. Fair enough.

I took out the knife I'd used for blood-charms my whole career — small, honest work, a drop in a bottle to bind a luck-spell, nothing anyone would hang you for. My hands were steady, which surprised me. Underneath the steadiness, somewhere down deep where I kept the things I didn't look at directly, there was a howling. But up top, where it counted, I was calm as a shopkeeper counting change.

I opened my palm over the mortar line and let the blood fall. Three drops. The ledger had been specific: three, no more, because the wall remembered generosity as greed.

For a moment, nothing. The blood sat on the blue-grey mortar like blood on any wall, and I had time to think one complete and humiliating thought — *you've murdered your palm for nothing, Thackeray, buy some salve* —

Then the wall breathed.

Not metaphor. The bricks expanded, very slightly, the way a chest does, and the mortar drank the blood, and a seam appeared down the center of the wall — hairline, glowing, the color of dawn through a keyhole. Warm air came through it, smelling of cut grass and woodsmoke and rain on stone, a smell I had never known and recognized instantly, the way you recognize your own name shouted across a crowded room.

Home. That was the smell of home. A home I'd never stood in.

"Neth," I said, and my voice cracked on it, which ruined all my careful composure, and I found I didn't care.

She put her small hand flat against the seam, beside my bleeding one, and said, in a voice like a bell rung once in an empty church:

*"Open the door. I am home."*

* * *

Later — much later, when I had time to be precise about anything — I would understand that we were early. That the gate was meant to open on the thirteenth turn, gently, in its own season, and that opening it seven turns ahead was like cracking a dam with a hammer instead of waiting for the spillway. Sable's ledger had warned me. I had read the warning. I had decided, in the way of all Thackerays, that warnings were for other people.

The seam split.

Light poured out — not the polite dawn-colored glow but a flood, white-gold, silent, total, and with it came everything else. I felt it go through me like cold water and hot iron at once: magic, raw and unrationed, the thing this city had been starved of for sixty years, returning all at once to soil that had forgotten how to hold it. The drying herbs on the wall above us burst into bloom. Every bottle in my cellar lit from within like stained glass. Somewhere overhead, in the shop proper, I heard my entire stock of charms go off simultaneously — a carillon of luck-bells, love-knots, and one extremely enthusiastic firework I'd been saving for a customer I disliked.

And beyond the walls of the shop, the city itself woke up. I felt it the way you feel a crowd go quiet: fountains running backward, gutter-lamps flaring green, old sealed wells singing. Sixty years of suppressed enchantment, everywhere at once, in a city whose Wardens had spent those same sixty years perfecting the art of suppression.

"They'll have felt that in the Citadel," Tam said. He wasn't panicking, which I appreciated; he'd simply gone very still and very pale, the way men do when the map stops matching the territory. "Lior. They'll have felt that across the whole city."

"I know."

"The gate's open. Can they come through now? Can we?"

I looked at the doorway in the wall. Beyond it — impossibly, wonderfully — there was a hillside. Long grass moving in wind. Distant mountains wearing snow like old lace. A road going down toward a valley where lights were beginning to kindle, one by one, as if the country itself was striking matches.

Vessany. Returned. Real. Breathing.

"It's open," I said. "But it's not stable. Look at the edges."

The edges of the doorway shivered like heat-haze. Through them I could see the grass, and through the grass, faintly, the cellar wall — both at once, overlapping, the Veil torn but not fallen, a wound rather than a door. Anyone crossing now would cross into a place that was only half-arrived. And behind us, distant but unmistakable, bells had begun to ring — not the hour bells. The Warden bells. All of them.

Merrit gripped her walking stick. "How long do we have, girl?"

I listened to the bells. Counted them. Ilsa had taught me the pattern without meaning to, complaining about it over tea: *three rings means sweep, five means cordon, seven—*

Seven.

"Cordon," I said. "Every street around the market quarter. They'll seal us in and then come down our throats. We have perhaps an hour." I turned to face them — eleven exiles and one heir, all looking at me as if I were the sort of person who knew things. The joke of the century. "An hour to get everyone through a wound in the world that might unmake you, while the entire Warden corps of Corvane comes to watch."

Fen, sixteen and immortal, grinned at me. "So we run, then."

"So we run," I agreed.

I picked Neth up — she was light as a bundle of sticks, another crime I intended to answer for someday — and stepped toward the shining, shivering edge of home.

Behind me, up the cellar stairs, the shop door splintered.

They were early too.
