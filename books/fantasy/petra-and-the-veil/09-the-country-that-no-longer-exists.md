## 9. The Country That No Longer Exists

*Lior*

The gate had been open for eleven minutes when the first bell rang.

I counted them the way you count anything when counting is all that's left of your dignity — one bell per Warden tower, and Corvane has nine towers, and by the fourth I'd stopped pretending to myself that they might be ringing for something else. A festival, perhaps. A very enthusiastic wedding.

"Keeper." Old Maren's voice, cracked as river clay. She was the oldest of us, older than the Veil itself in her bones if not her years, and she stood at the threshold with her hands full of nothing — she'd left everything behind, like the rest of them, forty-one exiles carrying their whole country in their pockets and their throats. "How long do we have?"

"As long as it takes," I said, which was a lie, and she knew it, and let me have it anyway. Kindness comes in strange shapes at the end of the world.

The gate beneath my shop had never been meant for crowds. It was a door, singular, domestic, sized for a grandmother slipping through with a lantern and a debt. Now it gaped wide enough to drive a cart through, its edges bleeding light the color of a bruise deciding whether to heal, and every soul who stepped through it gasped — because on the other side, visible now, undeniable, was Vessany.

Not the Vessany of stories. The real one: hills the green of old bottle glass, a river running silver-brown with honest silt, terraced orchards gone wild in sixty years of no one picking them. A country that had been holding its breath since before I was born, and was now, audibly, exhaling.

Neth went through third, after Maren and the twins from the tannery district. The girl paused at the threshold, looked back at me, and said something in Old Vessani that I understood without translation, which is how I knew the Veil was already dying inside me too:

*You are coming. You promised.*

"I'm coming," I said. "Keepers go last. It's in the contract."

There was no contract. There was a ledger, and a bargain, and a ghost waiting at the heart of the gate who had been my grandmother's shadow for sixty years, and I was fairly sure none of those things were going to let me keep even this small lie.

Behind us, up the cellar stairs, through the shop floor, the front door of Thackeray Charms splintered off its hinges.

* * *

*Ilsa*

I had burned my orders an hour ago. The paper had gone up beautifully — Wardens' orders are written on good vellum, which burns slow and smells faintly of lavender, because someone in the Quartermaster's office has a sense of humor about damnation.

Now I stood in the wrecked doorway of a charm shop with six of my people behind me and a choice already made sitting in my chest like a swallowed coal.

"Sergeant," I said. "The shopkeeper and the fugitives are below. There will be a door down there that does not appear on any survey."

Corporal Dain frowned. "Captain, the surveys—"

"The surveys," I said, "are wrong about more than doors." And then, because loyalty deserves honesty even when it's being broken: "I am going down those stairs. What you do is yours to decide. But know that what's below isn't treason. It's the thing we were all made to forget, and I am done forgetting."

Dain looked at me for a long moment. Then he unslung his truncheon — not his sword, note, the truncheon, the tool of arrests rather than executions — and said, "I'll hold the street, Captain. In case anyone else comes asking."

"In case anyone else comes asking," I agreed, and we both pretended that wasn't the same as him choosing sides, and we both knew perfectly well it was.

The stairs smelled of lamp oil and old stone and, underneath it, something that made my eyes sting without warning. Rosemary. Rain on warm slate. A smell I had no memory attached to and grieved anyway, the way you grieve a name torn out of a letter.

At the bottom, the light was the color of a healing wound, and a woman with soot on her sleeves was arguing with a doorway.

* * *

*Lior*

Sable's guardian stood in the heart of the gate, and it wore her face, and it was not her.

I want to be clear about that, because grief makes liars of us all. The thing standing between my exiles and home had my grandmother's cheekbones, her crooked left eyebrow, the small scar on her chin from a kettle she'd sworn for fifty years had been a duel. But its eyes were the Veil's eyes — patient, gray, absolutely without appetite — and when it spoke, it spoke in Sable's voice the way a music box plays a song: faithfully, and dead.

"You cannot pass, keeper," it said. "The terms stand. One country hidden, one keeper bound, until the thirteenth turn closes all accounts."

"The thirteenth turn is four days away," I said. "And yet here we all are, early. You'll forgive me for renegotiating."

"It cannot be renegotiated. It was sealed in blood and name."

"So was mine," I said, and held up my hand, and let it see the cut I'd made on the stairs — keeper's blood, heir's voice, the old recipe, already drying brown along my palm. "Everything worth signing is. That's rather the point of signatures, isn't it? They're supposed to mean something later. Otherwise we'd just shout our promises into wells."

The guardian tilted Sable's head. Somewhere behind me, Maren made a sound that was half prayer and half laugh; the Vessani had clearly decided I was either their salvation or their lunatic, and were prepared to embrace either outcome.

"What would you offer?" the guardian asked.

"Not offer. Amend." My heart was going like a drummer boy's, but my voice came out steady, and I've decided to be proud of that forever. "My grandmother bought Vessany's hiding with her name. Fine. A name buys hiding. But hiding was always meant to be temporary — she wrote that herself, in the ledger, page forty-one, in ink she mixed with her own tears because she was exactly the sort of dramatic woman who would. *Until the country can stand in daylight again.* Well. Look at it." I pointed past the guardian, at the hills, the river, the wild orchards heavy with unpicked fruit. "It's been standing in the dark for sixty years, doing push-ups. It can stand."

"The Veil protects—"

"The Veil *erases*," I said. "It erased Neth's parents' names off their own gravestones. It erased a Warden captain's childhood and left her crying at a coin she couldn't explain. Protection that costs the protected their existence isn't protection. It's just a smaller cage with better curtains."

The guardian was silent. The gate hummed. Behind me, boots on the stairs — Ilsa, arriving at the worst possible moment, or possibly the best; with Ilsa it had stopped being possible to tell the difference around chapter four.

"And your terms?" the guardian asked at last. "What would you write instead?"

I took a breath. This was the part I'd rehearsed on the stairs while Wardens kicked in my front door, and it still came out half-sure, which I've come to believe is the only honest way anything true gets said.

"One country, returned, whole, remembered — every gravestone restored, every stolen name given back, starting with hers." I nodded at Ilsa without turning around. "In exchange: one keeper, bound half to each side. Not a prisoner. A hinge. I stay at the border — the new border, the one we're about to invent — and I hold the door *open*. Permanently. No more bargains that end in forgetting. Just a door, and someone whose job it is to keep it unlocked."

"That is not hiding."

"No," I agreed. "It's the opposite of hiding. It's the scariest thing anyone's ever proposed in this cellar, and my grandmother hid an entire country down here."

The guardian looked at me with Sable's face, and for one moment — one flicker, like a candle remembering fire — something moved behind the gray eyes that was not the Veil at all. Something tired. Something that had been keeping a dead woman's promise for sixty years and wanted, desperately, to put it down.

"She would have hated this," the guardian said, in Sable's voice, but warmer now, less music box. "She hated doors left open. Drafts."

"She hated a lot of things," I said. "She did them anyway. It's genetic."

The guardian extended Sable's hand, palm up. On it lay a pen.

Of course it did. Of course the final instrument of a blood bargain struck by a charm-witch would be a pen. I took it, and it was warm, and it weighed exactly as much as everything I was about to lose and everything I was about to choose, which is to say: not much, and everything.

"Sign," said the guardian, "and it is done."

I signed.

* * *

*Ilsa*

I arrived in time to watch a shopkeeper sign a country back into existence.

I want to report that I did something useful. I want to report that I fought, or covered the retreat, or stood guard with drawn steel while destiny got notarized. What I actually did was stop three steps from the bottom of the stairs, hand on my sword I had no intention of drawing, and watch Lior Thackeray — soot-faced, bleeding, terrified, magnificent — trade her whole life for a doorway, and think, with perfect clarity:

*That is what service looks like. I have been wearing the wrong uniform.*

The gate flared white. The guardian — Sable's face, Sable's not-face — folded inward like a letter being closed, and the last expression on it, the last thing before it went, was relief. I know relief. I've worn it myself, once, burning vellum in a cold office.

Then the light passed through all of us like weather, and I remembered.

All of it. A kitchen with rosemary hanging from the beam. A mother singing in a language the Wardens' schools would later beat out of me. A coin pressed into my child's palm — *for luck, little fox, for luck across the water* — the day the soldiers came and the maps changed and the word *Vessany* began to taste like nothing at all.

I sat down on the stairs. Dignity is for people with somewhere better to be.

"Captain Vey," Lior said, offering me a hand up, her own still bleeding. "Welcome home. Or — welcome adjacent to home. We're still working out the geography."

"The Wardens are coming," I managed. "Six of mine are holding the street. Forty more are not."

"Then I suggest," said the oldest woman I had ever seen, straightening up with the grin of someone twenty and immortal, "that we all go home very quickly."

* * *

*Lior*

We went home very quickly.

Forty-one exiles, one defected captain, one mute queen, and me, last through the gate as promised, feeling the threshold take its toll as I crossed — a coldness in the left side of my chest, a faintness in my left eye's colors, the exact half-price of a hinge's life. I'd known the cost when I signed. Knowing didn't make the left side of the world any less quietly dimmer. But the right side was Vessany, whole and loud and smelling of rain on warm slate, and I found that the arithmetic worked.

Behind us, the gate narrowed, then settled — not shut, never shut again, but *sized*: a door once more, domestic, modest, standing open in a hillside above a river, with a charm-shop keeper's whole heart hung on it like a sign.

Neth took my right hand — the good one — and spoke, and this time everyone understood her, because the language had come home too:

*"Now what?"*

I looked out over the returned country, at the exiles kneeling in the grass of an orchard that hadn't existed an hour ago, at Ilsa Vey standing very straight with tears she'd deny to her grave, at the far hills where, faint but unmistakable, the lights of Corvane were visible for the first time in sixty years — two countries seeing each other across a border that was now, officially, my problem.

"Now," I said, "we negotiate."
