## 10. A Door Out, A Door Open

The morning Vessany came back, the first thing it did was ruin my floor.

I want that on the record. Not the trumpets, not the moon standing still in the sky like a coin someone forgot to take off the table — the *floor*. The gate beneath my shop had been a door for as long as I'd owned the building, and doors are polite things: they open, they close, they mind their own business. But when the Veil came down and the threshold stopped being a door and started being a *place*, the boundary between Corvane flagstone and Vessani meadowgrass dissolved into something more like a suggestion. By dawn I had moss in my stockroom, dew on my ledger, and a very confused hedgehog sitting in the jar where I kept the luck-charms.

"Is it supposed to do that?" Neth asked, crouched at the edge of the grass with her bare feet in the borderland, watching the hedgehog trundle home across two countries at once.

"It's not supposed to be anything," I said. "That's rather the point."

She grinned at me — she'd been grinning for three days straight, ever since the crown-wringers of the restored court had coaxed her out of exile rags and into something with actual buttons — and went back to her work, which was teaching the returned country how to say hello to the one next door. She did it the way she did everything: wordlessly, patiently, with her hands. Old Vessani has no word for *ambassador*. It turned out it didn't need one. It just needed a girl who could stand in a doorway and mean both sides of it.

Me, I got the paperwork.

* * *

You would think a country erased from every map and memory for sixty years would come back quietly. You would be wrong. Vessany returned the way a held breath returns — all at once, everywhere, knocking over furniture. Rivers remembered their old courses and cut new ones through Corvane farmland overnight. Roads that had ended in hedges for six decades suddenly continued, and farmers on both sides woke to find their neighbor's dog sleeping on their porch like it had always done so. In a dozen villages along the old border, people who had spent their whole lives within sight of an impassable wall of nothing walked out their front doors and found a market town where the fog used to be, full of cousins they couldn't name but recognized anyway.

Corvane's Council handled this with all the grace you'd expect from a government whose founding principle was *magic is outlawed, please forget we said otherwise*. There were proclamations. There were committees. There was, for eleven days, an armed standoff at the village of Harrowmere that ended when a Corvane sergeant and a Vessani shepherd discovered they were second cousins and went to the pub instead. The pub, notably, straddled the border now. Nobody has decided which side the taproom falls on, so the answer is both, and the beer is legally contraband and perfectly licensed depending on which stool you sit on.

And me? I became what Sable's ledger called — in a margin note I found only after everything, tucked under a pressed sprig of something that no longer grows anywhere but here — *the hinge*.

Half-tied. That's what the bargain left me. When I rewrote my grandmother's terms at the gate's heart, I paid with the only coin I had that mattered: I split myself. Half my blood belongs to Corvane, half to Vessany, and neither side will let go. It sounds dramatic. Mostly it means I can't sleep properly in either country — too much of me is always somewhere else — and it means I'm the only person alive who can carry a sealed writ from the Corvane Council across the border without it bursting into flame, or bring Vessani moon-wool into Corvane without it forgetting how to exist. Trade needs a hinge. So does diplomacy. So, apparently, does the postal service, because I am now also the postal service.

Sable would have laughed herself sick.

* * *

Neth's coronation was small, which surprised everyone who expected thrones. The court-in-exile had spent sixty years keeping the forms alive — the oaths, the regalia, the genealogies recited like prayers against forgetting — and when the moment came, Neth took one look at the ceremonial hall and held the ceremony outside instead, in the meadow that had been my stockroom, with the hedgehog in attendance.

She was crowned with the seal of the royal house, the same sigil she'd worn into my shop on a cord around her neck, barefoot and mute and hunted. She spoke for the first time since I'd known her — Old Vessani, three sentences, translated by the assembled exiles in a ripple like wind through wheat. I caught enough to understand. *The door is open. Come home if you wish. Stay away if you need to.* Both halves true. That's the kind of queen she was going to be.

Ilsa Vey stood at the back, out of uniform, holding her hat.

She'd burned her Warden orders in the gate chamber weeks before — I saw the ash myself — but burning orders isn't the same as choosing, and Ilsa is a woman who does nothing by halves except decisions. For a month she'd lived in the space between, technically a deserter, functionally a ghost, showing up at odd hours to fix my shutters and glower at the border-grass like it owed her money. The fragments of homeland the Wardens had carved out of her hadn't come back with the Veil. That was the cruelty of it: Vessany returned to the world, but the piece of it inside Ilsa stayed gone. She remembered nothing. She only grieved, without knowing for what.

At the coronation, Neth found her afterward. No speech. The girl simply took Ilsa's hand, pressed it flat against the grass of the meadow, and held it there while the sun went down. Then she said one word — the Vessani word for *guest*, which is also, I've learned, the word for *the person the house has been waiting for* — and Ilsa sat down right there in the wet grass of a country she couldn't remember and cried like the homesick child the Wardens had made her before she could walk.

She took citizenship papers the following week. Exile-side, she insisted — meaning she chose to live in Vessany as a foreigner rather than claim a birthright that had been stolen from her memory. "You don't get to hand me back my childhood," she told the registrar, in the flat voice she uses when she's about to win an argument. "I'll come in through the front door like everyone else." The registrar, a tiny ferocious woman named Odile who had waited out the entire erasure in a cellar in Corvane keeping the census alive by candlelight, wrote it down and then hugged her so hard I heard ribs creak.

Ilsa runs the border watch now. Her own design, her own rules: no Wardens, no Veil, just a woman who knows exactly how borders fail because she watched one eat a country, walking the line every day to make sure this one stays open. She still glowers at the grass. But she brings bread to the shepherds at Harrowmere, and last month I saw her laughing at the two-sided pub, and if you'd told me a year ago that the Warden Captain Ilsa Vey would become the most beloved official on either side of the border, I'd have checked your drink for enchantment.

* * *

As for me — everyone assumed I'd stay. Of course they did. The hero goes home; that's how stories work, and I've read enough of them to know the shape. The exiles thanked me with that particular warmth reserved for people they expect never to see again. Neth offered me a tower, a title, anything. Odile tried to put my name in the census under *returned daughter of Vessany*, and I let her, because arguing felt ungracious.

But here's the thing nobody tells you about going home: I already had one. It had a charm shop above a sealed gate and a kettle that whistled in E-flat and a floor that was mostly flagstone and is now, infuriatingly, mostly moss. Home wasn't behind me or ahead of me. It was *underneath* me, this stitched-together strip of two countries where the hedgehog commutes freely and the dew comes up through Corvane stone smelling of Vessani hay.

So I stayed at the hinge. I reopened the shop — charms legal now, which took some getting used to; I keep reaching for the blackout curtains out of habit and finding sunlight instead. I carry the writs and the wool and the mail. I sleep badly in both countries and dream, most nights, in the language I shouldn't understand but do, and wake with grass stains on my feet.

Last week Neth came down the stockroom stairs — she uses the shop as her study now, claiming the light is better, which is a lie, since half the light comes from another country — carrying a letter with the Council seal of Corvane on one side and the royal sigil of Vessany on the other.

"You're the ambassador," she said. "Officially. They argued about it for nine days and then gave up, because there wasn't anyone else who could read both seals without bleeding."

"I'm a shopkeeper," I said.

"You're a shopkeeper," she agreed solemnly, "with a diplomatic pouch." She set the letter on my counter, on the exact spot where a dead charm once bloomed under a mute girl's fingers, and looked at me with the expression she'd learned from watching me lie to a Warden: patient, knowing, entirely unfooled.

I broke the seal. Both of them. Inside was a single page, written in two hands, requesting — formally, jointly, with all the weight of two governments learning to share a border — my presence at Harrowmere. Because the pub, it seemed, had finally become a problem. A wedding party from each side had booked the taproom for the same night, both claims were legally valid, neither party would yield, and the Councils had concluded that only one person in the world could adjudicate a dispute that existed simultaneously in two countries.

I read it twice. I put the kettle on. Somewhere below my feet, in the grass between worlds, the hedgehog rustled past on its evening commute, belonging to both sides of everything, at home in the seam.

"Well," I said, to Neth, to the letter, to the two countries breathing together under my floorboards. "Let's see what the law says about a border that runs through the beer."

It says, as it turns out, whatever I decide. Which is, I suspect, exactly what my grandmother intended — and exactly what she was afraid of. But that's a problem for tomorrow. Tonight, the door stands open, and I am home.
