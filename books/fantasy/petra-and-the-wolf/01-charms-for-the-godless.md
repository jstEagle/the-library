## 1. Charms for the Godless

The rain came down on Greyhollow like it always did — patient, grey, and utterly indifferent — and Petra Jonah Whitlock stood under her awning in Tanners' Row and sold the godless their small comforts.

"Mind how you hold it," she said, turning the luck-knot over in her palm before she passed it across the stall. "Cord's waxed hemp, three strands, braided nine times. No more than that. Nine is a working number."

The widow — Marta Osgood, if Petra remembered the ledger right, though she never wrote real names in the ledger — took the knot with both hands, the way people took things they were afraid to want too much. Her knuckles were swollen with old work. Her eyes had the particular rawness of someone who had cried herself dry and then kept crying anyway, out of habit, because stopping would mean admitting there was nothing left inside.

"It's just cord," Marta said. Not accusing. Asking permission to believe.

"Just cord." Petra smiled the smile she'd practiced for twelve years in a dozen cities that all smelled of fish or coal or fear. "A braid for your wrist, a thought for your grief. The rest is yours to do."

Marta tied it on anyway, fumbling, and when the knot cinched tight against her pulse she let out a breath like she'd been holding it since the funeral. Which, Petra thought, she probably had.

"How much?"

"Two coppers."

"That's nothing."

"It's what it costs."

Marta paid, and lingered, and did the thing they all did — looked at Petra's hands. Everyone looked at her hands eventually. They were good hands for a charm-seller's trade, broad-palmed and steady, but the backs of them were mapped with old scarring, pale ridges running from wrist to knuckle like river systems drawn by a cartographer who hated rivers. Lichtenberg figures, a physicker in Vail had once told her, back when she'd been someone else. Ferning. The mark of lightning that has passed through living flesh and deigned to leave it living.

Petra told people she'd been burned as a child. A kitchen fire. It was close enough to true that it never needed defending.

"You've the look of someone who's lost someone too," Marta said quietly.

"I've the look of someone who sells cord, mistress Osgood."

That earned a wet half-laugh. Marta touched the knot once more, murmured something — not a prayer, Greyhollow didn't permit prayers, not since the Edict of Quiet had scraped every shrine from the city forty years ago; just words, just a name, her son's name, Petra guessed, said into the space where a god used to be — and went out into the rain.

Petra watched her go until the crowd folded her away, and then she looked at her own hands, and the ache started, the way it always did after a sale.

Not guilt. She'd made her peace with guilt years ago, or buried it deep enough that it only surfaced as weather. This was something else. Something older and more frightening.

Because the knots worked.

* * *

Greyhollow was a magic-free city the way a locked room is an empty one — the ban didn't remove the thing, it only insisted loudly upon its absence. The Edict of Quiet had pulled down the shrines, melted the bells, driven the hedge-witches and bone-readers into the hills or the grave. What remained was a city of honest commerce and dishonest comfort: charms sold as trinkets, blessings sold as superstition, hope sold at two coppers a length of braided hemp. The Wardens tolerated the charm-stalls the way a man tolerates a scar. Proof of an old wound. Nothing living in it.

And so Petra sold cord, and salt-iron nails for doorframes ("old custom, no meaning to it"), and little clay whistles shaped like wrens ("children's toys"). And every item left her stall carrying exactly as much magic as she could pour into it without spilling.

Which was very little. Which had to be very little. Because the last time she had spilled —

She flexed her fingers under the counter, out of sight, and felt the scars pull.

Twelve years since Caelfall. Twelve years since the sky over her family's valley had turned the color of a bruise and answered her the way it always answered her, too well, too much, and taken everything green within ten miles and left it glassed and black. Twelve years since her mother had put Petra on a smuggler's cart with a new name stitched into her collar and said, *don't call anything, ever again*, and Petra had understood — even at fifteen, even screaming — that her mother wasn't warning her.

Her mother was begging her.

So she didn't call. She braided. Nine turns of the strand, a whisper of intent, a thread of the old pressure that lived coiled behind her sternum like a second heart — enough to tip a widow's luck, to turn a fever, to keep a drunkard's feet on the icy stair. Small magics. Deniable magics. Magic measured out in drops from a cup she was terrified to lift.

Except lately the cup had been filling on its own.

It had started a month ago, subtle as a change in wind. A knot she braided without intent still hummed faintly against her palm. Rain on the awning stuttered when her mood darkened, drops hanging a half-second too long, like the sky was listening. And yesterday — yesterday she'd reached for a coil of cord at the back of the shelf and every loose hair on her arms had risen, and somewhere far off, beyond the city walls, thunder had cleared its throat.

There had been no storm forecast. There was never any storm in Greyhollow; the mountains broke them up before they arrived, which was one reason the god-fearing had built the city here, in the shadow of Mount Cael, beneath a sky scrubbed clean of divinity.

Petra had dropped the cord. Stood in the empty stall with her heart slamming. And the thunder had not come again — but it had not gone away either. It had waited, the way weather waits.

Something was changing. Something was coming back. And every instinct she owned, every scar, every night of twelve years spent being nobody — all of it screamed the same word.

*Run.*

But she had run for twelve years, and running had bought her nothing but this stall, this rain, this loneliness with a roof over it. So instead she did what she always did. She finished the day's stock. She counted her coppers twice. She closed the shutters against the dusk and told herself the trembling in her hands was cold.

* * *

She lived above a chandler's shop on Wick Lane, three rooms that smelled permanently of tallow, and she cooked her supper alone the way she had cooked eleven thousand suppers alone, and it was while she was salting the pot that the knock came.

Three raps. Then, after a pause, three more — the rhythm of someone uncertain whether they wanted an answer.

Petra set down the salt. In Greyhollow you learned quickly which knocks meant trouble: the Wardens knocked like they owned the door. This was not that. This was softer. Sadder.

She opened it on Marta Osgood, soaked through, standing in the lamplight with rain streaming off her chin and her eyes wide and wet and shining with something that was not entirely grief anymore.

"He woke up," Marta said.

"Mistress—"

"My son. Tam. He took the fever in autumn and the physickers gave him up, they told me Tuesday, they told me *Tuesday*, mistress Whitlock, make his arrangements. And I wore your knot. I know it's cord, I know, I'm not a fool, I know what the Edict says, but I wore it and I sat with him and I—" Her voice cracked and rebuilt itself. "He woke up this morning asking for broth. Asking for *broth*. Like he'd been sleeping. Like none of it happened."

The lamp flame between them leaned, very slightly, toward Marta. Petra watched it happen and felt the floor tilt under her understanding.

"The physickers were wrong," Petra said carefully. "Fevers break. It happens."

"Not like this." Marta gripped the doorframe. Her thumb found the luck-knot at her wrist, worrying it. "They're saying things, in the lanes. That Widow Corren's dead husband came to her in a dream and told her where he'd hid his savings, and it was *there*, under the hearthstone, exactly where he said. That the fisherfolk on the weir caught a haul like the old stories. That somebody heard the chapel bell — the one they melted forty years ago — ringing under the ground where they buried the pieces." She lowered her voice to almost nothing. "They're saying the gods are answering again."

The rain against the eaves went quiet. All of it. Every drop, hanging.

Petra became aware of her own heartbeat, and of the pressure behind her sternum rising like water finding its level, and of the fact that the storm she'd been feeling for a month was not hers alone. It was everywhere. It was in the walls of the world. Something vast and long-asleep had rolled over in its bed, somewhere beneath the mountains, and the whole sky had begun to lean toward Greyhollow like a listener bending toward a whisper.

"Say the Edict's wrong," Marta whispered. "Say they're listening again. Would it be so terrible? People prayed for centuries and mostly got silence. Now my boy is alive. Is that so terrible?"

The rain resumed. One drop struck the sill like a small deliberate finger tapping.

"Mistress Osgood," Petra said, and her voice came out steadier than she had any right to expect, "I sell cord. Whatever woke your son, it wasn't me, and it wasn't a knot. Go home. Sit with him. Count yourself lucky and don't say so out loud."

Marta searched her face for a long moment — and whatever she saw there, the scars, the fear, the twelve years of careful nothing — made her nod slowly.

"Thank you," she said. "Whatever it was."

She went. Petra shut the door, put her back against it, and slid down until she was sitting on the cold boards with her scarred hands open in her lap.

The lamp flame stood perfectly straight. Outside, the rain fell in sheets, and beneath it — faint, rhythmic, patient as a heartbeat under floorboards — she could have sworn she heard thunder. Far off. Under the earth. Coming from the direction of Mount Cael.

"Go back to sleep," she whispered to it, in the old tongue, the tongue of her childhood, the language storms spoke and mothers forbade. "*Go back to sleep.*"

The thunder paused.

Then, unmistakably, it answered — one low roll, shaped almost like a word, almost like a name, almost like *Petra* — and every hair on her arms rose, and the salt cellar on the shelf rattled once, softly, like something clearing its throat.

She hadn't been called Petra in twelve years.

She sat in the dark of her rented rooms, in the magic-free city, with her hands shaking around a luck-knot's worth of borrowed hope curdling fast into dread, and understood with perfect clarity that the life she had built out of silence was over.

Somewhere out in the rain, a stranger was praying. And something old was answering back.
