## 2. The Prayer That Answered Back

The market square of Greyhollow smelled of wet wool and frying onions that morning, the way it always did after rain, and Petra Jonah Whitlock moved through it the way she had learned to move through everything in this city: quickly, quietly, with her scarred hands tucked into her sleeves.

She had a stall to open. Luck-knots didn't sell themselves — not honestly, anyway, and she had promised herself twelve years ago that she would never again sell them dishonestly. The knots were just knots. Cord and patience and a pretty story about sailors' wives. Whatever power had once lived in her fingers was supposed to be dead, burned out of her the night her mother signed her name in a book Petra had never been allowed to see.

She was arranging her little table under the awning of the tanner's row when the stranger knelt down in the middle of the square.

Everyone saw it happen. A man in a fishmonger's apron, big through the shoulders, gray at the temples, dropped to his knees between the vegetable carts as though his legs had simply stopped being his own. He did not look like a praying man. He looked like a man who had run out of every other option in the world and knew it, and his body had made the decision for him before his pride could argue.

Petra watched from behind her stall with the particular stillness of someone who has spent twelve years practicing how not to be noticed.

*Please,* the man said. Not loudly. Not even really aloud — more like the words were being pulled out of him by something, the way a splinter pulls toward a magnet. *Please. My daughter is on the water. The *Merry Adeline*. She went out before the storm warning. Please. Anyone. Anything.*

Greyhollow was a godless city. That was its whole identity, printed on the gate in letters of iron: no temples, no shrines, no prayers answered here. The founders had built the walls specifically to keep such things out. People still mouthed words over their dead, because grief needed somewhere to go, but everyone understood those words went nowhere. They fell into the cobblestones and died there like spilled wine.

Except this morning they didn't.

The answering voice came up out of the ground.

There was no other way to describe it. It did not come from the sky, which would have been frightening but comprehensible. It came from beneath — from under the cobbles, under the foundations, under whatever old bones Greyhollow's founders had built their godless city on top of without ever asking what the bones were. The voice rose through the stone the way water rises through chalk, slow and patient and utterly indifferent to the fact that it was not supposed to exist here.

And Petra knew it.

That was the thing that turned her blood to ice water. Not the miracle itself — miracles were for other people, people who hadn't spent half a childhood learning which words made storms sit down and listen. What froze her was the timbre of it, the grain, the way it shaped the silence around each word. She had heard this voice before. She had heard it when she was nine years old, kneeling on a mountainside with her brother beside her, both of them small enough that the wind could have carried them off if the wind had wanted to. She had heard it answer her mother. She had heard it say her family's name.

*The Merry Adeline turns,* the voice said, and the whole square heard it, every soul in earshot, and several hundred people stopped moving at once. *The child lives. The child is cold. The child will be cold until sundown, and then she will be warm forever, and she will be grateful, and she will owe.*

It sounded almost kind. That was the horror of it. Old things that live under mountains learn to sound kind the way fishermen learn to gut a mackerel — efficiently, without waste, with a certain terrible gentleness. Every word was soft. Every word was a hook.

*Bring her home,* the voice said. *I have waited so long to be asked for anything.*

The fishmonger wept. Of course he wept. His daughter was alive; a voice out of the bedrock had told him so in front of forty witnesses; whatever the price was, it was a problem for a future version of himself, and future selves are always strangers. He got up off his knees glowing like a man who had seen the face of God, and half the square glowed with him, because hope is contagious in a way that fear only wishes it were.

Petra did not glow. Petra stood behind her luck-knots with her hands going white inside her sleeves, listening to a voice she had last heard on the worst day of her life, and understanding — slowly, sickly, with the particular clarity that arrives only after it can do the most damage — that something underneath Mount Cael had woken up.

Something underneath Mount Cael had woken up, and it remembered her name.

* * *

She closed the stall early. She never closed early. Closing early meant losing coin, and coin meant food, and food meant another week of not having to decide between eating and buying lamp oil. But her hands were shaking too badly to tie knots, and there was a Warden walking the tanner's row already, and Wardens did not walk anywhere without a reason.

Greyhollow's Wardens were not soldiers, exactly. They were something older and duller than soldiers — a civic order that had grown up around the city's founding law the way moss grows around a statue, taking its shape. Their mandate was simple: nothing touched, nothing answered, nothing believed. For generations they had mostly chased smugglers and fined people for leaving offerings at crossroads, work so quiet it barely qualified as work. Petra had sold charms within sight of them for years. They looked at her stall and saw cord.

This afternoon they did not look at her stall. This afternoon they looked at people.

By the time the light went amber over the rooftops, there were three wagons in the square and Warden-captains reading names off lists, and the mood of the crowd had curdled from wonder into something uglier and much more familiar. Petra had seen this before. Not here — never here — but in the city where she'd grown up, in the years before the purges, when the temples still stood and the priests still walked and everyone agreed that magic was a blessing right up until the day everyone agreed it wasn't. Fear moves through crowds the way fire moves through dry grass, and it burns hottest around the people who were warmest an hour ago. The fishmonger who had wept with joy at noon was being questioned by dusk. The woman who had shouted *praise be* loudest was the first one dragged to a wagon, because someone had remembered her shouting, and remembering was now a crime's first cousin.

They were calling it *god-touched*. Someone official had coined the term by late afternoon — you could tell because it appeared on the notice boards in careful printed letters, as though it had been drafted in advance, as though someone had been waiting years for an excuse to use it. *All persons bearing marks of divine favor or communion are to report for examination.* Examination. The word had a clean bureaucratic smell, and underneath the smell, teeth.

Petra read the notice twice, standing in the crowd with her hood up, and felt the old familiar arithmetic begin in her chest. *Do I look god-touched? Could I pass? If they take my hands and turn them over, what do they see?*

Her scars told stories. That was the trouble with scars; they were testimony that couldn't be silenced. Twelve years ago, in another city, under another name, a circle of storm-priests had held her wrists against an altar stone while the binding-iron cooled, and the marks they left were not subtle. Thin white lines, radiating from each wrist like the branches of a frozen river — anyone who knew what bindings looked like would know. Most people in Greyhollow wouldn't. But most people wasn't the standard anymore. The standard was *anyone*, and *anyone* included, somewhere in its vast indifferent reach, at least one person who had come from the coast, who had seen a binding done, who knew that those marks meant a storm-caller had been collared and that collared storm-callers sometimes slipped their collars.

She walked home the long way. She always walked home the long way now; it had started as caution and become habit and become, in some unexamined way, the truest shape of her life. The long way went along the canal, past the shuttered granary, under the aqueduct where the pigeons roosted, and it took eleven minutes longer than the direct route, and in those eleven minutes nobody could see where she lived.

Halfway along the canal she stopped. Not because of anything — that was what unnerved her. There was no reason to stop. No sound, no movement, no threat. Just a feeling rising in her like water coming to a boil in a sealed pot, pressure with nowhere to go, and she recognized it because she had spent her entire childhood learning to recognize it: the feeling of a storm gathering somewhere far away and turning its attention toward her.

Her gift had been bound. Her gift had been *sold*. She had felt it leave — or she had believed she felt it leave, all those years ago, the night her mother came back down from the mountain alone and would not say what she had traded. Since then her hands had been just hands. Scarred, capable, ordinary. She had rebuilt herself around their ordinariness the way a tree rebuilds around a lightning scar, and she had been proud of the rebuilding, in the secret way one is proud of things that cost too much to mention.

But tonight, standing over black canal water, her palms itched.

Not hurt. *Itched.* Deeply, structurally, the way a lost limb itches in the place where it used to be. And underneath the itch, faint as a pulse heard through a wall, something was keeping time. Something was counting. She could feel it counting — slow, patient, enormous, a rhythm like a heartbeat played at the speed of geology — and when she let herself listen, just for three seconds, just three, she understood what it was counting toward.

A turn. A moon-turn. Thirteen of them, and then—

She made herself stop listening. Three seconds was already two too many. She pressed her scarred wrists against the cold stone of the canal rail until the itch drowned in pain, and she walked the rest of the way home counting lampposts instead of heartbeats, because lampposts didn't count anything at all.

* * *

Her room above the chandler's shop was one window wide, and from the window she could see, if she leaned out at exactly the right angle, a sliver of the market square. She had chosen the room for cheapness and kept it for the angle, though she'd never admitted that to anyone, least of all herself.

Tonight she leaned out at exactly the right angle and watched the square by lantern-light, and the square was full of Wardens, and the Wardens were building a bonfire.

Not a burning. Not yet. A *burning of things* — confiscated charms, prayer-rags, votive candles seized from houses whose owners would be fined by the candle. The flames climbed orange against the dark, and the crowd around the fire had the flat, obedient look of crowds watching authority perform competence, and every few minutes a Warden would call out a name and a wagon would roll, and Petra counted the names the way she counted lampposts, because counting was the only prayer she allowed herself.

Somewhere in that crowd, she knew, were people who had heard the voice today and felt what she had felt — that awful double motion, terror and homesickness in the same breath, the same nerve struck twice. She knew because she was one of them, and she had spent twelve years assuming she was the only one, and today had proven that assumption wrong in the worst possible way. There were others. There were always others. Exile made you believe your loneliness was a law of nature, and then the world did something like this, and you learned the loneliness had just been waiting.

Because here was the thing she could not stop thinking about, sitting in the dark with her back against the wall and her scarred hands open in her lap like caught birds:

The voice had been beautiful.

She hated that it had been beautiful. She hated that some traitor part of her, some nine-year-old part that had never been bound or sold or renamed, had *leaned toward it* — had wanted, for three forbidden seconds on the canal bank, to answer back. To kneel down on Greyhollow's godless cobbles and say *yes, I hear you, I remember you, you knew my mother, you knew my brother, do you know where my brother is?* Because that was the ache underneath everything, the ache that homesickness was only the polite word for: the voice from under the mountain was the last living witness to the worst day of her life, and some starved corner of her wanted to ask it questions.

*What did she trade? Who did she trade? Is Aldric still—*

She didn't finish the thought. She never finished the thought. Finishing the thought meant saying his name, and saying his name in her head was as close as she could bear to come to hoping, and hoping was a debt she couldn't afford interest on.

Instead she did what she always did. She took out the cord. Plain hemp cord, bought honest from the ropewalk, and she tied a knot, and then another, working her fingers because working her fingers kept them from itching, kept them from listening, kept them from remembering what they used to be able to do. Knot after knot after knot, the same pattern her grandmother had taught her, the pattern that had never once been magic no matter what the customers liked to believe.

On the ninth knot, the cord moved.

Not in her hands — *under* them. Through them. A tremor passed up from the floorboards, from the joists, from the deep stone the whole city sat on, and it passed through her wrists, through the old binding scars, and for one moment — one single, unrepeatable, never-to-be-spoken-of moment — the knot tightened itself. Drew snug. Settled into its pattern with a precision her fingers had not achieved since she was nineteen years old.

Then it stopped, and the cord was just cord again, and her hands were just hands, shaking.

She sat very still for a long time. Long enough that the bonfire below burned down to embers, long enough that the chandler's dog stopped barking at the wagons, long enough that the moon cleared the eastern rooftops and laid a thin silver line across her floor — and she looked at that line, and she counted, without meaning to, without wanting to, and the count came out wrong. Moons didn't come out wrong. Unless there were more of them than there should be. Unless somewhere, in some ledger kept by something patient, this month had a thirteenth turn in it that no astronomer had charted and no calendar admitted to.

The itch in her palms settled into a rhythm again. Slow. Patient. Counting.

*Twenty-eight days,* said the thing under the world, in the voice from her childhood, so softly that it might have been her own thought, might have been memory, might have been nothing at all. *I know your name, little storm. I have kept it safe for you. Come and see what your mother bought.*

Petra blew out her candle, though the candle had nothing to do with anything, and lay down on her cot with her boots on, because a girl who had grown up running learned to sleep ready to run.

Below her window, at the edge of the ember-light where the square met the dark, something grey was standing very still, watching her window. Four-legged. Patient. The shape of it was wolf, but the stillness of it was something else entirely — the stillness of a person deciding whether tonight was the night.

It watched her for a long time. She watched it back.

Neither of them looked away.
