## 3. The Wolf at the Threshold

The wolf was still there in the morning.

Not at her window — that would have been madness, a thing to be reported, a thing to be killed — but across the square, folded into the shadow of the tannery's overhang like a piece of the dark that had forgotten to leave with the rest of it. Petra saw it from her window as she pulled on her boots, and her hands went still on the laces.

Greyhollow had dogs. Greyhollow had rats bold enough to argue with cats, and gulls that came up the river in winter, and once — memorably — a bear that had wandered down from the high passes and been shot by six Wardens who each claimed afterward to have fired the killing shot. But wolves did not come into cities. Wolves especially did not come into *this* city, where every animal larger than a cat wore a collar or a brand or a butcher's mark, because the Wardens of Greyhollow held that anything wild was one step from magic, and magic was one step from the pyre.

The wolf sat in the tannery's shadow with its paws neatly together and watched her window, and when she stepped back from the glass it tilted its head — slowly, deliberately, the way a person tilts their head when they are listening for something behind a door.

Petra made herself turn away. She made herself go downstairs, unlock the charm-stall's shutters, and set out her wares: the luck-knots and river-glass beads and knotted red thread that were, by law and by her own careful design, nothing but string and glass and thread. She made herself smile at the fishwife who bought a knot against storms at sea, though there had not been a storm on the coast in three years and everyone knew the knots were nonsense, which was the point of them. Nonsense was legal. Sense of a certain kind got you burned.

All morning she felt the weight of being watched, the way you feel a draft from a window that isn't open. She did not look toward the tannery. Looking was an admission, and admissions in Greyhollow had teeth.

But at midday, when the market crowd thinned and the light went flat and silver behind the clouds, she finally let herself glance — one flick of the eyes, casual, a woman checking the weather.

The wolf was gone.

She told herself the relief she felt was simple sense. A predator leaving is good news; any farmer knows it. But the relief lasted only until she noticed what the wolf had left behind, pressed into the mud between the cobbles where it had sat all morning: a single print, deep and clean, the size of her spread hand.

And around it, faintly, in a ring no wider than the wolf's haunches, the mud was *dry*. Not scorched. Not marked. Simply dry, in the middle of a square that had been rained on at dawn, as if the ground beneath the animal had refused, quietly and completely, to be touched by water.

Petra stood very still among her trinkets and thought about her scarred hands, and about the tremor she had felt in them two nights ago when the widow Maren's luck-knot had actually worked, and about how in Greyhollow the punishment for a first offense was the pyre and there was no punishment for a second because there were no second offenses.

*It's not me,* she told herself. *I haven't called anything. I haven't done anything.*

The thought should have comforted her. Instead it frightened her more, because if she hadn't called it, then something had come to her uninvited — and in her experience, the things that came uninvited were the things that already knew your name.

* * *

It followed her home that night. And the night after. And the night after that.

Not close. Never close. It kept to the edges of things — the seam where lamplight failed, the mouths of alleys, the long shadow of the aqueduct — so that a person glancing down the street would see nothing but grey-on-grey, stone and dusk. But Petra had spent twelve years learning to notice what noticed her, and the wolf's attention had a texture to it, a pressure, like a hand hovering just above the back of her neck without touching.

On the fourth night she stopped pretending.

She turned off Cooper's Row into the cut-through behind the chandler's, a narrow brick throat with one lamp at either end and no windows facing it, and she waited in the dark halfway along, her back to the wall, her heart going hard and stupid against her ribs. Footsteps passed at the far end. A cart rattled somewhere. Then, from the mouth of the alley she'd just left, the wolf came walking.

It came without caution. That was the worst of it — the *confidence*. Animals moved in straight lines only when they were hunting or fleeing, and this wolf did neither. It walked down the center of the alley at an easy amble, and it stopped six feet from her, and it sat, and it looked up at her face with eyes that caught the distant lamplight and held it wrong — too steady, too patient, eyes like a man's seen through a mask of fur.

"Go away," Petra whispered.

The wolf's ears tipped forward.

"I mean it." Her voice came out low and rough. "Whatever you are — whatever sent you — I don't want it. I don't want any of it. I paid everything I had to not want any of it."

The wolf did not growl. It did not bare its teeth. It did the thing she had begun to dread most, the thing no honest animal should be able to do: it listened. Its head cocked at exactly the words, its gaze fixed on her mouth as though her voice were something it could read.

And then, softly, almost gently, it made a sound. Not a bark. Not a whine. A low huff through the nose, twice, rhythmic — and Petra's whole body went cold, because she knew that sound. Every child raised in the high valleys knew that sound. It was the sound a shepherd's dog made when it counted the flock at dusk, the little double-breath that meant *all here, all accounted for.*

It was counting her. It had been counting her every night, from the edge of the square, the way you count something that belongs to you.

"No," she said, and her voice cracked on it. "No. I'm not yours. I'm not anyone's."

The wolf rose, shook itself once — a ripple of grey from ears to tail, unhurried, almost human in its indifference — and walked past her down the alley, close enough that its fur brushed her skirt, close enough that she felt heat come off its body like a banked stove. At the far end of the cut-through it paused and looked back at her, and in the half-second before it stepped into the dark, Petra could have sworn its eyes were not amber at all but a pale storm-grey, the exact color of the sky over Mount Cael before lightning.

Then it was gone, and she stood alone in the alley with her arms wrapped around herself, shaking, saying aloud to no one, in the smallest voice she owned:

"Aldric."

She didn't know why she said it. That was the truth of it, the ugly truth she would not look at directly: the name simply came out, the way a splinter works its way up out of the skin, and once it was out she could not push it back.

Her brother had been dead twelve years. Drowned, her mother said. Lost under the ice at Mirror Lake in the last winter before the exile, before the trial, before the storm-calling charge and the pyre-that-wasn't and the long road east to a city with no gods in it. There had been no body. There had been a funeral anyway, because her mother insisted, and Petra remembered standing at the graveside with an empty coffin going into the ground and thinking even then, at fourteen, even in the fog of grief: *there is no body because there was no body. Where is the body?*

She had never asked. Asking questions had been dangerous in those days; the Wardens of her homeland were already circling their family like gulls over a gutted fish. So she had buried the question with the empty coffin and let the earth have it.

Now the earth was giving it back, one grey shape at a time.

* * *

That night, the dream came again — and this time she understood that it had come before, that it had been coming for weeks, worn smooth by repetition the way a stair is worn by feet, and she had simply refused to remember it upon waking.

She stood on Mirror Lake.

It was winter in the dream, true winter, the kind they'd had in the old country before the warm years took it. The lake lay white and endless under a sky the color of iron filings, and the ice sang — that long, groaning, whale-deep note that lake ice makes when it is settling, a sound like the world clearing its throat. Petra knew the ice was thick enough to walk on. Everyone knew it. The village children skated on it every year.

But in the dream she could see through it, the way dream-eyes see, down through the blue-green layers to the black water below —

— and there was a boy under the ice.

He hung in the dark water with his hair drifting around his face like ink poured upward, and he was not struggling. That was the detail that broke her heart anew each time the dream returned: he was not struggling, not pounding the ice, not screaming bubbles. He floated with his arms slightly out, palms up, in the posture of someone waiting to be lifted, and his eyes were open, and they were fixed on hers through six feet of frozen lake with perfect, terrible calm.

His lips moved. No sound crossed the ice. But she had known him for eleven years before she lost him; she had taught him his letters and he had taught her the names of every bird on the mountain, and she could read his mouth the way she read her own reflection.

He was saying her name. *Petra. Petra. Petra.* Patiently. The way you say the name of someone who keeps not hearing you.

"I can't," she told him, and in the dream her breath didn't fog, and her voice fell through the air and died against the ice like a moth against glass. "Aldric, I can't. You're dead. You've been dead for twelve years. If I dig you up again they'll burn me, they'll burn me this time, they'll —"

The boy under the ice tilted his head.

It was the same tilt. The exact same tilt, the slow deliberate incline, listening-for-something-behind-a-door — and the recognition landed in her chest like a dropped stone, and the lake shuddered, and the singing of the ice climbed and climbed until it wasn't ice at all but wire, wire under tension, wire tuned to a note just below hearing —

Petra woke with her hand thrust out over the edge of the cot, fingers spread, and the copper basin on her washstand was trembling. Not falling. Trembling, the water inside it shivering into rings, ringing like a struck bell, and outside her window the night was utterly silent except for one sound:

Breathing. Slow, even, doubled — a great nose-huff, in and out, at the base of the wall below her window. All here. All accounted for.

The basin stilled. The breathing continued, patient as tide.

Petra lay in the dark with her scarred hands pressed flat against her sternum, feeling her own heartbeat hammer against them, and made herself think it through the way she thought through everything now: coldly, legally, like a woman reading a contract in a language she hated.

Wolves did not come to cities. Wolves did not sit in rain-shadowed squares and leave dry ground. Wolves did not count their flock. And dead brothers did not stand under frozen lakes saying a living woman's name — unless the death had been a lie, unless the coffin had been empty for a reason, unless somewhere in the twelve years between then and now her mother's silence had not been grief at all but *guilt*, the specific heavy guilt of a woman who knows exactly where her son is and cannot say.

*One soul,* whispered a voice in her memory that might have been memory at all. *The elder child.*

No. That was nothing. That was a fragment of some smuggler's tale, some fireside story about the old bargains, the kind of story exiles told each other to explain why the world had taken everything from them. It meant nothing. It could not mean anything, because if it meant anything then her mother had not lost Aldric — her mother had *spent* him, had traded him away to buy her daughter's life, and every kindness Petra had ever counted in that cold difficult woman — the forged papers, the money sewn into her coat, the last fierce embrace at the border stone — every one of them became a price tag.

Petra rolled onto her side and stared at the wall until dawn came up grey through the shutters, and the breathing below her window never changed, never left, keeping its slow faithful time like a sentry who had been posted there by someone with authority over both of them.

* * *

On the seventh day, the wolf broke the pattern, and the pattern-breaking was how she finally understood.

She was closing the stall at dusk, stacking her trays, when the market square went quiet in the particular way that meant not emptiness but *attention*. She looked up. The wolf was crossing the open square toward her in full view of a dozen stragglers — broad daylight dying into dusk, no shadows to hide in, no pretense — and the people of Greyhollow parted around it the way water parts around a keel, without comment, without alarm, with the mild baffled courtesy of people who have decided not to have seen something.

That was wrong. That was the most wrong thing yet. A wolf in a market square should have brought shouting, should have brought the Wardens running with their pikes and their nets. Instead a baker drew his child gently behind his legs and went on weighing bread. A carter stepped aside and touched his cap. As if it were a dog. As if it were a *person*.

The wolf reached the stall and sat, and looked up at her, and its eyes in the dying light were grey as storm-water.

"You're doing that," Petra said quietly. "You're making them not see."

The wolf's tail moved once against the cobbles. Once. Like a nod.

Her mouth had gone dry. She crouched slowly behind the stall's counter, out of the sightline of the square, bringing her face level with the animal's, close enough to see the fine scars threading through the fur of its muzzle — old scars, healed white, the kind a muzzle gets from a cage that is too small.

"If you're him," she breathed, "then blink twice."

The wolf held her gaze. The market sounds went distant, wool-wrapped. Somewhere behind her ribs her heart was trying to climb out through her throat.

The wolf blinked. Once.

And then, unhurried, deliberate, it blinked again — and in the space of that second blink, the lamplight caught its eyes and they flashed not amber but that pale storm-grey, and beneath the fur of its throat, visible for just a moment as the light shifted, there was a mark. A small pale crescent, like a bite of the moon, like a brand.

Like the seal-mark the old stories said the Court Beneath the Mountain pressed into the throat of every soul it owned.

Petra's knees nearly went. She gripped the counter's edge and stayed crouched, breathing, while the wolf watched her with her brother's patience and the market went on around them, blind and ordinary and blessedly unaware.

Twenty-eight days. She didn't know yet how she knew the number — it arrived in her head the way the name *Aldric* had arrived, rising up out of some ledger she'd never seen, some debt she'd never signed — but she knew it the way you know a bell has finished tolling. Twenty-eight days, and then something beneath Mount Cael would finish turning, and every bargain ever struck in the dark would come due.

The wolf rose. It pressed its muzzle briefly, gently, against her scarred knuckles — a touch so familiar it stole her breath, because Aldric had said goodbye to her exactly that way at the border stone, forehead to her hand, twelve years ago — and then it turned and trotted away into the gathering dark, unhurried, unafraid, a creature with somewhere to be.

Petra stayed crouched behind her counter for a long time.

When she finally stood, her legs stiff and her mind made up in the way that cold water makes things up — suddenly, all at once, irreversibly — she found she was not alone. Across the square, leaning against the Warden post with her arms folded and her grey coat buttoned to the chin, a woman was watching her. Not the wolf. Her.

The woman had a Warden's badge and a face like a closed ledger, and she had been watching long enough that her stillness had a professional quality to it, and when Petra met her eyes the woman pushed off the post and began, unhurried, to cross the square.

"Charm-seller," the woman said, as she came near. Her voice was pleasant the way a locked door is pleasant. "We should talk. My name is Vess. Warden Ilse Vess."

Behind her, at the edge of the lamplight, the wolf had stopped. It stood looking back at them both — at Petra, at the Warden — with its head tilted, listening for something behind a door.

And it bared its teeth.
