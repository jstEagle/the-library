## 4. Warden Vess

The rain had been falling on Greyhollow for six days, and Ilse Vess had stopped believing in coincidence somewhere around the third.

She stood under the awning of the tallow-chandler's shop on Cordwainer Lane and watched the girl with the scarred hands across the street. The girl — *Mira Selk*, according to the residency rolls, though Vess had learned long ago that names were the first thing fugitives forged and the last thing they perfected — was selling small knotted things from a tray. Luck-knots. Thread charms. The kind of harmless-looking nonsense that old women bought against colic and young men bought before dice games, and that every Warden in the city had been trained to ignore for thirty years because they were, officially, nothing at all.

Officially.

Vess turned her hand over and looked at the mark on her own palm: a ring of ash-grey ink stamped that morning by the Provost's clerk, still faintly wet at the edges. *God-touched inquiry.* Three words that meant the rules had changed. Three words that meant every charm-seller, every hedge-prayer, every whispered invocation in Greyhollow was suddenly worth a cell and a question and possibly a rope.

The orders had come down nine days ago, after the incident in the market square. A dyer's apprentice named Corren Hale had stood in the middle of Fishmonger's Square in the middle of market-day and prayed aloud — actually prayed aloud, hands raised like a madman or a saint — for his drowning brother pulled from the harbor, and something had *answered*. Forty witnesses. The boy coughing up seawater on dry cobbles, alive, while a voice like a bell rung underwater said words no one could repeat afterward and half of them couldn't stop hearing since.

The Wardens had arrested Hale within the hour. It hadn't helped. Since then, Vess had logged eleven more answered prayers in her district alone. Eleven. In a city built specifically, deliberately, on the principle that gods did not exist here — that Greyhollow's founders had carved their godless charter into the foundation stones themselves, and the stones had held for three hundred years.

The stones were not holding anymore.

And now there was this girl with the scarred hands, whose luck-knots worked.

Vess had watched her sell four of them in an hour. That was not remarkable. What was remarkable was the widow Tam Bracken, who had bought one for her drowned son's grave, pressing it to her lips and whispering into the thread the way you whisper to a priest — and who had walked away with her shoulders straighter than Vess had seen them in two years, because when she'd asked the charm for a sign, the wind off the harbor had died completely, all along the street, for exactly as long as it took her to finish speaking.

The girl had gone white as milk. Had packed up her tray with shaking fingers. Had looked, for one unguarded moment, like someone standing on ice and hearing it crack.

*You felt it too,* Vess thought. *You felt your own work answer back, and it frightened you.*

That was interesting. Frightened practitioners ran. This one stayed. Which meant she had nowhere to run to, or something holding her here, or both — and either way, she was exactly the kind of thread Vess needed to pull.

She crossed the street through the rain.

* * *

Petra saw the Warden coming from twenty feet away, because Wardens moved differently than everyone else in Greyhollow — weight forward, eyes doing arithmetic on every face, hands never far from the short iron batons they carried instead of swords because iron didn't spark and steel might be mistaken for ceremony.

This one was tall, grey-streaked, maybe forty-five, with a face like a closed ledger and a Warden's oilskin that had been mended more times than replaced. She walked directly to the stall. No browsing. No pretense.

*Mira Selk*, Petra reminded herself. *You are Mira Selk, thread-mender, late of the coast, no history worth asking about.* She arranged her fear into the shape of mild wariness — the expression any honest peddler wore when law approached — and smiled.

"Good morning, Warden. Charms are just thread and knots, if that's your concern. Nothing in them but patience."

"Is that so." The woman picked up a luck-knot between two fingers, turning it. Her eyes were pale grey, the color of the weather, and they did not move from Petra's face. "Mira Selk."

"Yes."

"Resident eight months. Lodging above the sailmaker's on Ropewalk. No family listed. No prior trade." She set the knot down. "Before that, nothing. A person doesn't spring out of the ground at twenty-six years old, Mistress Selk."

"People do, if the ground was bad."

Something flickered behind the Warden's eyes — not amusement, exactly. Recognition, maybe. "I'm going to ask you some questions," she said. "I'd like you to think very carefully about how much lying is worth to you today, because I'll tell you plainly: I've hanged people for less than what you're carrying in that tray, and I've let worse walk, and the difference between those outcomes has never once been whether they lied to me. It's been whether they lied to me *well*."

Petra's mouth went dry. "I don't—"

"Don't." The word came out quiet and flat as a door closing. "Not yet. Save it. Here's what I know." She glanced down the lane — empty but for rain — and then leaned in slightly, and her voice dropped to something almost conversational. "Widow Bracken asked your knot for a sign yesterday, and the wind stopped for her. I know because I was forty feet behind her, and I have been behind her every day since her son drowned, because grief is where the god-touched surface first. So we can stand here in the rain while you tell me about thread and patience, or you can listen to what I actually came to say."

Petra said nothing. Her heart was a fist against her ribs. *Run*, said the oldest part of her, twelve years trained. But running confirmed everything, and this woman already knew enough to bury her.

"I'm listening," she managed.

"Good." The Warden straightened. "My name is Ilse Vess. Senior Warden, Harbor District. And I am about to commit a crime, so I'd appreciate it if you'd keep your face boring." She took off her hat, letting the rain hit her hair, which somehow made her look less like an officer and more like a tired woman standing in bad weather — which, Petra suspected, was the truer picture. "The Provost wants god-touched practitioners in cells. All of them. He thinks if he wrings the city hard enough, the gods will wring out with us. I think he's wrong, and I think the arrests are making it worse — every practitioner we take, two more start praying desperate enough to be heard. Fear is fertilizer for this particular crop."

"That sounds like sedition, Warden."

"It is. Are you keeping count?" Vess put her hat back on. "Here is my offer. Immunity. Full immunity — your name stays off the lists, your stall stays open, and whatever you were before Mira Selk stays buried. In exchange: names. Not the widow Bracken; I don't care about grieving women tying knots. I want the ones who *call*. The storm-callers, the grave-speakers, the ones whose prayers get answers instead of coincidences. There are perhaps a dozen real ones left in Greyhollow, and I know of six, and I need the rest before the Provost's men find them first, because his men don't distinguish between a hedge-witch and whatever is happening in the square these days. They just take everyone."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then I arrest you, and you go in a cell, and in a week the Provost's interrogators will have your real name out of you like a splinter, and whatever you're hiding under 'Selk' will be worth considerably more to them than it is to me." Vess's voice didn't harden when she said it; that was the worst part. It sounded like a woman reading a weather report. "I'm not threatening you, girl. I'm telling you the tide table."

Rain drummed on the awning. Somewhere down the lane a cart rattled past, and for a moment neither of them spoke, and Petra became aware that they were standing very close together in the gray light, two liars measuring each other.

"Why?" Petra asked finally. "Why would you burn yourself for strangers? You said it yourself — immunity for informants. That's not mercy. That's recruitment. What do you get?"

For the first time, the Warden's composure moved. Not much. A settling, like silt stirred in deep water.

"You ask good questions," she said. "That's going to make you harder to manage and easier to respect." She looked out toward the harbor, where the rain blurred the masts into grey pencil-strokes. "Come with me. Not to a cell. To my house. It's four streets away, and I'd rather have this conversation once than twice."

Every instinct Petra owned screamed against it. She went anyway, because the alternative was a cell, and because — though she would not admit this even to herself until later — there was something in the woman's voice that sounded like a person who had been carrying something heavy for a very long time and was tired of the weight.

* * *

Vess's house was narrow and clean and almost entirely bare, the way homes are bare when someone has removed all the fragile things rather than dust around their absence. One chair too many at the table. A child's height-marks penciled on a doorframe, stopped abruptly at four feet, seven years ago by the look of the faded graphite.

Vess caught her looking. Didn't flinch. Made tea instead, in silence, and set the cup down, and sat, and folded her hands.

"My daughter's name was Liss," she said. "She was nine years old when she got the fever. The sweating sickness, the year it came up the coast. Do you remember it?"

"No," Petra said carefully. "I wasn't living here then."

"No. You wouldn't be." Vess drank. "Greyhollow is a godless city. That's our pride, that's our charter, that's what the founders paid for in blood — no temples, no priests, no prayers. And it works fine right up until your daughter is burning at a hundred degrees in your arms and there is nowhere to kneel. Do you understand? There was literally nowhere. I stood in my kitchen with her in my arms and I did not know the *words*. We'd outlawed them. My grandmother's grandmother had outlawed them so that I would never have to learn them, and in that moment it felt like being locked out of my own house in a storm."

Petra's tea cooled untouched in her hands.

"There was an old woman," Vess went on. "Back-lane hedge-worker, the kind I'd arrested twice myself. I broke my own rule. I carried Liss to her door in the rain and I begged. The old woman took one look at her and said the sickness was past praying over — that the gods, if they heard at all, hear prayers like a man hears rain on a roof. Then she gave me a charm anyway. Out of pity. Told me to hold it and speak my daughter's name into it, and that if anything anywhere was listening, it would cost me nothing to try."

The kettle ticked as it cooled. Vess's hands were perfectly steady around her cup, and Petra understood suddenly that the steadiness was the most expensive thing in the room.

"So I did. I held it and I said her name. *Liss Vess. Liss Vess. Please.*" The words came out worn smooth, handled ten thousand times. "And she died anyway, at dawn, and I sat there with a knot of thread in my fist feeling like the biggest fool God ever refused." She looked up. "Nine days ago, a boy prayed in Fishmonger's Square and the sea gave his brother back. Nine days. Where was that voice when my daughter was burning? Where was it for the eleven years since? Because I'll tell you what I've concluded, sitting in my district watching miracles pop up like weeds: the gods aren't answering prayers now because they've grown kind. They're answering now because something changed — something *broke* — and whatever woke them up is hungry, and hunger makes even gods generous. Every prayer answered in this city lately has been a hook with bait on it. And the Provost wants to arrest fishermen's widows, and I want to find the thing that broke, and I cannot do either without someone who knows what prayer tastes like from the inside."

The silence stretched. Rain on the roof. The height-marks on the doorframe.

"You think I'm one of them," Petra said quietly. "A caller."

"I think your hands are scarred in a pattern I've only seen twice, and both times on people who could pull lightning out of a clear sky." Vess set down her cup. "I think you sold a luck-knot yesterday and were horrified when it worked, which means you know exactly what your gift costs and you've been hiding from it. And I think you are the best lead I have. So. Names, Mira Selk. Or whatever your name is. Immunity stands either way — I keep my word; it's the only currency I have left. But I'd rather have your help than your silence."

Petra looked at the dead daughter's doorframe. At the extra chair. At this hard, hollowed-out woman who had offered treason to a stranger over cold tea, and felt something twist in her chest that was dangerously close to kinship.

She could give her nothing true. That was the trap of it — the closest thing to honesty she possessed was a lie twelve years deep. *I'm nobody's caller. I'm a runaway and a coward and my whole gift is buried under scars I made myself.* None of it usable. All of it damning.

So she did what she'd done since she was fourteen years old. She chose the lie that kept everyone safest, and made it beautiful.

"You're right about one thing," Petra said. "I wasn't born Mira Selk. And you're right that I know what prayer tastes like from the inside." She let her hands tremble — no performance required. "But I'm not a caller, Warden. I'm what's left when one gets caught. My mother called storms. Real ones. The kind that sink ships. And when the old temples' inquisitors came for her, she burned the ledgers and scattered the coven and put me on a boat with a false name, and I have spent twelve years being grateful and terrified in equal measure. I don't call. I mend sails. I tie knots. And every day I pray to nothing, in a city with no gods, that it stays that way." She met Vess's eyes. "You want names? I don't have them. I've spent twelve years making sure I wouldn't. But I'll tell you what I *can* do — I can watch. Grief finds its way to my stall; you said it yourself. If something answers back near me, I'll feel it before your men ever do. That's worth more than six names, and it's the only honest thing I have to trade."

It was a good lie. It was layered with truth the way a smuggler's cargo is layered with legitimate goods, and it explained the scars, the fear, the false name, the flinch at working charms. Vess studied her for a long moment, and Petra watched the woman weigh it — watch her *want* to believe it, because believing it meant the world still contained frightened innocents and not just monsters wearing them.

"Twelve years," Vess said at last. "Your mother burned, or hanged?"

"The temples don't share their methods with godless cities."

"No. They wouldn't." Something passed over the Warden's face — not belief, not quite. Acceptance, provisionally, the way one accepts a bridge that hasn't collapsed yet. She reached into her coat and produced a small tin stamp and an ink pad, and pressed it to the back of Petra's hand: a circle of ash-grey, the same mark Vess wore. "District pass. Shows you're under active inquiry — mine. Any Warden who detains you answers to me. Understand what that means, girl. It protects you, and it chains you. You're marked now. Mine."

"I understand."

"Do you?" Vess rose, and for a moment she stood in her own doorway with the rain-light behind her, and she looked older and wearier and more dangerous than any officer Petra had ever feared. "I let you lie to me today because your lie was well-built and because I think you're more afraid of the thing waking under this city than you are of me. That's the correct order of fears. But I will find out what you're hiding, Mira Selk — not because I doubt you, but because I doubt *everything* now, and the gods taught me at nine years old's expense that hope is just a debt you haven't been billed for yet." She opened the door. "Watch your stall. Watch your dreams. And if anything answers you back — anything at all — you come to me before you run. Running is what they expect. It's how they herd us."

Petra stepped out into the rain. At the corner she paused, unable to help herself.

"Warden. Your daughter. If a god had answered — truly answered, in time — would you have taken it? Knowing what they might want back?"

Vess didn't hesitate, and that was the terrible thing.

"In a heartbeat," she said. "That's why I scare myself. Good day, Mistress Selk."

* * *

Petra walked home through the rain with the ash-grey mark drying on her hand like a brand, turning the meeting over and over, hunting the seams in her own performance. It had held. It had *held* — and yet the woman had marked her anyway, and beneath the mark, beneath the bargain, one sentence kept surfacing like a drowned thing:

*I will find out what you're hiding.*

Because Vess was right about one thing, and Petra knew it with a certainty that sat in her chest like swallowed stone: she *was* more afraid of what was waking than of any Warden. She had felt it for weeks now — the tremor in her scarred palms on stormy nights, the way her luck-knots worked too well, the sense of something vast turning over in its sleep beneath the world and murmuring her childhood name in a voice she had spent twelve years trying to forget.

Her mother's voice had sounded a little like that voice. That was the part she told no one.

She climbed the stairs to her room above the sailmaker's, barred the door, and lit no lamp. Outside, the rain thickened toward evening, and far off over the harbor a single roll of thunder went through the clouds — long, low, patient, like something enormous shifting its weight.

Petra sat in the dark and pressed her scarred hands flat against her knees, and listened to the storm call her by name.

* * *

Across the city, in a room with one lamp and one chair too many, Ilse Vess unlocked a drawer she opened once a year and took out a letter written in a child's careful hand.

*Lovely Mother, I prayed to you tonight even though we arent spost to. I prayed you would come home from patrol safe and also that you would say yes to the festival. I no the second one is selfish. Liss.*

Both prayers had been answered eventually. Just not in time, and not in order, and not by anything that deserved the word *god*.

Vess read it by lamplight, the way she always did, and then she took out a fresh sheet of paper and began, for the first time in seven years, to write down the names of everyone in Greyhollow who had reported a prayer answered — dates, streets, witnesses, weather. Eleven entries. Twelve. Thirteen.

On the thirteenth line she stopped.

The entry was from tonight. A dockhand on Ropewalk swore that at dusk, thunder had rolled over the harbor with no storm near enough to make it — and that in the pause afterward, he'd heard, plain as a bell, a woman's voice saying a name he didn't catch, *from inside the thunder itself.*

Vess sat very still.

Then she wrote down the address of a certain sailmaker's shop, and underlined it twice.
