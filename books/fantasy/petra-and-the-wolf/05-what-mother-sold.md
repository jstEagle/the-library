## 5. What Mother Sold

The cache was where Sera the smuggler said it would be: beneath the third flagstone in the cellar of the drowned house on Candlemaker's Row, under a stone carved with a fish that had never swum anywhere but Greyhollow.

Petra went at dusk, when the Wardens changed shift and the streets filled with the smell of frying onions and the ordinary noise of people who did not know their prayers had started answering back. She carried no lamp. She didn't need one — not anymore. For three nights now her hands had been doing things she hadn't asked them to do, and light was the least of it. The scar tissue across both palms, twelve years old and smooth as candle wax, had begun to itch. Sometimes, when she pressed her fingers flat against wood or stone, the grain of the thing rose up in her mind like a map: every knot, every crack, every place the world was weak.

She tried not to think about what that meant.

The drowned house had been empty since the river took its lower floor eleven years ago. Its owner had drowned with it, and Greyhollow, being Greyhollow, had simply stepped around the ruin ever since, the way you step around a grave. Petra let herself in through the back door, which hung by one hinge, and descended into the cellar's cold dark, where the air tasted of river silt and old paper.

Third flagstone. The fish.

She knelt and worked her fingers into the seam. The stone came up easier than it should have — as if someone had loosened it recently, or as if the house itself were tired of keeping secrets. Beneath it lay an oilcloth bundle tied with waxed cord, and inside the bundle, wrapped in a wool blanket gone grey with damp, was a ledger.

She knew it before she touched it. She knew it by the shape of it, by the weight of it, by the way her stomach dropped like a stone down a well.

Her mother's handwriting was on the cover. Not her name — her mother had never been careless enough to write *Maren Whitlock* on anything — but the little pressed-flower mark in the corner, a sprig of mountain thrift, flattened and brown. Her mother had pressed them from the high meadows above Caelfall, before Caelfall stopped being home. Before everything.

Petra sat down on the cold flagstones because her legs told her to, and opened the book.

* * *

It began innocently enough, which was somehow the worst part. Accounts. Charms sold, charms traded, materials bought. A column of luck-knots sold to fishermen at two coppers each. A column of fever-charms given away free during the winter the coughing sickness came through the valley — free, because her mother had never once charged a grieving family, only the comfortable ones who could pay. Petra had forgotten that. Twelve years of exile will sand a memory down to its sharpest and most bitter edges, and what remained of Maren Whitlock in her daughter's mind was mostly the last night: the shouting, the door, the road south, the wolf-cries behind her that she had told herself for years were only wolves.

But ledgers are honest things. They record what happened, in order, whether you want them to or not.

Page by page, the accounts changed. The charm entries grew sparser. Other entries crept in, written smaller, in a hand that pressed harder into the paper as though the writer were trying to drive the words through it like nails.

*Consultation, the ferryman of the Greywater. He says the Court still keeps its books. He says there is always a door.*

*Paid passage to the shrine below the tree-line. The priest there is blind but he sees fine.*

*They will not take coin. They never wanted coin. I have been a fool.*

And then, near the end, a page that was different from all the others. Not an account. A copy — copied out carefully, in her mother's best hand, the letters tall and formal, the way you write when you are copying something whose original must not be seen.

Petra read it three times before the words stopped sliding off her mind like water off oiled cloth.

*Be it known to the Court Beneath the Mountain, and entered into its ledger of debts perpetual:*

*I, Maren Whitlock of Caelfall, storm-caller, being of sound mind and desperate heart, do hereby sell unto the Undercourt one soul entire, unencumbered, freely yielded:*

*One soul. The elder child.*

*In exchange: that the younger child, Petra Jonah Whitlock, be suffered to live, and to go into exile unmolested, her death neither sought nor purchased by any party, mortal or otherwise.*

*Signed and sealed this day, the ninth of Harvestmoon, in the year of the Long Watch.*

Below the words, where a seal should have been, her mother had drawn the storm-sign — the coiled spiral with its three crossing bars — and Petra felt her scarred palms burn as if the sign itself remembered her.

*One soul. The elder child.*

Aldric.

Aldric, who had been fourteen. Aldric, who had stood in the doorway the night the Wardens' riders came up the valley road, and put himself between them and his little sister, and said, *take me instead,* because he thought he was bargaining with men.

He hadn't been bargaining with men.

Petra became aware, distantly, that she was making a sound. A low, continuous note, like wind through a broken shutter. It took her a moment to understand it was coming from her own throat, and another moment to stop it, because sound carried, and there were Wardens on Candlemaker's Row now, and god-touched marks were being arrested, and she could not afford to grieve loudly in a drowned cellar in a city that hanged practitioners.

She sat with the ledger open on her knees and let the silence press in around her like deep water.

* * *

Twelve years, she had believed a version of that night. The version she'd built to survive it went like this: the storm-gift had shown itself too early, too publicly; the village elders had called the Wardens; her mother, in a final act of whatever love she had left after Father died, had gotten Petra out the back and down the goat-path while the riders hammered at the front door. Exile instead of the pyre. A mother's mercy, bought with a mother's ruin. Petra had hated her for it — cleanly, usefully, the way you hate a dead person precisely because they cannot complicate the hatred. Every cold night in Greyhollow, every charm sold under the counter, every flinch when a Warden's boot rang on cobblestone, she had laid at Maren Whitlock's feet like an offering.

The ledger did not argue with that story. It simply made it small.

Because exile wasn't mercy. Exile was the *price*. Her mother hadn't saved her out of love alone — she had *purchased* her life, and the currency had been Aldric. One soul, the elder child, freely yielded. And the Court Beneath the Mountain, whatever it was, had honored the bargain: Petra lived, unmolested, these twelve years. No Warden had ever quite caught her scent. No informer's tip had ever quite landed. She had thought herself clever, careful, lucky.

She had been *paid for*. She had been walking around inside the terms of a contract, safe as a coin in a locked box, while her brother —

While her brother what?

The wolf.

It arrived in her mind not as a thought but as a certainty, sudden and total, the way a storm-front arrives over a ridge — one moment clear sky, the next, pressure and shadow. The wolf that followed her home each night through Greyhollow's crooked lanes. The wolf that sat at the edge of the lamplight with eyes that were not animal eyes, that watched her with something patient and grieving and *familiar*. The wolf that had appeared — when? She counted back, and her breath caught. The wolf had appeared the same week the prayers started answering back. The same week the old gods woke. The same week, if the astronomer-priests were right, that the seal beneath Mount Cael began to fail.

Something has broken beneath Mount Cael. Something is coming due.

Debts come due.

*Oh, Aldric.*

She pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes until she saw stars. Because now she understood the dreams too — the frozen lake, the boy beneath the ice, his mouth moving, his hands pressed flat against the underside as if against glass. She had dreamed it four times now, and each time she woke with the wordless conviction that he wasn't drowning. He was *knocking*. Twelve years beneath something cold and bright and sealed, and he had spent them knocking on the inside of the world, waiting for someone to hear.

The elder child. Sold so the younger could live.

And the worst of it — the part that made her fold forward over the ledger until her forehead nearly touched the page — was that it had *worked*. That was the obscenity of it. Her mother had done a monstrous thing and it had functioned perfectly, the way a well-made trap functions, and Petra had lived twelve whole years inside the purchase price without ever knowing she was bought. Somewhere beneath Mount Cael, her brother had paid the bill every single day, and here she'd been, selling luck-knots at two coppers, calling herself free.

*You forgave her too easily,* she told the ghost of her mother. *Or not easily enough. I don't even know anymore.*

The cellar was very cold. The river murmured somewhere beyond the wall, close and constant, like a crowd deciding something.

* * *

She read the rest of the ledger by touch and moonlight through the grate, because she could not make herself leave yet. There wasn't much more. Two pages after the contract-copy, the entries stopped altogether, mid-sentence almost, as if the book had been closed in haste:

*If anyone finds this — the debt is mine alone. Do not let them tell you otherwise. What I signed, I signed for both my children, and I would sign it again, and I am not sorry, and God forgive me, I am not sorry—*

The sentence ended there. Whatever came next had never been written, or had been torn out, or had been taken.

Petra closed the ledger gently, the way you close a coffin.

And here was the strange thing, the thing she would turn over for many nights afterward: the devastation did not hollow her out. She waited for it to. She braced for the grief to swallow her the way it had swallowed her the night of exile, the way grief had been waiting at the edges of her life for twelve years like a dog at a gate. But instead, as she knelt in the dark of the drowned house with her brother's death-warrant in her lap, something else rose in her — something with structure to it, something with edges. Purpose, arriving the way cold arrives before snow. Not comfort. Direction.

Because the contract said *one soul*, and the Court kept its books, and books could be read. Books could be *checked*. If her mother's copy existed, the original existed, filed somewhere in whatever frozen cathedral the Undercourt kept beneath Mount Cael. And if the seal was breaking — if the thirteenth moon was turning, if debts were coming due, if the gods themselves were waking hungry — then for the first time in twelve years, the door her mother had written about might actually be open.

*There is always a door.* The ferryman's words, recorded in her mother's hard little hand.

Petra stood. Her knees cracked. In the darkness her scarred palms were glowing faintly, a pale storm-light leaking between her fingers, and for once she didn't clench them shut. She looked at the light the way you look at a tool you've finally found a use for.

"You're alive," she said aloud, to a boy beneath a frozen lake, to a wolf at the edge of the lamplight. "You're alive, and you've been *here* the whole time, following me through the streets like I needed guarding." A breath that was half laugh, half sob. "Stubborn idiot. You couldn't just haunt me like a normal ghost."

The glow in her hands steadied. Outside, far off toward the harbor, thunder rolled — a single, dry, impossible roll, in a season with no storms, in a city where weather obeyed no one. The Wardens would log it. The priests would fret over it. Petra Jonah Whitlock stood in a drowned cellar holding her mother's ledger and understood it for what it was: an answer. Something old, hearing her.

*Careful,* whispered the part of her that had survived twelve years by staying small. *Careful. Things that answer prayers collect on them.*

"Then let them collect," she said to the dark. "I'm done paying other people's prices."

She wrapped the ledger in its oilcloth, tucked it inside her coat against her heart, and climbed the stairs out of the drowned house into the blue evening. On Candlemaker's Row the lamps were being lit one by one, and the smell of onions and woodsmoke lay over the street like a blessing, and the people of Greyhollow walked home to their suppers not knowing that their city held a storm-caller with a contract in her coat and a countdown in her blood.

At the corner where her lane met the Row, the wolf was waiting.

It sat exactly where it always sat, at the precise edge of the lamplight, grey as river-fog, watching her with those unbearable human eyes. Tonight, for the first time, Petra did not hurry past. She stopped three paces away, close enough to see the frost of its breath, and held her glowing hands open at her sides like a woman showing she carries no weapon.

"Aldric," she said.

The wolf's ears went forward. Its whole body went very still, the stillness of a creature hearing its own name for the first time in twelve years.

"I found Mother's ledger," Petra said. "I know what she sold. And in twenty-eight days, when the moon finishes its thirteenth turn—" her voice cracked, and she let it, "—I'm going under the mountain, and I'm bringing you home."

The wolf made a sound she had never heard a wolf make. Low, broken, almost human — a sound like a boy crying on the far side of ice.

Then it turned and ran, not away from her but *toward* something, north and east, toward the black line of Mount Cael rising over the rooftops against the first stars — and paused at the lane's end, looking back. Waiting. Asking, in the only language it had left, whether she meant it.

Petra pulled her coat tight around the ledger and started after her brother.
