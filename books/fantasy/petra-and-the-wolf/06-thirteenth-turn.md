## 6. Thirteenth Turn

The Temple of the Measured Sky sat at the top of Lantern Hill like a dropped coin — small, round, and mostly ignored. Greyhollow had no gods to worship and no patience for those who pretended otherwise, but it kept one priest on the municipal payroll because someone had to tell the harbor when the tides would turn, and the tide-priests of the old faith had all been hanged or scattered a generation ago.

The man who remained was called Brother Ossian, and he was ninety if he was a day, with hands that shook like leaves and eyes that did not shake at all. He spent his nights in the temple's open dome, cataloguing stars for an audience of no one, and his days selling almanacs from a folding table to farmers who trusted him more than they trusted the weather.

Petra found him at dusk, three days after she'd followed the wolf out of the market square and then lost it in the warren of streets below Lantern Hill. She'd told herself she was here for practical reasons. The ledger in her satchel mentioned the Court Beneath the Mountain; the mountain was visible from half the city; and if anyone in Greyhollow knew what happened on that mountain, it would be the last man alive who still looked up.

She hadn't told herself anything about the wolf. She wasn't ready to say that word yet — *brother* — even inside her own skull, where saying it made it real, and real things could be taken away again.

Brother Ossian looked up from his table as she approached, and his gaze went straight past her face to her hands, folded tight around the strap of her satchel.

"You walk like a woman carrying something heavy," he said. "And I don't mean the bag."

"I want to buy an almanac."

"Everyone wants to buy an almanac." He gestured at the stack. "That one's last year's. Half price."

"I want this year's. And I want to know about the moon."

Something shifted behind his eyes — not surprise, exactly. Recognition. The look of a man who has been waiting a long time for a particular question and is almost disappointed to have received it.

"Sit," he said, and pulled a stool out from under the table with his foot.

* * *

The dome above them was open to the darkening sky, and through it Petra could see the first stars pricking through, and beneath them, low on the eastern horizon, the moon — swollen, pale, and wrong in a way she couldn't name until Ossian named it for her.

"Count the craters," he said.

She squinted. "I don't—"

"Count them. Go on."

She counted. Counted again. Her mouth went dry.

"Thirteen," she said. "There are thirteen."

"There have been thirteen since midsummer." Ossian folded his shaking hands on the table. "In sixty years of watching, I have charted that moon ten thousand times. It had twelve great craters, arranged so regularly that the ancients called them the Twelve Sisters, each one marking a turn of its cycle. Twelve turns. A perfect circle. The oldest calendars in the world were built on it — every culture that ever looked up agreed on that much, whatever else they fought about." His voice went flat and careful. "Three months ago, a thirteenth crater opened. No impact. No debris. It simply *was*, one morning, as though it had always been there and we'd somehow failed to notice. And now the moon takes thirteen turns where it took twelve, and every astronomer who isn't me has decided their instruments are faulty rather than consider the alternative."

"Which is?"

"That the moon isn't broken." He looked at her steadily. "It's *counting*."

The wind came down through the dome and moved the loose papers on his table, and among them Petra saw star-charts covered in annotations so dense they were nearly black, and — her stomach turned — a page of calculations ending in a date, circled twice, with the words *all debts due* written beside it in a hand that had pressed hard enough to tear the paper.

"How long?" she asked.

Ossian drew the page toward him, though she'd already read it. "Twenty-eight days," he said. "On the night of the thirteenth turn, the cycle completes. A full circuit of thirteen. Whatever the moon has been counting toward, it arrives then." He paused. "You already know what it's counting toward. Don't you. You didn't come here to learn it. You came here to be told you're wrong."

"The Undercourt," Petra said. The words came out of her like teeth being pulled. "Beneath Mount Cael. There's a seal. Thirteen moon-cycles. When the seal opens—"

"—every debt comes due." Ossian finished it without surprise. "Yes. That's the old doctrine. The Court Beneath the Mountain, the Quiet Ones, the gods-that-sleep — call them what you like. Every faith that predates Greyhollow agrees on the shape of it: something under Cael holds the old gods down, and it holds them by contract. Debts. Pledges. Souls given freely, keeping the machinery oiled." He rubbed his eyes with thumb and forefinger. "When I was a young man, I thought it was metaphor. Pretty mythology for people who needed their suffering to mean something. Then the thirteenth crater opened, and the prayers started getting answered, and I stopped thinking it was a metaphor very quickly indeed."

"You've felt it too, then. In the city."

"Girl, I've had three separate people come to this table this month asking me to bless things. *Me.* A tide-keeper with arthritis." He laughed, and there was no humor in it. "The godless city is springing leaks. And in twenty-eight days, the whole dam goes."

Petra's hands had gone cold around the satchel strap. Twenty-eight days. The ledger hidden in her room, with its columns of figures and its terrible final line. *One soul, the elder child, delivered to the Court Beneath the Mountain, in perpetuity.* Twelve years of interest accruing somewhere in the dark while Aldric ran on four legs through the streets of a city that would hang them both if it knew what they were.

Twenty-eight days to get to the mountain, get under it, and steal her brother back from the dead.

"Brother Ossian," she said. "If someone owed a debt to the Undercourt. An old debt. Is there any way to know its terms? To see the contract?"

He studied her for a long moment, and she watched him decide — watched the loneliness of ninety years of unshared knowledge lose, finally, to the relief of handing it off.

"The Court keeps its own books," he said. "But contracts leave copies. Everything that binds leaves a copy — that's what binding *is*. If your hypothetical debtor wanted to know what was written about them..." He leaned forward. "They'd go to the mountain. Down past the Gate of Teeth. And they'd better arrive before the seal opens, because after that, the Court stops taking visitors and starts taking payment." He tapped the circled date. "Twenty-eight days. And the road to Cael is four days' walk if the passes are clear, which in late autumn they will not be."

"Then I should leave tomorrow."

"*We* should leave tomorrow," Ossian said, "if I were forty years younger and a braver man than I am. As it stands—" He pulled a fresh almanac from the stack, wrote something on the flyleaf, and pushed it across the table. "Buy this. It's got my charts in it, and my charts are worth more than the paper. And listen to me, whoever you are, whatever name you're wearing: the Court does not make fair bargains, it makes *legal* ones, and legal ones are worse. Read everything twice. Sign nothing. And if something down there offers you a trade that sounds generous—" his voice dropped, "—that's how it gets you. Generosity is the hook. Fairness is the bait. The only safe dealing with the Undercourt is none at all."

"And if you're already in a deal?" Petra asked quietly. "Made by someone else. Years ago. Without your consent."

Ossian's face did something complicated — pity and fear wrestling for the same ground.

"Then may whatever's left of mercy go with you," he said, "because law won't."

* * *

She bought the almanac. She climbed down from Lantern Hill with the book against her ribs and the circled date burning behind her eyes, and the wolf was waiting for her at the bottom of the stairs, sitting in the shadow of a shuttered chandler's shop as though he'd been there for hours.

Maybe he had. Maybe he'd been there for twelve years, in one shape or another, waiting for her to catch up.

"Aldric," she said — out loud, for the first time, and the name cracked something open in her chest on its way out. The wolf's ears came forward. His tail moved once, slowly, against the cobblestones. "Twenty-eight days. Do you understand me? We have twenty-eight days."

He stood. He turned north and east, toward the black mass of Mount Cael wearing its first dusting of snow like a shroud, and he looked back at her, and his eyes in the lamplight were amber and ancient and achingly familiar — her mother's eyes, God help them both, before her mother had become the kind of woman who sold children.

"Tomorrow," Petra said. "I need tomorrow. There are things I have to arrange. Money. A way past the Wardens on the north road. I'm not walking into that mountain with empty pockets and a wolf at my heel and nothing else in the world."

The wolf held her gaze for a long moment. Then he lay down, right there on the cold cobbles, chin on paws, and closed his eyes.

Waiting. Asking her, again, whether she meant it.

"Yes," she whispered. "I mean it. I've meant it since the lake. I just didn't know it yet."

* * *

That night, for the first time since childhood, Petra dreamed in someone else's head.

She was running — not with legs but with the whole body, the way a wolf runs, spine flexing, world streaming past in smells instead of sights — and she was running through snow toward a door made of teeth. Vast jaws, fossilized, set into the living rock of the mountain's roots, and between the teeth a darkness that breathed. Cold came out of it. Not winter-cold. *Court*-cold. The cold of a place where warmth was currency and no one had any to spare.

*Turn back,* said a voice that was not a voice, resonating up through the stone and the snow and the pads of her feet. *The account is not yet open. The account is never open. Come back when the moon completes its—*

She woke gasping, heart slamming, in her narrow rented bed with frost ferning the window, and the dream did not fade the way dreams do. It stayed, vivid and granular, and she understood with a lurch that it hadn't been hers. It had leaked through the bond between them — whatever the Court had done to Aldric, whatever thread still connected the sold soul to the sister it had been traded for, the proximity of the seal was loosening it. He was dreaming toward the mountain. And now, apparently, so was she.

She lay in the dark and let the fragments settle, cataloguing them the way she'd once catalogued storm-signs: pressure, taste of metal, the color of the sky before lightning.

A gate of teeth. A breathing dark. A voice that spoke of accounts.

And underneath it all, threaded through the terror like wire through wax, another memory — older, smaller, human. A kitchen. Flour on a wooden table. A boy's hands, smaller than hers, shaping dough into a lopsided bird while their mother hummed. *When I'm big,* the boy was saying, *I'm going to see the mountain. Everyone says it's cursed but I think it's just shy.*

Petra pressed her palms against her eyes until she saw sparks.

"He was six," she said to the empty room. "He was six years old, and she fed him to the dark to save me, and he doesn't even remember her face anymore, does he. Just flour. Just humming. Just a mountain he thought was shy."

The frost on the window caught the lamplight from the street and threw it back in thin white rays, and for one vertiginous moment the rays looked like the spokes of a wheel, thirteen-fold, turning.

Twenty-seven days now. The clock had started the moment the thirteenth crater opened, and it did not care that she was afraid, or broke, or hunted, or that the only creature in the world who shared her blood ran on four legs and dreamed in teeth.

She got up. She dressed. She took out the ledger and read the contract line again, and then she took out a scrap of paper and began, in her tight merchant's hand, a list:

*Money. Passage. A story good enough to fool a caravan master. Salt pork. Rope. The almanac. The wolf.*

At the bottom, after a long pause, she added one more line, and looking at it written down made her breath go strange:

*A way to take back what was sold. Or a way to pay for it. Whichever comes first.*

Outside, far to the east, Mount Cael stood against the stars with its new snow glowing faintly — and Petra could have sworn, though she would never say so aloud, that the glow pulsed. Slowly. Patiently.

Like something counting.

* * *

Dawn came grey and early. Petra packed in the dark, left her rent paid through the month out of a superstition she refused to examine, and slipped down into the waking streets with her satchel, her almanac, and eleven silver marks to her name.

The wolf fell into step beside her at the corner of Tanner's Row, materializing out of the fog like a rumor, and together they walked north through the godless city, past shuttered stalls and sleeping houses and the first Warden patrol of the morning, whose lantern-light swept over them and moved on without interest — a woman going to market, a stray dog following her.

Four days to the mountain, if the passes held.

Twenty-six days on the clock.

And somewhere beneath the snow and the stone, in a cathedral of frozen petitioners, a clerk with no face turned a page in a ledger older than kingdoms, and noted — in a hand like frost forming — that the elder child was coming home at last.
