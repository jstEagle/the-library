## 7. The Smuggler's Road

The caravan master's name was Doss Marrow, and he smelled of tallow and river mud and the particular sourness of a man who had lied to Wardens so often it had become his native language.

"Passage to Caelfoot is forty crowns," he said, chewing on the stem of a pipe that hadn't been lit in years. "Fifty if you're carrying anything I need to know about."

"I'm not carrying anything," Petra said.

He looked at her hands. She'd learned, over twelve years in Greyhollow, exactly what people saw when they looked at her hands: a laundress's knuckles, a burn-scar across the left palm from a kettle she'd never actually owned, calluses where a woman who worked for her bread would have them. What they did not see — what no one in Greyhollow had ever seen — was the faint silver shimmer beneath the skin of her fingertips when the storm came close.

But Doss Marrow wasn't looking at her hands the way a Warden would look at them. He was looking at them the way a man looks at a lock he suspects he can pick.

"Fifty-five," Petra said, "and you don't ask."

His eyes flicked past her shoulder to the wolf.

It sat at the edge of the wagon-yard like a piece of furniture someone had left out overnight — patient, grey, entirely unbothered by the noise of mules and men. It had followed her here from the boarding house, down three streets and through the fish market, at a distance that never changed, and every person who passed it gave it a wide berth without appearing to decide to. It was the wolf's stillness that did it, she thought. Animals fidgeted. This one *waited*.

"That animal rides with you?" Marrow asked.

"He rides with me or I don't ride at all."

"A wolf on my caravan." He said it flatly, testing the weight of the words. "You know what the Wardens pay for god-touched beasts? More than they pay for god-touched women, last I heard. There's a bounty board in Harrowgate with its pelt price posted next to the reward for a storm-caller out of the east." He smiled with too few teeth. "Funny thing, that. Two bounties, walking into my yard together."

Petra felt the cold slide down her spine and kept her face still. "Then you'll want to be careful how you spend fifty-five crowns' worth of goodwill."

Marrow laughed — a short, surprised bark, as if the laugh had escaped before he could tax it. "Oh, you're a cold one. Fine. You ride in the third wagon with the Tallowwife. Beast walks. If it eats one of my mules, you owe me a mule."

"It won't eat your mules."

"How do you know?"

Because it's my brother, she almost said. Because Aldric never could resist feeding things — sparrows off the windowsill, beggars outside the chapel, me, always me, always slipping me the larger half. Because whatever the Undercourt made of him, some part of him still remembers being a boy who cried when Father drowned a litter of kittens.

"They can tell," she said instead. "Animals. They know who feeds them."

* * *

The caravan left Greyhollow before dawn on a morning thick with river fog, nine wagons strung together like beads on a dirty cord, rolling north along the old smuggler's road toward the mountains. Petra rode in the third wagon among barrels of lamp oil and crates of false-bottomed crockery, beside a broad, silent woman everyone called the Tallowwife, who rendered fat by day and, Petra slowly realized, pressed luck-charms into candles by night.

"You're the charm-seller," the Tallowwife said on the second evening, not looking up from her work. Her knife moved through a block of tallow in slow, even strokes. "Greyhollow market. Little knots of red thread. Widow Corrin bought one off you in spring."

Petra's mouth went dry. "I sell trinkets."

"Mm." The knife kept moving. "My cousin's wife bought one too. For her lungs. Bad lungs, family curse, doctors said she'd be dead by midwinter." The Tallowwife finally looked up, and her eyes were shrewd and unafraid. "She sings in the choir now. Whole church says it's a miracle. Church miracles get reported to the Wardens, you understand. So does charm-work that actually works." She went back to her cutting. "So either you're a fraud, girl, or you're the most dangerous thing on this caravan besides the wolf."

The fog pressed against the canvas. Somewhere ahead, a mule complained.

"What do you want?" Petra asked quietly.

"Nothing. Just wanted you to know I count heads before we cross a checkpoint, and I count yours twice." A pause. "And that if you're going to do whatever it is you do, do it small. Small keeps us all breathing."

That night, in the dark of the wagon, Petra took out the little bundle of red thread she'd brought from Greyhollow — six knots, unsold, meant for nothing — and held them in her cupped palms.

She hadn't meant to bring them. She told herself they were merchandise, inventory, habit. But her hands had shaken when she tied them, and her hands only shook now for one reason.

Since the widow Corrin's charm had worked — since the tremor in her scarred palms, the taste of lightning at the back of her teeth, the way the rain over Greyhollow had bent toward her like iron filings toward a lodestone — the gift had been waking in her the way a limb wakes after sleeping wrong: painfully, in pins and needles, in floods of sensation she couldn't control. Twelve years of starving it, and now it fed itself on every stray emotion. Grief made static crawl up her arms. Fear made the air smell of ozone. And underneath it all, growing louder each day, something that was not quite a voice and not quite a hunger, saying *come north, come north, come home.*

She untied the first knot.

It was such a small magic. That was the lie she told herself, anyway — that there were sizes to it, that a luck-knot was a candle and a storm-call was a bonfire and never the twain should meet. She wove the thread between her fingers, whispered the old words her mother had taught her before the kitchen fire, words about finding and keeping, about roads bending kindly —

— and the knot tightened on its own, snug as a fist, warm as a held hand.

Exhilaration went through her like current. Not the grey, rationed trickle she'd allowed herself in Greyhollow, selling charms she'd half-convinced herself were superstition. This was real. The thread hummed against her skin. The night seemed to lean in. For one dizzy moment she was eight years old again, standing on a hillside above the sea with her arms flung wide while her mother laughed and the thunder answered her like a dog answering its name —

Aldric's low growl broke the spell.

The wolf had risen at the wagon's tail, ears forward, staring north. Following his gaze, Petra saw it: far off, above the black shoulder of the hills, a light that was not a star and not a lantern, pulsing slow and green against the clouds. The direction of Mount Cael. The direction of the thirteenth moon.

The warmth in her hands curdled.

Every charm she worked was a flare on a dark hillside. Every knot, every whisper, every accidental bend of weather was a message written in a language the Wardens read fluently. The Tallowwife counted her twice at checkpoints because Petra's survival was chained to nine wagons full of innocent smugglers, mule-drivers, a cook with a bad hip, a boy of maybe fourteen who whistled while he greased axles. If Vess came — and Petra knew, with the certainty of the hunted, that Vess would come — the Warden would not care whose oil burned or whose blood spilled, so long as the witch was taken.

She retied the knot loose and dead, plain thread, and put the bundle away.

Outside, the green light pulsed once more, and went out.

* * *

They crossed the border stones at Harrowgate on the fourth day, and the checkpoint nearly ended everything.

Wardens lined the road in their slate-grey coats, checking papers, prodding cargo with iron rods that sang a thin note when they touched charmed goods. Petra sat in the wagon with her hands folded in her lap like a good laundress, heart hammering, while a young Warden with a chapped face ran his rod along the Tallowwife's candle crates.

The rod touched the third crate and sang.

"Open it."

The Tallowwife didn't move. "Candles," she said. "Tallow and rushlight. You want to smell them?"

"Open. It."

And Petra felt it happen before she chose it — the fear rising through her like floodwater, the gift rising to meet it, the air above the wagon beginning to prickle, her fingertips shining faintly silver where they rested on her knee. *No,* she thought, clamping down, *not here, not now, not with all of them watching* — but the power didn't listen anymore; twelve years of silence had broken something in the seal of her own skin, and the storm wanted out, wanted to defend, wanted to turn the Warden's iron rods into lightning rods —

The wolf stepped between the wagon and the Warden.

It happened smoothly, terribly naturally: the great grey shape flowing down from the roadside, crossing to stand before the young Warden, sitting back on its haunches and regarding him with those human eyes. The checkpoint went silent. Mule-drivers froze. Even the wind seemed to hold its breath.

"Warden," said Doss Marrow, materializing with his oily smile and a purse that clinked meaningfully, "you'll forgive the beast. Caravan mascot. Half-trained dog, really. Now, about those candles — rushlight and tallow, like the lady said, though I'll grant the wicks are imported, which is why your rod got excited —"

The young Warden stared at the wolf. The wolf stared back. Something passed between them that Petra couldn't read, and then the Warden stepped back, pale, and waved the wagon through, and did not look at the singing rod again.

It wasn't until they were a mile past the stones that Petra understood what she'd seen. The wolf hadn't threatened the Warden. It had *looked* at him — looked at him the way Aldric used to look at her when she was about to do something stupid, steady and sad and knowing — and the Warden had seen whatever lived behind those eyes and lost his nerve entirely.

God-touched, the bounty boards said. The Wardens knew what wolves like this meant.

Word would travel. Word was already traveling.

* * *

On the sixth night, camped in a hollow below the foothills with Mount Cael a white-capped suggestion against the stars, Petra couldn't sleep. She climbed down from the wagon and walked to the edge of the firelight, and the wolf came with her, as it always came, falling into step at her left side the way it had walked beside her in another life, another body, another name.

"You're getting careless," she murmured to it. "Or I am. Both of us, maybe."

The wolf said nothing. Wolves did that.

"Twenty-two days," she said. "The priest in Harrowgate confirmed it — I heard the pilgrims talking. Twenty-two days until the moon finishes its thirteenth turn, and the seal opens, and every debt comes due." She wrapped her arms around herself against the mountain cold. "I keep thinking about what Mother wrote in the ledger. *One soul, the elder child.* One soul. So why do I dream about a frozen lake? Why do I wake up tasting ice water? Why does the court feel like it knows me, Aldric? Why does it feel like it's been waiting for me?"

The wolf turned its head and looked at her, and in the firelight its eyes were the exact grey-green of the ones she remembered from a kitchen twelve years and a lifetime ago, and for a moment — one impossible, aching moment — she saw the boy inside them so clearly she raised her hand toward its face.

It leaned into her palm. Warm fur. A heartbeat beneath, steady and slow and terribly alive.

"I'm coming for you," she whispered. "Whatever it costs. I'm coming."

Far to the north, above the shoulders of the mountains, the green light bloomed again — brighter now, closer, pulsing in a rhythm she was horrified to realize matched her own heartbeat. And along the ridge behind the camp, silent as falling snow, a line of torches appeared.

Not Wardens. Wardens carried lanterns. These flames burned green.

Doss Marrow's voice cracked across the campsite, sharp with a fear she'd never heard in it: "Lights on the ridge! Get to the wagons — *get to the wagons!*"

The wolf's hackles rose. Its growl rolled out low and endless, and beneath it, so faint she might have imagined it, Petra heard something else — a voice, threaded through the growl like wire through wool, hoarse from twelve years of disuse:

"*Run.*"
