## 8. Village of Answered Prayers

The rain began three miles before they reached the village, and it did not fall so much as arrive — a wall of grey water standing between the hills like a curtain someone had forgotten to take down.

Petra felt it through the wagon boards first. A hum in the iron-banded wheels, a taste of copper at the back of her throat. Old instincts she had spent twelve years strangling stirred in their sleep, and she pressed her scarred palms flat against her thighs until the feeling passed.

"That's not weather," said Maren, the caravan mistress, reining the lead mule to a halt beneath a leaning pine. She was a broad woman with a smuggler's squint and a voice like gravel in oil, and in six days on the road Petra had learned that Maren feared exactly two things: Wardens, and anything the gods had touched.

"It's rain," said Doss, the tanner's boy who rode rear guard.

"It's *rain that doesn't stop*," Maren said. "There's a difference, boy, and the difference is whether we turn around."

The wolf sat at the edge of the road where the trees broke open, water sheeting off his grey coat, and looked back at Petra as if to say: *you wanted to see what the gods have been doing.*

She hadn't wanted to see it. But she had needed passage east toward Mount Cael, and charm-smugglers were the only ones mad enough to travel the old mountain roads now that prayers had started answering themselves, and so here she was, soaked to the bone before she'd even agreed to go forward, watching a village drown under a sky that would not close.

"We go around," Maren decided. "High road, past the ridge."

"The high road adds two days," protested Osk, the salt merchant whose wagon carried more charms than salt these days, hidden under tarps of dried fish. "And the ford's washed out either way. We need provisions, and the miller at Harrow's Bend trades fair."

"Harrow's Bend is *under there*." Maren jabbed a thumb at the curtain of rain.

"Under the rain, not under the water," Osk said. "People still live there. People always still live there."

The wolf made a sound low in his chest — not quite a growl, not quite agreement. Petra found herself listening for it the way she used to listen for Aldric's opinions from across a room, back when he'd been a boy who communicated mostly in sighs and meaningful silences. The thought arrived, as it did every day now, with its familiar weight: *the wolf knows my name.* She pushed it down. There would be time on the mountain. There had to be.

"We'll walk in," Petra said.

Every head turned. Six days of silence, and now the quiet one spoke.

"You want to *walk* into that?" Maren asked.

"I want to know what we're passing," Petra said. It was even true. Chapter by chapter, mile by mile, she was learning the shape of what had broken beneath Mount Cael — not from priests or scholars, but from places like this. Places where the prayers had gone through. If she was going to steal a soul out of the Court Beneath the Mountain, she needed to understand what answered prayers actually *were*. What they cost. What they left behind.

Besides. Somewhere under that rain was a harvest coming in, and Osk had said the word *teeth* last night by the fire, laughing, the way men laugh at things they don't believe.

*They say the wheat came up with teeth this year. Harvest of teeth, ha! Mountain folk and their stories.*

Nobody was laughing now.

* * *

Harrow's Bend sat in a bowl of hills where two streams met, and every roof wore moss like a second shingle. The rain fell straight and endless, drumming on the wagon-covers, drumming on Petra's hood, drumming into the mud until the whole world was the sound of it. She had called storms once — real ones, with lightning walking the ridgelines like a god's own sentries — and even those had ended. Storms ended. That was what made them storms.

This was not weather. This was an answer that had never been told to stop.

The villagers moved through it without hurrying. That was the first horror: not screaming, not drowning, but *adaptation*. A woman led goats along a covered walkway built low and long, its roof pitched steeply to shed the water. Children splashed in channels cut between houses — drainage ditches worn deep as creeks, lined with smooth stones. Boats hung under eaves, upside-down, waiting. Everyone Petra saw moved with the weary competence of people who had done this for a long, long time.

"How long?" she asked the first person who would speak to her, a bent old man mending a net under an awning, though there was no river within a mile worth netting.

He didn't look up. "Eleven years."

Eleven years. The seal had been cracking for eleven years, then, in small ways, in far villages, in places no Warden bothered to ride to. The gods waking hungry — no. The gods leaking. Seeping through cracks in the thirteenth seal like light under a door, and everything in their path getting answered.

"What was the prayer?" Petra asked.

Now he looked up. His eyes were pale as river stone and just as cold. "Girl comes home safe from the war." He went back to his net. "Whole village prayed it, the night the levy took our children. Prayed and prayed. And she came home. Came home the next spring, right as rain."

The pun landed without humor. Petra waited.

"She came home," the old man said, "and she never stopped coming home. Walks up that hill every evening at dusk, wet as the rest of us, and every morning she walks down again. Been doing it eleven years. We buried her twice." He tied off a knot. "The prayer keeps answering. Nobody thought to tell it when to stop."

* * *

They found lodging at the mill, because the mill was the only building in Harrow's Bend with a foundation above the flood line and a miller rich enough to keep it repaired. Brenna Harrow was a stout woman with forearms like hams and a manner that suggested she had stopped being surprised by anything around year four of the rain.

"Room and board, eight coppers, and you keep your god-business outside," she said, looking pointedly at the wolf, who had trotted in behind Petra trailing water and an air of ownership. "I don't care what it is. It eats what it catches."

"He's not—" Petra started, then gave up. "Eight coppers."

The mill worked, improbably. Water power was the one thing Harrow's Bend had in obscene abundance; the wheel turned day and night, grinding grain carted in from drier valleys, and half the village's income came from flour instead of farming. Adaptation again. Petra watched the great wheel churn through the window and thought about how people could get used to absolutely anything, given enough years, and how that was either humanity's greatest strength or its most terrible flaw.

It was over supper — barley stew, dark bread, cheese that tasted of the damp — that Osk finally asked the question the whole caravan had been chewing since the ridge.

"The harvest," he said. "The stories about the teeth. True?"

Brenna Harrow set down her ladle. Around the common room, conversation died the way it does when everyone has been waiting for someone else to ask.

"Aye," she said. "True enough."

"Teeth," Doss repeated faintly. "In the wheat?"

"In the fields." Brenna wiped her hands on her apron with the deliberation of a woman buying time. "Three years back. Drought hit the valley over — real drought, dust to the knees, cattle dying. So we prayed. Whole parish, on our knees in the church, praying for plenty. For abundance." Her mouth twisted. "We got abundance."

She led them out into the rain, because she said you couldn't understand it dry, and took them up the covered walkway to the edge of the south field, where she lifted a shuttered lantern against the dark.

The field grew something tall and pale and jointed, stalks nodding under the perpetual rain, and where the seed heads should have been, the plants bore — Petra's mind reached for *wheat*, for *grain*, for any word that belonged to the world she knew — clusters of teeth. Human teeth, small and white and perfect, dozens to a stalk, hundreds to a row, rows marching away into the rainy dark to the horizon. They gleamed in the lantern light. They were unmistakably, horribly, *healthy*.

"Somebody's milk teeth," Brenna said quietly. "Somebody's wisdom teeth. All of them. Every tooth anyone ever lost in this valley, growing out of the ground, because we prayed for plenty and the ground heard *plenty* and it had plenty to give." She lowered the lantern. "You can't eat them. You can't burn them — they won't burn. You can't plow them under; they come back by morning. So we harvest them. Twice a year. Sacks and sacks of teeth, and we stack them in the tithe barn, and every year the barn fills faster."

"Why harvest them at all, if they're useless?" Petra asked.

Brenna turned and looked at her, and in the lantern light her face was very old.

"Because if we stop harvesting," she said, "they spread."

* * *

Petra did not sleep. She sat in the mill loft with the wolf's warm flank against her knee and listened to the rain hammering the shingles and tried to make the arithmetic come out merciful. It wouldn't.

Here was what she had believed, in the way you believe things without ever saying them aloud: that the gods' return meant *help*. That somewhere beyond the broken seal, ancient powers were stirring and stretching and looking down at a hard world with something like pity, and the prayers going up like smoke were finally being gathered in by hands instead of falling back as ash. She had hated the gods for twelve years — hated them for taking her brother's soul as payment, hated them for the silence that followed — but some traitorous chamber of her heart had whispered, lately, on the road, watching strangers pray at crossroads shrines and *be heard*: maybe it was different now. Maybe the silence had been the malfunction, and this was the cure.

Sitting in the dark above a mill in a drowned village, Petra understood that she had been a fool.

The gods were not returning. The gods were *leaking*, and what leaked through was not mercy. It was power with no judgment attached — raw answer, unmediated by priest or ritual or the slow wise friction of a god deciding *how much, and when, and at what cost*. A village prays for rain, gets rain forever. A village prays for plenty, gets a harvest of teeth. A mother prays for her daughter's safety and the daughter walks home every night for eleven years, dead or dreaming, nobody knew which, because the prayer had no ending written into it.

Prayers harvested like ore, she thought, and didn't yet know why the phrase came to her so easily, so specifically, as if she'd already seen the mine.

The wolf shifted beside her. In the dark his eyes caught the thin light from the window and held it, gold and patient, and Petra found herself talking to him because there was no one else.

"My mother sold your soul to buy my life," she said. It was the first time she had said it aloud to anything, even a thing that might be him. "Twelve years ago. One soul, the elder child. I found the ledger." Her hands ached, the scars itching the way they did when storm-power pressed too close, and she wondered distantly if the rain counted, if the endless answering sky overhead could feel her gift like a lit candle in a cellar. "I'm going to get it back. Whatever it costs."

The wolf laid his great head on her knee. His eyes closed. And for one strange moment Petra felt — not heard, nothing so clean as hearing — a pressure against the inside of her skull, wordless and aching, like a boy pressing his palm against the underside of ice.

Then it was gone, and the rain went on, and somewhere below a child laughed in a drainage ditch, playing in the flood that had eaten her village's future, because she had never known anything else.

*Gods,* Petra thought, staring at the ceiling. *If you can hear me — and I know now that you can — hear this: stay away from my brother. Stay away from me. Whatever you're giving, we can't afford it.*

Outside, the rain answered by raining harder.

* * *

Morning brought company, and company brought trouble.

Petra came down into the mill yard to find the caravan clustered tight around a figure on the road — a rider, mud-spattered, leading a lathered horse, wearing the slate-grey coat and iron badge of a Greyhollow Warden.

Maren spotted Petra and her face did something complicated. "Warden's asking after travelers from the city," she called, too casually. "Asking after a woman, particular. Dark hair, sells charms." Maren's eyes flicked meaningfully toward the road behind the rider, the empty road, and Petra understood: the caravan hadn't given her up. Not yet. Not for free.

The Warden turned. Young, Petra noted. Rain running off the brim of her hat. Tired in a way that went deeper than the road.

"Name?" the Warden asked.

Petra weighed her lies and chose a small one. "Jonah. Petra Jonah. I mend kettles."

"Mm." The Warden studied her, and Petra watched her catalog the details — the scarred hands, the wolf, the way the caravaners stood too carefully apart from her. "Kettle-mender. Then you'll want to know the mountain road's closed past the shrine at Coldmere. God-touched trouble. Wardens are turning pilgrims back at the pass."

"We're not pilgrims."

"No," the Warden agreed. "Pilgrims pray. And out here, friend, praying is the most dangerous thing a body can do." She swung back onto her horse, and paused, and looked down at Petra with an expression that might have been warning or might have been something else entirely. "There's a Warden captain named Vess on this road. Ilse Vess. If she finds who she's looking for, there'll be no kettle-mending afterward. Ride fast. Ride quiet."

She spurred away into the rain, toward the mountain. Toward Coldmere. Toward the shrine.

Toward, Petra realized with a cold drop in her stomach, the exact road they would be taking in the morning.

The wolf pressed against her leg, and this time the pressure in her skull came sharp and clear, almost words, almost a voice twelve years dead and twelve years patient:

*She's coming, little storm. Hurry.*
