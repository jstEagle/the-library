## 9. Warden's Bargain

Ilse Vess had been following the witch for eleven days, and in those eleven days she had learned three things.

First: the woman called Petra Marsh traveled with a grey wolf that walked beside her cart like a hired guard, and no caravaner thought this strange. Second: she sold charms at every crossroads market — luck-knots, fever-strings, small braided things of horsehair and river-reed — and every one of them worked. Third: Ilse Vess was afraid of her, and had not been afraid of anything since her daughter died.

She wrote these observations in her field journal by lamplight, in the careful hand the Wardens' Academy had drilled into her twenty years ago, and beneath them she wrote a fourth thing she did not say aloud to anyone, least of all herself:

*The gods are answering prayers again, and I do not know what I will do if one answers mine.*

The caravan had stopped for the night in the shadow of the first true foothills of Mount Cael, at a place called the Saddle Rest — a flat shelf of ground where three roads met and an old waystone leaned like a drunk against the sky. Vess watched from the ridge above with her spyglass, as she had watched every night since Greyhollow, and told herself the same lie she had told every night: *tomorrow*. Tomorrow she would ride down, put irons on the storm-caller, and take her back to the city where god-touched things were burned or bound according to the letter of the law. Tomorrow.

But tonight the witch sat apart from the caravan fires, and the wolf lay with its head in her lap, and she was singing. Vess could not hear the song at this distance. She could see the shape of it in the witch's shoulders, the slow rocking, the way her scarred hands moved over the animal's ears like a woman touching something she was afraid to break.

Vess lowered the spyglass.

Her daughter had sung like that. Off-key, stubbornly, with her whole chest. Marta had been seven years old when the fever came, and Ilse had prayed — she had prayed the way soldiers charge, desperate and stupid and certain that someone, somewhere, was keeping score. She had prayed to every name she knew and some she invented. She had knelt on the cold flagstones of the sickroom until her knees bled through her trousers, and Marta had died at dawn anyway, quietly, between one breath and the next, while Ilse's prayers fell upward into silence like stones into a well with no bottom.

Twelve years of silence. And now, suddenly, the well had a voice in it. Now prayers were being answered all across the northern provinces — answered loudly, answered literally, answered wrong — and the Wardens were burning hedge-witches for it, because someone had to be blamed, and witches were easier to find than gods.

*They answer too late,* Vess thought, the old thought, the worn groove of it. *Every one of them. They answer too late or they answer wrong.*

She banked her fire and did not sleep.

* * *

It rained before dawn, a thin cold rain off the mountain, and Vess used it. She came down off the ridge while the caravan slept, moving the way the Academy taught — heel to toe, breath timed to the wind — and she reached the edge of the camp before the sentry's dog even lifted its head.

The witch's cart stood at the perimeter, half-sheltered under a lean-to of oiled canvas. The wolf was gone from sight; later Vess would learn it had circled behind her within thirty heartbeats of her entering the camp, silent as falling snow, and had been sitting ten feet away watching her the entire time. At the time she knew only that she had crossed the open ground, reached the cart, and found the canvas flap already open, and the witch already awake inside it, sitting up in her bedroll with a hooded lantern lit, looking at her without surprise.

"You've been on the ridge for eleven days," Petra Marsh said. Her voice was low and even. "Your fire smokes when the wind turns north. You should dry your wood longer."

Vess drew her baton and then, absurdly, felt foolish about it. The woman had not moved. Her hands rested open on her knees, palms up, the scars across them pale as fish-belly in the lantern light. Old burn scars. The kind the Wardens themselves made, once, in the years before the ban softened into law.

"Ilse Vess," Vess said. "Warden Second Class, Greyhollow garrison. Petra Marsh, you're detained under the Statute of Quiet—"

"That's not my name."

"—under whatever name you're using," Vess continued, "for unlicensed practice of the gifted arts within city limits, transport of charmed goods across provincial lines, and—" she hesitated, because the last charge was the one that mattered, the one that had brought her out of Greyhollow herself, past the boundary stones, into country where her warrant weighed less than her sword-arm, "—and suspected consorting with the returned powers."

Something flickered in the witch's face at that. Not fear. Something older and sadder.

"Consorting," she repeated. "Is that what they're calling it now."

"Come quietly and it goes easier."

"No," the witch said, "it doesn't. We both know it doesn't. Come quietly means the quiet room, and the quiet room means questions until you get the answers you want, and then the pyre or the binding, depending on which magistrate draws the lot." She tilted her head. "You've seen the quiet room, Warden. You've probably run it. So let's not insult each other."

Vess's grip tightened on the baton. Outside, the rain ticked against the canvas. Somewhere in the sleeping camp a mule shifted and stamped.

"I had a daughter," Vess heard herself say, and did not know why she said it, except that the witch's calm was unbearable and the truth was the only weapon she had left that might crack it. "Marta. Fever, twelve years back. I prayed. Do you understand? I am a Warden of Greyhollow, I have spent my life putting people like you in irons, and when it was my child dying I got down on my knees and prayed like any ignorant farmwife, and nothing answered. Nothing." Her voice came out lower than she intended. "And now the sky is full of voices again, and every one of them is answering somebody, and none of them are answering right, and I want to know if you know why."

The witch looked at her for a long moment. Rain drummed. When she spoke, her voice had changed — gentler, and more dangerous for it.

"I know why," she said. "And I'm going to fix it. And you can arrest me, or you can let me ride up that mountain and do it. Those are your choices, Warden Vess. There isn't a third."

"There's always a third," Vess said. "You're under arrest."

And that was when the screaming started.

* * *

It came from the south road — not one voice but many, a ragged chorus of terror and ecstasy tangled together so completely that Vess could not tell where the fear ended and the joy began. The camp erupted. Caravaners spilled from their wagons with knives and firehooks. The sentry's dog bolted, howling.

Through the rain, down the south road, came the god-touched.

Vess had read the reports from the eastern provinces and had not believed them. She believed them now. There were perhaps forty of them, men and women and children, walking barefoot through the mud, and they were *lit* — not burning, lit, a soft gold radiance coming up through their skin like lamplight through horn. Their eyes had gone white as boiled eggs. Some of them wept as they walked. Some of them laughed. A boy of no more than ten walked at the front with his arms spread wide, and where his feet touched the ground, the mud bloomed with white flowers that opened and rotted and opened again in the space of a single breath.

"They came through Harrow Ford two days ago," gasped the caravan master, stumbling up to Vess's side. "Answered-prayer folk. The god got into them and wouldn't let go. It talks through them, Warden, it talks all at once through all of them—"

The mob stopped forty paces out. All forty heads turned together, smooth as a flock of starlings turning, and every white eye fixed on the witch's cart.

*"FOUND HER,"* they said. Forty mouths, one voice, and the voice was vast and glad and utterly inhuman, a choir compressed into a single throat. *"THE STORM THAT SLEEPS. THE DEBT THAT WALKS. THE MOTHER-MOTHER'S CHILD COMES HOME TO PAY."*

"What debt?" Vess shouted, because she was a Warden and asking questions under fire was the only prayer she trusted. "What debt? Answer me!"

*"ALL DEBTS,"* they said sweetly, and began to walk forward again, faster now, flowers bursting and blackening under their feet, and the light in their skin brightening toward something that Vess's instincts, honed on twenty years of riots and fires, understood perfectly: these people were going to burn. Whatever rode them meant to burn everything between it and the witch, and the people carrying it would burn along with it, gladly, gratefully, singing.

"Scatter!" the caravan master screamed. "Run, run—"

Vess did not run. She drew her iron baton and her warding-plate and planted herself between the mob and the cart, because that was the job, that had always been the job, and because in the last mad hour she had begun to think of the witch as *hers* — her capture, her case, her responsibility — and no one took what was hers.

The light hit her like a wall. Heat without flame. The warding-plate went hot in her hands and she smelled her own hair scorching, and through the glare she saw the boy at the front reach toward her with arms spread wide in blessing or embrace, and she thought, with strange clarity: *Marta would have been seventeen now. Almost this tall.*

Then the world turned upside down.

* * *

Later — much later, in the hospital tent at the Saddle Rest, with her eyebrows singed to stubble and her hands still shaking — Vess would try to reconstruct what happened, and fail, and try again, and fail better.

The witch had come out of the cart. That much was certain. Vess remembered the canvas tearing back, remembered a slight figure in a grey traveling coat stepping past her into the killing light, unarmed, empty-handed, and shouting one word at the sky in a language that hurt to hear — not because it was loud but because it was *familiar*, the way a childhood house is familiar, the way a dead mother's voice is familiar.

Then the storm-caller had raised her scarred hands, and Vess had seen — everyone saw, the whole camp saw, forty god-touched mouths went silent to see it — the clouds above the Saddle Rest tear open like wet paper.

There was no lightning. That was the terrible part. Lightning would have been weather, explainable, survivable. What came instead was pressure — a sound below sound, a thunderclap that never ended, the air itself folding — and the hillside south of the camp, the hillside the mob had come down, simply *lay down*. The whole slope, a hundred feet of grass and granite and standing pines, dropped flat with a groan like a dying animal, and a ring of flattened earth spread outward from the witch's feet in a perfect circle, and every god-touched body within it went down as gently as children falling asleep.

The light went out of them. Not all at once — it drained, like water finding its level, streaming up out of their skins and away into the clouds in thin golden threads. The boy at the front landed in the mud among his own rotten flowers, and his eyes came back — brown, ordinary, a child's eyes — and he began to cry, ordinary crying, the crying of a boy who has woken somewhere far from home with no memory of walking there.

The rain kept falling. The witch stood in the center of the circle with her arms still raised, and Vess saw that she was crying too, silently, the tears cutting clean lines through the mud on her face, and that her scarred hands were shaking as though the storm had passed through them and left wreckage behind.

The wolf appeared at her side out of nowhere, pressing its grey shoulder against her hip, holding her up.

* * *

By noon the survivors had organized themselves into the grim machinery of aftermath. The caravaners dug a common grave for the three who hadn't survived — not killed by the mob or the storm, but by their own hearts stopping when the god left them, as though the light had been the only thing holding them upright. The boy from the front line sat wrapped in blankets by the cookfire, drinking broth, asking over and over who anyone was and how he'd gotten here. His name, he thought, was Tam. He was from somewhere east. He could not stop touching his own face, as if checking it was still his.

Vess found the witch at the edge of the camp, sitting on the tongue of her cart with the wolf's head heavy in her lap, staring at the ruined hillside.

"That was a level of force the Statute reserves for acts of war," Vess said. It came out harsher than she meant. Everything came out harsher than she meant lately.

"Yes," the witch said simply.

"Forty people."

"They'll live. He'll live." She scratched the wolf's ear. Her hands had almost stopped shaking. "I pulled the call wide and shallow. No kill-strike. If I'd wanted them dead, Warden, there'd be nothing on that hillside but a stain."

Vess should have arrested her then. The moment was there, offered, undefended — the witch exhausted, drained, her gift visibly spent, the wolf the only guard she had left. Every regulation, every oath, every year of training said *now*, said *irons*, said *the law does not bend*.

Instead Vess heard herself ask: "How long since you've done that?"

The witch was quiet long enough that the rain filled the silence.

"Twelve years," she said finally. "I buried it. I buried it deep. And it's waking up anyway, and I don't know if that's me getting stronger or the mountain pulling it out of me, and either way—" she looked up, and her eyes were the grey-green of deep water, and very tired, "—either way I have twenty-six days before something under Mount Cael opens, and when it opens, every god currently loose in the world comes home hungry. Your answered-prayer folk, Warden. That boy. That's what a god's attention does to a person when it's *leaking*. Imagine it awake. Imagine it aimed."

"And you mean to stop it."

"I mean to settle a debt."

"The debt they named." Vess crouched down, slowly, until they were nearly eye to eye. Twelve years a Warden had taught her that the truth lived at eye level. "The mother-mother's child. They knew you. By name, near enough. What did your mother do, witch?"

Something closed in the witch's face, a door shutting over warmth. "That's my burden. Not yours."

"Everything on that road is mine now. You leveled a hillside in front of forty witnesses and a Warden of Greyhollow. There is no version of this where I ride back and file no report."

"Then file it," the witch said, "when it's done. Twenty-six days, Warden. Arrest me after, if there's still a law to arrest me under. Or—" and here she did something Vess had not expected, something that cracked her open like a struck bell: the witch smiled, small and crooked and human. "Or come with me. You wanted to know why the gods answer too late. I'm going to the place where they keep the answers. I could use someone whose faith is already broken. Broken things fit through cracks."

Vess stood. Her knees ached. Her hands, she noticed, had finally gone still.

She owed this woman her life — that was the plain arithmetic of the morning, and Ilse Vess had spent twelve years keeping accounts with death, and she did not carry debts. But more than that, worse than that: she had stood in the killing light with her daughter's height measured against a stranger boy's shadow, and when the storm had come down it had come down *for* her, over her, around her, and for one suspended heartbeat Ilse Vess had felt held. Held by the thing she had hunted all her life. Answered, at last, twelve years too late and by entirely the wrong messenger.

Her certainty did not break. Certainties don't. It *cracked* — one fine hairline fissure running through the middle of everything she believed, and through the crack she could hear, faintly, unmistakably, the sound of something vast turning over in its sleep beneath a mountain.

"I'll ride with you as far as the shrine at Coldmere," Vess said. "Warden's privilege. Prisoner transport." She fixed the witch with a look that dared her to smile again. "You're still under arrest, storm-caller. Don't make me regret the escort."

The witch stood, and the wolf rose with her, and together they looked up the road — the long grey road climbing toward Mount Cael, where the clouds had begun, very faintly, to glow.

"Twenty-five days now," Petra Marsh said. "We should hurry. My brother's waiting."

Vess frowned. "You said nothing about a brother."

"I know," the witch said, and turned to harness the cart. "That's the trouble with debts, Warden. Everyone in them is family somehow."

Behind them, unnoticed by either woman, the boy called Tam had stopped crying. He sat by the cookfire with his broth untouched, and his brown eyes had gone briefly, softly white, and his mouth moved with words that were not his own:

*"SHE BRINGS THE KEY. LET THE COURT MAKE READY."*

Then his eyes faded back to brown, and he asked the nearest caravaner, again, please, what day it was, and whether anyone knew the way home.
