## 10. The Wolf Speaks

The shrine appeared out of the snow like something remembered rather than built.

Petra saw it first as a smudge of grey against the white shoulder of the mountain — a ring of standing stones, half-swallowed by drifts, their carved faces worn to suggestions. Then the caravan's lead driver crossed himself with the wrong hand, the old way, the way people did when they weren't sure anymore which gods were listening, and said they'd camp there for the night because nothing else on this stretch of road had a roof or a reputation.

The wolf stopped at the edge of the shrine's shadow and would not come further.

It had traveled beside them for nine days now — trotting at the cart's wheel, sleeping against her back in the cold hours, watching her with those eyes that were never once animal. The caravaners had made their peace with it the way people make peace with weather: uneasily, and without understanding. But here, at the boundary of the stones, it sat down in the snow and looked at Petra with an expression she could only call *waiting*.

"You're not coming in?" she asked, and felt foolish immediately, crouched in the slush, speaking to a wolf in front of six smugglers and a Warden who was pretending very hard not to listen.

The wolf stood. Shook the snow from its coat. And then, before her eyes could make sense of what they were seeing, it was not a wolf.

It happened between one heartbeat and the next — a folding, a pouring, the way water becomes ice becomes water again. What stood where the wolf had stood was a man: tall, gaunt, naked except for what the snow allowed him, his skin pale as the underside of stones, his hair the same iron grey as the wolf's coat. He swayed once, caught himself on one of the standing stones, and looked up at her.

He had her mother's jaw. He had Aldric's eyes — the ones she'd spent twelve years refusing to remember, brown and warm and always slightly amused, except that now they were full of something older than amusement.

"Petra," he said. His voice was rusted from disuse. "You got tall."

* * *

She did not remember crossing the distance between them. She only knew that her hands were fisted in his arms, that he was cold as riverbed stone, that she was saying his name over and over like a charm that might break if she stopped.

"Aldric. Aldric. Aldric."

"Careful," he managed, and laughed — a broken, wonderful sound. "If you say it a thirteenth time I think something happens."

She pulled back to look at him. Up close the wrongness showed: his edges shimmered faintly, like heat-haze in winter, like a thing held together by proximity and permission rather than flesh. She could see the standing stone through his elbow if she didn't blink.

"How long do you have?" she asked, because she was her mother's daughter after all, and grief had always shared a bed with arithmetic in her.

"One hour." He glanced up at the sky, where the moon hung swollen and wrong, thirteen days past its proper death and still growing. "The seal's close enough now that the court's grip goes... thin. Like a hand going numb." He flexed his fingers, watched the light move through them. "Up here, for one hour a night, I get to be a boy again instead of a dog."

"Why didn't you tell me? Nine days, Aldric. Nine days you walked beside me and said nothing."

"What was I going to say?" Something moved across his face — hurt, quickly mastered. "*Hello, Petra, I'm the brother you haven't spoken to since you were fourteen and I was sold? Please don't scream.*" He shrugged, and the gesture was so familiar, so achingly their mother's shrug, that her throat closed. "Besides. You needed to trust the wolf first. People trust animals. They don't trust ghosts."

"You're not a ghost."

"No," he agreed quietly. "Worse. I'm collateral."

* * *

They built a fire inside the ring of stones, because Aldric asked for it — said he wanted to feel warmth on human skin while he still had skin to feel it on. The caravaners kept their distance, pretending to be busy with tack and stew. Vess did not pretend. She sat twenty feet off with her crossbow across her knees, watching the dead man's sister talk to the dead man, and said nothing, which from Ilse Vess was practically a blessing.

Aldric wrapped himself in Petra's spare cloak and held his hands to the flames.

"I remember everything," he said. "That's the part nobody tells you about being sold. You'd think they'd take the memories too — make it clean. But the court wants you to remember. Remembering is part of the payment." He turned his palms, let the firelight play over them. "I remember the night Mother took me up the mountain. I was eleven. She told me we were going to pray for rain."

Petra's hands went still in her lap.

"She knew by then, didn't she," Aldric said. Not asking. "About you. About what you were. The storm-calling. The Wardens had been asking questions in the village, and there was a price on your head, and she had two children and enough coin for exactly one miracle." He looked at her, and his eyes were kind, and that was the unbearable part. "So she prayed at the shrine under Cael, and something answered, and it said: *one soul, the elder child, and the younger walks free.*"

"She never told me," Petra whispered. "Twelve years. She let me believe I'd run. That I'd abandoned—"

"She let you believe whatever kept you alive," Aldric said. "That was the whole point of me, Petra. I was the lie she bought so you could live one."

The fire cracked. Somewhere above them, wind moved through the higher passes with a sound almost like voices.

"I hated her," Petra said. It came out flat, ugly, true. "For twelve years I hated her for the exile, for the running, for dying before I could ask her why. And then I found the ledger, and I hated her worse, because at least hating her for selling *me* would have meant I mattered. But she sold *you*. She looked at both of us and chose, and I lived twelve years hating the wrong person for the wrong crime, and you—" Her voice failed. "You've been a *wolf*, Aldric. You've been a dog at my heel for nine days and I petted you and fed you scraps and complained about your shedding—"

That startled a laugh out of him, sudden and real, and for a moment he was eleven again, sprawled on the kitchen floor laughing at nothing, and Petra's chest ached so badly she thought something in it had actually torn.

"It wasn't so bad," he said. "Being the wolf."

"Don't you dare—"

"No, listen. Listen." He leaned forward, and the firelight moved through his translucent fingers. "The court holds my soul, but a soul is just the debt, not the debtor. The body they gave me — the shape — it has its own small life. Instincts. I run, and it feels good. I sleep in the sun, and it feels good. And when I found you in Greyhollow — three years ago, outside the tannery, when you dropped your scarf — I knew you. Not with my mind. With my teeth. With whatever's left of me that isn't ledger-paper." His voice roughened. "Do you know what it's like to spend twelve years as a debt and then smell your sister on the wind? I followed you for three days before I let myself be seen. I told myself it was strategy. It wasn't strategy. I missed you so much I would have crawled to Greyhollow on my belly if that's what it took."

"Aldric—"

"And here's the thing I need to say, and then you're going to let me say it, because I've had twelve years to rehearse and you've had twelve minutes." He took a breath he didn't technically need. "It wasn't your fault."

"I know that."

"You don't. You think you do, but you're building a cage out of it anyway — I can see it, I've watched you build it for nine days. Every time you look at me you flinch. Every time I eat from your hand you apologize with your whole spine." He shook his head. "Petra. You were fourteen. You were hiding in a root cellar while the Wardens burned our barn. You didn't sell me. You didn't even know I was the price until last month. There is no version of this where the guilt is yours."

"She should have let them execute me."

"Don't." The word came out with an edge she'd never heard from him — quick, fierce, gone again. "Don't you ever say that. I have been property for twelve years. I know exactly what I'm worth, down to the copper. And I am telling you, as an expert in the field, that you were worth more. That she was right. That given the choice, I would make it again, every time, in every world, and I would not even hesitate." He smiled, and it wobbled. "That's the joke of it, see. The court took my soul and left my love, because love isn't legible on a contract. So I sit in the dark under the mountain holding the one thing they forgot to inventory, and it's yours, and it always was, and I forgive you, Petra. I forgave you before you finished reading the ledger. I'll forgive you tomorrow when you trade your storms away for me, and I will hate you a little for doing it, and I will forgive that too."

She was crying. She realized it distantly, the way you realize rain has started — already wet before you noticed.

"That's worse," she said finally. "Blame I could carry. Blame is a weight with a shape. This is just—" She pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes. "You forgiving me is worse than any curse the court could write."

"Good," said Aldric. "Hold onto that. Anger's useful where we're going. Grief's useful. Guilt is just the court's currency wearing your face, and I won't have you paying them twice."

* * *

Later — how much later she couldn't say; time went strange inside the stones — he asked about her life. Twelve years of it, compressed into stories small enough to tell by firelight. She told him about Greyhollow, about the luck-knots and the false name, about the widow Marta whose prayer had actually worked. She told him about the market square, the voice from her childhood answering out of the sky. She told him about Vess, and nodded toward the Warden, and Aldric studied the woman with open curiosity.

"She shot at me once," he offered.

"I know. She apologized to me about it, in her way."

"Hm." Aldric considered Vess the way he'd once considered difficult sums. "She smells like grief. Old grief. The kind that's become furniture."

"She lost a daughter. A prayer that never got answered."

"Ah." Something flickered across his face — recognition, maybe, of a fellow resident of the court's long shadow. "Then she should come with us all the way. The Undercourt keeps records of unanswered prayers. Every one of them. Filed and shelved like tax documents." His voice hardened, and for a moment the wolf looked out through the man's eyes. "There's a whole cathedral of them, Petra. Prayers that were heard and priced and set aside because the petitioner couldn't pay. Do you understand what the gods have been doing all these centuries? They don't answer prayers. They *appraise* them."

The moon had climbed. Petra felt the change before she saw it — a thinning in the air around her brother, a suggestion of edges beginning to blur.

"Your hour," she said.

"I know." He looked at his hands, watched the firelight start to win its argument with them. "Petra. Before I go back. One more thing, and this one's not forgiveness, it's a warning."

"Tell me."

"The queen knows you're coming. Of course she does — the court wrote the contract; the contract is walking toward it. She'll offer you the trade, my soul for your gift, and it will seem fair, and it will even *be* fair, by the letter of the law down there. Their law is iron, Petra. It's the only honest thing in the mountain." He gripped her wrist, and though his fingers were nearly transparent now, the pressure was real. "But read everything. Read the contract twice. Read the parts they don't show you. Mother signed something that night under Cael, and I've had twelve years in the dark to wonder about the fine print, and there's a clause I can't remember — I can *almost* remember it — something about blood, something about—"

The hour ended.

It was not violent. That was somehow the worst of it. He simply poured back into the other shape, the cloak collapsing empty into the snow, the man becoming wolf between one breath and the next — and then the wolf stood there in the firelight, shaking itself, and looked at her with Aldric's eyes, and could no longer say any of the ten thousand things that were plainly still in them.

Petra knelt in the snow and put her forehead against his.

"Blood," she whispered. "Something about blood. I'll find it."

The wolf made a low sound, almost a word, and pressed back.

Across the fire, Warden Ilse Vess rose, slung her crossbow, and walked over. She looked down at the witch kneeling in the snow with her arms around a wolf, and her face — usually a locked door — was something else tonight.

"My daughter's letter," Vess said. "The one I told you about. The unanswered one." She paused. "Does the court really keep them?"

"They keep everything," Petra said.

Vess nodded slowly, once, as if closing a ledger of her own. "Then tomorrow we walk faster."

Above the shrine, behind the ragged edge of cloud, the thirteenth moon rolled onward — fuller now, and wronger, and closer to the mountain with every hour — and far below them, beneath miles of stone, something ancient turned a page and began, patiently, to read.
