## 11. The Gate of Teeth

The mountain did not want them.

Petra had known it from the moment the caravan's road ended and the pilgrim path began — a goat-track stitched up the flank of Mount Cael, switchbacking through pine shadow into a silence that felt less like absence and more like attention. The stone itself seemed to lean away from her boots. The wind, which had howled for three days across the high passes, dropped dead at the tree line as though something below had put a finger to its lips.

Aldric walked beside her, grey and soundless, his paws finding purchase on scree where hers slipped. He had not spoken since the shrine. He didn't need to. Every few hundred yards he would stop, lift his muzzle, and taste the air, and whatever he found there made the fur along his spine rise in a slow wave, like wind moving through wheat.

"You feel it too," she said. Not a question.

He huffed once. *Yes.*

They climbed through the afternoon while the moon fattened overhead — wrong, still, somehow, though it was only a day from full. Petra kept catching herself counting its curve the way the astronomer-priest had taught her to count anything: by turns, by debts. Twenty-seven days since he'd traced the thirteenth cycle on his star-charts with a hand that shook. One day left. Somewhere beneath her feet, behind miles of granite and older things than granite, a seal was thinning like ice before spring.

She tried not to think about what she was walking toward. She failed constantly.

*One soul, the elder child.* Her mother's handwriting, neat as prayer. *Signed under witness of the Court Beneath the Mountain.*

And now the court would have its audience.

* * *

They made the last camp in a hollow above the tree line, where the stars came down close enough to bite. Petra built no fire. Fire drew eyes, and eyes were the one thing she could not afford — Warden Vess might be two days behind them or half a day, and there were other hunters on this road besides Wardens. She'd seen their signs: shrines stripped bare, offerings scattered, a pilgrim's corpse folded neatly into a cairn with his god-touched hands left exposed on top, as if someone wanted the mountain itself to know what he'd been.

Aldric lay against her back, a wall of warm fur between her and the wind. In the blue hour before sleep took her, she felt him shift, and then — the seal's proximity working its strange alchemy even here, even this far out — his voice, rough and low, right against her ear.

"Petra."

She went very still. "You shouldn't spend it," she said. "Shape. Words. Whatever it costs you."

"It costs nothing I won't pay anyway." A pause. "I need to ask you something before we go down."

"Ask."

"Why are you doing this?"

She almost laughed. It came out as something broken instead. "Because you're my brother."

"That's not an answer. That's a reason. They're different things." His breath stirred her hair. "Twelve years, Petra. Twelve years I've been — *below*. You don't know what they do with souls down there. What I am now. And you're going to walk into their cathedral and offer yourself up like — " His voice snagged. "Like she offered me."

Petra stared at the moon. "Then say what you mean."

"I mean you should turn around." The words came out flat and careful, each one placed like a stone in a wall. "I mean I have spent twelve years learning exactly what they take from people, and I am telling you that whatever you think you're trading, they will find a way to make the price larger. I mean—" and here the flatness cracked, just slightly, "—I watched Mother sign. From inside. Do you understand? I was already *in* the ledger when she wrote my name. I felt the ink go down. And I have never once, in all that time, thought *she should have saved me instead of you*, but I have thought, every single day, *don't let her trade you too*."

The wind held its breath. So did Petra.

"Aldric." She turned, awkwardly, until she could see the pale gleam of his eyes in the dark. "Do you remember what I said at the shrine?"

"You said you weren't sorry. You said being sorry was easy and you weren't going to insult me with it."

"And?"

He was quiet long enough that she thought he wouldn't answer. Then: "And that you were going to get me out anyway. Even if I told you not to."

"So don't waste your hour telling me not to." She reached back and gripped the ruff of his neck, hard, the way she had gripped it when he was a boy and she was smaller and the storms came off the sea and they hid under the same blanket. "We go down together. We come up together. That's the whole plan. Everything else is negotiation."

"That's not a plan, that's a—"

"Family motto," she said. "We're workshopping it."

She felt more than heard his laugh — a shudder through the fur, a huff of warm air. And then, quieter, so quiet she nearly missed it:

"They'll take something from you to let you in. The gatekeeper. That's how the old courts worked. Memory, usually. It's cheaper than blood and it can't be counterfeited."

"What kind of memory?"

"Any kind. But they choose." His voice had gone strange, distant, the voice of someone reading from a text learned in a place without light. "They always choose the thing you'd least afford to lose. That's the toll. Not the memory itself — the *knowing*, afterward, that you paid with the truest coin you had."

Petra lay back against him and watched the moon and did not sleep for a long time.

* * *

The gate was not where any map said it should be.

It was where the mountain wanted it to be, which was a different thing entirely. They descended through a fissure Aldric seemed to smell before he saw — a seam in the rock breathing cold air that smelled of iron and candle-smoke and, underneath both, something sweeter and far worse, like flowers rotting in a locked room. The passage narrowed, widened, narrowed again, and the walls began to change. First scratches: pilgrims' marks, centuries of them, prayers carved in forty dead languages. Then the scratches became deliberate. Then they became *neat*. Then, somewhere in the deep dark, the walls became smooth as glass, and Petra realized with a lurch that the smoothness wasn't stone at all. It was packed earth, compressed by hands, or by time, or by the slow patient pressure of ten thousand answered prayers settling like sediment.

And threaded through it all, faint as veins in marble, ran lines of silver light. They converged ahead of them, downhill, inward, the way streams converge on a lake.

Toward the gate.

It stood at the end of a cavern that opened around them like a held breath — a vault of black rock ribbed with those silver veins, all of them flowing into a single archway perhaps thirty feet high, set into the living mountain with no doorframe, no masonry, no seam. The arch was made of teeth.

Not metaphorically. Petra's first thought, absurdly, was of the market square in Greyhollow, of the fishmonger's stall with its rows of glittering knives. This was like that, if the knives were alive. Thousands of teeth — molars the size of fists, canines like curved daggers, incises worn blunt with age — set into the arch in interlocking rows, and every one of them was faintly, rhythmically, *moving*. Grinding. Slowly. The sound they made was below hearing, a vibration she felt in her back teeth and the roots of her hair, and it took her a long moment to understand what she was listening to.

The gate was chewing. On nothing. On everything. On the slow endless meal of the world's prayers, ground fine and swallowed below.

Before it, on a floor of silver-veined earth, sat a man.

Or the shape of a man. He was robed in grey so faded it might once have been white, and he sat cross-legged with his hands open on his knees, palms up, in the posture of a beggar or a saint. His head was bowed. When Petra stopped ten paces away, he raised it, and she saw that his face was — absent. Not scarred, not masked. There were features, technically: the suggestion of a nose, a mouth, sockets where eyes belonged. But looking at them was like looking at a face seen through frosted glass, or remembered by someone who hadn't loved it enough. The details slid away whenever she reached for them.

"Hollow Priest," Aldric breathed, barely a sound. "Don't look directly at the face. It's not a face. It's a *hole* wearing the idea of one."

"I heard that," the priest said. His voice was mild, dusty, almost kind. It was the worst thing about him. "Most visitors don't. Most visitors are too busy screaming." He rose, unfolding upward and upward, taller than any man had a right to be, robes pooling around him like spilled ash. "But then, most visitors come alone. You've brought a wolf to the Court Beneath the Mountain. That's either very brave or very funny, and I have been gatekeeper so long that I no longer remember which."

"We've come for what's ours," Petra said. Her voice held. She was proud of that. "Passage to the court."

"Everyone who comes here has come for what's theirs." The Hollow Priest drifted closer, and where he passed, the silver veins in the floor dimmed, as if drinking from them. "Grief. Debt. A name. A face. A child. The court keeps excellent records, and the court does not give refunds. But it does—" the hole where his mouth should be tilted, a gesture that might have been a smile, "—admit visitors. For a price."

"The toll," Petra said.

"The toll." He spread his empty hands. "Memory. Only memory. Blood is vulgar, gold is meaningless down there, and souls—" his voice went soft, almost tender, "—souls are *inventory*. But a memory freely given, truly given, held nothing back — that is honest coin. That is the only currency the gate respects. Choose carefully, storm-caller. Or rather—" the tilt again, that awful gentle smile, "—let the gate choose for you. It always knows."

"How?" Petra asked. "How does it choose?"

The Hollow Priest regarded her for a long moment. If he had eyes, they would have been pitying.

"The same way a moneylender chooses," he said. "It looks at what you carry, and it takes the piece that hurts most to part with. Come. Stand in the arch. The teeth will weigh you."

Aldric's growl started low in his chest and built like a storm front. "No. Find another way. There are other gates, other roads, there's—"

"There aren't," Petra said quietly. "You know there aren't."

"Petra—"

"Aldric." She crouched, took his face in her hands — the broad, warm, unfamiliar-yet-known architecture of his wolf skull — and pressed her forehead to his. His breath came fast and hot against her cheek. "Whatever it takes," she whispered. "Remember? Family motto."

"This isn't what I meant."

"I know." She kissed the top of his muzzle, the way she had when he was small and frightened of thunder, back when thunder had been the only thing they needed to fear. "Wait for me on this side. That's your job. Guard the door. You've had practice."

She stood, and walked toward the arch of teeth, and did not look back, because if she looked back she would see his eyes and she knew — the way you know the weight of a thing before you lift it — that she could not carry his eyes and whatever came next.

The Hollow Priest fell into step beside her, impossibly silent for something so large.

"A word of counsel, freely given," he murmured, "since you paid me the courtesy of not screaming. The gate will show you what it means to take. You will feel it leave. Some fight it, at the end. Fighting costs extra."

"Costs extra how?"

"The gate takes the fighting too. As memory. You'd be surprised how many people arrive below having forgotten why they came." He paused at the threshold of the grinding arch, and the teeth parted around him like a crowd around royalty. "Do you understand what I'm telling you, daughter of Whitlock?"

"That I should let go cleanly."

"That you should decide, *now*, what you are willing to lose — because the gate reads intention as easily as it reads memory, and if you stand under those teeth thinking *anything but this*, it will take the *anything but* instead." His hollow face bent close, and for one vertiginous second she saw all the way into it: depthless, starless, patient as geology. "So. What will you hold in your mind, storm-caller, when the weighing comes? Think fast. The moon is nearly round."

And Petra, standing at the edge of the arch with the grind of eternity in her bones, thought of her mother.

Not the ledger. Not the signature. Not the woman who had sold her son to save her daughter and bought twelve years of exile with a child's soul. She thought of a kitchen in a house that no longer existed, on a coast that had probably forgotten them both. Flour on her mother's hands. A smudge of it on her cheekbone, high on the left, where Petra had put it herself, age six, reaching up to be picked up. The way her mother laughed when she noticed — not the careful laugh she wore outside the house, but the real one, the one that crinkled her eyes and showed the gap in her teeth and made Aldric laugh too, helplessly, contagiously, until the whole kitchen shook with it.

That laugh. That flour-smudged cheekbone. Those crinkled eyes.

*Her mother's face.*

Not the traitor's face. Not the face from the ledger. The face from before — the one that had sung to her through fevers, the one she had loved first and longest and most hopelessly, the one that no amount of betrayal had ever managed to kill. The truest coin she had.

The gate would want it. Of course it would. The gate always chose the thing you'd least afford to lose.

*Good,* Petra thought, with a cold clarity that surprised even her. *Then it will believe it has taken everything.*

Because that was the strategy — the only strategy she had left, the smuggler's arithmetic she'd learned in Greyhollow's back alleys: never pay with what you can spare; pay with what makes the creditor stop counting. Let the gate gorge itself on her mother's face and call it ruin. Let it think the account settled. Grief was heavy, but grief was not the cargo. The cargo was Aldric, and the plan, and the name of every Whitlock living and dead that she had spent twenty-seven sleepless nights memorizing against exactly this moment.

*Take my mother's face,* she thought at the grinding teeth, offering it up like a cupped flame. *Take it clean. Take it all. And in exchange, forget to look at what else I'm carrying.*

She stepped under the arch.

The teeth closed around her like a jaw.

There was no pain. That was the horror of it — no pain at all, only a sensation of being *read*, page by page, by something vast and unhurried. Silver light poured through her, sifting. She felt it touch her childhood and slide past. Touch her exile, her scars, her storm-gift coiled sleeping in her ruined hands — and pause there, interested, hungry — and then move on, because that was not what she was holding out. It touched the wolf waiting beyond the arch, the debt, the ledger, the names—

—and then it found the kitchen. The flour. The laugh with the gap-toothed warmth. The face.

*Yes,* said the gate, in a voice like mountains settling. *This. This is the truest thing you carry.*

*Take it,* Petra answered, and meant it, and let go.

It went the way a tide goes out — not torn, not stolen, but simply *withdrawn*, gently, completely, leaving her standing on a beach of herself where something enormous used to be. She gasped. Her knees hit the silver-veined floor. And for one terrible, endless second she reached for her mother's face and found —

nothing. A shape where a face should be. A hole wearing the idea of one.

She understood, then, with a scream she didn't let out, why the Hollow Priest looked the way he looked.

"Paid in full," said the priest, somewhere above her, mild and dusty and kind. "The gate accepts. Pass, daughter of Whitlock. And take this counsel too, since you've paid so handsomely: down there, when they offer you a fair trade — and they will, they always do, the fair ones cut deepest — remember that you once stood in an arch of teeth and gave away the truest thing you owned, and survived it. Whatever they ask next, you already know the price of letting go."

The teeth ground open before her, revealing stairs descending into silver dark, into the sound of ten thousand frozen voices waiting like ore in a vein.

Behind her, at the mouth of the cavern, a wolf threw back his head and howled — grief and fury and love, all of it aimed at her back, all of it following her down.

Petra wiped her face, found it wet, found she could not remember why, and began to descend.

Below, in the dark, something ancient turned over in its sleep — and, sensing her coming, smiled.
