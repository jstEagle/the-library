## 12. Court Beneath the Mountain

The stairs went down for a long time.

Petra counted the first hundred steps out of habit — a smuggler's trick, mapping distances in her body so that fear had something else to do. By two hundred she stopped counting, because the numbers stopped meaning anything. The stairwell was cut from stone so old it had forgotten how to be rough; every surface was worn smooth as river glass, and it gave back her footsteps in a voice not quite her own, an echo half a heartbeat late, as though the mountain were repeating her to itself, learning her.

The silver dark was not empty. It breathed.

She could feel it against her skin like the pressure before a storm front rolls in — that thick, charged stillness that makes dogs bark and old women close their shutters. Except here the charge was not lightning. It was *want*. Ten thousand wants, pressed flat and stacked like slate, and every one of them humming at the edge of hearing.

*Please. Please. Please let him live. Please let it rain. Please, please, please—*

She put her hand against the wall to steady herself and snatched it back. The stone was warm. Not fire-warm. Body-warm, the way a sleeping animal is warm, and beneath her palm she felt — faintly, horribly — a pulse.

The mountain had a heartbeat. She was walking down its throat toward it.

"You knew this," she said aloud, to no one, to the memory of her mother's ledger burning a hole in the satchel at her hip. "You knew what you were paying into."

The echo answered, half a beat late: *into. Into. Into.*

She kept descending.

* * *

The stairs ended where the dark ended, and the dark ended all at once, the way a held breath ends.

Petra stepped out of the throat of the mountain and into a cathedral.

There was no other word for it. The cavern opened vast as a sky turned inside out, its ceiling lost somewhere above in a haze of pale light that fell like snowfall and never landed. Pillars rose around her — hundreds of them, thousands, a forest of them marching away into distance her eyes refused to measure — and only when she was three rows deep did she understand that they were not pillars.

They were people.

Each one stood encased floor to crown in crystal-clear ice, or something like ice, something with a faint amber grain running through it like honey caught mid-pour. They stood upright, hands folded or raised or reaching, mouths open on words twelve years old, fifty years old, older. A farmer with his hat pressed to his chest. A girl no higher than Petra's hip, both arms flung up as if catching something falling from a great height. An old man whose face was transfigured with such naked hope that Petra had to look away, because hope like that, frozen, was worse than any scream.

They were petitioners. Every one of them. Everyone who had ever knelt in a field or a sickroom or a gutter and asked the old gods for a miracle — and been answered.

And never released.

"They're not dead," Petra whispered, and her voice came back wrong again, late, learned. "They're *harvested*."

She walked between them. Her boots made no sound now; the cathedral swallowed even that. Frost bloomed and died beneath her heels in little fern-patterns, the only mark she left. Close up, she could see through the amber grain into the faces, and the faces were not suffering. That was the worst of it. They were *rapturous*. The farmer looked like a man watching his firstborn take its first step. The girl looked like she was laughing.

Answered prayers felt good. Of course they did. And these people had been caught at the moment of feeling it, forever, their joy mined out of them one glittering seam at a time while they stood smiling in the dark and never got to spend a single coin of it.

Petra's scarred hands curled into fists. The scars ached — they always ached down here, where the air was thick with everything she'd spent twelve years pretending not to be — but the ache was almost welcome. Pain was honest. Pain was hers.

Ahead, the forest of petitioners thinned, and the light changed.

* * *

Beyond the last row of frozen souls, the cathedral floor dropped away into a bowl of worked stone, terraced downward in concentric rings like the inside of a struck bell. And rising from the center of it, filling it, was the machinery.

There was no gentler word. Petra had seen ore-works in the border towns — the great iron lungs that pumped smoke, the waterwheels taller than houses, the chains and gears and sluices that turned mountains into money. What stood below her was that, if you squinted, if you were very drunk, if you wanted desperately to believe the world was simpler than it was.

Rivers of light ran down channels cut into the terraces — thin streams of gold-white radiance, moving slow as honey, converging ring by ring toward the center. Each stream passed through structures that might have been looms, might have been presses, might have been hearts: vast wheels of black iron turning without sound, staves of pale bone arranged like organ pipes, and threaded through all of it, everywhere, filaments of that same amber grain she'd seen in the ice. Prayer-stuff. Raw want, drawn up out of ten thousand standing bodies and funneled down into the works.

At each wheel, the light was *wrung*. Petra couldn't describe it better than that. The gold went in bright and came out dimmer, grayer, spent — and the gray ran off along separate channels toward the cavern walls, where it seeped away into the rock like dirty water into a drain. But the bright part, the concentrated part, the part that still sang — that went deeper. Down. Toward the pulse she'd felt in the stairwell wall, which she could feel now through the soles of her boots, slow and enormous, patient as geology.

Something at the bottom of the world was drinking.

"Twelve years," Petra said. Her voice cracked on it. "Twelve years I thought the gods were gone. Dead. Sleeping like the priests said, dreaming themselves thin." She laughed, and hated the sound. "They weren't sleeping. They were *eating*. We just stopped setting the table."

Except the table hadn't stayed empty, had it? Something under Mount Cael had broken — the seal, the thirteenth moon-cycle, whatever chain had held this place shut — and the prayers had started leaking out again. Answering. Because the machinery was waking up hungry, and hungry things grab whatever crawls past.

She thought of the widow Marta, clutching her luck-knot in a Greyhollow alley, whispering her son's name. Petra had felt the charm catch, felt the storm stir in her own scarred hands, and called it a miracle.

It wasn't a miracle. It was a *hook*. Somewhere down here, a wheel had turned, and a thread of Marta's grief had been spooled onto it, and one day — unless someone stopped this — Marta would be standing in a field of ice with her mouth open, smiling, forever.

"No," Petra said. Louder. The cathedral took the word and repeated it, late, learned, and for the first time the echo sounded less like mimicry and more like mockery. "No. You don't get them. You don't get *him*."

The pulse beneath her feet stuttered.

Just once. Just slightly. Like a sleeper shifting when a draft touches their face. But Petra felt it, and something in the machinery felt her too: along the nearest terrace, the golden streams slowed. The black wheels kept turning, but the light passing through them dimmed, hesitated, the way conversation dies when a stranger walks into a room.

Every filament of amber in sight bent, very slightly, toward her.

Toward the storm sleeping in her hands.

"Oh," Petra breathed, understanding arriving cold and complete. "That's why you let me in."

Her gift wasn't just power. It was *vintage*. Twelve years of exile, of suppression, of a storm-caller's fury compressed under scar tissue and fear — she was a cask of the strongest stuff this court had ever tasted, walking down its throat on her own two legs. The Hollow Priest hadn't taken a toll of memory because he needed tolls. He'd taken it because he was a cellarman checking the quality of what the mountain had lured inside.

Her mother hadn't sold one soul to buy her daughter's life. She'd made a *down payment* — and the court had waited twelve years for the balance to come due, walking toward it on its own feet, wearing its own face.

Anger came up through Petra like floodwater through a mine shaft, fast and total and strangely clarifying. It burned off the awe. It burned off the fear. It left something hard and bright and simple standing in the wreckage:

*This machine eats people. My brother is a part in it. And I am going to break it.*

"I'm not your harvest," she told the listening dark. "I'm the weather."

The pulse beneath her feet quickened — and this time it did not sound patient at all.

* * *

She followed the brightest channel down into the terraces, because there was nowhere else to go and because the channel pulled at her the way rivers pull at dropped coins. The light ran warm over her boots. Where it touched her scars, they sang — a fine high note in her bones, her gift straining toward kin, and she clenched her jaw and kept walking and did not let it out. Not yet. Not until she knew the shape of the cage.

The petitioner-forest gave way to smaller chambers carved into the terraces' inner faces, alcoves like reliquaries, and in them the work became intimate. In one, a prayer hung suspended in a globe of amber the size of a fist — a child's voice, still audible, still asking, *please let Mother stop crying* — being polished by nothing, turned by nothing, slowly brightening as some distant grief fed it. In another, shelves of the stuff stood in rows like jars of preserves, each labeled in script that moved when she wasn't looking directly at it.

In a third alcove, she found names.

Thousands of them, scratched into the stone in a dozen hands across a dozen eras — not petitions but records, tallies, a debtor's wall. *Ondrel of the Fenmarches, paid: forty years of rain-prayers.* *Sela Vane, paid: one miracle, one daughter, balance outstanding.* And there, low near the floor where someone had knelt to carve it in a hurry, in handwriting Petra would have known anywhere, in any century, in the dark, at the bottom of the world—

*Whitlock. One soul, the elder child. Received in full.*

Below it, in fresher cuts, a second line she had never read but understood instantly, because it was written in the court's own hand, precise and patient:

*Collateral: the blood entire. Claim dormant. Claim waiting.*

Petra put her fingers on the second line and the stone was warm, body-warm, pulsing.

"Waiting for what?" she whispered.

The answer came from everywhere and nowhere, from the walls and the wheels and the ten thousand frozen smiles, in a voice like snowfall on a frozen lake — soft, vast, and terribly awake:

*"For you to finish coming down."*

The golden channels blazed. The wheels spun faster. And ahead of her, at the heart of the machinery, where all the light in the world drained into the dark, a door of black iron swung open onto a chamber full of quiet —

and in the middle of the quiet, seated on a throne of unspooled prayer, something that wore the shape of a woman turned its head, and smiled with Petra's mother's face.

*"Come in, storm-child,"* said the Queen of Quiet Things. *"We've been keeping your brother warm."*
