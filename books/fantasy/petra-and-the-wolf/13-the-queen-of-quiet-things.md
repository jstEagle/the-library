## 13. The Queen of Quiet Things

The queen did not sit upon a throne. That was the first thing Petra understood, and the last thing she would ever stop understanding: thrones were for kings who needed to be seen, and this creature had spent so long unseen that being seen had become a kind of wound.

She stood instead at the center of her court, in the exact place where the frozen petitioners converged — the still point of a wheel made of people. Thousands of them, suspended mid-gesture, mid-plea, mid-tear, arranged in spirals around her like rings in old wood. And she was small. Smaller than Petra had imagined from the Hollow Priest's trembling reverence. A woman-shaped absence, grey as ash and thin as the space between heartbeats, wearing a gown that might have been woven from the pauses between words.

Her face was Petra's face, roughly. The way all faces are roughly the same if you squint at them through grief.

"You came down the long stair," the queen said. Her voice arrived inside Petra's chest rather than her ears, the way thunder does. "Most of them are carried. You walked."

"I had company." Petra did not look back at Aldric. Looking at him would cost her something she could not yet afford to spend. "The priest took my mother's face as toll. I'd like it noted that I paid honestly."

"Everything here is noted." The queen turned, or seemed to turn — motion on her was more suggestion than fact, a candle flame deciding which way to lean. "That is the whole of what we are, storm-caller's daughter. We are the noting. Come closer. You have traveled far to hear an accounting, and accounts are my native tongue."

Petra's boots rang against stone that was not stone. The floor beneath her had the give of packed snow and the cold of deep water, and when she looked down she saw faces in it — faint ones, pressed flat, like flowers kept between the pages of an enormous book. She made herself walk anyway. Fear was a currency too, and she had learned in Greyhollow that the poor survive by spending carefully.

Around her, the frozen petitioners hung in their thousands. A farmer with his hands cupped as though catching rain that had never stopped falling. A girl no older than twelve, mouth open around a word, eyes bright with the particular terror of someone whose wish has come true. An old man folded over a cradle that held nothing but shadow. In the last chamber, before the Gate of Teeth, Petra had thought the cathedral of the frozen was the horror. Now, walking its living center, she understood it was only the antechamber. This was the body of the thing. These were the organs.

"What are they?" she asked, though some traitorous part of her already knew.

"Answered," said the queen. "They prayed, and they were answered, and they never let go. That is the whole disease of your kind, child. You ask for things. And then you keep asking, even after you've received, because receiving is not the same as being filled. So we hold them. Gently. Mostly."

*Mostly.* The word went through Petra like a splinter working toward the bone.

* * *

The queen led her — or perhaps Petra simply followed while the queen remained stationary and the court rearranged itself around them both; distance behaved strangely here — to a well. Not a well of water. A shaft lined with silver-grey light, descending further than the eye could follow, and rising out of it came a sound Petra recognized with a lurch that nearly dropped her to her knees.

Praying. Hundreds of voices, thousands, murmuring up from the dark. *Please. Please. Please let her live. Please let him come home. Please make it rain. Please make it stop raining.* The sound braided together into a single low tone, and the tone was not unpleasant. It was almost beautiful. That was the worst discovery of the day: that harvested prayer sounded like music.

"This is the ore," the queen said, resting one insubstantial hand on the well's rim. "Every word of want your kind has ever spoken downward. Do you know what gods eat, Petra Jonah Whitlock? Everyone believes it is worship, incense, sacrifice, song. No. Worship is seasoning. What gods eat is *wanting*. The raw ache of it. A god who is fed grows drowsy and kind. A god who starves—" She paused, and the pause itself had weight, pressing on Petra's shoulders like hands. "You have seen the village on the smuggler's road. The rain that will not end. The harvest of teeth."

"The gods did that?"

"The gods *dreamed* it. There is a difference, and the difference is everything. Bound gods do not act. They dream, and their dreams leak, and whatever leaks through the seal lands on someone's field. For twelve years the seal has been weakening, and for twelve years the dreams have been leaking faster. Your village of answered prayers is what happens when a starving god dreams about generosity." The queen's voice did not rise, but the whole court seemed to lean in around the word *starving*, the way a room leans in around the word *fire*.

"And the souls?" Petra asked. "Aldric's soul. My brother's soul. Where does it fit in your machinery?"

Something moved across the queen's face. On a human face it might have been called regret. Here, it was more like weather crossing a window.

"Souls are the mortar," she said. "Wanting leaks. Souls hold. Every soul pledged to the Court Beneath the Mountain becomes a stitch in the seal — a knot of selfhood, dense with memory and love and grievance, and nothing in creation holds shape like a soul that was taken unwillingly. Your brother has been holding up the sky for twelve years, storm-caller. He has been very good at it. He is the strongest stitch in the eastern seam."

Petra's hands closed into fists, and her scars — the pale lightning-branch marks the Wardens' iron had left, the marks she'd spent twelve years hiding under gloves and lies — began, very faintly, to warm. She felt the storm-gift stir under her skin like a sleeping animal hearing its name. *Not here,* she told it. *Not now.* But the gift had never listened well, and the well of praying voices seemed to lean toward it, hungry in a way that mirrored its own hunger.

"You know what I am," she said.

"I know what you carry." The queen drifted nearer, and nearness to her felt like standing in a doorway between seasons. "Your mother sold one soul outright and pledged the rest as surety. But surety is not the same as ownership. The court cannot take what remains of the Whitlock blood unless it is given. Freely. Which brings us—" and here the queen smiled, and the smile was the most terrible thing in the entire mountain because it was *kind* — "to why you were permitted past the Gate of Teeth. Why the priest took only a memory and not your life. Why every door between the surface and this well opened for you without protest."

Petra's mouth had gone dry. "Because you wanted me here."

"Because I need something from you, and I am old enough to know that taking is cheap and asking is expensive, and expensive things last." The queen spread her hands, and the whole cathedral of the frozen shifted with the gesture, ten thousand suspended figures swaying like kelp in a tide only they could feel. "The seal breaks in nineteen days. When it breaks, the gods wake, and they wake hungry, and the first thing a starving god eats is the country that fed it. I can slow it. I cannot stop it — the arithmetic no longer balances. Unless."

"Unless."

"Unless the seal is restitched with something stronger than a stolen boy's soul. Something with *weather* in it." The queen's grey eyes fixed on Petra's scarred hands. "You felt it in Greyhollow, didn't you? The tremor. The gift waking in you again, now that the old powers are stirring. Storm-calling is not a trick, child. It is a fragment of the waking world itself — the sky's own temper, lodged in a mortal nervous system. One such gift, freely surrendered, could re-tie the eastern seam for another century. Perhaps two."

The silence that followed was enormous. Somewhere far above, muffled by miles of stone, the moon was doing its patient arithmetic.

"And Aldric?" Petra heard herself ask. Her voice sounded very small in the cathedral of the wanting. "If I give you my gift. What happens to my brother?"

"He goes free." The queen said it simply, the way one says *the sun rises*. "His soul returns to him. He walks out of this mountain a whole man, mortal, unremarkable, and free. You walk out beside him. Powerless. But free, and alive, and together — which is, I believe, more than either of you dared hope when this road began."

* * *

And there it was. The trap, except it wasn't a trap, that was the unbearable thing — it was a *fair trade*, almost, laid out clean as a merchant's scale. Her storm-gift, which had brought her nothing but exile and scarring and twelve years of flinching at thunder, weighed against her brother's soul, which she had crossed half the world to recover. Any reasonable person would sign. Any reasonable person had already signed, somewhere in the future, and Petra could feel that future version of herself waiting behind her ribs, nodding along.

She thought of Greyhollow. Of the luck-knot she'd sold the widow Marta, and how it had worked, and how she had stood in her narrow room afterward with her hands shaking, hating the warmth in them. Twelve years she had treated the gift as a curse. The Wardens had confirmed it — magic was outlawed, god-touched children dragged into wagons, and Petra had learned to be grateful for the ban because the ban kept her small and small meant safe.

But she thought, too, of the hillside in the storm, of Vess's face when the lightning had come down like an answer. Of the widow weeping over a charm that worked. Of the village of teeth, and the rain that wouldn't stop, and the girl frozen mid-prayer ten paces away.

"It's a fair trade," Petra said slowly. "Almost."

"*Almost,*" the queen repeated, tasting the word.

"That's what worries me. Everything in this mountain is fair the way a snare is fair. The rabbit walks in on its own feet. Nobody forces it." Petra lifted her chin. "You said everything here is noted. Then note this: I didn't come down here to negotiate blind. Before I give you anything — my gift, my word, one single hair of my intent — I want to see the original contract. My mother's ledger. All of it. Every clause, every pledge, every line of collateral. You showed me the price. Show me the whole bill of sale."

For the first time, the Queen of Quiet Things went entirely still. Not the stillness of patience — the stillness of a card player watching an opponent reach for the wrong deck, or the right one. Around the court, ten thousand frozen petitioners creaked, almost imperceptibly, like a ship changing its mind about sinking.

"You would audit the Undercourt," the queen said.

"I would audit the Undercourt," Petra agreed. "You taught me the word myself. Accounts are your native tongue. Well. I'm told I read very fast."

The silence stretched. In the well below, the murmured prayers changed key — Petra couldn't have said how she knew, but she knew, the way animals know weather. Somewhere in that braided chorus of wanting, something had begun to pay attention.

"Very well," the queen said at last, and her voice had gone quiet in a new way, the way water goes quiet just before it freezes or just before it boils. "The ledger will be brought. Read every line, storm-caller. Read it twice." A pause, delicate as a knife laid on silk. "I suspect you will find the reading instructive."

From the dark of the well, faint and far below, Petra thought she heard — impossibly, absurdly — a voice she knew. A woman's voice. Reading aloud.

Her mother's voice.

The ledger was coming up the shaft on its own.
