## 15. What Vess Carried Down

The mountain had been swallowing her for three days before Ilse Vess admitted it to herself.

Not in any way the Wardens' manuals would recognize. There were no tunnels marked on her maps, no passages in the smugglers' ledgers she'd confiscated over eleven years of service. But the road had simply *ended* — one moment gravel and switchback under a sky bruised purple with storm-light, the next a corridor of black stone that breathed cold against her lantern. The walls sweated frost in patterns like script. She had walked in because walking back meant admitting the world above still made sense, and Ilse Vess had stopped believing that somewhere around the moment a witch's lightning split a hillside to save her life.

She carried two things: her Warden's blade, which she had not yet decided whether to keep or throw away, and a letter folded so many times its creases had gone soft as cloth.

The letter was twelve years old. It was written in a child's hand, laborious and misspelled, addressed to no god in particular because Maren had never learned to narrow her prayers. *Please make Mama stop coughing. I will be good forever. I will give anything.* Vess had found it after — wedged behind the loose brick where her daughter hid sweets and stolen ribbons and other small treasures from a mother too tired to notice. She had read it perhaps four hundred times. She had read it standing at the graveside. She had read it the night she took the Warden's oath, and the night she signed her first arrest warrant for a hedge-witch who'd cured a fever with words, and every night in between when the question came back the way questions do:

*Why did no one answer?*

The city said the gods were dead. Greyhollow had been built on that saying, its laws raised on it like foundation stones: no charms, no prayers answered, no god-touched marks permitted within the walls. And Vess had believed it, or something near enough to belief to kill for it — until the night in the market square when an old man's desperate whisper was answered by a voice out of the air, deep and patient and *wrong*, and she had stood in the crowd with her hand frozen on her baton while something ancient spoke a stranger's name.

Now she walked down into the dark beneath Mount Cael, following the witch.

* * *

She caught up to them at the gate of teeth.

She heard it before she saw it — a grinding, like millstones the size of hills — and then the passage opened into a cavern so vast her lantern-light gave up trying to reach its ceiling. Bones. That was her first thought, and her second, and her third: the archway ahead was built of teeth, thousands upon thousands, fused into a gate tall as a cathedral spire, and before it stood two figures she knew.

Petra Whitlock — though Vess knew her as Petra Jonah, charm-seller, quiet woman with scarred hands — stood with her palm pressed flat against the smallest tooth, and beside her, grey and enormous and utterly still, the wolf.

Vess had hunted practitioners for eleven years. She knew what fear looked like on them: the darting eyes, the bargaining voice, the sudden desperate magic. Petra turned slowly, and none of those things were in her face. Only a kind of exhausted recognition, as if she'd been expecting this since the hillside.

"You followed me down," Petra said.

"Someone has to bring you back to stand trial." Vess's voice came out flatter than she intended. "Storm-calling. Destruction of Crown land. Assault on a Warden."

"I saved your life."

"That's what makes it assault." She stepped closer, boots crunching on something that glittered faintly — frost, or ash, or ground bone; she declined to examine which. "There's a whole caravan of smugglers up top who'll swear you leveled a hillside."

"They'd also swear the wolf talks. Would you believe them?"

The wolf's ears swiveled toward her. Its eyes caught her lantern-light and held it, amber and unblinking, and there was — Vess had seen wolves before, had shot them off livestock, knew their animal arithmetic of threat and hunger — there was nothing animal in that gaze at all. It looked at her the way a magistrate looks at a petitioner. Weighing her.

"The seal is breaking," Petra said quietly. "In four days. Everything whose prayer was ever answered by the old gods — they're all down here. Frozen. Harvested. Twelve years of answered prayers, Vess, and every single person who prayed them is standing in that court like ore in a mine shaft." Her hand curled against the tooth-gate. "I'm going in to get my brother's soul back. You can arrest me after. Or you can help me. But either way, I'm going through this gate."

"And if I say no?"

"Then you can explain to your superiors why their prisoner walked into a hole in the world and didn't come out."

It was absurd. It was treason. Every instinct drilled into her across eleven years of service said *draw the blade, take the witch, drag her back to cells and confession*. Vess's hand actually moved toward her sword hilt —

— and stopped, because the wolf had risen to its feet, and it was looking at her not with menace but with something worse. Pity.

"Your daughter," the wolf said.

The cavern went very quiet. Even the grinding of distant stone seemed to hold its breath.

Vess's hand dropped from the hilt. Not by decision. Her arm simply forgot how arms worked. "What did you say?"

"He can smell grief," Petra said softly. "He's carried his own long enough. He doesn't mean cruelty, Warden. He means — he knows."

"I don't know what you're talking about." The lie came out cracked down the middle. "I have no daughter."

"She died twelve years ago," the wolf said, gentle as snowfall. "Of a sickness in the lungs. You prayed. Everyone prays, in Greyhollow, even though it's forbidden — they pray quietly, into pillows, into cupped hands, thinking no one hears because the gods are dead." He lowered his great head. "Someone heard."

The letter. The folded, softened, twelve-times-read letter, and Vess realized with a lurch that her hand had gone to her breast pocket without her permission, that she was gripping it through the wool of her coat like a woman gripping a wound.

"How do you know that?" she whispered.

"Because I was sold here," he said. "And I have spent twelve years listening to everything this place keeps."

* * *

The gate opened for them. Vess never understood how — Petra paid something, Vess saw her pay it, saw the woman's face go suddenly blank and bereft as if a room inside her had been emptied, and afterward Petra could not remember her own mother's face and wept about it once, briefly, furiously, wiping her eyes with the heel of her hand like someone angry at weather. Then the teeth parted, and they went down.

The Court Beneath the Mountain was a cathedral, and the cathedral was full.

They stood on a gallery of black glass overlooking a nave that went down and down into blue gloom, and filling that nave — tier upon tier, aisle upon aisle, thousands upon thousands — were people. Standing. Still. Frosted over like the windows of abandoned houses, each one wearing the expression they'd worn at the moment their prayer was granted: joy, mostly. Relief. Gratitude. Faces lit from within by a pale gold light that pulsed slow as a heartbeat, and Vess understood, in the way one understands terrible things all at once and completely, that the light was being *taken* from them. Drawn upward along threads too fine to see, gathered into the vaulted dark above where something vast slept and fed.

"Gods above," she breathed.

"There are no gods above," Petra said. Her voice was shaking. "That's rather the point."

Vess walked the gallery like a sleepwalker. She looked down into faces. An old man frozen mid-kneel, tears of joy icing on his cheeks. A young woman cradling a bundle — a child? a lamb? — both of them frosted, both of them glowing. A fisherman, a soldier, a girl no older than Maren would have been now, and Vess's chest seized so hard she had to grip the railing.

Every one of them had prayed. Every one of them had been answered. And every one of them had been kept, like a coin in a miser's purse, because a prayer answered creates a debt, and the Undercourt collected.

"My daughter prayed," Vess heard herself say. The words came out of her like blood from a reopened cut. "Twelve years ago. For her mother's life. She wrote it down and hid it behind a brick because she thought praying was naughty." She laughed, and the laugh was the worst sound she had ever made. "Her mother died anyway. So the prayer went unanswered, and I thanked every dead god in Greyhollow that my girl hadn't been — hadn't been *harvested*, hadn't ended up standing in some pit like — like *stock*—"

Petra touched her arm. Lightly. The witch's scarred hands were steady.

"But they're answering again," Vess went on. "In the market square. In the villages. All these years of silence and now suddenly—" She turned, and her voice rose, and she didn't care what heard it. "Why *now*? Why answer the baker's prayer for rain and let my Maren die? Where was this — this *machine* when a seven-year-old begged it for her mother's life?"

No one answered. Of course no one answered. That was the whole of it, wasn't it — the whole rotten architecture of faith and law and grief. Prayers went up like smoke and nothing came back, and you built a city on the silence and called the silence safety, and meanwhile beneath the mountain the silence had a *ledger*.

Then the light changed.

It began at the far end of the nave — a ripple through the golden glow, a shudder in the sleeping congregation, and the temperature dropped so fast Vess's breath plumed white. From the vaulted dark above, something descended. Not a body. A presence, vast and slow, folding itself down through the architecture like ink through water, and where it passed, the frozen petitioners' light flared brighter, straining toward it, and Vess felt her own heart stutter in sympathy, felt something in her lean toward that light like a plant toward sun.

*The Queen of Quiet Things,* Petra had called it, in the low urgent briefing she'd given on the stair. *She keeps the ledger. She speaks in bargains.*

The presence settled before them, and where it settled, the darkness shaped itself into a figure — tall, robed in shadow, crowned with what might have been moons or might have been coins. When it spoke, its voice was not sound so much as *settling*, the way dust settles, the way snow settles, the way twelve years of unanswered grief settles into the bones of a woman and becomes her posture.

*A petitioner comes bearing an old petition,* it said. *Unpaid. Unanswered. Carried against the heart for twelve years.*

Vess's hand flew to her pocket.

"No," she said. "No, you don't get it. You don't get to look at it. You had your chance. You had *twelve years*—"

*All petitions are received,* the Queen said, with the infinite patience of a thing that has never once been hurried. *Timing is the province of need. The Court answers when the balance requires answering.*

"The balance." Vess tore the letter from her pocket. Her hands were shaking so badly the paper rattled. "My daughter knelt behind a brick wall with a stub of charcoal and promised you *anything*, do you understand? Anything. And you let her mother die, and you let the girl grow up motherless, and you let *me* spend twelve years hating the gods so much I made a career of hunting anyone who still believed in them — and now you want to *process the petition*?"

*Yes,* said the Queen.

And the letter left her hands.

Not snatched. Not pulled. It simply rose, gently, the way a leaf rises on warm air, unfolding as it climbed, crease by crease opening like a flower forced into bloom, until it hung in the air between them, glowing faintly, and Vess could see the childish letters standing out sharp and clear in the golden light. *Please make Mama stop coughing. I will be good forever. I will give anything.*

*Maren Ilse Vess,* the Queen read, and hearing her daughter's full name spoken by that voice was like being flayed. *Age seven. Petition received. Assessment: sincere beyond measure. Remedy requested: restoration of the mother's health.* A pause, vast and terrible. *Remedy available.*

"*Available?*" Vess's voice broke apart entirely. "*AVAILABLE?*"

*The remedy existed. The remedy was withheld. The Court does not apologize. The Court balances.* The Queen's shadowed head inclined, and the whole nave of frozen petitioners leaned with it, ten thousand bodies tilting in perfect unison, and Vess thought she would go mad, she would go mad right here on this black glass floor. *The mother's illness was required elsewhere. The daughter's grief was required here. Grief of that purity feeds long. Your sorrow has kept a lamp burning in this court for twelve years, Warden. You have been more useful grieving than your wife would have been living.*

The words landed like a hammer on ice.

Vess stood very still. Somewhere below, a frozen petitioner's light pulsed. Somewhere above, the sleeping gods turned over in their vaulted dark. And inside Ilse Vess, something that had been cracking for three days — since the hillside, since the lightning, since a witch spent her forbidden miracle on a woman sworn to destroy her — finished breaking.

Not into despair. Into clarity.

"So that's the truth of it," she said quietly. "It was never mercy and it was never malice. It's *accounting*. My daughter's love was a resource. My grief was a crop. You people put souls in the ground like seed potatoes and call it divine order." She looked at Petra, and Petra looked back, and between them passed the understanding that needed no words: *this is what we're standing in. This is what we end.*

"Witch," Vess said. "Whatever mad plan you've got — whatever you're going to try in the next four days — I'm in. Not for immunity. Not for the city. For her." She nodded at the hanging letter, at the small determined handwriting of a dead child. "You're going to break this system if it's the last thing either of us does. And when it breaks, I want to watch every lamp in this pit go out."

The Queen regarded her for a long moment. Then, gently — horribly gently — the letter folded itself closed and drifted down into Vess's open hands.

*The petition is answered,* the Queen said. *Twelve years late, as all things come to those who wait. The mother's suffering ended. The daughter's love proved sufficient. Consider the account... balanced.*

And Vess understood, with a final wave of cold, that this was the cruelest answer of all: true, useless, and delivered only now, only here, only because a debt needed settling in front of witnesses. The gods did not answer prayers. They *collected* them.

Above them, far up in the vaulted dark, something immense shifted in its sleep — and the first hairline crack of moonlight fell through the cathedral's unseen ceiling, thin and silver and wrong, and Petra whispered:

"It's starting. The thirteenth turn. Four days early."

The frozen congregation, ten thousand strong, began — very faintly, very terribly — to hum.
