## 16. The Thirteenth Turn Begins

Petra felt the moon turn before she saw it.

She was standing in the cathedral of frozen petitioners, three days past the Gate of Teeth, with Aldric's wolf-shape pressed warm against her leg and Vess's lantern guttering at her shoulder, when the light changed. Not brightened — *changed*, the way a held breath changes just before it breaks. The pale glow that seeped from every wall of the Undercourt, the glow that had no source and no shadow, shivered like water struck by a stone.

Above them, impossibly far above them, through leagues of mountain and stone and older dark, the thirteenth moon completed its turn.

The first crack sounded like ice on a winter lake. Petra knew that sound. She had heard it in her dreams for twelve years, beneath the frozen lake where a boy drowned over and over, his hands pressed flat against the underside of the ice, his mouth shaping words no one above could hear.

*That's him,* she'd thought, the first night she dreamed it. *That's always been him.*

Now the whole world was making the boy's sound.

"Petra." Vess's voice was low, steady, the voice of a woman who had spent twenty years walking toward things other people ran from. "Tell me that was the mountain settling."

"It isn't."

A second crack, longer, running away into the deeps of the court like a whip being cracked down a corridor. Somewhere in the cathedral's vast dark, something that had been still for a very long time shifted its weight.

The petitioners hung everywhere — that was the only word Petra had found for it. Suspended in columns of amber light like flies in honey, thousands upon thousands of them, men and women and children caught mid-gesture: a farmer with his hands cupped as if catching rain, a bride mid-turn, an old man kneeling with his forehead to the floor. Every one of them wearing the same expression. Not pain. Not joy. *Waiting.* The expression of someone who has asked the world for something and been told yes, and is standing in the doorway of that yes, forever.

Prayers harvested like ore. She had walked among them for three days and had not stopped wanting to be sick.

"Aldric," she said softly. The wolf's ears were up, his hackles lifted, his grey body rigid. His eyes — those human eyes, her brother's eyes — were fixed on the nearest column of petitioners, on a woman frozen with her arms raised toward some heaven that had never answered her. "Can you feel it?"

He couldn't speak here, not below the shrine where he'd held his hour of human shape. But he made a sound then, low in his chest, that wasn't quite a growl and wasn't quite a whine, and Petra understood it anyway because grief sounds the same in every shape.

*Yes. I can feel it.*

The third crack did not sound like ice at all. It sounded like a door.

* * *

Warden Ilse Vess had arrested forty-one god-touched practitioners in her career, burned eleven charms in public squares, and told herself every night for twelve years that the gods were dead and good riddance, and none of it — not one year, not one arrest, not one careful lie told to herself in the dark — had prepared her for what it was like when a god began to wake up.

It started in her chest. A pressure, like a hand pressing flat against her sternum from the inside. Then a sound, or the memory of a sound, or the promise of one: a voice so old and vast that hearing even the edge of it felt like standing too close to a waterfall. It wasn't saying anything yet. That was the worst part. It was clearing its throat.

"Petra," she said, and her voice came out smaller than she wanted. "How long?"

"The astronomer said the turn completes at moonrise." Petra's face was turned upward, toward the invisible ceiling of the world, and the lantern-light carved her young and terrified and absolutely unmovable. "Moonrise was four minutes ago."

Vess counted. She was good at counting; it was how she'd survived Greyhollow's streets, how she'd tracked a smuggler's caravan across two hundred miles of bad country, how she'd kept her daughter's face fixed in her mind long after grief should have worn it smooth. She counted to sixty. At thirty-one, the fourth crack came, and this time it didn't stop.

It ran. It went on and on, a splintering roar that traveled through the floor and up through the soles of her boots and into her teeth, and all around the cathedral the amber columns of light flickered like candles in wind.

And the petitioners began to move.

Not all at once. The woman with the raised arms first — a tremor in her fingers, small as a bird's heartbeat. Then the bride, turning another degree of her endless turn. Then the kneeling old man, whose head lifted by the width of a hair. Small motions. Terrible ones. Because Vess had stood watch over enough dying people to know what it looked like when stillness ended, and this was that, scaled up to the size of a cathedral, scaled up to the size of a *world*.

"They're waking up," she said. "All of them. They're going to wake up and find out what they are."

"What they've been," Petra said. "Ore. Fuel. Twelve years of prayers, Vess. Twelve hundred years. All of it poured into keeping something asleep, and now the seal's cracking and the debt comes due and—" She stopped. Her scarred hands had closed into fists, and Vess saw the scars shift, saw the faint blue-white shimmer beneath the skin that she'd seen once before, on a hillside, in the moment before a storm-call leveled half a mountain. "They're going to scream."

"Why?"

"Because they'll remember." Petra's voice went quiet. "Being answered. And being kept."

The fifth crack split the silence like the world's spine breaking, and the first petitioner screamed.

* * *

Her name had been Marta Hale, and she had prayed for rain.

That was all. That was the whole of it. Drought year, dead fields, a husband buried under the dry clay with his mouth full of dust, and Marta Hale had knelt in her ruined barley and prayed to whatever was left listening, *please, please, let it rain.*

It rained.

It had been raining for nine years when the Wardens finally sealed her village, and it was raining still, somewhere far above, on a village of drowned houses and drowned graves and children who had never once seen the sun. And Marta Hale herself had been taken — she remembered hands, hooded figures, a road down into the dark — and brought here, and hung in the light, and asked nothing more, ever again.

Now the light flickered, and the seal cracked, and after nine years of perfect stillness Marta Hale's mouth opened.

The scream that came out of her was not a sound. It was a *removal* — everything she had swallowed, everything she had been forced to hold, coming out of her all at once. It hit the cathedral walls and came back doubled. It woke the others.

The bride screamed next, mid-turn, her wedding joy curdled into horror as she understood where she was and how long she had been here. The kneeling old man screamed without lifting his head, a low continuous keening like wind through a broken window. And then it spread, column to column, petitioner to petitioner, hundreds of voices, thousands, a tide of screaming that rose and rose until it filled the Undercourt the way floodwater fills a cellar, and there was nowhere in the world, Petra thought wildly, nowhere in the whole wide world, that sound would not reach.

Beside her, Aldric threw back his head and howled.

It cut through the screaming like a blade through rope — one clear grey note, her brother's note, the note she'd heard across farmyards when they were children and the dinner bell rang. And the nearest petitioners, the ones close enough to hear it, faltered in their screaming. Turned. Looked at the wolf with wet, newly opened eyes.

One of them spoke. Her voice was rust and dust, unused for decades: *"Is it over? Is the praying over?"*

"No," Petra said, and her storm-gift rose in her scarred hands like water finding its level, blue-white light crawling up her wrists, and she did not fight it, not this time. "But it's about to be."

* * *

The Queen of Quiet Things did not scream.

Petra found her at the heart of the court, in the chamber of the original seal, and the queen was standing exactly where she had stood when Petra last saw her — tall and grey and crowned with moth-wing silver, her face beautiful the way an empty house is beautiful. But around her, the court was coming apart. Petitioners stumbled free of their dissolving columns, weeping, clawing at their own mouths. The machinery of divine power — the great slow wheels of harvested prayer that Petra had seen turning behind the walls, grinding hope into sleep — were shuddering, jamming, throwing off sparks of raw unanswered want.

And the queen's hands were pressed flat against the cracked seal, and she was holding it shut the way you'd hold shut a wound.

"You," the queen said, without turning. Her voice carried through the chaos untouched, which was somehow worse than screaming would have been. "You're still here. You came all this way, and you're still *here*."

"I came for my brother's soul."

"Then you came at the worst hour in ten thousand years." Now the queen turned, and Petra saw that her eyes were wrong — not cold anymore but *frightened*, and the fright in something that ancient was more terrifying than any rage. "Do you know what's under the seal, little storm-caller? Do you know what your mother's bargain helped hold down? The gods don't sleep because they're tired. They sleep because they are *hungry*, and sleeping is the only mercy we ever taught them. Every prayer harvested, every soul bought, every petitioner hung in the light — that was the price of the world getting to keep its mornings. And now—"

The seal cracked again, directly beneath the queen's palms, and the sound it made was not stone. It was a voice. Enormous, slurred with sleep, and *awake* now, awake and asking the oldest question in the world:

*Who is praying?*

"Twenty-eight days I gave you," the queen said, and for the first time her composure bent, cracked along fault lines of its own. "Twenty-eight days to bring me your gift freely given, to feed the seal one more turning, to buy the world one more century of quiet. And instead you stand there with lightning in your hands and do *nothing*."

"I'm not doing nothing." Petra's voice surprised her with its steadiness. Beside her, Aldric pressed against her leg, solid and warm and alive, and above them the screaming rose, and beneath them the god-voice asked again, louder, *who is praying, who is praying, WHO IS PRAYING* — and Petra looked at the queen and felt the thing she had been feeling since the market square in Greyhollow, since the stranger's prayer was answered by a voice from her childhood, finally take its true shape.

Not fear. Purpose.

"I'm reading the contract," she said. "All of it. Before you take one more thing from my family, I'm going to know exactly what my mother signed, and exactly what you're owed, and exactly what happens if the seal fails — because it *is* failing, Majesty, right now, under your hands, and we both know it. So here's my offer, and it's the only one you're getting: tell me the terms. All of them. Or I swear by every storm my blood ever called, I will spend my last hour in this mountain teaching every waking soul in this room exactly how to break the rest of your law."

For a long moment, the Queen of Quiet Things said nothing at all.

Then, above them, the last of the thirteen cracks ran through the seal from crown to root, and the lights of the Undercourt went out — all of them, every column, every lamp, every pale sourceless glow — and in the sudden absolute dark, the sleeping gods finished waking up.

And the rules, Petra thought, as the darkness filled with the sound of ten thousand freed voices and something vaster than weather beginning to stir —

the rules were finally, gloriously, breaking.

* * *

In the dark, Warden Ilse Vess put her hand on her sword hilt, knowing it was useless, and reached with her other hand for the letter in her breast pocket — her daughter's letter, twelve years unanswered, the prayer that had finally received its reply too late — and found, with a strange calm, that she was smiling.

Whatever came next, she intended to be standing in its way.

"Petra," she called into the black. "Whatever you're planning — plan faster."
