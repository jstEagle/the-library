## 17. Storm-Caller's Choice

The court was screaming.

Petra had thought she understood screaming. She had heard the storm howl through the passes above Cael's shoulder, heard widows keen in market squares, heard her own voice tear itself raw the night the Wardens came for her mother. None of it was this. This was a thousand throats thawing at once — the frozen petitioners of the Undercourt waking from their long, glassy sleep, ice cracking off their faces like eggshell, and every one of them discovering, in the same terrible instant, that their prayers had been answered and they had never been let go.

The cathedral of frozen figures shuddered. Chandeliers of moonstone swung on chains that had not moved in centuries. Somewhere far above, muffled by a hundred fathoms of mountain, the thirteenth moon completed its turn with a sound like a bell being struck inside Petra's own skull.

And the Queen of Quiet Things stood at the center of it all, unmoved as an iceberg, holding out her hand.

"The trade stands," the queen said. Her voice did not rise over the chaos; it simply made the chaos irrelevant, the way deep water makes ripples meaningless. "Your brother's soul, released whole, into your keeping. In exchange: the storm. Freely given. You have until the last seal cracks to decide."

Petra could feel the decision pressing against her ribs like a second heart.

It was a fair trade. That was the obscene part. She had lain awake half the night before the Gate of Teeth turning it over — *the storm for Aldric, the storm for Aldric* — and every time, some small treacherous part of her had whispered that it was almost painless. The gift had brought her nothing but exile and fear. Twelve years of scarred hands and false names. Twelve years of flinching whenever the air went green before hail. What was she really surrendering? A curse wearing a crown.

But then she had found the ledger. And the ledger had changed the arithmetic forever.

Her mother hadn't sold one soul. Her mother had pledged the bloodline — *all of it*, every Whitlock yet unborn, held as collateral against a debt no one had ever explained. Which meant the queen's claim on Petra wasn't a bargain she could settle. It was a hook set so deep in her family's flesh that paying it once would only prove she could be paid from.

She looked at the queen's outstretched hand. Pale as lake ice. Patient as winter.

"No," Petra said.

The word was small. It vanished into the roaring dark like a pebble into a flood. But the Queen of Quiet Things heard it — Petra saw the stillness of her change quality, the way a pond changes when something moves beneath it.

"Say it again," the queen said softly.

"No." Louder now, and Petra felt something old uncoil in her chest, something she had spent twelve years strangling. "I won't take your trade. I won't pay your price. And I am done letting you tell me what the terms are."

Above them, the ceiling of the court split along a seam of blue-white light. Dust of crushed moonstone sifted down like snow. One of the frozen petitioners — a farmer, by his coat, his hands still curled around a prayer-scroll he'd carried into the dark forty years ago — opened his eyes and began, very quietly, to weep.

"You are in no position to refuse," the queen said. "The seal is breaking. When it does, the gods wake hungry, and everything in this room — you, your brother, every soul I hold — becomes food. My offer is the only door out."

"Then I'll build my own door." Petra's hands were shaking. She let them shake. "You keep saying *your* law. *Your* contract. Fine. Then answer me as your law requires. Where is the original instrument? Not your copy. The ledger. The one my mother signed."

Something flickered across the queen's face — not anger. Interest. The dangerous kind.

"You wish to audit?"

"I wish to read what I'm accused of."

For a heartbeat the court held its breath — all thousand of its newly waking breaths at once — and then the queen turned her hand palm-up, and the contract came to her out of the dark. It came slowly, reluctantly, dragging a wake of frost behind it like a boat pulling ice. An iron-bound book, tall as a gravestone, its cover stamped with thirteen moons.

It landed between them with a weight Petra felt through the floor.

"Read quickly," the queen said. "The world is ending on a schedule."

* * *

Petra knelt in the ruin of the court with the Ledger of Debts open across her knees, and the mountain screamed around her, and she read.

The pages were not paper. They were something older — vellum stretched thin as onion skin, covered in script that rearranged itself when she blinked, as though the words were embarrassed to be seen. She found her mother's entry on a page near the front, where the debts were oldest. There it was, in her mother's careful merchant's hand, the handwriting Petra knew better than her own face:

*One soul, the elder child, rendered to the Court Beneath the Mountain, in perpetuity, in exchange for the life and liberty of the younger.*

Beside it, the signature. Beside that, the seal — a drop of blood pressed flat and ancient.

And beneath both, in smaller script, in the court's own cold formal hand, the clause Petra had crossed a mountain to find:

*Should the named soul prove unavailable, unclaimable, or otherwise withheld, the pledge reverts to the blood entire. All descendants of the signatory, equally and severally bound, unto the last generation.*

*Equally and severally.*

Petra read it three times. The first time her eyes blurred with tears. The second time her hands shook too hard to follow the lines. The third time she read it the way her mother had taught her to read contracts, back before everything, back when they were still a family with a shop and a name and a future: *slowly, aloud, and looking for the trap.*

There was no trap. That was the miracle of it. There was only a clause written by lawyers so arrogant they had never imagined anyone would use it against them. The court had demanded collateral so total, so greedy, that it had bound itself to accept *any* distribution of the debt — so long as it fell on the bloodline, equally, severally, every descendant sharing the weight.

A debt spread thin enough cannot choke anyone.

"Queen," Petra said. She closed the ledger. The sound of it was very loud. "I invoke the reverting clause."

The chaos faltered. Around the edges of the great chamber, petitioners who had been stumbling free of their ice-coffins paused mid-step, their heads turning in unison, as though the words had hooks in them. Of course they did. They were debtors. Every soul in this room was a debtor, and somewhere in the machinery of the Undercourt, her words had just been entered into evidence.

"You cannot invoke what you do not understand," the queen said. But she had come closer. Close enough now that Petra could see that her eyes were not eyes but moons, waning, patient, hungry. "The clause binds the bloodline to the court. It does not release it. Invoking it changes nothing except the paperwork."

"Wrong." Petra got to her feet. Her legs were unsteady and her voice was not. "The clause says *equally and severally*. Equally. Severally. Do you know what those words mean, queen? They mean the debt belongs to all of us together and each of us alone. They mean you cannot take it from one. You have to take it from *everyone*. From every living Whitlock. Equally."

"There is only you," the queen said. "And the wolf."

"Is there?" Petra smiled, and it was not a kind smile, and she was distantly amazed to find it on her face. "Did you check? Did your clerks actually count us? Or did you simply assume that a woman who sold her children would leave no others behind?"

The queen said nothing. And in that silence, Petra understood that she had guessed right — that the Undercourt, for all its frozen grandeur, kept its books the way frightened institutions always had: carelessly, because no one had ever dared ask.

"My mother had four sisters," Petra said. "Three lived past childhood. One died childless — but two didn't. Aunt Maren has three sons. Aunt Odile has a daughter and two grandchildren in the coast towns. And Odile's daughter married a fisherman named Corwin Hale, and they have a baby who was born in spring, and that baby is a Whitlock's blood whether anyone bothered to write it down or not." She was counting on her fingers now, aloud, in front of the whole waking court, her voice rising over the groan of the breaking seals. "Seven. Eight with the baby. Nine if you count my cousin Tam's bastard, and Tam always counted him. Nine living descendants of Maren Whitlock, née Whitlock, signatory of this contract."

"You would doom them all," the queen breathed. "Spread your sin across innocents. Children. A babe in arms."

"I would *share* it," Petra said. "Which is what families do. What mine should have done twelve years ago, instead of feeding one child to the dark to save the other." Her throat tightened, and she let it. "You want to know what I learned from my mother, queen? I learned what a bargain looks like when one person carries it alone. It looks like a boy under a frozen lake. It looks like a girl with scars on her hands who can't touch her own name. I am *done* with bargains like that. So here is mine, offered openly, in your own hall, under your own law:

"Divide the debt. Divide it nine ways — or ninety, or nine hundred, I don't care, count every cousin twice removed if your clerks need the work. Take from each of us our equal share. Mine, you may have in full." She lifted her scarred hands, palms out, the way you show a magistrate you carry no blade. "Take the storm. All of it. Right now. Tonight. I give it freely, and gladly, and I will even say thank you.

"But you will take *only* my share. And Aldric walks out of here owing nothing, because his share dies with the division. And every Whitlock after him owes nothing, because a debt divided past recovery is a debt *extinguished* — and your own law says so. Article and verse. Look it up."

The silence that followed was the deepest thing in the mountain.

Far above, the thirteenth seal cracked like a glacier calving. Light poured down through the wound in the world — not moonlight, not sunlight, something older and hungrier, the first gray dawn-light of gods opening their eyes. The frozen petitioners were awake now, all of them, thousands upon thousands, standing in the ruins of their long captivity and staring at the small human woman who was shouting terms at a queen.

"Child," the queen said at last, and her voice had changed. The patience in it had gone brittle. "Do you know what happens to mortals who litigate with the Undercourt?"

"They win," said a new voice. "Occasionally. It's why we have the clause."

Petra turned so fast her vision swam.

Vess stood at the shattered mouth of the gate, Warden's coat scorched, one arm hanging wrong, her daughter's prayer-letter clutched in her good fist like a weapon. Behind her, filling the passage, came the freed — hundreds of them, thousands, petitioners streaming out of the deep courts with their answered prayers still glowing in their hands, and every one of them, Petra realized, was a debt the court owed and had never paid.

"You wrote the law to protect yourselves," Vess said, limping forward, her voice raw with twelve years of rage finally finding its target. "Answered prayers held forever. Souls kept as ore. And then you got so fat and safe you stopped reading your own fine print." She held up the letter — her dead daughter's childish handwriting, *please bring Mama home from the sea* — and the paper blazed with light twelve years late. "Well. Someone's reading it now."

The queen's moons-eyes fixed on Vess. On the letter. On the crowd behind her.

And Petra watched — she would remember this until she died — she watched the Queen of Quiet Things become afraid.

"The debt," the queen said, and it was almost a whisper. "Divided. Entered. Witnessed." The words sounded less like speech than like a machine engaging, gear after ancient gear. "By the law beneath the mountain... so it is done."

The storm left Petra then.

It did not hurt the way she had braced for. It was worse than pain and stranger than pleasure — a pouring-out, a long silver thread unwinding from her spine through her palms and into the queen's waiting dark, twelve years of swallowed lightning leaving her all at once, and with it every memory of what it felt like to be the sky's favorite child. She felt her knees buckle. She felt Aldric — grey, enormous, real — catch her before she hit the ground, his fur rough under her cheek, his heartbeat fast and mortal and warm.

And in the last instant before the gift was gone entirely, she felt it answer one final time. Not a call. Just a farewell. The taste of rain on a wind that had followed her since childhood, brushing her face once, gently, the way her mother used to — and then gone, gone, gone, and Petra Jonah Whitlock was only a woman kneeling on the floor of a dying underworld, holding onto a wolf, empty-handed and lighter than she had ever been in her life.

"So it is done," the court repeated, everywhere at once, in ten thousand thawing voices.

The seals blew outward. The gods woke.

* * *

The chamber was coming apart in slow motion, the way mountains do — stone flowing like syrup, moonstone chandeliers raining sparks, the great dark filling with a light that had no source and no mercy in it. Through it all, the freed petitioners were running for the gate, and the gate was holding, because gates built to keep souls *in* had never once been asked to let them out.

"It won't open," Aldric said. He had human words again — the seal's breaking had bought him back his voice if not his shape — and his voice was hoarse with disuse, beautiful, infuriatingly calm. "Not for them. Not for us. It opens for payment, and everyone in this room just spent theirs."

"Everyone?" Petra pushed herself upright against his flank. Her hands were just hands now. She kept looking at them as though expecting lightning. "Everyone in this room?"

"Almost." His ears pricked toward the far end of the court, where the queen still stood amid the ruin, diminished now, her moons dimming, her long reign ending in real time. "She hasn't paid. Queens never do."

Petra looked at Vess. Vess looked at the gate. And then Vess did the thing Petra would owe her forever, the thing that made a Warden of Greyhollow turn her back on every oath she'd ever sworn: she raised her daughter's blazing prayer-letter over her head like a torch, and she walked toward the queen, and she said, in a voice that cracked but did not break —

"One more debt to enter. Yours. Answered prayers, held twelve years past due, plus interest. I'm the creditor now, queen. And I'm calling it in."

The gate shuddered.

Somewhere beyond it, up through the broken seals and the flooding light, the first of the waking gods rose over the world like weather — and the mountain began, at last, to sing.
