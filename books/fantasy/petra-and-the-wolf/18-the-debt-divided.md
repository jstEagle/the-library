## 18. The Debt Divided

The mountain was singing, and the Queen of Quiet Things did not like the song.

Petra felt it in her teeth before she heard it anywhere else — a low harmonic rolling up through the cathedral floor, through the ice that held ten thousand petitioners frozen mid-plea, through the soles of her boots and the marrow of her scarred hands. The thirteenth seal had cracked like river ice in spring. Somewhere above them, light was pouring down through fissures in the world, and somewhere below them, things older than prayer were turning over in their sleep.

The queen rose from her throne of folded prayers.

She had been beautiful once, Petra thought — in the way that a frozen lake is beautiful, in the way that stillness is beautiful when you don't yet know what's underneath it. But the waking had gotten into her. Her gown of grey silk moved now when there was no wind to move it. Her eyes, which had been the color of closed doors, were opening by degrees, and what looked out of them was hungry.

"You are owed nothing," the queen said. "You are *owed*. There is a difference, little storm-caller, and the ledger knows it."

"Then let's read the ledger." Petra's voice came out steadier than she felt. Behind her, Aldric stood in his borrowed hour of human shape, his time nearly spent, his edges already going soft and grey at the cuffs like a man photographed in fading light. He couldn't speak here — the court had taken his voice with his soul, twelve years ago, and even now, half-free, he could only watch her with those eyes that were too human for a wolf and had been all along.

*Say it for both of us,* those eyes said. *You always could.*

So she would.

"The contract," Petra said, and lifted the page she had torn from the Ledger of Debts itself — her mother's signature at the bottom, the ink still wet after twelve years because some inks never dry, not down here where nothing was allowed to finish. "One soul, the elder child. That's what she wrote. One soul. But collateral — collateral is a different animal, isn't it? Collateral isn't what you sell. Collateral is what you promise *if*."

The queen's head tilted. Around the cathedral, the frozen petitioners had begun, very faintly, to shiver in their ice. Not awake. Not yet. But dreaming harder.

"You read the pledge clause," the queen said.

"I read every word. I had twenty-eight days and nothing else to do." Petra made herself smile. It felt like baring teeth, which was appropriate; they were standing in the Gate of Teeth's own heart, and she intended to bite. "The bloodline of Whitlock stands as surety against default. All of it. Every descendant of the signer, held in pledge until the debt is discharged. Those are your words, queen. Your law. The law your court has enforced since before my mother's mother learned to pray."

"It is," the queen said slowly.

"And has the debt defaulted?"

The silence that followed had texture. It had weight. Somewhere high above, through the cracked seals, moonlight — wrong moonlight, thirteenth-moonlight — came slanting down into the cathedral and laid a pale stripe across the frozen petitioners, and Petra watched the queen understand, one slow degree at a time, exactly what had been brought into her house.

"The debt was paid," the queen said. "Twelve years past. One soul, received and held—"

"One soul was *delivered*," Petra said. "Paid? No. Because payment wasn't the bargain, was it? I've been reading your law since the gate. You don't buy souls down here. You *hold* them. Against something. A soul in the Undercourt isn't currency — it's collateral. My brother's soul sits in your vaults the way a coat sits in a pawnshop: waiting for someone to come back and redeem it. And no one ever came back. So it just... sat. Accruing."

"Accruing," the queen repeated.

"Interest," Petra said. "You taught me that word yourself, an hour ago, when you tried to charge it to me. Answered prayers held past due accrue interest. Well. So do held souls. Twelve years of it. And here's what I think, queen — here's what I think your own ledger will show if anyone dares to open it while the seals are cracking and the gods are waking and every rule in this house is straining at its seams—" she took one step forward, and the storm in her scarred hands woke and hummed, "—I think the debt has defaulted. I think a pledge unclaimed for twelve years, with the creditor asleep and the seal broken and the whole court coming apart at the joints, is a pledge in breach. And I think when a pledge defaults, the surety comes due. *All* of the surety."

The queen went very still.

It was the stillness of a trap recognizing its own jaws.

"No," the queen said.

"You wrote it down," Petra said gently. "That's the thing about creatures like you. You can't help writing it down. Law is the only immortality you have, and you'd rather be bound by it than be nothing at all. So bind us. Bind us *all*."

She turned — slowly, deliberately, the way her mother had once turned to face a room full of neighbors before denouncing her own daughter — and she began to name them.

"Maren Whitlock, of the coast road, third of her name, keeper of the lighthouse at Greyhollow Point. Descendant of the signer. Held in pledge."

The words left her mouth and did not fall. They hung in the air of the cathedral like frost forming, and then they flew — she saw them go, actual letters, actual weight, streaming up toward the great Ledger that hovered behind the throne, and she heard the scratch of the pen as the court recorded them, because the court could not *not* record them. That was the beauty of it. That was the terrible, perfect machinery of it. The Undercourt was a place built entirely out of bargains, and bargains, once spoken in this hall, had to be entered.

"Tomas Whitlock, ferryman on the Vell, second of his name. Descendant of the signer. Held in pledge."

*Scratch.*

"Ilsa Whitlock, weaver, of the village of Answered Prayers — yes, queen, I walked through it, I saw what your mercy does — descendant of the signer. Held in pledge."

*Scratch.*

"Jonah Whitlock, dead these nine years, but dead men keep their names, and their names keep their claims. Descendant of the signer. Held in pledge — posthumously, contingently, against whatever comes after, if anything does."

The pen hesitated. Then scratched.

Petra kept going. She had spent three nights on the smuggler's road doing nothing but remembering — lying in the dark of a wagon while the wolf watched the stars, cataloguing every cousin, every removed relation, every half-forgotten branch of a family scattered across two kingdoms like seed from a burst pod. Her mother had been thorough in selling. Petra would be thorough in saving.

"Aldous Whitlock, tinker. Brigid Whitlock, midwife. The twins Sef and Sorrel, who I'm told pick pockets in Greyhollow's lower market, and who I hope never learn why luck runs in their fingers." She was smiling now, really smiling, and it hurt her face. "Every one of them a descendant of the signer. Every one of them held in pledge. Do you feel it, queen? Do you feel the debt dividing?"

The queen had begun to walk down from her throne. Each step made no sound, and each step was slower than the last, as though the air between her and Petra were thickening into glass.

"You cannot dilute a claim by naming strangers—"

"They're not strangers. They're *kin*. That's the entire point of a bloodline clause — it binds the blood, all of it, equally, or it binds nothing. You can't have it both ways. You can't hold the whole family hostage and then collect from one member of it. That's not law. That's appetite." Petra's hands were burning now, the old scars gone white-hot, the storm pressing up under her skin like water under ice. She let it press. She wanted the queen to see it. *This is what you want,* she thought. *Look at it. Want it. And watch it slip away.* "So divide it. By your own law, the pledge spreads across every living descendant — and I have named nineteen, queen, and I'll name nineteen more if you make me, and cousins' children, and children unborn, because a bloodline doesn't end, it *branches*, and every branch carries the same root. Nineteen parts of one debt. Thirty-eight. Fifty. Divide my brother's soul fifty ways and what do you have? A whisper. A cold spot in a room. Nothing you can hold, nothing you can feed a waking god, nothing worth the price of keeping this court open another century."

"Stop," the queen said.

"Make me," said Petra. "Oh — wait. You can't. Because stopping me would require breaking your own law, and if your law breaks, everything breaks, everything you *are*, and the gods wake anyway, except now they wake with no rules at all." She spread her arms. The storm-light ran along them like veins of lightning in dark glass. "Your move, majesty. I've read the book. Have you?"

For a moment — one long, ringing moment, while the mountain sang and the ice groaned and the frozen petitioners shivered in their thousand-year dreams — the Queen of Quiet Things simply stood there, and Petra saw her do the arithmetic.

She saw it happen behind the queen's opening eyes: the counting of descendants against the dwindling value of a divided soul; the weighing of law against hunger; the horrible, dawning recognition that a creature made of contracts could be destroyed by one, that the same instrument which had bought a boy's soul twelve years ago was now, in the mouth of his sister, being used to pry the vault open with its own edge.

The queen stopped walking.

"You would leave yourself nothing," she said quietly. "No power. No protection. Mortal, in a world where the gods are waking hungry. That is a foolish trade, storm-caller."

"It's not a trade." Petra's voice cracked, finally, on the truth of it, and she let it crack. "It's an inheritance. My mother gave me one — a debt, a chain, a brother sold out of his own body. I'm giving my family a different one. Cheaper. Fairer. The kind you can live under." She swallowed. "That's all inheritance ever is, I think. Whatever you hand down. Chains or keys. She chose chains. I choose keys."

Behind her, Aldric made a sound — not a word, not quite, but something between a wolf's whine and a boy's breath, and it was the loudest thing in the cathedral.

The queen looked at him. Then at the Ledger, where nineteen new entries gleamed like fresh scars, each one a fraction of a claim, each fraction smaller than the last, the whole debt now scattered so thin across the living world that no single soul among the Whitlocks owed the court more than a shiver, a dream, a moment of unexplained sadness on a winter night.

And the Queen of Quiet Things — who had slept fat and safe on harvested prayers for an age, who had answered a widow's grief and a farmer's drought and a child's plea for rain with the literal, mindless precision of a machine that did not care — bowed her head.

"The claim is divided," she said, and the words came out of her like teeth being pulled. "By the law of the court, so entered. The elder child's soul... may be released."

The Ledger turned its own pages. Somewhere deep beneath the cathedral, a vault door that had not opened in twelve years began, with a sound like a glacier clearing its throat, to unlock.

Petra's knees nearly went. She locked them. Later — later she would shake, later she would scream or laugh or both, later she would fall apart in her brother's arms on the far side of the sky. Not here. Not in front of the queen. Not while the last part remained, and the last part was hers to pay.

"But," the queen said, and the word dropped into the cathedral like a stone into a well, "the court's law requires balance. Release must be purchased. The gift freely offered stands as tender — the storm-gift, surrendered whole, its power poured into the court's reserves to feed what wakes." Her eyes lifted, and they were fully open now, and they were terrible, and they were almost — almost — respectful. "Will you pay, storm-caller? Knowing what it costs? Knowing what you become?"

Petra looked at her hands. At the scars her mother's iron had left there, the ones that ached before every storm, the ones that had defined every day of her exile, her fear, her hiding, her whole small gray life in a city without magic. The gift was the worst thing that had ever happened to her.

The gift was the only true thing about her.

"Yes," she said.

Aldric lunged forward — caught her arm — his grip already going translucent, his hour expiring, his eyes wild with everything he couldn't say. *Don't.* She covered his fading hand with her burning one.

"I spent twelve years learning how to live without magic," she told him softly. "Turns out I'm good at it."

Then she turned back to the queen, lifted her arms, and opened the storm.

It came out of her the way a river leaves a canyon — terribly, beautifully, tearing as it goes. Light poured up out of her chest and her scarred hands and the roots of her hair, silver-blue and roaring, twelve years of swallowed thunder given back to the dark all at once, and the cathedral filled with the sound of weather being born and dying in the same instant. The frozen petitioners' ice flashed white. The Ledger blazed. High overhead, through the cracked seals, the waking gods turned toward the light like flowers toward a sun —

— and Petra Jonah Whitlock stood in the center of it, emptying, becoming ordinary, becoming free, and did not look away even once.

When it was done, she was on her knees on cold stone, and her hands were just hands.

And the vault was open. And something small and bright and unmistakably *alive* came up out of the dark of it, streaked across the cathedral like a swallow, and struck her brother in the chest —

— and Aldric cried out, a real cry, a *boy's* cry, twelve years late, and fell forward into her arms as the last of his borrowed hour burned away and the wolf-shape took him back, whole now, soul and skin together, his fur bristling with returned life.

He pressed his forehead against hers. Wolf-eyes, human eyes, her brother's eyes.

*Run,* they said. *Now. While it's falling.*

Because it was falling. The queen stood motionless on her throne-step, watching the last of her reserves pour away into the waking dark, and around her the cathedral was coming apart — ice sublimating into screaming light, petitioners stirring, the seals above splitting wide as the thirteenth moon reached its fullness and the gods came up out of their long sleep like storms off a black sea.

Petra got her arms around her brother's neck. He was strong — stronger than any wolf had a right to be, soul-fed and furious and alive — and he took her weight without effort, and they ran.

Behind them, the Queen of Quiet Things spoke her last words in that hall, and Petra carried them out through the Gate of Teeth like a stone in her shoe:

"*Nothing is forgiven. Nothing is forgotten. Ledgers close, little storm-caller. They do not burn.*"

The mountain shook. The gate loomed ahead, its teeth grinding, its toll-keeper gone, its laws dissolving even as they passed through — and beyond it, impossibly far below and impossibly near, the first of the freed petitioners came stumbling up out of the dark, blinking in the moonlight, ten thousand souls released into a world that had forgotten how to hold them.

Aldric ran faster.

Above them, through the splitting roof of the world, the gods were rising — and one of them, vast and slow and crowned in static, turned its attention downward, toward the two small fleeing shapes of a mortal woman and a wolf, and began, with the patience of weather, to follow.
