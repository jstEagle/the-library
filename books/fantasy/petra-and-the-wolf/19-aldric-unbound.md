## 19. Aldric Unbound

*Petra*

The storm-gift had a shape. She had never thought of it that way before — she had carried it so long it had become the shape of her hands, her breath, the static hum beneath her skin on dry days. But standing before the Queen of Quiet Things with the contract unrolled between them and the mountain groaning around them like a ship in a gale, Petra saw it clearly for the first time: a knot of silver light coiled behind her sternum, wound tight as a charm-knot, humming the note of distant thunder.

The queen wanted it.

And in exchange, the ledger would burn one line. *One soul, the elder child.* Aldric's line. His name written in ink that looked like frozen water, twelve years of his life held in a column of figures.

"Freely given," the queen said again. Her voice was the sound of snow settling on snow. "That is the law of the court. Nothing taken. Everything traded."

Around them, the cathedral of frozen petitioners was beginning to thaw. Ice cracked along the walls in long white veins. Somewhere far above, the thirteenth moon was completing its turn, and the seal beneath Mount Cael was failing, and every prayer ever answered and never released was starting to remember it had a mouth.

Petra looked at Aldric.

He stood at her shoulder in the half-shape the court permitted him here — not wolf, not quite the boy from the frozen lake, but something between, grey-eyed and grey-haired, wearing a face she had spent twelve years forgetting and could never forget again. He shook his head. Slowly. Once.

"Don't," he said. It came out rough, a voice unused to being used. "Petra. Don't."

"You'd do it for me," she said.

"That's not the same."

"It's exactly the same." She turned back to the queen. The storm in her chest beat against its cage like a bird against a window, and for the first time in her life she understood that it was afraid. "I'll give it. Freely. But I want it witnessed. All of it. Every clause read aloud."

The queen's smile was patient and old and terrible. "You have been reading our law very carefully, little storm-caller."

"I had a good teacher." A dead smuggler with a ledger habit. A mother who kept accounts. "Read it aloud."

The queen raised one hand, and the contract rose with it, unfolding into the air like a moth made of paper and frost. And the court began to speak the terms — not the queen alone, but the walls themselves, the frozen petitioners mouthing the words in silent chorus, the whole cathedral reciting the debt of the Whitlock bloodline in a voice like an avalanche deciding where to fall.

Petra closed her eyes. She found the knot behind her sternum.

She had spent twelve years strangling this thing. Charming luck-knots with a fraction of its power and hating herself for the relief of it. Flinching from rain. Wearing gloves in summer because her scars ached when storms gathered over Greyhollow's harbor. She had called it a curse, then a burden, then — in the dark hours, in the honest hours — the only part of her mother she had ever loved.

It was none of those things. It was hers. And she was about to give it away.

"Petra." Aldric's hand on her arm. Cold through her sleeve. "There has to be another way. You said there was always another way. You're the one who taught me that."

"The other way is the gamble." Her voice didn't shake. That surprised her. "And the gamble needs both of us alive to see if it worked. This is the sure thing, Aldric. Take the sure thing."

"There are no sure things under a mountain full of waking gods—"

"Aldric." She opened her eyes and looked at him, really looked, and let him see everything she had been carrying since the market square, since the wolf at her threshold, since the ledger with its terrible neat handwriting. "Twelve years ago Mom sold you so I could live. I have been paying off that debt my whole exile without even knowing it. Let me finish the payment. Let me be the one who pays, just once. Please."

He stared at her. And in his face she saw the boy on the lake ice and the wolf at her door and something older than either — her brother, all the way down, hers, and she was his, and no court beneath any mountain could put a price on that no matter how neatly they wrote their ledgers.

"If it hurts," he said finally, "you scream. You don't be brave about it. You scream."

A laugh broke out of her, wet and startled. "Deal."

She reached into her own chest.

Not with her hands. With the gift itself — the way she had once reached into clouds, into the static charge of coming weather, and asked it to move. She found the knot of silver light and she unwound it, loop by loop, the way she had taught herself to undo charm-knots in a market stall in a city that hated what she was.

It did not want to go gently.

* * *

*Aldric*

He had forgotten what screaming sounded like when it came from someone who loved you.

The wolves he had run with for twelve years screamed too, sometimes — caught in traps, dying under winter — but it was a simple sound, an animal sound. What tore out of Petra now was nothing simple. It was the sound of a person being unmade in one particular place, the place that made her most herself, and the court drank it in with relish, the walls swelling with light, the frozen petitioners turning their thawing faces toward her like flowers toward a sun.

Her storm-gift left her the way a river leaves a valley in flood.

Silver light poured from her mouth, her eyes, the scars on her hands — the scars he remembered her getting, aged nine, when she'd called lightning down onto the lake ice to show him how it was done and it had come too close and kissed her knuckles. The light rose through the cathedral in a slow spiral, and everywhere it passed, ice shattered. Petitioners gasped awake mid-scream. The pillars of the Undercourt, carved from compressed centuries of unanswered prayer, split from crown to root.

And Aldric felt himself come back.

It started in his hands. He watched them change — the grey fur receding, the knuckles rising, the nails going blunt and human and dirty. Then his arms, his chest, breath flooding into lungs that had forgotten they were anything but decoration. His heart slammed once, twice, and on the third beat it hurt so much he doubled over, because a heart that beats is a heart that can break, and twelve years of not-feeling arrived all at once like a wave hitting a seawall.

He hit the floor on his knees. Human knees. They hurt. Everything hurt. It was the best pain he had ever felt.

"Petra—" His voice. His real voice, cracked and rusted and *his*. He crawled toward her through the chaos, through the shrieking of waking souls and the grinding of the mountain's bones. "Petra, look at me—"

She was on her back on the black glass floor, and she was smiling.

That was the worst of it. That was the image he knew he would carry for the rest of his mortal life: his sister lying in the ruins of the Court Beneath the Mountain, drained pale as the moon, smiling up at the spiral of her own stolen storm as it climbed toward the cracking seal above.

"Told you," she whispered. "Sure thing."

"You absolute liar," he said, and gathered her up.

She weighed almost nothing. When had she gotten so light? He stood, legs shaking, remembering how legs worked, and looked around the collapsing cathedral for the first time with living eyes.

The court was drowning.

Not in water — in people. The frozen petitioners were awake now, thousands of them, tens of thousands, peeling free of the walls where they had hung like preserved fruit, staggering, weeping, laughing, screaming prayers that had already been answered and wanting them *taken back*. An old man clutched a jar of returned years and didn't know where to put them down. A woman cradled a resurrection that smelled of grave-dirt and wouldn't stop crying. Everywhere, the machinery of divine power — the great wheels and channels where harvested prayer had flowed upward to keep the gods asleep — was seizing, jamming, running backward.

And through it all, the queen still sat upon her throne of quiet, watching Petra with those ancient patient eyes, and Aldric understood with a lurch that the trade had been honored. The ledger line was burning. He could feel it — or rather, he could feel the absence of it, a weight gone from his soul that he'd carried so long he'd mistaken it for his spine.

"Go," the queen said. Not to him. To Petra, in his arms. "The gate will hold open for as long as the seal is breaking. After that, the mountain closes, and everything inside it belongs to what wakes."

"What wakes?" Aldric demanded.

The queen's gaze slid to him, and for one moment he saw past the patience to something underneath — something vast and tired and, impossibly, frightened. "What your mother fed," she said. "Run, children. Run while you are only mortal. Mortal things are beneath their notice."

Then the throne room tilted, and the floor became a slope, and the exodus began.

* * *

*Petra*

She drifted in and out. Later she would piece it together from fragments, the way you piece together a dream:

Aldric carrying her through corridors that had been halls of judgment and were now rivers of fleeing souls. His arms around her, human arms, trembling with the effort of a body hours old. His voice in her ear, counting her breaths, the way she used to count his when he was small and feverish and she was the only one who could sit with him through the night.

The Hollow Priest at the Gate of Teeth, holding out his hand for a toll of memory — and Aldric snarling at him, actually snarling, wolf-sound from a human throat, until the priest looked at the burning contract still spiraling above their heads and stepped aside. *Paid in full,* the priest said. *Paid beyond measure.*

The gate itself, passing between teeth the size of ships' prows, cold air pouring down from somewhere above, real air, mountain air, night air.

And through all of it, the silence in her chest.

She kept reaching for the storm the way a tongue returns to a missing tooth. Twelve years of exile, and she had always known the power was there, caged, waiting — her secret, her shame, her inheritance. Now the cage was empty. Now there was just Petra Jonah Whitlock, a woman in a smuggler's coat, being carried up a mountainside by her brother, and when thunder rolled distantly over the peaks she felt nothing at all.

She should have wept. She found, instead, that she was laughing.

"Stop that," Aldric panted. "You'll rattle us off the path."

"Can't help it." Her voice was a rasp. "Do you know — how long it's been — since I heard thunder and it wasn't *mine*? Since it was just — weather?" Another laugh, giddy and broken. "It's just weather, Aldric. It's just the sky."

He stopped. They were on a ledge below the gate, the mountain shuddering behind them, red light pulsing from its broken crown like a heartbeat. Below, far below, the world spread out black and starlit, and rising from the horizon in three directions — south, east, west — were shapes that were not clouds.

They were too vast for clouds. They moved with intention. Where they passed, the stars went out, and the land beneath them lit faintly gold, like fields under a harvest moon, like altars catching fire.

The gods. Awake. Hungry. Loose.

Aldric set her down carefully against a boulder and crouched beside her, and for a moment neither of them spoke. His hand found hers. Her scarred, empty, ordinary hand.

"We're free," he said. It sounded like a question.

"We're free," she said. It sounded like a promise.

Behind them, deep in the mountain, something immense turned over in its sleep and began, slowly, to wake — and far below in the valley, a single lamp was climbing the switchback road toward them, small and stubborn and human, and Petra squinted against the dark and realized she knew that stride, that posture, that grim relentless pace.

"Aldric," she said. "Is that who I think it is?"

The Warden's lantern swung higher up the trail. And the mountain behind them opened its eye.
