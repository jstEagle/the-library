## 20. The Gods Wake Hungry

Ilse Vess had spent twelve years learning how to run toward danger while everyone else ran away, and so it was almost funny — in the way a knife between the ribs is almost funny — that when the mountain finally began to die, she was the one running *away*.

The corridor behind them had stopped being a corridor. The frozen petitioners of the Undercourt were waking, and their waking was not gentle. It came up through the stone like a scream through water: first a shudder, then a groan, then the sound of ten thousand throats remembering they had throats. Ice that had held human shapes for decades — centuries, maybe; Vess could not begin to guess what the Court counted as time — cracked and sloughed and fell in glittering sheets, and beneath the ice the people moved. Stumbled. Wept. Some of them simply stood where their statues had stood, blinking at the cathedral dark as if the last prayer they'd whispered had been only moments ago.

"Keep moving," Petra said. Her voice was raw. Whatever she had done back there in the heart of the court — whatever bargain she had rewritten with her own blood and her brother's name — it had cost her something Vess could see even in the strobing light. The storm-caller walked like a woman whose spine had been replaced with someone else's.

Aldric carried her anyway. He had been a wolf for twelve years and a man for perhaps two hours total, and he moved like both at once — low, fluid, watchful — with his sister draped across his back as if she weighed nothing, as if carrying her were the one thing in all the collapsing world he knew how to do without thinking.

Vess brought up the rear, because someone had to, because she always did, and because she had not yet decided whether she was leaving this mountain at all.

* * *

They climbed. The Gate of Teeth had opened outward when the seal broke — the Hollow Priest's toll-booth now stood abandoned, its ledger of paid memories fluttering loose pages into the dark like black moths — and beyond it the smuggler's road switchbacked up through the mountain's throat. Every hundred paces the ground bucked. Dust fell in ropes from the ceiling. Once, an entire gallery of frozen petitioners calved off the wall ahead of them and shattered on the road, and the people inside the ice woke mid-fall, screaming, and landed, and did not get up, and Vess made herself look at every single one of them because she had promised herself, years ago, that she would never again look away from a body.

"You're limping," Aldric said. His voice was strange to hear — rough, underused, a boy's voice aged wrong. He had not put her down since the court flooded, though his arms must have been screaming.

"I'm old," Vess said. "It's different."

Petra laughed — a small, broken sound, half delirium. "She's lying. She's deciding something. I can tell. She gets quiet when she's deciding."

Vess said nothing. The mountain said enough for all of them. Somewhere far below, the Queen of Quiet Things was either dying or being born, and Vess had seen the queen's face in the last moments before the flight — had seen the hunger come into it, vast and patient and *new*, the hunger of something that had slept on a diet of harvested prayers for a thousand years and woken to find its pantry emptied by a girl with a ledger and a grudge.

The gods would be hungry too. That was the part no one was saying out loud. The seal hadn't just caged the Court Beneath the Mountain. It had caged everything older than the Court — the things people had prayed to before there were ledgers, before there were bargains, before worship became commerce. The thirteenth moon-cycle had been a lock, and Petra Whitlock had picked it, and whatever came through the lock now would not be bound by contract or toll or the polite machinery of answered prayer.

It would be bound by nothing at all.

* * *

They reached the surface near dawn, if dawn still meant anything.

The sky over Mount Cael was wrong in a way Vess had no vocabulary for. She had served twenty years in Greyhollow's Warden corps; she had seen storm-fronts, ash-falls, the green-black wall of a twister walking the plains. This was not weather. This was *presence*. Shapes were rising out of the earth itself — out of mountainsides, out of the sea beyond the eastern hills, out of the cracked plain where the village of answered prayers had drowned in its own granted rain — and they rose the way heat rises off summer stone, slow and shimmering and immense, and the eye kept insisting they were clouds until the mind caught up and understood that they were *faces*, or the memory of faces, or the idea of faces worn smooth by ten thousand years of being looked at.

One hung over the western ridge like a fogbank with eyes. One coiled around the peak of Cael itself, unwinding from the broken seal below like smoke from a snuffed candle, and where it passed, the stars went out politely, one by one, as if making room.

"They're not attacking," Petra murmured. Aldric had set her down against a boulder; she stood now, swaying, her scarred hands open at her sides. Vess noticed the hands were empty of lightning for the first time since the hillside. Empty of everything. Whatever the court had taken as payment, it had taken cleanly. "Look. They're just — looking."

"They're hungry," Aldric said quietly. "You can see it. Like wolves at a fence."

And Vess could see it, God help her. The shapes drifting over the wounded world were not wrathful. Wrath would have been simpler. They were *wanting* — wanting with the whole of their weather-vast bodies, wanting in the way of things that had been fed on schedule for millennia and had just discovered the schedule was dead. Below them, in the valleys, the waking petitioners of the Undercourt were streaming out of the mountain's mouth in their rotted funeral clothes, and the gods' attention bent toward them like light bending toward a mass, and Vess understood with cold clarity what the petitioners were, now.

Not people. Not anymore. *Leftovers.* Prayers that had been eaten and partially digested and never released. And the gods, waking, were looking at the leftovers the way a starving man looks at scraps.

"We have to warn them," Petra started forward, and Aldric caught her arm, and Vess stepped between them all and the mountain's mouth, because that was her job, that had always been her job, standing between the city and the thing coming out of the dark.

"No," Vess said. "You two are done. You hear me? You paid. Both of you, more than anyone should. You walk down that road to Greyhollow and you don't stop until you're behind walls, and you let me handle what comes next."

Petra stared at her. Even hollowed out, even mortal, the girl had a stare like a drawn blade. "Handle it *how*? You're one Warden with a crossbow and a bad knee."

"I'm one Warden," Vess said, "who is standing in front of a god right now, and it hasn't killed me yet. Do you know why?"

She didn't wait for an answer. She reached inside her coat, past the warrant-book, past the iron charm-cuffs she'd carried for fifteen years, and drew out the letter.

It was soft as cloth now, handled so often. A child's careful handwriting. *Dear Lady of the Deep Water, please make Mama stop crying. I will give you anything. I have three teeth that came out and a blue ribbon. Please.*

Mara's letter. Written six weeks before the fever took her. Carried by a Warden who had prayed at every shrine in Greyhollow and received, in twelve years, exactly nothing — because the gods were asleep, because the Court ate the prayers and gave back silence, because the machinery of heaven had been powered by stolen souls and her daughter's small desperate voice had gone into the dark and been *filed*.

Except now the machinery was broken. And the letter in Vess's hands was trembling, and the trembling was not hers.

Light was coming off the paper. Faint, deep-water green, the color of the bottom of a lake at dusk. And beneath the wind and the grinding of the dying mountain, Vess heard — very softly, very clearly, in a voice like current over stones —

*Received.*

Twelve years late. Twelve years, four months, eleven days late. Her daughter's prayer, finally, *received.*

Vess did not cry. She had used up all of that years ago, in a Warden barracks bathroom with the taps running. What she did instead was lift her head and look directly into the fog-shape hanging over the western ridge, into those vast slow eyes, and she spoke the way she spoke to criminals and superiors and anyone else who thought she could be moved.

"She asked you to make her mother stop crying. Did you do that part? Or do you only take delivery?"

The shape regarded her. The wind died. Somewhere below, a petitioner screamed, and the scream cut off as if filed.

Then the voice came again, and it was closer, and it was everywhere, and it said: *THE DEBT IS ACKNOWLEDGED. THE ACCOUNT REMAINS OPEN.*

"Good," Ilse Vess said, and folded her daughter's letter, and put it away, and felt something settle in her chest that she recognized, with grim surprise, as purpose. "Then we have business, you and I. All of you. Every hungry thing that just woke up on my world. Because I've spent twelve years watching what happens when prayers get answered by things that don't care about the people praying — I watched a village drown in granted rain, I watched a harvest come up teeth, I watched my own daughter die waiting on a receipt. So here's my offer, and it's the only one you're getting."

She took out her warrant-book. The little grey book, the one she'd written ten thousand names in, god-touched and charm-sellers and luck-knot hags, the book that had made her the terror of Greyhollow's magic quarter. She turned to a fresh page.

"You want to eat," she said. "Fine. But you'll learn to ask first. You'll learn what a *no* means. You'll deal with the living the way the living deal with each other — openly, or not at all. And the first lesson starts with me, because I'm the only fool standing here willing to teach it."

* * *

Later — much later, when the sun had actually risen and the world had failed to end and the two siblings were a speck on the smuggler's road heading south — Vess would sit on the broken threshold of the Gate of Teeth with her book open on her knees and try to remember what she had said next, and find that she couldn't. Memory had gone strange up there, stretched thin as the light. What she remembered was simpler:

She remembered that the god listened.

She remembered that it did not agree — not yet, not cleanly, nothing with these creatures was ever clean — but it *listened*, and listening, she suspected, was the first miracle the world had honestly witnessed in a thousand years.

And she remembered that when the second god came over the ridge, and the third, drawn by the scent of an open account, she did not reach for her crossbow. She reached for her pen.

* * *

The road south was long, and Vess watched it empty from the mountain's shoulder, and she was not sorry.

That was the thing she would never be able to explain to anyone, if she lived — and she suspected she might live; the gods seemed to find her useful, the way men find a mule useful, and mules outlast most riders. She was not sorry to stay. Greyhollow had given her a badge and taken a daughter, and neither trade had ever balanced. But this — sitting at the door of a broken heaven with a warrant-book full of blank pages and a child's unanswered letter warm against her heart — this was the first work in twelve years that felt like it might actually *count*.

Below her, the world was changing. She could see it spreading: the gods rising over the far countries like new weather, rain that might bless or might drown, harvests that might feed or might bite, and no one anywhere yet understanding that the rules had changed, that prayer was no longer a coin dropped into a machine but a word spoken aloud in a room where something hungry was listening.

Someone would have to teach them. Someone would have to stand in the doorway and say *ask first, mean it, accept the answer.*

She flexed her bad knee. She uncapped her pen.

On the road, tiny in the morning light, Petra Whitlock stopped once and turned back — Vess saw it, saw the girl raise one scarred hand in something that was not quite a wave and not quite a salute — and then the siblings walked on, mortal and memory-broken and free, and Vess lifted her hand in return and held it up until they were gone.

Then she turned back to the mountain, where the first petitioner had begun to climb toward her, blinking, weeping, asking in a voice rusted by decades of ice: *"Is it over? Is it finally over?"*

"No," Ilse Vess said, and offered the woman her hand. "But it's starting. And this time, somebody's taking notes."
