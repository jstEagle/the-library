## 21. Greyhollow After Rain

The road into Greyhollow smelled of wet stone and woodsmoke, and Petra stopped at the crest of the last hill just to breathe it.

Rain had come through the valley in the night — real rain, ordinary rain, the kind that fell because clouds were heavy and not because something old and hungry had been asked for it. It had washed the dust of the mountain road off the rooftops below, and the whole city gleamed in the grey morning light like a coin someone had polished and then thought better of spending. Greyhollow. Ugly, cramped, magic-free Greyhollow, with its crooked chimneys and its tannery stink and its Wardens walking their rounds in oilcloth cloaks.

She had spent twelve years thinking of it as a cage.

Now, with the storm-gift gone from her hands the way a tooth is gone from her jaw — she kept reaching for it, kept finding the absence — it looked more like a door left open behind her.

"You're doing it again," Aldric said.

He stood beside her in his human shape, pack over one shoulder, hair still carrying the mountain's cold. He could hold this form now for most of a day; the further they walked from the Undercourt, the longer he lasted, as though his soul remembered itself by inches. But at dusk, always, the wolf came back to him. He said it didn't hurt. She had learned not to believe everything her brother said.

"Doing what?"

"Looking at things like you're about to lose them." He shifted the pack. "We're not going back up there, Petra. The ledger's burned. The debt is divided so thin even the queen couldn't find a thread of it to pull."

"I know."

"You keep checking anyway."

"So do you," she said. "You count your fingers every morning."

He looked down at his hands, flexed them once, and had the grace to look caught. "They're new," he said. "A man counts his money when he's been robbed."

She laughed — actually laughed, out loud, on the open road where anyone might hear — and the sound startled a crow off the fence post beside them. Twelve years since she'd laughed like that. It felt like a small magic all its own, and no one came to collect it.

They walked down into the city together.

* * *

Greyhollow had changed while they were gone, though only someone who had lived under the ban would notice how.

The Wardens still patrolled, but Ilse Vess was gone — transferred, the crier said, or resigned, or dead; the rumors disagreed on everything except that Warden Vess had gone up Mount Cael with a witch and had not come back down. In her absence the god-touched arrests had sputtered and stalled. There seemed little point now. Half the city wore charms openly. A shrine to some nameless hearth-god had sprouted in Tanner's Row, built of river stones and candle stubs, and nobody had torn it down. Prayers drifted up from doorways at dawn like smoke from a hundred small fires.

And the gods answered. That was the thing Petra could not make herself say aloud: the gods answered, still, everywhere, loose and unbound and unregulated, and every answer was a little wrong. A fisherman prayed for calm seas and got a windless week and empty nets. A mother prayed for her fevered son and woke to find the fever gone and the boy's hair white as ash. The gods had been asleep for centuries, fed on harvested prayer, and now they woke hungry and half-remembering what prayers were *for*, granting wishes the way a drunk man gives directions — generously, confidently, and straight into the river.

So the city had done what cities do. It adapted. It learned to pray carefully, in small words, with conditions attached. It learned, slowly, the grammar of bargaining with something vast.

And in the middle of it all, in the same cracked flagstone square off Cooper's Lane where she had sold luck-knots to a grieving widow in another life, Petra Whitlock set up her stall again.

Only now she sold nothing but thread, buttons, ribbon, needles, and honest knots tied with no charm in them at all.

* * *

The first morning, business was slow.

The second morning, the widow Marta came — the same widow, older, her grief worn smooth now like a sea stone — and stood before the stall turning a frayed luck-knot over in her fingers.

"They stopped working," she said. Not accusing. Just tired. "After the moon broke. Everyone's charms went quiet. I kept yours anyway." She set it on the counter between them. "I don't know why I'm telling you this."

"Because I used to sell you lies," Petra said, "and you're too kind to say it plainly."

Marta's mouth twitched. "Are you saying your knots never held any luck?"

"I'm saying luck isn't a thing you can tie." Petra took the frayed knot, untied it slowly, and retied it — a plain knot, a strong one, the kind sailors use, the kind that holds because of tension and care and nothing else. "But a knot tied well is worth something. And company is worth more. Sit awhile, if you like. Business is slow."

Marta sat. She stayed two hours. When she left she bought three plain knots and paid full price, and told her neighbors, and the neighbors told theirs, and by the end of the month the stall off Cooper's Lane was known for two things: good thread, and a shopkeeper who would listen to you for as long as it took, and who never once offered to fix your life for a copper.

It was, Petra discovered, the busiest she had ever been.

* * *

Aldric worked the mornings at the tannery, hauling hides, earning wages like any man, and came to the stall at midday smelling of oak bark and salt. The neighborhood children adored him, which amused Petra endlessly, because the children also adored the enormous grey dog that sometimes slept across the threshold of their rooms at night, and none of them had yet put the two together.

"He knows," Aldric said one evening, nodding at Tam o' the cooperage, a boy of eight who was watching the wolf with the particular intensity of a child assembling a theory. "I saw him counting my fingers yesterday."

"Children count fingers?"

"This one does." Aldric scratched the wolf's ears absently — scratching his own ears, in a way, which still made Petra's chest ache in a way she hadn't found words for. "Let him figure it out. Kids need one mystery in this town that isn't going to eat anyone."

"That's the spirit of Greyhollow," Petra said dryly. "Manageable mysteries."

But she slept better with the wolf at the door. She'd stopped pretending otherwise around the second week.

The dreams had mostly gone. The frozen lake, the boy beneath the ice — those images had followed her for years, and now they came rarely, and when they came, the boy in the dream climbed *out*. He pulled himself through the black water onto the ice and lay there breathing, and she woke with her heart hammering but her face wet with something that wasn't terror.

Some nights she dreamed of her mother instead, and those were harder, because the Hollow Priest had taken her mother's face as toll at the Gate of Teeth, and in the dreams her mother was a warm blur at the end of a corridor, a voice without features, hands without a face above them. She knew she had loved her. She knew she had been betrayed by her. Both truths sat in her chest like stones in a riverbed, and the water moved over them, and neither one washed away.

"She did it to save you," Aldric said once, when she'd told him as much as she could bear to tell.

"She did it to save *you* from being me."

"Same thing, maybe, from where she stood."

"It wasn't fair."

"No," he agreed easily. "Nothing much is. But we're here, and the ledger's ash, and I get to be a wolf whenever I want, which is honestly better than most men can say." He grinned — that lopsided grin she was still relearning, twelve years newer than the one she remembered. "Stop auditing the past, Petra. You'll find errors, and you can't collect on them."

She threw a rag at his head. He caught it without looking. Some things, apparently, survived the Undercourt intact.

* * *

Autumn deepened. The rains settled in the way they did in Greyhollow, patient and grey, and the city pulled its shutters against the dark and lit its lamps early.

The gods were still loose. That was the other thing everyone had learned to live with, the way one lives with weather. Sometimes a god passed over the city at night — you could feel it, a pressure like a change coming, a taste of iron on the tongue — and in the morning there would be some small strangeness: every clock in a street running four minutes fast, or a well that gave sweet water for a day, or a patch of frost shaped like a handprint on a single window. The Wardens had stopped arresting people and started keeping lists. Somebody high up had understood, finally, that you cannot outlaw rain.

Petra watched it all from behind her counter with the wariness of a woman who had seen the machinery underneath. Every answered prayer, however gentle, made her think of the cathedral of frozen petitioners, the harvest-vaults, the ore-carts of longing wheeled through the dark. The gods were awake now and feeding themselves directly, and perhaps that was better, and perhaps it was worse, and mostly it was simply beyond her — and for the first time in her life, that was a relief rather than a failure. Her part in it was finished. She had signed nothing, owed nothing, carried nothing but an ordinary woman's share of the world's trouble.

Her hands, though. Her hands still ached in storms.

Not with power — that was gone, scoured out of her, the price paid and receipted. They ached the way an old break aches, a memory in the bone. When thunder rolled off the hills she would stand in the doorway of the shop with her scarred palms turned up to the sky, feeling nothing but cold rain, and she would stay there until the shower passed, letting the nothing soak into her skin. Grieving, Aldric called it. She preferred *remembering*. The distinction mattered to her, though she couldn't have said why.

On the first of Frostmonth, a girl came into the shop out of the rain — fourteen, maybe, sharp-faced, hood dripping, with the wary eyes of someone who has learned young that asking for things costs. She hovered by the ribbon bin and didn't buy anything, and didn't leave, and finally Petra set down her ledger and said, "Out with it, then."

The girl flinched. Then, in a rush: "They say you used to sell charms. Real ones. Before."

"I sold knots," Petra said. "Knots are all I've got."

"But—" The girl's hands twisted together. "My da's boat. The *Kindly Ann*. She went down off the point in the storm last week, and the gods—everyone prays to the gods now, everyone says they answer, but they answered wrong, they brought up the wrong bodies, they—" Her voice cracked and steadied with an effort that hurt to watch. "There's six families waiting on the dock every morning for their dead, and the sea won't give them up. And I thought — I heard you listened. I heard you didn't promise things you couldn't do."

Petra was quiet a moment. Rain ticked against the shutters.

"I can't raise the drowned," she said. "No one can. Not gods, not witches. If anyone tells you different, they're selling you something."

The girl's shoulders dropped.

"But I can walk down to that dock with you," Petra said, "and I can sit with the families, and I can write letters for them — proper ones, to the harbormaster, to the Wardens, to whoever needs writing to — and I can make sure somebody keeps asking after the *Kindly Ann* until the sea gets tired of saying no. That's not a charm. That's just work. It's the only kind I do now."

The girl stared at her. "That's it? That's all?"

"That's everything," Petra said. "Everything I've got left, anyway. It turns out it adds up."

The girl wiped her nose on her sleeve, nodded once, sharply, like sealing a bargain, and pushed back out into the rain. Petra watched her go, then reached for her coat and her oilcloth and called through to the back room:

"Aldric! Walk with me. There's work on the docks."

The wolf lifted his head from the hearth, yawned enormously, and shook himself into his human shape with the ease of long practice — though his eyes stayed amber a moment longer than they should have, and always did, and probably always would.

* * *

They spent the evening on the dock in the drizzle, six grieving families and a shopkeeper with a borrowed lantern and a wolf-man who carried crates and spoke gently to the old men about tides and salvage. Nothing magical happened. No god stooped from the clouds. The sea kept its dead, at least for now.

But the letters got written. The harbormaster promised a drag-net crew by week's end. The widow Osgood, who hadn't spoken since the funeral-that-wasn't, held Petra's hand hard enough to bruise and said thank you twice, and the second time she meant it.

Walking home through the wet streets near midnight, Aldric said, "You know the queen would laugh herself sick if she saw this. All that storm in you, traded away — for *paperwork*."

"The paperwork holds," Petra said.

"So did the storms."

"Not like this." She stopped under the eave of the chandler's shop and looked back the way they'd come: the lamplit dock, the huddled families, the dark patient water. "The storms held for a moment and cost everything. This—" she gestured at the small, stubborn lights along the shore, "—this holds because people keep holding it. That's better magic. Don't tell the Undercourt."

"Wouldn't dream of it."

They walked on. At the corner of Cooper's Lane the rain thinned to mist, and above the rooftops, through a tear in the clouds, the moon showed itself — waning now, ordinary, one moon among the stories men told about it, its thirteenth turn long completed and its debts long scattered into dust.

Petra looked up at it for a long moment. Somewhere far to the north, gods were waking like weather, and wars were probably brewing over who would kneel and who would pray, and Vess — wherever Vess was, alive or dead or something in between — was standing in front of something enormous with nothing but a dead daughter's letter and her own unbending spine. The world was remaking itself, and it would need people for that too, someday, and maybe she and Aldric would answer that call when it came.

But not tonight. Tonight there was a shop to open in the morning, thread to sort, a girl named Nettie whose father was lost, a brother who would be a wolf by the time she finished banking the fire.

Tonight there was only Greyhollow after rain, shining and ordinary and enough.

"Come on," she said, and shouldered the door open onto warmth and lamplight and the smell of home. "We're expected tomorrow."

Behind them, far off over the hills, thunder muttered once — soft, aimless, belonging to no one — and went quiet.
