## 22. Petra and the Wolf

The first frost of true winter came to Greyhollow on a Tuesday, silvering the gutters and stiffening the laundry lines until the sheets hung like sails of glass. Petra woke before dawn to the particular silence that meant snow was coming — a hush so complete it felt like the city holding its breath — and lay in her narrow bed above the charm stall, listening to her own heartbeat.

It was an ordinary heart. That still surprised her some mornings. She would press two fingers to her wrist, half expecting to feel the storm ticking there beneath her skin like a second pulse, the way it had for as long as she could remember. But there was nothing. Only blood, only warmth, only the slow animal machinery of a woman with no more magic in her than the bread on her shelf or the boots by her door.

She had expected grief. Everyone had told her to expect grief — Aldric, in his halting human voice; Vess, in the one letter she'd sent from the mountain passes before the roads closed; even the Hollow Priest, who had watched her walk out of the Gate of Teeth with something like pity in his eyeless face. *You will mourn it,* he'd said. *A gift taken is a wound that never quite closes.*

But what she felt, most mornings, was lighter than grief. Lighter than anything she'd known since childhood. There were moments — reaching for a high shelf, laughing at Aldric's terrible attempts to cook porridge, standing in the market square where a stranger's prayer had once been answered by something old and hungry — when she caught herself waiting for the tremor in her scarred hands, the hum of gathering thunder. And when it didn't come, instead of loss she felt a strange, clean emptiness, like a room after the furniture has been carried out and the windows opened at last.

*Inheritance is not destiny.* She'd said it to the Queen of Quiet Things with a storm breaking behind her teeth. Now she said it to herself each morning, quieter, like a prayer — except prayers didn't work anymore, not really, not the way they had when the gods were caged and desperate enough to answer anyone. Now a prayer was just words spoken into cold air, and cold air kept them, and that was all.

She got up, dressed in layers, and went down to open the shutters.

The wolf was already there.

He sat in the alley beside the stall, grey as the coming snow, patient as stone, his amber eyes catching the last of the starlight. He had been there when she woke every morning for three weeks now — ever since the first hard frost — and every morning she pretended to be surprised, and every morning he pretended not to notice the pretense.

"You could knock," she said, kneeling to scratch behind his ears. "We do have a door."

His tail thumped twice against the cobbles. His eyes said what his throat no longer could, most days: *the door is yours, and I am not sure yet which shape I am.*

That was the thing no one had warned them about — not the priest, not the queen, not the ledger of debts with its careful columns of souls and sorrows. When the court tore Aldric's soul free and gave it back to him, it came back *loose*. Twelve years of wolf-shape had worn grooves into him, deep ones, and some mornings he woke as a man and some mornings as a wolf, and sometimes — most times, lately — he chose. He chose the wolf the way another man might choose comfortable old boots: because they fit, because they asked nothing, because in that shape the memories came gentler. A man remembered being sold. A wolf remembered only running, and being followed home by someone who smelled like storms and childhood.

Petra understood. Her own memories had been paid away too — her mother's face, gone down the Hollow Priest's gullet like a coin into dark water. She knew what it was to reach for something essential and find smooth blankness where it used to be. So she never pushed. She set out two bowls each morning, one on the table and one on the floor, and let him choose.

Today he chose both — wolf for breakfast, then a long shudder and the crackle of changing bones, and then her brother stood up in the alley, tall and gaunt and wrapped in the spare coat she kept hanging on the stall's back hook for exactly this purpose.

"Snow," he said. His voice was better these days, less rusted, though he still spoke like a man rationing water. "Before noon. Heavy."

"You always say that now."

"I'm always right." He took the bowl of porridge she handed him and ate standing up, wolf-quick, wolf-hungry. "Vess's letter came?"

"Yesterday. The pass road's closed till spring." Petra folded the letter smaller and smaller between her fingers. "She says the negotiations are — 'ongoing.' That's Vess for *nobody's killed anybody yet*."

"That's Vess for *I've threatened to kill somebody and they're considering it*," Aldric said, and for a moment they grinned at each other across the steam of the porridge, two people who had learned the same joke in different cages.

Below the window, Greyhollow was waking. The Wardens' new patrol clattered past the end of the alley — a changed patrol, these days, carrying bread and coal notices instead of manacles, because Warden-Captain Ilse Vess had left very specific instructions before she rode into the mountains, and the Wardens of Greyhollow had discovered they preferred following instructions to arguing with her ghost. Somewhere a baker's cart rattled. Somewhere a child shrieked with laughter over nothing. Somewhere, faintly, a church bell rang the hour into air that no longer answered.

The gods were loose. That was the truth under everything now, the way bedrock lies under a city. They rose off Mount Cael like weather systems, vast and unbound and unfed, wandering the world's edges, and no one knew yet what they wanted, or whether they wanted anything at all. Vess had stayed behind to find out. Petra lit a candle for her every evening — not a prayer, just a flame, just a small stubborn human light — and told herself that if anyone could stare down a hungry god and make it blink, it was a woman who had already waited twelve years for an answer and planned to collect interest.

"Open the stall?" Aldric asked.

"Open the stall."

* * *

They sold honest trinkets now, and business had never been better.

That was the joke of it, the quiet cosmic joke that made Petra laugh sometimes at odd hours: in the years when her charms had been real — luck-knots actually knotted with fortune, fever-salves that genuinely drew heat, little woven charms against sorrow that worked exactly as promised — she had sold perhaps three a week, terrified, hiding her gift inside ordinary craft. Now that she had nothing but thread and beads and good intentions, the whole city beat a path to her door.

Because everyone in Greyhollow had seen the sky change. Everyone had felt the thirteenth moon hang fat and wrong over the rooftops for one impossible night before finally, mercifully, setting. Everyone knew someone who had prayed in those weeks when the seal cracked and the old voices answered — and been answered *wrong*, grotesquely, literally, like the village on the smuggler's road where the rain still fell and the harvest of teeth still gleamed in the fields. The city had learned what Petra had learned on that terrible afternoon: that divine attention is not love. That being heard by something enormous is not the same as being helped.

So they bought trinkets instead. Knots of red thread that meant *someone thought of you today*. Carved wooden wolves for doorposts. Little tin moons on strings, thirteenth-moon souvenirs that Petra hammered out herself and sold with a straight face and a private ache. People hung them in their windows and felt, wrongly but usefully, that they had some small purchase on fate. And maybe they did. Maybe luck had always been mostly the believing.

An old woman came at midmorning, the widow Marta whose luck-knot Petra had charmed a lifetime ago — a year ago, eleven months, though it felt like another age. Marta bought a wooden wolf without saying why, and pressed Petra's hand, and neither of them mentioned the night a grieving woman's prayer had worked and a storm-caller's hands had trembled. Some transactions don't need narrating.

A boy came wanting a sword. He got a wooden knife instead, and a lecture about the difference between wanting and needing that he clearly intended to ignore entirely. Good, Petra thought. Let him ignore things. Let him be young in a world where ignoring dangerous things might actually be possible again.

And once, near dusk, a stranger stopped at the stall — a traveler in road-dusted grey, face shadowed under a hood, who looked at the tin moons for a long time and then looked at Petra with eyes that held far too much weather.

"They speak well of you below," the stranger said quietly.

Petra's hands went still on the counter. Beside her, Aldric — wolf-shaped since noon — rose slowly to his feet, hackles lifting.

"The court is dissolved," Petra said. "The ledger burned. Whatever you are—"

"A petitioner." The hood tilted. Underneath, briefly, she saw something vast trying to wear a small face, and not quite managing. "Once. Long ago. We walked out with the rest, when your cleverness broke the doors." A pause. "Some of us are grateful. Some of us are merely awake. It is difficult, at present, to tell the difference — even among ourselves."

"What do you want?"

"Nothing." The word seemed to cost it effort, as if *wanting* were a language it was relearning. "Only to see the ones who did it. To see you selling tin moons in the snow." Something that was almost a smile crossed the almost-face. "It is a strange thing, freedom. We had forgotten it has such small furniture."

Then the traveler stepped back into the dusk, and the dusk accepted it, and the street was empty except for snow beginning to fall — the first real snow, fat flakes spinning down through the lamplight, settling on the awning, on the wooden wolves, on the wolf at Petra's side who watched the place where the stranger had stood for a long moment before shaking himself and leaning, deliberately, heavily, against her leg.

"It's starting," Aldric said, when he found his voice. "Them walking around. Being *out*."

"I know."

"Are you afraid?"

Petra considered it honestly, the way she tried to consider everything now that honesty was the only currency she had left. Snow gathered in her hair. Down the street, the lamplighter made his rounds, and a woman called a child in from the cold, and the world went on being ordinary and enormous at once.

"Sometimes," she said. "But I spent twelve years afraid of things I couldn't see. This is just afraid of things I can. That's practically luxury."

* * *

That night, Aldric chose the wolf.

She knew before he said anything — knew by the way he lingered at the stairs, by the restless way his human hands wouldn't settle, by the look he gave the door as if measuring it for something. On bad-memory nights he chose the wolf early. Tonight he held out until the lamps were down, and then she heard the soft crackle of change in the dark of the next room, and heavy paws padding to the doorway of her bedroom, and the great sigh of a wolf arranging himself across the threshold with his nose toward the window and his body squarely, deliberately, between her and the world.

She slept better this way. She'd stopped pretending otherwise. Partly it was simple — a wolf at the door hears everything, and Greyhollow's nights were full of new sounds lately, sounds the city was still learning to name. But partly it was older than sense. Their mother had sold one soul to save another, and the arithmetic of that had broken both children in different places, and somehow the repair had come out looking like this: a woman in a narrow bed, a wolf in a doorway, each keeping watch over the other's sleep. Family was what survived. Not bloodlines, not bargains, not the long ledgers of debt that mothers and queens and gods kept — just this. Just two scarred creatures choosing, night after night, to guard each other's breathing.

She woke once, near midnight, to moonlight.

The storm had passed through while she slept, dropping its snow and moving east, and now the sky was scoured clean and brilliant, and there in the western window hung the moon — waning now, ordinary, *thirteenth* no longer, just the moon, doing its slow lawful work. But for a moment, half-asleep, she saw it as it had been that final night above the mountain: swollen with stolen centuries, cracking along its face like an egg, pouring out the light of everything it had eaten.

Beside the door, the wolf lifted his head. He was watching it too.

For a while neither of them moved. The moon slid westward, patient, mortal-bound, keeping its new-old schedule. Somewhere below, snowmelt ticked in the gutters. Somewhere across the city a baby cried and was hushed, and no god answered, and that was right — that was how it should be, the small cries answered by small arms, the great silences left to hold their own peace.

*Aldric,* she thought, not speaking, knowing he'd hear the shape of it anyway. *Do you regret it? Any of it?*

The wolf turned his head. In the moonlight his eyes were amber and calm and unmistakably her brother's, and they held everything he couldn't say and everything he could: the frozen lake, the drowning years, the hour at the shrine when he'd forgiven her and nearly broken her with it. Then he stood, stretched until his spine curved like a drawn bow, and padded to the window to sit beside her — wolf and woman, shoulder to shoulder, watching the thirteenth moon go down over the rooftops of Greyhollow for what they both silently understood was the last time either of them needed to count.

It touched the horizon. It hesitated, the way endings do. Then it set, and the sky went on without it, and the first pale edge of morning came up in the east like a page turning.

"Come on," Petra said aloud, her breath fogging the glass, her voice rough with sleep and something warmer. "The stall won't open itself. And I hear it's going to snow."

Behind her, in the grey-gold light, the wolf laughed — a huffing, tail-thumping, unmistakable laugh — and went to wake her brother, so they could begin the day.
