## 1. The Ledger of Small Debts

The trick to being invisible in Corvane was not to hide. Hiding drew the eye the way a drawn curtain draws a hand. The trick was to be *useful* in the smallest possible way—so small that no one thought to look twice at the source of their comfort.

Freya Voss had been practicing smallness for fifteen years.

They set up the stall at dusk, as they always did, in the crook of the night market where Tanner's Row bent around the old cistern house—a bend so slight that most shoppers passed it without noticing there was a bend at all. Perfect. The canvas awning went up first, patched in three places with thread that matched nothing, because matching thread invited comment and comment invited memory. Then the tray: forty-one charms laid out on faded velvet like a jeweler's poor cousin.

Luck-knots woven from red string, three coppers. Damp-warding sigils for the fishmongers' cellars, five. A charm against nightmares—technically a sleep-smoothing charm, because *nightmare* implied dreams worth having, and the Temple frowned on anything that promised too much—seven coppers, and worth every one if the buyer never learned how well it worked.

None of it was real magic. That was what Freya told the market wardens when they drifted by on their rounds, lanterns swinging, faces bored. *Charms, honored ones. Knots and ink and superstition. Nothing that bends.* And it was true, in the way a half-truth is true: the knots were only knots, the ink only ink. What bent reality was the breath behind them, the fifteen years of practiced restraint that let Freya press intention into an object without ever letting the intention show its teeth.

A real mage could set a ward that would stop a knife. Freya's damp-sigil just made the cellar walls forget how to sweat. A real mage could call fire. Freya's hearth-charm made a dying coal remember, briefly, what warmth felt like. Small things. Harmless things. Things the Temple of the Veiled Word had decreed, in its great mercy, beneath prosecution.

Mostly.

"Voss." Old Tam the tanner leaned over the tray, smelling of lime and hides as always. "You've got the nightmare ones back?"

"Never ran out." Freya slid one across the velvet. "Your granddaughter still sleeping?"

"Like the dead. Better than the dead—the dead don't drool." He paid with a copper worn smooth as a river stone. "You're a good sort, Voss."

*I'm a criminal,* Freya thought, smiling. *Just a very careful one.*

"Evening to you, Tam."

The market filled the way water fills a basin: gradually, then all at once. Lamplighters made their rounds. The spice sellers opened their shutters and the whole row went gold with the smell of cumin and pepper-wine. Somewhere a fiddler tuned, badly, and someone shouted at him to tune it quieter. Freya sold a luck-knot to a young wife whose husband sailed the lake barges; sold a traveler's-blessing to a carter who clearly couldn't afford it and took two coppers instead of four without saying why; sold nothing at all for a long stretch and watched the crowd move past the stall like a river moves past a stone.

That was the other half of invisibility: you got to watch everyone, and no one watched you back. Fifteen years of watching. Freya knew which of the fishwives beat her daughters and which pretended to; knew the lamplighter on Tanner's Row was saving for his mother's funeral; knew the temple acolyte who came through every third evening buying luck-knots he never used, because he liked to talk to someone who wasn't afraid of him. Freya knew the whole market the way a wall knows the people who lean against it.

And the market knew Freya the way people know a wall. Fondly. Without looking.

"You'll want the tally," said a voice at the edge of the stall, and Freya's stomach performed its nightly ritual of dropping through the floorboards.

Sister Eluned stood in the lamplight in her grey habit, ledger under one arm, face composed into the particular gentleness the Temple wore like armor. She was not unkind. That was the worst part. Cruel collectors could be hated; Sister Eluned could only be endured.

"Of course." Freya lifted the counting board from under the counter and turned it so she could see. "Forty-one charms sold this week at an average of—" they did the arithmetic aloud, because arithmetic was safe, arithmetic was honest work "—four coppers. Call it thirteen silvers, less the tithe of one part in ten, less the license fee of two silvers per quarter-day. Which leaves—"

"Eleven silvers, four coppers," Sister Eluned said, before Freya finished. She always finished first. "Against a principal of nine thousand marks."

Nine thousand marks. Fifteen years of selling charms at four coppers apiece, and the number hadn't moved by so much as a breath. It wasn't meant to. Freya had done the actual sums once, alone, at midnight, and worked out that at current earnings the debt would be paid off in roughly eleven hundred years, assuming they lived that long and never ate.

"And the interest?" Freya asked, because they asked every week, because hope was a habit even when it was a wound.

"Compounded at the feast of the Veiled Word, as always." Sister Eluned wrote the figure in her ledger with a neat, dry scratch of the pen. "You're current, though. I'll note that. Current is... respectable."

Current. Not *good*. Not *safe*. Just current—one missed payment away from the Temple's attention, and everyone in the market district knew what the Temple's attention looked like when it settled on a debtor. There had been a glassblower on Cooper's Lane, once, who fell three weeks behind. They'd bound him as a ward-tapper, siphoning his gift into the border wards until there was nothing left of him but a man-shaped tiredness who wept at bright colors. He'd died within the year. Or stopped living; the distinction was theological.

Freya had been eight years old when the Temple bought their debt from the traveling company that had bought them from the parents who couldn't feed them. Eight years old, and already showing the gift—little things, sparks in the dark, a broken cup knitting itself when they cried over it—and the Temple's assessors had priced that gift the way merchants price cloth. Nine thousand marks. A fortune for a child. A fortune for anyone.

*We own your magic until it's paid for,* the assessor had said, kind as Sister Eluned, kind as everyone in Corvane was kind right up until the moment kindness became expensive. *Practice nothing above a charm. Practice nothing that shows.*

So Freya had spent fifteen years practicing nothing that showed.

"Voss?" Sister Eluned was looking at them with something almost like concern. "You went somewhere."

"Just counting my fortune," Freya said. "It takes a while."

The sister's mouth twitched—not quite a smile; sisters of the Veiled Word weren't issued smiles—but close. "Keep current, Voss. In times like these, current is worth more than you know."

"In times like these," Freya repeated. "What times are these?"

And here was the thing Freya would turn over later, later, when everything had gone to ruin and starlight: Sister Eluned hesitated. A collector of fifteen years' standing, a woman who had recited interest rates through plague rumors and bread riots and the death of two High Cantors, and she *hesitated*, glancing once over her shoulder at the darkening sky as if checking whether it was still where she'd left it.

"The wards along the border," she said finally. "There have been... reports. Fluctuations. The Temple is attending to it." She closed her ledger. "Pay no mind. Good evening, Voss."

She moved off down the row, grey habit swallowed by the crowd, and Freya sat with the counting board in their lap and a cold finger drawing slowly up their spine.

Fluctuations. The border wards had held for six hundred years. They were the reason Corvane existed, the reason the lowlands didn't flood with wild magic, the reason the stars—so the priests said—kept their appointed rounds. The wards were the Temple's proudest work and its favorite sermon topic. *As the wards hold the dark without, so does doctrine hold the dark within.* You could buy the sermon printed on a broadsheet for a copper.

Fluctuations.

"Charm against bad news?" said a voice, and Freya startled badly enough to knock two luck-knots off the tray. The acolyte—the one who came every third evening—caught them before they hit the ground, which was more grace than the Temple generally taught. He was young, round-faced, with the permanent apologetic look of a boy who'd joined a choir and found out choirs had politics. "Sorry. Didn't mean to sneak."

"You didn't sneak. I was elsewhere." Freya reset the tray. "Bad news isn't for sale tonight. Bad news is free and everywhere, like weather."

"Then I'll take a luck-knot. For my mother." He counted out coppers with the care of someone whose allowance was audited. "How is business?"

"Thriving. I'm nearly rich. Another thousand years and I'll consider retiring." Freya tied the knot off with a flourish that was pure street performance and not one drop of craft, and handed it over. "There. Luck for your mother, and none of it illegal."

He laughed, then glanced around, then lowered his voice. "Have you heard the choir lately?"

"I hear it from here, some evenings. Carries down from the temple mount when the wind's right."

"It's changed." He said it quickly, guiltily, the way people say things they've been told not to say. "The hymns. The old verses—they've been scraped off the boards. Replaced with new words nobody knows. Cantor Ovelith's own composition, they say. We're not to sing the old ones anymore at all." He turned the luck-knot over in his fingers. "My grandmother taught me the old verses when I was small. Before I was accepted. She said her mother sang them, and hers. Six hundred years, same words. And now—" He stopped himself, visibly, the way a man stops himself at the top of a stair he suddenly realizes is rotten. "Forgive me. I'm chattering. Forget I said it."

"Forget what?" Freya said, obligingly.

He smiled, thanked them, and melted into the crowd, and Freya watched him go and thought about scraped boards and fluctuating wards and Sister Eluned checking the sky, and decided—with the particular discipline of a person whose survival depended on not thinking about things—that it was none of their business. Wards were the Temple's problem. Hymns were the Temple's problem. Freya's problems were eleven silvers, four coppers, and a principal of nine thousand marks, and those problems were perfectly manageable if managed forever.

The evening deepened. The fiddler gave up tuning and started playing, which was worse. Freya sold a damp-sigil, a hearth-charm, another luck-knot to a gambler who bought one every single week with the faith of a true believer and the results of a true fool. The moon rose over the temple mount, fat and white, and below it the great dome of the Veiled Word caught the light like a second, colder moon.

And Freya tallied.

This was the private ritual, the one no collector saw. Under the counter, in a cheap notebook with a cracked spine, Freya kept a second ledger. Not debts owed *to* Freya—there were hardly any of those—but debts owed *by*. Tam's granddaughter's nightmare-charm, given free, because children shouldn't pay for sleep. The carter's two-copper blessing, the difference quietly forgiven. The bowl of stew Maren the landlady left on the stairs every seventh day, "because it was going to waste," though it never was. Fifteen years of small kindnesses received, entered in a careful hand, each one marked *unpaid*.

The page was very long. The page was the realest thing Freya owned.

Because here was the secret, the one Freya kept even from themselves most days: the Temple thought it owned Freya's magic, and maybe it did, paper and seal and compounded interest. But the ledger under the counter recorded a different economy entirely, one the Temple had no instrument to measure. Every free charm, every forgiven copper, every bowl of stew was a thread, and Freya had spent fifteen years telling themselves the threads were nothing, were sentiment, were weakness—

—and had spent those same fifteen years tying them anyway, compulsively, the way a drowning man ties knots.

"Somebody's thinking hard," said Maren herself, appearing at the stall with a basket on her hip and flour on her forearms. She was sixty, built like a doorframe, with the eyes of a woman who had raised four children and buried two husbands and regretted neither expenditure. "You've got your merchant face on. The one that says *everything's fine* while your hands shake."

"My hands don't shake."

"They do when you count." She set the basket down. "Bread ends and a heel of cheese. Don't argue, it was going stale."

"It was not."

"No, it wasn't. Eat it anyway." She looked around the stall with proprietary disapproval. "Business good?"

"Thriving. Eleven hundred years to solvency."

"Mm." Maren picked up a luck-knot, examined it, put it down. She'd never bought one. Freya had never offered. Between them lay an unspoken treaty: Maren did not ask what the charms really were, and Freya did not ask why a landlady with no obligation to a tenant kept leaving food on the stairs of a room rented at cost. "Temple woman came by earlier. Grey habit. I saw her checking the sky after she left. Like a hen expecting a hawk."

"People do look at the sky, Maren. It's traditional."

"Not Eluned. Fifteen years I've watched that woman walk this market and she looks at ledgers, not clouds." Maren fixed Freya with the look that had ended a hundred arguments before they began. "Something's coming. I don't know what, and I don't much care, but when it comes, you'll be up those stairs and behind my door before it finishes arriving. Yes?"

"Yes," Freya said, because arguing with Maren was like arguing with weather.

Maren nodded once, satisfied, took up her basket, and paused. "The charms you sell," she said, not turning around. "The nightmare one. For Tam's granddaughter. Does it work?"

Freya weighed the question the way they'd weighed it a thousand times. "It works," they said.

"Good." And she was gone into the crowd, a doorframe of a woman, solid and unhurried, and Freya sat in the lamplight holding a heel of cheese and feeling the dangerous, terrifying thing they felt most evenings and admitted to no one, least of all themselves:

Contentment.

Not happiness—happiness required a future, and Freya's future was a column of figures ending in nine thousand marks. But contentment. The market noise, the fiddler's crimes, the smell of cumin, the weight of the little ledger full of unpaid kindnesses. Fifteen years of smallness, and somewhere in the smallness Freya had built a life, or the shape of one, the way water builds a channel: patiently, invisibly, and entirely by flowing around every obstacle instead of through it.

*It's enough,* they told themselves, packing the tray as the market thinned. *It has to be enough. It's all there is.*

They doused the lamp. They climbed the stairs to the room above Maren's shop, ate the bread and cheese standing up, and sat at the window with the second ledger open on their knees, adding the day's entries in the last of the moonlight. Below, the city settled into its nighttime murmurs. Above, the stars wheeled in their appointed rounds, six hundred years unbroken, steady as doctrine.

Freya looked up at them while the ink dried, and felt—for no reason they could name, no reason except a grey habit checking the sky and a boy talking about scraped hymnals—a flicker of the old fear, the eight-year-old fear, the fear that had never really slept:

*One day they'll notice. One day the smallness won't be small enough.*

Overhead, very faintly, very briefly, a star flickered.

Like a lamp deciding whether to stay lit.

Freya stared at it until it steadied, told themselves they'd imagined it, closed the ledger, and did not sleep.

* * *

In the morning, the market woke to the sound of temple bells ringing at the wrong hour, and Sister Eluned did not come for the tally, and far away along the northern horizon, where the border wards had held the dark for six hundred years, the sky began—very quietly, like a page being erased—to go out.
