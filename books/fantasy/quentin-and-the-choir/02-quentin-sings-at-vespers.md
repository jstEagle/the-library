## 2. Quentin Sings at Vespers

The upper choir of the Temple of the Veiled Word was built to hold a hundred voices, and it held one the way an empty house holds a cough.

Quentin felt the absence every time he climbed the spiral stair at dusk—felt it in the way his footsteps echoed off the vaulted stone, in the rows of empty stalls with their worn kneelers, each one carved with a name of a boy who had once sung there and now sang nowhere. The acoustics had been tuned by hands long dead for a chorus, not a soloist. When he opened his mouth, the room took his note and multiplied it, gave it back to him from every direction at once, so that singing alone felt less like music and more like being surrounded by ghosts who knew all his parts.

He had learned, over eleven years, to love them anyway.

"From the top," said Cantor's Aide Pellin, from the floor below, where he sat with his tuning fork and his ledger of corrections. "And mind your breath on the third phrase. You're rushing toward the *Gloria* like it's supper."

Quentin grinned down at him. Pellin was sixty, dry as old bread, and had been correcting Quentin's breath since Quentin was six years old and could barely see over the stall rail. He was the closest thing to family the temple had left him.

The vesper hymn was the *Canticle of Returning Light*, the same hymn sung at dusk in Corvane for as long as there had been a Corvane. It was the first thing Quentin had ever memorized, before he could read, before he understood that the words meant anything. He set his feet, let the room gather around him, and began.

*"O lights above the border-stone,*
*keep faith with us who walk alone—"*

His voice went up into the vaults and came back changed, warmer, larger than he was. That was the trick of the upper choir, the thing no other room in the city could do: it didn't just carry a voice, it *believed* it. Down in the streets, Quentin was a small boy with ink-stained cuffs and a cowlick the barber never quite tamed. Up here, he was a bell.

*"—and when our watching eyes grow dim,*
*sing down, sing down, remember him."*

He held the final note until it thinned to nothing, until the stone itself seemed to be humming it after he'd stopped, and then he let it go and stood in the ringing silence, chest heaving, heart full.

"Better," Pellin called up. "Much better. Again."

Quentin laughed. "You'll wear me out before the Cantor even hears it."

"The Cantor hears everything." Something flickered across Pellin's face—there and gone, quick as a moth—and he looked back down at his ledger. "Again. From the top."

Quentin sang it again. And again. Four times through, while the light through the eastern windows went from gold to copper to a bruised purple, and each time Pellin found something—a vowel too open, a phrase too sweet—and each time Quentin fixed it and sang again, because this was what he was for. Eleven years ago the temple had bought his voice from his parents for three silver marks and a promise of education, which was how the temple bought everything in those days: legally, gently, with paperwork. His parents had wept at the gate. He remembered being confused by that. He wasn't confused anymore.

He was seventeen now, and he was the last.

There had been nine boys in the choir when he arrived. Then five. Then two. The others' voices broke, as voices do, and were moved down to the lay congregation or out into apprenticeships, and new boys were brought in—but for six years now, no new boys. The recruitment had simply stopped. Master Verel, the old choirmaster, had petitioned the vestry twice; both petitions had come back unopened. When Verel died two winters past, no replacement was named. Pellin kept the ledger now, and ran rehearsals, and signed the supply requisitions, and waited, in the way of men who have served an institution long enough to know that institutions do not explain themselves.

Which left Quentin. The last unbroken voice in the Temple of the Veiled Word. The last throat in Corvane trained to feed the gods their supper.

That was how Verel had put it, once, wine-flushed and sentimental, on a night Quentin was never supposed to remember: *Every hymn is a meal, boy. Every true voice is a hand that carries it. The gods don't eat praise. They eat attention.* Quentin had been twelve. He'd thought it was a metaphor.

He thought about it more often lately.

* * *

The High Cantor arrived during the fifth repetition.

Quentin heard him before he saw him—not footsteps, exactly. Ovelith moved through the temple the way cold moves through a house: you noticed the change in the air first. The candles along the gallery guttered in sequence, left to right, though there was no draft. The two novices stationed at the stair bowed so low and so fast they nearly collided.

"There," said a voice from the gallery above the choir stalls, warm as honey poured over something sharp. "Don't stop, child. I've been listening from the cloister. You've grown into that upper register beautifully."

Quentin did not stop—he would sooner have stopped his own heart—but something in his spine straightened beyond mere posture. He finished the verse, let the room take the echo, and turned to look up.

High Cantor Ovelith stood at the gallery rail in robes the color of deep water, his white collar stark against them, his hands folded. He was not a large man. He was, in fact, remarkably slight—fine-boned, fine-featured, with silver hair cut close and eyes of such a pale grey they seemed almost without color, as if someone had diluted them. He was fifty-some years old and looked forty, and he smiled the way stained glass glowed: beautifully, and only because there was light behind it.

"Your Holiness." Quentin bowed. "We weren't told you'd attend."

"I rarely announce myself. Announced listeners perform. Unannounced ones merely sing." Ovelith descended the gallery stair with a softness that seemed wrong for a man his rank—no echo, no click of shoes on stone—and came to stand at the rail of the choir stalls, looking up at Quentin with his head tilted, attentive as a heron. "Again. The third phrase. I want to hear how you take the descent."

Pellin had risen from his chair, and there was a stiffness in him Quentin had never seen. "Your Holiness, the boy has sung five times through already. His voice will want rest before—"

"It will want nothing of the kind." Ovelith said it mildly, pleasantly, without looking away from Quentin, and Pellin sat back down as if a hand had pressed on his shoulder. "A voice like this one isn't a muscle, Aide. It's an instrument, and instruments are kept in tune by use. Again, if you please."

Quentin sang the third phrase. The descent—the melody falling note by note through the word *remember*, like light going down behind a hill. Ovelith closed his eyes while he listened. When Quentin finished, the Cantor stood silent a moment, and then nodded slowly, twice, as if confirming something to himself.

"Yes," he murmured. "Yes, that will do."

"That will do for what, Your Holiness?" Quentin asked, before he could stop himself.

For a heartbeat—one heartbeat—Ovelith's pale eyes focused on him with an intensity that made the hair on Quentin's arms rise. It was like being looked at through a keyhole. Then the warmth returned, smooth as water closing over a dropped stone.

"For Vespers tomorrow, naturally. The Feast of the Veil approaches. I want my last choirboy at his very best." He smiled. "Do you know why I keep you, Quentin? Why the vestry has not filled your stall these six years?"

"No, Your Holiness."

"Because they broke." Ovelith said it simply, sadly, a doctor delivering a diagnosis. "Every voice breaks, given time. Yours has not. Eleven years, and still pure, still *unbroken*. Do you understand how rare that is? How precious?" He reached out and touched the rail of the stall, lightly, the way a man touches a railing of a ship he trusts. "Some things are worth preserving exactly as they are. Don't you think?"

"I—suppose so, Your Holiness."

"Mm." Ovelith turned away, and the moment passed, and Quentin breathed again without knowing he'd been holding air. At the foot of the gallery stair the Cantor paused. "Aide Pellin. The rehearsal copies for tomorrow. Have the boy use the new folios."

Pellin's pen stopped moving. "The new folios, Your Holiness? The traditional text has served the Feast for—"

"The new folios, Aide." Still mild. Still pleasant. "I did not ask for a history lesson."

* * *

They were waiting on his stall desk when Quentin came up after the evening meal: two folios of heavy cream paper, bound in grey leather, the temple's seal stamped in wax on the cover. Fresh copies. New calligraphy, precise and lovely, done by a professional scribe's hand—someone paid by the page, not by the prayer.

He opened the first one to the *Canticle of Returning Light*, and frowned.

The words were wrong.

Not all of them. The opening was intact—*O lights above the border-stone*—but as his eye traveled down the page he kept snagging, the way a finger snags on a splinter. Verses he had known since before he could read had gaps in them. Whole lines replaced with newer, smoother phrasing. The second verse, the one about the gods keeping faith with the city, had been cut from eight lines to four. The fourth verse—

The fourth verse was gone entirely.

Quentin set the folio down, picked up the old copy from the shelf beside his desk—the one he'd used since he was ten, its margins soft with thumb-grease, its corners rounded by a decade of handling—and laid the pages side by side in the candlelight.

*Old:* *"And when the veil between us thins, / speak, O singers, speak our kin / across the dark where they abide, / and wait upon the other side."*

*New:* *"And when the veil between us thins, / the Word alone shall bridge the wide divide."*

It scanned worse. It rhymed worse. It was, musically speaking, a worse line in every way a line could be worse, and some tired apprentice scribe had copied it out anyway, in beautiful letters, on beautiful paper, because someone had ordered it copied.

And there was something else. Quentin tilted the old folio toward the candle and saw, faintly, in the raking light—the ghost of scraped parchment. Not on the new copy. On the *old* one. On the master text, the one chained to the lectern downstairs, the one he'd sung from his whole life. Someone had taken a knife to it. Carefully, recently, within the last season—the exposed parchment was still bright, not yet yellowed. Lines removed. Words scraped away and written over.

He sat back on the stool, and the chapel suddenly felt colder, and larger, and full of listening.

Hymns didn't change. That was the whole point of hymns. The *Canticle of Returning Light* had been sung at dusk since the founding; Master Verel used to say it was older than the city, that the first wall of Corvane had been raised by masons who sang it to keep their courage up. You could change a sermon. You could change a doctrine, a vestment, a holiday. But the hymns were the covenant's own handwriting—or so every teacher Quentin had ever had had told him, back when there were teachers, back when there was a choir.

Someone had edited the gods' mail.

"Boy." Pellin's voice from the stair, quiet. "You should sleep. Early call tomorrow."

"Pellin." Quentin didn't look up. "When did the master texts change?"

Silence, long enough that Quentin finally turned. The old aide stood at the top of the stair with his ledger under his arm, and his face in the candlelight looked the way Quentin imagined a man's face looked standing on a shore watching a ship go down that he couldn't reach.

"They haven't changed," Pellin said at last. "They've been *maintained*. There's a difference. Learn it, and live longer." He hesitated, and then, in a lower voice, almost gentle: "Sing what's written, Quentin. Tomorrow, at the Feast—sing what's written, and look at no one, and ask nothing. Can you do that for me?"

"Why?"

"Because you are the last unbroken voice in this temple," Pellin said, "and I am too old to bury another singer." He turned and went down the stairs, and his footsteps faded, and Quentin was alone with the two folios and the candle and the scraped-away words.

* * *

He did not sleep.

He told himself he would, and blew out the candle, and lay in the narrow dormer cell that had been his for eleven years, and stared at the ceiling beams, and did not sleep. Below him, the temple settled into its night sounds—the tick of cooling stone, the distant shuffle of the night brothers, the wind worrying the bells in the south tower. Above him, through the little window, the sky.

He got up and looked at it, because he always did. The stars over Corvane were the finest sight in the world, everyone said so; the city sat high on its plateau with clean air and no fog, and on nights like this the heavens were a spilled treasury, thousands of lights, the whole shining host of the covenant arrayed from horizon to horizon. The gods' faces, the priests called them. The lamps they lit so mortals wouldn't lose the road home.

Quentin had sung to them every evening of his life. *Keep faith with us who walk alone.* He knew their patterns better than his own face in a mirror. The Shepherd's Belt low in the east. The Sisters, close together, inseparable. The Wandering Star that was never in the same place twice.

He counted them now, idly, the way you count sheep. Belt. Sisters. Wandering Star. The Cradle. The Long Chord, that faint ribbon of clustered lights—

He stopped counting.

He counted again, slower, forcing himself to name each pattern, and each pattern was there, and yet the sky seemed—thinner. Dimmer. As if a film of breath had been breathed onto glass. The Sisters were there, but he could not have sworn, looking at them, that they were as bright as last week. The Long Chord was a smudge where it had been a stitchwork.

Or perhaps it was the candle smoke in his eyes. Perhaps it was exhaustion. Perhaps it was the scraped parchment working on his mind, making him see erosion everywhere, in the sky itself.

Below, far below, at the edge of hearing, a bell tolled midnight, and then another bell answered it from a different tower, and then a third, and Quentin realized with a small cold start that the bells were not tolling the hour. The hour had passed. These were the alarm-rings, the pattern rung only in drills, only twice in his lifetime—

—and even as he placed the pattern, even as he leaned toward the window, the light in the sky *flickered*.

All of it. Every star, horizon to horizon, the whole burning host, guttered at once like a candle in a draft.

And did not come back.
