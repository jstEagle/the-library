## 3. The Night the Stars Went Out

Freya had always known the exact weight of a lie.

It was four drams of riverstone, three of ash-glass, and one of something older that they had never been able to name—the residue, perhaps, of whatever magic had been wrung out of Corvane when the Temple of the Veiled Word outlawed it. Every charm Freya sold at the night market carried that weight: a love-knot that only untangled headaches, a luck-braid that did nothing but smell faintly of rain, a door-wax that kept moths honest. Harmless things. Things that could be laughed off as folk superstition if an inquisitor ever turned one over in his gloved hands.

Fifteen years of harmless things. Fifteen years of measuring out their gift in thimbles so no one would ever see the well beneath.

That night the market was loud in the way it got before a festival—lantern-sellers shouting, a fiddler tuning badly, Maren's stall next door doing brisk trade in honeyed pears. Freya sat behind their little table of charms, tallying the week's ledger by lantern-light, adding another line to the debt column that never seemed to shrink no matter how many coins they scraped together for the Temple's collectors. *Interest on interest*, the ledger said. *Assessed per annum, in perpetuity.* A debt designed never to be paid. Freya had stopped reading the numbers closely years ago. Reading them too closely was a kind of drowning.

They were mid-stroke on a figure—a seven, its tail curling wrong—when the first charm flared.

Not flickered. *Flared.*

The little headache-knot, sitting in its tray of felt, lit up like a coal breathed upon, glowing white-hot through the riverstone. Then the luck-braids. Then the door-wax, which began to smoke though nothing burned it. One after another down the table, every piece of quiet craft Freya had ever made woke at once, as if someone had shouted a word into the world's ear and everything enchanted had turned to listen.

"Fire!" someone screamed from the far end of the row. "The charms are burning!"

They weren't burning. They were *singing*—or trying to. Freya could feel it through their fingertips where they rested on the table: a vibration like a held breath, like a choir tuning to a note no throat could reach. The charms weren't malfunctioning. They were answering something. And whatever they were answering was going out.

Freya looked up.

The sky above Corvane's rooftops was clear—autumn-clear, the kind of cold clean dark that usually showed every star the city ever had—and it was empty. Not clouded. Not dimmed. *Empty.* From horizon to horizon, not one point of light. The moon itself was gone, and where it had hung there was only a hole in the black, a perfect absence shaped like absence, ringed by nothing.

The market went silent the way a forest goes silent. The fiddler's bow skidded off the strings. A lantern-seller dropped his pole and forty paper lights went rolling into the gutter, still burning, absurdly small against all that dark.

"Stars don't just—" began the fishwife from the corner stall, and then she stopped, because there was no word for what stars had done, and her voice cracked in the middle of having none.

Freya stood. Their hands were shaking, and the shaking had nothing to do with fear—or not only fear. Every hair on their arms was lifted, every tooth ached faintly, and somewhere behind their sternum something old and half-asleep had opened one eye. *This is what I am,* the feeling said. *This is what you've been starving for fifteen years.* The wards along the border, three hundred miles east—that was the thought that came unbidden, arriving whole like a memory that wasn't theirs. The wards had been fed by starlight. Everyone knew that much, even here, even in the city where knowing such things aloud was a fineable offense. The wards drank the sky. And the sky had just been cut off at the throat.

Which meant the wards were dying right now. Which meant—

The second wave hit.

Every charm on the table went off simultaneously—not light this time but sound, a chord, a single vast harmonic that Freya felt in their molars and their ribs and the wet architecture of their inner ear. Customers staggered. Glassware rang in sympathy three stalls away. And Freya, who had spent fifteen years teaching their gift to whisper, felt it rise in them like floodwater against a door, wild and enormous and *theirs*, demanding to be let out, to answer, to add its small voice to whatever vast broken thing was crying out across the world.

They clamped down on it. Barely. It cost them something—they'd know by morning what, exactly; there would be blood to spit and a tremor in their writing hand—but they clamped down, because a scribe who flared in public during the worst night in living memory would be dead before dawn, and the Temple's inquisitors were already moving. You could hear them. Boots, many boots, and the low chime of censers swung in patterns meant to smother stray enchantment.

"Everyone inside!" a crier was bawling, his voice thin with his own terror. "By order of the Temple! Curfew! Curfew until the Veiled Word restores the heavens!"

*Restores.* As if the Temple could put back what had fallen out of the sky. As if the Temple hadn't—

Freya closed that thought like a hand closing over a candle flame. Later. Think it later, in the dark, behind a locked door, where thoughts couldn't be read off your face.

They swept their charms into their satchel—still warm, still humming faintly, like birds with their hearts beating too fast—and folded the ledger shut on the impossible arithmetic of their life. Around them the market was emptying in a slow stampede, neighbors clutching children, the fishwife weeping without noise, an old man standing perfectly still in the middle of the lane with his face tipped up to the starless black, mouthing something over and over. A prayer, probably. Or a question. Freya didn't stay to learn which.

At the mouth of the lane, under the last working lamp, a temple acolyte was marking doors with chalk—a sigil Freya didn't recognize, sharp-angled, new, drawn quick and nervous. Marking which households had shown signs. Which families to visit come morning.

The acolyte's eyes passed over Freya's stall, over Freya, ordinary and grey and bent over their accounts like any tired tradesman closing early.

They moved on.

Freya exhaled, shouldered the satchel, and slipped into the crowd flowing toward Maren's street, telling themselves the tightness in their chest was only fear.

It wasn't only fear. It was also, and this was the terrible part, the part they would carry like a swallowed coal for weeks afterward—it was also *awe*. Something had happened tonight that was real. After fifteen years of pretending nothing real existed, the sky itself had torn open and proved them right about everything they'd ever hidden. The stars were gone, the wards were failing, the world was breaking—and some buried, unforgivable part of Freya Voss had stood under that murdered heaven with their heart hammering and thought:

*So it was true. All of it. And now everyone will know.*

Then they hated themselves for the thought, and walked faster, and did not look up again.

* * *

Quentin had been singing vespers alone in the upper chapel when the world ended, and afterward he would always believe that was why he survived it intact—because he was already halfway into the song when the song was needed.

He loved the hour after compline, when the other boys had gone down to the dormitory and the High Cantor had withdrawn to his tower, and the chapel belonged to Quentin and to the dark and to the particular silence of a room built for voices. He'd climb to the loft with its view of the rose window, and he'd sing the old verses—the ones that were being scraped away, letter by letter, from the great hymnals downstairs. He wasn't supposed to know them anymore. Three revisions ago the Cantor's office had issued new editions, and the old words had been rasped off the parchment so thoroughly that the pages wore thin as cloth in places. But Quentin had a boy's memory and a chorister's ear, and the old melodies lived in him the way rivers live in stone.

Tonight he sang the Seventh Antiphon, the one that began *O light beyond the lattice of the sky*, and he sang it quietly, apologetically, the way you'd speak to someone you weren't sure could still hear you.

Because lately, singing felt like speaking into a room where the furniture had been covered in sheets.

He couldn't have said when he'd first noticed. The gods had never answered him loudly—he was eleven, and disgraced, and the son of a laundress, and no god had ever owed him anything. But there had always been a *texture* to the silence after a verse. A listening quality. Like the pause after you knock on a door and hear, faintly, someone shifting their weight inside. For ten years that texture had been the private floor he stood on, the secret that made the discipline and the cold and the loneliness bearable: *they can hear me. Someone can hear me.*

For the past season, the texture had been changing. Thinning. The silence after his verses had gone flat, acoustic, ordinary—the silence of an empty room rather than an occupied one. And tonight, singing the antiphon, Quentin had felt it go out entirely, the way a fire goes out—not dwindling, not banked, just *gone*, between one note and the next.

He stopped. Sat on the loft bench. Told himself he was tired, that his voice was changing anyway, that a boy of sixteen couldn't trust his own ears.

And then the rose window shattered.

All of it. At once. Not inward—the glass blew *outward*, toward the graveyard, every pane bursting from its leads in a single breath, as if the window had been holding a note of its own for six hundred years and had finally been made to stop. The sound was enormous and strange, less like breaking than like a bell struck dumb—a crash with a scream inside it. Shards rained down over the tombs, glittering for one impossible instant in the light of the sanctuary lamps, and then the lamps guttered, all together, down to blue pinpricks.

Quentin was on his feet without deciding to stand. His heart was slamming. The cold poured in through the empty window frame, and with the cold came the sky.

He looked up through the naked tracery, expecting clouds, expecting the ordinary merciful dark of a city night.

There was nothing. No stars. No moon. He crossed to the window's edge and leaned out over the drop, over the graveyard with its broken glass lying on the graves like frost, and scanned the whole bowl of heaven, and it was *bare*, blacker than any night he had ever seen, a darkness with nothing left in it to be dark *about*. Even the temple's own star-mural, painted on the ceiling of the pilgrims' nave, suddenly seemed like a taunt.

"No," he said aloud, uselessly, to the sky. "No, no—"

And then, because he was a chorister and terror has always found its way out through trained throats, he sang.

It wasn't a choice. It came up out of him like water finding a crack—one long unbroken tone, high and pure and frightened, the old antiphon's opening note sustained past all sense, past breath, past reason. He sang because the silence was wrong and his body knew something his mind refused to accept: that the silence was not empty. That somewhere on the far side of it, something vast was hurt, and hurt things call out, and calls deserve answers.

His voice filled the gutted chapel and kept filling it, past the point where a boy's voice should have been able to fill anything, and the sound grew strange—it acquired harmonics he wasn't singing, overtones stacked on overtones like light through a prism, until the whole ruinous beautiful room rang like the inside of a bell. The stone floor hummed under his feet. Dust fell from the rafters in slow silver threads through the lamplight.

And the glass in the sacristy windows—thick, leaded, ancient glass—cracked. Not broke. *Cracked*, in patterns, in spirals, in feathering radiating from centers that followed his melody the way iron filings follow a magnet. He could see it happening, pane by pane, each fracture blooming in time with his phrase-endings, and he understood, with a clarity that terrified him more than the empty sky had, that his voice was doing something voices were not permitted to do anymore. That the ban on magic had never covered *him*, because no one had believed a voice could be this. That he was standing in a wrecked chapel singing a forbidden hymn to gods who might or might not exist, and the building itself was testifying on his behalf.

He should have stopped. Some cold practical corner of him knew he should have stopped.

He didn't stop. He sang the whole antiphon, every scraped-away verse, at full strength, tears running freely down his face, and on the final cadence—*and return to us the lights we cannot relight ourselves*—something answered.

It was not a voice. It was barely a presence. It was a pressure against the silence, the way deep water presses against a hull, and it lasted less time than a heartbeat, and it was the most real thing that had ever touched Quentin Aldery in his entire devout, disciplined, half-believed life.

*We hear you,* it said, or said the shape of saying it. *We are still here. We are being taken. Find—*

Gone. The pressure lifted. The silence went ordinary again, flat and final, and Quentin stood in the ruined chapel with blood-warm tears on his cheeks and his voice ringing in his own bones, utterly alone, absolutely certain.

Below him, in the nave, a lamp flared back to life.

Footsteps. More than one set. The heavy measured tread of men whose business was other people's secrets, and the low chime of a censer swinging its smothering pattern.

"In the loft," a voice called upward, flat and official. "We heard it from the cloister. Who is up there?"

Quentin wiped his face with his sleeve, uselessly. The cracks in the sacristy glass were still spreading, still blooming, still keeping time with a melody that had ended. There was no explaining them. There was no explaining any of it—except truthfully, and the truthful explanation was a death sentence, or close enough.

He descended the loft stair slowly, hands visible, throat bare, and gave his name to the first inquisitor's shadowed face: *Quentin Aldery, third form, upper choir.*

They wrote it down. They wrote down the time. They asked him, gently, in the gentle way that was worse than harshness, whether he had been singing, and what, and whether he had noticed anything unusual in the heavens this evening.

He told them the truth the way a drowning man gives up his gold: reluctantly, completely, watching it leave him.

"Yes," he said. "I was singing the old antiphon. And yes." He looked past their shoulders, out the empty window frame, at the naked dark. "The stars are gone."

One of the inquisitors—a young one, freckled, whose hands shook slightly on his stylus—glanced up at the sky through the tracery and then quickly away, as if looking too long might make it contagious. The senior inquisitor, a broad woman with a scar through one eyebrow, studied Quentin's face for a long moment. Studied the cracked glass. Made a small mark in a leather book, the kind of mark that did not mean *nothing*.

"The Cantor will want to speak with this one," she said to her junior, quietly, and Quentin's stomach dropped through the floor of the world.

They escorted him down through the nave, past the shattered rose window, past the graves wearing their glitter of glass, out into the coldest, darkest, most impossible night Corvane had ever known. Other inquisitors were moving through the temple grounds with lanterns, knocking on doors, marking lintels with chalk. Somewhere beyond the wall, the whole city was making noises—bells rung wrong, crowds, dogs, a sound like distant weeping carried on the wind from districts he couldn't name.

And overhead, where the Pleiades should have been, where the Shepherd's Star should have been, where every light his grandmother had ever named for him should have been—nothing. A black so total it looked solid, like a ceiling lowered overnight, like a lid.

Quentin walked between his escorts with his head bowed as ordered, and did not look up again, and held the answer inside him like a coal in cupped hands: *we are still here. We are being taken. Find—*

Find what? The message had cut off. Find whom? Find what? A voice, maybe. A singer. Someone the muffled ones could still reach.

He was sixteen years old, disgraced, marked in an inquisitor's book, summoned to the Cantor's tower at dawn. He had no plan, no ally, no idea what came next.

But he knew, with the absolute certainty of a boy who had spent his life listening to silences, that the thing he had heard tonight was real, and desperate, and asking for help—and that somewhere out there in the starless dark, in the choking curfew streets of Corvane, someone else had heard it too. Someone whose charms had flared. Someone marked, like him. Someone the chalk sigils were hunting even now.

He just had to find them before the Temple did.
