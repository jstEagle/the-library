## 4. Inquisitors at the Door

Freya had learned to measure danger in the sound of boots.

Fifteen years in Corvane's night market taught you the difference between a drunkard's stagger and a watchman's patrol, between a merchant's hurried heels and someone walking with purpose toward *you*. The temple's inquisitors had a particular tread—measured, unhurried, absolutely certain that no door in the city would stay shut against them. They didn't hurry because they never needed to.

So when Freya heard that tread coming up Candlemaker's Row at half past midnight, they did not panic. Panic was a luxury for people whose names appeared on no ledgers. They simply began, with the calm of long practice, to destroy their livelihood.

The charm-stall came apart in under a minute. False bottoms opened; the good stock—the real work, the pieces that would get a person burned—slid into a lead-lined case beneath the floorboards. The harmless trinkets stayed out: love-knots woven from ordinary thread, "luck" coins stamped with nothing holier than a rabbit, little glass beads that were only glass. If the inquisitors searched, they'd find a scribe-turned-peddler selling superstition to superstitious people. Nothing worth an arrest. Nothing worth a pyre.

Freya was tamping down the last of the sawdust when the light arrived.

It came through the shutters first—a cold white glare that had nothing of fire in it, the color of the wards' death still burning in the sky where stars should have been. Then voices, moving door to door along the Row. Not shouting. That was the worst part. The inquisitors never shouted. They asked questions in gentle voices, and the questions themselves were the threat.

"Open in the name of the Veiled Word."

Old Cobb the candlemaker's door creaked. Low murmuring. A woman's voice, clear and tired: *"No, nothing here. Thank you for your cooperation."* Cooperation. As if there were another option.

Freya pulled their coat tighter, snuffed the stall's single lamp, and stood very still in the dark, doing what they did best: being no one.

*You are a scribe with a bad cough who sells rabbit-luck to gullible sailors. You have papers. Your papers are in order. Your debt is paid down to the copper, tallied every week, your name in the temple's books as a model of indentured obedience.*

That last part had always been the shield. Fifteen years of perfect payments. Fifteen years of being so small, so punctual, so *harmless* that the Temple of the Veiled Word had never once looked closely at the charms that sometimes, if you knew how to look, actually worked.

But tonight the wards had fallen. Tonight the stars had gone out. And tonight—Freya's hands went cold remembering it—every charm in their stall had flared like festival-lanterns, all at once, unbidden, screaming light into a street full of witnesses.

Someone had seen. Someone always saw.

The boots stopped outside.

Not at the stall—at the door behind it. Maren's door. The landlady's house sat tucked against the back of her shop like a hen sheltering a chick, and Freya rented the attic room above it, had done for eleven years, and in all that time the inquisitors had never once climbed these stairs.

Three knocks. Even, patient, terrible.

"Maren Doss." The woman's voice again, close now, muffled by oak. "Inquisitor Vane. Open, please."

Freya heard Maren's footsteps inside—heavy, unhurried, the tread of a woman who had buried a husband and outlived two landlords and did not startle for anyone—and then the bar scraping loose.

The door opened. Cold white light spilled across the floorboards and up the stairs, and Freya pressed flat against the wall of the stairwell's turn, heart hammering somewhere up around their ears.

"You're awake," Inquisitor Vane observed. Her voice was dry as old paper. Through the gap in the banister Freya could see her: grey cloak, grey eyes, the silver stylus-and-shackle pin of the Order at her collar. Younger than her voice suggested. Tired around the mouth in a way that wasn't boredom.

"I'm always awake at this hour," Maren said. "Pickling. You want to search my house, or do you want to stand in my doorway letting the cold in?"

"We're looking for a practitioner." Vane held up a slate, and even from the stairs Freya felt their stomach drop through the floor. On it, sketched in chalk-white lines, was a sigil—the flare-pattern. The shape the runaway magic had burned into the air over Candlemaker's Row when the wards broke. "Several witnesses reported charms igniting without a hand near them. Uncontrolled thaumaturgy. In this district."

"Frightening," Maren said flatly. "Was anyone hurt?"

"No."

"Pity," said Maren, "that the stars went out instead. Now. Are you coming in to turn over my pickling crocks, or—"

"We will enter every premises on this row." But something in Vane's tone had gone slightly reluctant, the way a dog sounds when it's been told to bite a friend. "Standard procedure."

"Then wipe your boots."

Freya listened to the inquisitors move through Maren's house. There were three of them—Vane and two silent men in grey. They checked the cellar, where Maren kept her stores. They checked the ground-floor shop, where she sold salt fish and lamp oil and, in winter, secondhand coats. Their boots thumped overhead, into the two middle rooms Maren let to a widow named Pell and a dockhand who was forever behind on his rent.

They did not go up to the attic.

Freya understood why a moment later, and it nearly undid them more thoroughly than any arrest could have.

"The attic?" Vane's voice, directly below the trapdoor. Directly below Freya.

"Storage," Maren said. "Smells of mice. My lodger keeps a room up there but he's out—works nights at the tally-house. You can see his rent book if you like. Paid through the month, every month, eleven years. Most reliable tenant I've ever had."

A pause. Freya did not breathe.

"Your lodger is a scribe?"

"Tally-house clerk. Counts other people's money and goes home. You want me to fetch his ledger?"

Another pause, longer. Then Vane said, quietly, almost to herself: "Paid through the month. Every month." And there was something odd in the way she said it—not suspicion. Recognition. As if she were reading a familiar handwriting in a place it shouldn't be.

"No need," she said at last. "We're done here."

Boots descended. The door opened. Cold rushed in, and then the door closed, and the bar scraped back into its brackets, and Freya discovered that they had been gripping the banister hard enough to leave marks in the wood.

For a long moment they stayed exactly where they were, listening to the white light recede down Candlemaker's Row, door by door, until even the memory of it faded.

Then the trapdoor at the top of the attic stairs creaked open, and Maren's voice floated up, dry as ever:

"Well? Are you going to sit on that banister all night, or are you coming down for tea?"

* * *

Maren Doss's kitchen was the warmest room in Corvane, which was not saying much on a starless night in autumn, but tonight it felt like a different country. The stove was lit—which it should not have been, with curfew on and inquisitors sniffing for smoke—and a kettle muttered on the hob, and the table was already laid for two, which meant Maren had known, had *known*, since the moment the sky went dark, exactly who would come down those stairs shaking.

"How—" Freya started.

"Sit down before you fall down." Maren poured tea without asking how they took it. She was a broad woman built like a dockside crane, sixty-odd years old, with iron-grey hair pinned up in a style that hadn't been fashionable in forty years and a face that suggested fashion had once tried her patience and lost. "You looked like a ghost at the window an hour ago. I put the kettle on then. Figured you'd either come down or you wouldn't."

"You knew I was watching the door?"

"I know everything that happens in my house. It's the one advantage of being old and terrifying." She set the cup down in front of Freya. It was the good cup, the one with the painted wren on it, the one that never came out except for funerals and births. Freya stared at it. "Drink. You've gone the color of my dishwater."

Freya drank. The tea was black and bitter and perfect.

"They'll come back," Freya said finally. "The one with the slate—Vane. She'll think about it. Rent paid through the month, every month, eleven years—that's not nothing. That's *pattern*. People who hide things pay cash and keep quiet, but people who hide things don't usually have spotless temple debt records, and I do, and she noticed, I saw her notice—"

"Freya." Maren sat down across from them, and the kitchen went very quiet except for the kettle. "Child. Look at me."

Freya looked. It was difficult. Looking at people directly was not a skill they'd practiced much.

"Why did you flare?" Maren asked. "Tonight. When the wards broke. What happened?"

"I don't know." It was the truth, mostly. "Everything I've got—the real stock, the lead-lined case—it all lit at once. Like something called it. Like something *pulled*." Freya wrapped both hands around the wren cup. "Magic's been dead in Corvane for fifteen years, Maren. Outlawed, suppressed, whatever the temple calls it. Everything I sell is supposed to be trickery and suggestion. Ninety percent of it is. But tonight it all woke up at once, and I don't know why, and the inquisitors have witnesses, and my name is in their books, and—"

"And you've been paying that debt like clockwork so nobody would ever look at you twice."

Freya's jaw tightened. "You knew about that too."

"I know you're a scribe who could earn triple at the tally-house and chooses to sell luck-charms to sailors at midnight instead. I know you flinch when temple bells ring. I know you've never once, in eleven years, told me your family name." Maren's voice wasn't angry. It was worse than angry. It was gentle. "I'm not blind, child. I'm a landlady. It's practically the same profession."

The kettle ticked as it cooled. Somewhere outside, far off, a temple bell tolled the hour—one o'clock, in a city with no stars, where the curfew drums had been beating since sundown.

"So," Maren said. "Here's what happens now. You're going to finish that tea. Then you're going up to the attic, and you're going to sleep, because you look like a scarecrow left out in the rain. Tomorrow night, after dark, we're going to talk about whether that stall of yours opens again or whether it's time for a different arrangement. And if the grey-cloaks come back—"

"Maren." Freya's voice cracked on the name. "If they come back and find me here, harboring an unregistered practitioner—they won't just take me. Harboring is a crime. You'd lose the house. Everything. You've seen what they do to—"

"I've seen what they do to everyone," Maren said. "My husband owed them nothing and they took him anyway, in the purges, year three of the ban. Wrong name on a list. By the time they corrected the error he'd been dead a fortnight." She said it the way she said everything, flat and steady, but her hands, folded on the table, had gone white at the knuckles. "So don't you lecture me about what the temple takes. I've done my arithmetic. I've had eleven years to do it."

"But why?" It came out of Freya before they could stop it, raw and young and fifteen years overdue. "Why would you risk that? For me? You don't—you don't owe me anything. I pay my rent. That's the whole arrangement. That's the *only* arrangement. Nobody does something like this for nothing, there's always a price, there's always a ledger, so just—tell me what it costs. Tell me now, so I can—"

"Stop." Maren reached across the table and put one broad hand over Freya's fluttering fingers, and Freya went still as a caught bird. "Listen to me very carefully, because I'm only going to say this once, and then we're never going to speak of it again."

Freya waited.

"There is no price," Maren said. "There is no ledger. Somebody once did this for me, a long time ago, when I was younger than you and twice as frightened, and I never got the chance to pay her back because they took her too. So this isn't a debt, child. This is *interest*. It's been accruing for thirty years, and you happen to be standing where it landed." She withdrew her hand and picked up her own cup, businesslike again. "Now drink your tea. It's getting cold."

Freya drank their tea.

It should have been a small thing. It was, objectively, a small thing: a cup of tea, a lie told to an inquisitor, a warm kitchen in a cold city. People did small kindnesses all the time. Freya had spent fifteen years cataloguing the exact size and weight of every favor ever done them, because in Corvane favors were currency and currency was debt and debt was chains, and they had sworn, sworn on everything, that they would never be chained again.

But nobody had ever lied to an inquisitor for them before. Nobody had ever put the good cup out for them. Nobody had ever said *there is no price* and meant it, and Freya sat in the warm yellow light of Maren Doss's kitchen with tears running silently down their face into their tea, and could not, for the life of them, have said whether the feeling in their chest was gratitude or grief or terror or simply the shock of a lock opening that they hadn't known was locked.

Maren pretended not to notice. She refilled the cup once, without comment, and talked about pickling.

* * *

Freya did not sleep.

They lay in the attic room under the sloped roof, in the dark, listening to the city breathe its frightened breath, and they thought about the flare. About the pull. Something had *called* their magic tonight—all of it, at once, like a hook set in their chest. And the wards had broken the same hour. And the stars—

The stars hadn't gone out. Freya had watched the sky for fifteen years from this same attic window, had learned its every pattern the way a debtor learns a creditor's face. Stars didn't simply stop. They were *taken*. All of them, at once, cleanly, the way a merchant lifts coin from a purse.

And the temple's inquisitors weren't hunting whoever took them. They were hunting the people whose magic woke up screaming when it happened.

Which meant the temple knew what had happened. Which meant, possibly, the temple had *done* it.

Freya turned the thought over, cold and bright as a stolen coin, and found it fit their hand far too well.

Below them, through the floorboards, they heard Maren moving—banking the stove, barring the door, the small nightly liturgy of a careful woman. Then silence. Then, faintly, from the direction of the market square, a sound that raised every hair on Freya's arms.

Singing.

Distant, thin, carried on the wind from somewhere across the rooftops—a boy's voice, high and clear and utterly unafraid, singing something that was not any hymn Freya had ever heard in the Veiled Word's chapels. It sounded older than hymns. It sounded like the space where stars used to be.

And in the lead-lined case beneath the floorboards of their ruined stall, Freya felt every charm they owned wake up and answer it.

They sat bolt upright in the dark, heart slamming, and stared at the ceiling as if they could see through it—through the roof, across the sleeping, terrified city—to wherever that voice was coming from.

*There you are,* the pull seemed to say, wordless and vast. *There you are. Come and find me.*

Downstairs, Maren's floorboards creaked. She'd heard it too.

Everyone in Corvane had heard it too.

Freya dressed in the dark, hands shaking, and reached for their boots.
