## 6. What the Wards Were For

The knock came at the third hour past midnight, and it was Maren's knock—two sharp raps and a pause, like a woman deciding whether she meant it.

Freya had not slept. Sleep was for people whose world still had stars in it. They sat instead at their narrow table with every scrap of paper they owned spread out before them: charm-notes, half-finished ward diagrams, receipts from the Temple of the Veiled Word that they kept not out of sentiment but because debt was a language and they intended to read it fluently until the day it killed them. A single candle burned down to a thumb's width of tallow. Outside, Corvane lay under a sky like a closed lid.

Maren let herself in without waiting, which was her way. She carried a covered basket and an expression that meant trouble had been delivered to her door and she had opinions about it.

"You're awake," she said.

"I'm always awake now. The dark is very loud."

"The dark is very *wrong*," Maren corrected, setting the basket down. From it she produced bread, hard cheese, and—a gesture so absurdly domestic that Freya's throat tightened—a jar of pickled onions. "Eat first. Then look at what my nephew dragged home."

"Your nephew steals things."

"My nephew *liberates* things. There's a difference, and the difference is whether I get a cut." She set a bundle of oilcloth on the table between them, heavy for its size, wrapped tight and tied with cord. "He was running messages near the temple district when the wards went. Says the clerks came pouring out of the Scriptorium like ants from a kicked hill, carrying everything that wasn't nailed down. He picked up what they dropped."

Freya looked at the bundle and felt something cold settle behind their ribs. Fifteen years of keeping their head down, of being small and harmless and forgettable, and here was Maren putting contraband from the heart of the temple on their table as casually as she'd put down the cheese.

"Maren. If anyone finds this here—"

"If anyone finds this here, I'll tell them you forced me. You've got a criminal face. Very shifty." She pulled out a chair and sat, arms folded, daring them to argue. "You've been scratching at those fragments since the night the sky went black. Don't think I haven't seen you. Now open it before I do it myself and break something valuable."

Freya untied the cord.

Inside were seven pieces of vellum and one thing that made their breath stop: a fragment of ward-stone, no bigger than a fist, its surface carved with script so old it predated the ban by centuries. The stone was dead—no hum, no warmth, nothing—but dead things could be read. Dead things were often more honest than living ones, because they'd stopped pretending.

"This is border-ward material," Freya said quietly. "This shouldn't exist outside the boundary line. These stones are supposed to be *rooted*. Grown into bedrock over generations."

"And yet." Maren nodded at the papers. "Read."

* * *

Freya had spent fifteen years teaching themselves to read the way other people taught themselves to hold their breath: carefully, invisibly, in stolen moments. The temple's scriptorium trained scribes to copy, but copying teaches you more than the temple likes to admit. To reproduce a hand, you must understand it. To understand a hand, you must understand the mind behind it—the hesitations, the corrections, the places where the pen pressed too hard because the writer was afraid or excited or lying.

The first three vellum pieces were inventory lists. Boring, on their face: columns of quantities, dates, requisition signatures. Freya almost set them aside. Then they noticed the dates ran backward.

"Look at this," they said, turning the page toward Maren. "Requisition for 'clarity-grade lumen, forty measures.' Dated nine days before the wards fell. And here—" another sheet, "—'lumen, clarity-grade, sixty measures,' dated twelve days before. And here, twenty days before, eighty measures."

"So they were buying up light?"

"They weren't buying it. Lumen isn't bought; it's *drawn*, and only through sanctioned channels. This is withdrawal. Someone inside the temple was pulling starlight out of reserve stores in increasing amounts, week after week, like a man bleeding himself slowly to see how much he could lose and stay standing." Freya's finger traced the signature column. Every entry bore the same mark: a stylized word, elegant, compressed. "And every withdrawal authorized by the same seal."

Maren squinted. "Whose?"

Freya didn't answer immediately, because answering meant saying it aloud, and saying it aloud made it real. But the seal was unmistakable. They'd seen it stamped on their own indenture ledger a hundred times, on every notice nailed to the market posts, on the tithe-boxes at every chapel door. The interlocked V and O of the High Cantor's office.

"Ovelith," they said. "Every measure of hoarded light, signed out under the High Cantor's own seal."

The candle guttered. Neither of them moved to fix it.

* * *

The ward-stone fragment took longer.

Old ward-script wasn't writing exactly—it was closer to music frozen mid-note, each glyph a chord that wanted to sound. Freya had learned the basics from a smuggled primer back when they were young enough to believe knowledge was worth the risk, and they'd spent years since picking up scraps: a glyph here from a crumbling milestone, a phrase there from a tomb inscription nobody else could read. Their charms—the little harmless ones, the luck-knots and headache-eases they sold in the night market—were built on that scavenged grammar. Small words. Safe words.

The stone's script was not small.

They worked by candlelight, tracing each glyph with a fingertip, sounding the silent chords in their head the way a musician reads a score. It came slowly, then all at once, the way understanding does when it has been waiting patiently for you to catch up.

"It's a maintenance record," they said at last. Their voice sounded strange to them, flat and far away. "A log. The border wards are alive, in a way—they need feeding. That was the whole point of the covenant, the original one, before the temple rewrote it. Voices fed the gods, gods held the wards. The wards keep records of what feeds them. When they were fed. By whom. How much."

"And this one says?"

Freya turned the stone over. On its underside, where no casual eye would look, the final entries had been carved deep—deeper than routine notation, carved by someone who wanted them legible even if the rest of the stone shattered.

"It says the feeding stopped." Freya heard their own voice go very quiet. "Not failed. Not weakened. Stopped. There's a difference. A ward that fails degrades—you'd see months of thinning entries, irregular feedings, distress marks. This shows perfect regularity right up until a specific night, and then a clean severance. One stroke. Deliberate. Like a throat cut."

Maren had gone pale in the candlelight. "Someone cut the wards off from their food."

"From inside." Freya laid the inventory sheets beside the stone, dates aligned. "The withdrawals started weeks ago. Whoever was pulling the lumen wasn't stealing it for profit—they were emptying the reserves so that when the severance came, there'd be nothing left for the wards to fall back on. You don't drain the cistern unless you plan to burn the well." They looked up. "The wards didn't fail, Maren. They were unmade. From inside the temple. By someone with authority to sign withdrawals and access to the root-chapels where the feeding happens."

"The Cantor."

"The Cantor."

Saying it twice didn't make it easier. Saying it twice made it worse, because once might have been a mad thought and twice was a theory, and theories demanded consequences.

* * *

The last piece of vellum was different from the others. Smaller, torn unevenly, as if ripped from a larger document in haste or anger. The handwriting was cramped, hurried—not a clerk's fair hand but someone's private notes. Freya recognized the style of shorthand used in the upper scriptorium; they'd forged it often enough for clients who wanted temple documents copied without the temple knowing.

It took them the better part of an hour. Maren fell asleep in her chair around the second hour, snoring gently, refusing on principle to leave. Freya worked with the candle stub balanced on a saucer, and the sky outside stayed black and starless, and the city murmured its uneasy sleep, and the shorthand gave itself up line by line:

*—the resonance holds if the vessel is pure. The old covenant diluted the light across ten thousand voices. Waste. Inefficiency. Gathered, concentrated, the full radiance of the choir can be held by ONE—*

*—the gods will not object. The gods cannot object. A god fed is a god bound; a god starved is a god silent. We have starved them for a generation already. They have forgotten how to say no.*

*—the stars are not lost. Nothing is ever lost. The stars are WITHDRAWN, held in reserve against the day of the new covenant. Let the people look up and see nothing. Fear empties the chapels of doubt and fills them with bodies. They will beg us for light. They will thank us for returning it.*

*—when the hearth is lit and the vessel sings, all that was scattered will be gathered to the one voice, and the one voice will be mine, and the realms will be ordered at last—*

The rest was torn away.

Freya sat back. The candle had burned to nothing while they read; the room was lit now only by the faint grey suggestion of a window, and by the glow of their own hands, because their magic had been leaking since the night the stars vanished and they had stopped bothering to fully suppress it. Light pooled in their palms like water finding its level.

Cold clarity. That was the feeling, and they examined it the way they'd examine a new glyph: turning it over, testing its weight. Not rage—rage would come later, rage was a luxury. This was colder and more useful. The specific calm of a person who has finally read the whole ledger and knows exactly what they owe and to whom.

The stars hadn't fallen. They'd been taken. Banked like coin in a vault, hoarded by a man who believed starvation was stewardship, who had spent a generation muzzling gods and calling it devotion—and who intended to relight the world himself, alone, as its sole intermediary, its single indispensable voice. Every prayer routed through him. Every miracle stamped with his seal. A covenant of one.

And the people—the ten thousand voices the notes dismissed as waste, dilution, inefficiency—would kneel and be grateful.

Freya looked at their glowing hands. Fifteen years of hiding. Fifteen years of small safe charms and a debt that could never be paid and a self folded up so small it fit through the cracks of the world. They had told themselves the hiding was survival, and it was, but underneath it had always been something shabbier: the hope that if they were quiet enough, none of this would ever be their problem. Let the temple do what temples did. Let the sky do what skies did. Keep your head down, scribe. Sell your knots. Be invisible.

But invisible people see things. That was the whole trick of it. Nobody checks what the furniture witnesses.

And the furniture had just watched the High Cantor of the Veiled Word rob heaven.

"Maren," Freya said softly.

The old woman cracked one eye. "Mm. Found your answer, then?"

"The stars aren't gone. They're locked in the temple's vaults. Ovelith took them, and he means to give them back on his terms—through himself, controlled, owned. He's been starving the gods for years to make them compliant, and the wards were just the first thing he cut loose." Freya stood. The light in their hands threw their shadow huge against the wall, and for once they didn't flinch from it. "There's going to be a moment—I don't know when—when he needs a scribe. Someone to write the new covenant. Someone literate in the old scripts who can make his theft look like law. And there's exactly one unregistered scribe in this city who reads ward-script and owes the temple a debt they can call in whenever they like."

Maren sat up fully now, awake in an instant. "They'll come for you."

"They'll come for me. And when they do, I intend to know more than they think I know, and I intend to be harder to find than they expect, and somewhere between those two things—" Freya flexed their fingers, watching the borrowed light shift, "—I'm going to find a way to give the stars back."

"That's madness."

"It's arithmetic." Freya began gathering the fragments, wrapping them in the oilcloth with hands that no longer shook. "He thinks light is a currency and he's the only bank. But light was never his. It belonged to everyone who ever sang to it. Which means it can be *returned* by everyone who remembers the song." A pause. "We just have to find someone who still remembers how."

Outside, in the starless dark, a bell began to ring from the direction of the temple district—not the measured hour-bell but something faster, urgent, repeated. A summons bell. Or an alarm.

Maren and Freya looked at each other across the table, across the wrapped fragments, across fifteen years of careful silence about to end.

"That'll be for you," Maren said grimly, reaching for her shawl. "Or for someone. Either way, I'd get dressed."
