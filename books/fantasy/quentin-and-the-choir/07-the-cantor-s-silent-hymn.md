## 7. The Cantor's Silent Hymn

The light came to him at the hour of Lauds, as it always did.

Ovelith stood alone in the Cantor's Gallery above the Great Nave, where no choir had sung in thirty years—not since he had sealed the doors and hung the veil of grey silk across the archway. Below him, through the gallery's pierced screen, the morning office proceeded without him: rows of cowled voices rising in the approved hymns, the ones with the scraped verses, the safe words. He did not need to be among them. He had not needed to sing in a very long time.

He only needed to listen.

The light rose out of the nave like heat-shimmer off summer stone. To any other eye the air was empty; to Ovelith it was a slow golden snowfall, drifting upward instead of down—every note of every hymn shedding its radiance as it left a human throat. The worship of ten thousand faithful, distilled. It gathered along the vaulted ceiling in rivers that only he could see, and there the silver funnels waited, the ones the glasswrights had spent nine years installing behind the rose windows, drinking the light away into the dark.

He watched it go, and he felt no shame at all.

*They are saving it,* he reminded himself, as he reminded himself each morning, the way other men said their prayers. *I am saving it.*

Behind him, soft on the gallery floor, Brother Cassian cleared his throat.

"The ward-stone reports are ready, Your Reverence."

"Leave them."

"They asked whether—" Cassian hesitated. Cassian was young enough that hesitation still looked like doubt rather than wisdom. "The border wards failed three nights ago, Your Reverence. The northern garrisons are asking why. The Council of Bells is asking why. They are asking *me*, because I carry your correspondence, and I do not know what to tell them."

Ovelith turned from the screen. In the grey half-light of the gallery his face was long and mild, the face of a patient schoolmaster, and his voice when he used it was famously gentle. Whole parishes had wept under that gentleness. It was one of the many instruments he had learned to play.

"You will tell them," he said, "that the wards were old, and that all old things fail, and that the Temple of the Veiled Word has already begun the work of replacing them. You will say it sorrowfully. You will say it with your head bowed, because grief is becoming on a messenger." He smiled, and watched the young man's shoulders loosen, obedient even to comfort. "And then you will bring me the tally from the deep vaults. I want the count of vessels filled by dawn office."

Cassian bowed and withdrew. His footsteps faded down the spiral stair, and Ovelith let out a breath he had been holding for three days—the breath of a man who has just set down something breakable.

The lie had gone smoothly. Lies always did, now. That was the part no one warned you about: not that lying became easier, but that it became *boring*. There had been a time, forty years ago, when a falsehood stuck in his throat like a fishbone. He had been a choirboy then, in this same temple, with a voice so fine that the old High Cantor—Vessia, may her name rot gently—had called him the finest instrument the gods had given Corvane in a century.

He had believed her. That was the joke of it. He had stood where Quentin—where the boy stood now, though he did not yet know the boy's name mattered—and sung the covenant-hymns with his whole heart poured into them, certain that somewhere beyond the veil of stars the gods were listening, were pleased, were *receiving* him.

And they had refused him.

He remembered the night of his testing as clearly as he remembered yesterday, because he had made himself remember it, had turned it over ten thousand times until every edge was familiar. Eighteen years old, voice at its perfect peak, standing alone in the First Hearth chamber high on Mount Cael with the cold hearthstone before him and Vessia watching from the doorway. The old rite. The true rite. Sing, and if the gods accept your voice, the hearth relights.

He had sung until his throat bled. He had sung the way only the desperate and the devoted can sing.

The hearthstone stayed cold.

Vessia had put her hand on his shoulder—kindly, oh, so kindly—and told him that the gods' silence was not a judgment but a mystery, that some voices were meant for leading the faithful rather than speaking *to* heaven, that he would make a magnificent servant of the Temple. And he had believed that too, for a while. Believed it through twenty years of rising through the ranks, through the death of the old Cantor, through his own investiture, through decade after decade of hymns flung at a sky that never once answered.

Until the night he stopped believing, and started *counting*.

* * *

The deep vaults lay beneath the reliquary, seven levels down, past doors that answered only to his voice—and to his voice alone, now; he had seen to that after the third warden asked too many questions. Warden Aldric had asked whether the vessels were sanctioned by canon. Warden Brenna had noticed that the starlight tallies exceeded anything the old liturgies described. Both had been reassigned to coastal parishes with excellent views of the sea and no view whatsoever of the vaults. Neither had been harmed. Ovelith was proud of that. Even now, with everything at stake, he had harmed no one who could be spared.

He descended with a single lamp, because the vaults did not need light, and that was precisely their glory.

Seven hundred and forty-one vessels stood in their niches, floor to ceiling, tier upon tier, filling the great round chamber like the pipes of an organ built by giants. Each was a globe of veined crystal the size of a skull, and each held what the vessels in the smuggler bazaars held—what the temple's own surplus-dealers sold in back alleys to fools who wanted a lamp that never guttered—but held it *pure*, undiluted, caught straight from the source before it could scatter.

Starlight. The hoarded radiance of the gods.

He walked the aisles slowly, as he did every day, reading the tallies chalked beneath each niche. The first vessels, nine years ago, had taken a month apiece to fill—a trickle, a whisper, barely worth the glasswrights' fees. But he had learned. He had learned which hymns shed the most light (the old ones, always the old ones, the verses Vessia's generation had scraped from the walls because they were "too passionate"—fools, they had been scraping away the richest veins). He had learned that grief-filled voices gave more than joyful ones, and that fear gave most of all. He had learned that a congregation praying toward a vanished star gave twice what a congregation praying toward a visible one gave, because longing was a funnel and contentment was a leak.

And three nights ago, he had learned the last lesson of all.

Ovelith stopped at the newest tier, the bottom ring, where the vessels stood fuller than any others. Seven of them, each glowing faintly through the crystal like drowned suns. When the border wards had come down—when *he* had brought them down, unmaking from inside the temple the great lattice that Brannoc and his smiths had spent a lifetime weaving—he had expected a surge. What he had gotten was a flood. Every prayer on the borderlands, every terrified hymn from every village that suddenly looked up into a starless sky, had come pouring into the nave's hidden funnels, and the light had run so hot that two of the older vessels had cracked, and he had lost nearly a vessel's worth of radiance hissing away into the stone.

A regrettable expense. An investment, really. Fear, it turned out, was the richest ore in the world, and he had just opened the largest mine in history.

He laid his palm against the warm crystal of the nearest vessel, and inside, the light pressed against his hand like a living thing. Like a bird in a cage. Like a voice.

"I know," he murmured. "I know it hurts. It hurts me too."

And the terrible thing, the thing he admitted to himself only here, seven levels down, in the dark where no one could see the High Cantor of Corvane kneel—it was true. It hurt him. He had loved the stars. He had loved them as a boy on the temple roof, naming constellations with the other novices, certain each one was a listening ear. Taking them had been like taking the eyes of someone he loved.

But love, he had learned, was not the point. Love was what you felt while the world went dark around you unless you acted.

Because here was the truth that Vessia had died never understanding, that the whole soft-hearted hierarchy had never understood: the gods were *dying*. Not angry, not absent, not mysterious—dying. He had found the evidence in the deepest archive fifteen years ago, in a ledger older than the temple itself: the covenant ledger, the accounting of the first bargain. Mortal voices fed the gods; the gods held up the wards and hung the stars and kept the dark things beyond the border where they belonged. A simple economy. A fair one.

And for three hundred years the deposits had been shrinking. Fewer true voices every generation—the choir schools closing, the old hymns scraped away, the gift of song taxed and licensed and forbidden outside temple walls until the whole kingdom sang through a keyhole. The gods had been starving politely, dimming year by year, too proud or too gentle to say so, and everyone above him in the hierarchy had responded with incense and mystery and the scraping of walls.

Ovelith had done the arithmetic that no one else would do. At the current rate of decline, the last god would gutter out within his lifetime. And when the last god went, the wards would fail anyway—messily, everywhere at once—and the stars would go out anyway, and the things beyond the border, the things the first covenant had been written to hold back, would come through into a world that had forgotten how to fight them.

Unless someone reforged the covenant.

That was the word the old ledger used, and he had clung to it like a drowning man to a spar. *Reforged.* It could be done. It had been done once; it could be done again. But the old covenant had been a commons—a thousand voices feeding a thousand gods, no one in charge, no one accountable, everything entrusted to faith and goodwill. And look what faith and goodwill had bought: a starving heaven, a failing border, a kingdom sleepwalking into the dark.

No. If the covenant was to be remade, it would be remade *properly*. With the light gathered and guarded, not scattered. With one keeper of the treasury instead of a thousand careless spenders. With a single voice at the center—one voice, tested and proven and *accountable*—standing between mortals and gods, giving each exactly what the other needed, wasting nothing, losing nothing, answerable for every drop.

His voice.

He had thought, in the early years, that the plan required humility, and had practiced saying so. But somewhere in the last decade the practice had become unnecessary, because the truth had settled into him the way sediment settles in still water: it was not pride. Pride was wanting the role. This was different. This was *arithmetic*. Someone had to hold the covenant, and every candidate but one had already failed. The gods had refused Vessia's chosen singers for three centuries—including, first and foremost, an eighteen-year-old boy who had bled on the hearthstone of Mount Cael. The gods had made their preferences clear by omission. What remained was the man who had spent thirty years learning the exact weight and measure of divine light, who understood the economy of heaven better than heaven's own clerks, who had sacrificed his music, his honesty, his reputation, and nearly his soul to keep the light from being wasted.

There was no one else. There had never been anyone else. That was not vanity; that was the census.

*And when it is done,* he thought, walking the aisles, lamp held high, *they will understand. When the wards stand again—stronger, mine, permanent—and the stars return one by one at my decree, and the border-things press against a wall that does not break—they will fall on their knees in the nave and weep and call me savior, and I will tell them: do not thank me. Thank the covenant. I am only its steward.*

He believed it. That was the thing his enemies—if he had enemies, if anyone ever saw the shape of what he built—would never grasp. He was not a thief. Thieves take for themselves. He was taking for *everyone*, holding in trust, and one day he would give it all back with interest. Every drop of hoarded light would pour back into the sky. He simply intended to be the hand that poured it, because every other hand he had ever observed had trembled.

* * *

At the hour of Sext, the summons came that he had been waiting three days for.

Brother Cassian again, pale this time, genuinely frightened, which improved him. "Your Reverence—the chapel of the upper choir. During rehearsal. The glass."

"Speak plainly."

"The east rose window shattered, Your Reverence. During evensong rehearsal. The acoustics master swears it was a voice—a boy's voice, one note, and the whole rose came down like rain. And there's more." Cassian swallowed. "The light-readings in the nave spiked. Off every scale we keep. Whatever the boy did, the funnels drank more in that one moment than in the whole month prior."

Ovelith set down his pen very carefully.

A boy's voice. One note. Glass broken not by volume—he had heard shattering voices before, brute instruments, loud as landslides—but the acoustics master would have known the difference, and the *light*—the light was the signature that mattered. Resonance. The old ledgers called it *true voice*: a throat tuned to the covenant's original pitch, the frequency on which the gods themselves had been addressed. Three centuries since the temple had recorded one. Three centuries of Vessias scraping walls and calling the silence a mystery.

And it appeared now, at the precise moment he needed it most. Reforging the covenant required a true voice—that much the ledger was explicit about. He had planned, with dull resignation, to use his own: aged, imperfect, refused once already, but sufficient. He had made his peace with insufficiency.

Now heaven had sent him an instrument.

"Where is the boy?" he asked.

"Gone, Your Reverence. Fled before the brothers could question him. We have his name—Quentin. Orphan-chorister, sixteen years of age, no family, no patron. And—" Cassian faltered. "There is a complication. The inquiry into the wards has turned up a name in the night market. A charm-seller. Unlicensed craft, possibly deep-magic. Some of the brothers believe the two events are connected. The fugitive boy was seen near the market district."

*Connected.* Of course they were connected. Everything was connected; that was the entire principle of his life's work. A true voice appearing in the same season the wards fell, drawn like iron to lodestone toward whatever scrap of unauthorized craft still smoldered in the gutters of his city—

He should have been alarmed. A fugitive chorister and an outlaw mage, loose together in the dark districts, carrying between them exactly the two components the old covenant required. If he had been a smaller man he would have been afraid.

Instead Ovelith felt something he had not felt in forty years, something dangerously close to gratitude. Because the pieces were not scattered. The pieces were assembling. A true voice, a worker of forbidden craft, the hoarded light waiting in its vessels—all of it drifting, unknowing, toward the shape he had spent nine years carving for it. He did not need to catch them. He needed only to leave the right doors open and the right pressures applied, and they would walk into the covenant's cradle on their own feet, believing it was their own idea.

Faithful people always did.

"Double the watch on the mountain road," he said. "Quietly. No warrants, no banners—I want shepherds, not hunters. And send for Inquisitor Vane."

Cassian blinked. "Sera Vane, Your Reverence? For a runaway chorister?"

"For a *resource*," Ovelith said mildly. "Bring the boy to me alive and unharmed, and tell whoever brings him that he will want for nothing for the rest of his life. Tell him the High Cantor has heard of his gift." He smiled, and it reached his eyes, which made it worse. "Tell him the truth, Brother. Tell him he is the most important singer in Corvane. Every word of it will be accurate."

Cassian bowed and hurried out, and Ovelith returned to the gallery above the nave, where the afternoon light was beginning its long gold slide across the floor.

Below, vespers would begin within the hour. Ten thousand throats, warming up. Ten thousand little lamps, unknowingly pouring themselves into his funnels. He took his place at the rail and folded his hands and listened to the first hymn rise, and inside it, faint as a heartbeat, he could feel the new note already—the boy's note, the true pitch, echoing somewhere out in the dark city, drawing closer.

"Sing," Ovelith whispered to the gathering crowd below, to the caged light overhead, to the silent sky. "All of you. Sing as you've never sung. Heaven is listening again—and this time, it will have to go through me."

Far to the north, past the broken wards, past the sleeping mountain, the First Hearth sat cold in the dark.

It would not stay cold much longer. He intended to reach it first.
