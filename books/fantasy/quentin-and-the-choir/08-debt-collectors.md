## 8. Debt Collectors

The summons came at dawn, folded into the mouth of a temple crow that perched on my windowsill like a debt with feathers.

I knew what it was before I touched it. Temple crows don't deliver bread. They don't deliver love notes. They deliver arithmetic—the cold, patient kind that the Temple of the Veiled Word had been doing on me for fifteen years. The bird watched me with one brass-ringed eye as I took the paper from its beak, and when I'd read it, it waited, patient as interest accruing, until I fed it a crust from last night's supper. Then it flew off toward the spires to report that the scribe Voss had received the message and had not, so far as it could tell, died of fright.

*Present yourself at the Office of Reckonings, third bell after dawn. Bring your ledger-mark. Come alone.*

I sat on the edge of my narrow bed in Maren's attic room and did the thing I do best, which is count.

Fifteen years ago, the temple had bought my indenture for forty marks—my parents' debts, rolled into mine, plus the fee for the binding-ink they'd put under my skin so my gift couldn't run loose. Fifteen years of charm-work sold cheap in the night market, fifteen years of tithes skimmed off every coin before it reached my pocket, fifteen years of payments that never seemed to touch the principal. The ledger said I still owed two hundred and eleven marks. I had done the sums myself, once, with a scribe's precision: by any honest accounting, I had overpaid them years ago. But honest accounting was not what the Veiled Word traded in. The debt wasn't a number. It was a leash, and leashes don't get shorter just because you've walked far.

And now there was Quentin.

The choirboy had been in my stall for three nights, sleeping in the back room behind the hanging charms, eating Maren's soup, flinching at bells. His voice was a wound wrapped in silence; he hadn't sung since the chapel glass broke, and I could feel him not-singing the way you feel a held breath in a small room. The temple wanted him back. The temple wanted him very badly back. And now the temple wanted *me* at the Office of Reckonings, which meant only one thing:

They knew where he was. Or they suspected. And they were going to make me prove which side of the ledger I stood on.

Maren was already downstairs, banging pans around the way she does when she's worried but won't say so. I found her in the kitchen feeding the fire, and she looked up at me with those flat grey eyes that miss nothing and comment on less.

"Crow came," she said. Not a question. Crows are loud.

"Crow came."

"You going?"

"I have to be somewhere," I said. "Third bell."

She stirred the pot harder than the pot deserved. "You know, in thirty years of renting rooms, I've learned there's two kinds of people who come down stairs quiet. Thieves and people saying goodbye." She didn't look at me. "Which are you this morning, Voss?"

"Neither," I said, and it was mostly true. "I'm going to go stand in front of some clerks and lie beautifully. It's practically a religious observance."

"Mm." She ladled soup into a bowl and shoved it at me. "Eat first. Lying's hungry work."

It was such a small thing—a bowl of barley soup, thrust into my hands without a price attached—and it caught in my throat more than the food ever could. Fifteen years, and everything in Corvane had a cost except, apparently, Maren's soup. I ate it standing up because if I sat down I might have to think about why my eyes were stinging, and I didn't have time to think. Third bell waits for no one, especially not debtors.

* * *

The Office of Reckonings occupied the temple district's least impressive building, which was itself a message: your debts are beneath the gods' notice, handled here among the plumbing. Inside, it smelled of ink and lamp oil and the particular staleness of rooms where people cry regularly. Clerks moved between desks like patient beetles. Everything was recorded. Everything was always recorded.

They put me in a small room with a table, two chairs, and a man I recognized with a lurch of old dread: Auditor Pellin, who had administered my indenture since I was twelve years old. He hadn't aged so much as condensed—same grey robe, same ink-stained fingers, same face like a receipt left out in the rain. He had watched me sign my first tithe schedule with a child's unsteady hand. He had complimented my penmanship.

"Scribe Voss." He gestured to the chair. "Sit. This will be brief, which I suspect will please us both."

I sat. On the table between us lay my ledger—not a copy, the ledger, the actual bound book with my blood-mark seared into the first page—and beside it, a second object that made my stomach drop through the floor and keep falling: a scrap of blue cloth, frayed at one edge, embroidered with a single thread-of-silver star.

Quentin's choir-robe trim. Torn off, I'd have guessed, on the night he ran. Someone had been carrying it around the night market, showing it to stall-keepers, asking questions.

"We recovered the fugitive's trail as far as Tanner's Row," Pellin said, folding his hands. "Witnesses describe a boy matching his description sheltering near a charm-stall operated by an indentured scribe. You'll understand that the coincidence interests us."

"I sell charms to half the district," I said. My voice came out steady, which surprised me. "Half the district sleeps within fifty feet of my stall. If I harbored every runaway who passed my awning, I'd need a bigger awning."

"Mm." He opened the ledger to my page and turned it toward me, and there it all was, my whole life in columns: forty marks borrowed, fifteen years of payments, the balance that never shrank. "Two hundred and eleven marks outstanding. Do you know what happens to an indentured debtor whose debt is called in full?"

"They pay it," I said.

"If they can." He produced, from his sleeve, a document I had heard about in whispers my whole adult life but never seen: a ward-tapper contract. The parchment was pale, almost translucent, the way things get when they've been drained of something. "The temple maintains certain civic works. Border wards, primarily—or rather, we *maintained* them, until recent events required... reallocation. A ward-tapper is a gifted individual bound directly into the ward-lattice. Their gift feeds the working. Continuously. It is not painful, precisely. It is simply constant. Tappers typically serve their term in eight to twelve years."

"And then?"

He looked at me with something that in a kinder face might have been apology. "The term is renewed as needed. Most tappers are quite devoted by the end. The work simplifies them."

*Simplifies.* Like a candle being simplified into light. I thought of the border wards going dark, of the empty sky, of the temple's hunger for whatever it had taken, and suddenly the shape of it was very clear: they needed new fuel. The stars were gone and the wards were starving, and here was the temple, holding out a contract written in the only currency I had left.

"Betray the boy," Pellin said, "and the debt is dissolved. Full remission. Your name comes out of our books entirely—you walk free, Voss, truly free, for the first time since you were a child. Refuse, and we bind you at sundown. Those are the options. I'm instructed to give you until noon, but between us—" he tapped the ledger, gently, almost fondly, "—there is no third column. There never has been. That is what a ledger *is*. Every entry balances. Everyone pays."

I looked at the ledger. My blood-mark on the first page, brown now, the brown of old rust, of dried-out things. Fifteen years of believing that if I stayed small enough, harmless enough, useful enough, the arithmetic would eventually release me. Fifteen years of selling little lies to frightened people—charms against headaches, charms for luck, charms that worked just well enough to seem worth the coin and just poorly enough to stay beneath notice. Fifteen years of being invisible on purpose, because invisible people don't get collected.

And here was the collection.

Here was the moment the whole architecture of my careful life revealed itself for what it always was: not safety. Deferral. I had been paying interest on my own existence, and the temple had decided the principal was due, and the principal was either a terrified boy with a broken voice or the rest of my life poured slowly into a machine built to swallow gods.

Pellin slid the quill across the table. "The remission order requires only your mark and a statement of the fugitive's location. Or your best estimate. We're reasonable about estimates."

I picked up the quill.

I want to be honest about what happened in my chest in that moment, because it wasn't courage. Courage is clean, and what I felt was a mess. I felt terror, first, bright and total, the animal knowledge that I was about to burn down the only shelter I had. Underneath the terror, fury—fifteen years of it, compacted like coal. And underneath the fury, something quieter and worse: relief. The terrible liberty of a person who realizes the trap was never going to open no matter how patiently they waited, and so the only question left is whether they spring it themselves or let someone else do it.

There was no third column. So I would write one.

"The boy," I said, "is somewhere you'll never find him, because everyone in the night market who's ever bought a luck-charm from me knows exactly what your covenants are worth. And I am somewhere you'll never collect from."

I stood up. I picked up the ledger—it was heavier than I remembered, or I was lighter than I'd realized—and I walked past Pellin, past his startled reach, out of the little room and into the long hall of desks where the beetle-clerks scratched and scratched at other people's lives.

There was a brazier by the door. Of course there was; clerks seal documents with wax, warm their hands, burn their mistakes. The temple's own fire, free for anyone's use.

"Scribe Voss—" Pellin's voice behind me, sharp now, the fondness gone.

"Fifteen years," I said, to the room, to him, to the ledger in my hands. "You should have checked my arithmetic."

I dropped the book into the brazier.

It caught slower than I expected—old vellum resists, even betrayal resists—and for one horrible heartbeat I thought it wouldn't take, that I'd have to stand there in front of forty clerks holding my own ruin like a damp match. Then the flame found the blood-marked first page and went up all at once, eager, and the heat washed my face, and the smell was ink and char and something underneath both that smelled, absurdly, like freedom.

The hall went silent. Forty pens stopped. Forty clerks stared at me, and I stared back, and I felt something I hadn't felt since I was a child before the binding-ink: my magic rising in me unchecked, unhidden, flaring along my arms like sunlight through water. Every charm I'd ever woven flickered awake in the city behind me—I felt them, dozens of them, my little harmless lies suddenly burning true—and somewhere a temple bell cracked mid-ring, though whether that was me or the general unraveling of the world, I couldn't say.

"Seize her," Pellin said, hoarse. "Seize—"

But the clerks of the Office of Reckonings were not soldiers. They were accountants. They looked at the burning ledger, at the flames eating the record of my debt, and I watched something pass across their faces that I will remember until I die: envy. Pure, scandalized, magnificent envy. Not one of them moved.

I walked out of the Office of Reckonings through a hall of frozen pens, past the brazier where my fifteen-year leash curled into ash, and out into a morning that had no stars in it anywhere and somehow felt, for the first time in fifteen years, like it belonged to me.

Behind me, faintly, I heard Pellin shouting for guards, for inquisitors, for the law itself. Let him shout. The book was burned. Whatever they did to me now, they'd be doing it to a woman who owed them nothing.

* * *

I didn't go straight home. Home was the first place they'd look, and besides, my legs had opinions of their own, and they carried me through the waking market streets in a daze of adrenaline and disbelief, past stalls opening, past bread-smoke and fish-ice, past ordinary people living ordinary indebted lives. Twice I caught my reflection in shop glass—a thin, tired face with soot on one cheek—and twice I almost laughed, because the face looked exactly the same and nothing was the same at all.

By the time I doubled back through the tannery lanes and slipped into Maren's kitchen door, my hands had started shaking. That's how it goes: brave in the burning, coward in the aftermath. Maren took one look at me, put down her knife, and pointed at a chair.

"Burned it," I said. My voice came out strange, high, giddy. "Maren. I burned the ledger. In their own brazier. In front of the auditor. I'm—" I pressed my palms against my eyes. "I'm ruined. I'm finished. They'll bind me or hang me or worse, and I dragged you into it, and Quentin, and I—"

"Sit down before you fall down," Maren said, and pushed me into the chair, and set tea in front of me, and then—to my everlasting astonishment—she laughed. Short and rough, like a cough, but a laugh. "Fifteen years I've rented to you, Voss. Fifteen years of watching you apologize to furniture. Best day's work you ever did."

"They'll come here."

"They'll come everywhere." She said it plainly, no comfort in it and no panic either. "Question is whether we're here when they do." She glanced toward the back stairs, where a floorboard creaked with deliberate gentleness—Quentin, awake, listening. "Boy! Get down here. We're leaving."

He came down the stairs pale and rumpled, the blue star-trim gone from his collar, and he looked from Maren's packed expression to my soot-streaked face and understood instantly, the way frightened children understand everything. "What did you do?"

"I stopped paying," I said.

Something moved through his face—awe, fear, and under it, for the first time since the glass broke, a spark of something that might have been hope. "Is that possible? Can you just—stop?"

"No," I said honestly. "Not usually. Usually the debt collects *you*." I flexed my hands, watching the faint light still coiled under my skin, my magic loose and unhidden for the first time in fifteen years, terrifying and bright as an open window in winter. "But the book's ash, and ash doesn't argue. Which means as of this morning, I'm not a scribe of the Veiled Word. I'm just a criminal with a talent. Same as you."

Maren was already hauling her market cart out from under the stairs, throwing a canvas over it, herding us with the brisk tyranny of a woman who has decided survival is a chore list. "Vegetables on top, you two underneath. Brannoc's bridge, if he's sober enough to open his door. Move."

I paused with one leg over the cart's rim and looked back once at the kitchen—at the table where I'd eaten a hundred silent suppers, at the attic window where the crow had landed, at the whole small careful life I'd built inside the trap. I'd spent fifteen years learning to be invisible. It occurred to me, climbing in beside Quentin among the cabbages, that I'd never once asked whether invisible was the same as safe.

It wasn't. It never had been. And now, hiding under vegetables with a fugitive choirboy while my landlady drove us toward the lawless end of town, hunted by the most patient institution in Corvane—I found I didn't miss it.

The cart lurched forward. Behind us, bells began to ring—not the hour, but the alarm pattern, spreading from the temple district outward, street by street, like a stain.

"They know," Quentin whispered.

"They knew before I did," I said. "Now they're just angry about it. Hold on."

Maren whipped the mule forward, and the cart rolled out of the yard and into the waking city, carrying two debts the temple would never stop chasing—and, for the first time, carrying them together.
