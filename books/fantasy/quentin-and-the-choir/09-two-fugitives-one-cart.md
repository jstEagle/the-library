## 9. Two Fugitives, One Cart

The cart smelled of cabbage and secrets.

Freya lay flat on their back between two crates of winter turnips, one arm flung over their eyes to block the grey pre-dawn light leaking through the slats of Maren's cart canopy, and listened to the city of Corvane decide whether to let them go.

Above them, wheels ground through mud and old snow. Beside them, wedged against a sack of onions, Quentin breathed too fast—the quick, shallow rhythm of someone who had learned in the last three days that breathing could be dangerous. And somewhere behind them, back toward the temple district, bells were ringing. Not vespers. Not matins. Something arrhythmic and urgent that Freya had never heard in fifteen years of living under the Temple of the Veiled Word's bell schedule.

Alarm bells. Or worse: hunting bells.

"Are they following us?" Quentin whispered.

"I don't know." Freya kept their voice level, which took effort, because their heart was doing something undignified against their ribs. "If I say yes, will you stop asking?"

"If I say no, will you lie to me?"

Freya lifted their arm just enough to look at him. The choirboy was seventeen at most, all elbows and hollow cheeks, wrapped in one of Maren's dead husband's coats with the sleeves rolled four times. His hands were folded in his lap like he was still sitting in a choir stall, like good posture might save him. There was a bruise along his jaw from where an inquisitor's gauntlet had clipped him at the market gate, and he kept touching it absently, the way you touch a thing to confirm it happened.

"No," Freya said. "I won't lie to you. I've decided lying to you is more trouble than it's worth."

"That's not the same as deciding to tell the truth."

"It's how scribes tell the truth. We annotate it."

Something flickered across Quentin's face—not quite a smile, but the place where a smile would go once he'd decided whether he could afford one. Then the cart jolted over a rut, and both of them grabbed for handholds, and the moment went into the ditch with the rest of the morning's dignity.

They were past the tanneries now. Freya knew it by the smell, which cut through even the cabbage. The East Gate would be next—Maren's cart had a legitimate pass for the gate, because Maren sold vegetables to three villages along the Corrow road and paid her taxes with a punctuality that made her invisible in exactly the way Freya had spent fifteen years teaching themselves to be. Nobody searched Maren Ostwick's cart. Maren Ostwick was a fixture. Maren Ostwick was furniture.

Except this morning Maren Ostwick was up on the driver's bench with a knife under her shawl and two fugitives in her cabbage, and when the gate guards waved her through without so much as glancing at the canvas, Freya felt something crack open in their chest that they didn't have a name for yet.

The bells faded behind them. The road opened ahead: bare fields, hedgerows black with frost, and above it all—

Nothing. No stars. It was nearly full dark still, the sun barely thinking about rising, and the sky overhead was a smooth, starless grey like the inside of a closed eye. Fifteen days since the night the heavens had gone out all at once, and Freya still couldn't look up without their stomach turning over. You didn't notice a sky until it was empty. Then it was all you could see.

Quentin noticed them looking. "It doesn't feel right," he said quietly. "The sky. Like a room with the hearth swept out."

"You've said that every day."

"It keeps being true."

* * *

An hour out from the city, Maren pulled the cart off the road into a copse of bare alders, climbed down, and stuck her head through the canvas flap with the expression of a woman who had been composing a lecture and intended to deliver it in full.

"Right," she said. "Rules. One: nobody speaks above a whisper while we're on open road. Two: if anyone in a temple-grey cloak so much as sneezes near this cart, you're both under the turnips and I'm a widow selling cabbages, understood? Three—" she pointed a flour-dusted finger at Freya, "—you eat something. You look like a debt collector's idea of a bad investment."

"Maren," Freya began, "you've done enough, truly, we can walk from—"

"You'll finish nothing of the sort." She thrust a cloth bundle through the flap: bread, hard cheese, an apple with a bruise shaped like a crescent moon. "I've fed you for fifteen years, Freya Voss, mostly without your noticing, and I'm not stopping because the sky broke. Now eat. Both of you. Starving fugitives make poor company and worse decisions."

She withdrew before either of them could answer, and the cart rocked as she climbed back up to the bench. Freya stared at the bundle in their lap.

Fifteen years. They had lived in Maren's attic rooms for eleven of those years, and in all that time they had assumed—had carefully, deliberately assumed—that the landlady's kindness was transactional. Rent on time, roof repaired, small favors accruing interest like everything else in Corvane. That was how the world worked. That was what the ledger taught. Every kindness was a column, every favor a debt, and Freya had spent their whole adult life keeping accounts so precise that no one could ever hold power over them the way the Temple did.

And Maren had hidden them from inquisitors, lied to a Veil-captain's face, given up her cart and her safety and possibly her life, and asked for nothing. Not coin. Not a favor owed. Nothing.

"What?" Quentin asked.

"She didn't put it on the account," Freya said, half to themselves.

"The what?"

"Nothing." They split the bread and handed him the larger half, then pretended not to notice when he tried to give it back. "Eat your apple, choirboy."

* * *

The bickering started, as most things did, over trust.

It began innocently. Freya was reviewing their route in their head—Corrow village by midday, the river ford by dusk, Hollow Bridge by tomorrow if the roads held—and they made the mistake of saying some of it aloud.

"Hollow Bridge?" Quentin sat up straight, dislodging an onion. "That's exile country. The ward-smiths' quarter was purged years ago. Everyone says there's nothing left under there but drunks and ghosts."

"There's someone I need to see."

"A drunk or a ghost?"

"Potentially both." Freya kept their tone dry. "His name is Brannoc. He helped build the border wards. If anyone knows why they fell, it's him."

"And you trust him?"

"I trust that he owes the world an apology and I'm offering him a chance to pay it."

"That's not the same as trusting him."

"Neither is this," Freya said, gesturing at the space between them, "and here we are anyway."

Quentin's chin came up. He had a way of going very still and very upright when he was frightened, like a boy bracing for a conductor's wrath, and Freya was beginning to learn that the stillness meant the next thing out of his mouth mattered to him. "You haven't trusted me since the night we met. You checked my boots for temple sigils while I slept. Don't think I didn't wake up."

"You were awake?"

"I'm always awake. Do you know what it's like being hunted by people who trained you? Who taught you your scales?" His voice cracked on the last word—not with magic this time, just with age and exhaustion—and he pressed his lips together until he had himself again. "You don't trust me. You tolerate me. Because I'm useful, because my voice does things, because I'm evidence of whatever conspiracy you've decided the temple committed. But you don't trust me."

The accusation hung in the cold air of the cart. Freya found, to their irritation, that it stung—which meant it was at least partly true, which meant they had to decide quickly whether to admit it.

"You're right," they said.

Quentin blinked. Clearly he had prepared rebuttals.

"You're right. I checked your boots. I check everyone's boots; I checked Maren's shoes for tracking charms for eleven years and she never once wore any, and I kept checking anyway. Trust isn't—I don't have a lot of practice. When you grow up indentured, trust is just a slower way of being sold." Freya turned the bruised apple over in their fingers. "But I'll tell you what I do have. I have fifteen years of reading ledgers, and I can tell you what I see in yours. A boy who ran from the only home he had rather than sing a hymn that felt wrong. That's not evidence. Evidence can be forged. That's character, and character has to be earned the slow way, and you're earning it. Every day you don't betray me, another line gets written. That's the best I can offer right now. It's honest, at least."

Quentin was quiet for a long moment. Outside, the wheels sang against frozen ruts, and somewhere a crow complained about the state of the world.

"My voice healed a bird once," he said.

Freya looked up. "What?"

"When I was twelve. First year in the upper choir." He was looking at his hands. "There was a wren got into the cloister—a cat had it, or almost. Wing torn, neck bent wrong. The brothers said throw it out, it was done. But I picked it up and it was shaking, and I don't know, I hummed. Just the first line of the evening canticle, the old version, before they changed the words. And it stopped shaking. And its wing—" he made a small unfolding gesture, "—went back where wings go. It flew off over the wall. I stood there like an idiot with my hands still cupped."

"Why are you telling me this?"

"Because you said character gets written line by line. Well. There's my first line." He finally met Freya's eyes. "I didn't imagine it. I need you to know I didn't imagine it. The Cantor says the gods are silent, that the covenant is dead, that voices are just voices now. But that bird flew, Freya. Whatever else is true, that bird flew."

The cart swayed. In the front, Maren hummed tunelessly to herself, off-key on purpose, Freya suspected, so no one would ever ask her to sing anything that mattered.

"I believe you," Freya said.

And then, because fair was fair, and because the words had been sitting in their chest for fifteen years like a stone swallowed whole:

"My charms are real."

Quentin frowned. "Your charms?"

"At the market. The luck-knots. The fever-ribbons. The little stitched stars I sell to girls who want their sweethearts to write back." Freya laughed, but there wasn't much humor in it—it came out thin and strange, the sound of a lock opening after decades. "Everyone thinks they're nonsense. Little stitches and superstition, harmless folk craft, nothing the ward-inspectors bother with. That's the point. That's always been the point. I made them small enough to be dismissed. But they work, Quentin. Every one of them. The fever-ribbons pull heat out of a sickroom. The luck-knots nudge probability like a thumb on a scale. I've been doing magic in the middle of Corvane's biggest market for fifteen years, in plain sight, because I made it boring enough that no one ever looked twice."

The silence that followed had weight. Quentin was staring at them with an expression Freya couldn't parse—wonder, maybe, or recalculation, the sound of a ledger being rewritten.

"Fifteen years," he said finally. "You hid for fifteen years."

"You hid too. You sang their hymns and kept the old words alive in your mouth and told no one."

"I didn't know I was hiding anything. I thought I was just—" He gestured vaguely at his own throat. "Broken. Wrong. The Cantor said my voice was prideful, that I held back, that I needed correction. I thought something was wrong with *me*."

"Something is wrong with *them*," Freya said, and heard the steel come into their own voice, surprising them. "The wards didn't fall, Quentin. Wards don't fall—they're maintained, they're fed, they fail slowly, with warning, like teeth rotting. These shattered all at once, clean, from the inside. And the stars—" they looked up through the canvas slats at the blind grey sky, and the old cold clarity settled over them like a coat. "Stars don't go out all at once either. They were taken. Someone reached up and took them, and I'd bet my last charm-stitch it was the same someone who's been scraping the old words off your hymnals and calling your voice prideful. The Cantor isn't mourning the silence. He's conducting it."

"You don't know that."

"I know ledgers. And everything I've seen since that night balances. Too neatly. Somebody's been keeping books, and the entries all lead uphill to the temple."

Quentin absorbed this. He hugged his knees, making himself small inside the dead husband's coat, and for a while the only sounds were the wheels and the wind and Maren's deliberate, defiantly bad humming.

"If my voice can reach them," he said at last, very quietly, "the gods—if they're muffled, if they're not dead—then maybe I could—"

"Don't." Freya said it sharper than they meant to. "Don't decide what you're for. Not yet. That's what the Cantor did to you—decided what your voice was for. You get to choose. That's the whole difference between a singer and an instrument."

Quentin looked at them for a long moment. "Has anyone ever told you that you're kind underneath all the arithmetic?"

"Once. I charged them double."

This time he did laugh—a short, startled, rusty sound, like a door opening in a house long shut. And Freya, watching him laugh, felt the strange new thing in their chest crack a little wider, and decided not to look at it directly. Some accounts you don't audit. Some you just let run.

* * *

They made Corrow village by midday and passed through without incident—Maren trading cabbages, exchanging gossip, playing her role as furniture so well that Freya nearly applauded from under the canvas. By late afternoon the road bent toward the river, and the hills rose up ahead of them, and with the hills came the towers.

Freya saw them first: three slender spires on the far ridge, black against the darkening sky, too regular to be trees, too tall to be ruins. Ward-towers. Old ones, from before the ban, their tops sheared off cleanly as if snapped by a giant's fingers.

Then the light came.

It started as a glow at the base of the nearest tower—a sickly amber pulse, like a heartbeat seen through skin. Then a second tower answered. Then the third. And rising between them, threading upward into the starless dark, came a column of pale radiance that bent and twisted like smoke in a draft, and within it—Freya's breath stopped—within it were shapes. Points of light. Hundreds of them. Drifting, swirling, *caged*.

Stars. Or pieces of stars. Hoarded, corralled, herded like sheep toward the distant silhouette of the temple city.

Beside them, Quentin had gone rigid. His lips had parted, and though no sound came out, Freya saw his throat working, saw the tendons stand in his neck, and felt—actually felt, through the floorboards, through the turnips, through the soles of their feet—a resonance. As if the column of stolen light had struck a tuning fork buried in the world, and everything alive was humming in sympathy.

"He can hear it," Freya breathed. "Quentin. What is it saying?"

Quentin's eyes were wide and wet and fixed on the distant glow. When he answered, his whisper shook.

"It's not saying anything. It's *screaming*. Freya—it's screaming in harmony. All of them together. They're trying to reach someone." His hand found Freya's sleeve and gripped it hard enough to hurt. "And I think—I think they just heard *me*."

Far away on the ridge, the column of light shuddered, flared—and turned, all of it at once, like a school of fish changing direction.

Toward the cart.

Toward them.

"*Maren!*" Freya shouted, scrambling for the canvas flap. "*Drive!*
