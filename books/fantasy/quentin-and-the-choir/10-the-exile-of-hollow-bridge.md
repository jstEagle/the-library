## 10. The Exile of Hollow Bridge

The river under Hollow Bridge ran grey even before the stars went out, and Brannoc had learned to be grateful for that. Grey water asked nothing of him. It didn't glitter. It didn't remind him.

He woke the way he always woke now: in stages, each one a small argument against the day. First the cold came up through the pallet of sacking and river-reed he'd laid on the stone shelf beneath the bridge's western arch. Then the smell—mud, old tar, his own unwashed self, and the sweet rot of spilled ale soaking into wood that had been someone's door once, before it became his table. Then, last and worst, memory.

He kept a clay jug by his head for exactly this reason. He drank before his eyes were fully open, the way other men said their morning prayers. It was, he supposed, a kind of prayer. *Let me forget what I built. Let me forget what I broke.*

The jug was nearly empty. That was the trouble with prayers made of ale; they required constant renewal.

Brannoc hauled himself upright, and the bridge groaned above him—a long wooden complaint, joints shifting, timbers settling. He knew every voice that bridge had. He'd spent eleven years learning them, because a ward-smith who can no longer touch wards learns to listen to anything that will hold still. The bridge talked in its sleep. The pilings muttered at high water. The iron shoes nailed to the ferry-planks rang different notes depending on who crossed, and Brannoc had catalogued them all: the baker's heavy tread, the tax-clerk's quick nervous patter, the soldiers' boots in matched pairs, four-four-four-four, like a drum counting out the city's obedience.

This morning the notes were wrong.

Not many crossings yet—it was barely light—but the ones that came were fast. Running-fast. And between them, voices, pitched high and thin across the water:

*Did you see it? Did you see?*

*There's nothing up there. Look. Nothing.*

*My gran always said the stars were lamps. Said somebody had to tend them—*

*Somebody's stopped tending, then.*

Brannoc stood, slowly, and put his head through the gap between two of the bridge's cross-braces, and looked up through the lattice of timber at the sky.

It was the first time in eleven years he had looked at the sky sober.

He wished, immediately and violently, that he hadn't. The black up there was total. Not cloud-black—clouds had texture, edges, the grey suggestion of something behind them. This was a hole. A smooth, polished absence, as if some enormous hand had wiped the night clean and forgotten to draw the stars back on. Where the Wanderer should have hung over the eastern hills, where the Cradle should have arched above the temple spires, where the Shepherd and his Dog should have chased each other along the horizon—nothing. Nothing, nothing, nothing, all the way up.

His hands started shaking. He watched them shake with a strange detachment, as if they belonged to a younger man, a man with soot on his knuckles and a smith's calluses and a commission signed by the Temple of the Veiled Word itself.

*Ward-smith Brannoc Hale, licensed for works of containment and boundary. By order of the High Cantor.*

Eleven years ago those hands had set the last keystone in the border wards. Eleven years ago they had sung the closing verse of the binding—because wards weren't only stone and sigil, whatever the temple taught its apprentices; they were songs hammered into matter, and Brannoc had one of the finest singing-hammers in Corvane, and he had swung it, and the wards had closed like a fist around the whole frontier of the realm.

And last night, apparently, that fist had opened.

*They didn't fail,* said a cold little voice in him, the voice that never drank. *Wards don't fail, Brannoc. You built them too well. If they're down, someone unmade them. From inside.*

He sat back down on his pallet and reached for the jug and found it empty and discovered, with genuine surprise, that he was crying.

* *

By midmorning the bridge was a carnival.

Word had spread the way word did in Corvane—faster than fire, faster than plague, faster than any honest thing. People crowded the span above him, leaning over the rails, pointing up at the insulted sky. Preachers had already arrived, three of them, competing from opposite ends of the bridge: a temple crier bellowing that the stars' absence was a test of faith, that the Veiled Word would restore them when the city's devotion proved sufficient; and a ragged woman in mismatched robes shrieking the opposite, that the gods were dead or fled and good riddance, that she'd known since her sister died in childbirth; and between them a small boy of perhaps ten, standing on an overturned crate, saying nothing at all, just holding up a drawing he'd made of the night sky with all its stars still in it, which was somehow more devastating than either sermon.

Brannoc stayed under the bridge and listened, and drank what was left of a second jug he'd bought on credit from the taphouse on the corner—the taphouse keeper extended credit to Brannoc not out of kindness but because a drunk ex-ward-smith under your local bridge was, it turned out, excellent for business. People came to gawk. *That's him, that's the one they exiled, helped build the wards and then—* and here the story always diverged, because the official version and the true version had parted ways a decade ago, and the truth had gotten thinner every year, worn down like a coin passed through too many hands.

The official version: Brannoc Hale's work was found flawed. Negligent. Possibly sabotaged. He was stripped of license and guild-mark and sent into the outer dark, and the temple's own engineers had repaired his errors, and all was well.

The true version: Brannoc had found something in the ward-lattice during the final inspection. An anomaly. A thread of the great song that didn't belong—not a flaw, precisely, but an addition, a verse woven in by another hand, subtle as a smuggler's false bottom. He'd brought it to his superiors. His superiors had brought it to the temple. And three days later he'd been exiled for negligence, and the anomaly had been quietly absorbed into the design rather than removed, and Brannoc had spent eleven years and roughly four thousand jugs of ale trying to decide whether that meant what it obviously meant.

Because you didn't add verses to a binding song unless you wanted the binding to do something it wasn't designed to do.

And now the stars were gone.

"Oi." A boot prodded his sacking. "Oi, bridge-man."

Brannoc opened one eye. Standing at the mouth of his arch, silhouetted against the morning crowd on the bank, were two figures. One was young, slight, wrapped in a borrowed coat too big for them, with ink-stained fingers and the wary posture of someone who had spent a lifetime being overlooked and had made an art of it. The other was younger still—a boy, fourteen maybe, with a scarf wound high over the lower half of his face and eyes that kept flicking to every temple livery on the bridge above.

"We've been looking for you," the ink-stained one said.

"People usually look *away* from me," Brannoc said. His voice came out gravelled. "It's one of my features."

"You're Brannoc Hale."

"That depends who's asking, and whether they've got a warrant."

"No warrant," the scribe said. "No guild. No temple. Just us." They glanced upward, at the crowd, at the black hole in the sky beyond the bridge's rail. "Though I'd argue we're the only authority left worth anything, given recent events."

Brannoc laughed, and it hurt, because laughing was a muscle and he'd let it atrophy. "Recent events. Right. Let me guess. The stars go out, and suddenly everyone remembers there's a disgraced ward-smith under Hollow Bridge who might know something. Where were you all eleven years ago?"

"Being sold into debt-bondage," the scribe said flatly. "I was six."

That took the wind out of him. Brannoc squinted at them properly now. Ink-stained fingers. A scribe's callus on the middle finger, deep and permanent. But underneath the grime and the fear, something else—he'd seen it a thousand times in apprentices, back when he'd been allowed to teach. The look of someone whose hands itched to make things true.

"Freya," the scribe said. "Freya Voss. And this is Quentin." The boy shifted, and the scarf slipped a fraction, and Brannoc saw the shape of his jaw, the set of his mouth—and something else, something that raised every hair on his neck. The boy had a *resonance*. Even standing silent, even terrified, he hummed faintly at the edge of hearing, the way a struck bell keeps murmuring after the sound seems done. Brannoc hadn't felt resonance like that since—

Since the last time he'd stood in a room with a true singer. Since the ward-closing, when the cantors had come to sing the keystone home.

"You're a choirboy," Brannoc said. "Or you were."

"He's a fugitive," Freya said. "As of two nights ago. As am I, as of last night—I burned my indenture ledger rather than hand him over. So we're not here on temple business, Master Hale. We're here because the wards are down, the stars are stolen, and you're the only person alive who might understand how both could happen at once."

* *

They sat under the arch while the crowd thinned and the preachers wore themselves hoarse, and Brannoc told them everything, because he was drunk enough to be honest and sober enough to mean it, which was a narrow window he rarely occupied.

He told them about the anomaly in the lattice. He drew it in the mud with a stick—the ward-song rendered as interlocking lines, the way smiths actually saw it, not the pretty diagrams in the guild manuals. "A ward isn't a wall," he said. "Everyone thinks wall. Wall's wrong. A ward is a *promise*, held in tension. Stone on one side, song on the other, and the god's attention stretched between them like a rope bridge. Long as the god keeps attending, the promise holds. The border wards were the biggest promise ever made—nine hundred miles of covenant, anchored at seven keystones, fed by—" He stopped.

"Fed by what?" Freya asked. Their eyes had gone sharp. Scribe's eyes, hungry for the missing line in a ledger.

"The choir." Brannoc rubbed his face. "Every temple choir in the realm, singing the maintenance verses at vespers, every single day. That's what the hymns are *for*, girl—or boy—or whatever I'm clumsily failing to ask. Nobody remembers. The temple certainly doesn't teach it. They think the singing is worship. It's not. Or it is, but it's also *work*. Mortal voices feed the gods; the gods hold the wards and hang the stars. That was the covenant. That was the whole arrangement, older than the city, older than writing. And the choir has been getting quieter for decades—they cut singers, they shortened services, they simplified the verses. I flagged it in my inspection report. *The lattice is underfed,* I wrote. *The promise is being maintained on starvation rations.* Do you know what the temple did with that report?"

"They buried it," Freya said quietly.

"They buried it, and then they wove an extra verse into the keystone-song to compensate—a patch, a splint, holding the starving structure up artificially. And I want you to appreciate the craftsmanship, because whoever wrote that patch was brilliant, absolutely brilliant, and I have hated them with my entire soul for eleven years. Because a patch like that doesn't fail. It can't fail. It can only be *released*. Deliberately. From inside." He looked up at the black sky through the braces. "Someone in that temple decided to stop feeding the gods. And when they did, the whole covenant came down at once. Wards, stars, all of it. One mechanism."

Silence, except for the river and the muttering bridge overhead.

"The hymns changed," Quentin said.

Both of them turned. The boy had spoken for the first time, and his voice—even muffled by the scarf, even cracked with fear—landed in Brannoc's chest like a hammer on a tuning-fork. Gods. No wonder the temple wanted him. No wonder the temple wanted him *controlled*.

"I sang vespers for six years," Quentin went on, pulling the scarf down. "Older words got scraped off the hymn boards. Replaced. I thought it was just... revision. New cantor, new style. But some of the scraped words were the maintenance verses, weren't they. The feeding verses. He replaced them with silence dressed up as music."

"Him?" Brannoc said.

"The High Cantor," Freya said. "Ovelith."

Brannoc sat with that. Ovelith. He'd met him once, years ago—young then, ambitious, a rising voice in the temple hierarchy, present at some ceremony or other. Brannoc mostly remembered the man's hands. Beautiful hands. A musician's hands. Hands that would have been wasted on anything less than the total refashioning of heaven.

"And your plan," Brannoc said slowly, "is what? Walk into the holiest fortress in the realm, where the most powerful man alive is sitting on a hoard of stolen starlight, and... sing at him?"

"Our plan," Freya said, "is to reforge the covenant. Properly. Not the temple's version—the real one. Which requires three things, according to a fragment I recovered: a true voice, a willing scribe, and the First Hearth." They ticked them off on ink-stained fingers. "We have a true voice. I'm told I'm the scribe. What we don't have—" and here their composure flickered, just for a moment, and Brannoc saw the exhausted, terrified twenty-something underneath the sharp ledger-keeper's mask— "is anyone who understands how the old covenant was physically built. The hearth is a ruin, Brannoc. Cold for centuries. Someone has to teach us how promises are made of stone and song. Someone who's done it before. Someone who knows exactly how they come apart, because he watched his own work get unmade."

"I watched it get *unmade* last night," Brannoc said bitterly. "I didn't watch anything. I was under a bridge."

"Yes," Freya said. "You were. For eleven years." They leaned forward, and their voice dropped into something almost gentle, which was somehow worse than if they'd shouted. "I read your inspection report. It survives in the archive annex—I spent fifteen years copying archives, Master Hale, I know what's in them. Your report on the choir deficit was accurate, detailed, and correct. The temple called it negligence and destroyed your life for telling the truth. And then you came here and drank yourself half to death under a bridge, and I have to tell you, as someone who has also spent years making themselves invisible so the world couldn't hurt them—" a breath— "*it doesn't work.* Invisibility doesn't keep the stars in the sky. It doesn't keep the wards standing. It just means that when the catastrophe comes, you're drunk under a bridge instead of standing where you're needed."

The words went into him like a chisel finding the fault-line. He wanted to throw the jug at them. He wanted to weep. He did neither.

"You don't know what they took from me," he said.

"I know exactly what they took from you," Freya said. "They took your name. I'm going to get it back. That's rather the point of the whole enterprise."

* *

He made them wait while he thought, which was to say while he walked to the taphouse, bought a final jug with his last coin, carried it back under the bridge, and then—deliberately, ceremonially, with the gravity of a man performing a rite—poured the entire thing out into the mud.

Quentin watched the ale soak away and said, "Was that necessary?"

"It was a funeral," Brannoc said. "Mine. The one I should have held eleven years ago." He rolled his shoulders, and something in his spine crackled, and beneath the ache and the tremor and the shame he felt, faintly, absurdly, like sun through fog, the old itch in his hands. The maker's itch. Eleven years dormant and waking up furious.

He dug through the debris of his arch until he found it, wrapped in oilcloth behind a loose stone: his singing-hammer. Guild-marked, temple-inspected, confiscated-in-absentia and never retrieved because no one had thought a drunk exile worth robbing. The head was silvered steel, rune-scored, balanced for a two-handed swing. He hefted it and his arms remembered the weight and his heart broke a little more, and healed a little, in the same motion.

"First Hearth," he said. "Where is it?"

"The mountains," Freya said. "North-east. A shrine called Vaelmoor, past Ashford Crossing. I have maps. Partial."

"Partial maps, a fugitive choirboy, a debt-burned scribe, and a drunkard with a hammer." Brannoc slung the tool across his back and looked up one last time at the obscene, starless sky. "Well. I've built worse teams into better ones. When do we leave?"

"Tonight," Freya said. "Before the temple widens its search. There's a smuggler route through the—"

They stopped. All three of them stopped, because the bridge above had gone quiet—not empty, but quiet in the specific way a crowd goes quiet when it's listening to something. And threading down through the timber, faint and impossible, came a sound from the far end of the span.

Singing. A single voice, high and clear and utterly untrained, drifting from somewhere near the bridge's northern foot. A child's voice, or close to it, singing no hymn Brannoc knew—an old melody, or the bones of one, half-remembered, made up on the spot, rising toward the hole in the sky like a question.

And in the east, low over the hills where the Wanderer should have been, something answered.

It wasn't a star. Not yet. It was a *flicker*—a pulse of light, there and gone, there and gone, keeping imperfect time with the song, like a heart remembering how to beat.

Under the bridge, Brannoc Hale, exiled ward-smith, felt every ward-lattice he had ever built shiver in sympathy down the length of his spine, and heard himself say, in a voice rough with eleven years of dust:

"Gods above. It's still listening. It's *still listening*."

Beside him, Quentin pulled his scarf down entirely, and his eyes were wet and blazing, and he said, "Then we'd better give it something worth hearing."
