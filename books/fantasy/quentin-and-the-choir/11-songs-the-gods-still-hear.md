## 11. Songs the Gods Still Hear

The shrine found them before they found it.

They had been walking three days since Hollow Bridge, following a road that was barely a road anymore—old covenant-road, Brannoc called it, though he said it the way a man says the name of an ex-wife, with a flinch buried somewhere under the word. The land on either side had gone strange since the stars went out. Not dead. Strange. Crops stood in fields with their leaves curled inward, as if in prayer or in hiding. Rivers ran clear but silent, without the chatter of frogs or the slap of fish. And overhead, where the sky should have held its thousand watchful lights, there was only a smooth black nothing, like a ceiling painted by someone who had never seen night.

Quentin kept looking up anyway. He couldn't help it. Fifteen years in the temple choir had taught him to orient himself by the rose window, by the light falling through colored glass at particular hours; now he oriented himself by absence, by the hole where heaven used to be.

"You'll trip," Freya said, not looking up from the charm they were stitching into the hem of his coat—a small thing of thread and river-glass that was supposed to keep his footsteps quiet. They'd made four of them already, one for each of them, and worked on them the way other people worried bread: constantly, compulsively, because stopping would mean thinking.

"I never trip."

"You tripped twice yesterday."

"Once," Quentin said, "and the ground moved."

Brannoc snorted from up ahead, where he walked with his ward-smith's kit clanking against his hip like a guilty conscience. "Ground's been moving for fifteen years, boy. We just didn't notice till the sky went out." He said it lightly, but Quentin had learned to hear what lived under Brannoc's lightness—the same way he'd once heard what lived under the High Cantor's sermons, all those years of listening for the note beneath the note.

It was Brannoc who stopped first, mid-stride, so suddenly that Freya nearly walked into his back.

"What?" Freya asked.

Brannoc didn't answer. He was staring off the road to the east, at a fold of hillside overgrown with blackthorn, and his face had gone through several expressions in quick succession—recognition, disbelief, and then something older and sadder than either.

"That's not possible," he said.

* * *

The shrine had no name that any of them knew, which Brannoc said was impossible, because every shrine had a name, because naming was half of what a shrine *was*. It sat in a hollow between two hills like a cupped hand, its walls of pale stone furred with moss, its roof long collapsed into a lacework of beams and bird nests. Wild roses had taken the place of gardeners, climbing through the empty windows and dragging briars across what had once been a processional path. At the center, half-swallowed by greenery, stood a basin of white stone—and above it, set into the surviving arc of the eastern wall, a ring of empty sockets carved in the shape of stars.

Nine sockets. Nine hollows where starlight had once been set into the stone like jewels.

"Way-shrine," Brannoc said quietly. He had taken off his hat, which Quentin had never seen him do, not even under the bridge. "Covenant-era. There were hundreds of them along the old roads. Travelers would sing at them and the star-stones would—" He stopped. His jaw worked. "They'd answer. That was the whole point. You sang, and heaven answered back, and you knew the road you walked was watched over."

"And now?" Freya asked.

"Now it's just rocks." But he didn't put his hat back on, and he didn't move from the doorway, and when Freya brushed past him into the shrine he let them go as if moving through water.

Inside, the air was different. Quentin felt it immediately, the way you feel the difference between a room where someone has just been weeping and one where someone has just been laughing—not a temperature, exactly, more a pressure, a quality of attention. The moss was soft underfoot. Dust hung in the light that came through the broken roof in slow golden columns, and the dust did not swirl. It hung as if listening.

Freya crouched at the basin and ran their fingers along its rim, and Quentin saw them go very still.

"There's residue," they said. "Old ward-work. This wasn't decoration, Quentin. These walls were tuned. Someone built this place the way you build an instrument." They looked up at him, and in the green-filtered light their eyes had an expression he was learning to dread and crave in equal measure: the look of a scribe who has found a page that shouldn't exist. "You should sing here."

"No," said Brannoc, from the doorway.

"It's a ruin, Brannoc."

"It's a ruin with *ears*." Brannoc came inside at last, boots crunching on fallen tile, and jabbed a thick finger at the ring of empty sockets. "You don't know what answered these things before. I helped build the border wards, girl. I know what the gods' attention feels like when it lands on you. It doesn't land gently. It lands like weather."

"The gods are dead," Freya said. "Everyone says so. The temple says so. If they're dead, what's the harm?"

"If they're dead," Brannoc said, "then why are you whispering?"

Quentin hadn't realized he was. His voice had dropped without his permission, down into the register he used in the choir's upper gallery when the Cantor was listening too closely—the register that carried to the rafters and to nothing else. He looked at the ring of empty sockets. Nine dark hollows in pale stone, each one the size of his two fists together, each one shaped precisely like the missing stars.

He thought about the hymnal in the temple's upper choir, the old one, the one with words scraped away. He thought about how the scraping had been done carefully, reverently even, the way you'd handle something you feared rather than something you despised. He thought about the High Cantor's face during vespers, eyes closed, listening—not to Quentin, Quentin understood now, but *through* him, the way you press your ear to a wall to hear what's happening in the next room.

"I want to try," he said.

"Boy—"

"You said it yourself. You built the wards. You'd know if singing here was dangerous." Quentin turned to face the old man fully. "Would it?"

Brannoc's mouth opened. Closed. He looked at the sockets, at the basin, at the patient listening dust, and Quentin watched thirty years of guilt and drink and self-imposed deafness war behind his face.

"I don't know," Brannoc said finally, and it seemed to cost him enormously to say it. "That's the truth. I don't know. Nothing like this has been sung in since the wards went up, and the wards went up before I was born." He dragged a hand down his beard. "Sing something small, then. Not the high stuff. Whatever you do, don't reach."

Quentin nodded. But privately, standing there in the green silence with the empty stars watching him, he knew he was lying.

* * *

They made camp in the shrine that night, because none of them could bear to leave it. Freya rigged charms at the four compass points—small ones, Quentin noticed, deliberately small, the magical equivalent of speaking in a library—and Brannoc built a fire in the basin itself, muttering apologies to nobody. They ate hard cheese and apples Maren had packed, and Quentin caught himself doing the math again: three days out of Corvane, Maren a day behind them with the cart, Sera Vane somewhere behind *her*, and ahead of them nothing but mountains and a hearth that had gone cold centuries ago.

He lay on his bedroll and looked up through the broken roof at the blank sky.

In the temple, he had never been allowed to look at the sky while singing. The choir loft faced inward; the windows faced the altar. He had spent fifteen years pouring his voice toward a god whose face he was never permitted to see, and he had told himself this was humility, and mostly he had believed it. Now, lying in a ruin with no roof and no rules, he understood that it had also been a kind of theft. Somebody had decided, a long time ago, that the singers shouldn't see what they were singing to.

"Quentin." Freya's voice, low, from the other side of the dying fire. "You're doing the thing."

"What thing?"

"The thing where your face goes somewhere else and the rest of you stays here being annoying about it."

Despite everything, Quentin smiled. "I'm going to sing," he said. "Before we sleep. Just—something small. Like Brannoc said."

Freya sat up. In the ember-light their face was all planes and shadows, the scribe's ink stains still visible on their fingers even after three days of scrubbing. "You don't have to prove anything."

"I'm not proving anything." And that was true too, though not in the way Freya meant. He wasn't proving anything to them. He was proving something to himself—that the voice the temple had prized and policed and finally tried to silence still belonged to him. That it could be given instead of taken. That somewhere in the world there was still a listener worth singing to, even if that listener was only moss and stone and memory.

Brannoc turned his back pointedly, which Quentin chose to interpret as permission.

He sat up. He breathed the way Master Ellery had taught him, fifteen years ago, in a practice cell that smelled of candlewax: from the floor of himself, slow, until the breath became a column and the column became a pillar and the pillar held up something larger than his body. He chose the song without deciding to—it rose in him the way water rises in a well when you've finally hit the vein. It was the oldest thing he knew. A cradle-hymn, really, the first one every choirchild learned, four notes rising and falling like breathing:

*Lay you down, the day is done,*
*the shepherds home, the stars begun—*

His voice filled the shrine.

He kept it small, the way he'd promised. He didn't reach, didn't push, didn't open the door in his chest all the way—just cracked it, just enough for the melody to slip through. The song moved through the ruin and the ruin *took* it. He felt that happen, physically, the way you feel a handshake: the moss drank the sound, the stones gave it back warmer, the dust in the moonless air began at last to move, turning in slow spirals above the basin as if the song had weight and the air had decided to carry it.

Four notes, rising and falling. Lay you down, the day is done.

He sang it through once. Twice. On the third pass he stopped holding the door shut at all—not reaching, never reaching, but no longer pretending he wasn't standing at the threshold—and the last line left him and went up through the broken roof into the blind black sky, and he watched it go, and nothing happened, and he felt foolish, and he lowered his head—

—and the third socket from the left flickered.

Not blazed. Flickered. A single pulse of light in the empty star-shaped hollow, faint as a match struck at the far end of a cathedral, blue-white and trembling, there and then gone and then there again, keeping time—Quentin's whole body went rigid—keeping time with the echo of his last note still fading in the stones.

"Freya," he whispered. He couldn't make his voice work properly. "Freya. Look. *Look.*"

Freya was already looking. They had come up onto their knees without seeming to move, and their face in the flickering light was stripped of everything—every wry defense, every careful blankness—down to something raw and young. "That's not reflection," they said. Their voice shook. "There's no light source. There's nothing to reflect off. Quentin, that's coming from *inside the socket*, that's—"

The light pulsed again. Stronger. And with it came something else—not sound, not quite, but the sensation of sound, the way you feel a bell through a wall. A pressure against the ear. Against the chest. Against some third organ Quentin had no name for, the one that had ached ever since the stars went out, the one he'd assumed was grief.

And through that pressure, faint as a voice through fathoms of water, came a single syllable of attention.

It was not a word. It was older than words. It was the feeling of being looked at by something vast and exhausted and *grateful*, and it lasted less than a heartbeat, and Quentin would spend the rest of his life trying to describe it and never succeed. It was the feeling of a drowning man feeling a rope brush his hand. It was the feeling of a locked door hearing a key turn in the lock on the other side.

Then the light guttered, steadied, dimmed to an ember-glow no brighter than a firefly, and held there—faint, stubborn, alive—in the hollow where a star should have been.

Nobody moved. The fire crackled. Somewhere outside, for the first time in three days, a frog spoke—one tentative croak, then another, as if remembering an old skill.

"Gods below," Brannoc breathed. He had turned around at some point; his hat was crushed in his fist. "It answered. By the nine, it actually—" His voice broke off into something that might have been a laugh or a sob. "We were told they died. We were *told*—"

"They didn't die." Quentin heard his own voice as if from far away, steady and strange and absolutely certain. "They're muffled. Wrapped up. Smothered under something." He was thinking of the scraped hymnal, of the silent hymn the Cantor had demanded, of a man pressing his ear to a wall while he stuffed wool into the mouths of everyone on the other side. "You can't smother something unless it's still breathing."

Freya reached out slowly and touched the rim of the glowing socket, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal, ready to withdraw. The light warmed under their fingers—or leaned into them, Quentin thought, the way a cat leans—and Freya made a small sound low in their throat.

"It's grieving," they whispered. "I can feel it. It's been alone in the dark for—how long? Weeks? Months?" They looked up at Quentin, and their eyes were wet, and they didn't seem to know it or care. "It heard you. Out of everything in the world, it heard *you*."

Quentin knelt beside the socket. Up close, the glow resolved into something finer than flame—a shimmer, a stirring, like light caught in moving water. And in it, impossibly, he could almost see shapes: towers, roads, faces, a city wearing its stars like a crown. Everything the light remembered. Everything it had been taken away from.

"We're going to give it back," he said. He didn't decide to say it. It simply arrived, the way the song had arrived, the way true things arrive—already complete. "All of it. Every star. We're going to unwrapped them, unsmother them, whatever the Cantor did, we're going to undo it."

"Quentin." Freya's voice was gentle and frightened at once. "Do you understand what you're saying? The Cantor took the stars with the entire temple behind him. We're four people and a vegetable cart."

"Five," Quentin said. "Maren's coming. She'll kill us if we leave her out." And then, because the light was pulsing faintly against his palm like a second heartbeat, like something clinging: "Besides. It's not four people. Did you feel it? We've got at least one god on our side. Even if it's a small one. Even if all it can do right now is flicker."

The socket pulsed. Once. Twice. And Quentin would have sworn on anything he had left—which was his voice, and after tonight he was no longer sure even that belonged to him—that the rhythm of it matched laughter.

Brannoc sat down heavily by the fire and put his hat back on, backwards, without noticing. "Right," he said, to the flames, to the frog, to the ruins of his certainties. "Right. Then we'd best get to the mountain before whatever answered him decides to answer us louder. Because mark me—" he pointed his chin at the glowing socket, and his hand was not quite steady— "attention like that doesn't stay private. Not in the old stories. You ring a bell in the dark, boy, everyone hears it. *Everything* hears it."

Quentin was still looking at the little light. At the small, muffled, breathing god in its hollow of stone, keeping time with his heart.

"Good," he said.

He meant it. Hope had entered him like a lit lamp entering a locked room, and it was terrifying, and it was bright, and it showed him every corner of the ruin including the corners he'd rather not see—how few they were, how far the mountain was, how loud the bell had rung.

Three days later, on a ridge overlooking the Ashford Crossing, a woman in temple grey would lower her spyglass and write in her report: *the fugitives camped at a way-shrine. Unexplained luminescence observed. Recommend immediate interception.*

But that night, Quentin slept better than he had in fifteen years, under a roofless sky, beside a star that glowed like a promise: small, and stubborn, and awake.
