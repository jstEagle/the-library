## 12. Inquisitor Sera Vane

The trail went cold at the Ashford road and then, three miles on, went warm again in a way that made Sera Vane's teeth ache.

She crouched beside the wheel-rut where the mud held the print of a boot too fine for a vegetable-seller's cart driver—leather stitched in the temple style, choir-issue—and beside it, faint as breath on glass, a residue of charm-work that prickled against her trained senses like static before lightning. Someone had warded this cart. Not clumsily. Not desperately. *Well.*

"Vane." Corporal Doss stood at the treeline, sweating through his grey despite the morning chill. "Nothing. Again."

"You're looking for people," Sera said. She rose, brushing grit from her knee. "Look for what they touched. People lie. Mud doesn't."

Doss had been temple guard for eleven years and still flinched when she spoke plainly about evidence, as though deduction were a form of blasphemy. Most of them did. The Order of the Veiled Word taught that truth was not found but *received*, handed down from the Cantor's office like bread rationed to the faithful. Sera had been receiving it since she was six years old, when the sisters had pulled her out of a famine-orphan row in the Lowward district because she could recite a catechism after hearing it once and could pick a lock with a hairpin after watching someone else fail to.

Twenty-two years of service. Nine as an inquisitor. Forty-one souls brought in for unlicensed craft, every one of them processed according to rite: confession, penance, binding or burning depending on severity.

She had never once doubted a warrant.

She was trying very hard not to start now.

* * *

The fugitives were two, per the writ: a scribe named Freya Voss, indentured to the Temple since childhood, wanted for heresy and the practice of deep craft; and Quentin Marsh, last of the unbroken voices, fled vespers four nights ago and presumed taken by the scribe. The writ used the word *rescued* for the boy. It used the word *cleansed* for the woman.

Sera had read the writ six times on the ride from Corvane, and each time her eye snagged on the same seam: the dates. The border wards fell on the night of the Dark Moon. The stars went out the same hour. And the writ authorizing pursuit of Voss was sealed—the wax still soft in her memory pouch—*one hour before* the wards fell.

Warrants took days to prepare. Names, evidence, countersignatures. She had drafted enough of them herself to know the machinery's pace. A seal cut an hour before catastrophe meant either prophecy—which the temple officially did not recognize—or foreknowledge.

Which meant planning.

Which meant someone inside had known.

She told herself there were explanations. There were always explanations; the world was generous with them. But explanations were like coins, and you learned to bite each one.

"Corporal," she said. "Set the men along the hedgerow. I want the next mile walked, not ridden. Cart tracks, hoofprints, anything burned, blessed, or bleeding."

"And if we find them?"

"Then we find them." She pulled her gloves on, grey leather gone soft at the fingers from twenty years of gripping things. "No one dies today unless they make it necessary."

Doss hesitated. "The directive says—"

"I've read the directive, Corporal." She had read it eleven times, too, and it was the eleventh reading that had turned her stomach. *Apprehend if practicable.* Four words doing terrible work. Every other warrant she'd carried said *apprehend*, full stop, because inquisitors were the temple's hands and hands caught things; they didn't crush them unless ordered. This one read like it had been written by someone who hoped the fugitives wouldn't survive the journey back to Corvane. Dead witnesses made tidy stories.

She hadn't shared that observation with anyone. Some doubts you carried like a stone in the boot: painful, private, and proof with every step that something was wrong.

* * *

They found the shrine at noon.

It wasn't marked on any map Sera knew—a wayside chapel older than the temple's current dynasty, sunk into a hillside with its door hanging open like a slack mouth. The men wanted to pass it by; shrines were everywhere in the countryside, most of them empty since the stars went out, since folk decided the gods had stopped listening and stopped bothering to knock.

But the dust inside had been disturbed, and Sera had never in her life walked past disturbed dust.

Inside: cold hearth, broken pews, a roof half-open to a sky that was wrong in the way it had been wrong for five days now—not black, exactly, but *blank*, a ceiling of nothing where the stars should have hung. Sera had grown up under those stars. In the orphan dormitory at Lowward, Sister Anselm used to say the stars were the gods' open eyes, watching over even the smallest sinners. Sera had fallen asleep every night of her childhood feeling watched, and had hated it, and had missed it terribly these last five nights, the way you miss a weight removed from your chest only after you realize the chest was being held together by it.

The disturbance led to the altar. Kneeling prints in the dust—two sets, one small and light, one broader. Candle stubs, burnt down. And on the altar itself, chalk.

Chalk marks. Craft-marks. Sera knew the canonical ones from her training—the warding circle, the severance sigil, the names of the Nine in their approved forms. These weren't those. These were older, angular, written in a hand that moved fast and sure, and around them the air still hummed faintly, the way a bell hums after the clapper falls still.

"Heresy," Doss said behind her, with relief, as though labeling it settled it. "Deep craft, plain as sin. We should mark the site and report."

"Yes," Sera said, though she was looking at the marks and thinking something else entirely, which was: *these are ward-craft. Old ward-craft. The kind they don't teach anymore because the wards already stand.*

And then she saw the third thing on the altar, and stopped thinking altogether for a moment.

A hymnal. Temple issue, choir edition, the spine stamped with the Veiled Word's ninefold knot. It had been left behind—or dropped, or hidden under the chalk where only someone searching would find it. Sera opened it with gloved hands.

Pages had been scraped.

Not torn—scraped, carefully, with a blade, the old words lifted off the vellum and new ones inked above the ghosts of them. She tilted the book toward the light falling through the broken roof. Under the current text of the Vespers canticle—the one every choir in Corvane had sung for the last year, the one Quentin Marsh had refused to sing, according to the report from his flight—she could see the shadow of what had been there before. Older words. Words she half-remembered from childhood services, before some revision or other, before the liturgy was *harmonized* the way everything got harmonized eventually.

Except harmonization didn't require scraping knives. You printed a new edition. You retired the old books gently, with ceremony. You did not send someone through the choir library erasing the past out of the canon like a sinner scrubbing a stain.

Unless the past was inconvenient.

Unless someone might someday *read* it.

"Madam Inquisitor?" Doss again, at the door. "Rider coming up the lane. Temple colors."

She closed the hymnal. For one absurd moment she considered hiding it inside her coat, and the moment told her more about herself than she wanted audited. She put it in her evidence satchel instead, properly, in full view of God and the blank sky, and told herself the difference mattered.

It was getting harder to tell herself things.

* * *

The rider was a courier from the Corvane chapterhouse, young, wind-burnt, carrying a dispatch case chained to his wrist. Sera signed for it, dismissed him, and broke the seal standing alone in the shrine's doorway while the men watered the horses.

Two documents.

The first was a status order: the pursuit was to accelerate. The fugitives were believed headed toward the hill country, toward the old pilgrimage roads. All inquisitors were instructed that the boy Quentin Marsh was to be recovered *unharmed*—his voice was temple property, the order said, consecrated asset—and the scribe Voss was to be dealt with *as circumstances required*. No escort detail was assigned for prisoners. No provisions. As if no one expected there to be prisoners.

The second document was shorter, and it was the one that made her sit down on a broken pew in front of her own corporal like a recruit with weak knees.

It was a list. Six names, hers first. Inquisitors currently assigned to the Voss-March matter. And beneath the names, in the High Cantor's own cipher—the private cipher, the one used for matters touching the Ninefold Office—one line:

*Upon resolution, all participants to submit to rite of forgetting. No exceptions.*

A rite of forgetting. Memory-burning. It existed; every inquisitor learned of it in the final year of training, the way soldiers learn where the officers' graves are. It was reserved for catastrophes—for the time the Third Ward cracked and the temple erased the whole garrison rather than admit a failure. Sera had always understood it as the temple's deepest shame, invoked once in a century, a wound dressed with amnesia.

Now it was standard procedure. For the hunters themselves. Whatever they found out here—whatever they *learned*—was to be cut out of them like a tumor, and they would walk back into Corvane smiling, hollow, certain of nothing except their orders.

She looked at the line a long time.

Doss was watching her. "Bad news, Madam?"

"The weather," she said, and folded the papers away, and marveled at how easily the lie came, how many lies she must have told before this one without noticing, how a career built on extracting truth from others could be built so comfortably on withholding it from everyone including yourself.

* * *

They picked the trail up again at dusk, and lost it again an hour later, and camped in a hollow with the horses staked and the men grumbling over cold rations. Sera took first watch, which surprised no one; she always took first watch. Vigilance was the virtue they'd bred into her earliest. Watch, and you will be safe. Watch, and you will be worthy. Watch, and you will never be the hungry child in the Lowward row again.

She sat with her back against a stone and opened the stolen hymnal across her knees, and by the light of a shielded lamp she began, methodically, to read the scraped pages.

Under the new canticle, the old one emerged word by word, and it was not a variation. It was not a revision. It was a different doctrine entire.

The new version—the one sung in every temple for the past year—taught that the gods were distant, silent, *finished*; that their work was done and the covenant complete, requiring only maintenance, obedience, and the intercession of the temple. Standard modern teaching. Sera had memorized it at eight.

The scraped-away version said otherwise. It said the covenant was *alive*, renewed continuously, fed by mortal voice—that the gods were not finished but *listening*, and that silence between realms was not completion but starvation. It named the singers' duty explicitly: not performance, but *provision*. The choir did not sing for the gods. The choir sang *to* them, and what passed between was not music.

Food. It was food. Or light. Or whatever a god ate.

Sera sat very still in the dark with the lamp hooded and thought about the stars going out all at once, in an instant, like lamps snuffed by a single hand—and about the wards falling the same night, the wards that had stood for centuries, and about a warrant sealed an hour before either happened, and about a list of hunters scheduled to forget everything they found.

You didn't erase the old words unless the old words were true.

You didn't blind the sky unless you needed the sky not to see.

And you didn't steal a god's light unless you intended to spend it.

Her hands were shaking. She watched them shake with a strange detachment, as though they belonged to a novice, as though the woman holding the lamp were someone she was interrogating. *Tell me, Inquisitor Vane. When did you begin to suspect? What did you see? Who gave the order?* Twenty-two years of training, and it all worked just as well pointed inward. That was the joke no one had warned her about: give a person the tools to break anyone, and one day they'll use them on the person in the mirror.

The terrifying thing was not that the High Cantor might be guilty. Men were guilty; she had built a life on that bedrock. The terrifying thing was that Ovelith might be *right*—that he might believe, with the full shining conviction she'd seen in his eyes at her induction, that he was saving the world, and that the machine she'd served for two decades would grind forward to help him do it, gratefully, singing.

She thought of the fugitives. The scribe who wrote charms in a night market for fifteen years without ever once, per the records, harming a soul—fifteen years of quiet crime that hurt no one, which meant fifteen years of discipline most ordained priests couldn't manage. And the boy. The last voice. Consecrated asset. Property.

*Rescued,* the writ said. From whom? He'd run from the temple. You couldn't rescue a man from his rescuers.

Far off across the hills, in the direction the cart tracks had gone, something flickered at the edge of the dark. Once. Twice. Like a star remembering itself, or like a charm flaring in a fugitive's hand, or like hope—though Sera had been trained out of hoping long ago and distrusted the sensation the way she'd distrust any contraband.

She closed the hymnal.

"Corporal," she said quietly, and Doss looked up from the fire. "At first light, we continue the pursuit."

"Good," he said. "They can't have far to go."

"No," Sera agreed. "They can't."

She wrapped the hymnal in oilcloth and tucked it beneath her coat after all, against her ribs, where the temple's seals couldn't reach it, and told herself she was preserving evidence.

Evidence. Yes. That was the word she'd keep using, right up until the day she had to decide what it was actually for—and somewhere out in the black and starless hills, the people she was hunting were sleeping in shifts beside a vegetable cart, and one of them, the last voice in Corvane, was dreaming songs that gods could eat.

Sera Vane kept watch, as she had every night of her life, and for the first time she could not have said whom she was keeping it for.
