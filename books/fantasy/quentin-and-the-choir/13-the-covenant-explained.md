## 13. The Covenant Explained

Brannoc had built the border wards with his own two hands, and for thirty years he had told himself he'd only helped.

That was the lie he'd carried under the bridge like a stone in his boot—small enough to walk on, sharp enough to bleed him slow every time he thought about it too long. *I only helped.* He'd cut the stone, yes. He'd set the ward-anchors into the mountain passes at Hollow Bridge and Greyfen and the Salt Stair, yes. He'd sung the binding verses with the temple's own cantors standing beside him, their voices rising into the cold air while his hands still shook from the work of it. But he hadn't *designed* it. He hadn't decided what the wards would be fed on. That decision had been made a hundred feet above his head, by men in grey robes who smiled at him and called him *master smith* and never once told him what he was really building.

He was drunk when Freya found him, but not as drunk as he wanted to be, and that turned out to matter.

Now they sat around a fire in the ruined shrine where Quentin had sung three nights ago—the one where something had answered, a flicker in the dark where a star should have been—and Brannoc held a piece of chalk in fingers that hadn't drawn a ward-line in fifteen years, and they were all looking at him like he was going to explain the end of the world.

"You said you helped build them," Freya said. Not unkindly. Freya was never unkind, exactly; they were just economical with kindness the way a poor man is economical with coin. "The wards. So tell us what they were."

Brannoc looked at the chalk. Looked at the fire. Looked at the boy—Quentin, seventeen years old maybe, wrapped in Maren's spare cloak, watching him with those choirboy eyes that made Brannoc feel like a man being audited by something holy.

"All right," he said. "But nobody's going to like it."

* * *

"Everyone thinks wards are walls," he began, and drew a line in the ash at the edge of the firelight. "Walls keep things out. That's what a wall is for. But the border wards weren't walls. They were—" He stopped. Started again. His voice came out rougher than he meant it to, and not just from the drink. "They were hands. Holding on."

"Holding on to what?" Sera asked. She sat apart from the rest, the way she always did, her inquisitor's coat folded beside her like a shed skin she wasn't sure she'd finished shedding. Even defected, even half-frozen and half-forgiven, she asked questions like an interrogation. Brannoc found he didn't mind. Questions were honest, at least.

"The sky," he said. "The stars. The gods."

Silence, except for the fire.

"You have to understand how old this is," he went on, because if he stopped now he might never start again. "Older than Corvane. Older than the temple, though the temple will gut you for saying so. Back before anyone wrote anything down—or almost back—people noticed something. The gods were real, but they were *thin*. They'd push against the world and the world barely noticed. A god could whisper in a dream and you'd wake up thinking it was your own idea. They couldn't hold anything. Couldn't keep a promise, couldn't keep a border, couldn't keep a star burning steady in its place. Stars wandered. Winters came wrong. Things from outside the dark came *in*."

He drew a circle in the ash. Then, inside it, small marks—dots, like seeds.

"So someone—some singer, some nameless fool with a voice like a bell—worked out the bargain. The first covenant. Mortals would sing to them. Not prayers. Prayers are asking. This was *feeding*. Every voice raised in true devotion, every hymn sung honestly, every lullaby a mother meant with her whole chest—that all flowed up, somehow, along lines nobody fully understood, and the gods drank it, and in exchange they held things. They held the stars in their courses so the seasons stayed sane. They held the border between what's inside the world and what's outside it. They held the dark off us." He tapped the circle. "Voices in. Holding out. That was the covenant. That was the whole shape of it."

"And the temple knew this," Sera said. It wasn't quite a question.

"The temple *was* this, once," Brannoc said. "Before it rotted. The choirs weren't decoration, girl. The upper choir, the one your choirboy here sang in—that was the engine room. The great hymns at solstice, the vespers, the dawn-song—they weren't for the congregation. The congregation was the fuel. Every honest voice in that hall fed the covenant whether they knew it or not. The Cantor's job, way back, was just... tuning. Keeping the song clean. Making sure nothing false got mixed in, because false singing feeds nothing—it's like drinking smoke."

Quentin had gone very still. Brannoc saw it happen—the boy's face doing the thing faces do when a beautiful thing turns out to have been a machine all along.

"I sang there my whole life," Quentin said quietly. "Nobody ever told me what it was *for*."

"No," Brannoc agreed. "They wouldn't. Knowing changes the singing. A man who knows he's hauling water pumps harder and means it less, and the meaning is the whole point. So they kept it secret. Kept it sacred. And secrets rot." He rubbed his face with a hand scarred white across the knuckles from old stone-work. "Which brings us to your High Cantor."

* * *

He told them about Ovelith the way he'd pieced it together—not from confession, because he'd never been close enough to power to hear confessions, but from the edges. From the orders that came down. From the cantors who drank too much and talked. From the last time he'd stood in the temple's undercroft, fifteen years ago, and seen the thing that ended his career and started his exile.

"Fifteen years back, I was summoned to Corvane. Not the public temple—the deep parts. There's a chamber under the choir floor that doesn't appear on any plan, and I know, because I've seen every plan. They brought me down blindfolded and took the blindfold off in front of the anchor-stone. The master anchor. The point where all the feeding-lines from every choir in the realm converge." He paused. "You could feel it. Like standing next to a river underground. You couldn't hear it but your teeth knew it was there."

"What did it look like?" Maren asked. She was mending a strap while he talked, hands busy, eyes sharp. Brannoc had decided in the first two days that Maren Ostell was the most dangerous person in their little company, because she was the only one who refused to be impressed by anything, including the end of the world.

"Like a well of light with a lid on it," he said. "And the lid was new. Fresh-cut stone, mortar still pale. Somebody had capped the anchor." He looked around the fire. "I asked why. That was my mistake. I asked why you'd cap the well that everything drinks from, and a cantor I'd known twenty years looked at me with such *pity*, and said, 'Master Brannoc, the flow is being redirected to higher purposes.' Higher purposes. And then they escorted me out, and my commissions stopped coming, and within the year I was exiled for 'irregularities' in ward-work I'd done perfectly, and I understood that either I left quiet or I didn't leave at all."

"So the Cantor dammed the river," Freya said. Their voice was flat and fast, the scribe's voice, the one that came out when they were arranging facts into rows. "Fifteen years ago. He stopped the feeding. But the wards kept working—for a while—because the gods had reserves. Stored light. Stored strength. And over fifteen years the reserves ran down, and the wards got thin, and thin wards fail, and everyone called it a collapse. An accident."

"Not an accident," Brannoc said. "A *draining*. He didn't break the wards. He starved them. And every drop the gods couldn't spend holding the borders and turning the stars—" he snapped his fingers, and the sound cracked loud in the shrine, "—went somewhere. Into whatever vessel he built under that lid. Fifteen years of a nation's devotion, hoarded in the dark. And then, when he had enough, he took the rest. The stars didn't go out, friends. They were *harvested*. The wards didn't fall. They were *unmade*, from the inside, by the man whose job was to maintain them."

Sera had stopped pretending to sit apart. She was leaning forward now, elbows on knees, and her face was the face of a woman watching her whole life reorganize itself around a new center of gravity. "The order came down the night it happened," she said slowly. "Seal the city. Blame smugglers. Blame hedge-mages. Find someone to punish *publicly*." She laughed, short and ugly. "We were the mop. We were sent out to clean up a crime we were never told was a crime."

"That's how it works," Brannoc said. "It's how it always worked. The temple doesn't lie to you, girl. It just tells you a smaller truth than the one that's true."

* * *

Maren put down her mending. "So it's bad, and it's worse than we thought, and the man responsible believes he's a saint. Fine. We've established the disease. Somebody in this conversation is going to tell me there's a cure, or I'm going back to bed."

Brannoc almost smiled. Almost. "There's a cure."

"Then say it plain, for once in your life."

He picked up the chalk again. His hand shook, and he let it. "Reforging the covenant. Undoing what he did. It needs three things, and each one is worse than the last." He drew them in the ash: a mark like a mouth. A mark like a quill. A mark like a hearth-flame.

"A true voice. A willing scribe. And the First Hearth."

"Explain each," Freya said. "Plainly. No poetry."

He loved them a little for that. "A true voice. Not a trained one—a *true* one. The covenant runs on meaning, remember? Meaning is the food. Most singers can make pretty sounds. Maybe one in a generation can sing something so honest that the gods can actually drink it. Your choirboy cracked chapel glass the night the stars went out. I heard what happened at the shrine here. He's not one in a generation. He might be the only one alive." He glanced at Quentin, who had pulled the cloak tighter and said nothing, and Brannoc thought: *he already knows. Some part of him has known since the glass broke.*

"A willing scribe." He drew the second mark harder, until the chalk squealed. "The covenant isn't just sung. It's written. Every covenant has terms, and terms need a hand to set them down—an ordinary mortal hand, unranked, unbought, writing the bargain in their own script, in their own blood-price of effort. The original was written by somebody whose name is lost, which tells you how the temple treated its founders. The new one needs a scribe who chooses it. Who understands the cost and signs anyway." He met Freya's eyes. "Debt-bound scribes know more about signing things they'll regret than anyone living. That's not a compliment. It's just true."

Freya's expression didn't change. Brannoc noted that, and filed it away, and felt the first cold thread of something he'd later recognize as dread.

"And the third?" Sera asked.

"The First Hearth." He drew the flame, and then, around it, a mountain. "Where the first covenant was struck. Not a temple. Older than temples. A shrine in the mountains north of here—three weeks' hard travel, longer with winter coming on. The original anchor-stone. The one place the old lines still run true, because the Cantor never found it, or never bothered, because it's been cold for centuries and he thought it dead." He looked at the flame-shape in the ash. "To reforge the covenant, it has to be relit there. At the source. With a true voice singing the terms, and a willing scribe writing them, and—" he hesitated.

"And?" Maren said.

"And I don't know the full cost," Brannoc admitted. "The texts I read, back when I still had access—I was young, and arrogant, and I skimmed the parts that frightened me. There's a price on the reforging. There's always a price. The old writers were very clear that it's paid by the singers and the scribe, personally, and they were very unclear about what it was, and I have spent fifteen years telling myself that was because I forgot." He stared into the fire. "I didn't forget. I just couldn't make myself read it twice."

Nobody spoke for a moment. Outside the shrine, wind moved through the dead orchard, and somewhere far off a night-bird made a sound like a question.

"Three weeks north," Maren said finally, practical as a hammer. "In winter. Past whatever the Cantor has watching the roads, with a fugitive choirboy, a burned scribe, a defected inquisitor, a drunk ward-smith, and me." She tied off her mending with a vicious little jerk. "Well. I've provisioned for worse. Barely."

"Why you?" Quentin asked suddenly. He'd been quiet so long that his voice startled them all. He was looking at Brannoc with those audit-eyes. "Why does it have to be you? The ward-craft. Why does any of this need a man who's been hiding under a bridge for fifteen years?"

And there it was. The question Brannoc had been drinking around for a decade and a half.

"Because the anchor-stone at the Hearth is broken," he said. "Or it will be, by the time we get there—the Cantor's people will have seen to that if they know we're coming, and men like him always know. And a broken anchor-stone can't be replaced. It can only be *rebuilt*, by someone who knows the old craft, the pre-temple craft, the craft they exiled me for knowing." He turned his hands over, palms up, the scars silver in the firelight. "I helped build the border wards, boy. Which means I know exactly how they come apart. And exactly how they go back together." He closed his fists. "I ran from it for fifteen years because I thought my part was done and my guilt was paid. Turns out neither is true. So. That's why me."

He expected pity. He got something worse: Freya nodding slowly, already pulling out a scrap of paper, already sketching a route north in that quick economical hand, already treating his confession as a supply list.

"We leave at first light," Freya said. "Maren, how much of the cart can we strip without losing the axle? Sera—which roads does the temple watch hardest this time of year? Quentin—"

"Yes," Quentin said, before they could ask.

"—you should sleep. All of you. Three weeks is three weeks."

They scattered to their bedrolls, and Brannoc sat alone by the fire a while longer, looking at the three marks he'd drawn in the ash. The mouth. The quill. The flame.

Then he looked closer at the second mark, the quill, and his stomach turned over, because in the oldest fragment of the covenant-texts—the one he'd skimmed, the one he'd fled—one phrase had stuck like a splinter all these years, surfacing now in the firelight with terrible clarity:

*A scribe willing to sign away what cannot be signed back.*

He raked the ash flat with his boot, erasing the marks, and told himself it meant nothing, and did not believe it, and went to sleep.
