## 14. Market of Stolen Light

The bazaar smelled of tallow smoke and wet iron, and it breathed.

That was the first thing Freya noticed as they descended the switchback stairs into the old quarry—how the whole place seemed to inhale and exhale, lanterns guttering in unison with some vast hidden rhythm, hundreds of bodies moving through the cavernous dark like blood through a lung. Hollow Bridge's exile market had no name on any map. You found it by knowing someone who knew someone, or by following the sound of haggling down into the earth until the sky disappeared overhead and the only stars left were the ones people were willing to sell you.

Freya kept their hood up and their hands in their sleeves, the way they'd walked through Corvane's night market for fifteen years. Old habits. But the habits fit differently here. In Corvane, invisibility had been armor. Here, among ward-breakers and relic-thieves and exiles of every flavor, a hood marked you as either dangerous or frightened, and Freya hadn't yet decided which they wanted to be.

"Stay close," Brannoc muttered at their shoulder. He'd shaved for the occasion, which somehow made him look worse—like a man who had cleaned himself up specifically to face something he was ashamed of. "And don't look at anyone longer than you have to. Curiosity gets remembered here."

"I've spent fifteen years not being looked at," Freya said. "It's my one true talent."

Brannoc snorted. Ahead of them, Quentin moved through the crowd like a candle flame everyone unconsciously arranged themselves around—not because he drew attention, but because he didn't, quite, and people made room without knowing why. He wore one of Maren's old traveling cloaks, too big in the shoulders, and he kept touching the hem of it the way he touched everything now: carefully, as if the world might be taken from him at any moment.

He'd been quieter since the shrine. Since the night something had answered his song—a flicker where a dead star should have been, a pulse of light like a heartbeat heard through a wall. He hadn't talked about it much. He didn't need to. Freya had seen his face when the light came: hope and terror in equal measure, the expression of a man who has prayed into the dark for years and is suddenly afraid the dark might actually be listening.

Now they were here, three days' travel from the ruined shrine, following Brannoc's half-remembered directions to the one place in the borderlands where stolen holy things changed hands.

"The temple surplus comes through twice a season," Brannoc said, low, as they passed a stall hung with censers still bearing the Veiled Word's stamp, defaced but legible. "Relics pulled from closed chapels. Vestments. Sometimes—" he hesitated, jaw working, "—sometimes worse. Things I helped build the wards to contain, back when I still thought the wards were mine."

"And you're sure we'll find what we're looking for?"

"No." He said it flatly, honestly. "But if there's starlight being hoarded anywhere outside the temple itself, it'll be here. The Cantor's quartermasters aren't priests. They're men. Men sell things."

* * *

They found the stall an hour later, and Freya knew it before they saw it.

Not by sight—the stall was nothing, a folding table under a canvas awning at the bazaar's far edge, tended by a woman with a ledger and a bored expression. Freya knew it the way you know a room where someone has died. Something in the air around it was wrong. Thin. Grieving.

The vials sat in rows on black velvet, each one the size of a thumb, each one glowing faintly with a light that did not behave like fire or lamp-oil or any light Freya had ever sold charms beside. It didn't flicker. It *pulsed*—slowly, arrhythmically, each vial keeping its own broken time—and the glow was silver-white shot through with veins of blue, and looking at it made Freya's teeth ache and their eyes sting and their chest tighten with a sorrow that had no business living inside them.

Starlight. Hoarded. Bottled. *Priced.*

"There," Quentin breathed. His hand found Freya's sleeve. "Freya. That's—"

"I know what it is." Their voice came out strange. Flat. "I can feel it from here."

And they could. That was the part that undid them. Fifteen years of harmless charms, of masking their gift behind trinkets and misdirection, and the moment they came within ten paces of those vials, every buried nerve in them lit up like a struck bell. This was real magic. Old magic. The light of beings who had held the border wards and hung the stars and listened, once, to every voice raised in every choir across two realms—decanted into glass and set out for sale between a stall of cursed dice and a man selling pickled eels.

"How much?" Brannoc asked the vendor, casual as a man asking after turnips.

The woman looked up. She had the heavy-lidded patience of someone who had heard every kind of customer. "Depends which one. The weak ones run eight crowns. The strong ones—" she tapped a vial near the center, one that pulsed faster than its neighbors, brighter, "—forty. Temple surplus, cleared inventory. Perfectly legal this side of the border, if that's your worry."

"Where does the temple get them?" Freya asked.

The question came out sharper than intended. The vendor's eyes narrowed, reassessing them—hood, gloves, the careful blankness of their posture. "Same place temples get everything," she said. "Confiscation. There was a purge in the capital districts after the wards fell. Anyone caught with unauthorized light had it seized. The seizure crews don't destroy it. They bottle it." She shrugged. "Waste not."

*Confiscation.* Freya turned the word over and felt it cut. After the wards fell, after the stars went out, the temple had swept the city for anyone whose magic flared uncontrollably that night—people like Freya, people whose gifts had betrayed them in a single terrible hour—and called it law. And the light those people carried, the light the gods had seeded in mortal bloodlines since the first covenant—

No. Not that. They looked again at the vials, really looked, and understood.

This wasn't mortal light. Mortal magic had a warmth to it, a human frequency. This was colder and older and sadder. This was what the Cantor had *taken*—the gods' own radiance, stripped from the heavens the night the stars went out, skimmed off the covenant like cream, and now leaking out the back doors of the temple in quartermaster lots because even miracles generate surplus.

"They're dying," Quentin whispered.

Freya looked at him. He stood over the vials with his hand pressed flat against his sternum, his face gone grey.

"What?"

"The light. It's—" He swallowed. "You can't hear it. I can. It's like—" His voice cracked, and he lowered it to almost nothing. "It's like hearing a choir through a locked door. Everyone singing. No one able to stop singing. And getting quieter." His eyes came up to Freya's, wet and furious. "They're in there. Pieces of them. And they can't get out."

The vendor had stopped pretending boredom. Her hand had drifted below the table, toward whatever merchants in places like this kept below tables. "You buying or preaching?" she asked. "Preaching costs extra."

"We're browsing," Brannoc said smoothly, stepping half in front of Quentin. "My associate gets emotional about inventory. Occupational hazard—he's a lamplighter."

"A lamplighter," the vendor repeated, in a tone that suggested she had met many lamplighters and believed none of them.

* * *

They withdrew to the shadow of a buttress between stalls, and Freya's heart was hammering in a way it hadn't hammered even when the inquisitors had come to their door.

Because here was the arithmetic, and it was ugly. Forty crowns for the strong vial. They had, pooled between the three of them, eleven crowns and a brass button Quentin insisted was lucky. Even if they sold everything—even if they hawked Maren's cart and the horse and Freya's entire stock of charm supplies—they couldn't buy back more than a fraction of what sat on that table. And that table, Brannoc said, was one of four. The rest of the stolen light was deeper in the temple's vaults, feeding the Cantor's silent hymn, fueling his great remaking of the covenant with himself as its sole throat.

"We could come back with coin," Brannoc said. "Maren's got savings. If we send word—"

"Maren's three days away and the bazaar moves twice a season, not twice a week." Freya was watching the stall. Watching the vendor wrap a weak vial in cloth for a buyer—a fat man with rings, who tucked it into his coat like a sweetmeat. "And every day we wait, they get quieter. Quentin said it himself. They're fading in there."

"So what are you saying?"

Freya didn't answer immediately. They were doing something they hadn't done in fifteen years: planning a theft. Real theft, not the petty sleight-of-hand of a charm-seller shorting a customer's change. And the strange thing, the thing they would examine later, in the dark, in the cold, was how *easy* it felt. How natural. As if fifteen years of smallness had been the disguise, and underneath it had always been this—someone willing to burn things down for what mattered.

"I'm saying," they said quietly, "that I've spent my whole life making myself invisible so no one would look twice at me. Tonight I'd like to use it for something."

Quentin stared at them. Then, slowly, terribly, he started to smile—that choirboy smile, bright and slightly unholy. "You're going to steal from the temple."

"I'm going to steal back from the temple. There's a difference. Legally, probably not. Morally, entirely."

"It's guarded," Brannoc hissed. "You think a woman with a crossbow under her table is the whole security? There are watchers. There are fences who report to fences. If you're caught—"

"If I'm caught, I'm a nameless debt-scribe nobody's ever looked at, caught shoplifting in an outlaw market where nobody wants temple attention any more than I do." Freya flexed their fingers inside their gloves. "Besides. I'm not going to lift a vial. I'm going to do something better."

They explained it in a whisper, fast, while the bazaar roared on around them. Brannoc's objections grew weaker with each sentence, not because Freya's plan was flawless—it wasn't; it had holes you could drive Maren's cart through—but because, Freya suspected, the old ward-smith was tired of being a man who only ever said *no*. Guilt makes cowards of exactly the people most desperate for redemption.

"Fine," Brannoc said at last, sounding like a man agreeing to his own execution. "Fine. But if this goes wrong, I'm telling Maren it was entirely your idea."

"It was entirely my idea."

"I know. That's why I want it on record."

* * *

The distraction was Quentin's, and it cost him more than he let show.

He waited until the bazaar's breathing swelled—some argument boiling over at the eel stall, the crowd pressing toward the noise—and then he began to hum. Just a thread of melody, barely voiced, pitched beneath hearing for most. But the quarry walls were stone, and stone carries song the way water carries blood, and within moments the humming seemed to come from everywhere at once—from the buttresses, from the stairwells, from the dark mouths of the old mine-shafts. People stopped. Turned. Frowned at empty air.

A ghost-song in an exile market full of people who had fled ghosts.

The crowd's attention fractured beautifully. The vendor rose halfway from her stool, scanning the galleries, her hand finding her crossbow. Brannoc lurched against a support post with a practiced drunkard's stagger, knocking a stack of crates into the aisle, swearing magnificently, drawing the last eyes that needed drawing.

And Freya walked to the stall, hood up, face down, one customer among forty, and laid both gloved hands flat on the velvet beside the vials.

Up close, the light was worse. Up close, Freya could feel it the way Quentin heard it—not sound, but pressure, a grief with weight and texture, like standing in a house where mourning has soaked into the plaster. Each vial held a fragment of something that had once been vast enough to hang in the sky. Each fragment was trying, in its slow arrhythmic pulsing, to keep time with its siblings—to find the rhythm it had been torn away from—and failing, always failing, the way a severed hand twitches toward motions it can no longer complete.

*I'm sorry,* Freya thought, absurdly, to the light. *I'm sorry we let them do this.*

Then they closed their eyes and did the thing they had never dared do in fifteen years of hiding: they used their whole gift, openly, in the open air, trusting the noise and the dark and their own long-practiced nothingness to cover it.

Magic poured out of them like breath after a long dive. Not a charm. Not a trick. A working—small, precise, and utterly forbidden: they reached into the strongest vial's glow and *asked* it to dim. Not extinguish. Dim. To fold its light inward, tight as a fist, until it looked like one of the dead vials at the row's end—the duds, the spent ones, the ones the vendor had priced at two crowns and ignored. And into that folded darkness Freya slipped their own light, their own small mortal spark, wearing the stolen radiance like a mask, so that any watcher's eye would read it as merchandise and nothing more.

The vial went quiet in their palm. The grief inside it surged against their touch—startled, desperate, *hopeful*—and Freya nearly dropped it, because hope was so much harder to hold than sorrow.

*I've got you,* they thought. *Hold still. Hold quiet. I'm getting you out.*

Their hand closed. Their sleeve fell over it. They stepped back from the table, murmured something about thinking it over, and walked away at the pace of a person with nowhere in particular to be.

No one shouted. No bell rang. The vendor was still staring at the galleries, hunting a ghost that was already pocketing his melody and strolling toward the stairs.

* * *

They didn't stop walking until they'd put three galleries and a waterfall of dripping rock between themselves and the stall, and then they ducked into an abandoned ore-cut, a dead-end seam in the quarry wall where the lantern-light didn't reach, and Freya finally opened their fist.

The vial woke.

Light unfurled from it slowly, like a flower deciding whether it was safe to bloom—silver-blue, trembling, filling the little stone seam with a radiance that had no source and no shadow. Quentin made a sound Freya had never heard from him, low and broken, and sank to his knees in front of it.

And Freya—Freya felt it all.

Because their magic was still tangled with the shard's, their spark still wrapped around it like a cupped hand, and through that contact the grief came pouring in. Not abstract. Specific. They felt the weight of a sky that had been carried for millennia. They felt the covenant like a memory of warmth—countless voices rising from countless hearths, feeding upward, and the answering grace flowing down, a circuit older than cities, older than names. And then they felt the severing: the Cantor's hymn turning inward, the feeding channels clamped shut one by one, and the gods' light pooling in the temple vaults like blood in a closed wound while the realms above and below went dark and cold and alone.

They felt the gods' confusion. That was the worst of it. Not rage—confusion. The bewilderment of beings who had given everything for ages and could not comprehend why the giving had been answered with theft.

A single tear slid down Freya's face, and another, and they didn't wipe them away. There didn't seem to be any point.

"They didn't abandon us," they heard themselves say. Their voice sounded scraped raw. "Everyone says the gods went silent, the gods failed us, the gods are dead. They didn't go anywhere. They were *robbed*. They're sitting in jars in a cellar while the man who robbed them tells the world they abandoned it." The anger arrived then, right on schedule, and it was the cleanest thing Freya had felt in fifteen years—cleaner than fear, cleaner than shame, hot and clarifying as a forge. "He bottled them. He's *selling* them. And half the realm will buy a little piece of heaven off a smuggler's table and never ask why it was for sale."

Quentin looked up at them, lit silver from below, tears cutting tracks through the dust on his face. "What do we do?"

Freya looked at the vial—at the small captive splendor of it, pulsing now in something that was almost, almost a steady rhythm, as if proximity to Quentin's kneeling presence had given it a beat to follow.

"Now?" They wiped their face with the back of their wrist, and when their hand came down it wasn't shaking anymore. "Now we get this to Brannoc, and we find out if a shard of the old light is enough to show him what the wards used to be. Because if we're going to reforge the covenant, we'll need more than a song and a scribe." They closed their fingers gently around the vial, and the light seeped warm between their knuckles, like a small creature settling into a held hand. "We'll need to take back everything he stole. Every vial. Every vault."

"That's the temple treasury," Quentin said faintly.

"Yes."

"You're smiling."

"I know," Freya said, and found that they meant it. "Fifteen years of being invisible, Quentin. I'm finding I rather like being seen doing this."

From somewhere above them, muffled by stone, came the sound of the bazaar's commotion dying down—and beneath it, fainter, a different sound. Voices at the top of the stairwell. Several voices. Organized ones.

Brannoc appeared at the mouth of the ore-cut, moving fast, his face drained of everything but urgency. "We've got a problem," he said. "Temple colors at the north gate. A patrol, maybe more—and they're not raiding the market." He swallowed. "They're asking questions. Showing a sketch. Two sketches." His eyes went from Freya to Quentin and back. "One of them's yours, Freya. The other one's his."

The vial pulsed in Freya's hand—once, twice—like a heartbeat quickening.

"Well," Freya said quietly, into the dark. "I suppose we'd better not be here when they finish asking."
