## 15. Maren Comes After Us

The shop had never once been closed in Maren Ostwick's thirty-one years of keeping it. Not during the fever winter when her husband coughed himself into the ground and she'd sold tinctures with shaking hands. Not the night the temple criers announced the border wards had fallen and half of Corvane stood in the streets screaming at a sky with no stars in it. Not even the day they buried her daughter — though that one had been close, so close that old Tamsin from the dye-house had stood behind the counter all afternoon just in case.

So when the neighbors saw the shutters come down on the morning after Vespers-of-the-Dark-Moon, three days after the stars went out, they came running like it was a fire.

"Maren!" Tamsin hammered on the door. "Maren Ostwick, are you dead in there?"

Maren was not dead. She was upstairs, packing.

She packed the way she did everything: ruthlessly, in order of usefulness. Dried sausage first, because hunger made people stupid and stupid people died. Then the good bandages, the willow bark, the needle and gut thread she used for mending sails' worth of torn coats and, twice, for sewing men back together when the night market turned ugly. A jar of her pepper pickle, because if she was going to die on some muddy road she refused to do it eating food that tasted like wet rope. The heavy wool cloak she'd been saving against a winter that hadn't come yet. And last, wrapped in oilcloth and tucked against her spine where no pickpocket alive would find it, the little strongbox of coin that was supposed to be her old age.

She looked at the bed, at the dent in the pillow she'd never bothered to fluff since Hal died, and she said out loud, to nobody, to the room, to him wherever he'd got to: "You'd tell me I'm a fool."

The room agreed with nothing. Rooms don't.

"Good," said Maren. "Then we're settled."

* * *

The truth of it — the truth she'd have cut out her own tongue before admitting to Tamsin or anyone else on Copperlane Row — was that Maren had known something was wrong with Freya Voss for fifteen years, and had chosen, deliberately, every single day, not to look too closely at it.

A scribe who paid rent on time. That alone should have been suspicious. Scribes were temple property, mostly, indentured ink-monkeys with their ledgers chained to their wrists, and here was one renting her back room, selling little charms at the night market — luck-knots and headache-tokens and charms against damp, harmless things, blessedly dull things — and paying every week without fail, in coin that always smelled faintly of candle smoke.

Maren had run a shop in the market district long enough to know what people hid and how they hid it. The butcher's boy who was too polite. The widow who bought two of everything. The priest who came on Thursdays and never bought anything at all. You learned to see the shape of a secret by the shadow it cast, and Freya's shadow had always been the shape of someone waiting for a knock on the door.

Well. The knock had come. Inquisitors in their grey-and-silver, sweeping Copperlane like lice through a seamstress's hair, and Maren had looked down the barrel of a templar's questions and lied the way she breathed — easily, thoroughly, with detail. *Freya Voss? Rents my back room, yes. Left two nights past, owed me a week. Went south, toward the river gates.* She'd even given them a description that fit three different people and none of them.

And then she'd gone inside, put her hand flat against the hidden panel behind the flour shelf, felt the warmth of the person crouched in the crawlspace beyond it, and thought: *well. Here it is, then. The bill for fifteen years of not asking.*

They'd slipped out the coal chute at midnight, Freya and that ragged choirboy, and Maren had stood in her kitchen holding a lamp and watching them go, and she had said nothing, done nothing, except press a sack of bread and hard cheese into Freya's hands and say, "South road's watched. Take the tanner's lane."

She'd told herself that was enough. Kindness has a natural size, she told herself. You give what fits through the door and no more.

It had lasted exactly two days.

What ended it was a boy named Pip from the fish stall, who came in for his mother's order and gossiped like a cracked pipe. Temple riders on the south road. Checkpoints at the toll bridges. And — this was the part that made Maren's blood go cold and then hot — word that the Cantor himself had put a price on a choirboy's head. Not capture. The fish-stall boy didn't know what he was saying, but Maren did: *a price on a head* meant the head could arrive separately from the body.

Freya Voss, who couldn't lie to save their life and had never once in fifteen years asked Maren for anything beyond a roof. Out there on the south road with a boy who sang like the inside of a bell, carrying nothing but bread and cheese, walking straight into a net.

Maren took down the strongbox.

"You'd tell me I'm a fool," she said again, to Hal, to the empty house, to the ghost of a girl she'd raised for six years before the fever took her, the girl whose name she still didn't say on ordinary days because saying it cost something and today she needed all her strength for other things. "Both of you. Fools, the pair of you, and me the biggest."

She blew out the lamp, locked the front door, and left the key under the loose brick where she'd left it for twenty years, because a locked shop tells the neighbors you're coming back, and Maren Ostwick fully intended to come back, and anyone — temple, god, or Cantor himself — who suggested otherwise was welcome to try and stop her.

* * *

She caught up with them on the third evening, at a crossroads shrine so old its saint had been worn faceless by weather, and she knew it was them before she saw them because she heard them arguing.

"—all I'm saying is, if you'd let me sing at the toll bridge, we'd have walked through—"

"And I'm saying, Quentin, that your voice makes glass crack and grown men fall down weeping, and you want to use it to *distract a toll collector*—"

"It worked on the bird."

"The *bird*, Quentin—"

Maren came around the shrine with the cart rattling behind her and said, "The bird didn't have a crossbow, dear. Toll collectors do."

The effect was everything she could have hoped for. Freya went white as flour and grabbed Quentin's arm like a mother hen that had found its chick wandering the henyard. Quentin simply stared, mouth open, looking — Maren noted with satisfaction — exactly like a boy caught stealing jam.

"You—" Freya started.

"Shutters are down," Maren said briskly, unhooking the cart's tailgate and beginning to unload with the efficiency of a woman who had decided the argument was already over. "Tamsin thinks I've gone to visit my sister in Lowmarsh. My sister has been dead nine years, which makes it a very restful visit." She thrust a sausage at Freya. "Eat. You look like a scarecrow that's been left out over winter. Both of you. Stars gone, wards broken, temple hunting you across half the country, and you're living on bread and cheese like a pair of storybook orphans. Sit down before you fall down."

"Maren." Freya's voice had gone strange. They were still holding the sausage like it might be a trap. "You can't be here. If they trace you—"

"They'll trace me to a shut shop and a sister in Lowmarsh." She spread the oilcloth on the shrine's step and laid out the food with a flourish she'd deny under torture. "I lied to an inquisitor's face three days ago, love. Do you imagine I'm in their good books regardless? Whatever happens to you happens to me now; the only question is whether I stand in it freezing and hungry or fed and armed." She patted the cart. "There's a boat hook under the turnips. I sharpened it."

Quentin made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled at birth. "You *sharpened* a—"

"A woman who keeps a shop in the night market learns to sharpen things, choirboy. Eat your sausage."

They ate. Of course they ate; they were young and starving and the food was good, and watching them demolish her stores gave Maren more satisfaction than any ledger entry ever had. Freya ate slowly, watching her over the top of the sausage the whole time, with that look — the look of someone trying to work out the price of a thing that had been handed to them free.

"Stop it," Maren said.

"Stop what?"

"Whatever sum you're doing behind your eyes. 'Why is she here, what does she want, what will it cost.' I've watched you do it for fifteen years, Freya Voss, every time I brought up soup when you were sick or fixed the draft in your window without being asked. You tally everything. Well, tally this: some things aren't debts. Some things are just what people do." She pointed the paring knife at them for emphasis. "And if you argue with me about it I'll make you peel the turnips."

Freya's mouth opened. Closed. Opened again. "You knew," they said finally, quietly. "All these years. About the charms. You knew they weren't just charms."

"I knew you were hiding something, and I knew it wasn't anything cruel, because cruel people don't pay on time and don't charm the warts off Tamsin's pig for free." Maren began packing the cloth back up, briskly, because her hands wanted something to do and her eyes were doing something embarrassing and she refused to permit it. "I didn't know *what*. Didn't want to know. Knowing things is dangerous in this city. But the stars going out changed the arithmetic, didn't it? Can't hide under a rock that's rolling downhill."

* * *

They made camp that night in the lee of the shrine, and Maren learned quickly that traveling with fugitives meant learning new trades. She'd always been the one who knew things — prices, people, which alderman drank too much and which temple clerk could be bribed with candied ginger. Now she was the one who didn't know things. Which stars had been which. What the wards had actually held back. Why Quentin flinched at the word *vespers*.

But she knew other things, and it turned out those mattered too.

She knew that Quentin hadn't eaten properly in weeks and needed feeding in small amounts or he'd make himself sick. She knew that Freya talked endlessly when frightened and went silent when truly afraid, and that tonight they were silent, sitting apart, turning something small and bright over in their fingers — a shard of something that gave off light like a dying coal. She knew that when she draped the second blanket over the boy's shoulders without comment, he leaned into it like a plant toward sun, and that no one had done that for him in a long time either.

"So," she said, settling beside Freya with the deliberate weight of a woman claiming territory. "Tell me where we're going, since apparently we're all going there."

Freya hesitated. Then, in a low voice, with Quentin drifting over to listen, they laid it out: Hollow Bridge, a ward-smith named Brannoc, fragments of evidence that the wards hadn't failed but been unmade, that the stars hadn't died but been *taken*, hoarded in the temple like grain in a miser's cellar. A covenant to reforge. A hearth in the mountains. Names Maren didn't know and dangers she could guess at.

When they finished, the silence rang.

"Well," said Maren. "That's the stupidest plan I've ever heard, and I once watched a man try to smuggle a live bear through the customs house in a hat box."

"Maren—"

"It's also the only plan I've heard," she went on, "so I suppose we'll make it work. First rule of shops and sieges both: you don't need a good plan, you need supplies and stubbornness. We have the stubbornness." She counted on her fingers. "We need the supplies. There's a factor in Millbrook, two days south, who owes me money and owes the temple nothing. He'll sell us travel rations, oil, and a second cart at honest prices, which is to say robbery, but honest robbery. After that—"

"After that we're fugitives buying groceries," Quentin said, and there was wonder in it, and something loosening in his shoulders.

"People have founded empires on less," said Maren. "Sleep. Both of you. I'll take first watch, and before you protest — I've been keeping watch over this one for fifteen years." She jerked her thumb at Freya. "Habit."

Freya opened their mouth — to argue, to thank her, Maren neither knew nor wanted to know — and then closed it, and something passed between them that needed no words, which was fortunate, because Maren's throat had gone tight and unreliable.

* * *

Two days later, at the Millbrook factor's, she haggled the man down from robbery to mere extortion, acquired the second cart, forty pounds of hardtack, a keg of oil, and — through means she declined to explain — a short hunting bow and twelve arrows, which she handed to Quentin with the instruction "pointy end away from family."

It was Quentin who spotted the rider, on the morning they turned onto the south road proper.

He'd gone quiet in the way he did sometimes, head tilted, listening to something no one else could hear — the gods, maybe, or whatever was left of them. Then he said, "Dust. On the ridge. Moving fast. Too fast for farmers."

Maren shaded her eyes. The dust plume hung above the Ashford Crossing like a smudge of ash against the pale sky, and beneath it, glinting, the unmistakable grey-and-silver of temple steel.

"Well," she said calmly, taking up the boat hook and checking its edge with her thumb. "I did sharpen it."

Beside her, Freya went very still, and Quentin's breath caught — and far off, at the edge of hearing, someone on the ridge began to blow a horn.
