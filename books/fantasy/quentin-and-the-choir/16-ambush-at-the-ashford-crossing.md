## 16. Ambush at the Ashford Crossing

* * *

**Sera**

The Ashford Crossing was a lie of geography.

On the survey maps in the Temple archive it was marked as a ford—a place where the Oldwater spread shallow and slow over pale stones, easy to cross in any season. What the maps did not show was that the river had moved forty years ago, leaving behind a mile of soft ground stitched with alder roots and standing water, and that the only dry path through it ran along a raised causeway barely wide enough for one cart.

Which meant that anyone fleeing Corvane on foot, heading east toward the mountain shrines, would have to cross that causeway in single file, visible from either bank, with nowhere to run.

Sera Vane stood at the edge of the alder brake and watched the mist burn off the water, and felt nothing at all, which was how she knew she was doing her job correctly.

"Positions," she said quietly.

Her squad of six melted into the reeds and root-hollows on both sides of the causeway. Two archers high in the alders. Two blade-hands at the near end, hidden under woven brush. Two at the far end to close the trap. She herself took the center, crouched in a hollow where an old willow had fallen into the water, because she had learned long ago that a commander who watched from safety commanded nothing worth having.

The warrant in her breast pocket was warm against her ribs. It always felt warm. She had stopped telling herself it was her imagination.

*Fugitives: one scribe, unregistered talent. One choirboy, voice intact. One ward-smith, exiled. One shopkeeper, civilian accessory.* The descriptions were precise. The instruction at the bottom was precise too, in the Cantor's own cipher: *Recover the boy alive. The others as circumstances require.*

She had read that phrase a hundred times since leaving the city. *As circumstances require.* It was elegant. It was deniable. It meant whatever she needed it to mean, and the Cantor would never have to say the word *kill* out loud, because the Veiled Word did not deal in words it could be held to.

A jay called twice from the eastern bank—the sentry's signal. Travelers on the road.

Sera settled her weight, drew an arrow, and nocked it without sound. Her hands were perfectly steady. They had been steady since she was nine years old, when the temple took her off the charity steps and put a bow in them and told her that trembling was a form of lying.

Through the grey morning light she saw them come: a vegetable cart moving slower than it should, drawn by a mule that had seen better decades, and beside it four figures who were trying very hard to look like farm laborers and failing in four different ways.

The shopkeeper walked like a woman who owned a shop. The old smith walked like a man whose knees had opinions about causeways. The scribe kept glancing at the water with the fixed attention of someone counting exits. And the boy—

The boy walked like a boy. That was all. Sixteen, maybe seventeen, shoulders still deciding what they would become, head turning toward every birdcall as if birds might have something to say to him.

*Recover the boy alive.*

Sera breathed out slowly and waited for them to reach the middle of the crossing, where the reeds grew tallest and the water lay deepest on either side, and there was no direction in the world they could go but forward or back.

* * *

**Freya**

Freya smelled the ambush before they saw it—or thought they did, which amounted to the same thing, because fifteen years of selling charms in a night market had taught them that the difference between paranoia and perception was mostly whether you survived.

"It's wrong," they murmured, not turning their head. "The reeds."

Quentin, walking beside the cart, didn't look over. "What about them?"

"They're growing in rows." Freya kept their eyes on the mule's ears, on the road, on anything but the banks. "Nobody plants reeds. They seed themselves, messy, everywhere. These are spaced. Someone thinned them to make sight lines."

Ahead, Maren snorted. "If you're about to tell me we're walking into a trap, I'd like to register that I said we should take the south road, and I'd like my agreement noted for posterity."

"You said the south road added two days," Brannoc rumbled from the cart's bench, reins loose in his scarred hands.

"And I'd spend those two days alive, which is my preferred condition for travel."

"We're already in it," Freya said. "Whatever it is. If we turn around now, we show them we know, and they stop being patient."

They came around the bend where the causeway narrowed between two pools, and Freya felt it then—not saw, *felt*, the way they felt a charm about to fail: a wrongness in the air pressure, a held breath. The kind of silence that has people inside it.

"Maren," they said softly. "Get down."

The first arrow took the cart's rail a hand's width from Quentin's shoulder.

* * *

**Quentin**

The world went loud all at once.

Men rose out of the reeds on both sides of the causeway—six, eight, more than Quentin could count while his heart was trying to climb out through his throat—and the arrows came with them, and the shouting, and the terrible organized calm of people who had done this before.

"Off the road!" Brannoc hauled the mule's bridle and the cart slewed sideways, wheels screaming on wet stone. Maren grabbed Quentin's collar and pulled him down behind the cart bed as a second arrow buried itself in the wood where his head had been. Freya was already moving, low and fast, and Quentin saw their hands flick through a gesture he'd only seen once before, in the market, the night everything ended—and every charm on their belt lit at once, blue-white, and the air above the cart went hard as glass.

Three arrows struck it and shattered like icicles.

"That won't hold!" Freya shouted. "It's a market charm, it's not built for—"

An inquisitor in grey reached the causeway's edge, sword out, and swung at the shimmering barrier. The barrier rang like a struck bell and held, but Quentin felt Freya stagger, felt the effort of it across the space between them like a rope going taut.

There were too many. There were so many, and they were spread along both banks, and the shield was a bowl and they were pouring into it from every side, and Quentin crouched behind the cart with his hands over his ears and understood, with a clarity that felt almost peaceful, that they were all going to die here on this ugly wet road because of him.

Because of his voice. The thing the Cantor wanted. The thing the hymns had been scraped away to hide.

*Sing,* said something in the back of his skull. Not a thought exactly. A memory of a thought. He'd sung to a dying bird once, cupped in his palms in the temple garden, and the bird had not died, and Brother Aldous had looked at him afterward like he was looking at a lit candle in a room full of oil.

The next arrow came through the shield.

It didn't shatter. It slowed, quivered, dropped—but it came *through*, and Freya made a sound like they'd been punched, and the blue-white light guttered.

Quentin stood up.

He didn't decide to. Later he would try to remember deciding and there would be nothing there, just the moment before, crouching, and the moment after, upright in the open with arrows singing past him, drawing the breath that the choir masters had spent eleven years teaching him to draw—from the floor, from the gut, from somewhere below the body entirely.

And he sang.

Not words. There wasn't time for words. He sang the first note of the oldest hymn he knew, the one with the scraped-away verses, the one whose true melody lived in his body deeper than the temple's edits could reach—and the note went out across the crossing like a wall of warm water.

Every arrow in flight bent.

Not broke—*bent*, curving midair as if the wind had changed its mind, splashing harmlessly into the pools. The inquisitors on the banks stumbled, hands flying to their ears. One dropped his sword and knelt down in the mud, slow, like a man falling asleep. The mule stopped screaming. Even the river seemed to quiet, the current smoothing flat.

And Quentin felt what it cost, immediately, intimately: each second of song was a coin drawn out of his chest. His voice was not infinite. He had known that since the night the stars went out and his note cracked the chapel glass—he had felt something *leave* him then and not come back. Every note now was another handful of whatever that something was, poured out onto the water.

*Hold,* he told himself, and held the note, and the shield of Freya's charms steadied and brightened, fed by the sound the way a fire is fed by breath.

* * *

**Brannoc**

Brannoc had built wards for thirty years, and he knew breaking when he heard it.

The boy's song was holding the crossing, yes—holding it beautifully, the way a child holds back a flood with cupped hands. But a shield is a structure, and a structure under load tells you where it will fail, and Brannoc could hear the failure coming: a thin whine at the top of the melody, a harmonic strain, the sound a ward-stone makes in its last hour.

And he could see the flaw. Not in the song—in the *geometry*. The shield was a dome centered on the cart, and domes are strongest at the crown and weakest at the springline, low on the flanks, where the curve turns to meet the ground. On the western flank, maybe forty paces out, an inquisitor had found it. The man wasn't attacking the shield. He was walking along it, patient as a carpenter, tapping with his sword pommel, listening.

Looking for the seam.

Brannoc had spent five years under Hollow Bridge drinking himself stupid because he'd helped build the border wards and they'd come down anyway, and every night the same arithmetic had eaten him: *thirty years of craft, and it failed, so the craft was worthless, so you are worthless.* He had believed that arithmetic completely. It had been the most reliable thing in his life.

But the boy was spending himself to hold a line, and the shopkeeper woman was swearing magnificently as she dragged the scribe behind the cart, and nobody under this bridge of a sky was worthless, and Brannoc found—to his genuine surprise—that his legs worked better than they had in years.

"There's a seam!" he bellowed, over the song. "West side! Grey cloak!"

The scribe's head snapped around. "Can you close it?"

"Not from here!" Which was true. And also not the whole truth, because there was a way, an old ugly way, the way ward-smiths used before anyone invented standing safely behind things: you put your body where the structure needed mass. You became the stone.

"I can," Brannoc said, to no one in particular, and climbed off the cart.

The song wrapped around him as he ran—warm, enormous, heartbreaking, a boy's voice holding up the sky—and Brannoc crossed the forty paces of open marsh with mud sucking at his boots and arrows bending away around him like startled fish, and he reached the seam just as the grey-cloaked inquisitor did.

The man was fast. Brannoc was not, and no longer pretended to be. He didn't try to fight. He stepped *into* the gap—into the exact place where the shield met the ground, where the geometry wanted weight—and he planted his feet and spread his arms and let the structure lean on him.

It was like catching a falling cathedral.

The shield's weight came down through his shoulders, his spine, his teeth. Light crawled over his skin. Somewhere very far away the boy's voice wavered as the whole dome redistributed itself around the new stone in its wall, and Brannoc laughed, actually laughed, because it *worked*, the old ugly way worked, and he had forgotten—five years of drink had let him forget—that he had ever been good at anything.

Then the inquisitor, being a professional, stopped tapping and started cutting.

The sword went through the gap before Brannoc could seal it—through the space his body filled imperfectly, flesh being a poor substitute for granite—and Brannoc felt it enter below his ribs on the right side, a cold expanding point, almost polite in its precision.

He got his hands on the man's gorget anyway. Thirty years of lifting ward-stones lived in those hands. He closed them, and turned, and threw the inquisitor off the causeway into the deep pool, and then Brannoc sat down heavily in the mud, one hand pressed to his side, feeling the warmth pulse out between his fingers, and thought, with distant irritation:

*Well. That's blood in the water. Literally.*

* * *

**Sera**

From her hollow beneath the fallen willow, Sera Vane watched her operation come apart, and began, methodically, to count the reasons.

Reason one: the boy. No report had mentioned *this*. The file said *voice intact, potential covenant-conduit, recover alive*. It did not say the boy could bend arrows out of the sky with a single sustained note, or that his voice carried something in it that made grown soldiers kneel in the mud and forget why they were standing. Sera had trained under Master Corvess, who could freeze a man's sword arm with a spoken word of High Litany, and even Corvess had needed *words*. The boy needed nothing. The boy simply opened his mouth and the world rearranged itself around his pitch.

Reason two: the smith. Ward-craft was a dead discipline; the temple had spent a century making sure of it. And yet this drunk, gray-bearded exile had just done something Sera had only seen in diagrams from the founding era—he had taken a living shield's failure-point into his own body and *closed it*. Her squad's doctrine had no answer for that. Doctrine assumed the enemy would behave like people, not like architecture.

Reason three, and this was the one she kept returning to: the scribe.

She had them in her sights. Had had them for a full minute now—the slim figure crouched behind the cart, hands bleeding light, holding the dome together by main force while the boy's song poured strength into it. An easy shot. Forty yards, broadside, no wind. Sera had made this shot in her sleep since she was twelve years old.

*As circumstances require.*

She thought about the phrase the way she'd been taught to think about difficult scripture: break it into its parts, examine each, reassemble. *Circumstances:* a shielded position, a defended cart, a target of unknown but evidently considerable power. *Require:* what did the mission require? The mission required the boy alive. Did it require the scribe dead? Dead, the scribe's shield collapsed and the squad could take the boy intact. Alive, the scribe remained a threat to her squad's lives.

The logic was clean. The logic was flawless. Sera had been built, brick by brick, out of exactly this kind of clean flawless logic, and she had trusted it the way other people trusted gravity.

So why was her bow hand waiting?

Below her, the wounded smith slumped against the shield's edge, red spreading under his arm. The shopkeeper was crawling toward him, cursing with a fluency that would have gotten her excommunicated in any parish in Corvane. The boy's song was thinning—she could hear it fraying at the edges, a thread about to snap—and when it snapped, the dome would go, and the moment after that would decide everything.

*Straighten your spine, Vane,* said Master Corvess's voice in her memory. *Trembling is a form of lying.*

Her hands were steady. They were always steady. That was the horror of it—her hands would stay steady no matter what they were aimed at, and somewhere in the last six days, without her noticing the exact moment, the steadiness had stopped feeling like virtue and started feeling like the scariest thing about her.

The boy's voice cracked.

The dome flickered—and in that flicker, the scribe looked up. Directly at her hollow. Across forty yards of reed and rain-grey air, their eyes found hers, and Sera had one absurd, disorienting instant of recognition: the look on the scribe's face was not defiance or fear. It was *pity*. Pity, aimed at her, from the person she was hunting, as if the scribe could see the war behind her ribs and was sorry for her about it.

Sera loosed.

The arrow went wide. Not wildly wide—two fingers' breadth from the scribe's shoulder, close enough to sell it, far enough to miss. It buried itself in the cart's wheel with a sound like a knock on a door.

No one saw. Everyone was watching the boy, the blood, the fire of the failing charms. Only Sera heard her own heartbeat, very loud, very steady, in the space where the shot should have been.

*As circumstances require,* she thought. *And what do I require?*

She didn't have an answer. She had a hole where the answer used to be, and through the hole she could see the crossing, and the fugitives scrambling back onto the cart, and the boy hauling the wounded smith up by the collar with arms that shouldn't have had the strength, and the whole ragged impossible convoy lurching eastward off the causeway into the alder brake where her squad's encirclement had a gap exactly one cart wide—because she had positioned it that way, hours ago, for reasons she had told herself were tactical.

"Let them run," she said aloud, to her nearest lieutenant, and was grateful the mist swallowed how it sounded.

* * *

**Freya**

The cart crashed through the alder brake with branches whipping the lot of them, and then trees, and then more trees, and then—finally, blessedly—quiet, except for the mule's indignant breathing and Maren's voice saying, "Brannoc. Brannoc, look at me, you great ruined bridge-troll, keep your eyes open."

Freya scrambled over the cart's tail to find the old smith stretched across the seed sacks, gray-faced, Maren's hands already working a compress against the wound below his ribs. The blood was bad. Not arterial-bad, but deep-bad, the slow kind, the kind that whispered instead of screamed.

"I'm fine," Brannoc said. "I've had worse from badly stacked stones."

"You have not," Maren and Freya said together.

Quentin sat at the man's head, holding his hand, and his voice—when he tried to speak—came out as a rasp, a ruin, a whisper of smoke. He'd given too much. Freya looked at him and felt something twist in their chest that they didn't have time to name.

"The archer," Quentin rasped. "Did anyone—there was an archer, dead center, best shot of the lot. Why am I alive?"

Freya said nothing. Because Freya had looked too. Had met the archer's eyes across the killing ground, had seen the arrow come, had braced for it—and had seen, in the half-second before impact, the shot *change*. Not the aim. Something behind the aim.

"They missed," Freya said finally. "Inquisitors don't miss."

Maren tied off the bandage and looked up, sharp-eyed. "Then either the temple's standards have slipped, or one of them has a conscience, and I don't like either option. Can he walk?"

"He'd better," Brannoc said, and fainted.

They moved him into the cart's bed, and Maren took the reins, and the cart rolled east through the dripping trees toward the mountains, slower now, heavier by one wound and one unanswered question.

At the rear, watching the grey veil of the crossing disappear behind them, Freya touched the fresh split in the cart's wood where the first arrow had struck—a hand's width from Quentin's shoulder—and thought about the archer's steady hands, and the shot that hadn't been one, and wondered what it costs a person to miss on purpose, and whether they'd ever get the chance to ask.

Behind them, unseen, Sera Vane stood alone on the empty causeway, holding a warrant that suddenly weighed as much as a ward-stone—and burned it.
