## 17. What I Was Trained For

The warrant arrived at dawn, and Sera Vane knew it was wrong before she broke the seal.

She had carried temple orders for eleven years. She had learned to read them the way other people read weather—the weight of the wax, the cut of the paper, the particular hand of whichever clerk had copied them out in the scriptorium above the Hall of Records. Most warrants were routine things, dry as old bread: *pursue, apprehend, deliver to the Office of Inquiry for examination.* The formula never changed. The formula was the point. A hunter who trusted the shape of her orders could move fast; a hunter who had to parse every clause hesitated, and hesitation in the field got people killed.

This one was different.

The wax was black instead of the usual temple-red. The paper was heavier, finer—vellum from the Cantor's own stores, not the cheap rag stock the clerks used. And the hand was not a clerk's hand. It was small, precise, self-consciously beautiful, the kind of script that belonged to someone who wanted his writing admired.

High Cantor Ovelith had written this himself.

Sera stood in the doorway of the waystation at Ashford Crossing, the morning fog still clinging to her cloak, and read it three times while her squad saw to the horses behind her.

*To Inquisitor Sera Vane, First Chair of the Office of Pursuit. You will continue west along the river road. The fugitives—the scribe, the choirboy, the ward-smith, and any who aid them—are to be stopped by whatever means necessary.*

*By whatever means necessary.*

Not *apprehended.* Not *delivered.* Not even the euphemism the older texts favored, *brought to account.* She turned the vellum over, half expecting to find a second page, some clarification, some clerical error she could point to when the questions came. There was nothing. Just the seal pressed deep into the wax, the Veiled Word's open eye stamped so hard it had torn through.

Behind her, Corporal Dennick laughed at something one of the others said, and the sound was ordinary and bright and made her stomach turn.

* * *

She told herself, riding west through the grey morning, that there were explanations.

There were always explanations. That was the first thing they had taught her, in the white room with its single window, when she was nine years old and still answering to the name they'd given her before Vane: *the Order does not lie to you, child. It simply knows more than you do. Your confusion is a failure of your understanding, not of its honesty.* She had been a frightened girl from a debtors' row in Low Corvane whose parents had signed her over for a remission of forty crowns, and the white room had felt like salvation. Warm meals. Clean linen. Adults who looked at her like she mattered, because she did—because she could be shaped into something useful.

She had been good at being shaped. That was the truth of it, the truth she had spent thirty years not looking at directly. Sera Vane was not a woman who had survived the Order. She was a thing the Order had made, and she had been proud of it. Proud of her clearance rate. Proud of the way fugitives' faces went slack when they saw the eye-and-veil on her breastplate. Proud, once, of a capture in the Salt District where the man had begged and she had not even slowed her stride, because begging was noise and noise was irrelevant.

*Whatever means necessary.*

She rode with the words turning over and over, and she catalogued the explanations the way she'd been trained to catalogue evidence, impartially, exhaustively:

One: the phrase was boilerplate. Old legal language, meaning nothing more than urgency. Except she had read four hundred warrants and the language was never that. The Order was precise about violence the way surgeons were precise about cutting. It named what it wanted done. It did not leave room.

Two: the Cantor feared the fugitives would resist capture. Except they were five people, one of them wounded, one of them a boy, hauling a vegetable cart up a mountain road. They were not a threat that required annihilation. They were an embarrassment that required silence.

Three—

Three was the one she kept circling back to, the one that sat in her chest like swallowed ice: *the fugitives know something.* The scribe's fragments. The ward-smith's testimony. Whatever the choirboy had seen in the upper choir before he ran. If the wards had been unmade from inside—if the stars had been taken rather than lost—then everyone who could prove it was not a criminal to be tried but a witness to be erased.

And Sera Vane had spent her whole life being the eraser.

They made camp that night in a hollow below the ridge, and she walked the perimeter alone in the dark, checking the pickets twice, three times, because walking was better than thinking and thinking was better than feeling and she could not afford to feel. Frost was coming down out of the mountains early this year. Her breath hung silver in the torchlight.

Dennick found her at the north picket. He was twenty-three, earnest, the best tracker she'd produced since her own promotion, and he looked at her the way she had once looked at her mentor—with total, unexamined faith.

"Orders say we press hard tomorrow," he said. "We'll have them by dusk."

"You've read them?"

"Of course." He smiled. "Whatever means necessary. About time they let us off the leash, honestly. These magic-felons, after what they did to the wards—" He shook his head, and his face held no doubt at all, none, and she understood suddenly that she was looking at a mirror of herself at twenty-three, and that if she said nothing, in ten years he would be standing where she stood now, reading a black-sealed warrant and finding it clean.

"They didn't take down the wards," she heard herself say.

He blinked. "Inquisitor?"

"The evidence doesn't support it. The breach patterns at the border stones—unmaking leaves a different signature than failing. I've seen both." She had seen both. She had inspected the border stones herself, two days after the collapse, before the Office sealed the site. She had filed a report. She had never received a reply.

Dennick's smile faltered into something careful. "The Cantor's office reviewed—"

"The Cantor's office wrote the warrant in his own hand," she said, "and ordered no survivors, and I want you to sit with that sentence tonight, Corporal, and tell me in the morning what kind of crime requires killing five people on a mountain road where no one can count the bodies."

She left him there with his faith cracking audibly behind her, and she did not sleep.

* * *

At midnight she built a small fire apart from the camp, in a fold of rock where the light wouldn't carry, and she laid the warrant on a flat stone and interrogated it properly, the way she'd been trained to interrogate people.

Not the words. The words were clear enough. She interrogated the silences.

No destination for the prisoners, because there would be no prisoners. No requirement of report, because reports created records, and records could be subpoenaed someday by someone honest. No countersignature from the Office of Inquiry, which meant the regular machinery of justice had been bypassed entirely—this order existed outside law, the way a knife exists outside law. And the date. The warrant was dated three days ago. Three days ago, the fugitives had still been inside Corvane. Which meant the Cantor had decided they must die before he even knew whether they'd escaped, before they'd committed any further offense beyond running.

You did not kill witnesses in advance unless you already knew what they'd witnessed.

Sera thought about the border stones. She thought about her own report, filed and unanswered. She thought about the hymns—she had grown up on those hymns, sung them in the Order's chapel, and something in them had changed in the last years, older verses scraped away, and she had noticed and said nothing, because noticing was not her function. Her function was pursuit. Her function was the leash.

Her whole life, she had believed she served the Veiled Word—the god behind the veil, the listening dark, the mercy that watched without judging. But the Veiled Word had gone silent with the stars, hadn't it? Every god had. And a god who cannot speak cannot correct the men who claim to interpret him. She had given her life to an institution, and somewhere in the last decade the institution had quietly eaten its own god and left a man wearing its robes.

The fire cracked. Somewhere upslope, a wolf sang, long and lonely, and no star answered it because there were no stars, only the flat blind ceiling of cloud.

Sera Vane took the warrant from the stone.

She had imagined this moment, in the way you imagine your own death—abstractly, from a distance, with a certain professional curiosity. What defection would feel like. Whether it would feel like falling or flying. It felt like neither. It felt like the white room, all over again: a door opening, cold air coming in, and the understanding that everything warm on the other side of it had been purchased with something she could never get back.

Forty crowns. Her parents had sold her for forty crowns, and she had spent thirty years proving the purchase sound.

"No survivors," she said aloud, to the fire, to the wolf, to the girl in the white room. "Not capture. No survivors."

Then she fed the warrant to the flames.

The vellum caught slowly—it was fine stock, treated against damp—and she held it by one corner and watched the Cantor's beautiful handwriting curl and blacken, the open eye blistering, the seal cracking like a knuckle. When it was ash she ground the ash into the dirt with her boot heel, and then she sat back on her heels and waited to feel free.

She did not feel free. She felt empty, scoured, like a house after the furniture's been repossessed. Everything she was had been issued to her: the name, the rank, the doctrine, the purpose. Burn the warrant and what remained? A woman of forty-one with a sword, a horse, and a talent for hunting people, in a world with no stars.

But emptiness, she thought, was at least honest. Emptiness had room in it.

She rose, went back through the dark to the camp, and took three things from the supply packs without waking anyone: dried meat, a flint, and the map of the western passes. Then she paused at Dennick's bedroll. He slept with his warrant case under his head like a child with a doll, and his face in sleep was young, so young.

She crouched, and for a moment her hand hovered near his shoulder—to wake him, to warn him, to say *run, boy, run while the running's good.* But she had no right. She had helped build the cage he slept in. All she could give him was the same thing no one had given her: a chance to notice.

So she left instead a note, three lines in her precise clerk-taught hand: *Read your orders again. Ask who benefits. The border-stone report is in the Office archive, unopened, filed under my name.* Let him find it or not. Let him burn or bloom. She could not carry him too.

Then Sera Vane walked out of the camp of the Order of the Veiled Word, past the sleeping horses, past the picket line she herself had designed, and no one stopped her, because no one had ever imagined needing to.

* * *

The mountains did not care that she had burned her warrant.

That was the lesson of the next two days, delivered without mercy. She had planned for a hard climb; she had not planned for the storm, which came down off the high passes on the second evening like something summoned, wind that screamed in the crags and snow that fell sideways and a cold that got inside her cloak, inside her gloves, inside the spaces between her thoughts. Twice she lost the trail the fugitives' cart had left—a cart! They were fleeing the most powerful institution in the world with a vegetable cart, and somehow that undid her more than anything, that small stubborn domestic image—and twice she found it again only by luck and stubbornness, reading wheel-ruts under new snow like scripture.

She ate the dried meat. She ate, on the third day, things she did not name. The flint failed in the wet and she spent one night walking in circles to keep her blood moving, talking aloud to keep her mind anchored, reciting—incredibly, helplessly—the catechisms of her childhood, *the Word is veiled, the veiled word listens,* until she caught herself and swore and started reciting ward-smith's cant instead, technical nonsense she'd memorized from confiscated books, anything, anything but the prayers.

On the fourth day she understood she was going to die on this mountain, and the strange thing was how little it frightened her. She had died already, in a way, four nights ago beside a small fire. This body just hadn't gotten the message yet.

It was nearly dark when she smelled woodsmoke.

She followed it the way a drowning woman follows light—not skill anymore, just animal need—down a sheltered fold of the slope to where a fire glowed orange through the snow-haze, ringed by figures. She saw the big silhouette of the ward-smith first, then a broad woman with her sleeves rolled despite the cold, stirring a pot with the authority of someone who had fed people her whole life. A thin figure sat close to the wounded man's bedroll. And near the fire, wrapped in a blanket, a boy with a face she recognized from a hundred temple portraits—Quentin, the last voice, the fugitive the Cantor wanted silenced.

Every trained instinct in her said: *this is the arrest of your career. Walk in, name yourself, take them.* She had rehearsed arrests her entire life. She knew exactly how it would go, the first thirty seconds of it, word for word.

Instead Sera Vane fell to her knees in the snow at the edge of the firelight, and the words that came out were not the words of the Order at all.

"I burned it," she said. Her voice came out ruined, scraped raw by wind and by something else. "The warrant. It said no survivors. He wrote it himself—I have the memory of it in my hands, the wax was black, he wrote it himself—and I burned it, and I've been walking for four days, and I don't—" Her teeth were chattering too hard to finish.

The camp had gone still. The big man, Brannoc, was on his feet with a hammer in his fist. The landlady had put herself between the boy and the stranger, wooden spoon raised like a weapon, and Sera almost laughed—at the spoon, at herself, at the whole absurd tableau—except that laughing would have shattered something she needed intact.

The scribe stepped forward. Freya. She knew the face from the wanted circulars, though the circulars had made them look dangerous and they looked mostly exhausted, sharp-eyed, wary as a market cat. Freya studied her for a long moment, head tilted, and Sera had the vertiginous sense of being read like a document—her frost-burned cheeks, her temple-issue boots, the absence of any weapon drawn.

"You're Vane," Freya said. "The hunter."

"I was."

"Hunters don't kneel."

"This one's been untaught," Sera said. "Four days ago I'd have put a bolt through the boy and called it duty. I'm telling you that so you know exactly what you're deciding about. I'm not asking you to trust me. I'm telling you I burned the only proof of who I was, and I walked into your fire because I don't have another one."

Silence, except the wind. The boy, Quentin, was looking at her with an expression she couldn't read—fear, or pity, or something older than either.

It was the landlady who moved first. Maren—she knew that too, from the circulars, *aiding and abetting*—Maren set down the spoon, looked her up and down with the frank appraisal of a woman pricing damaged goods, and said:

"She's half-dead and she'll freeze before midnight. Get her by the fire. We'll decide what she's worth when she can hold a cup without shaking."

Brannoc grunted. "Maren—"

"She can hold a cup without shaking," Maren repeated, "or she can be somebody else's problem. Those are the options. Sit down, Inquisitor. Soup's on."

And that was how Sera Vane, First Chair of the Office of Pursuit, defected: dragged by the collar of her frozen cloak to a fire that wasn't hers, handed a tin cup by a woman who owned her nothing, while a boy with a broken voice watched her across the flames with eyes that seemed to weigh not what she had done, but what she might yet do.

She drank the soup. It was the best thing she had ever tasted, and she hated herself for thinking so, and she drank it anyway.

Above the fold of the valley, the clouds shifted for a moment—and in that moment, impossibly, faintly, one point of light showed through where no star should be able to shine. Quentin went very still, staring up at it, his lips parting around a soundless note.

"There," he whispered. "Did you see—"

The clouds closed. But no one at the fire moved for a long time afterward, and Sera Vane, holding her cup in both scarred hands, understood that she had walked out of one war and straight into the edge of another—one fought with songs instead of swords, on a mountain where the gods themselves were beginning, very carefully, to listen.
