## 18. Trust Is a Debt Too

The fire had burned down to its red bones by the time anyone spoke again.

Freya sat with their back against the cart wheel, Maren's wool blanket around their shoulders, and watched the inquisitor across the flames. Sera Vane had not moved from the spot where she'd half-collapsed an hour ago—knees drawn up, hands still wrapped around her cup as if it were the only solid thing left in the world. Snow melted in her hair. She hadn't brushed it away.

Brannoc stood at the edge of the firelight with his arms crossed, and even from here Freya could see the tremor in them—not fear, exactly. Something older than fear. The look of a man watching a wall he'd built himself come walking toward him wearing armor.

"So," Brannoc said. "Are we going to talk about it, or are we all going to sit here pretending she's not temple?"

"Sera," the woman said quietly. "If we're going to argue about me, you might as well use my name."

"Oh, we're on first-name terms now, are we?" Brannoc's laugh was a dry, ugly thing. "I built those border wards, girl. Fifteen years of my life bent into stone and oath. And when they came apart like wet paper, do you know who came to ask me questions? Men and women dressed exactly like you. They burned my forge. They put my name on a list." He jabbed a finger toward her. "You're wearing that list on your chest."

"I know what I'm wearing." Her voice didn't rise. That was the unsettling part, Freya thought. Inquisitors were supposed to be loud. This one spoke like someone setting stones in a wall—one at a time, carefully, because she knew what would happen if any of them shifted. "I've been wearing it since I was nine years old. That's rather the point."

"Point being?"

"Being that I took it off." She set the cup down in the snow beside her, deliberately, the way a soldier lays down a weapon where everyone can see it. "And walked three days through a storm to get here. You can check my boots if you doubt it."

Nobody checked her boots.

Quentin sat closest to her—of course he did; Quentin had no survival instincts whatsoever—and Freya wanted to shake him for it. The boy had draped his own cloak over her shoulders when she'd arrived, half-frozen and stammering out her defection like a confession, and now he sat there watching the argument with his throat bare and his whole stupid trusting face turned toward the firelight.

Maren broke the silence the way she broke everything: practically, and slightly too loud. "Well. She's not going to freeze to death sitting at my fire, that's decided. Anyone who wants her gone is welcome to carry her somewhere warmer themselves." She looked around the circle, daring them. "No? Then pour the girl some more tea and let's hear it properly."

"There's nothing to hear," Brannoc said. "It's a trick. It's the oldest trick there is. You crack one of them, you turn them, and you send them walking back to their handlers with a story about a crisis of faith. Meanwhile they've counted our numbers, learned our route, and marked where we sleep." He spat into the snow. "I've seen it done. I helped design the protocols, gods rot me."

"You designed the wards too," Freya said.

Everyone looked at them. Freya hadn't meant to speak. But the words were already out, hanging in the cold air, and there was nothing to do but finish.

"And how did that work out?"

Brannoc flinched as if struck. "That's not fair."

"No," Freya agreed. "It isn't. Nothing about this is fair. That's rather the whole shape of the problem." They pulled the blanket tighter and made themselves keep looking at Sera Vane—at the frostbitten tips of her ears, the way her weight favored her left side, the small telltale stiffness of someone who had marched too far on a bad ankle and said nothing about it. "But if we're going to decide whether to trust her, let's at least decide based on something better than whose guilt is oldest."

* * *

Here is what Freya did not say, sitting there by the fire: that eleven days ago, in a back room above a dye-shop in Corvane, a soft-voiced temple clerk had slid a ledger across a table and offered them a choice.

*Your debt, discharged in full,* the clerk had said. *Fifteen years of interest, forgiven. All you need do is describe the choirboy. Where he sleeps. What he eats. What makes him laugh.*

They had known Quentin for four days at that point. Four days, and the clerk had somehow known it, which was its own kind of horror—the temple's eyes were everywhere, had always been everywhere, and Freya's fifteen years of careful invisibility had been a curtain, not a wall.

They had looked at that ledger for a long time. Long enough that the clerk had smiled, thinking the silence was calculation.

Then Freya had asked to see the interest calculations, and while the clerk's finger traced the columns, they had memorized the seal impression on the wax, the watermark in the paper, the exact phrasing of the forgiveness clause—and walked out with nothing but their soul intact and a plan forming that would take another six days to bear fruit.

They had never told Quentin. There hadn't seemed to be a reason. It was over; the ledger was ash; the debt collectors had learned what happened when you cornered a scribe who kept better records than you did.

But sitting here now, watching Brannoc build his case against Sera Vane brick by reasonable brick, Freya found the memory rising like water in a cellar. Because the clerk had been right about one thing: everyone could be bought. Everyone had a price, or a fear, or a person they couldn't bear to lose. The temple's entire architecture rested on that certainty. Freya had simply been lucky—or stubborn enough—that their price had turned out to be higher than fifteen years of servitude.

What if Sera's price was lower? What if hers had already been paid?

* * *

"Say we believe her," Maren said, refilling Sera's cup without asking. "Say she's genuinely turned her coat. What then? She's still trained. Still dangerous. Still—" Maren waved a hand at the sword lying in the snow beside the inquisitor's knee, "—armed."

"I'll give up the blade," Sera said.

"Don't be absurd," Brannoc snapped. "You'll need it. We all will, if half of what the boy says about the mountain is true. Giving up your sword doesn't make you safe, it makes you useless, and useless people get other people killed." He rubbed his face with both hands. "Gods. I can't believe I'm arguing for the inquisitor to keep her weapon."

"That's because you're honest," Sera said, "even when it costs you. It's rarer than you think. In the Order we called it a liability."

"What did you call it," Quentin asked softly, "when you stopped believing?"

The question landed in the middle of the fire like a dropped coal. Sera stared at him for a long moment, and Freya saw something move behind her eyes—something that had been held very carefully in place for days, maybe years, and was now beginning, against her will, to shift.

"We had a saying during training," she said finally. "*The Word is veiled so that the unworthy cannot read it.* I recited it every morning for twenty-two years. And then I found a requisition order signed by the High Cantor's own hand authorizing the removal of ward-stones from the border—removal, not repair—and I went to my mentor, the man who taught me everything, the man I loved like a father. And you know what he said?" Her mouth twisted. "He said, *Vane, the veil exists for a reason. Some words are not for us.* As if that were an answer. As if 'not for us' had ever once, in the history of the world, been a reason instead of an excuse."

She picked up a stick and drew a line in the snow between herself and the rest of them. Deliberate. Visible.

"I'm not asking you to trust me. Trust is earned over years and I have given you hours. I'm asking for something smaller. Let me walk with you as far as the mountain. Put me on the outside of every camp. Give me the worst watch. If I betray you, you lose nothing you wouldn't have lost anyway—and you gain a sword arm and twenty-two years of knowing exactly how the temple hunts." She looked up, and her eyes were steady and terribly tired. "That's not trust. That's arithmetic. I'm told scribes respect arithmetic."

Freya's breath caught, just slightly.

Because that was exactly the offer Freya would have made, in her position. Exactly the terms. Start small. Prove the sum before asking anyone to carry it forward.

* * *

"She's right, you know," Freya said.

Brannoc turned on them. "Whose side are you—"

"The side that gets us up the mountain alive." Freya stood, letting the blanket fall, and stepped into the space between the argument and the fire. Their heart was pounding, which annoyed them; this wasn't supposed to be difficult. It was supposed to be obvious. "Let me tell you all a story, since apparently we're having confessions tonight."

Quentin went still. He knew. Freya could see it in his face—he knew what story was coming, or at least suspected, and his eyes went wide and worried in the firelight.

"Eleven days ago," Freya said, "a clerk from the Temple of the Veiled Word offered to erase my debt. All of it. Fifteen years, gone, in exchange for one small service." They paused. "They wanted me to give up Quentin."

The sound Maren made was half gasp, half growl. Brannoc swore. Quentin just closed his eyes, briefly, as if he'd already done his grieving for it and come out the other side.

"You didn't," he said. Not a question.

"I burned my ledger instead. You were there for that part." Freya tried for a smile and mostly managed it. "But here's the thing I want you all to understand. For about ten seconds—maybe less—I considered it. Ten seconds where freedom looked like a door standing open, and all I had to do was walk through it and leave a boy behind." Their voice caught on the last word and they pushed through it. "Ten seconds. I'm not proud of them. But I know what they felt like. I know how reasonable the door looks when you're the one standing in front of it."

They turned to face Sera Vane directly.

"So when I look at her, I don't see a temple hunter pretending. I see someone who stood in front of the same door I did—and I know exactly how heavy it is, because I've leaned on it. And I know that when she says she's not asking for trust, she means it, because that's what you say when you understand that trust isn't yours to demand anymore." Freya spread their hands. "She's offering arithmetic. Fine. Let's do the arithmetic. One inquisitor who knows the Cantor's methods, his routes, his mind—and who has already proven she'll burn her own warrant rather than follow orders she knows are wrong. Against the risk that she's the best actress the Order ever produced." They shrugged. "I've seen the Order's work up close for fifteen years. They don't make actresses. They make hammers. Hammers don't defect."

"Hammers don't cry either," Brannoc muttered, "and I saw her crying when she walked in."

"Brannoc." Maren's voice cut like a knife through bread. "You cried for a week under that bridge and nobody questioned your loyalty."

"That was different."

"It was exactly the same, and you know it." Maren planted her fists on her hips, and suddenly she was every landlady who had ever defended a tenant against the world, five feet of fury wrapped in a shawl. "That woman walked through a mountain storm on a bad ankle because she read one piece of paper and couldn't un-read it. I've run a shop in Corvane for thirty years. I know what conviction looks like when it costs something. It looks cold, and hungry, and it doesn't ask for anything." She jerked her chin at Sera. "Has she asked for a single thing since she got here?"

Silence.

"Well?" Maren demanded.

"No," Quentin said quietly. "She hasn't."

* * *

They talked until the moon crossed the ridge and started down the other side.

Brannoc held out longest—not out of malice, Freya realized eventually, but out of grief. Every argument he made was really about the wards, about the forge, about the name on the list, about the fifteen years he'd spent drinking under Hollow Bridge because the world had taken his life's work and used it as a murder weapon. Sera Vane was just the nearest target wearing temple colors.

But near the end, something shifted. Sera said, quietly, almost to the fire: "I knew your name. From the files. Brannoc Ostrewein, ward-smith, first grade. Your joint-work on the Ashford anchor-stones was required reading. We studied how you'd balanced the flows." A pause. "It was beautiful work. Whoever unmade it understood it well enough to hurt it precisely. That takes... a terrible kind of respect."

Brannoc stared at her. And Freya watched something pass across his ruined face—shame meeting a mirror, guilt recognizing guilt—and when he finally spoke, his voice was rough as gravel.

"The anchor-stones were the weak point. I always knew it. I told them. Nobody listened." He looked away. "Fine. She walks with us. Outside of camp. Worst watch. But if she so much as hums a hymn, I'm throwing her off the mountain myself."

"Noted," Sera said, and there was—gods help them all—a flicker of humor at the corner of her mouth. Brief. Quickly mastered. But real.

Maren declared the matter settled by fiat, the way she settled everything, and began banking the fire for the night with the satisfied air of a woman who had known how the vote would go before anyone opened their mouths.

* * *

Later, when the others had rolled into their bedrolls, Freya stayed up. Old habit. Someone had to keep the last watch, and their eyes had never needed much sleep.

Sera was on the far edge of camp, as promised, sitting upright against a boulder with her sword across her knees. When Freya padded over and sat down an arm's length away—close enough to talk, far enough to honor the terms—she didn't react beyond a slight tilt of her head.

"You lied," Freya said quietly.

"I beg your pardon?"

"In there. You said you weren't asking for trust. Just arithmetic." Freya pulled their knees up, watching the dead stars. "But you cried when you walked into camp. Brannoc was right. Hammers don't cry. Whatever brought you here, it isn't arithmetic. It's the same thing that was in that ledger room with me eleven days ago. The thing that makes ten seconds feel like drowning."

Sera was silent for a long time. Snow ticked softly down through the pines.

"My induction class had forty children in it," she said at last. "They took us at eight, nine years old. Taught us that the veil protected us, that the old magic was a disease, that mercy toward practitioners was a contagion we had to be stronger than." Her thumb moved along the flat of her blade, back and forth, a metronome. "Thirty-one of us survived training. I told myself for twenty years that the ones who died were the price of order. And then I read that requisition order, and I thought: if the veil protects us, why does it need to lie to us?" She looked over at Freya, and in the dark her eyes were very clear. "You're right. It isn't arithmetic. Arithmetic is what I tell myself so I can stand to be near people I've spent my life hunting. The truth is I'm asking. I just can't afford to let it look like asking."

Freya nodded slowly. "I know the feeling."

"Do you." Not quite a question. Sera's gaze drifted toward the cart, where Quentin slept curled under three blankets with his mouth slightly open, breathing the slow deep breaths of someone who had sung himself empty and would sing again tomorrow regardless. "You gave up fifteen years of safety for him. After four days."

"After four days," Freya agreed.

"Why?"

The question hung there. Freya turned it over, examining it from all sides the way they examined everything, and found—to their mild surprise—that they actually had an answer. Had probably had it for days, tucked away where they didn't have to look at it.

"Because he sang to a dying bird once," Freya said. "And the bird lived. And when he told me about it, he wasn't proud. He was frightened. He said, *what am I, that things live when I ask them to?*" They smiled at the remembered absurdity of it. "Fifteen years I hid what I was because I thought power was something that got people killed. And then along comes this ridiculous boy with the most dangerous gift in the world, and all he wants to know is whether it makes him good or bad. As if it works that way. As if the gift decides." Freya shrugged. "I couldn't hand a person like that to the temple. It would've been like handing them a hymn and telling them it was a curse."

Sera was quiet for a moment. Then, unexpectedly, she laughed—a short, rusty sound, like a hinge remembering how to move.

"A hymn and telling them it was a curse," she repeated. "That's the Order in one sentence. We should put it on the door."

"Careful," Freya said. "That was almost wit. Brannoc will throw you off the mountain."

They sat together in the cold after that, not talking, and it was a strange and fragile thing, this almost-companionship—but Freya had learned to recognize fragile things. Charms were fragile. Wards were fragile. Covenants most of all. Fragile was not the same as doomed. Fragile was just honest about what it cost to hold.

Somewhere past the smallest hour of the night, Sera spoke again, barely above the wind.

"There's something I haven't told the others. I was waiting until—" She stopped. Started again. "The requisition order I found. It wasn't only about the border wards. There was a second page. Supply manifests. Starlight vials, hundreds of them, routed to a place called the First Hearth. Months ago. Before we ever fled the city."

Freya went cold all over, in a way that had nothing to do with the snow.

"He's already there," Freya said.

"He's ahead of us," Sera corrected grimly. "There's a difference. It means we still have a road. But Freya—" she turned, and the starless dark seemed to press in close around her words, "—whatever he's building at that hearth, he started building it before any of us knew there was a war. We aren't chasing him. We're arriving late to something he's been planning for years."

Above the ridge, the clouds shifted again. Held, this time. And through the gap, faint and trembling and impossibly far away, two points of light showed where the stars should be—as if whatever muffled voice answered Quentin's songs was learning, note by note, to push back.

Freya looked at them and felt the old fear and the new purpose settle together in their chest, twined tight as a knot.

"Then we'd better not be late twice," they said, and reached for the map.
