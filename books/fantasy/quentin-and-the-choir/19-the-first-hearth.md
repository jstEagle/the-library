## 19. The First Hearth

The mountain had a name once. Every map in Corvane called it something different—Vael's Crown on the temple charts, the Old Man's Tooth in the market districts, the Spine of the World according to Brannoc, who claimed he'd helped set the last ward-stone on its summit and had the scars to prove it. But Quentin had learned, somewhere between Hollow Bridge and the foothills, that the mountain's true name was the one nobody said aloud: *the place where singing began.*

He felt it before he saw it. Three days into the climb, when the pines gave way to bare grey stone and the wind came down off the snowfields carrying nothing but cold and silence, he felt it—a pressure behind his breastbone, like a held breath. Like standing in the choir loft before the first note of vespers, when the whole chapel waited for him.

"It knows we're coming," he said.

Maren, hauling the supply sledge over a shelf of ice, didn't look up. "It can know we're coming and still be a mountain. Mountains don't care about much."

"This one does." He pressed his mittened hand flat against his sternum. "It's like—like an empty church. You can feel where the music used to live."

Brannoc stopped beside him, squinting up the switchback trail toward the white shoulder of the peak. The old ward-smith's face was weathered to leather by forty years of exile, but just then it looked younger and more afraid than Quentin had ever seen it. "Aye," Brannoc said quietly. "That's the hearth calling. Or what's left of it. I haven't heard that sound since—" He stopped. Swallowed. "Since the day they sealed me out."

* * *

They climbed because there was no other way. The pass was closed by storm for half the year, Sera said, and the other half by things worse than weather; their only window was now, in the thin amber weeks of late autumn, and even now the mountain did not forgive mistakes.

Quentin counted their company each morning the way he'd once counted the choir stalls: seven voices, six of them mortal. Freya walked at the front with the hearth-shard wrapped in three layers of wool against their chest, because the shard had begun to hum whenever Quentin sang, and humming shards attracted attention they couldn't afford. Maren drove the sledge and complained about the altitude with the fluency of a woman who had run a shop for thirty years and considered weather a personal insult. Brannoc navigated by scars—literally, tracing the old ward-lines with his fingers against cliff faces only he could read. Sera scouted ahead in her stripped-down inquisitor's leathers, moving through snow as if she'd been born to it, which, Quentin suspected, in some terrible way, she had.

And Freya—

Freya walked beside him. Not always. Not obviously. But whenever the trail narrowed, whenever the wind screamed off the cornices, whenever the world got too large and too white, Quentin would look sideways and find them there, close enough that their shoulders nearly touched, saying nothing, offering nothing except the fact of themselves.

"You're staring," Freya said on the fourth morning, without turning their head.

"I'm not."

"You've been staring since the second switchback. It's very distracting. Some of us are trying to calculate how many days of dried plums we have left."

"Eleven," Quentin said. "Twelve if Maren stops eating them straight from the bag."

"Thirteen if you two stop gossiping about my plums," Maren called back, and the little laugh that went down the line of them—small and breathless in the thin air—was worth more than Quentin could say. He hoarded those laughs now. He'd learned on this climb that laughter was a ration too, and that a group that stopped laughing started thinking about turning back, and a group that turned back died in the lowlands under inquisitor blades.

So he laughed, and climbed, and tried not to think about what waited at the top.

* * *

The mountain tested them one at a time. That was the thing Quentin understood only slowly, watching his family come apart and knit itself back together across five brutal days: the First Hearth did not want just anyone. It wanted people who would keep walking.

It tested Maren first. On the second night above the treeline, her hands—the hands that had hidden Freya in a root cellar, that had packed this whole expedition's food, that had never once in Quentin's hearing trembled—began to shake so badly she couldn't strike flint. She sat in the lee of a boulder with her fists in her lap and stared at them as if they belonged to a stranger.

"Fifty-three years old," she said to no one. "Fifty-three years of kneading dough and hauling crates, and now they're giving out on a mountainside like an old woman's hands. Which is what they are. An old woman's hands."

"They're the hands that saved my life," Freya said, crouching in front of her. "Twice. Once from the inquisitors and once from your own cooking. I've never told anyone about the stew incident, and I never will."

Maren snorted despite herself. But it was Quentin who took her shaking hands in his and held them until the trembling slowed—not with magic, just with warmth, just with waiting—and when she finally got the fire lit herself, refusing all help, she looked around the circle with wet eyes and dared anyone to comment.

Nobody did. But that night Sera took over the dawn watch without being asked, and Maren slept four hours longer than she had any night before.

It tested Sera next. On the third day they crossed a field of old battle-bones—ward-stone fragments, Brannoc said, from the purge two centuries past, when the temple had shattered every free shrine in the high country. Sera walked among the ruins of her order's great work and said nothing for nine hours. At dusk Quentin found her standing over a broken lintel carved with singing figures, their mouths open, their throats deliberately chiseled away.

"I trained in a hall built from stones like these," she said. Her voice was flat as slate. "I prayed every morning beneath a ceiling made of stolen shrines, and I never once asked where the stones came from." She looked at Quentin. "You should hate me. It would be simpler if you hated me."

"I don't do simple," Quentin said. "Ask anyone."

Something moved at the corner of her mouth—not quite a smile, but the ground where a smile might someday build. She walked on. And the next morning she was the one who found the safe line through the icefall, reading the mountain the way she'd once read fugitives, and Quentin understood that the hearth hadn't been testing whether Sera deserved to climb. It had been testing whether she'd stop climbing. She didn't.

It tested Brannoc worst of all. On the fifth day they reached the sealed gate.

* * *

It stood in a notch of the ridge like a door left ajar by giants: two pillars of black ward-stone, twenty feet tall, framing a passage of pure darkness that no wind entered and no light escaped. The stone was covered in script—covenant-script, the old tongue that predated the temple, that predated writing itself, if Brannoc was to be believed. And across the threshold lay a seal of white light, thin as frost on a window, humming faintly.

Brannoc stood before it and did not move.

"Brannoc?" Freya said gently.

"I put this seal here." His voice came out scraped raw. "Two hundred years ago. No—" He dragged a hand down his face. "Forty years ago. It feels like two hundred. They brought me up here in chains, after the trial, and they made me seal my own master's door. Made me sing the closing-verse myself, because a ward laid by a willing smith holds better, and I was—" His laugh was terrible. "I was still willing, then. Still believed the temple knew what it was doing. So I closed the door on the First Hearth, and I walked down this mountain, and I drank for forty years because every time I closed my eyes I heard the door shut."

The seal hummed. Quentin stepped closer, and the pressure behind his breastbone swelled until it ached. Through the white light he could almost see—shapes, movement, a vast dim room beyond, and at its center a darkness deeper than the dark, a cold hearth like a dead eye.

"It won't open for force," Brannoc said. "Nothing forces this door. It opens for—" He stopped.

"A voice," Quentin said.

Everyone turned to him.

"The covenant-script. Look at it." He traced the air above the carvings without touching. "It's not words, exactly. It's notation. It's a *line of music*. The whole gate is one long held chord, and the seal is the rest between phrases." His heart was pounding strangely. "Someone has to sing the next phrase. Someone has to take up the song where it stopped."

"And if you sing wrong?" Maren asked.

"Then presumably the mountain eats us," Freya said. "Cheerfully. In alphabetical order."

"Freya."

"I'm serious. Look at his face, Maren. He's going to do it regardless. We might as well let him."

Quentin unbound his scarf. The cold hit his throat like a blade, and he welcomed it—welcomed anything sharp enough to cut through the fear. Because here was the truth he hadn't spoken aloud, not to Freya, not to anyone: ever since the ruined shrine, ever since the flicker where a star should be had answered his voice, he had known what the reforge would cost. Brannoc's research in the antechamber texts was still ahead of them, but Quentin had felt the shape of the price in his bones the way you feel weather coming. A true voice. Given, not lent.

*Given.*

He looked at the door. He thought about the dying bird in the temple courtyard, twelve years old, its heart stuttering under his cupped hands, and the impossible warmth that had poured out of him and into it, and how the bird had flown away and he had lost his voice for three days and wept the whole time, not from pain but from *fullness*, from having held more than a body could hold.

"Stand back," he said.

"Quentin—" Freya started.

"Stand back. Please."

They formed a crescent behind him: Maren with her jaw set, Brannoc with his eyes closed, Sera with her hand resting on a knife she wouldn't use, and Freya closest of all, close enough that when Quentin breathed in, he breathed in their steadiness too.

He sang the phrase.

He didn't choose it—it chose him, rising out of the pressure behind his sternum like water finding a crack. It was old. It was older than the temple, older than Corvane, older than any hymn scraped or unscraped, and it moved through him the way grief moves through a house, room to room, touching everything. The seal brightened. The covenant-script along the pillars began to glow, character by character, following his melody like a congregation finding the page. The white light thinned, stretched, shivered—

—and parted, folding aside like a curtain drawn by courteous hands.

Cold air rolled out of the dark, ancient and mineral-smelling, and beneath it, faint as a memory of a memory, Quentin caught it: the ghost of ten thousand voices, layered centuries deep, still echoing in a chamber built to hold song.

The First Hearth stood open before them.

"Right," said Maren, into the silence. "Who's carrying the torches?"

* * *

The descent into the mountain took an hour and lasted a lifetime. Torches hissed and guttered in air that seemed to drink flame; the stair spiraled down through rock veined with dull silver—dead starlight, Brannoc whispered, the residue of every covenant ever sung. Quentin kept one hand on the wall and one on the strap of Freya's pack, and neither of them mentioned it, and both of them knew.

The chamber, when they reached it, stole the breath from everyone at once.

It was vast beyond sense—a hollowed heart of the mountain, its ceiling lost in darkness overhead, its walls carved tier upon tier like an amphitheater, like a choir loft built for gods. And at its center, sunk into the living stone, lay the hearth itself: a basin thirty feet across, ringed with seven thrones of black basalt, holding nothing but ash. Grey, fine, undisturbed ash, deep as snowdrifts, the sediment of a fire that had gone out so long ago that empires had risen and fallen in the dark it left behind.

Quentin walked to the edge of the basin. His footsteps echoed absurdly loud. He knelt, and pressed his palm to the ash, and it was warm.

Not hot. Not alive. But warm the way a bed is warm after someone has just left it.

"It's still here," he whispered. "Underneath. It never fully died."

"No," said a voice from the shadows of the thrones.

Every torch swung up. Sera had her knife out before the echo faded—but the figure rising from the seventh throne was no soldier. It was barely a figure at all: a suggestion of robes, a suggestion of a face, drawn in outlines of dim silver light, translucent as breath on glass. Where its eyes should have been there were two small points of darkness, and inside the darkness, impossibly, stars.

Quentin's knees nearly buckled. He had sung to a god once, in a ruined shrine, and received a flicker. This was the flicker's source. This was small—a diminished thing, worn down to a whisper of itself—but it was *vast* underneath, the way the ocean is vast underneath a tidepool.

"You came," the god said, and its voice was every voice Quentin had ever heard in a choir, layered and frayed and achingly tired. "Four hundred years, and someone finally climbed."

"We came," Freya said, stepping forward, one hand pressed to the shard beneath their coat. Their voice shook only a little. "But we need to talk about the price."

The god's star-filled eyes turned to Freya. Then to Quentin. And Quentin felt the hearth beneath his hand pulse once, faintly, like a heart remembering its work—and somewhere far above them, muffled by a thousand feet of stone, the first flakes of an early winter storm began to fall on the mountain's crown.

Behind them, unnoticed, at the mouth of the stair, the torchlight bent—as though the mountain had exhaled—or as though something else had just begun, far below, to climb.
