## 20. Voices in the Snow

The pass had no name on any map Freya had ever copied, which should have been the first warning. Unnamed places were where the ledger-keepers of the world stopped keeping accounts, and unaccounted things did as they pleased.

They had been climbing since dawn, and by midday the mountain had swallowed the sun. Snow came down in long, deliberate sheets, like a scribe ruling lines across the world before writing something terrible upon it. The wind found every gap in Freya's coat and wrote its own commentary there.

"Tell me again," Maren said from the front of the line, hauling the cart's remains—now little more than a sledge—over a shelf of ice, "why we couldn't have taken the valley road."

"Because the valley road," Brannoc said, "is where the Cantor's riders are."

"And this is better?"

"This," Brannoc said, gesturing at the white nothing around them, "has the advantage of being terrible for everyone equally."

Quentin laughed—a small, fogged breath of a laugh—and Freya felt it land somewhere under their ribs and stay there. Three days since the ruined shrine, three days since that flicker in the dark where a star should be, and the boy had not stopped humming. He hummed when he walked. He hummed when he ate. He hummed now, some shapeless ascending thing, and Freya noticed, with a scribe's precision, that the snow seemed to fall *around* the sound rather than through it.

Then the humming stopped.

Quentin stood very still in the middle of the trail, head tilted, the way a dog listens to a door no one else can hear knocking.

"What is it?" Sera asked. Her hand had gone to her sword out of pure habit; she made herself take it away again.

"There's someone singing," Quentin said. "Under the wind."

Freya listened. Wind, and wind, and wind—the mountain's one dull note. But then, beneath it, so faint it might have been blood moving in their own ears: a voice. A woman's voice, singing a lullaby in a dialect Freya didn't know and yet somehow knew, the way you know a word you've never read because your mouth was shaped by people who spoke it.

"Maren," Freya said quietly. "Do you hear that?"

Maren's face had gone grey. Not frightened, exactly. Something older than frightened.

"Aye," she said. "I hear my mother."

* * *

The echoes came for them one at a time, which was perhaps the only mercy in it.

It took Freya first. One moment they were walking behind Brannoc's broad, stooped back, and the next the snow had thickened into a wall, soft as wool, and when they turned to call out to the others there was no others. No trail. No pass. Only a kitchen.

That was the wrong part—that it wasn't snow at all anymore, but a kitchen, small and low-ceilinged, with a blackened kettle and a table scarred by knife-cuts and a window fogged with steam. Freya knew that table. They had cut their name into it with a nail when they were five, badly spelled, *FREYA* with the Y backwards, and their mother had laughed instead of scolding and said, *Well, the letters know who they belong to even if they're shy about showing it.*

The letters were still there. Thirty years of other hands had scrubbed that table and the letters remained, faint as a scar, and Freya stood in the doorway of a kitchen that had burned down before they turned ten and could not move.

"Hello, love."

She was at the hearth, stirring something. She looked exactly as she had in the last memory Freya owned of her—not aged, not idealized, just *herself*, sleeves rolled, flour to the elbow, that crooked smile she'd passed on to a child she would never see grow past six years old.

"You're dead," Freya said. Their voice came out flat and strange. "You died in the fever year. I checked. I'm a scribe, I check things. The parish rolls say—"

"The parish rolls say what the temple paid them to say." Their mother set down the spoon. "Sit down, love. You look half-frozen."

"I don't have time to sit down. I'm—" Freya heard themselves say it, the sentence they had built their whole life inside of: "I'm busy."

Their mother's smile went crooked in the other direction, the sad way. "Fifteen years hiding in a market stall, selling charms to people who'd burn you if they knew, and you tell your dead mother you're busy."

"That's not fair."

"No," she agreed. "None of it was. That's rather what I've come to say."

The kettle began to sing. It was the same note, Freya realized with a lurch, as the voice under the wind. The same note as Quentin's humming. Everything on this mountain sang the same buried song.

"They sold me," Freya said. It came out of them like a splinter coming out, sudden and bloody. "You sold me. To the temple. For the debt. I've spent fifteen years telling myself you had no choice, and another fifteen telling myself you chose it gladly because I was the extra mouth, the odd one, the one who made lights with their fingers when they should have been sleeping—and I never got to ask which lie was true, because you were *dead*, and now you're here, and I still can't ask, because you're not really here, you're snow and grief wearing your apron—"

"Freya."

"—and I am *busy*, I have a god to save and a boy to keep alive and an inquisitor I'm apparently trusting now, and I do not have time to be six years old again—"

"*Freya.*"

He was in the corner chair. Their father, who had never once appeared in any of Freya's memories of that kitchen without sawdust on his arms, sat with his hands folded, patient as furniture. He had Freya's eyes. Freya had spent thirty years thinking those eyes belonged to no one.

"We didn't sell you because you were too much," their father said. "We sold you because you were *visible*. Do you understand? A child who makes light in the dark gets noticed. Noticed children get taken anyway—taken by the wardens, taken worse. We heard what the temple paid for quiet gifts. A scribe's indenture meant ink-stained fingers and a warm room. It meant you'd live."

"It meant we'd live too," their mother said, simply. "There was the debt, and there was the winter, and there was you, and we did the arithmetic that parents do when all the sums are shameful. And then the fever came for us two years later, and I have wondered ever since, wherever I am, whether you ever learned that we didn't stop looking. Whether anyone ever told you we wrote. Every year. Petitions to the temple archive, asking after our child."

Freya's legs gave out. They sat down hard on a chair that was also snow, also memory, also nothing.

"Were they answered?"

"Filed," their father said. "And filed. And filed. Your name went into a ledger, love, and ledgers are how temples make things disappear while pretending to keep them."

Freya laughed. It was not a good laugh. "I know. I work in one."

"Not anymore," their mother said. She crossed the kitchen and knelt, and took Freya's frozen hands in warm ones that were not there, and Freya let her, because grief is not stupid—it knows a real thing when it holds one. "Listen to me. You're climbing to the First Hearth to write something true into the world. So hear a true thing first: you were not sold because you were unwanted. You were sold because you were loved by people with no power, and power takes what it wants and calls it accounting. Don't you dare carry their arithmetic up that mountain. Write *your* sum. Write the one where nobody is a line item."

The kitchen was going. The edges of it frayed into white, the kettle's song thinning into wind. Freya grabbed at their mother's sleeve and held snow.

"One more thing," the fading voice said, and the smile in it was the crooked one, the good one. "The backwards Y. Keep it. Somewhere in you, it's still there. Things written in love don't scrub out."

Then the pass. Then the cold. Then the others, shouting Freya's name, hands hauling them upright, and Freya standing in the falling snow with tears freezing on their face and something in their chest both broken open and, for the first time in thirty years, *set straight*.

* * *

Quentin's echo wore vestments.

That was the cruelty of it. He came around a bend in the trail and there was the upper choir of the Temple of the Veiled Word, sunlit, golden, perfect—and there was himself in it, eight years old, in a surcoat two sizes too big, singing the morning hymn with his whole small body. And there, in the shadows behind the organ pipes, stood the man who had found him.

Brother Aldous. The choirmaster. Dead four years now, and still the kindest man Quentin had ever known.

"You're doing it wrong," said the echo of Brother Aldous, in the exact tone he'd used a thousand times, gentle and merciless.

"I know," Quentin said. His voice cracked on the second word, and he hated that it cracked, and then he understood—he was speaking to snow, to memory, to whatever the mountain kept—and he said it anyway, louder: "I know. I've known since the night the stars went out. I've been doing it wrong my whole life."

"Oh?" The old choirmaster stepped out of the shadow, and his face was kind, and that was the trap, and Quentin walked into it with his eyes open. "And how is that?"

"You taught me technique. Breath, placement, diction. You taught me how to be a beautiful instrument." Quentin's breath fogged and tore away in the wind. "But instruments get played. That's what happened, isn't it? That's why you're here. Because I need to hear it from you, from the one person whose approval I never stopped trying to earn. The Cantor didn't corrupt the choir. He *used* it. He used me. And I sang whatever was put in front of me and called it devotion, right up until the hymns started scraping the old names off the page, and even then I only ran when they asked me to sing *silently*. I didn't leave because it was wrong. I left because it was *embarrassing*."

"Yes," said Brother Aldous.

Quentin blinked. Of all the answers he had rehearsed against, in all the sleepless nights on the road, agreement had never been among them.

"You were a child," the choirmaster said. "Children are supposed to be played. That is what teaching is—someone older lending their judgment until yours grows in. My sin was not that I let the Cantor use you. Mine was smaller and meaner: I was proud. I had one voice in forty years that could reach past the veil, and I showed him off like a jeweled cup, and I never once asked what holding such a gift costs a boy who just wants his supper and someone to be proud of him." The old man's image wavered, snow bleeding through his shoulders. "So hear the thing I died without saying. You were never an instrument, Quentin. You were always the musician. The Cantor stole the score. Take it back."

The choir dissolved around him. The last thing to go was the sunlight, and Quentin stood alone in the grey screaming wind, and found that he was crying and humming at once, and that the melody was new. It had never been sung anywhere. It was his.

He went to find the others.

* * *

Brannoc fought his echo, which everyone agreed afterward was very like him.

They heard it before they saw it—the crash of hammers, the ring of iron on stone—and found the old ward-smith swinging his maul at empty air, tears freezing in his beard, bellowing at figures only he could see.

"I KNOW," he was roaring. "Ye think I don't know? I *built* it! Stone by stone, oath by oath, I built the border wards with these hands, and I watched 'em rot for twenty years and told myself it was age, it was weather, it was anything but the temple hollowin' 'em out from inside while I drank myself blind under a bridge!" He swung again. The maul passed through a figure in old ward-smith's leathers—himself, younger, steady-handed, sober. "Say it plain, damn ye! Say I failed!"

The young Brannoc did not flinch. "Ye did," it said. "And then ye didn't."

"What?"

"A ward isn't the stone. Any fool knows that. A ward is the *oath*, and the stone just remembers it." The young man pointed the hammer at him, at the present Brannoc, at the drunk and the exile and the guilt. "Ye've been sayin' for twenty years that the wards failed. Wrong. The wards were murdered, and ye're the only soul alive who knows how they were built—which means ye're the only soul alive who knows how they can be *rebuilt*. Ye didn't climb this mountain to die in the snow, old man. Ye climbed it because some part of ye finally did the arithmetic. Shame's just love with nowhere to go. Give it somewhere to go."

Brannoc lowered the maul. Stood breathing. Then he picked up a fallen branch, snapped it to length, and began, with shaking hands, to trace a ward-sign in the snow—an old one, a foundation sign, the first thing an apprentice learns.

It held. The snow around it stopped churning, just there, just a small circle of calm in all that white.

"Huh," Brannoc said, to no one. Then, louder, wet-eyed, grinning like a man reprieved: "*Hah!*"

* * *

Sera Vane's echo was the hardest to watch, and mercifully, no one watched it.

The snow took her twenty paces off the trail, into a courtyard she had not stood in since she was nine years old: the inquisitors' training yard, the one with the sand floor and the bell that rang when a novice made her first kill-shot. Her instructor stood there. Mother Cassia, dead a decade, the woman who had lifted Sera out of a debtor's orphanage and told her she had been chosen.

"You hesitated," Mother Cassia said, in the courtyard, in the memory, in the moment that had made her. "At Ashford Crossing. On the ridge. Tell me why."

"She was shielding the boy," Sera said. "Maren. Old woman, fire poker, no chance. I had the shot and I—I moved my bow hand two inches left."

"Why."

"Because she reminded me of the matron at the orphanage. Because she'd fed us strangers the night before and asked nothing. Because—" Sera's voice did what voices do when they hit bedrock. "Because everything I've seen since I started following them says the Cantor is lying, and I have built my entire self on the belief that the temple doesn't lie."

Mother Cassia nodded slowly, the way she used to before the worst lessons. "When you were nine, I told you doubt was a crack in armor. That was a lie too, and I want you to know I knew it. Doubt is not a crack, Sera. Doubt is the feeling of a load-bearing wall being tested. If it holds, you'll stand truer on it than you ever stood on faith." The courtyard thinned. The last thing her teacher said, in a voice almost lost under the wind: "Test it. All the way up the mountain. And when it holds—*build on it.*"

Sera came back to the trail with her jaw set and her warrant—her false report already drafted in her head—burning brighter in her mind than any fire.

* * *

Maren's echo lasted the shortest, and she spoke of it least.

Later, by the fire, she would say only: "Mine showed me my shop. Empty. Shutters closed. Me on the step, wondering if I'd done the maddest thing of my life, leaving it to chase a scribe and a choirboy up a mountain." She snorted. "And then it showed me the shop full again. Full of *them*. All of you, eating at my table, arguing about salt. And the echo said—well. It said the shop was never the home. I'd known that for years, I suppose. I'd just been waiting for the family to walk in and sit down."

She wiped her eyes briskly, blamed the smoke, and demanded someone refill her cup.

* * *

They regrouped as the light died, six shapes converging out of the white, each carrying something invisible and heavy. No one asked what the others had seen. Everyone's face said enough. They made camp in the lee of a shattered waymarker—a shrine-post, Brannoc called it, covenant-era, its carved names worn to whispers—and Freya cast the small warming charm that was no longer a harmless charm at all, and the fire caught blue-edged and steady.

Quentin sat down next to Freya. Close. Closer than necessary. He was shivering, and Freya pretended not to notice, and put the cloak they were pretending not to offer over both their shoulders.

"Yours had a kitchen," Quentin said quietly. Not a question. The echoes left marks; you could read them on a person like marginalia.

"How did you—"

"You smell like bread." He almost smiled. "For about a minute, when you came back. Warm bread. Then it faded."

Freya stared into the fire. "My parents didn't sell me because I was a burden," they said. It cost more than the climb had. "They sold me to save me. And they spent the rest of their lives petitioning the temple to tell them where I was, and the temple filed the petitions, and I *transcribed some of them*, Quentin. Years ago. I copied a stack of unanswered family inquiries and I never once thought—" Their voice failed. Quentin's hand found theirs in the dark and stayed there, and Freya let it.

"Grief made useful," Brannoc rumbled from across the fire. He was carving ward-signs into the waymarker's base with his knife, quick and sure, the drunkard's tremor gone from his hands entirely. "That's what the mountain does. That's what the old covenant was *for*, maybe. Not to spare folk their regrets. To give the regrets a job."

"Then we're well armed," Sera said dryly, and for once no one bristled at the inquisitor's edge, because they could hear what was underneath it.

Maren ladled out stew. Quentin hummed his new melody—his own, unscored, unborrowed—and the fire burned steady, and the snow fell around the sound.

It was Freya, on last watch, who saw it.

A light on the mountain above them. Far up, near the shoulder of the peak where the First Hearth waited. Not starlight—there was no starlight. Not firelight either. It was a cold, organized glow, moving in formation, descending: lanterns in ranks, precise as a procession, precisely wrong.

Behind it, faint on the wind, came a sound that turned Freya's blood to ink-water. A choir. Hundreds of voices, singing in perfect unison—and singing *nothing*. A hymn with the music scraped off it. The silent hymn, given sound at last, and the sound was absence.

"Everyone up," Freya said. "He knows we're here. He's already on the mountain."
