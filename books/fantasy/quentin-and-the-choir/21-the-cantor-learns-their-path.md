## 21. The Cantor Learns Their Path

The report arrived at the hour when Corvane's bells had gone quiet and even the temple's own candles seemed to hold their breath.

Ovelith read it standing at the window of his study, high in the Tower of the Veiled Word, where the glass was thick enough to muffle the city and the view was all his: rooftops silvered by the hoarded light he had gathered into the vaults below, a whole skyline lit by what he had taken from heaven. No stars above, of course. There were no stars anywhere now. But there was *his* light, obedient light, light that did not require prayer or gratitude or the tedious negotiation of faith. He had made the sky himself, in a manner of speaking. It simply hung indoors.

The report was written in the cipher of the Ashen Ring, his network of beggars and toll-keepers and one remarkably well-paid apothecary in Hollowbridge. He read it twice, and on the second reading his thumb pressed hard enough against the paper to crease it.

*The scribe, the choirboy, the ward-smith, the landlady. Traveling east on the old pilgrim road. The exile Brannoc leads them past the drowned orchards toward the mountain passes.*

Toward the First Hearth.

He should have felt triumph. Instead he felt something older and less dignified — the cold drop in the stomach of a boy who has just heard footsteps behind him in a corridor where he is not supposed to be.

"They are going home," he said aloud, to the empty room, and heard how the word *home* cracked in his mouth like old glaze on a cup.

* * *

He had been nine years old the first time he stood in the hearth chamber, and he had wept, though he'd told the other novices it was the incense.

The shrine had still been tended then — this was sixty years ago, before the Quiet Edicts, before anyone thought to call the old ways dangerous. The First Hearth sat in its mountain cradle like a banked fire the size of a cathedral, and around it, carved into the living stone, ran the Choir Gallery: a crescent of worn steps where singers had stood for two thousand years, feeding their voices up into the dark. The gods had lived in that dark the way fish live in water. You could not see them. You could feel them take your song the way you feel a current tug your ankles.

Young Ovelith had climbed those steps with forty other children, all of them chosen from the provinces for pitch and piety, and when the master of the hearth gave the downbeat, they had sung the Opening Verse — the true one, not the bowdlerized thing printed in modern hymnals — and the fire had *risen*.

Not flared. Risen. Stood up out of its bed like something waking, and turned its attention on them, and every child in the gallery had felt themselves weighed.

Most of the children had been found sufficient. The gods drank their voices gladly; the boys and girls went home in spring with faint silver scars on their throats and stories no adult quite believed. Three children in that year's cohort were refused. The fire had passed over them like wind over a stone. One was a stuttering girl from the fen country who was sent home in disgrace. One fainted and was quietly dismissed as ill.

The third was Ovelith.

He remembered standing on the step afterward, throat aching, voice intact — that was the humiliation of it, his voice had never been touched, never marked, never *used* — while the fire settled back down and the master of the hearth put a hand on his shoulder and said, gently, the way you speak to the dying: "Some vessels the gods do not drink from, child. It is no fault of yours."

No fault of yours. As if that helped. As if being passed over by heaven itself could be borne as anything but the verdict it was.

He sang in that gallery for eleven more years. He learned every verse, perfected every interval, studied breath and resonance until masters wept at his technique. And every season, when the hearth rose and chose, it passed him over. Eleven times. He kept count the way debtors keep count. On the twelfth refusal he walked out of the shrine in the middle of the rite, down the mountain, through the snow, and did not stop until he reached Corvane, where he knelt in the Temple of the Veiled Word and prayed to a god who had never once refused him because it had never once answered.

That was the secret architecture of his life, and only he knew the blueprint: everything after — the ordination, the reforms, the Quiet Edicts, the unmaking of the wards, the theft of the stars — all of it grew from a single seed planted on a stone step by an indifferent fire.

They say I hate the gods, he thought, watching the city glitter with his stolen light. They are wrong. Hatred requires belief in the object. What I feel is closer to bookkeeping. A debt, owed to me, by things too vast to notice they owed it.

And debts, he knew better than anyone alive, must be collected.

* * *

"You've read it, then." Sera Vane's voice came from the doorway — she did not knock anymore, which was either insubordination or trust, and Ovelith had decided long ago to file it under trust, because Vane was useful and useful people should be permitted their small freedoms.

"I've read it." He set the report on the desk between them, face up, letting her see the route map sketched in the margin. "The drowned orchards. Then the pass at Grey Cairn, if the weather holds, which it won't." He allowed himself the ghost of a smile. "Brannoc is leading them. That drunkard knows exactly one road up that mountain, and it goes through the old pilgrim stations."

"Then we can intercept—"

"No."

Vane stopped. She was very good at stopping; he had trained her himself, back when she was a raw recruit with a dead father and a hunger for certainty, and watching her discipline assert itself over her surprise was like watching a well-made lock turn.

"No?" she said.

"No interception. Not yet." He crossed to the great table beneath the window, where a relief map of the eastern ranges lay under a skin of candlelight, and set his finger on the mountain's shoulder, below the summit, where the shrine crouched in its cirque of stone. "Think, Inquisitor. If we take them on the road, we take five bodies and learn nothing. The scribe dies with whatever fragments they've decoded. The choirboy dies with his voice full of songs we haven't catalogued. And the story spreads anyway — martyrs travel faster than fugitives." He moved his finger up the slope. "But if they reach the Hearth..."

"Then we arrest them in the act of—" Vane paused. He watched her choose her words with the care of someone walking on river ice. "In the act of what, Your Radiance? Forgive me. The warrants describe sedition and forbidden craft. But the shrine is... the shrine is nothing, now. Cold for centuries. What crime can be committed at a dead altar?"

Such a careful question. Such a careful, intelligent question. He had chosen her for that intelligence and there were nights it kept him awake.

"The crime of trespass," he said smoothly, "against the last holy place the old order left standing. The crime of attempting to restore powers the Concordat outlawed for excellent reasons." He let a little warmth into his voice, the warmth he knew she still mistook for paternal regard. "You have read the histories, Vane. The age before the covenant was *managed* — before the temple stood between mortal need and divine appetite — was an age of burning skies and voices stolen wholesale. Children fed to fires so that storms would stay offshore. We ended that. Whatever those five intend on that mountain, they intend to undo six hundred years of protection. That is not rebellion. That is a siege against the safety of every soul in Corvane."

Something flickered behind her eyes — he saw it, catalogued it, filed it away with the other small doubts he had been pretending not to notice for a month now. Doubt was a splinter. Left alone it festered; dug at carelessly, it drove deeper. The trick was to leave it undisturbed and keep the wound clean around it.

"You believe that," Vane said. It was not quite a question. "What you just said. You believe it."

"I have never believed anything more," Ovelith said, and this was true, and truth was the most economical lie available to a man in his position.

She bowed and withdrew. He listened to her boots fade down the stair and thought: soon. Soon I will have to decide whether she is a shield or a liability, and I find I am reluctant, and reluctance is a luxury I cannot afford.

* * *

Alone again, he did what he always did with reports that unsettled him: he went down.

The vaults beneath the tower were his alone to enter — not even the Ashen Ring knew the third door, the one behind the reliquary, the one that required a key of bone-white glass grown, not cut. Beyond it the stairs descended farther than any architect's plan admitted, into a spherical chamber the size of a chapel, and there the light waited.

It was not stored in vials here. Vials were for selling, for funding, for the small corruptions of smugglers' markets that kept his supply lines greased. Here it was free — a slow-turning cloud of radiance, dense as honey, silent as snowfall. Starlight. Six hundred years of covenant-light, drawn out of the sky through the wounds he had opened in the border wards, hoarded in the dark like a dragon's bed.

He stepped into it and it parted around him and closed again, and he stood in the center of the stolen heavens and breathed.

This was the part no report could touch. This was his.

He had expected the light to fight him, in the early years. He had braced himself for grief, for rage, for the screaming of murdered suns. What he had found instead was worse and better: the light was *asleep*. Muffled, dreaming, dimmed to embers — the gods within it reduced to something like the heat left in stones after sunset. His theft had not killed them. Nothing so clean. He had simply, carefully, over decades, stopped the feeding, and a god unfed does not die; it forgets. It dreams. It becomes weather.

Which meant, said a voice in him that sounded unpleasantly like the master of the hearth, sixty years dead, that they could wake.

If a true voice sang to them. If a willing hand wrote the old words. If someone reached the First Hearth and relit the banked fire —

"It would not be restoration," he told the sleeping light, and his voice echoed strangely in the chamber, doubled by the radiance, so that he seemed to argue with himself across a distance. "It would be a return to the famine years. To feeding. Do you know what the Opening Verse asks, old ones? It does not ask. It *takes*. It took children's voices and gave back silver scars and called it grace. I was there. I stood on that step eleven times and was found insufficient, and I thank the fire nightly for its honesty, because it showed me what the covenant truly was: an appetite wearing the mask of holiness."

The light turned around him, slow, indifferent, dreamlike. Somewhere inside it, perhaps, something stirred at the sound of a human voice — the way a sleeper stirs at a familiar word without waking.

"And I will give you better," he went on, softer now, almost tender, because this was the confession he could make only here, only to captives who could not repeat it. "I will feed you myself. All of you, equally, from one hand, through one voice. No more galleries of children hoping to be chosen. No more refusal. No more of you deciding which vessels are worthy. Every soul in Corvane will pray through me, and every drop of devotion will reach you measured and fair, and in exchange you will hold the wards and hang the stars and ask *nothing else*, ever again. That is my covenant. Not freedom — freedom is what starved you. Order. Fairness. An end to hope, which is the cruelest food there is."

He knew how it sounded. He had rehearsed the argument for sixty years, in the shower-stall of his own skull, against every judge who might someday summon him: the child refused by heaven, grown into the man who would refuse heaven. They would call it vengeance. Let them. Vengeance was merely justice that arrived late and angry.

But underneath the argument, in the place he visited rarely and never in daylight, another truth sat like a stone:

He wanted to stand on that step one more time. He wanted the fire to rise and turn its attention on him and *find him sufficient* — and since it never would, never had, never could, he had arranged the world instead so that no one would ever be chosen again. If the gods would not drink from him, he would teach them to drink only from him. It was not love. It was not hate. It was the logic of the ledger, carried to its final page.

He left the vault before the light could make him sentimental. Sentiment was how the five on the pilgrim road justified themselves, and look where it had gotten them: hunted, freezing, following a drunkard up a mountain toward a fire that had been dead since before their grandparents drew breath.

* * *

The orders went out by midnight.

To the Ashen Ring: seal the valley roads. Let no supply, no message, no mercy climb toward Grey Cairn. The pilgrims would arrive hungry and find the mountain already starving them.

To the storm-callers — twelve men and women whose craft he had licensed precisely because it was abominable: muster at the foothill station, await the second seal.

And to three riders he trusted the way one trusts knives: carry this to the shrine itself, ahead of the fugitives, and do what is written.

Because that was the move Vane would not anticipate, that the scribe might not decode in time, that the drunkard Brannoc — who had built the wards and should have understood, should have *seen it coming* — would realize too late:

One did not need to defend the First Hearth. One needed only to arrive first.

A hearth was a threshold, and thresholds could be dressed. He had spent thirty years studying the old rites in confiscated books the temple burned publicly and preserved privately, and there was a verse — a corruption of the Opening Verse, inverted, a hook instead of a cup — that could be sung into cold stone and wait there, patient as frost, for the moment the hearth relit. When Quentin's voice woke the fire, the fire would wake already leashed. When Freya's pen wrote the covenant, the covenant would write itself into Ovelith's hand. They would perform the restoration perfectly, at cost of everything, and hand him the keys.

Let them come, he thought, standing again at his window, looking out over a city that slept peacefully beneath a roof of stolen stars. Let them climb and bleed and sacrifice. Devotion was the finest raw material in the world. He had built an empire on lesser quantities of it.

In the morning he would begin the ascent himself. A man ought to be present when his life's ledger came due.

He blew out the candles. The room filled with his own hoarded light, gentle and gold and utterly silent, and High Cantor Ovelith stood in it a long moment, listening — the way he had listened on a stone step sixty years ago — for a god's voice that never came.

Then he packed for the mountain.
