## 22. Halfway Is Not Safety

The pass narrowed as the sun went down, and by the time they found a place to camp it had stopped being a road at all—just a shelf of grey stone stitched to the mountainside, wide enough for the cart if Maren drove it within a finger's width of the drop and no wider. She did it without comment, which was somehow worse than if she'd sworn about it.

They made camp in the lee of an old waymarker, a pillar taller than Brannoc with covenant-script worn down to ghosts of letters. Sera built the fire small and shielded it with stones, inquisitor habits dying hard, and Maren produced sausages from somewhere in her endless cart that no one asked about because the answer would only raise more questions.

Freya sat with their back against the waymarker and their ledger on their knees—the new one, the one they'd started after burning the old—and did what they always did when the world went quiet enough to hear itself: they counted.

Not coin. There was no coin left worth counting. They counted other things. Three days since the ambush at Ashford Crossing. One wound on Brannoc's side, knitting slow and ugly. One inquisitor turned fugitive, sitting across the fire with her sword across her knees like she still didn't trust her own hands to hold anything else. One choirboy, asleep against the cart wheel with his mouth slightly open, looking about nine years old instead of seventeen.

One mountain left.

*Halfway,* Freya thought, and wrote the word in the ledger without meaning to. *Halfway is not safety.* It was a thing scribes said to each other like a superstition, like *don't whistle in the archive*. Halfway was where debts came due. Halfway was where the road remembered you'd been walking it.

"You're doing the face," Maren said, settling down beside them with the particular groan of a woman whose knees had opinions about altitude. "The one where you look like you're doing arithmetic on the world."

"I am doing arithmetic on the world."

"How does it come out?"

"Ask me tomorrow." Freya closed the book. "Or don't. The answer changes every time I count."

Maren snorted and handed them a sausage on a stick, which was her way of ending conversations she didn't like, and for a while there was nothing but the fire ticking and the wind coming down off the snowline with a sound like someone breathing through cloth.

It was Quentin who broke the quiet, because of course it was. He'd woken without anyone noticing—he did that, drifted up out of sleep like something surfacing—and he scooted around the fire to sit on Freya's other side, close enough that their shoulders almost touched. Almost. He was careful about that lately, in a way he hadn't been in the first weeks, and Freya had decided not to decide what it meant.

"Can I ask you something?" he said.

"You can ask. Whether I answer depends on the question and my mood."

"What was your mood this morning?"

"Sullen."

"Right." He turned the stick in his fingers. He'd taken a sausage too but mostly wasn't eating it. "Why didn't you ever run?"

Freya looked at him. "Run where?"

"Anywhere. Before all this." He gestured vaguely—at the fire, the mountain, the whole shape of their lives since the night the stars went out. "You told me you knew your charms were real magic for fifteen years. You knew what the temple would do if they caught you. You could have walked out of Corvane any night you liked. You're clever, you're careful, you know how to be nobody." His voice did something complicated on that last part. "So why stay?"

It was a fair question. It deserved better than the flippant answers Freya kept stacked behind their teeth like coins in a false bottom. They ate the sausage first, buying time, and watched the fire, and when they finally spoke they were surprised by how steady their own voice came out.

"Invisible people can't be missed."

Quentin frowned. "That's not—"

"It is, though. Think about it. If I run, I become someone who ran. Someone who was somewhere, who is now somewhere else. Someone the temple looks for." Freya turned the stick over. "I spent fifteen years building a person so boring the gods themselves would skim past her. A debt-scribe with mediocre charm-work and bad posture. You know how many times an inquisitor looked straight at me and saw nothing? That wasn't luck. That was craft. And running ruins craft. Running is the loudest thing a person can do."

"So you stayed invisible to stay safe."

"I stayed invisible to stay *nothing*." The word came out with more edge than Freya intended, and they heard Quentin go still beside them. The fire popped. Somewhere above, ice cracked on the rockface, a sound like a knuckle. "There's a difference. Safe is a place. Nothing is a strategy. You make yourself so small and so dull that even the ledger forgets you. My debt to the temple was impossible—I've known that since I was twelve years old. No number of charms pays off what they say I owe. So the plan was never to pay it. The plan was to be so uninteresting that no one ever came to collect."

"And then the stars went out," Quentin said quietly.

"And then the stars went out, and a boy fell into my stall bleeding hymns, and suddenly I was the most interesting person in Corvane." Freya laughed, short and dry. "Fifteen years of craft, undone by one night of yours."

"Sorry."

"Don't apologize. It's the best thing that ever happened to me, which tells you something depressing about the fourteen years before it."

He was quiet for a moment. When he spoke again his voice had dropped into the register he used for singing, low and careful, though he wasn't singing. "But that's not really an answer either. You could have turned me away. You could have let me keep running alone. Instead you burned your ledger. You—" He stopped, and Freya watched him choose words the way he chose notes, testing each one before committing to it. "You gave up being nothing. On purpose. Why?"

Because you sang to a dying bird and it lived. Because you looked at my stall of trinkets and saw the truth under it and weren't afraid of me. Because when you sleep you look like someone who has never once calculated the cost of being seen.

"Because you'd miss me," Freya said.

It came out before they could stop it—smaller than they meant, plainer than they meant, aimed at the fire rather than at him. The silence afterward lasted three heartbeats, which Freya counted, because counting was what they did instead of feeling things at full volume.

Then Quentin said, "Yes."

Just that. No elaboration, no joke, no deflection. Just *yes*, in that voice, and Freya discovered that fifteen years of invisibility had not prepared them for being seen on purpose, in the dark, by someone who meant it.

"That's a terrible reason to burn a ledger," Freya managed.

"It's the best one I've heard." He shifted, and his shoulder came to rest against theirs—not almost, this time, but actually, warm through two layers of coat. "For what it's worth, I did miss you. Before. In the market, those first days, when I'd go back to the stall and you weren't there yet. Ten minutes and I'd already be watching the street for you." A pause. "I didn't know what to call that. I still don't."

"We could call it nothing," Freya said. "I have experience with nothing. It's very restful until it isn't."

He laughed—a real laugh, brief and startled out of him—and above them the clouds tore open for a moment, just long enough to show a smear of sky where stars should have been. Nothing there. The same nothing there had been for weeks now. But Quentin tilted his head back and looked anyway, the way he always did, as if absence were a kind of audience.

"They'll come back," he said. Not a hope. A fact, stated flatly, the way he stated facts about music. "When we do what we came to do. They'll come back."

"I know."

"How do you know?"

Freya looked at him—at the firelight moving on his face, at the bruised tiredness under his eyes, at the sheer unreasonable stubbornness of him—and found the honest answer rising without any craft at all.

"Because I'm starting to think everything real works the way you do. Quietly. Waiting for someone to sing to it."

Quentin went pink to the ears and studied his sausage very intently, and Freya pretended not to notice, and neither of them moved their shoulders apart.

* * *

Sera took first watch, because Sera always took first watch; it was the last piece of her old life she seemed willing to set down, and no one argued. Brannoc slept like a man hiding from dreams, which meant badly, waking every hour with a grunt and a muttered fragment of ward-notation. Maren slept like a woman who had driven a vegetable cart up half a mountain and earned it, flat on her back, snoring with dignity.

Freya couldn't sleep. This was normal. What was new was why: not fear, not arithmetic, but the ghost-warmth of a shoulder against theirs, replaying itself like a phrase of melody they couldn't stop humming.

Around midnight—or what passed for midnight under a starless sky; Freya measured it by the fire's mood—they gave up, wrapped their coat tighter, and walked the twenty paces to where Sera stood watch at the edge of the shelf, facing downslope toward the world they'd left.

"Anything?" Freya asked.

"Lanterns. Far below. Too regular to be travelers." Sera didn't turn. "A column, maybe. Moving slow. They'll reach the foothills in two days."

"The Cantor."

"He moves like a man who owns the road." Her jaw tightened. "He does own the road."

Freya stood beside her and looked down at the tiny procession of light crawling through the dark, and felt the old familiar cold settle into their chest—the collector's cold, the ledger's cold. Two days' lead. Maybe three, if weather favored them and the storm-callers held back.

"You should sleep," Sera said.

"I was going to say the same to you."

"One of us should mean it." She glanced sideways, and in the dark her face lost its inquisitor's armor for a moment. "You were awake thinking about him."

It wasn't a question, and Freya didn't insult them both by denying it. "Is that a problem, Inquisitor Vane?"

"Vane died at Ashford Crossing. Call me Sera." She looked back down at the lanterns. "And no. It's not a problem. It's the opposite." A pause, and then, gruffly, like she was paying a debt she resented owing: "People fight harder for something than against something. I spent ten years learning the first half of that sentence. Your choirboy taught me the second in about a week."

"He's not mine."

"No," Sera said, with the mild, infuriating certainty of a woman reading a warrant. "Of course not."

She said nothing else, and Freya stalked back to the fire with what little dignity remained, lay down, and did not sleep, and did not entirely mind.

* * *

Near dawn, the mountain spoke.

Not a voice—nothing so kind. A tremor, deep and rolling, the kind of shudder that comes from far underground or from vast weights shifting, and everyone was awake instantly, hands finding weapons, Quentin's breath catching in a note ready to defend them. But nothing came. The rocks held. The fire guttered and steadied.

Only Brannoc didn't reach for a weapon. He stood at the mouth of the antechamber they'd sheltered beside—the waymarker's hollow base, where ancient covenant-script curled into the stone—and stared inward with an expression Freya had never seen on him. Not drunk-guilt, not shame. Wonder, and underneath it, dread.

"There's a door," he said hoarsely. "Under the script. It wasn't open yesterday."

Maren lit a lamp. The light fell into a passage sloping down into the mountain's bones, walls crowded with writing older than the temple, older than Corvane, older perhaps than the idea of writing. Cold air breathed up out of it, smelling of stone and time and something faintly golden, like the memory of sunlight.

Brannoc swallowed. "This is a record-vault. Ward-smiths kept the covenant terms here. Every term." He looked back at the others, and his face had gone grey. "Including the price."

Freya felt Quentin go rigid beside them, and understood, a heartbeat later, why.

Somewhere below them, in the dark, the mountain waited with its arithmetic. And Freya—who had spent their whole life counting—suddenly, terribly, wanted to stop counting altogether.

They went down together.
