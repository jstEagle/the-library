## 23. The Price of a True Voice

Brannoc had spent three days in the antechamber of the First Hearth, and the room had not forgiven him yet.

He could feel it in the way the old ward-marks watched him from the walls—carved sigils worn smooth by centuries of candle smoke, each one a small accusation. He had cut marks like these once. Deeper ones. The border wards had been his masterwork, the pride of a young smith who believed that stone and song could hold back the dark forever. And then he had walked away, and drunk himself hollow under Hollow Bridge, and told himself for twenty years that what broke was never his to mend.

The antechamber disagreed.

It was a long, low room behind the hearth shrine itself, half-carved from the mountain and half-built, its ceiling lost in shadow above ranks of shelving that had once held scrolls, tablets, wax cylinders, everything a covenant-keeper might need to remember why the work mattered. Most of it had rotted or crumbled. But the mountain was cold and dry at this altitude, and cold and dry were kinder than men. Enough remained.

Freya had found the first intact codex within an hour of their arrival and had barely surfaced since, curled by lamplight with Quentin dozing against their shoulder, transcribing fragment after fragment into a fresh ledger with the fierce, patient joy of a scribe finally given real work. Sera stood watch at the door because standing watch was the only prayer she trusted anymore. Maren slept off the climb with her boots on, snoring like a woman daring the mountain to object.

And Brannoc read.

He read the way he used to read ward-schematics: fast, greedy, hunting for load-bearing lines. He did not want poetry. He wanted the engineering. Somewhere in these crumbling pages there had to be instructions—a sequence, a procedure, something a smith's hands could build and a singer's voice could light. He had promised them a reforge. He had promised himself one, too, though he'd have bitten his tongue bloody before saying it aloud.

On the third evening, he found the price.

* * *

It was a tablet, not a scroll—clay fired hard, the script pressed deep by a hand that had known exactly how important its words were. Brannoc almost set it aside. It looked administrative. Inventory lists, ration tallies, the dull bones of an ancient bureaucracy.

But the header stopped him. It was written in the old dual-script, mortal glyphs braided with godscript the way rope is braided from two strands, and he recognized the pattern because he had *built* that pattern. It was a covenant-formula. A founding document.

He carried it to the lamp and began to translate, lips moving, one finger tracing the grooves.

*Let the covenant be sung anew only thus:* the opening ran. Standard. Every reforging rite began with conditions; you didn't hand gods and mortals a contract without terms. His heart lifted. Terms meant structure. Structure meant he could plan around them.

*First, that a voice be given whole.*

He frowned. Given whole—yes, obviously, the singer sang the covenant-song, that was the entire architecture, voice as the pillar, voice as the bridge between realms. Quentin would sing. Quentin had been born to sing; the boy's voice had cracked chapel glass and called a flicker out of a dead sky. Fine. Next condition.

*Not lent. Not borrowed. Not offered in part.*

Brannoc's finger stopped on the groove.

*The voice that sings the covenant passes wholly into the singing. What is given to the gods is given forever. The singer will speak no word, hum no note, whisper no name, from the last syllable of the song until the end of their days.*

He sat very still.

The lamp flame leaned in some draft he didn't feel. Behind him, Freya's pen scratched steadily on, page after page, and Quentin breathed slow and even against their shoulder, and Maren snored, and the world went on being ordinary while Brannoc stared at a line of clay and felt the floor tilt beneath forty years of certainty.

*Forever.* He turned the tablet over, as if the rest of the sentence might be hiding on the bottom. It wasn't. Ancient covenants didn't hedge. That was the whole point of them—that they cost what they cost, openly, so that no one could later claim they hadn't known.

Quentin. Nineteen years old, maybe twenty. A boy whose voice had healed a dying bird, who hummed when he was nervous, who talked with his hands when the words came too slow, who had sung at vespers every day of his life since he was six years old because it was the only thing that had ever made him feel real. Take the voice and you took—

Brannoc set the tablet down with exaggerated care, the way you set down something that might go off.

*All right,* he told himself. *One price. Terrible, but one. Maybe there's another way to balance the formula. Maybe the old smiths left tolerances.* Smiths always left tolerances. You built for the worst day and hoped for the best.

He kept reading.

*Second, that the writing be made in flesh's own hand, upon the self, of the self.*

His mouth had gone dry somewhere in the last minute. He licked his lips and kept going.

*The covenant binds two realms with one knot. A knot of voice alone slips. There must be a counterweight: a scribe willing to be unwritten. Let the scribe take up the erasure page and write therein their own true name—not a copy, not a likeness, but the name itself—and let them hold the pen until the last letter leaves the world.*

*What is unwritten cannot be mourned, for mourning requires memory, and memory requires a name.*

*This is the price of the knot. Voice for the pillar. Name for the anchor. Both freely given, or the hearth stays cold.*

Brannoc read it four times. Then a fifth, slower, because the fourth time he had been reading it while thinking about something else entirely, which was the face of the person across the room.

A scribe willing to be unwritten.

There was exactly one scribe in the party. There was exactly one scribe in the world, probably, mad enough and generous enough and *indebted enough* to even consider it. Fifteen years of hiding, fifteen years of harmless charms and invisible living, and now the oldest document in creation said the magic needed someone to erase themselves, and there she sat—or he sat, or they sat, Brannoc had never quite untangled it and had learned it didn't matter—with ink on their fingers and Quentin's head on their shoulder, copying fragments by lamplight like the answer to everything was just one more page away.

*No,* Brannoc thought. *No, no, no.* There had to be another reading. Old script was treacherous; half the time a glyph meant two things and the context decided, and context rotted faster than clay. Maybe "unwritten" meant something gentler in the original tongue. Maybe it meant exiled, or renamed, or—

He was a liar, and he knew it, because he had helped draft the modern glosses of the older ward-contracts back when the temple still employed honest smiths, and he knew exactly what *unwritten* meant in covenant-language. It meant *never was*. It meant the person had not merely died but been subtracted—no grave, no grief, no gap where they'd stood, because a gap requires edges and edges require a shape and a shape requires a name. The gods had designed it that way deliberately. Mercy, of a kind. No one left behind to ache.

Which meant no one left behind to *remember*, either.

He looked at Freya across the lamplight—at the sharp clever face, the ink-stained fingers, the way they absently tucked a blanket tighter around the sleeping choirboy without ever lifting their eyes from the page—and felt something crack open in his chest that had nothing to do with guilt over wards.

They'd know. That was the worst of it. Freya would read this tablet and understand it in a tenth of the time it had taken him, and they wouldn't flinch, because Freya Voss had spent fifteen years practicing disappearance and here was the universe offering them a promotion.

* * *

He didn't tell them that night.

He told himself it was prudence—verify the translation first, cross-reference the glossaries, don't start a panic on a maybe. He told himself it was kindness. He told himself a lot of things while he sat up alone in the antechamber with the tablet in his lap and the lamp burning low, listening to the wind worry the mountain pass outside like a dog at a door.

Near midnight, Sera came in from watch, stamped snow off her boots, and glanced at him with those hunter's eyes that missed nothing and pretended to miss less.

"You've been staring at that same tablet for six hours," she said quietly. "Either it's fascinating or it's terrible."

"Both," Brannoc said.

She crouched beside him, close enough to read by his light, and he angled the tablet toward her without deciding to. She read faster than he did—he'd forgotten temple-trained eyes—and halfway through the second condition her face went through a journey he recognized from mirrors: comprehension, denial, bargaining, and then a flat, cold stillness that was worse than any of them.

"That's Freya," she said. Not a question.

"That's Freya."

"And the first condition—" She stopped. Started again. "The boy gives up his voice. Permanently. He'd live fifty more years and never say another word."

"Aye."

Sera was silent for a long moment. Outside, the wind rose and fell. In the corner, Maren muttered something in her sleep about turnips.

"When I took my inquisitor's oath," Sera said at last, "they made us swear a clause most of us never thought about. *That I will not ask any soul to bear a burden I would refuse myself.* I always thought it was decorative. Oaths are mostly decorative." Her jaw tightened. "I'd refuse both of these. Voice or name. I'd refuse them both, and I'm the one trained to die for the temple. So how dare we ask it of a choirboy and a scribe?"

"We're not asking," Brannoc said. "That's the trap of it. The covenant won't accept coercion—the other tablets are clear on that. It has to be free. Which means all we can do is tell them the truth and then stand there while they choose." He laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "Twenty years I drank under a bridge because I couldn't face one mistake. Now I get to walk over to those two and hand them this."

Sera reached out and closed her hand over the tablet, gently, like covering a wound.

"Then don't hand it to them tonight," she said. "Sleep on it. Check your translation. If it still says what it says tomorrow—" She shrugged, helpless, the first helpless gesture he'd ever seen from her. "Then tomorrow we find out what kind of people we are."

* * *

He checked the translation anyway. Of course he did. He spent until dawn with the glossary fragments, chasing every alternate reading, and the covenant-language was merciless: *unwritten* meant unwritten, *whole* meant whole, *forever* meant forever. The ancients had built their mercy into the aftermath instead of the act, and there was no tolerance, no tolerance anywhere, because the First Hearth had never been engineering. It had been a promise, and promises don't have safety margins.

Toward morning he found one more fragment, wedged in a scroll-case behind the shelf, and it was worse than the tablet. It was a marginal note in a later hand—some covenant-keeper, centuries dead, annotating the founding terms:

*Three reforgings attempted in the age of our grandmothers' grandmothers. Two failed for want of a willing scribe; none could be found, and rightly. One succeeded. The singer was a woman of the northern shrines; she lived forty years after, deaf to music, mute to speech, and the people who loved her learned her language of hands. The scribe was her brother. We do not speak his name, because there is no name to speak. We light a candle at each new moon regardless. Habit, perhaps. Or perhaps the mountain remembers what we may not.*

Brannoc set the scrap down and put his face in his hands.

*Learned her language of hands.* Forty years. Quentin humming at a market stall and catching himself mid-note. Quentin trying to say *thank you* and producing silence. Quentin, who had already lost his place in the choir, his city, his faith's roof over his head—losing the one thing that had never once betrayed him.

And the brother. The brother whose name nobody could say, whom everybody lit candles for without knowing why. Some animal part of Brannoc's mind was already doing the arithmetic it refused to finish: Maren's sharp tongue going quiet about a person she couldn't recall loving. Sera's ledgers full of gaps. Quentin's own melody carrying a shapeless grief with no face attached.

*We do not speak his name, because there is no name to speak.*

* * *

He heard them wake, in the next chamber—Maren grumbling the stove awake, Quentin's sleepy laugh, Freya's low voice answering something he couldn't catch. Ordinary sounds. Breakfast sounds. The sounds of people who did not yet know that the road they'd climbed together ended at a door with two prices carved above it, and that both prices had names.

Brannoc stood, knees cracking, and weighed the tablet in his hands one last time. Clay was heavy. Truths were heavier. Twenty years under a bridge had taught him exactly one useful skill: recognizing the moment when the drinking was over and the walking had to begin.

He squared his shoulders, picked up the lamp, and went to call the family together.

Freya looked up as he entered, pen still moving, and smiled at him—easy, tired, trusting—and asked, "Find anything?"
