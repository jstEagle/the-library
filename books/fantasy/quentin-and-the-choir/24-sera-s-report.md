## 24. Sera's Report

The relay station at Coldmere was a stone box with a brazier, a desk, and a pigeon coop that smelled like the end of the world. Sera Vane had stopped at a hundred of them in her career. She knew their grammar by heart: the scratch of the quill, the wax softened over flame, the rider or bird or—on the mountain roads, where nothing else survived—the signal mirror flashing her words down the range in stuttering silver.

She sat at the desk with a blank report form in front of her and did not write.

Outside, the wind came off the pass with teeth in it. Somewhere above the tree line, four fugitives and a landlady were climbing toward the First Hearth with a wounded ward-smith and no idea that she had decided, three days ago on a frozen ridge, to stop hunting them.

Or rather: she had decided to hunt them better than anyone else could. That was the lie she was still telling herself, and she held it up to the light now, turning it, the way she'd once turned suspected charms in evidence bags. *I am not defecting. I am pursuing a deeper investigation.* The lie fit poorly. Lies about the temple always did; they were cut for people who wanted to believe them.

Sera Vane had never wanted to believe anything. Believing was what happened to you when you were seven years old and a woman in grey measured your skull with knotted cord and pronounced you *clear-sighted*.

* * *

They had taken her from the tannery rows in Vessick, where the air ate the skin off children's hands, and she had gone willingly because the woman in grey promised her bread with butter on it every day, and a bed that was hers alone, and books. Her mother had stood in the doorway of their one room and not said goodbye. Sera remembered that with more clarity than the induction itself: her mother's hands, dyed black to the wrist, hanging at her sides, and the way she looked at Sera—not with grief but with arithmetic, counting what a seventh child cost against what the temple's coin would buy.

The induction hall smelled of cold incense. Forty children, maybe fifty, all of them thin, all of them watching. The Preceptor of Inquiry had walked the line slowly, and when he reached Sera he had crouched to her height, which no adult ever did, and asked:

"What do you see when you look at me?"

"A man," she had said.

"And what do you *know*?"

She hadn't understood the question. She understood it later, after years of training, when knowing and seeing had been fused into a single instrument and she had learned that the difference between them was the whole of an inquisitor's soul. Seeing was eyes. Knowing was the thing the temple built in you instead of a heart: the certainty that behind every face was a debt, and behind every debt a truth, and behind every truth either the Veiled Word or nothing at all.

They had given her the grey cloak at fourteen. They had given her her first warrant at sixteen—a counterfeiter in the river districts, a sad man with ink-stained fingers—and they had given her, folded inside the warrant, a second paper she was not supposed to read, ordering that the counterfeiter be drowned before trial because his testimony might implicate a deacon. She had read it anyway. Reading things she wasn't supposed to read was the first crack in any vessel, and the Preceptors knew it, and they let it happen, because an inquisitor who never doubted was only half a blade.

They wanted blades that knew they were blades.

She had drowned him herself. She told herself it was mercy—he'd have suffered worse under the questioners—and for twenty years that sentence had held the weight of everything she'd done. It was a load-bearing lie, like the one she carried now. Inquisitors were built out of lies the way bridges were built out of stone: carefully, and with full knowledge of what would happen if any single one failed.

* * *

The report form waited. She dipped the quill.

*To the Office of Inquiry, Corvane, by hand of the Coldmere relay. From Inquisitor First Rank Sera Vane.*

Her hand was steady. Twenty years of writing reports had made her hand steady even when the rest of her was falling.

*Pursuit continues along the pilgrim road. Subjects observed at Ashford Crossing two days past, moving westward in company of an unidentified older woman. Ward-smith Brannoc sustained a crossbow wound during engagement; subject is slowed and unlikely to survive the high passes.*

That much was true. Every good lie needed a spine of truth; the Preceptor had taught her that too, in the same voice he used for scripture. *A lie without truth in it is noise, child. A lie with truth in it is architecture.* She had built her whole career on that principle, and now she built her treason with it, and the symmetry was so bitter she almost smiled.

*Subject Quentin, former chorister, remains the priority. Voice confirmed active as of Ashford Crossing. Recommend redirecting pursuit assets to the southern passes, where terrain favors interception and the subjects' route through the high country appears to be a feint.*

There it was. The whole architecture in one sentence. The southern passes were three days' hard ride away, through country the storm-callers had already churned to slush. Any unit sent there would find nothing but weather. By the time Corvane realized the feint was the report and not the fugitives, the mountain shrine would be days behind her, and whatever happened at the First Hearth would happen without a squad of inquisitors standing on its doorstep.

She was buying them time. Time was the only currency worth anything on this mountain, and she was spending her life to mint it.

Because that was what the false report cost, and she knew the accounting to the copper. A first-rank inquisitor filing a deliberately misdirected report was not making an error; errors were survivable. This was fraud against the Office itself, discovered the moment anyone walked the northern road and found no pursuit, no camp, no sign of her. And discovery would not bring a trial. The Cantor's new orders—she had read them, three nights ago, in the field satchel of a dead lieutenant whose face she still saw when she closed her eyes—were explicit in a way old orders had never been.

*No survivors. Not capture. No survivors.*

She had thought, reading it, of the counterfeiter in the river, of the water closing over his head, of how young she'd been and how thoroughly she'd believed the drowning was necessary. The Cantor had simply done to the whole world what the temple had always done to individuals: decided that some truths were too dangerous to leave alive.

And then she had thought of Freya Vane—or Voss, the name kept sliding, the scribe who had stood in front of a squad of crossbows at Ashford Crossing with their arms spread wide and said, in a voice that cracked straight down the middle, "You'll have to go through us." Not brave. Terrified. Brave and terrified at the same time, which was the only kind of courage Sera had ever trusted, because it was the kind that couldn't be trained into you.

She had missed her shot. Everyone had seen it. Deliberate misses were legible; the squad had read hers perfectly, and she'd watched the understanding move through them like wind through wheat. By morning two of her own soldiers would be composing reports about her. Unless—

Unless her report reached Corvane first, and was good enough, and bought enough days that by the time the truth arrived, the truth wouldn't matter anymore.

She finished the page. Signed it. Sealed it with her ring, pressing the sigil of the Veiled Word into soft wax for what she understood, with perfect clarity, was the last time.

* * *

The relay keeper was a deaf old man named Ostrec who had served the mountain stations for forty years and communicated in grunts and gestures. He took the sealed packet, weighed it in his palm the way he weighed everything, and squinted at her.

"Northern dispatch," she said loudly, pointing down the valley toward Corvane. "Urgent."

He grunted. He limped to the coop, chose a bird, and then paused—one of those pauses that deaf men develop instead of suspicion, a stillness that made you wonder exactly how much they heard with their eyes. Sera stood very still and let him look at her: the grey cloak crusted with road salt, the frostbitten cheek, the warrant tube on her back.

He released the bird. It beat up into the grey sky and banked south, toward the city, carrying her career in a cylinder on its leg.

Ostrec looked back at her. Then, slowly, he raised one hand and tapped his own chest twice—an old relay-keeper's sign she'd seen once before, years ago, from a station master who had helped her track a smuggler through a blizzard. *Safe roads,* it meant. Or possibly *good luck*. The two had never been clearly distinguished on the mountain.

"Thank you," she said, and he grunted again, and went back to his birds.

She stood outside the station until the pigeon vanished into cloud. Then she took off her ring and looked at it for a long moment—the sigil worn nearly smooth from twenty years of sealing warrants, most of which had ruined someone's life. She did not throw it away. Throwing it away would be theater, and theater was for people who needed an audience. Instead she put it in the inner pocket of her coat, over her heart, where she kept the other things she couldn't yet decide about: a pressed sprig of ash-leaves from her mother's grave, and the dead lieutenant's order paper, folded small.

Sacrifice, the Preceptor had taught them, was not dying. Dying was easy; the temple had martyrs by the cartload, embroidered onto banners. Sacrifice was giving up the thing that made you *you*, while you still lived, and continuing to live afterward as a person shaped differently. She had sacrificed her family at seven, her doubt at fourteen, her mercy at sixteen. Each loss had been presented to her as a promotion.

This was the first sacrifice she chose herself. It felt less like a promotion and more like amputation. She kept waiting for the relief and it didn't come; what came instead was a strange, clean fear, the fear of a woman walking off a map.

Good, she told the fear. Fear meant the stakes were real. Certainty was the Cantor's disease, and she would rather be afraid for the rest of her short life than certain for another day.

* * *

She caught the fugitives' trail above the treeline by mid-afternoon, though *trail* was generous—four sets of boots and a cart's worth of drag-marks through knee-deep drift, already filling with snow. But she didn't need tracks. She knew how Brannoc walked, favoring the bandaged side; she knew Maren's stubborn straight line across switchbacks everyone else cut; she knew Quentin drifted toward Freya the way iron filings drift toward a lodestone, and that wherever the two of them stood close together, the others orbited.

She found their camp at dusk in the lee of a shattered wayshrine, firelight low and gold against the blue snow. They heard her coming—of course they did; she made no effort at silence, because silence would have been worse—and by the time she stepped into the light, Maren was on her feet with a fire poker and Brannoc had a ward-stone cupped in his palm like a thrown egg, and Quentin had positioned himself in front of Freya with his chin up, ready to sing.

"It's me," Sera said, and pulled back her hood, and let them see her face: frost-nipped, exhausted, stripped of the grey cloak, which she dropped in the snow between them like a shed skin.

"We know it's you," Maren said. "Question's what you're *for*."

"I sent a false report to Corvane this morning. Misdirected pursuit to the southern passes. It buys five days, maybe six, before anyone walks the northern road and finds I lied." She said it flatly, a report to the only officers she had left. "After that, they'll know. And they won't send soldiers next time. They'll send the storm-callers, and they'll send enough of them to bury this mountain."

Silence, except the wind. Freya looked at her for a long moment—looked *through* her, the way scribes read documents, checking for forgery—and whatever they found there must have satisfied them, because they said quietly, "She's telling the truth. I've spent fifteen years learning what lies look like written on faces. Hers is true."

"Hers is *cold*," Maren muttered, but she lowered the poker.

Quentin didn't lower anything. He was staring at her with an expression she recognized from the chapel glass, from the night his voice had cracked it: the look of someone hearing a note no one else can hear. "You gave something up," he said. "Coming here. Something big."

"My name," Sera said. "My rank. My—" She stopped, because the word *faith* was in her mouth and she found she couldn't say it, not because it hurt but because it wasn't accurate anymore. Faith implied a god on the other end of it. What she'd given up was the architecture of lies she'd lived inside for twenty years, and the honest word for that was simpler and worse.

"Everything," she finished. "I gave up everything. So if any of you are wondering whether I can be trusted, don't wonder. Trust isn't the question. The question is whether you want someone with my training beside you when the storm-callers come. Because they're coming. And I'm the only person on this mountain who knows exactly how they'll do it."

Brannoc snorted from his place by the fire, wincing as the laugh pulled his wound. "An inquisitor asking to join the outlaws. Fifteen years ago I'd have called it a bad joke."

"Fifteen years ago I'd have arrested you," Sera said, and something that was almost a smile passed between them, brief and wary, the first shared thing.

Maren looked her up and down one final time, with the appraising eye of a woman who had fed strangers for thirty years and regretted it perhaps twice. "You'll take the watch till midnight," she said. "And you'll eat something. You're thin as a confession."

It was as close to welcome as the language allowed. Sera sat down at the edge of the firelight, accepted a bowl of barley soup from Quentin's hands, and felt the strangeness of it settle over her: warmth, on a mountain, among enemies, having just burned her entire life to buy these people six days.

Above the wayshrine, through a tear in the clouds, a single star was visible—impossible, since the stars had gone out weeks ago, and yet there it was, faint and flickering, like a candle relit in a far window. Quentin saw it too. He went very still, bowl halfway to his mouth, and Sera watched hope move across his face like dawn moving across a valley.

"The hearth's waking," he whispered. "It knows we're coming."

And below them, far to the south, Sera saw something else: a line of lights ascending the distant foothills, orderly as a rosary, each one burning cold white.

Storm-callers. Not five days out. Two.

Her report had bought less time than she'd promised—or the Cantor had never needed her reports at all.
