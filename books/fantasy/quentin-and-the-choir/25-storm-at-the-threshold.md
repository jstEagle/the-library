## 25. Storm at the Threshold

* * *

**Sera**

She saw the storm before anyone else did, because she had been trained to see storms—had spent eleven years learning to read weather the way other people read faces, and this was not weather.

It came up the western valley in a wall of black that moved against the wind. Clouds should have drifted; these climbed. They hauled themselves over the ridgeline like something with hands, and beneath them walked figures in temple grey, a dozen of them spaced across the snowfield in a formation Sera knew by heart: the Vesper Line, the standard assault wedge for flushing heretics out of fortified ground.

"Storm-callers," she said, and her voice came out flat and professional, which was its own kind of betrayal. "Twelve. Maybe more behind the front."

Freya looked up from the fire they'd been coaxing against the shrine's broken wall. "How long?"

Sera did the arithmetic without letting herself feel it. "Two hours. Less if they're pushing."

"They'll be pushing." Freya stood, brushing ash from their knees, and for a moment Sera saw them clearly—not the scribe who'd hidden in a night market for fifteen years, but someone doing sums of their own, and not liking the total. "Brannoc. How are the outer stones?"

Brannoc was already at the ward-line, palms flat against the first of the old hearth-stones he'd spent three days rebuilding. He'd gone grey around the mouth. "Three hold. Two are... optimistic." He said it like a man describing a bridge made of paper. "If they hit all five at once, I can't say what happens. If they hit one, I can hold one."

"Then we make them hit one at a time," Sera said.

Maren snorted from where she was repacking the supply sled with the brisk violence of a woman who had run a shop through two tax audits and a flood. "And how do we do that, exactly? Politely ask twelve armed servants of a god-thief to form an orderly queue?"

"No," Sera said. "We give them a reason to funnel. The pass narrows at the Threshold—that shelf below us. If Brannoc anchors the wards to the narrowest point, they can't bring the whole line to bear. They'll have to come through in file."

"And then?"

"Then Quentin sings," Sera said, and hated herself for how easily the plan used him, how much it sounded like the Cantor's own arithmetic—a voice deployed as an instrument. She pushed past it. There would be time for guilt later, if there was a later. "The pass amplifies sound. One voice in that throat of rock will carry like twenty."

Quentin stood at the edge of the camp, looking down into the valley where the black wall crawled closer. He'd been quiet all morning, and when he turned, his face had the stillness Sera remembered from choirboys about to be graded. "I've never sung at anyone before," he said. "Only for."

"I know," Sera said. "I'm sorry."

He nodded once, and went to warm up.

That was the thing about the boy that kept catching Sera sideways: no argument, no theatrics. Just a nod, and a boy walking to the edge of a cliff to stand between his family and a storm.

She checked her bowstring, told herself the tightness in her chest was the cold, and started setting the field.

* * *

**Brannoc**

The stones fought him.

Not metaphorically—he'd rebuilt enough wards in his life to know the difference between stone that resisted and stone that *objected*. These were hearth-stones, older than the border works, older than Corvane itself, and they remembered being sung to. His hammer-work alone wasn't feeding them right. Every joint he sealed went soft again within the hour, like bread going stale, and he could feel the mountain's disapproval humming up through his boots.

"You're doing it wrong," Maren said, appearing at his elbow with a mug of something hot and terrible. She thrust it at him. "Drink. You look like something a cat dragged halfway up a mountain."

"I am doing it *exactly* right," Brannoc said, taking the mug anyway because refusing Maren was not among the available options. "It's the stones that are wrong. They want—" He stopped. Frowned. Listened, the way you listen to a wall.

In the distance, faint under the wind, Quentin had begun to sing. Not the covenant-song, nothing with weight in it—just scales, warming up, a boy's voice running through intervals the way a runner runs through stretches. And the stone under Brannoc's palm *leaned toward it*, the way a cold man leans toward a fire.

"Well," Brannoc said slowly. "That's interesting."

"What is?"

"He's been singing for an hour and the joints are holding better." He pressed harder, feeling it out: the micro-vibration of a living voice threading through the stone matrix, waking up channels his tools couldn't reach. "Old wards weren't built. They were *sung into place*. That's why the Cantor's people can't rebuild them—they've got plenty of power and not one honest throat between them." He laughed, short and disbelieving. "Fifteen years I've been hitting rocks with a hammer, and the answer was standing right there humming."

"So use him."

"That's the problem, isn't it." Brannoc drained the mug and grimaced. "Every plan we've got runs through that boy. The wards need him, the hearth needs him, the gods need him. And now the storm's coming and I'm supposed to ask him to stand in a killing ground and be our keystone." He looked down the valley. The black wall had swallowed the tree line. "When does he get to be a person instead of a pillar?"

Maren was quiet for a moment. When she answered, her voice had lost its sharpness. "When we win," she said. "So don't lose."

The first thunder rolled up the valley—not sky-thunder. Stone-thunder. The storm-callers had begun breaking the lower wards, and each one that died went out with a sound like a bell falling downstairs.

Brannoc spat on his hands and picked up his hammer. Two hours, the inquisitor had said. He gave it ninety minutes, and started working like a man who believed in miracles but didn't intend to wait around for one.

* * *

**Freya**

They had done everything a scribe could do. Sigils chalked on every approach, alarm-threads strung through the scree, escape routes marked in code only they could read. It wasn't enough and they knew it wasn't enough, and so Freya did the last thing left: they sat down with the hearth-shard wrapped in cloth against their chest, opened their ledger—the new one, the unburned one—and began writing out the battle before it happened, because a plan on paper survives panic longer than a plan in a head.

*Storm-callers work in threes,* they wrote. *One calls, two anchor. Kill the anchor, the call collapses.*

*Sera knows this. Trust Sera.*

*Maren will not stay behind the line. Plan for Maren.*

*Quentin must not stop singing, whatever happens. Whatever happens.*

Their hand paused on that last line. Ink pooled at the nib.

Because they had read the antechamber texts with Brannoc, and they knew what the covenant would cost, and they had told no one yet—not Quentin, not any of them—that the price included a name written out of existence. There hadn't been time. There was never time. And some cowardly, practical corner of Freya's soul whispered that it was easier to spend yourself if no one was watching the ledger.

The alarm-thread sang.

A high, thin note from the eastern scree—one of theirs, tuned to Freya's own pitch, a charm disguised as a wind-chime. Then a second. Then a third, and the third one snapped mid-note, which meant someone had cut it, which meant outriders ahead of the main force, moving fast, moving quiet.

"They're early!" Freya was on their feet, shouting down the wind. "East scree—skirmishers! Sera!"

"I see them." Sera was already moving, low and fluid, bow in hand, and Freya watched her go with the strange double vision that had plagued them since the Ashford Crossing: seeing the woman who had hunted them and the woman who had burned her warrant in the same body, both of them real, neither of them canceling the other out.

Below, the black cloud-wall broke apart as it reached the pass, shredding into tendrils that poured into the narrows like smoke down a chimney. Through the gaps Freya caught glimpses of grey robes, of raised staves, of light gathering at the staves' tips that was wrong in a way Freya felt in their teeth—the same stolen starlight from the smuggler bazaar, hoarded and weaponized, the gods' own grief turned into ammunition.

Fury rose in them, clean and total, hotter than fear.

"QUENTIN!" they roared. "NOW!"

And the boy at the cliff's edge drew breath, and sang.

* * *

**Maren**

Later, Maren would remember it in pieces, the way you remember anything that matters: fragments with the connective tissue burned away.

The boy's voice going down into the pass like a blade into water.

The storm-callers stumbling as the sound hit them—their formation buckling, two of them dropping staves to clutch their ears, the stolen light guttering in their hands like candles in a draft.

Sera on the ridge, picking anchors, three arrows loosed so fast they sounded like one, and two grey figures folding, and the third stagger-stumbling backward off the path.

Brannoc roaring somewhere below, hammer ringing on stone, and the ward-line flaring blue-white along the narrows, beautiful and terrible, holding, *holding*—

—and the fourth storm-caller, the one nobody had counted on, the one who'd circled wide through the scree while everyone watched the pass, rising out of the rocks ten yards from Quentin with his staff already alight.

Maren didn't think. Thinking was for afterward.

She was closest. She was always closest; that was her whole function in this ragged family, the one who was there, the one with the supplies, the one who noticed you hadn't eaten. So she was there, and there was a fire poker in her hand because she had carried a fire poker up a blessed mountain because you never knew, and she put herself between the staff and the boy the way she'd once put herself between a tax collector and a starving apprentice, except this time the man on the other end wasn't carrying paperwork.

The light took her across the chest and side.

It didn't burn, exactly. It was worse than burning—it was *unmaking*, a cold that went in past skin and muscle and started arguing with the idea of her, and she heard herself scream from very far away, and she heard Quentin scream too, close and raw, and she heard his song shatter into something that wasn't music anymore, that was grief given a melody, and the whole mountainside *answered* it.

The storm-caller flew apart. Not killed—*dismissed*, unwritten, scattered into snowflakes and silence.

Maren fell. Snow was very cold. She'd known that for sixty years but it seemed worth confirming.

Then hands were on her—Freya's hands, small and shaking and impossibly strong, pressing the wound, and Freya's face above hers, white as the snow, saying words she couldn't quite catch. And Quentin kneeling in the drifts with blood at the corner of his mouth, singing to her now, just to her, a small fierce thread of a song that said *stay, stay, stay*.

"Fool boy," Maren tried to say. "Don't waste the voice on me. Save it for—for the hearth—"

Her own blood was on her lips. That seemed administratively sloppy. She had always balanced her books.

The dark came up the mountain to meet her, and it was almost a relief, because she was so tired, and the last thing she thought—clearly, stubbornly, in the tone she used for final offers—was:

*I did not walk away from my shop to die on a hill. Somebody write that down.*

* * *

**Freya**

They got her inside the shrine's broken walls as the storm broke over the mountain proper.

Sera held the doorway, shooting anything grey that came up the switchbacks, buying seconds at a time. Brannoc abandoned the outer wards entirely and threw everything he had into sealing the shrine itself, hammer and hands and half a lifetime of craft, sweat freezing on his face. And Freya knelt in the ruins of the old sanctuary with Maren's head in their lap and blood soaking through their fingers, and did the forbidden thing, the deep-magic thing, the thing that had gotten magic outlawed in the first place.

They reached into the wound.

Not with hands—with craft, with the slow patient magic of mending that they'd spent fifteen years pretending was charmwork. They found the unmaking where it gnawed at the edges of Maren, cold and hungry, and they set their own warmth against it like a wall, like a ledger entry written in the only currency that mattered: *years*. Their strength. Their time. They paid it out steadily, deliberately, the way they'd once tallied debts to the Temple, except this debt they chose, this debt was love with numbers attached, and they would have paid it twice over without blinking.

"Hold on," they whispered. "You impossible woman. You hid me in a cupboard and asked for nothing. You crossed a country with a vegetable cart. You do *not* get to leave the books unbalanced now."

Maren's breathing slowed. Evened. Did not stop.

Outside, the storm raged against Brannoc's seal, and the stolen light hammered the walls like fists, and somewhere in the middle of it Quentin sang until his voice cracked and then sang anyway, hoarse and bleeding sound, holding the line the only way he could.

And Freya, kneeling in the dark with their life pouring out through their hands, felt the hearth-shard pulse against their chest—once, twice—like a heart remembering its rhythm.

It was answering Quentin's song.

Which meant the hearth was awake.

Which meant they had run out of road, and the final chamber waited above them, and the price written in the antechamber texts waited with it.

Freya looked down at Maren's grey face, at Quentin's ruined voice, at Brannoc's bleeding hands, at Sera silhouetted in the doorway with an empty quiver and a drawn knife, and understood, with the cold clarity of a ledger finally balanced, that every one of them had already paid more than their share.

There was only one account left open. And it had their name on it.

Above them, through the shattered roof, the clouds tore open—and for the first time in the whole long night, Freya saw the summit of the First Hearth, black against the churning sky, with a thin red light kindling in its ancient window.

Something up there was waiting for them.

Something up there had lit a candle.
