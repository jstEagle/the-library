## 26. While She Sleeps

The storm had followed them into the cave like a debt collector who knew your name.

It howled at the mouth of the hollow where they'd dragged Maren after the last of the storm-callers' lightning had found her — not the clean white fire of the sky, but the temple's kind, threaded with something that ate. Freya could see it even now, three hours later, kneeling in the wet dark beside her landlady: a grey threadwork spreading out from the burn on Maren's side, delicate as frost on a window, patient as interest accruing.

Corruption. The word Brannoc had used, and then spat into the fire as if the word itself were temple property.

"She's sleeping," Quentin said, for the fourth time.

"She's unconscious," Freya said. "There's a difference, and it's measured in whether she wakes up."

He flinched. Freya watched him fold in on himself — shoulders drawing toward his ears, hands curling around the empty place where his voice used to live. He hadn't spoken since the hearth-song's first notes had begun to take their price; he couldn't. He communicated now in gestures, in the press of a hand, in the way he looked at you when he wanted you to understand something too large for pointing. And right now he was looking at Freya with an expression that said, plainly, *that was cruel.*

"I know," Freya said quietly. "I'm sorry. I'm tired, and I'm frightened, and I take it out on the wrong people." They reached over and squeezed his wrist. "She's strong. She crossed half of Corvane to find us carrying a sack of turnips and opinions. A little lightning won't finish her."

But the grey threadwork said otherwise. It had moved since Freya last looked. Two finger-widths closer to Maren's heart.

* * *

The cave was one of Brannoc's old ward-shelters, back from the years when he'd built things instead of drinking himself numb beneath Hollow Bridge. It still held a whisper of his craft — lines of faded script grooved into the stone, mostly dead, humming faintly like a struck bell long after the sound should have ended. Sera had taken first watch outside. Brannoc sat by the fire with his ruined tools spread around him, re-grinding a chisel that would never again hold an edge, because his hands needed work the way other men needed prayer.

Freya knelt and did what they knew how to do. Small magic. Safe magic. Fifteen years of harmless charms: warmth against chill, luck against mischance, the tiny domestications of a world that had outlawed wonder. They laid a charm of rest against Maren's brow. They tucked a stitch of mending-light into the bandage. They whispered the little cantrips their mother — no. Not their mother. The woman who had sold them. The woman whose face they'd seen in the snow, weeks ago now, and still hadn't finished grieving.

The charms helped the way a candle helps against a flood.

The grey threadwork crept on.

"Freya." Brannoc's voice, low, without looking up from his chisel. "You're doing the thing where you count."

"I'm not counting."

"You're counting. Your lips move. You counted the whole climb up the mountain, you counted the storm-callers' arrows, and now you're counting that poison in her side." He set the chisel down. "What is it?"

Freya looked at Maren. Really looked. At the woman who had hidden a stranger in her cellar while inquisitors kicked through her shop overhead, and asked for nothing. Who had demanded nothing again and again, all the way across the country, until demanding nothing became its own kind of gift so relentless that Freya had stopped knowing how to stand under it. Who had thrown herself between a storm-caller's bolt and a choirboy because that was simply what Maren did — she stood in doorways, and she stood in front of people, and she charged fair prices and gave unfair kindnesses, and she had never once, in all the years Freya had rented her attic room, made Freya feel like a tenant rather than a person.

Two more finger-widths. Maybe three hours. Maybe four.

"There's a way," Freya said.

Brannoc went very still. Even the fire seemed to lean in.

"It's called deep-magic," Freya continued, before courage could fail. "Not charms. Not craft. It's the old stuff — the stuff the ban was actually written about, underneath all the laws about fortune-tellers and hedge-witches. You don't shape it. You *spend* it. Your own strength. Years of it. You pour your life into the working and the working does what a lifetime of small magic never could." They swallowed. "My grandmother taught me one working of it, once, when I was small enough to be taught things. A slowing. It doesn't cure corruption. It argues with it. Slows it down until the body can win on its own terms — or until someone smarter than me finds a cure."

"How many years?" Brannoc asked. Ward-smiths understood cost. It was the first lesson of wards: everything holds only if something pays for it.

"That depends on how much I argue."

"And if you argue too hard?"

"Then there isn't much left of me to argue with." Freya met his eyes. "Brannoc. She hid me from inquisitors. She fed us off her own stock when we had nothing. She came after us into the mountains at sixty-one years old because she decided we were hers. What exactly do you think I'm weighing here?"

"I think," Brannoc said slowly, "that she'd weigh it differently. That's all. That's the whole of what I think." He picked up his chisel again. "But I'm not going to stop you. I've spent fifteen years learning what it costs to do nothing."

Quentin touched Freya's shoulder. When Freya turned, he pointed at himself, then at Maren, then pressed both hands flat over his own heart — *me too, whatever it is, let me give too.*

"You can't," Freya said gently. "Every drop of you is already promised. You're spending yourself note by note up that mountain, and the covenant needs every note. If you pour anything else out, there'll be nothing left for the hearth." They managed a smile. "Besides. This is scribe's work. Ledger-work. Balancing accounts." They looked down at their hands, ink-stained even now, even here. "It's the one thing I've always known how to do."

* * *

Preparation took an hour, and Freya spent it the way they spent everything: writing.

They drew the circle in chalk first, then deepened it in ink mixed with ash from the fire and a single drop of their own blood — deep-magic wanted to know whose account it drew upon. Around Maren's sleeping form they wrote the slowing-verse, the old words their grandmother had murmured over a fevered child in a village Freya could barely remember, the words that had gotten the grandmother taken by inquisitors two winters later. Freya had memorized them at seven years old and carried them, unspoken, through fifteen years of selling lucky pennies to night-market drunks.

*Slow, slow, the river in its banks.*
*Spend, spend, the candle for the flame.*
*What I give, I give to keep.*
*What I keep, I lose by name.*

That last line had frightened them as a child. It frightened them less now. Names, they had learned, were negotiable.

Sera came in from the watch as Freya finished the final glyph, snow melting off her shoulders, her former mentor's warrant-burn still visible on her palm. She read the circle in one sweep — she'd been trained to read circles, though hers had been for hunting them — and her jaw tightened.

"That's third-rank forbidden," she said. "The kind they don't put in the public edicts. The kind they teach inquisitors to recognize so they can burn the caster on sight."

"Yes," Freya said.

"The sentence for this, alone, is—"

"Sera." Freya kept their voice level. "I've been sentenced my whole life. I've just finally picked the crime myself. There's a freedom in it I'd recommend."

Something moved behind Sera's eyes — the same crack that had been widening since Ashford Crossing, since the order for no survivors, since the night she'd walked into camp half-frozen and been allowed, unbelievably, to stay. She crouched at the circle's edge, studied the verse, and said, in the flat tone of a professional correcting an amateur:

"Your anchor-glyph is mirrored. Left-hand casters anchor right. You'll bleed the working sideways and pay double."

Freya stared at her. Then laughed — a short, cracked, astonished sound — and turned the glyph.

"Thank you."

"Don't thank me." Sera stood. "Just come out the other side of it. All of you. I didn't defect to bury half a family in a hole in a mountain." She paused at the cave mouth, snow swirling in around her boots, and added without turning: "For what it's worth — the woman in the circle would do the same for you. She told me so on the second day, when she thought I was going to kill everyone. Said if I had to choose, spare the scribe, the boy needs them." Her voice roughened. "So. Spare her back."

She walked out into the storm.

* * *

Freya sent the others away. Brannoc resisted for exactly as long as it took Freya to explain that deep-magic worked best witnessed by no one, because witnesses leaked hope, and hope leaked into workings and soured them. Quentin resisted longer — he planted himself at the circle's edge and pointed fiercely at his own chest, at Maren, back at himself, an entire argument conducted in eyebrows and jabbing fingers. Freya took his face in both hands and pressed their forehead to his.

"If this works," they whispered, "she wakes up tomorrow and yells at me for being dramatic. If it doesn't — you finish the song. You hear me? Whatever happens in this circle, the song comes first. Promise me."

Quentin's eyes were wet and furious and, finally, obedient. He squeezed Freya's hands once, hard, and went to sit by the fire where Brannoc could hold him there.

Freya stepped into the circle and knelt at Maren's head.

Up close, asleep, she looked older than sixty-one. The storm had scoured her; the grey threadwork showed faintly through the bandage now, like roots under ice. But her breathing was steady, and her brow was furrowed the way it furrowed when she was doing sums, and Freya thought: *even unconscious, she's tallying. Probably my rent. Probably fifteen years of back rent she never charged.* A laugh caught in their throat and came out as something else.

"All right, old woman," Freya murmured. "Let's talk terms."

They placed both palms over the wound, over the bandage, over the creeping grey, and began.

Deep-magic did not feel like casting. Charms felt like breathing — a small exhale of self, easily recovered. Deep-magic felt like being opened. Like a ledger with the spine cracked and pages torn out one by one and each page *burned* to make light. Freya spoke the slowing-verse and felt the first year go — not pain, exactly, but subtraction. A memory lost its color. A scar faded. Somewhere in them, a version of themselves that had existed at twenty-five simply ceased, paid out like coin, and the working drank it and pushed back against the grey.

The threadwork slowed.

Freya poured another year. The frost-roots halted at the edge of the bandage, quivered, and began — barely, grudgingly — to recede. The corruption fought. It was temple-made, Cantor-made, and it knew what it was doing; it wrapped itself around Maren's vitality and *held*, and Freya had to argue with it line by line, verse by verse, buying back ground with years they could not spare.

Time went strange inside the circle. The fire burned low. Snow ticked against the stone. Freya lost track of whether they had given three years or ten, and stopped caring, and that was the danger their grandmother had warned of — deep-magic was honest at the start and greedy at the end, and the caster's love was the very lever it used. *It will ask for as much as you're willing to feel,* the old woman had said. *And you will always be willing to feel too much.*

Somewhere past the point where counting stopped meaning anything, Freya's vision greying at the edges, the working shifted under them.

It stopped asking for years.

It asked for the name.

*What I keep, I lose by name.* The old rhyme turned over in Freya's mind like a card from a deck they'd thought fully dealt. The working had slowed the corruption to a crawl — but a crawl was not a stop, and to stop it outright, to buy Maren the days she needed for her body to win, the magic wanted something larger. Not the name entirely. Not yet. But a piece of it. A letter, perhaps. A syllable. Some part of *Freya Voss* surrendered to the flame, so that the flame would have a true thing to burn instead of mere time.

And some cold, clear, exhausted corner of Freya's mind — the corner that had spent fifteen years watching the erasure page in the First Hearth's antechamber, that had read Brannoc's research and understood exactly what the covenant would one day demand — looked at that request and thought, with terrible calm:

*Practice.*

If the end of the road required their whole name written out of existence, then what was one syllable spent early, spent well, spent on the woman who had hidden them? Better it go to love than to abstraction. Better it be *chosen* here, in a circle of their own making, than seized there, in a chamber of gods.

Freya gathered the syllable. Felt which one it was — the working chose, not them; it took the soft middle of them, the vowel at their center, the sound that made their name singable — and offered it.

The magic took it gently, almost kindly, the way Maren took payment: fairly, without gloating.

The grey threadwork stopped.

Not slowed. Stopped. Froze mid-creep like a snake surprised by winter, and then, inch by grudging inch, began to die, turning brittle and dark and crumbling into the bandage as ordinary dead tissue, as merely a wound, as something a body could fight on its own terms with rest and broth and yelling.

Freya swayed. The circle guttered. Their hands slid off Maren's side and they caught themselves before they fell — barely — and realized distantly that they were crying, though whether from loss or relief or the sheer animal shock of having paid, they couldn't say.

Something was missing. They could feel the absence the way you feel a pulled tooth: a small warm hole in the middle of their name. When they tried to think the word *Freya*, it came out slightly wrong, slightly thin, a bell with a chip in it. No one else would ever notice. But they would. Every time. For whatever remained.

Worth it, they thought, looking down at Maren's face, where the furrow of sums was smoothing into actual sleep — real sleep, healing sleep, the sleep of a body that had been given back its war.

"Worth it," they said aloud, hoarsely, to no one.

The circle broke. Freya got one knee under themselves, then the other, and stood swaying in the ruins of chalk and ash and blood, feeling decades lighter and somehow emptier, the way a purse feels after paying a debt you'd have paid twice.

By the fire, Quentin was staring at them. He must have felt the working end — or seen it; his eyes had always been strange about magic, choirboy's eyes, god-adjacent eyes. He was on his feet now, and his expression wasn't relief. It was fear. He pointed at Freya, then at his own mouth, then shook his head hard, over and over, and mouthed something with such force that sound almost came anyway:

*What did it take?*

Freya smiled, and found the smile cost more than the spell had.

"Nothing important," they said. "Go to sleep, Quentin. We climb again at dawn."

And far below them, down in the valley, through the storm, lights were moving — a long procession of lanterns winding up the pilgrim road, orderly and unhurried and utterly wrong, like a wound opening in the dark.

Brannoc saw them too. His chisel stopped mid-stroke.

"That's not a patrol," he said quietly. "Patrols hurry. That's a man who thinks the mountain's already his."

At Freya's feet, Maren slept on, safe, saved — and the Cantor was coming.
