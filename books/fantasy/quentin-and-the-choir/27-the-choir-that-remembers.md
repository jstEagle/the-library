## 27. The Choir That Remembers

The First Hearth did not look like much, at first.

Quentin had imagined something grander—a cathedral of stone and starlight, a throne of song. What he found instead was a room at the mountain's heart, round as a cupped hand, its walls of dark granite veined with quartz that caught the little light Brannoc's lanterns gave and refused to give it back. The hearth itself sat in the center: a ring of blackened stones no taller than Quentin's knee, cold for centuries, holding nothing but ash so old it had turned to fine grey silk.

He stood in the doorway a long time before he could make himself enter.

It wasn't fear. Or it wasn't only fear. It was the way you felt standing outside a sickroom, knowing that whatever waited inside would ask something of you.

"Go on," Maren said from behind him, her arm still bound tight against her side, her voice rougher than usual because of it. "We didn't climb a cursed mountain so you could admire the doorframe."

"Maren," Sera said quietly.

"What? He's been humming at every campfire since Ashford Crossing like a kettle deciding whether to boil. Either he sings or I'm going to start throwing things."

Quentin almost smiled. Almost. He stepped over the threshold instead, and his boots made no sound on the stone, and he thought—absurdly, desperately—*good, good, the room is listening already.*

Behind him, Freya's charcoal scratched against paper. They'd been sketching since they entered, quick and restless, mapping the chamber in that way of theirs, turning mystery into geometry so it couldn't frighten them anymore. Brannoc knelt by the hearth-ring itself, running scarred fingers along the stones, muttering ward-cant under his breath like a man greeting colleagues he'd believed long dead.

"They're not dead," Brannoc said suddenly. His voice came out strange, thick. "The stones. There's still—I can feel the channels. Empty, but whole. Like pipes after the water's been shut off." He looked up, and in the lantern light his face was wet without him seeming to know it. "This was built to be sung *into.* Not at. Into."

"Then sing," Freya said, not looking up from their page.

Quentin's throat tightened. Since the temple, since the night he'd refused the Cantor's silent hymn and run, singing had stopped being simple. Every note cost him something now; he could feel the gods' absence on the other end of his voice like shouting down a well and hearing no splash. But he had not climbed three days through snow to stand mute at the bottom of another empty well.

He closed his eyes. Found the pitch the way he always had—not in his throat but behind his sternum, where the choir masters said the true voice lived—and let it out.

The note was small. Deliberately small. A testing note, a *may I come in* note.

The room answered.

Not with sound. With *presence.* The quartz veins in the walls kindled faintly, a blue-white shimmer that moved like breath, and the air changed texture—thicker, older, full of the smell of cold incense and beeswax and something green and living that had no business existing this far above the treeline. And then, out of the walls themselves, out of the stone as if the stone were remembering—

*singing.*

Quentin's eyes flew open. The chamber was full of voices.

They came from everywhere and nowhere, layered like sediment: high boyish sopranos, cracked adolescent lines, deep adult basses, women's voices, voices in accents dead a thousand years, singing fragments of hymns Quentin knew and hymns no one alive had ever written. They weren't ghosts exactly. They left no chill, no dread. They were more like echoes that had never finished echoing—every voice that had ever been given to this hearth, still here, still held, the way a bell holds the shape of every strike.

"Oh," Quentin breathed, and the sound of his own voice joined theirs, and the room took it gently, wove it in, and for one vertiginous moment he was singing in a choir of centuries.

"*What is it?*" Freya was beside him now, charcoal forgotten, their eyes wide and reflecting the wall-light. "*Quentin, what's happening?*"

"It's the singers." His own voice sounded far away to him. "Everyone who ever sang here. They're all still—" He laughed, or sobbed, or both. "Freya, they're all still *here.*"

Brannoc had gone very still by the hearth. Sera stood in the doorway with her hand on her sword hilt out of pure habit, though her face said she understood perfectly well what she was looking at, and that it frightened her more than any ambush.

Quentin walked toward the walls.

As he moved, the voices sorted themselves, the way a crowd parts around someone important—or around someone they've been waiting for. The nearest stratum of song thinned, and beneath it he began to hear individual voices distinctly, each one anchored somewhere in the stone, each one singing its small piece of the great covenant-hymn. He put his palm flat against the granite, and the voice under his hand sharpened into clarity: a woman, alto, singing a line about *the lamp that is not ours to carry but ours to light,* and underneath the words he felt her—the shape of her, a broad-shouldered farmer's daughter who had walked up this mountain two hundred years ago with chilblains on her feet, and sung, and gone home, and died old, and left this much of herself behind like footprints in wax.

"She's happy," Quentin said wonderingly. "Freya, she's not trapped. She's *glad.* This was the best thing she ever did and she got to keep doing it."

"That's what the covenant was," Brannoc said hoarsely. "That's what he took from us." He didn't say *the Cantor's* name. None of them did anymore; the name had started to feel like an invocation. "Every singer got to stay. A piece of them. Not enough to hurt, just enough to—" He wiped his face with the back of his wrist. "I helped build the pipes. I never once heard the water."

Quentin moved along the wall, hand trailing the stone, gathering voices. A tenor who sang like rain on rooftops. Two sisters whose harmonies braided so tightly he couldn't find the seam. A child's voice, very old, very clear, that made everyone in the room stop breathing for a moment. The strata went down and down—deeper songs, stranger modes, scales that predated Corvane, predated the temple, maybe predated the mountain having a name. At the deepest layer the voices stopped being words at all and became pure vowel, pure offering, the first covenant sung by people who hadn't yet invented theology to complicate it.

And there, at the very bottom of the chamber's memory, in the place where the first stones had been laid—

a voice he knew.

Quentin froze.

It was a young voice. A boy's voice, unbroken, bright and disciplined and *hungry*, the voice of someone who had practiced until his family told him to stop and practiced anyway. It was singing the covenant-hymn flawlessly, every interval exact, every phrase shaped with a precision that bordered on violence—and it was beautiful, and it was terrible, because Quentin knew that voice.

He had spent six years in the upper choir of the Temple of the Veiled Word. He had stood three rows behind that voice at every vespers, every ordination, every solemn procession. He knew how it had darkened over the years, how it had learned to fill the nave without effort, how the whole city had learned to call it holy.

High Cantor Ovelith had sung here. Here, at the First Hearth, before he had ever risen, before the temple, before the theft—he had climbed this mountain as a boy and given his voice to the hearth like everyone else.

"No," Quentin whispered.

The echo kept singing. Young Ovelith's voice rang off the ancient stone, flawless and fervent, and Quentin stood in front of it shaking, because now that he listened—*really* listened, the way he'd never been allowed to listen in the temple, where the Cantor's presence flattened everything into deference—he could hear what was wrong with it.

Nothing was wrong with it. That was what was wrong with it.

Every other voice in the wall had some human grain in it. The farmer's daughter rushed her phrases when she got excited. The sisters leaned into each other. The child wobbled on the high notes and didn't care. Even the wordless first singers had breath in them, bodies, the honest imperfection of people singing because they could not help it.

Young Ovelith's voice was perfect the way a blade is perfect. Every note placed, every dynamic controlled, nothing wasted, nothing *given.* He was singing at the hearth, not into it. Performing devotion instead of feeling it. And beneath the flawless line, so faint Quentin might have imagined it, there was something else—a strain, a white-knuckled grip on the music, the sound of a boy trying so hard to be worthy that he had forgotten how to simply *be.*

"He was refused," Quentin said aloud. His voice cracked on it.

Freya came to stand beside him. "What?"

"The hearth. It didn't take him." Quentin pressed his hand harder against the stone, as if he could reach through to that boy across the centuries. "Listen—it's all technique. There's no opening in it. The covenant needs you to give something, and he—he was holding everything. Holding himself so tight, wanting it so badly, that there was no room to actually—" He stopped. Swallowed. "The gods didn't refuse him because he was bad. They refused him because he wouldn't let go."

The echo faded as he spoke, the boy's voice sinking back into the deep strata, and Quentin felt its going like a door closing.

He thought of the Cantor as Quentin had last seen him: vast in his vestments, lit from below by stolen starlight, telling Quentin with perfect sincerity that the new hymn—the silent one, the hoarded one—was mercy. *Why should the gods eat our voices and give back only weather?* he had said. *I am going to feed them once, from a treasury instead of from throats, and then no child will ever again be taken up a mountain and poured out.* He had meant it. That was the horror of Ovelith; he had always meant it.

But he had been a boy here first. A boy with a voice like a drawn bow, who had offered everything he had to the hearth and been passed over—not punished, not cast out, just *not chosen*—and had walked back down the mountain with the refusal lodged in him like a splinter, and had spent fifty years building a theology around the wound.

*If the gods will not take my voice freely,* the splinter whispered, in a voice Quentin knew intimately, *then I will take theirs instead.*

"Quentin?" Freya's hand hovered near his shoulder, not quite touching. They had gotten better at that, these last weeks—better at asking with their hands instead of taking. "You've gone grey. What did you find?"

"Him." Quentin's mouth was dry. "Ovelith. He's down there. He sang here when he was young—younger than me. He tried to join the covenant and the hearth wouldn't have him." He turned, and the wall-light made Freya's face look carved out of the same blue-white quartz. "Freya, he's not doing this because he's evil. He's doing this because he auditioned for godhood when he was twelve years old and got rejected, and he has never, not for one day in fifty years, let himself grieve it. He skipped grief and went straight to conquest."

Freya was quiet a moment. Then, in the dry tone they used when they were about to say something that would reorganize the entire problem: "So we're not fighting a monster. We're fighting a choirboy who never got a second audition."

"Don't," Quentin said, and heard the edge in his own voice. "Don't make me feel sorry for him. Not him. He silenced the stars, Freya. He's going to kill Maren's recovery with worry if she hears how bad the roads are, he sent Sera's squad to murder us, he—" His voice broke again, uselessly. "I sang next to him for six years. I *worshipped* him. And the whole time, the thing driving him was a hurt he could have fixed by just—by letting one note go wrong. One honest, ugly, human note."

"And you think that's easy?" Brannoc said from the hearth. He hadn't moved. His hands were still on the stones, and his voice had a rasp in it Quentin had never heard before. "Boy, I built wards for thirty years and drank for ten because I couldn't forgive myself for one bridge. People don't skip grief because they're strong. They skip it because the grief is bigger than anything they've got to hold it with." He looked up, and his eyes were red-rimmed and steady. "Fifty years is a lot of grief to carry uphill."

Silence settled over the chamber, broken only by the faint layered murmur of the remembered choir, singing on in the walls as they had sung on through every century, faithful even when the hearth went cold, faithful even when the last pilgrim stopped coming. Quentin realized his cheeks were wet and didn't remember crying.

Sera spoke from the doorway, quiet and precise. "If he was refused here, he'll know this chamber. He'll know what it does. When he comes—and he'll come—he won't fight us for the room. He'll fight us for the *choir.*" Her jaw tightened. "Those voices are the covenant's raw material. If he can claim them, twist them, add them to his treasury—"

"He doesn't need to claim them," Quentin said slowly. The thought arrived cold, fully formed, the way the worst thoughts do. "He needs to do what he's always done. Make them an offer they can't understand well enough to refuse. He nearly did it to me with glory. He nearly did it to Freya with freedom." He looked around at them—at Freya with their charcoal-stained fingers, at Brannoc with his ruined hands, at Sera with her burned warrant, at Maren propped in the doorway refusing to sit down. "He's spent fifty years learning how to sound like the hearth. Perfect. Flawless. Everything a lonely person wants to hear."

Maren snorted from the doorway. "Then it's lucky for us," she said, "that nobody in this room is lonely anymore. Get over here and help me with the supplies, choirboy. If we're going to be besieged by a cantor, I want soup first."

A ragged laugh went around the chamber, and the walls seemed to take that too, folding it in among the centuries of song.

But later—much later, when the others slept in the antechamber and Quentin sat alone by the cold hearth keeping the first watch—the deep strata stirred again. The quartz veins brightened without his singing. And out of the oldest layer of the wall, the wordless first voices parted, the way they had parted for him before, and something else came forward.

Not a voice this time. A pressure. A warmth, faint as a coal remembered rather than seen, rising out of the hearth-stones themselves, gathering in the air above the ash. It had no shape yet. It was deciding whether to take one.

Quentin sat very still, his heart hammering the old choir rhythm—*ready, breathe, offer*—and the warmth hung above the dead fire, considering him, considering them all, and began, very softly, to listen.
