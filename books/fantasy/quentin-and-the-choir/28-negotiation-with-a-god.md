## 28. Negotiation with a God

The hearth-shard woke at moonrise.

I had been sitting with it for three hours by then, cross-legged on the cold flagstones of the antechamber, watching it the way you watch a banked fire—half hoping, half afraid of what the flame might ask of you. It was no bigger than my palm. A splinter of starlight, dull as river glass, wrapped in cloth that Brannoc had woven from ward-thread and old prayers. It sat in my hands like something sleeping, or pretending to sleep.

Quentin slept in the next room. I could hear him breathing through the wall—slow, even, the breath of someone who had spent himself empty that afternoon and was being refilled by sheer stubbornness. Maren was snoring in the way she denied snoring. Sera kept watch on the eastern stair, and Brannoc was somewhere below with his tools and his guilt, doing whatever ward-smiths do when they can't sleep.

So it was just me and the shard when it opened its eye.

Not literally. There was no eye. But there was a *looking*, sudden and total, like the moment a lamp is lit in a window across a dark valley—you realize you've been visible all along.

"Well," I said, because my mouth has always been braver than the rest of me. "You're awake."

*Small,* said the shard. The voice wasn't sound. It arrived behind my eyes, in the place where I keep my oldest memories—the smell of my mother's ink, the weight of my father's hand on my head. It used those things to speak, which felt like theft and like tenderness at once. *I am small now. Do not be deceived by what I was.*

"I'm not," I said. "I've read the ledgers."

A pause. Something moved in the shard's dimness, like a fish turning under ice.

*A scribe who reads ledgers. How rare. How dangerous.* Another turn. *You are the one who writes charms for coins in a night market. You are the one whose debt was fifteen years tall. You burned it.*

"News travels fast among the diminished."

*Among the dead, faster.* The shard brightened a fraction—a candle-flame deciding to be honest about itself. *We heard the ledger burn from very far away, little scribe. Paper screaming. Ink unbinding. Do you know what a name sounds like when it is freed?*

"No."

*Like a door opening in a house where every door has been locked for a generation.* The light steadied. *That is why I speak to you and not to the singer. He would give anything. You—* the shard seemed to consider me, weigh me, find me heavier than I looked— *you count first.*

My hands tightened around the cloth. "Is that an insult?"

*It is a job description.* And then, before I could decide whether to be flattered: *Ask your question. You have been holding one since you sat down. It is making your thoughts loud.*

I took a breath. Outside, wind worried the mountain's broken teeth; somewhere below, Brannoc's hammer ticked against stone, patient as a heartbeat.

"The covenant," I said. "Brannoc says it can be reforged. Quentin's voice, a willing scribe, the First Hearth. But he also says—" and here my throat did the thing it does when I don't let myself think too precisely, "—he says what it costs. A voice given permanently. A name written out. And I need to know if that's true, or if it's a price some god invented because gods have always been good at inventing prices."

The silence went on long enough that I counted forty of Brannoc's hammer-taps.

*True,* the shard said at last. *And not invented. And not mine to forgive.* The light guttered, steadied. *Understand what we were, scribe, so you understand what we are. When the covenant was whole, we held the wards and lit the stars, and your ancestors fed us with song—not worship. Song. We were not masters. We were... partners. The way a hearth is partner to the house. You fed us; we warmed you. Neither of us owned the other.*

"And Ovelith severed the feeding."

*He dammed it.* For the first time, something like anger moved through the voice, and the temperature of the room dropped two degrees. *He built his silent hymn and taught it to children who trusted him, and every note they did not sing was a thread he cut. He thought he was plugging a leak. He was strangling us in our sleep. Then he gathered what light we still had—our own blood, our own brightness—and called it temple property.*

"The vials," I said. "The smuggler bazaar."

*Our grief, sold by the dram.* Flat. Cold. *And now he comes here, to this hearth, to do it properly. To reforge the covenant with himself as its only mouth. One man between the realms. Every prayer passing through his throat. Every gift passing through his hands.*

"He told Quentin he'd be glorious."

*He tells everyone what they most hunger to hear. That is not a lie, exactly. It is bait.* The shard pulsed once, slow. *Listen to me carefully now, scribe, because this is the part I cannot say twice, and the part your friends must understand before he arrives—he will arrive; his storm-callers are already climbing the western face, and their boots make a sound like hail on a roof—*

My stomach dropped. "How long?"

*Two days. Perhaps less, if the weather serves him.* No apology for the interruption, no sense that our negotiation mattered more than its content. Gods, even small ones, are terrible accountants of human comfort. *Here is the part. If the covenant is reforged freely—if a singer gives his voice because he chooses, if a scribe gives her name because she chooses—then the bond that forms is a partnership. Wards that hold because both sides hold them. Stars that burn because both sides feed them. It will be smaller than the old covenant. Diminished, as I am diminished. Honest, as I am trying to be.*

"But if it's coerced—"

*Then it is not a covenant. It is a cage with better furniture.* The light flared hard enough to throw my shadow up the far wall, huge and wrong. *If Ovelith sings his version—if he forces the hearth open with stolen light and a hijacked melody—then the bond that forms runs one direction only. He becomes the tap. The realms become the cask. Mortals drained to feed his divinity; gods enslaved as engines. Both realms, scribe. Not one. He does not mean to save your world at the expense of ours. He means to own both, and call the ownership salvation.*

I thought of him in the choir loft, listening too closely. I thought of scraped-away words in old hymnals. I thought of a boy's voice cracking chapel glass on the night the stars went out, and how the temple had marked that boy for death rather than wonder.

"Why tell me?" I asked quietly. "Why not appear to Sera, or Brannoc, or Maren? They're soldiers and smiths and landladies. I'm a debt-bound scribe who sells luck-charms at four coppers."

*Because you asked the right first question.* The light softened. *Everyone else will ask what the covenant can do. You asked what it costs, and whether the cost was honest. That is the question the old covenant was built on, and it is the question Ovelith never learned to ask. He was refused, you know. Long ago. He sang at this hearth as a young man, and we did not take his offering, and he has spent fifty years deciding that our refusal was theft.*

Something in my chest ached, unexpectedly. Even villains, it turns out, have origin stories, and the worst ones are usually just wounds that learned to bite.

"What happens to him?" I said. "If we reforge it truly. What happens to a man who wanted to be the only door between heaven and earth?"

*That depends on whether anyone leaves him a smaller door to walk through.* The shard dimmed to embers. *But that is tomorrow's arithmetic. Tonight, I have one more thing to say, and then I must sleep again, because I am very small and speaking costs me what little I have left.*

"Say it."

*The price is real. The voice does not return. The name does not return. We cannot soften this, and any god who promises to soften it is lying, and I will not lie to the people who came all this way in a vegetable cart.* A pause, and in it, something almost gentle. *But hear this, Freya Voss. Freely offered does not mean gladly offered. It does not mean without fear, or grief, or rage at the unfairness of it. It means chosen. Chosen with shaking hands is still chosen. Chosen while crying is still chosen. The old covenant was sung by terrified people on a cold mountain, and it held for nine hundred years.*

I hadn't told the shard my name. I hadn't told it anything. It knew anyway, the way fire knows wood.

"And if we refuse?" My voice came out smaller than I intended. "If we decide the price is too high and walk away?"

*Then the realms go dark slowly instead of quickly, and I will grieve you, and I will not blame you.* The ember-light flickered. *Freedom includes the freedom to fail us, scribe. That is the entire point. That is the thing Ovelith never understood, and the thing you must carry down the mountain to your strange, ragged family: the moment any of you is compelled—by duty, by desperation, by each other—the covenant dies before it is born. It must be a gift. All of it. Or nothing.*

The looking withdrew. The shard cooled in my hands until it was just glass again, dull and quiet, a splinter of something vast folded up small.

I sat there a long while after, watching my breath fog in the dark, doing what I do best: tallying.

Two days. Storm-callers on the western face. A singer who'd give his voice without hesitation, which frightened me more than any enemy. A landlady already wounded. An inquisitor burning her career for strangers. A smith with everything to prove to a bridge and nobody under it.

And a scribe with a page waiting in her kit, blank except for one line she hadn't written yet.

*Chosen with shaking hands is still chosen.*

I pressed the shard gently back into its wrappings, stood, and went to wake the others—because the god was right about one thing more: we had two days, and a family to gather, and a choice that belonged to no one but each of us.

When I pushed open the antechamber door, Quentin was already awake, sitting up in the dark, eyes wide, hand pressed flat against his throat.

"Freya," he whispered. "I dreamed someone was singing. Under the floor. Someone *small*."

I looked at him—at the boy who could still reach the fading gods, who didn't yet know the full shape of what I'd learned tonight—and felt the ledger of my heart open to a new page.

"We need to talk," I said. "All of us. Now."
