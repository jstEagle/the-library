## 31. Quentin's Last Song

The hearth chamber smelled of cold ash and old stone, the way a room smells when it has been holding its breath for three hundred years.

Quentin stood at the edge of the First Hearth with his hands shaking—not from fear, he told himself, though it was partly fear, but because his voice had never been this loud inside him before. It had always been a thing he carried carefully, like a bowl of water across a slick floor. Now it was a tide against a door.

*Sing,* the hearth-shard had said, in that voice like wind through a canyon, like Freya's name spoken by someone who loved them. *Sing what was sung first.*

Around him, the others took their places. Brannoc knelt at the western ward-stone, his burned hands wrapped in Maren's linen strips, whispering the old calibration words under his breath like a man praying in a language he'd sworn off. Sera stood at the shattered doorway with her sword drawn and her back to the ritual, guarding them from what was coming up the mountain—and something *was* coming; the stones themselves had told them so, a tremor in the floor like a heartbeat accelerating. Maren sat propped against the eastern pillar, grey-faced, her bandaged side soaking through, holding a fire poker across her knees as if it were a scepter and she a very stubborn queen.

And Freya stood beside him, close enough that their shoulder touched his.

"You don't have to do this," Freya said quietly. "There might be another way."

"There isn't, and you know it better than I do." Quentin tried to smile. "You're the one who reads everything."

"I read everything," Freya agreed. Their eyes were wet and furious. "That's how I know this is going to hurt you."

"Everything worth singing does." He looked out over the cold hearth—the great ring of blackened stone, the ash of centuries, the single shard of starlight cradled in its center like an egg in a dead nest. "Fifteen years they kept me in that choir telling me my voice was a duty. A tool. A *resource*." His jaw tightened. "They never once said it was a gift I could give. There's a difference. Tonight I'm giving it."

Freya's hand found his and squeezed once, hard, and let go.

Then the mountain shook, and there was no more time for words.

* * *

He began where all songs begin—with breath.

He drew it deep, and the air of the hearth chamber came into him tasting of snow and iron and, faintly, impossibly, of honey—of vespers candles, of the incense he'd swung as a boy of six, of every morning for fifteen years when the choir assembled and the Cantor's baton rose. The air remembered. That was the first miracle: the air itself remembered the song.

He sang the first note.

It left him like a bird leaving a cage whose door had rusted shut—he felt it tear free, felt the place where it had lived go suddenly hollow, and he understood with terrible clarity what Brannoc's research had meant. *A singer gives their voice permanently.* Not all at once. Note by note. Each syllable of the covenant-song was a syllable he would never sing again, plucked out of him like a thread from a tapestry, and woven instead into the hearth.

The note struck the shard of starlight.

Light bloomed. Not the harsh white of temple lanterns but something warmer, older—a gold shot through with colors Quentin had no names for, the colors of stars seen through water. The ash in the hearth stirred. Somewhere far below the mountain, something vast turned over in its sleep and began, slowly, to listen.

The second note cost more. He felt it go—*mi*, the sweet interval, the one the choirboys used to pass between them in the cloister like contraband candy—and where it had been there was now a silence shaped exactly like itself. He could feel the shape of every note he lost. They were becoming a map of absence inside him.

But oh, the hearth was waking.

Warmth spread outward from the shard in rings, and frost that had furred the walls for three hundred years wept and ran. The ghost-echoes stirred in the antechamber behind him—that vast soft congregation of every singer since the first covenant—and Quentin heard them begin to hum beneath his melody, a drone like the earth itself humming, hundreds of voices folded into one sustained tone. They were carrying him. Every boy who had ever stood in a choir loft and given his voice to the gods was standing behind him now, and their hands were under his.

The third note. The fourth. The fifth.

With each one, the hearth brightened, and Quentin dimmed. He could feel it happening—his voice thinning at the edges, the way a candle thins as it burns down. He sang the rising phrase that meant *we offer freely*, and the word *freely* went out of him forever, and he thought: *I will never be able to say that word again. I will never be able to tell anyone I chose this.* Grief hit him sideways, sharp and stupid—grief for a single word—and he let it come, because Brannoc had told him grief made the song truer, and the gods could taste the difference between an offering and a theft.

Behind the melody, the drone of the ancestors swelled.

And then, from the direction of the stair, came a sound that did not belong.

* * *

It was singing too.

Ovelith's voice came up the stairwell ahead of him, and Quentin's whole body flinched, because he knew that voice—he had spent fifteen years tuning himself to it, bending his own pitch to sit beneath it, loving it and fearing it in equal measure. It was still beautiful. That was the horror of it. Fifteen years of hoarded godlight had not ruined the High Cantor's instrument; it had *polished* it, filled it with stolen resonance until every note rang with the borrowed brightness of a hundred dying stars.

Ovelith entered the hearth chamber the way weather enters a valley.

He came up the stair with his retinue behind him—six storm-callers in grey, their hands crackling—and he was already singing, and his song slid into the space Quentin's melody had opened like a knife sliding between ribs.

Quentin felt it happen. His covenant-song was a road being built note by note, a bridge between the hearth and whatever waited beyond the veil of the world—and Ovelith stepped onto that road and began to *redirect* it. The Cantor's stolen light poured into the melody, gilding it, warping it, and Quentin felt the bridge's far end swing away from the gods' diminished country and toward something else: a single point, a single throat, a covenant with one intermediary, one keeper, one king.

*Mine,* the hijacked melody said, in Ovelith's beautiful voice. *All of it, mine, and through me, mercy.*

"No," Quentin gasped—but the word came out as song, because he no longer had any other way to speak, and his refusal became just one more note for Ovelith to steal.

The hearth flickered. The warmth stuttered. In the center of the ring, the shard dimmed like an eye closing against glare.

"Quentin!" Brannoc roared from the ward-stone, his burned hands pressed flat against the rock. "He can't make new music—he can only take yours! Don't fight him on your melody—*change the melody!*"

*I can't.* The thought was a white flare of panic. *Every change costs another note. I'm running out of voice. I'm running out of—*

And then Maren was there.

She should not have been standing. She was grey as the ash, her bandage blooming red, and she should have been lying against her pillar dying quietly like a sensible woman. Instead she planted herself between Quentin and the Cantor, raised her fire poker like a conductor's baton, and shouted over the music:

"BOY! You sang at my shop when the flour delivery came and the cart horse died in the street! You sang it *wrong* and you sang it *loud* and you made up half the words! Sing it wrong NOW!"

Something cracked open in Quentin's chest.

Because she was right. That was the thing about the temple choir—it had taught him perfection, and perfection was a cage, and cages could be broken into. Ovelith knew the covenant-song. Ovelith had studied it for forty years; he had scraped the old words off the hymnals himself; he knew every canonical phrase, every sanctioned ornament, every note of every proper performance. He had prepared his entire life to hijack the covenant-song *as it was written*.

But Quentin had learned music twice. Once in the choir loft, from masters. And once before that, age four, sitting on a kitchen step in a village that no longer existed, learning songs from a woman who sang them differently every time because the song wasn't the notes—the song was the *saying of them*.

Quentin changed the melody.

He took the next phrase—the phrase meaning *we gather in thy light*—and he bent it, dragged it a third lower, syncopated it against the ancestors' drone, threw in a grace-note that would have gotten him whipped in the choir loft. It cost him two notes he'd been saving. He felt them go and didn't care. The hearth flared gold.

Ovelith's stolen harmony lurched, missed its grip, scrambled after him.

*There,* Quentin thought, wild with something that was almost laughter. *Chase me.*

He became, for the span of a few impossible minutes, the thing the temple had trained out of him and life had trained back in: an improviser. He bent phrases. He inverted them. He stole tricks from market ballads and funeral keens and the work-song Maren hummed while kneading bread, and each theft was a note spent, a hollow forming inside him where music used to live, and he paid and paid and paid and the hearth drank and blazed and grew.

Ovelith raged. The Cantor's voice climbed, gorgeous and furious, pouring more stolen starlight into his pursuit, and the storm-callers raised their hands and lightning crawled down the walls—but Sera met them in the doorway, steel ringing against storm, and Brannoc shouted calibration words and the ward-stones flared blue-white, and the battle raged around the hearth like weather around a lighthouse, and through all of it Quentin sang.

He was losing himself. He could feel it honestly now, without panic—each note a small death, each hollow a small peace. He was perhaps halfway through the covenant-song. Perhaps less. The remaining music in him was finite, a purse emptying coin by coin, and the hardest passages were yet to come, and somewhere in the last verse—Brannoc had warned him, reading from the scorched antechamber texts with shaking hands—somewhere in the last verse the song required a final word, a sealing word, and the sealing word required a scribe.

*Not me,* Brannoc had said. *The song provides the voice. But a covenant is a contract, and a contract needs a hand.*

Quentin sang the descending passage that meant *thy will and ours, one will*, and gave away the most beautiful interval he owned, and watched it become light.

* * *

Ovelith stopped chasing.

That was how Quentin knew the end was near. The Cantor's voice dropped away from the pursuit, fell back, gathered itself—and when it came again it came quiet, intimate, pitched only for Quentin across the blazing chamber, threading beneath the melody like a hand on the back of the neck.

"Boy." Gentle. Terrible. "I sang here too. Did the echoes show you? I was fourteen. I stood where you stand, and I offered everything, and the gods were *silent*. Do you know why?" The stolen light around him pulsed with each word. "Because they were already dying. Because the covenant was already rotten, and they took my offering and gave nothing back, and I swore no child would ever again pour themselves into that silence. Everything I have done—all of it—I did so that no one would ever stand where you are standing and be refused."

For one horrible moment, Quentin believed him. That was the Cantor's true weapon, he understood finally—not the storm-callers, not the hoarded light, but this: Ovelith had *been* him. A choirboy with a gift and a wound. The melody wavered. The hearth dimmed a fraction. The ancestors' drone faltered, listening.

And Quentin, voice nearly spent, did the only thing left. He answered in song, with the few notes remaining that he could bend to speech, and what he sang was simple, and true, and cost him the last of his high register:

*I am not afraid of silence. I am afraid of singing alone.*

The hearth *roared*.

Light erupted upward through the broken roof, through the snow, into the sky where no stars hung—and the sky answered. Far above, faint as memory, one point of light kindled. Then another. Then a scatter of them, trembling, uncertain, like a congregation rising one by one in a dark church.

The gods were listening. The gods were *coming*.

Ovelith screamed—a raw, unlovely sound, all the beauty stripped from it at last—and hurled everything he had left into the melody, a tidal wave of stolen starlight aimed at the hearth, at the bridge, at the fragile newborn link between realms. The light crashed against Quentin's song and the song bent, bowed, began to break—

—and Quentin reached for the final verse, the sealing verse, and opened his mouth, and found the place where the sealing word should be.

Empty. He had nothing left there. The last verse needed a voice, and his was spent, hollowed, a flute with no air, a lamp with no oil—

No. Not quite empty.

At the bottom of him, below all the spent notes, there remained one thing. Not a note. Smaller than a note. A single syllable's worth of sound, hidden so deep the temple had never found it, the Cantor had never guessed it, a thing he had never once sung aloud in fifteen years of vespers and rehearsals and perfect obedient performances.

His own name. The one his mother gave him, before Corvane renamed him *choirboy*, before the temple numbered him among its instruments. Three small sounds, saved like a coin sewn into a coat hem.

He could spend it on the sealing word. It would be enough. It would finish the song.

Or—

Across the chamber, through the blaze and the battle-smoke, Quentin found Freya's eyes. Freya stood at the hearth's edge with the erasure page open on their forearm, quill in hand, and they had heard the last verse coming the way they heard everything—by reading the structure of it—and they already knew what it required. Their face was calm in the way that Freya's face was calm, which was to say it was the calm of someone standing on a ledge deciding whether the fall or the staying was worse.

The sealing word needed a voice. The covenant needed a hand.

Quentin sang the last syllable he had—his hidden name, his mother's word, the smallest and largest thing he owned—and gave it to the melody as the seed of the sealing word, and the hearth caught it, and held it, and waited, glowing, for the hand that would write the rest.

The sky above the broken roof filled with returning stars.

And Freya stepped forward, quill poised over the erasure page, and Quentin—voiceless now, truly voiceless, a man made entirely of hollows shaped like music—could not even cry out to stop them.
