## 32. The Name Written Out

The hearth chamber was singing.

Not Quentin—not yet—but the mountain itself, a low harmonic rising through the black stone floor like water finding its level. The relit hearth burned at the center of the room in colors fire had no business wearing: blue-white, then gold, then a green so deep it looked like the memory of forests no one alive had seen. Every note of the covenant-song Quentin had given away so far hung in the air, visible now as threads of light, and the ghost-choir of every singer since the first covenant stood in rings around the flame, mouths open, adding their vanished voices to his.

Freya knelt at the edge of the hearth-ring with the erasure page across their knees.

It was smaller than they had expected. In Brannoc's research—in the fevered weeks of deciphering ward-fragments and shrine-graffiti and one mad monk's marginalia—the page had grown enormous in their imagination, a thing of consequence, heavy as a door. But it was only a leaf of vellum gone soft with age, blank except for the faintest impression of names that had been written out of the world long ago. When Freya tilted it toward the hearthlight, the impressions caught like breath on glass: *Aldric. Mave. Sef. Ilo.* Names without surnames, without histories. Just people who had once been willing.

"You don't have to read them," Quentin had said earlier, when Freya first unrolled the page. His voice was already half-gone—roughened, thinned, like a bell with a crack running through it. He could still speak, but every word cost him something now, and he spent them the way he'd learned to spend everything: carefully, and on other people. "You don't owe them mourning."

"I know." Freya had run a thumb over the ghost-impressions anyway. "But somebody should remember that remembering is a choice. That's rather the whole point of me."

That had been an hour ago. It felt like a year.

* * *

The battle was still happening around the edges of the chamber—Freya could hear it the way you hear rain from indoors. Sera's blade met her former mentor's somewhere behind the eastern pillars, steel ringing in rhythms too fast to count. Maren was shouting something ferocious about a fire poker and exactly where Ovelith's retinue could put their sanctified authority. Brannoc crouched at the chamber's far archway with blood on his hands that wasn't all his own, rebuilding a shattered ward-stone by feel, muttering the old smith-cant like a prayer: *hold, hold, you great stubborn rock, hold.*

And Ovelith was losing.

That was the strange part. The High Cantor of the Veiled Word stood at the opposite side of the hearth-ring in robes that blazed with stolen light—vials of starfire strapped across his chest like a general's medals, each one leaking radiance through the seams—and he was *losing*, because he had never understood what Quentin understood in his bones. Light hoarded is just weight. Light given is music. Every note Quentin surrendered made the hearth brighter; every vial Ovelith cracked open to fuel himself made him heavier, slower, more alone.

"Boy!" Ovelith's voice still filled the chamber; it had been trained to. "You are spending yourself like a drunkard spends coin! I am offering you *permanence*—a covenant that will not fade, that will not need shepherds and choirboys dying generation after generation—one voice, one will, one—"

"One man," Quentin said. He was swaying on his feet, both hands pressed to the hearthstone, and when he lifted his head there was pity in his face, which Freya suspected hurt the Cantor worse than any blade. "You keep saying *one*. That's how I know."

Ovelith's face went through something complicated and came out furious. He reached for another vial.

Which meant it was time.

* * *

Freya looked down at the page.

They had rehearsed this moment, in the way you rehearse anything unbearable: in snatches, sideways, always with an escape route in mind. On the cart out of Corvane, watching the walls shrink. In the ruined shrine while Quentin sang to a flicker where a star should be. Last night, in the snow, while the ancestral echoes showed everyone else their regrets and Freya lay awake composing arguments against themselves.

*You're a scribe,* they'd told themselves. *This is the one text you were born to write.*

But rehearsal, it turned out, was useless. Because here, now, with the hearth blazing and Quentin's half-spent voice threading through the stone and the smell of ozone and hot metal and Maren's spilled stew from three days ago somehow still clinging to Freya's coat—here, the sacrifice refused to stay abstract. It had details. That was the cruelty of it. Not *you will cease*. Smaller than that. *You will never taste stew again. You will never again feel Maren's hand on the back of your neck, rough and brief, the way she touched everything she loved without ever saying so. You will never finish teaching Brannoc to read past his letters, and he'll never know why you stopped coming to lessons. You will never hear Quentin speak your name.*

Freya's hands were shaking. They noticed this with a scribe's detached interest, the way they'd notice a smudge in an otherwise clean manuscript. Interesting. Regrettable. Continue.

There was a stylus tucked behind their ear—there always was; fifteen years of habit didn't dissolve in a crisis—and they took it down and weighed it. Cheap iron, chipped nib, bought from a night-market stall for two coppers during their second year in Corvane. It had written ten thousand harmless charms. Little lies of warmth: *this candle will not gutter in wind. This child's fever will break by morning. This lonely person will find one kind stranger before week's end.* All real. All magic, quietly, illegally, lovingly done. The temple had called it crime. Freya had called it the only honest work in the city.

The erasure page wanted more than ink, though. Brannoc had been clear about that, grim-faced over the antechamber texts. *It wants intent. It wants the whole of you behind the stroke. A name written out in fear stays half-written—a haunting, not a sacrifice. You have to mean it free.*

*Mean it free.*

Freya thought about debt.

Fifteen years of it. Sold into indenture at nine years old for a father's gambling losses and a mother's silence, both of which Freya had spent a lifetime trying to forgive and mostly succeeded at, lately, thanks to a snowfield full of ghosts who had shown them two frightened parents making the worst choice available to them and living inside it ever after. Fifteen years of tallies. The Ledger of Small Debts, kept in a neat hand in a locked drawer: *candle-tallow, 3 coppers. Room, winter term, 40 silver. Interest, compounding, merciless.* The temple's arithmetic had a genius for making freedom feel like a currency you'd never earn enough of. Every charm sold was a drop against an ocean. Every year older was a year less valuable. Freya had done the sums a thousand times and the answer was always the same: *not in this life.*

And then a choirboy had stumbled into their stall smelling of incense and terror, and a landlady had hidden them both without asking for a single coin, and a drunkard under a bridge had wept over a second chance, and an inquisitor had walked half-frozen into camp and been *fed*, and somewhere in all of that Freya had done new sums and gotten, for the first time, a different answer.

Because here was the thing the temple's ledgers could never hold: some debts you pay by emptying yourself out. And some you pay by finally letting yourself be full.

Quentin had said it at the fire, halfway up the mountain, in the dark, in that voice that was already going: *"Invisible people can't be missed. That's what you said. Well—I'd miss you. So stop being invisible. Even if it's only for one day. Even if it costs everything. Be missable, Freya. Let us know exactly what we're losing."*

So Freya did the last sum.

What they were giving: a name. A self. The specific, irreplaceable texture of being Freya Voss—the wry loneliness, the tally-marks, the fear under the contentment, the hands that knew how to make small warm things. Everything the family would lose. Everything Quentin would grieve without knowing why.

What they were buying: stars. Real ones, back in the sky, over Corvane and every city like it. Wards that held because gods held them, and gods fed because mortals sang to them freely, and a world where no child got sold for a parent's debts because the covenant itself would run on gift, not extraction. A diminished but honest light. Maren's shop with its shutters open. Brannoc teaching ward-craft to kids who'd never have to hide it. Sera sleeping without warrants under her pillow. Quentin—

Quentin, voiceless, alive, standing in a world with stars in it, humming melodies that carried love even when memory failed.

*Yes,* Freya thought. *That trade. I make that trade.*

Their hands stopped shaking.

* * *

"Freya." Quentin's voice, ragged, alarmed—he'd seen them move to the hearth-ring's edge, seen the page. "Not yet. Wait for the—wait for the seventh movement, the hearth has to be at full—"

"It'll get there about the same time I do." Freya smiled at him. It surprised them how easy the smile was. "Besides, if I wait for perfect conditions, I'll be waiting my whole life. I've had practice."

"Freya—"

"Quentin." They said it gently, and he stopped, because fifteen years of hiding taught a person how to put a whole paragraph into a name. "Listen. I need to say some things fast, and you're going to want to argue, and we don't have time for you to be right. So: hush."

He hushed. Of course he hushed. He was the bravest coward Freya had ever known, and he knew a last request when it stood up and introduced itself.

"Maren." Louder, across the chamber, over the ring of steel. Maren turned, poker raised, blood on her sleeve that Freya chose to believe was borrowed. "You hid two strangers in your cellar and asked for nothing. Do you know what that did to me? Fifteen years of arithmetic, and you broke the whole ledger in one night. Thank you isn't big enough. There isn't a word big enough. I'm going to spend eternity looking for it."

Maren's face crumpled and reformed into fury, which was her language for grief. "Don't you *dare* talk like—"

"Brannoc!" He looked up from his ward-stone, wild-eyed. "The letters. We got as far as H. You were doing beautifully. Finish the alphabet. Promise me."

"Aye," he said, and his voice broke on it. "Aye, I promise. Freya—lass—there has to be another—"

"There isn't, and you know the texts better than I do, so don't insult us both." Freya turned last to Sera, who had disengaged from her mentor just long enough to look—Sera Vane, defection earned, doubt survived, standing in the firelight with her sword point-down like a penitent. "You were asked to betray them. So was I. We both said no. Whatever else happens—" Freya's throat tightened; they pushed through it. "Whatever else happens, that's the whole covenant, right there. Two people saying no. It scales."

Sera bowed her head. Behind her, her mentor screamed something about traitors and lunged, and the duel swallowed her again.

Which left Quentin.

He had crossed to the hearth-ring despite everything, despite the song pulling at him, and he stood an arm's length away with tears cutting tracks through the ash on his face, and his mouth opened and closed and opened, and finally he said, in what remained of his voice, the smallest sound Freya had ever heard a person make:

"Why?"

And oh, that deserved better than speed. That deserved the long version, the version with the night market and the landlady's cellar and the bird he'd healed and the campfire and the word *missable*. But the hearth was cresting—the seventh movement building in the stone, the ghost-choir leaning in, Ovelith cracking his last vials and screaming scripture—and Freya had perhaps thirty seconds of ordinary time left to them, and they had spent fifteen years learning exactly what fit into thirty seconds.

So they gave him the true short version.

"Because you looked at me," Freya said. "Fifteen years invisible, and the first person who actually *looked* was a boy with a broken voice in a vegetable cart, and he looked at me like I was worth finding. Nobody gets to give me that and then watch me let the sky go dark. I won't do it. I'd rather be a star than a shadow, Quentin. I'd rather be remembered wrong by people I love than remembered right by no one."

"That's not fair," he whispered.

"No," Freya agreed. "It's a covenant. Fairness never entered into it."

Then they stepped inside the hearth-ring, and the light took them gently, the way water takes a stone, and the noise of the battle fell away like a door closing, and it was quiet—quiet the way the world had been quiet the night the stars went out, but warm instead of hollow—and Freya knelt, and smoothed the erasure page flat on the hearthstone, and set the chipped iron nib against the vellum.

The impressions of the old names seemed to lean closer. *Aldric. Mave. Sef. Ilo.* Fellow travelers. Freya hoped someone had sung for them, afterward. Someone would sing for Freya. They'd made sure of it—made sure of Maren's stubbornness and Brannoc's promises and Sera's conscience and Quentin's impossible, covenant-defying heart. Whatever the erasure took, it would not take the taking-away cleanly. Love would leave fingerprints.

*Last words,* Freya thought, nib poised. *Every scribe dreams of them. Make them good.*

They spoke aloud, to the hearth, to the god flickering inside it, to whatever dim vast attention was listening from beyond the muffled dark:

"For the record—it was freely offered. No coercion. No despair. I checked the arithmetic twice." A breath, and then, softer, the truth under the wit: "I was a debt for fifteen years. Let this be the payment that ends debt. Not mine. Everyone's. Write it however you like, but write it *kindly*."

And then Freya Voss wrote their own name.

Four letters and five, in the neat, unshowy hand that had written ten thousand small mercies. The nib moved like it was moving through honey, like the world resisted, like every thread that tied *Freya* to *Freya* pulled taut and sang—and they felt each one as it parted. The night market going. Maren's cellar going. The cold clarity of the deciphered fragments going. Their parents' faces, forgiven at last, going. The word *home*, going. And underneath all of it, holding steady to the very end, one thread that would not part, that hummed and strained and *refused*—a melody-line, golden, insistent, threaded through with a boy's half-spent voice—and Freya thought, with the last corner of themselves that could still think: *that one's anchored in him. Good. Hold on, Quentin. Hold on—*

The hearth flared white.

Not bright—*white*, total, the color that contains every color, the first page of the world before anyone wrote on it. The battle vanished into it. Ovelith's stolen light vanished into it, unworthy of it, hoarded weight dissolving like salt in a river. The stone floor, the mountain, the distinction between *here* and *everywhere*, all of it resolved into that single generous blaze, and in the last instant before even *Freya* dissolved—before the name finished leaving the page and the page forgot what it had held and the person holding the pen became a warmth in the flame and a thread of gold in a voiceless boy's throat—

—in the last instant, Freya was not afraid.

They were *full*.

* * *

Somewhere, a hearth burned steady and blue-white and gold.

Somewhere, a man named Ovelith fell to his knees in light he could no longer hold, and the vials shattered, and the stolen stars went home.

Somewhere, four people stood in a circle around a miracle, calling a name that none of them could remember hearing before, weeping at a grief that had no shape, reaching for a person who was not there.

And somewhere—very close, closer than breath, woven into a melody that would not stop—a scribe's last thought hung on, patient as ink, waiting to be read.
