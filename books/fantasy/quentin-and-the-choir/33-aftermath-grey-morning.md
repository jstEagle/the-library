## 33. Aftermath: Grey Morning

The light came back all at once, and no one saw it happen.

There was no trumpet of dawn, no slow kindling at the mountain's eastern shoulder. One moment the world was the color of wet ash, and the next the stars were simply *there*—thousands of them, hanging in the deep blue above the First Hearth's broken roofline like a congregation that had finally been allowed back into its own cathedral. They did not blaze. They glimmered, modest and warm, the way candles look in a window when someone is home.

Brannoc stood in the snow with his hands still raised from the last ward-stone he'd set, fingers blackened to the knuckle, and looked up until his neck ached.

"Stars," he said, because no one else had said it yet, and someone had to.

Maren sat propped against a fallen pillar, her bandages stiff with frost, a fire poker lying across her knees like a scepter she'd forgotten to put down. She followed his gaze upward. Her mouth worked once, twice, before any sound came out.

"Well," she managed. "About time."

It should have been a shout. It should have been weeping and singing and Brannoc hauling everyone into a crushing, bruising knot of arms. Somewhere below them in the hearth chamber, the new covenant hummed in the stone like a struck bell that would not quite finish ringing—a smaller sound than the old one, an honest sound, a sound with rough edges. The gods were dim but present. The wards along the border, a hundred miles away, would be knitting themselves back together thread by thread, and every soul in Corvane would wake to find stars over the temple spires for the first time in weeks.

And Freya—

Brannoc turned. He'd been turning all morning, actually, some instinct dragging his head around toward the hearth chamber door every few minutes, as if he expected—

As if he expected what?

He frowned. There was a shape to the thought and nothing inside it, like a glove frozen stiff on a branch, holding the memory of a hand.

"Where—" Maren started, and stopped, and her brow creased in exactly the same way. She looked at the doorway. She looked at the snow between herself and the doorway, where there were boot prints—small ones, scribe's boots, the left one worn on the outer heel—and she looked at them for a long time without seeing them at all.

"Where's the boy?" she said instead. "Quentin. Is he—"

"Here." Quentin's voice did not come.

He tried. He was kneeling in the hearth chamber with his hands flat on the warm stone, and his mouth opened, and nothing came out—not a word, not a breath-note, not even the small ordinary sounds a body makes. His voice was gone. Not hoarse. Not tired. *Gone*, the way a tooth is gone after the pull; you keep reaching for it with your tongue and finding only the strange smooth absence where it used to live.

That was the price. He had known it going in. He had sung himself out note by note while Ovelith's stolen light screamed against the melody, and each note had cost him something he could feel leaving—*this one for the wards, this one for the stars, this one for the covenant's root*—and he had kept singing because the alternative was a dark world, and because somewhere behind him Freya was writing, and he had wanted the song to reach wherever they were going.

The hearth relit. The song ended. And Freya—

Quentin's hands curled against the stone.

Freya. The name rose in him whole and burning and then, as he reached for the face that went with it, it slipped. Not faded—*slipped*, like trying to hold water in a fist. He could feel the weight of the name. He could feel that it mattered, that it mattered more than almost anything, the way you can feel a missing stair in the dark. But when he looked inward for the person—the wry voice, the ink-stained fingers, the way they'd said *exactly this* with their whole guarded heart—there was only a warm, aching hollow, shaped precisely like someone he could not remember.

He made a sound then. It wasn't a word. It came out of the place where his voice had been, raw and wordless, a note with no music in it, and Sera—who had come up the stairs two at a time with her sword still drawn—stopped dead at the sound of it.

"Quentin." She crouched beside him. "You're hurt? Where are you—"

He shook his head. He pointed at his own throat, then shook his head again, harder, and pressed his fist against his chest as if he could hold something in.

Sera looked at him. Then at the hearth, burning low and gold and *right*, somehow right, the way a fire looks when it has finally been fed what it was owed. Then at the chamber floor, where the erasure page lay open on its stand, blank.

Blank.

She remembered reading about such pages in her induction years—inquisitors studied the old covenant law, the deep law, the law the Cantor had spent decades burying. A name written into the erasure page did not die. Dying left a body, a grave, a grief with edges. Erasure left nothing. Not a body. Not a memory. The world closed over the absence like water, seamless, and even the people who loved the erased soul would turn in circles in their own kitchens wondering why the room felt too large.

Sera Vane had trained her whole life to believe the temple's version of events. She had burned her warrant rather than carry out one more of them. And now she knelt in the first room of the reforged world, looking at a blank page, and understood that the victory had a price none of them would even be allowed to see.

Someone had been here. Someone had finished it. The page was blank and the hearth was lit and Quentin was making that sound, that terrible soundless sound, and Sera realized she was the only one in the room who knew enough to grieve properly—and she hadn't even known them. Whoever they were.

She put her hand on Quentin's shoulder and stayed there, and let him lean.

* * *

In Corvane, the bells went mad.

Every tower in the city rang at once—not the temple's ordered peal but a chaos of clanging, because the bell-ringers had climbed their stairs half-asleep to see why the night sky had caught fire with little lights, and had dropped their mallets, and had picked them up again, and had rung whatever they could ring. People poured into the streets in nightclothes. Old women wept into their shawls. Children who had never seen a star stood in the middle of the Night Market with their heads tipped all the way back, mouths open, catching light that had traveled centuries to land on their faces.

Maren's shop stood dark, as it had for weeks—she'd abandoned it to chase her fugitives up a mountain, and the shop had sulked about it, shutters crooked, sign askew. But the neighbors noticed something odd by full morning. The little stall-space beside the shop, the one Maren had rented out years ago to a quiet tenant who sold charms, stood swept clean. The awning was straightened. Someone—or something, or some habit baked so deep into the wood that it outlasted memory—had left the counter tidy, the inkwell stoppered, a ledger's ghost-shaped absence pressed into the dust of the desktop.

The neighbor, a fishwife named Odell, stood looking at it with her arms crossed.

"Huh," she said. "Feels like I'm forgetting something."

"You're forgetting breakfast," said her daughter. "Come inside."

They went inside. The feeling passed, the way weather passes, leaving only a faint pressure behind the eyes that no one thought to examine.

Across the city, in the Temple of the Veiled Word, the surviving cantors stumbled through a dawn service with their hymnals open to pages that no longer made sense. The Cantor's silent hymn—the scraped-clean verses, the stolen words—had come apart overnight. The old text had resurfaced beneath it like bones under eroded soil, every original word restored, and the choir sang it shaking, not knowing why the harmonies felt like coming home after a long imprisonment. In the crypts below, the ward-anchors Ovelith had corrupted cooled and settled. In his private chapel, the High Cantor himself sat motionless among his shattered vials of starlight, surrounded by inquisitors who did not yet know what to charge him with, because the law they had was the law he had written.

But that was the city's business, and the city's business could wait a day. Up on the mountain, the living had more immediate work: blood to bind, wounds to clean, a storm-scarred shrine to make habitable, and a descent to plan before the passes closed again.

* * *

They buried no one, because there was no one to bury. But by afternoon Brannoc had built a marker anyway.

He didn't know why. That was the thing he couldn't stop chewing on. He was a practical man, a smith, a man who built things because they were needed—and nothing was needed. No body, no name, no reason. Yet his hands had gone looking for flat stones without consulting him, and he'd stacked them at the edge of the hearth terrace where the view opened south toward Corvane, and he'd stood back and looked at the little cairn and felt his throat close around a grief with no address.

"For who?" Maren asked, coming up behind him. She moved slowly now, one arm bound tight, but she refused all help getting anywhere, which everyone had learned not to argue with.

"Don't know," Brannoc said honestly.

Maren looked at the cairn. The wind moved her grey hair off her forehead. For a long moment neither of them spoke, and the silence between them had a strange texture—not empty, but *expectant*, like a chair someone has just risen from.

"Fifteen years I ran that shop," Maren said at last. "Rented the stall out front the whole time. Never met the tenant proper—came and went odd hours, paid in coin that was always exact, never haggled once in fifteen years, which in a night market is practically a miracle." She snorted softly. "I couldn't tell you their face if you held a knife to me."

"Why're you telling me that?"

"I don't know." She said it simply, without embarrassment. "Felt like it belonged here."

Brannoc set his big cracked hand on top of the cairn, the way he'd rest a hand on a finished piece of work. "Whoever it was," he said, "they finished something. Whole mountain's singing it. You can feel it in the stones." He paused. "I'd have liked to buy them a drink."

"You'd have liked to buy anyone a drink."

"Aye," he admitted. "But them especially. Don't ask me how I know."

Below them, Sera was teaching Maren's fire poker to behave like a quarterstaff, mostly as a joke, mostly because Maren kept demanding lessons and Sera had learned on the mountain that refusing Maren anything was a losing strategy. Their laughter drifted up the slope, thin and bright in the cold air. It was real laughter. They had earned it. The world had been saved by a voiceless boy, a drunkard smith, a defected hunter, and a landlady with a poker, and if that didn't deserve laughter, nothing did.

And yet.

All day, all four of them kept doing it—turning. Turning toward empty spaces. Setting out four bowls and staring at the fourth as though soup might explain itself into existence. Speaking a sentence and stopping halfway through, struck by the sense that it needed another voice to answer it, and waiting, and hearing wind.

Hollow victory, Sera thought, watching the sun go down behind the cairn. We won, and the win has a hole in it shaped like—I don't know what. Shaped like nothing. That's the worst part. It isn't shaped like anything at all.

* * *

Quentin did not sleep.

He sat by the hearth through the evening while the others talked quietly around him—about the descent, about Corvane, about what they would say and whom they would trust—and he nodded along, and smiled when smiling was called for, and inside he circled the same wound over and over like a tongue returning to the gap.

He had given his voice willingly. That part was clean. Every note had gone where he sent it, and the gods had taken it gently, and if he lived eighty more years in silence it would still have been worth it—he believed that down to his bones, the way you believe a load-bearing wall.

But there was something else. Something the song had carried that he hadn't put into it on purpose.

Near the end—near the white-out, near the moment the hearth flared and the world went blinding—he had felt the melody change under him. His own notes, yes, but braided now with another line, a second voice woven through his like ivy through a fence, and the second voice had been *laughing*. Not mocking. Delighted. The way Freya used to laugh when—

When.

The name again. He reached for it and it dissolved, and the grief that rushed in behind it had no object, no story, no face. It was just vast. It sat on his chest like snowfall. He found himself pressing his hand flat over his sternum as though something in there might still be handed back to him if he asked correctly, and he didn't know what he was asking for, and that was the cruelest thing: to mourn with no memory, to ache for a person the universe insisted he had never met.

The covenant had taken everything except the feeling. Some last mercy in the magic—or some last cruelty—had preserved the love and deleted the beloved.

Near midnight, unable to bear the warmth of the hearth and the others' careful kindness, Quentin pulled his coat on and went out onto the terrace.

The stars were thick overhead. Real stars, returned stars, each one a small act of grace. He tipped his head back and looked at them and his chest seized, because one of them—low in the southern sky, modest, steady—seemed to look back. Seemed to hang directly over the little stone cairn at the terrace's edge, as if assigned there. As if keeping watch.

Quentin walked to the cairn.

He didn't know why. His feet decided. He stood before the stacked stones in the freezing dark, and the shapeless grief rose in him like floodwater, and he opened his mouth—to scream, maybe, or to pray, or just to prove to himself how thoroughly the silence owned him now—

And instead, what came out was a hum.

It startled him. Sound, actual sound, rising from somewhere deeper than his ruined voice—from the place where the song still lived, the way heat lives in stones after the fire's out. It wasn't a tune he knew. It wasn't a tune at all, really. It was a phrase of melody, seven notes, looping, patient, and as he hummed it the grief in his chest began to move, began to organize itself around the notes like iron filings around a magnet, taking a shape.

A shape like someone standing beside him.

Quentin hummed the seven notes again. His breath fogged the starlight. And on the third repetition—so faint he might have imagined it, so faint it might have been only the wind moving through the cairn's gaps—something answered.

Not a voice. Not yet. Just a warmth against his shoulder, brief as a passing stranger's touch, and a sensation like ink drying, like a pen being uncapped, like a page waiting.

Down in the valley, far to the south, a lamp was being lit in a landlady's shop by hands that did not know whose lamp it was. In a drawer of that shop, wrapped in oilcloth against a dampness no one could explain, a scrap of burnt paper waited with writing on it that no one had read.

Quentin hummed the seven notes one more time, tears freezing on his cheeks, and made the mountain a promise he could not yet put into words:

*I don't know your name. But I know my heart knows you. And I am going to get you back.*
