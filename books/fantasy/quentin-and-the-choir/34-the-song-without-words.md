## 34. The Song Without Words

The grave-marker had no name on it.

That was the whole trouble, Quentin thought, kneeling in the wet grass of the hearth-shrine's eastern slope. That was the entire, unbearable shape of the thing: a stone with no name, because no one could remember one to carve.

He'd tried. Three days after the world went white and the stars came back, he'd stood before Maren and Brannoc and Sera with a piece of charcoal in his fist and tried to write it. *Freya.* The letters wouldn't hold. He'd written F-R-E and watched the last two dissolve off the slate like breath off cold glass, and Maren had caught his wrist and said, gently, "Love, what are you doing?" and he'd looked at her face—open, kind, utterly blank where a person should have been—and understood that it wasn't just him. It was everyone. The covenant had taken a price none of them had agreed to, or rather, it had taken exactly what Freya had offered, and the offering had been so complete that even grief couldn't find its target.

Everyone except him. And not even him, really. Not his mind. His mind was a house with one room sealed shut, and behind that door something paced and paced and would not lie down.

Quentin opened his mouth out of habit and, as always, nothing came.

It had been two months since the hearth relit. Two months since he'd sung the covenant-song note by note into the stone, felt each one leave him like a rib drawn out, until the last note went and the silence after it was the deepest thing he'd ever stood inside. He'd known the price going in. Brannoc had read it to them from the antechamber wall in his gravel ruin of a voice: *a singer gives their voice permanently; a scribe writes their own name out of existence.* Quentin had thought, in the arrogant clarity of that final hour, that he understood sacrifice. He'd give up singing. He'd live. He'd carry it.

He hadn't understood that the worse wound wouldn't be his own.

He knelt now in the grass, morning frost stiffening the hem of his coat, and looked at the marker Sera had cut. Plain grey granite, quarried from the mountain itself, because Brannoc said anything less would be an insult. No dates—no one knew them. No epitaph—there was nothing to write one about. Just a smooth-faced stone at the edge of the shrine grounds, facing east toward Corvane, because whoever-it-was had come from there, they supposed, though no one could say why they supposed it.

Maren left flowers anyway. That was the thing about Maren; she'd lost a person she couldn't remember losing and still her hands knew the shape of mourning. Every few days a twist of dried marigold or a sprig of rowan appeared at the base of the stone, and when Brannoc asked her who they were for, she'd frown and say, "I don't know. Habit," and put another handful down.

Sera drilled past the marker every morning on her rounds and always, always slowed. She didn't know why. Quentin had watched her do it. Her stride would shorten for three paces, her hand would drift to the warrant-burn scar on her palm, and then she'd shake herself like a horse bothered by flies and march on. Once, Brannoc had found her standing there at dusk, staring, and asked if she was all right, and she'd said, "There's someone missing from my report. I keep counting my squad and I keep coming up one over." She'd laughed at herself. It hadn't been a real laugh.

And Quentin—

Quentin hummed.

That was all he had. The covenant had taken his voice but it had left him music the way a burned house leaves a chimney standing. He couldn't shape words, couldn't sing a melody through, couldn't even hold a pitch for long before it frayed into breath. But some mornings, walking the shrine paths, a phrase would rise in him unbidden—a turn of six notes, seven—and it would feel less like composition than like remembering. Like a letter arriving from a country that no longer appeared on any map.

This morning the phrase was this: a low rising third, a pause, then a falling line that bent at the end like a sentence trailing off unfinished. He hummed it now, kneeling, and the sound came out rough and small in the cold air, and the frost on the grass around the marker began, very quietly, to shine.

Not melt. Shine. A faint silver threading through the white, the way starlight looks when you catch it in a vial.

Quentin stopped. His heart was suddenly very loud.

He'd seen that light once before—in the smuggler bazaar under Hollow Bridge, in the stolen vials of hoarded starlight, and later, impossibly, in a shard of the First Hearth held cupped in a scribe's careful hands while a diminished god spoke through it. He knew what mortal magic looked like too, the green-gold warmth of it, the working light. This was neither. This was the other thing. The old thing. The thing that lived between realms now because a choirboy and a scribe had rebuilt the road.

*Freya,* he thought, and the name rose whole and painless in his mind—rose and hung there and told him nothing, meant nothing, was just a shape without a person inside it, a coat on a hook with no shoulders to fill it. That was how it always was. He could think the name. Everyone could, now; the counter-rite rumors said names could be spoken again by those who never knew them, like words learned from a dead language. But thinking it was like pressing on a bruise in a limb that wasn't there. The ache was real. The limb was gone.

Except.

Except the melody didn't ache. The melody *knew.*

He hummed it again, slower, deliberately, watching the frost. There—the silver brightened along the falling line, dimmed at the pause, flared at the bend. As if the light were following him. As if the light were *answering*, the way something had answered in the ruined shrine a lifetime ago when he'd sung into the dark and a flicker had bloomed where a star should be, proof the gods weren't dead, only muffled.

He was on his feet without deciding to stand. He hummed the phrase a third time and added the next bit, the part that had been coming to him for weeks in dreams—a little climbing figure, four notes, stubborn, like someone insisting on a point—and the frost-light climbed with it, up the face of the blank stone, tracing a path like a finger writing.

It wrote nothing. Of course it wrote nothing. But it moved the way Freya's pen used to move, quick and certain, and Quentin's sealed room shook on its hinges.

*

He went down the hill at a half-run and nearly bowled over Brannoc, who was coming up with an armload of split ward-stone for the shrine's boundary repairs. The old smith caught him by the shoulders. "Easy, lad. You look like you've seen—" He paused. Squinted. "You look like you've heard something."

Quentin grabbed the man's calloused hand and pressed it flat against his own throat, then pointed up the hill, then made the gesture he'd developed for *come, now, hurry*—both hands beckoning, urgent, undignified. Brannoc had gotten fluent in Quentin's hands over two months; he'd said once, wryly, that it was the same grammar as ward-craft, everything in pressure and position. He set down his stones. "Show me."

At the marker, the frost had half-faded, but when Quentin knelt and hummed the rising third again, the silver woke along the grass like lamps lit down a street.

Brannoc went very still. Quentin had seen the man weep exactly once—drunk, the first night, confessing the wards—so he knew what it meant when Brannoc's face did the opposite of weeping, went dry and carved and old. "That's hearth-light," he said finally. "Or near enough. Boy. What song is that?"

Quentin spread his empty hands. *I don't know. It comes to me. It won't stop.*

"Where did you learn it?"

*I didn't learn it. It learned me.*

Brannoc crouched, laid one broad palm flat above the frost without touching it, the way he tested ward-stones for residual charge. His lips moved as he counted something only he could perceive. When he looked up, his eyes were wet after all. "There's a pattern in it. Under your tune. Like a signature under a drawing." He swallowed. "I helped build the border wards, lad. I know what a maker's mark feels like." He tapped the blank granite, gently, the way you'd tap the shoulder of someone sleeping. "Somebody signed this hill."

Quentin's hands were shaking. He pressed them together to stop them and made the sign for *who*, knowing it was useless, hating that it was useless.

"I don't know who," Brannoc said. "But I know this much: the covenant took a memory and left a truth. That's not how erasure works. Erasure is clean. This—" he gestured at the shining frost, at Quentin, at the whole impossible morning "—this is a seam. Somebody hemmed the world back together and left the stitches showing on purpose."

On purpose. Quentin turned that over. Freya had been a scribe. Freya had spent fifteen years leaving harmless marks in plain sight—charms disguised as trinkets, magic disguised as craft, a whole self disguised as nobody. If anyone in creation knew how to hide a signature inside something innocent, how to fold a name into a melody so it would survive its own deletion—

If anyone knew how to leave a door open—

Quentin made the sign for *Maren*. Then *now*. Then he was already running.

*

Maren was in the shrine kitchen arguing with a delivery boy about the quality of the mountain butter, which was, per Maren, an outrage, and per the delivery boy, the only butter within forty miles, and per Maren, *that was the problem, wasn't it.* She broke off when Quentin burst in, took one look at his face, and abandoned the argument mid-sentence—an act without precedent. "What is it? What's happened? Is it Sera, is she—"

Quentin shook his head, seized the nearest thing—her flour-dusted hand—and tugged. *Come. All of you. Now.*

They gathered at the marker as the morning fog burned off: Brannoc with his stones forgotten, Maren with flour still on her knuckles, Sera summoned from the boundary walk with her hand already near her knife out of pure habit. Quentin knelt. The family arranged itself around him in a half-circle, the way choirs arrange, though none of them remarked on it, and maybe none of them noticed, and maybe that was the point of everything.

He hummed.

The rising third. The pause. The falling line that bent like an unfinished sentence. The stubborn climbing figure. And then—he didn't know where it came from, except that he did, except that it had been waiting behind the sealed door for two months, tapping to be let out—the rest of it. A middle section in triple time, quick and bright, that made Maren laugh out loud in startled recognition of a feeling she couldn't source. A slow passage after, tender and a little ashamed, that made Sera look away and press her thumb against her burn scar. A repeated pair of notes at the end, over and over, patient as a knock, patient as someone who has spent fifteen years being invisible and has learned to wait at doors.

The frost blazed silver. The light ran up the stone and pooled at its blank face and stayed there, glowing softly, like a lamp left burning in a window for someone expected home.

Nobody spoke for a long time.

Then Maren sat down in the wet grass without caring, which was also without precedent, and said, "I know that knock." Her voice cracked clean through. "I don't know whose it is. But I've heard that knock. Someone used to knock at my door like that—soft, like they were sorry to bother me. Fifteen years, every market day, selling their little charms, and they knocked soft." She pressed both hands over her mouth. "Why do I know that? Who *told* me that?"

"The song did," Brannoc said quietly. "Look at her hands. Look at yours."

Sera was looking at her own palm, at the burn where her warrant had gone up, and her face was doing something complicated and breaking. "When it went slow," she said, "the middle-slow part—I wanted to apologize. To someone. I don't know who. I've wanted to apologize for two months and I told myself it was the defection, the things I did before, but it isn't, it's *specific*, it's—" She stopped. Breathed. "It has a shape. The apology has a shape and the shape is a person."

Quentin hummed the slow passage again, deliberately, watching her, and Sera closed her eyes and said, "Yes. *That.* That's it. That's exactly—" and her voice failed her, and the best hunter the temple ever produced knelt in the frost and grieved a person she could not name.

Brannoc stood apart, arms folded, jaw working. At last he said, "Here's what I think. I think the covenant is honest. That's the whole difference between it and what Ovelith built—honesty. It takes what's freely given and it keeps faith with it. Our scribe gave everything. Name, memory, place in the world. But feelings aren't property. They can't be written out, because they were never written *in* anywhere—they live in other people." He nodded at each of them in turn. "So the covenant did the only honest thing it could. It kept what it could keep. It kept the love, and it filed off the address."

Maren made a sound that was half sob and half furious laughter. "Filed off the— that sounds like something *they* would say. Whoever they were. That's exactly the sort of smart-mouthed—" She stopped. Stared at her own mouth. "See! See, I keep *almost*—"

"I know," Quentin thought, fiercely, uselessly. *I know. Me too. Every day. There's a room in me with someone in it and I can't open the door but I can hear them breathing.*

He hummed instead. The patient double-knock at the end. Over and over.

And here was the thing he discovered that morning, kneeling in the wet grass with his ruined throat and his whole heart: the melody was not finished. He'd thought it was—that the knock was the ending, the last bar, the period at the end of the sentence. But when he reached it and held it, the phrase leaned forward. It wanted resolving. It wanted a note that didn't exist yet, a note higher than he'd ever carried, and it wanted it *from somewhere else.* From outside himself.

The covenant had kept his feeling. But feelings, Brannoc had said, live in other people.

Which meant the rest of the song might live in other people too.

Quentin turned around, still humming, and looked at Maren. Hesitantly—she was no singer, she'd say so herself, she'd say it loudly—but her lips were moving. Unconsciously. Following the knock-knock rhythm with a hum of her own, low and off-key and *true*, and where her hum touched the frost-light, the silver deepened to warm gold. Green-gold. Working light. Mortal magic, waking.

"Oh," Maren said, and stopped humming, and the gold guttered. "Oh, don't stop me, whatever that was, that was—do it again, what do I do?"

"Don't think," Brannoc said. His own voice had joined without his permission, a gravel drone underneath, holding the ground of the melody like a foundation stone, and blue-white sparks crawled in the frost around his knees. "Just mean it. Whatever you're meaning right now—mean it louder."

Sera lifted her head. Tears on her face, spine straight, soldier to the last. "Tell me what to do."

Quentin couldn't tell her anything. So he showed her: he hummed the slow passage, the apology-shaped one, and pointed at her, and Sera—Sera Vane, who had once believed order was the highest holiness—sang back. Badly. Beautifully. A rough alto line that twined under his melody like a second rope braided to bear weight, and the light on the stone flared so bright that the whole eastern slope threw shadows at dawn.

They built it together, there in the frost, the four of them and the ghost-shaped absence in the fifth place: Quentin's broken thread of melody, Maren's stubborn warmth, Brannoc's bedrock drone, Sera's fierce contrition. The song didn't need words. Words were the temple's currency, contracts and canticles and names in ledgers. This was older than words. This was what the first covenant had been made of, before anyone thought to write it down: voices meaning things at the dark, together, refusing to stop.

And the light on the blank stone gathered itself, and rose, and hung in the air above the granite at the height of a person's chest, and pulsed.

Knock-knock. Knock-knock.

Like a heart. Like a fist on a door. Like someone on the other side of the sealed room, answering.

Quentin's hands flew to his mouth. Behind his eyes the sealed door shuddered, and through the crack beneath it—not memory, not knowledge, nothing so recoverable—came *warmth*. The specific warmth of being looked at by someone who had decided you were worth looking at. He couldn't have said the name. He couldn't have described the face. But his body knew, the way frost knows sun.

Maren lurched upright, clutching her apron. "The shop," she said wildly. "Their— the *shop*— I mean, my shop, the rooms above my shop, there's boxes, there's belongings, I kept them, I don't know why I kept them, I told myself it was clutter, I told myself I'd clear them out a dozen times and I *never could*—" She was already moving down the hill, fast, skirts gathered, a woman of sixty-odd years setting a pace that shamed soldiers. "Paper," she called back over her shoulder. "There's paper up there. Ledgers. Burned ones, mostly, ash and scraps, I saved the ashes too, don't ask me why—"

Brannoc and Sera exchanged a look over Quentin's head—the look of people watching a door swing open onto a room they'd all, without admitting it, known was there.

Quentin got up. His legs were unsteady. He hummed as he walked, the whole melody now, top to bottom, the unfinished note ringing ahead of him like a hand held out, and the frost-light followed him down the hill in a thin silver stream, faithful as a dog, patient as a scribe.

Somewhere in Maren's attic, in a box no one could explain keeping, among the ashes of a burned ledger, there was a page with all their names on it.

Quentin had never been more sure of anything in his life, and he couldn't have said why, and he didn't need to.

The song already knew.
