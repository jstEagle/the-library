## 36. Calling Someone Back

The fragment of the ledger lay on Maren's kitchen table like a live coal.

No one wanted to be the first to touch it again. They had all read it once—had wept over it, laughed over it, argued about it in low voices while the fire burned down—and now it sat there in the lamplight, Freya's handwriting marching across the page in that cramped, impatient script that slanted left as if the words were always trying to escape the margin.

*If you are reading this, then I am gone, and you have forgotten me, and you found this anyway. That means you loved me before you knew why. Good. Hold on to that.*

Brannoc broke the silence first, because Brannoc always did when silence got too heavy for the rest of them.

"So," he said, turning his cup of tea in his enormous scarred hands. "We've read it. We remember her. Now what? We just—we go on? We light a candle on her name-day and call it grief?"

"No." Quentin's voice was a ruin. It had been a ruin since the hearth chamber, since he had sung himself hollow note by note and given every last one of them away. What remained was a rasp, a whisper with edges, a voice like wind through a broken shutter. He had to stop between sentences sometimes, and no one ever rushed him. "The covenant didn't erase her. It *banked* her. Sera's heard the ward-scholars say it—the erasure page doesn't destroy, it holds. A name written out can be written back."

"By whom?" Maren asked sharply. She was knitting again, the way she knitted when she was frightened—needles clicking fast and furious, a grey scarf already long enough to strangle a horse. "By us? We're not scribes. We're not priests. Half of us aren't even lawful residents anywhere."

"You don't need to be a scribe," Sera said quietly.

Everyone turned to her. She sat apart from the table, as she still often did, near the door out of old habit—a hunter's habit, keeping sightlines clear—and her former mentor's dueling scars caught the lamplight along her jaw. Since the trial of Ovelith, since she had stood before gods and mortals alike and given testimony that had made even the diminished god in the hearth-shard lean close to listen, something in her had loosened. But only something. The rest of her still sat like a drawn bow.

"I spent eleven years studying covenant law so I could hunt the people who broke it," she said. "There's a rite. Old. Older than the temple—it predates the Veiled Word by centuries, which is exactly why they buried it. It's called the Counter-Writing. When a covenant takes something freely given, the covenant can give it back—but only if others choose to receive the debt. Not pay it. *Receive* it. Carry it willingly."

"Meaning what, precisely?" Brannoc asked. "In words a drunkard can follow?"

Sera almost smiled. Almost. "Meaning: Freya wrote their name out of existence to power the covenant. To write it back, each of us has to take on a piece of what that cost. And the rite doesn't accept payment in coin or blood or pretty speeches. It requires every participant to give something real. Something that's actually theirs. Something they'll feel the loss of."

The needles stopped clicking.

Maren looked down at the half-finished scarf as if seeing it for the first time. "Define 'real,'" she said, in the voice she used when a customer tried to haggle over eggs.

"That's the point," Sera said. "It defines itself. The rite knows. You offer, and either the offering is true or nothing happens at all. There's no faking it. There's no partial credit." She paused, and her voice dropped. "And there's a risk. If someone offers falsely—if anyone at the circle offers falsely—the whole rite fails, and the fragment burns, and whatever chance we have is gone forever. One lie among five people kills it."

Quentin closed his eyes.

* * *

They took three days to decide, and everyone pretended those three days were about logistics.

Brannoc went down to the river under Hollow Bridge and stood in the mud where he'd lived for nine years, staring up at the ward-stones he'd helped build and then helped fail, and came back smelling of wet stone and saying nothing to anyone.

Sera walked to the temple—not inside, never inside anymore—and stood in the square where she'd been inducted at seven years old, reciting the oaths she no longer believed, and came back with frost in her hair and her knuckles white around a warrant-stub she'd kept for fifteen years as a talisman against doubt.

Maren baked. Four loaves, then six, then a tray of honey-buns nobody touched, because baking was how Maren prayed and everyone in the house knew it and gave her room.

And Quentin sat by the window with a slate on his knees, because he couldn't speak for long and writing hurt less, and he wrote the same thing over and over until the slate was white with chalk dust:

*She stayed for me. She burned her ledger for me. Whatever the rite asks, I have the least left to give and I will give all of it.*

On the third evening, Maren set the table the way she set it for festival dinners—the good cloth, the candles, the chipped blue bowls that had survived two riots and a flood—and said, "Right. Enough moping. If we're going to do magic older than the temple in my kitchen, we're doing it fed."

* * *

Sera drew the circle herself.

Not in chalk—in salt and ash mixed with a thread pulled from the hem of Freya's coat, which Maren had kept hanging by the door for months without knowing why, the way you keep a key to a room you've forgotten exists. The line of it glimmered faintly once it closed, a shimmer like heat off summer cobbles, and the kitchen felt suddenly larger, suddenly older, as though the walls remembered being part of something vaster.

"The fragment goes in the center," Sera said. Her voice had shifted into something formal and precise—the inquisitor's cadence, but gentler now, bent to a new purpose. "Each of us speaks our offering aloud, places our hand on the fragment, and lets it go. Not gives it up—*lets it go*. There's a difference. The rite can tell."

"And if it's not enough?" Brannoc rumbled. "If what we've got isn't worth one erased scribe?"

"Then we'll know," Sera said simply. "And we'll live with it. That's what the rest of this year has been teaching us, hasn't it? Living with what can't be fixed." She looked around the circle—at the exiled smith, the landlady, the voiceless boy—and something raw moved behind her composure. "I'll go first. Someone has to show it can be survived."

She knelt at the circle's edge and pressed her palm flat above the fragment without touching it.

"I give my certainty," she said. "Fifteen years of it. Every day I woke up knowing exactly who I was, what I served, and that I was right. I don't want it back. I don't need it back. But it was mine, and it was real, and I give it—" her breath caught, steadied— "*willingly*. Let it count."

The candle flames leaned toward her, all at once, like grass under wind. Then righted.

Sera sat back, shaking slightly. "That's—that's it. It took it. Gods, it feels like a tooth coming out. You don't know how much space it filled until it's gone."

"My turn, then." Brannoc rolled his shoulders like a man preparing to lift a beam alone. He knelt heavily, the floorboards complaining, and laid both hands over the circle's edge. "I give the bridge." His voice cracked on the second word. He pushed through. "Nine years I lived under Hollow Bridge drinking myself blind because guilt was easier than work. And somewhere in those nine years that bridge became the only place in the world I understood. I give it. The sound of the water under it, the stones, the rats, the whole miserable sanctuary of it. I don't live there anymore. I don't need hiding places. Give it to her."

The flames leaned. Righted. Brannoc made a sound halfway between a laugh and a sob and wiped his face with a forearm the size of a ham.

"Maren," Sera said softly.

"I know, I know, I'm getting there." But Maren didn't move yet. She looked around her kitchen—at the good cloth, the chipped bowls, the scarf abandoned mid-row—and her chin trembled once before she mastered it. "Forty years I've run that shop. Forty years of counting everything. Counting stock, counting coins, counting who owed me and what they owed and when. Counting was safe. Counting meant nobody could get close enough to cost me anything I couldn't put a number on." She knelt, slower than the others, joints cracking, and pressed her hand down. "I give the counting. All of it. The ledgers in my head, the tallies, the arithmetic of keeping myself safe. I'd rather be uncounted and have her back than balanced and alone. Take it. Take all of it."

The flames bowed so deep this time that wax ran. The salt-and-ash circle flared briefly gold.

Maren sat back and stared at her own hands as if they belonged to a stranger. "Oh," she said faintly. "Oh, that's what that feels like. No wonder she was always so infuriatingly free."

* * *

Which left Quentin.

He had been waiting at the edge of the circle, slate in his lap, and now he set the slate aside and rose on unsteady legs. Without his voice he moved differently—careful, listening, as though he were navigating a world pitched too quiet. He knelt at the circle and looked down at the fragment of the ledger, at Freya's leftward-slanting handwriting, and his shoulders began to shake.

Sera reached toward him. He shook his head.

He couldn't do this aloud. So he did what he had learned to do in the weeks since the hearth: he sang it, silently, mouthing the shapes of it, letting his hands conduct a melody no ear could catch. And the circle *heard* him anyway. That was the terrible, beautiful truth of what remained of Quentin's gift—the music hadn't died with the voice; it had gone somewhere deeper, somewhere the covenant itself could witness.

His hands shaped the offering:

*I give the song.*

The candles guttered. The shadows lurched.

*I gave my voice to relight the hearth, and I would give it again, a thousand times. That gift is spent; it isn't mine to offer twice. But the song—the wanting to sing, the needing to, the place inside me where music lives whether any sound comes out or not—I give that. I give the thing that made me a choirboy before the temple ever claimed me. Let the covenant have the well, not just the water. Because she heard me sing when I was nobody, at a night-market stall, and she didn't look away. Nobody else ever did that. Nobody else ever—*

His hands faltered. His mouth formed the last of it barely moving, barely breathing:

*—nobody else ever stayed.*

The flames stood perfectly upright, perfectly still, burning without flicker, and the whole kitchen held its breath. Then every candle flame turned white—white as starlight, white as the moment the world had gone blank when Freya wrote their name away—and the fragment of the ledger lifted off the table, weightless, turning slowly, and ink began to rise off the page in fine dark threads like smoke reading itself backward.

"It's working," Sera whispered. "Gods above and below, it's *working*—"

The threads of ink gathered in the air above the circle, weaving, spiraling, forming letters that hung unfinished in the lamplight: F... R... E...

And then the circle flared crimson, and the letters tore apart.

* * *

"No!" Maren was on her feet. "What happened, what did we do wrong—"

"Nothing." Sera's face had gone grey. "Nobody lied. I'd know. The rite would have refused us clean, not like—like *this*. Look."

She pointed at the fragment. It had dropped back to the table, and across its surface new writing was appearing—not Freya's hand, older, deeper, scored into the paper as if by a stylus of iron. The hearth-shard, sitting in its dish on the mantel where it had rested since the trial, flared in answer, and through the shard a voice came—thin, diminished, tired beyond centuries, but unmistakably amused.

**Clever children,** the god said. **You have done the rite correctly. Your offerings are received and held. Do not mistake the flare for refusal.**

"Then what—" Brannoc started.

**The erasure page does not answer to the living alone.** The voice ground on, like a millstone turning. **A name written out must be written back by all who bore its weight. You four carried her in memory. But there is a fifth who carries her somewhere deeper than memory—who held the shape of her when the whole world forgot. The covenant banked her name against his song. The final hand must be his.**

Every head turned to Quentin.

He stood very still in the center of the dying circle, and in the white afterlight his face was unreadable—except that tears were running down it freely, and he wasn't wiping them away.

**One warning, choirboy,** the god said, softer now. **You have nothing left to give. Your voice is spent, your song is given, your grief is the only coin remaining in you. If you offer it—if you offer the grief itself—you may buy her back whole. Or you may find that grief was the last thing holding *you* together. The rite does not promise you will remain standing on the other side of it.**

Quentin looked at the fragment. At the half-formed letters still ghosting in the air—F, R, E, patient as stars waiting to be relit.

He thought of a night market stall. Of charms that glowed too honestly. Of someone who stayed.

He stepped forward and knelt at the center of the circle, and opened his hands over the fragment, and began—to the horror and awe of everyone watching—to hum.

It was not a voice. It was barely a sound. It was the ghost of a melody with the melody removed, a vibration more felt than heard, and yet the candles bent toward it, the salt-circle blazed, the ink-threads rose trembling into the air—and inside that ruined, wordless hum, Quentin offered up the one thing he had left: the hollow, howling, unbearable shape of missing her, every day of it, every hour, given *willingly*, given whole—

The world went white for the second time that year.

And in the whiteness, from very far away—or very near—from somewhere beneath the floorboards of reality itself, came a sound none of them had heard in months and all of them would have known anywhere:

Someone knocking. As if from inside.
