## 37. Freya, Remembered

The first thing I knew was warmth.

Not the warmth of a hearth exactly—though there was a hearth somewhere near, I could feel it ticking and breathing the way old stone does when fire has lived in it long enough—but a warmth *in* me, as if someone had banked coals behind my ribs and left them to glow. It was a strange sensation for me. Fifteen years in Corvane teaches you to run cold. Cold is safe. Cold doesn't flare, doesn't spark, doesn't get you noticed by men with grey hoods and ledgers.

I opened my eyes.

I was lying on stone before the First Hearth, and the hearth was lit.

Not burning—*lit*, the way dawn lights a valley, the way a struck bell keeps ringing after the sound has technically ended. The flame had no color I could name. It was every color at once, held very still, like a choir holding its final note forever. And rising off it, thin as woodsmoke but brighter than smoke could ever be, went threads of light—up through the broken roof, up past the mountain's shoulder, out into—

I sat up too fast and the world tilted.

"Careful," said Maren's voice, rough as sacking and twice as comforting. "Slowly, love. You've been dead."

"I have not," I said, and my voice came out wrong.

That was the second thing I knew: my voice had changed. It sounded the way starlight looks—there's no better way to say it. Each word carried a faint ring beneath it, a harmonic that hadn't been there before, as if I were speaking through a glass of clear water. I pressed a hand to my throat, half expecting to find it glowing.

It wasn't. But my hands were.

Not blazing, not alarming—just touched. My fingertips shimmered faintly when they moved, a dusting of white-gold light that clung to the skin like flour to a baker's hands. When I flexed my fingers, motes drifted off them and hung in the air, refusing to fall.

"Oh," I said. And then, because I am who I am even at the end of the world: "This is going to be hell for forgery work. No one will believe my handwriting isn't enchanted."

Maren made a sound that was half laugh, half sob, and then her arms were around me, and she smelled of woodsmoke and lamp oil and the particular stubbornness of a woman who had crossed a mountain range in winter because her people needed feeding. She held me hard enough to crack ribs, or would have, if I'd still had ordinary ones.

"You absolute fool," she whispered into my hair. "You absolute, self-sacrificing, ledger-burning fool."

"Maren." I pulled back enough to look at her face. It was lined deeper than I remembered—or perhaps I simply remembered it from before, from the night she hid me behind her flour sacks while inquisitors knocked, and grief and joy had both been carving at her since. "How long?"

"Three days." Her voice wobbled on the number. "Three days we sat here watching you not breathe, and Brannoc kept saying ward-smiths know death when they see it, and Sera kept saying nothing at all, which frightened me worse, and Quentin—" She stopped. Her jaw worked. "Quentin hasn't spoken since."

The cold that went through me then had nothing to do with the mountain.

"The song," I said. "He gave his voice. Note by note, the whole covenant-song—he poured it all into the hearth, I *watched* him—"

"And it worked." Maren gripped my shoulders. "Look around you, girl. Boy. Whatever you are this week. Look."

I looked.

Through the shattered crown of the First Hearth's chamber, where the roof had fallen away centuries ago, the night sky stood open—and it was *full*. Stars beyond counting, stars in rivers and clusters, stars so dense along the horizon they made a silver tide. The wards' light ran along the mountain's flanks like veins of blue fire, whole and humming, and far below, past the snowfields, the distant smudge of Corvane glimmered under a sky that remembered how to be a sky.

The realms weren't dark anymore. The gods weren't muffled anymore. Quentin had sung the covenant back into being with his own voice, and I had written my name out of existence to seal it, and both of those things had happened, and one of us had come back and one of us hadn't, not entirely.

"Where is he?" I asked.

Maren's expression did something complicated. "By the hearth. He hasn't left it since you—since the light took you. He sleeps sitting up. He eats what we put in his hand. He hums." Her eyes glistened. "Freya, he *hums*, all day, the same melody over and over, and none of us know what it means, and he can't tell us, and it's breaking my heart in a way I didn't have a shape for until now."

I got to my feet. My legs held, mostly. The starlight in my skin brightened as I moved, trailing faint ribbons, and I thought absurdly of the charm-stall in the night market, of harmless little trinkets that flickered just enough to seem like tricks. I had spent fifteen years making magic small enough to hide. Now I couldn't hide it if I tried. It came off me like heat off a forge.

Some part of me—the part that had learned invisibility as a survival art, the part that had once told Quentin that invisible people can't be missed—flinched at that.

A larger part of me, the part that had burned an indenture ledger and meant it, walked toward the hearth.

* * *

He was smaller than I remembered.

That made no sense—he hadn't shrunk; three days don't shrink a person—but he sat curled against the hearthstone with his knees drawn up and his hands loose in his lap, and he looked like the choirboy he must have been before any of this, before the High Cantor and the silent hymns and the glass cracking under his voice. His lips moved sometimes. Shaping words with no sound. Practicing, maybe. Or praying. With Quentin it had always been hard to tell the difference.

Brannoc stood a few paces off, arms crossed, watching me approach with the wary tenderness of a man who had spent three days guarding a corpse and didn't quite trust it to stay lively. Sera was there too, at the chamber's edge, still in her grey leathers but with the warrant-burn scar visible on her palm where she'd gripped the paper too tightly when it caught. Neither of them spoke. They let me pass. Later there would be questions—Sera would want a full report of whatever I'd experienced on the other side of erasure, because that was Sera, and Brannoc would want to examine the starlight in my skin with his instruments, because that was Brannoc—but now they only watched, and the watching felt like a kind of held breath.

I knelt beside Quentin.

Up close I could see what the song had cost him. There was a stillness in his throat, a settled quiet, like a room after the instruments have been packed away. His eyes were open and fixed on the hearth-flame, and they had shadows under them deep enough to hold water.

"Quentin," I said.

Nothing. Not even a blink.

"Quentin. It's me. I'm—apparently I'm difficult to get rid of. Ask anyone. Ask the Temple of the Veiled Word, they spent fifteen years trying to collect a debt from me and never managed it. Ask the void itself. I wrote my name out of existence and existence apparently filed a complaint."

His lips twitched. Almost. A ghost of a ghost of a smile, there and gone, and his gaze slid from the flame to the middle distance—to me, or near me, or through me. I couldn't tell which, and the not-knowing scraped at something raw in my chest.

Because here was the thing I had understood the moment Maren said his name: I had known, going in, what erasure meant. I had written the terms myself, in my own blood-ink, on the erasure page. The name goes; the person goes; the memory goes. Everyone who ever knew Freya Voss would wake with a soft gap where I had been, a missing stair in the dark they'd learn to step over without thinking. I had accepted that. I had *chosen* it, freely, which was the whole point—the god had been clear about that, and so had I.

But Quentin had given his voice knowing he'd remember me. That had been the bargain's cruel symmetry: he keeps the memory and loses the voice; I keep the self and lose the name. We had each assumed the other would carry the heavier half.

And instead the covenant had done something neither of us planned. It had taken my name and my memory from the world—but it had left *him* a melody. A shapeless grief he couldn't name, humming in his chest where his voice used to live. Maren said he hummed the same tune over and over.

I knew that tune. I had heard him practicing it in a ruined shrine weeks ago, when something flickered where a star should be. It was the first song the hearth had ever answered.

He was singing to me without knowing why. For three days.

"Quentin," I said again, softer. I reached out and took his hand.

My fingers, starlit as they were, passed a faint shimmer into his skin where they touched—a thread of white-gold light that sank in and stayed, glowing gently at the wrist like a pulse made visible. He looked down at it. Slowly. As if the movement cost him.

Then he looked up. At me. Actually at me, this time, his eyes finding mine and holding, and I watched something move behind them—not recognition, not yet, but its shadow. Its echo. The feeling that survives the fact.

His mouth opened. Worked. No sound.

"It's all right," I whispered, and my changed voice rang softly in the cold air, and I saw him hear it—I saw him *notice* the strangeness of it, the starlight harmonic, and I saw his breath catch, because he had heard that sound before. Somewhere. In a dream, maybe. In the white space between one world and the next. "You don't have to say anything. You gave enough. More than enough. You sang the sky back on, you impossible, brave, ridiculous boy. The rest of us can do the talking for a while. Maren certainly will. Brannoc has diagrams. Sera has opinions. It'll be terrible. You'll love it."

A tear slipped down his cheek. He didn't wipe it away. His hand turned in mine, slowly, until our palms pressed together, and the light between us brightened—not flaring, just deepening, the way embers deepen when breathed on—and I felt it travel up my arm and across the space between us, some current the new covenant had built into the world's foundations, a circuit completing itself.

His lips parted again.

And this time, sound came.

It wasn't a word at first. It was a note—one single, cracked, unpracticed, ruined-and-rebuilt note, dragged up from wherever the covenant had tucked the last ember of his voice, and it was the most beautiful thing I have ever heard, because it was broken and offered anyway, which is the entire secret of every true song.

Then, around the note, shaping it, giving it edges:

"*Freya.*"

Just my name. Two syllables, hoarse as a rusted hinge, wavering, imperfect, *his*.

The hearth flared. Every thread of light rising from it bent toward us for a heartbeat, like grass leaning toward wind, and high above, through the broken roof, I swear one star pulsed—once, deliberately, the way a friend squeezes your hand across a crowded room.

Quentin stared at me. His whole body was shaking. "*Freya,*" he said again, louder, wrecked, laughing and crying at once, the word tumbling out of him like water through a broken dam, "*Freya, Freya—you're—you're here, you're—*" And then the words failed him, because of course they did; he had spent his voice buying the dawn and had only scraps left, and he stopped trying to spend them and simply fell forward into me, arms locking around my shoulders, forehead against my neck, shaking, humming that same melody against my collarbone—except now it wasn't shapeless anymore. Now it had a name to land on.

I held him. I held him the way you hold the thing you wrote yourself out of existence to protect, which is to say with both arms and everything else you have.

"I'm here," I said into his hair, and my starlight voice wrapped around us both. "I'm here. I'm remembered. Someone kept a page of me, somehow—we'll figure out how later, Maren's already crying about a ledger fragment, don't ask—and I'm here, and I'm not going anywhere. Not because I can't. Because I choose to. That's the difference, Quentin. That was always the difference."

He pulled back just far enough to look at me, tears cutting tracks through the hearth-dust on his face, and he signed—clumsy, urgent, the gestures he'd been using for three days finally finding their target—*you? you're whole?*

I considered the question honestly. Considered the shimmer in my skin, the ring in my voice, the sense—faint but unmistakable—that somewhere very far away, something vast and diminished and grateful had set a small piece of itself inside me as change, as receipt, as *thanks*.

"Mostly," I said. "I appear to be approximately four percent star now. I'm choosing to consider it an improvement."

He laughed. It came out as a breath and a gesture and a crumpling of his whole face, and it was the best laugh I'd ever witnessed, because it was the first.

Behind us, Maren was sobbing openly into her apron. Brannoc had turned away to inspect the ward-veins on the far wall with tremendous intensity, his shoulders betraying him completely. Sera stood with her arms folded and her eyes wet and her expression daring anyone to comment.

* * *

Later—much later, after Maren had force-fed me an entire pot of barley stew and Brannoc had indeed produced diagrams and Sera had indeed produced opinions—we climbed to the lip of the broken roof to watch the sky do what it had been doing for three days without witnesses who mattered: being full.

Quentin sat beside me on a fallen rafter, wrapped in Maren's spare cloak, close enough that our shoulders touched. He couldn't speak more than a few words at a time; each one cost him visibly, like a man counting out coins he couldn't replace. So we had developed a language of gestures and glances and the occasional written word in the frost, and I found—with a surprise that shouldn't have been a surprise—that I understood him better this way than I had ever understood anyone through speech. Some people, it turns out, are loudest when they're quietest.

He tapped my arm. Pointed up.

I followed his finger. In the eastern sky, low over the mountains, a constellation burned that hadn't burned in fifteen years—or longer, according to Brannoc, who claimed the old charts showed it missing since before the border wards were raised. A small cluster of seven stars, arranged in a shape that was almost a circle. Almost closed. One point of it dimmer than the rest, flickering softly, as if it were still deciding whether it belonged.

"That's new," I said.

Quentin nodded. Then he pointed—at the dim star, then at me, then at his own chest, then at the space between us, drawing lines in the air with a finger that shook slightly from cold or emotion or both.

I looked at the incomplete constellation for a long time.

"No," I said finally, and my voice rang its strange silver note out over the snow. "No, I refuse. If the cosmos thinks it gets to make me a metaphor, it can at least wait until I've had breakfast."

Quentin grinned—his whole face doing it, unguarded, the grin of a boy who had once healed a dying bird in a temple garden and been punished for it—and wrote in the frost between us, in careful letters:

*the gods say thank you. i asked.*

I stared at him. "You *asked*? When? You haven't spoken since—"

He shrugged, and wrote: *they hear humming.*

Of course they did. Of course the muffled, starving, newly-woken gods of two realms could hear a voiceless boy humming in a mountain shrine, because that had been the truth at the bottom of everything from the start: the covenant was never really about power, or wards, or the Cantor's stolen light. It was about listening. About someone singing and someone answering, across any distance, through any silence.

Below us, the hearth-light rose through the broken roof in slow bright threads, joining the stars, and far away I could feel them—faint, plural, present—the way you feel a city sleeping around you. Not whole. Not yet. Maybe not for generations. But *there*, and *listening*, and bound to us now by a bargain written in a dead boy's voice and a living scribe's erased name and a landlady's vegetable cart and a drunkard's rebuilt stones and a traitor's burned warrant and a page torn from a ledger that should have burned with the rest.

We sat there until the cold drove us down, shoulder to shoulder, watching the almost-circle flicker.

And then, from the valley road below—distant but unmistakable, carried up the mountain on the clean night air—came the sound of many voices singing.

Not a hymn. Not the Cantor's silent ritual, not the scraped-and-sanitized verses of the Temple of the Veiled Word. Something older, looser, rougher—a work song, maybe, or a walking song, sung by a crowd on the move, dozens of voices at least, climbing the switchback trail toward the shrine with lanterns swinging.

Quentin stiffened. I stood. Down the mountainside, the lantern-line wound upward like a string of fallen stars coming home, and the song grew clearer with every verse, and I caught the words now, and my breath stopped.

They were singing the covenant-melody. Quentin's melody. The one he'd hummed for three days, the one the hearth had answered—they knew it. Dozens of strangers knew it. Which meant it had traveled, which meant someone had carried it down the mountain, which meant—

Maren appeared at the roof's edge below us, lantern in hand, squinting up the road. "Well," she called, in the tone of a woman whose shop had survived a magical apocalypse and who refused to be surprised by anything ever again, "either we've got pilgrims, or the whole country heard what happened up here. Brannoc! Put the kettle on. Looks like the First Hearth's about to get visitors."

Quentin's hand found mine in the dark. His skin shimmered faintly now too—just a trace, just enough—where my light had sunk into it and stayed.

Together, we turned to watch the lanterns climb.
