## 39. A City Learns to Sing

### I. Sera

The proclamation went up on the Temple doors at dawn, and by noon it had been torn down twice—not in anger, but in souvenir.

Sera Vane stood in the square with her hands folded behind her back and watched a grubby apprentice peel the second copy off the boards with the reverence of someone stealing relics. *By decree of the Provisional Council of Corvane, in concord with the Reforged Covenant: the Edict of Silence is repealed. The practice of magic is lawful within the city bounds, subject to registration at the Hall of Ledgers.* Below that, in smaller script, the line that had made three councilmen resign rather than sign their names beneath it: *All debts owed to the former Temple administration are hereby dissolved.*

The apprentice caught her watching and froze, paper half-crumpled in his fist.

"Go on," Sera said. "Take it home. Show your mother."

He fled. She almost smiled.

Six months ago she would have arrested him for defacing temple property. Six months ago there had been no Provisional Council, no covenant, no morning light coming down through air that felt—she still hadn't found the word for it—*thinner*, somehow, but cleaner, like a room after years of smoke. The stars were back. Not all of them, and not as bright as the old star-charts promised; the gods had returned diminished, honest about their limits for perhaps the first time in recorded history. The night sky over Corvane now held maybe two-thirds of its old constellation, with gaps where stars had been spent entirely and would not be relit for generations, if ever. Children were already inventing new stories for the empty places. That, Sera thought, was probably healthier than what had come before.

Her own status was more complicated than a proclamation could fix. The warrant for her arrest had been drafted four times and quietly shelved four times; the Council needed people who knew how the old Temple's machinery worked, and she knew it down to its rusted bolts. So instead of a cell she'd been given an office, a clerk, and the worst job in the new city: auditing the indenture ledgers. Fifteen years of them. Thousands of names, thousands of small debts compounded into lifetimes. She sat in a cold room every day reading the arithmetic of other people's captivity, and every day she found one more entry that made her want to burn the whole archive, and every day she didn't, because somewhere in those pages might be a name like Freya Voss's—a name someone would come looking for, someday, wanting proof of what they'd survived.

She hadn't told anyone that last part. Especially not Freya.

The bells rang noon from the old chapel tower—the same bells, retuned, because the crack Quentin's voice had put through the great bell had turned out to give it a sweeter tone, and the bellfounders had refused to repair it. Corvane kept its scars when they improved the sound. It was becoming something of a civic philosophy.

Sera buttoned her coat against the river wind and walked toward the Night Market, where a stall that sold charms now did so openly, behind a hand-painted sign, with a queue.

### II. Brannoc

Brannoc had never taught anyone anything in his life except, briefly and badly, how to lose at dice. Now he stood under Hollow Bridge—which wasn't hollow anymore, which had been swept out and lanterned and fitted with six workbenches and a slate board—and faced eleven students who ranged in age from a fourteen-year-old pickpocket to a grandmother who had been a hedge-witch before the Edict and had spent forty years pretending otherwise.

"Again," he said. "Marta, your lattice is crooked."

"It's *rustic*," said Marta the grandmother.

"It's going to fail in a high wind and drop a ward-stone on somebody's head. Rustic is fine. Crooked isn't." He limped along the row of practice frames—his leg still ached where the arrow had gone through at Ashford Crossing, and would ache forever, the healers said, which he'd decided was fair payment—and adjusted the girl's stone with two fingers. The ward-lattice hummed, steadied, settled into place like a dog lying down. "There. Feel that? When it sits right, you don't have to hold it. That's the whole secret. A ward isn't strength. It's agreement."

"Agreement with what?" asked the pickpocket, whose name was Pip and who asked this question every single session.

"With everything else." Brannoc tapped the bridge pillar above them, the ancient granite that his own hands had helped key into the border network twenty-five years ago, back when he'd believed the men who paid him. "Stone agrees with stone. River agrees with bank. You're not building a wall, boy. You're brokering a marriage. And like any marriage—" he thought of Maren, who had proposed to him in March with all the romance of a woman ordering ale, and whom he was marrying in June, and whom he loved with a ferocity that embarrassed them both— "it works only if both sides keep showing up."

Pip looked at him with the flat skepticism of youth. "My da says the wards failed anyway. Whole border, gone in one night."

"Your da's right." Brannoc didn't flinch from it; he never let himself flinch from it. "They failed because they were built to serve men who lied about what they were for. So we build different ones now. Slower. Smaller. Ones we can explain to the people living inside them." He picked up a chalk stub and wrote on the slate in his blunt smith's hand: *A WARD SHOULD BE ABLE TO TELL YOU WHAT IT DOES.* "First lesson of the new craft. If you can't explain your magic to the person next to you, you've already started building the next Temple."

The grandmother snorted approvingly. Pip wrote it down, which was more than Brannoc had managed at fourteen.

After the session, he stayed under the bridge alone, as he always did, and stood a while at the far end where the light didn't reach, where he'd lived for seven years drinking himself numb beside a brazier. The brazier was still there. He'd never moved it. Some nights he came down and sat beside it with tea instead of whiskey and let the guilt visit, because he'd learned—from a scribe with tired eyes and a stubborn jaw—that guilt you refuse becomes rot, and guilt you face becomes fuel.

Tonight he only touched the cold iron rim, said nothing to anyone, and climbed the steps back up into the daylight, where his students had left their practice stones arranged in a lopsided spiral on the flagstones, and someone—Pip, almost certainly—had chalked a crude drawing of Brannoc's scowling face with a halo over it.

He left the halo there. It would rain eventually. Let the city see it while it lasted.

### III. Maren

Maren's shop had never been so busy, and Maren had never been so tired, and Maren had never once considered being anything else.

"The turnips are *there*," she called without looking up from the ledger, as a customer drifted hopefully toward the cabbages. "And if you're buying turnips to make the soup for the choir school's opening, take the ones on the left, they're sweeter, I grew them myself and I don't say that about much."

The choir school. She still couldn't walk past the old chapel annex without hearing hammers and singing at once—carpenters raising benches, and children's voices stumbling through scales under the direction of a young man who couldn't sing a note himself and who taught better than anyone Maren had ever heard. They'd named it the Voss-Quentin School of Song, over both founders' objections. Freya had argued it should be called nothing at all; Quentin had argued, in writing, since he could no longer argue aloud, that it should be called *the Annex*. The Council had ignored them both, which Maren privately considered the first competent thing the Council had done.

She totaled the turnip sale, took the coin, and paused, as she did most days now, over the ledger page. Business was good—better than good; half the city wanted vegetables blessed or warded or simply grown by the woman who'd hidden a fugitive from the Inquisition, as if courage were a seasoning. But the ledger had another meaning for her these days. It had been her idea, months ago, to keep a second book alongside the accounts: a plain notebook where visitors to the shop could write things down. *What do you remember?* was the question printed on the first page, in Freya's careful hand, before anyone understood why it mattered.

Because memories, they had learned, could be given back. Because a name written out could be written in again, by those who chose to remember it. Because Freya Voss had erased themselves to save two realms, and the family had clawed them back with a burned ledger's surviving page and a rite that had cost each of them something real—Maren's contribution had been the memory of her own mother's face, freely surrendered, and she still sometimes reached for it in the evenings and found the shape of the love without the picture, and decided, again, that it had been worth it.

Freya was back. That was the miracle nobody in Corvane fully understood except the five people at Maren's kitchen table. Freya was back, partly starlight, sleeping without nightmares some nights, laughing more than they used to, free of debt for the first time in fifteen years and visibly unsure what to do with freedom, like a bird released from a cage that keeps returning to perch on the open door.

But the notebook's question remained on its first page, and Maren kept it there deliberately. *What do you remember?* Because Freya remembered everything now—except, in odd corners, small absences. A childhood song they couldn't place. A street they were sure they'd never walked and then dreamed of three nights running. The erasure had been undone, but undoing was not the same as never having happened, and some threads had frayed.

Maren meant to find every loose thread and knot it back in. She was, after all, very good with ledgers.

"Madam?" The turnip customer was still standing there. "You went somewhere."

"I'm always somewhere," Maren said, and gave her the sweet ones anyway.

### IV. Quentin

Quentin had learned to speak with his hands and his eyes and, when those failed, with a slate on a string around his neck, but teaching required none of these. Teaching required only listening, and listening was the thing he had always been best at, even before he'd known it was the thing.

He stood in the center of the choir room—he still thought of it as the annex, and always would—with thirty children arranged in a rough crescent, and he did not conduct them. He *listened* them. When a voice went sharp, he didn't correct it; he sang the note wrong himself, deliberately, extravagantly, until the children laughed and fixed him, and in fixing him they fixed themselves. When a small girl in the back row went silent because her voice cracked mid-phrase, he knelt beside her and pointed to his own throat, then drew on his slate: *Mine broke too. Loudest crack in the history of Corvane. Shattered a window. Ask anyone.*

She giggled. She sang the phrase again. It cracked again, and he applauded, and the whole choir applauded, and by the end of the hour cracking was fashionable.

Afterward, when the children had poured out into the courtyard like spilled music, he stayed behind in the empty room with the afternoon light falling through windows that had been reglazed but kept, in one corner pane, the old crack from the night the stars went out—the night his voice had broken the glass and changed everything. He touched the corner of it, the way he touched it every day. A scar that improved the view. Corvane's civic philosophy again. He suspected Freya had started it.

His voice was not coming back. He had known this from the first week, when the healers had examined his throat and shaken their heads with a gentleness that confirmed it. The covenant-song had taken it note by note, willingly given, and the gods—who were diminished but scrupulous—did not return gifts. What he had instead was stranger: the melody-memory. Sometimes, humming wordlessly at Freya's grave-marker in the weeks before the counter-rite brought them back, he had found that the tune itself carried what the words no longer could—carried *Freya*, carried the campfire and the cart and the argument about trust, carried it so completely that when Maren read the ledger page aloud and the memories flooded home, he had already known every beat of the story. His body had kept what his mind lost.

Now he used it in class. There was a hymn he taught no one else taught—the *Voss Round*, the children called it, though they didn't know why—which he hummed and the children learned by ear, and which had the peculiar property of making even tone-deaf children sound, for three bars, like something the gods might lean down to hear. The Council had asked him to write down the notation. He had declined, on his slate, with a small smile: *Some songs only work if they're passed hand to hand.*

That was the covenant now, really. Passed hand to hand. Mortal voices feeding the gods, gods holding the wards and the stars—but smaller, slower, local. A god of the river here. A hearth-spirit there. No more intermediaries hoarding the light; Ovelith's trial had seen to the principle of it, and the reforged covenant had seen to the practice. Quentin sang vespers on feast days, voiceless, mouthing the words while the congregation sang *for* him, and the gods heard it just fine. They had told him so, once, through the hearth-shard, in a voice like wind through a doorway: *We always heard you best when you weren't performing.*

Footsteps in the corridor—familiar footsteps, unhurried, with the faint chime of charm-charms on a bracelet. Quentin turned, and there in the doorway stood Freya, backlit by the courtyard sun, holding two meat pies from Maren's stall and wearing the expression of someone about to pretend this was a casual errand.

Quentin smiled, and reached for his slate.

### V. Freya

The pies were an excuse. Freya had gotten reasonably good, over fifteen years, at constructing excuses, and had gotten reasonably bad at needing them, and the tension between these two skills was, they suspected, the whole project of the rest of their life.

They handed Quentin a pie and leaned against the doorframe of the annex while he ate, and together they listened to the courtyard, where the choir children were teaching a skipping rhyme to a cluster of market kids, and beyond the wall, where the city went about its loud, repaired, imperfect business. Somewhere a hammer rang. Somewhere a ward-smith's apprentice swore cheerfully. Somewhere the new star-charts were being printed with honest gaps in them, and children were naming the gaps.

"You're staring," Quentin wrote on his slate.

"I'm assessing," Freya said. "You've got flour on your collar. Very dignified, founder of a school."

He wiped it, missed, gave up. Then he turned the slate around: *You came for a reason.*

Freya had come for a reason. They had been carrying it for three days, turning it over the way they used to turn over debt figures, looking for the angle where it hurt least. There wasn't one. There never was.

"Sera sent me a letter," they said. "Official seal and everything. She does that now—sends official letters to my house, knowing full well I'll read them at the kitchen table with Maren making comments." They pulled the folded paper from their coat but didn't open it. "They finished the audit. All fifteen years of ledgers. Every indenture the old Temple held."

Quentin set down the pie. He knew. Of course he knew; he'd been waiting for this letter almost as long as Freya had.

"Every debt dissolved," Freya said. "Every name released. Except—" Their voice did something complicated. "Except mine was never formally in the books. Remember? I burned my ledger. Page by page, the night I rebelled. The one surviving fragment was the letter to all of you, and that's been read, and honored, and it's done its work. But legally—" they laughed, short and disbelieving— "legally, as far as the Council's records are concerned, there exists one indenture in the entire city of Corvane that was never entered, never audited, and never dissolved. Mine. A ghost debt. A blank space where a contract should be. And Sera writes me this beautifully bureaucratic letter asking me to come to the Hall of Ledgers so that a clerk can enter my name into a fresh book and strike it out again, properly, officially, so the record shows what actually happened."

They stopped. The courtyard noise washed over the silence.

"Fifteen years I hid," Freya said. "Fifteen years I made myself small and harmless and invisible, because invisible people can't be missed. And now the entire city wants to write my name down—one last time—in a big official book, just for the pleasure of crossing it out." They looked at Quentin, and their eyes were bright and wet and furious with joy. "I'm going to cry in front of a government clerk. I hope you understand what you've done to my life."

Quentin was laughing silently, shoulders shaking. Then he wiped his slate clean and wrote, slowly, in his careful hand:

*Come to the Hall tomorrow. We'll all be there. Maren's closing the shop. Brannoc's wearing his good coat. Sera will pretend it's routine. And afterward—*

He paused, chalk hovering. Freya waited. Waiting for Quentin to finish a sentence had become, over the past months, one of their favorite activities; he composed the way he used to sing, and it was always worth it.

*Afterward,* he wrote, *there's something at the kitchen table. Everyone's bringing something. It's time we talked about what you'll do with your freedom.*

"My freedom," Freya repeated. The words still fit strangely, like a coat cut for someone braver. They looked out at the courtyard, at the children singing a round they'd learned hand to hand, at the repaired city glittering under two-thirds of its stars, and they thought: *exactly this*—but they didn't say it yet. Some answers deserved to be said at a kitchen table, surrounded by everyone.

"Tomorrow, then," they said, and squeezed Quentin's shoulder, and walked out into the sunlight with an official letter in their pocket and, for the first time in fifteen years, absolutely nowhere they had to hide.
