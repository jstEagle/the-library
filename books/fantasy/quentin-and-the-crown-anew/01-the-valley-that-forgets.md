## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The ink froze first, always the ink.

Cyrus Almeida had learned to warm the little stone basin against his chest before dawn, tucking it beneath his robe while the temple bells still rang the hour of ash, so that when he sat down at his desk in the Hall of Copies the ink would flow instead of clotting like old blood. It was a small trick. Small tricks were the only kind permitted in the Temple of the Ledger, and even they were frowned upon if noticed.

He was very good at not being noticed.

Around him, four hundred scribes bent over their desks in rows long enough to lose sight of both ends, each one a hooded shape hunched over vellum in the grey light that came through windows of horn and lead. No one spoke. Speaking was for the counting-hour, and only then in figures. The sound of the hall was a single sustained whisper—quills on skin, quills on skin, four hundred hearts keeping time—and Cyrus had long ago stopped hearing it, the way a man stops hearing a river he has lived beside all his life.

The ledger before him was the Debts of the Weavers' Guild, third recension, and it was dull work in the way that only other people's suffering can be dull: entry after entry of names, sums, terms, forfeitures. *Marrow Ovelin, weaver, owes 14 crowns 3 bits at interest compounded seasonally; term expired; forfeiture assessed.* The forfeitures were what the temple actually wanted copied, though no one said so aloud. A debt was an argument. A forfeiture was a conclusion. And conclusions, the High Scribe liked to say at the morning reading, were the temple's true product. *We do not lend, brothers. We remember.*

Cyrus dipped his quill and wrote *forfeiture assessed* in a hand so precise it looked printed, which was the point. Precision was anonymity. The masters could not single out a page that matched every other page. In eleven years of indenture he had been praised exactly once—a passing novitiate had remarked on his evenness of line—and Cyrus had spent the following month deliberately smudging his ascenders until the praise dissolved into the general grey of the hall. Being seen, in the Temple of the Ledger, was the first step toward being owed.

Outside, beyond the horn windows, Mount Vessel stood against the sky like a closed fist. Everyone in the valley grew up with it, the way you grow up with weather. The mountain did not enter conversation because there was nothing to say about it. It had no shrine, no road, no name in any hymn. The valley's gods had gone quiet before anyone's grandmother was born—the wards along the valley rim saw to that, great standing stones carved with spirals that kept magic out the way a dam keeps water out—and in a land where prayers went unanswered by design, people learned to pray the way mutes learn to sing: privately, badly, without expectation.

Cyrus had not prayed since he was nine years old.

He copied another line. *Forfeiture assessed.* Somewhere three rows back, Brother Anselm coughed, a wet rattle that had been getting worse since the frost came.

* * *

Anselm sat two desks behind and one row over, and he was the closest thing Cyrus had to a friend, which is to say that over eleven years they had exchanged perhaps four hundred words, all of them practical, and that Anselm once left half a honeyed pear on the corner of Cyrus's desk during the feast of Reckoning without either of them acknowledging it happened. He was sixty, maybe older—no one in the temple counted age, only terms—and his hands shook now when he held a quill, which meant his lines wavered, which meant the masters noticed him, which meant he was being moved down the roster toward the copying of the oldest ledgers, the ones whose ink was known to sicken the men who worked them.

On the fourth day of the frost, Anselm did not come to his desk.

Cyrus told himself it meant nothing. Scribes took to their cells with fevers every winter and some of them came back. But at the midday bell, when the hall emptied for bread, Cyrus found himself walking the wrong direction—not toward the refectory but toward the infirmary stair, his feet making the decision before his mind agreed to it, and this frightened him more than the fever itself. Eleven years of discipline, undone by worry.

The infirmary smelled of camphor and cold stone. Anselm lay in the third cot, gray as unbleached linen, breathing in a way that made Cyrus think of a bellows with a split seam. A novice attendant dozed in the corner. No one else had come. Of course no one else had come. The temple did not visit its sick; it recorded them.

Cyrus stood at the foot of the cot like a man at the edge of a well.

"Brother Anselm," he said, too quietly. Then, because the room was already forgetting him, louder: "Brother."

One eye opened. It took a long time to focus. "Almeida," Anselm rasped, and something in Cyrus's chest pulled tight, because he could not remember the last time anyone had used his whole name. "You shouldn't be here. They'll note it."

"I know."

"The pear," Anselm said, and smiled with cracked lips. "I never regretted the pear."

Cyrus knelt. He did not know what one did next. There was no office for this in the temple, no form, no recension. The sick were entered in a ledger and the dead were struck through, and between those two entries the temple had nothing to say. So Cyrus did the only thing left outside the ledgers. He took Anselm's shaking hand, bowed his head, and prayed.

It was not a good prayer. It was barely words. It was the prayer of a nine-year-old still living somewhere under eleven years of careful erasure, rising up through the sediment all at once: *Please. Please, whoever kept the valley before the wards, whoever the stones shut out—he was kind, he gave a pear, let him keep his breath, let someone notice him, please, I will pay, I will give anything—*

The candle guttered.

Not guttered—*bent*. The flame leaned toward the cot as if drawn, steadied, and turned a color Cyrus had no name for, a gold with green in it, the green of the spirals on the ward-stones. The air pressure changed. Cyrus felt it in his ears, in his teeth, a deep subsonic hum like the moment before an avalanche lets go.

And a voice—not heard, exactly, but *arrived*, the way cold arrives through a wall—said:

**Received.**

The word passed through the room and through Cyrus and kept going, down through the floor, down through the foundations, down through whatever lay beneath the Temple of the Ledger, and in its wake the candle burned upright and ordinary again, and Anselm breathed—one breath, clean and full, the split seam of him somehow patched—and opened both eyes.

"There you are," Anselm said, clear-voiced, astonished. "There you are, boy. I thought I'd lost the thread."

Cyrus could not speak. His hands were shaking around Anselm's hand. The prayer had been answered. Prayers were not answered. The wards—

In the corner, the novice attendant stirred, frowning at the candle, at the air, at some wrongness she could feel but not name. Her gaze swept across Cyrus kneeling at the cot, and stopped.

She looked at him. Not past him. Not through him. *At* him, the way you look at a door that has appeared in a wall where no door was.

"You," she said slowly. "I know you. You're—" She squinted, reaching for a name, and Cyrus watched her reach and watched her fail, watched her face go strange with the effort, because there was nothing to find. Four hundred scribes and the temple's own records could not produce him. "You're the one who copies the Weaver debts. Third desk, east row." Not a name. A location. "Why is the candle doing that?"

"I don't know," Cyrus said truthfully.

Above them, faint through layers of stone, the temple bells began to ring the hour—and missed their count. Struck thirteen. Stopped. Began again, correctly, as if embarrassed.

Anselm had fallen asleep, breathing easy. Cyrus rose on legs that did not feel attached to him and walked back down through the temple, past the Hall of Copies where the afternoon shift had resumed its whisper, and the whisper seemed different now, or he was different inside it, and he understood with a vertigo that nearly buckled him at the top of the stair that the thing he had spent eleven years building—his invisibility, his perfect grey evenness, his place among four hundred interchangeable hoods—had just been torn open from underneath by six words muttered over a dying man.

Someone up there had heard him. Something had answered. And an answer, unlike a prayer, could be traced.

* * *

That night in his cell, Cyrus did not sleep. He sat on his cot with his knees drawn up, watching the moonlight move across the floor, and tried to make the world fit together again.

The wards kept magic out. This was bedrock, older than scripture, the first fact taught to every child in the valley along with the names of the seasons. The old gods slept behind the wards because the wards were built to seal them out—or in, depending on which grandmother told the story—and no prayer crossed the boundary because nothing crossed the boundary. Yet a candle had bent. A voice had said *received*. The bells had miscounted, and the novice had looked at him and *seen* him, and Anselm was breathing easily in the infirmary as if death had simply forgotten his address.

Unless, said a colder voice in him, the wards are failing.

He thought of the ward-stones he had passed on childhood walks with his mother, before the temple took him—their spirals worn shallow, moss in the carvings, one near the western pass visibly leaning, tilted like a tooth going bad. Everyone said the stones were eternal. Everyone also said the temple was honest, and Cyrus had copied enough ledgers to know what temples did with honesty.

His mother. The thought arrived the way thoughts arrive at three in the morning, uninvited and total. She had sold him into indenture when he was seven—*apprenticed*, the document said, though everyone knew the word was a kindness—and she had died before his twelfth year, and in eleven years the temple had never once shown him her contract, her signature, the terms of the arrangement that had made him property. He had asked, once, at fourteen. The answering clerk had looked at him with mild surprise, as if a chair had spoken, and said the records were sealed to debtors.

Sealed. Everything was sealed. The contracts, the mountain, the gods, the reason a candle would bend for a scribe's desperate muttering.

Somewhere below the floor of the world, something had heard him say *I will pay, I will give anything*—and had written it down. That was the thought that finally made him stand up and pace the cell, five steps and turn, five steps and turn. Because he knew how this worked. He had copied ten thousand entries of how this worked. Nothing in the temple's whole vast machinery was given; everything was *entered*. A prayer answered was not mercy. It was a line in a ledger, and somewhere, in some recension he had never been permitted to see, there was now an entry with his name beside it—

Except he had no certainty it bore his name. That was the horror underneath the horror. If the court—if whatever had answered—came looking for the one who prayed, what would it find? A location. Third desk, east row. A man the valley itself had been trained not to see.

A knock at his cell door, soft as a moth against glass.

No one knocked. Novices were summoned by bell; masters entered without ceremony; no one in eleven years had ever knocked on Cyrus Almeida's door, because knocking acknowledged that someone was inside worth addressing.

The knock came again. Three taps, unhurried, almost playful.

Through the gap beneath the door, where moonlight lay in a pale stripe across the flagstones, a shadow crossed the stripe. It was small and low to the ground, and it had a tail, and it paused there—Cyrus could have sworn it paused—as if considering whether the room was worth entering.

Then a voice, light and dry and amused, spoke through the wood:

"Well," it said. "Found you."
