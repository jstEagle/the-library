## 2. A Fox at the Window

The prayer I had spoken over Tobin's body followed me home like a debt.

I lay in my cell in the scribes' dormitory and turned it over in the dark, the way I turned over ledger entries when a column refused to balance. *Let him be remembered.* Four words. I had said them without thinking, the way you scratch an itch, and something had scratched back. The candle in the corridor had guttered. The ink on my page had shivered. And in the space between one heartbeat and the next, I had felt—*seen*—the way you feel eyes on the back of your neck in an empty room.

The Temple of the Ledger taught that prayer was arithmetic performed into silence. You entered your petition; nothing answered; the discipline of asking anyway was meant to teach humility. For three hundred years, since the wards went up around the valley, that was simply how the world worked. Magic could not live here. The gods did not hear. We copied our debts, we paid our indentures, we died, and the ledgers closed over us like water.

Except last night, something had heard.

*You're tired,* I told myself. *You watched a man die and you invented a miracle to make it mean something.* This was sensible. Sensible was my native language. I had spent twenty-six years being sensible, being small, being the seventh scribe from the left in a row of forty, so interchangeable that the High Scribe had called me by three different names in a single afternoon and I had answered to all of them.

Sleep did not come. It rarely did. So when the scratching started at the window, I assumed it was a branch.

We were four floors up. There were no branches.

I sat up. Moonlight came through the leaded glass in gray coins, and against it, perched on the outside sill of a window that should have been impossible to reach, was a fox.

Not a fox. A fox-shaped opinion of a fox, too bright at the edges, its fur moving as if under water. Its eyes caught the moon and held it, amber coins in a dark purse, and it regarded me with the frank appraisal of a merchant deciding what a man was worth.

"You said the words," it said. Its voice was dry and quick, like a brush across paper. "Four of them. Over a dying man. Do you know how long it's been since anyone in this valley said the words and *meant* them? Not recited. Not bargained. Just—said?"

I did the sensible thing. I checked the door. Locked from inside. I checked the ward-charm above the lintel, the little iron tablet every cell in the dormitory carried, stamped with the Temple's seal. It was sweating. Actual beads of moisture ran down the iron and dripped onto the stone.

"That's not supposed to happen," I said.

"Very little of what happens this season is supposed to happen." The fox flowed through the window—not opened it, *flowed*, the glass rippling like the surface of a pond disturbed by a dropped stone—and landed on the floor without sound. Up close it was smaller than it had seemed and larger at the same time, the way a thing is large when it is entirely certain of itself. "You're the one who prayed for Tobin Hale."

"Tobin Hale is dead."

"Yes. Died owing nine years on his indenture, with a widow in the low town and a daughter who'll be conscripted into the copying halls at twelve unless someone pays his balance. The Temple will call it charity when they take her. They'll enter it in the ledger as *compassionate transfer*." The fox's tail flicked once, sharply. "I am Quentin. I hold the portfolio of small justices. Overdue apologies. Unpaid kindnesses. Debts of honor too minor for any court to bother with. Which is, I'll have you know, nearly all of them."

"Gods don't exist," I said. "Not here. The wards—"

"The wards are failing." He said it lightly, but the lightness had a blade under it. "Three hundred years ago your ancestors made a bargain to keep magic out of this valley, because magic, as they understood it, meant the old powers walking in and taking whatever they liked. So they walled it off. Every god locked outside, every spirit bound, every prayer falling on deaf air. And in exchange—" he paused, and his amber eyes fixed on mine, and I felt the moment tilt, the way a table tilts just before everything on it slides—"in exchange, certain payments were agreed upon. Certain collateral was pledged."

"I'm a scribe," I said. "Indentured, ninth year of fifteen. I don't have collateral. I don't have anything."

"That," said Quentin, "is precisely the problem."

He hopped onto the foot of my cot, which should have offended me and instead felt oddly like being chosen—a cat does this, I thought absurdly, a cat chooses you—and sat with his tail wrapped around his feet like a magistrate settling in to read a verdict.

"Do you know what happened to your mother?" he asked.

The question went through me like a splinter finding its depth. "She died when I was six. Fever year. She worked the dye-works. She paid her tithes. She—" My voice did something I didn't authorize. I stopped it.

"She loved you," Quentin said. "That part is true, and no one in what follows should be allowed to erase it. Hold onto it. You'll want to let go of it soon, and you mustn't." He began, very softly, to pace the length of the cot, three steps each way, a small gray engine of thought. "Your mother owed money. Dye-works wages, fever debts, a winter when the river froze and there was no work. Ordinary debts. The kind the Temple collects with interest calculated to the day. And in her second month of pregnancy, a man came to her door wearing temple gray, and he offered her a settlement."

"What kind of settlement?"

"The kind where the fine print is the whole document." Quentin stopped pacing. "They told her they would forgive her debts—all of them, everything she owed, a sum she could never have paid in two lifetimes—in exchange for a pledge against her unborn child. Not the child's labor. Not the child's service. The child's *soul*. To be collected by the Undercourt beneath Mount Vessel, payable upon the debtor's death or the debtor's majority, whichever the court found convenient."

The room got very quiet. Quiet the way the valley was quiet, the way everything here was quiet, sealed and airless and safe.

"That's not possible," I said. "Souls aren't—there's no such instrument. The Ledger doesn't trade in souls. It trades in years. Labor. Coin."

"The Ledger trades in whatever it can weigh," Quentin said, "and the Undercourt taught it how to weigh souls three centuries ago. Your temple's founding charter has a second page, scribe. Most people never see it. The valley's wards weren't built to keep the gods out. They were built to keep the *stock* in. A soul behind those wards can't be reclaimed, can't be argued for, can't run home to any power that might contest the sale. The wards are a warehouse wall. And the Temple has been warehousing this valley's souls for three hundred years, selling them downriver to the Undercourt to fund its own comfort, and calling the arrangement piety."

I laughed. I couldn't help it—one short, ugly syllable of it. "You're telling me the Temple of the Ledger is a slaver."

"I'm telling you it's a *broker*," Quentin corrected, "which is worse, because brokers keep immaculate records." His ears angled toward me. "And I'm telling you that your mother signed. Thumbprint in ochre, witnessed twice, filed beneath Mount Vessel. Contract number eleven thousand and something—I'd have to check—which makes you, Cyrus Almeida, the property of the Crown Beneath, outstanding and collectible."

"Don't call me that."

"It's your name."

"It's a *ledger entry*." I was on my feet now, though I didn't remember standing. My voice came out flat and fast, the voice I used for reading numbers aloud. "Everything is. My labor is an entry. My mother's death is an entry. Her love, apparently, is a contract clause. You stand there with your fox face and tell me the one true thing about her—that she loved me—is the same thing that sold me, and you expect me to—what? Grieve? Thank you?"

"Yes," Quentin said simply. "Both. That's what betrayal is. It isn't the absence of love. It's love with fine print."

I turned away from him. The window showed the valley below: the temple roofs, the dye-works' cold chimneys, the terraced fields going silver under the moon, the ward-stones on the ridgeline like a necklace of dull teeth. Everything I had ever seen. Everything I had ever been allowed to see. I had thought the walls kept us safe. I had thought the silence overhead was peace.

It was a lid.

"How do you know my name?" I asked quietly. "I never gave it to you."

"Last night, when you prayed for Tobin, the wards cracked." Quentin's voice lost its briskness. Something older moved underneath it, something tired and fierce. "A hairline fracture, no wider than a prayer. But prayers are pressure, scribe, and pressure finds cracks. The old gods are waking—the real ones, the ones your ancestors locked out—and every one of them can smell the failing wards the way sharks smell blood. I am not a great god. I am a very, very small one. Small enough to slip through a hairline crack while the others are still gathering their strength. And I came through looking for the person whose prayer made the crack."

"Why me? Hundreds pray. The whole temple prays on schedule. Recited, bargained, whatever—you said yourself—"

"They recite. You *meant* it. There's a difference, and the difference is load-bearing." He leapt from the cot to the sill, silhouetted against the moon, and for a moment his shadow on the floor was not a fox's shadow at all but something taller, thinner, crowned in thin flame. "But that's only half of why I found you. The other half is the contract. You see, the Undercourt's claim on you is already filed, already registered, already *counted*—and yet here you stand, alive, uncollected, your soul still sitting inside you like a coin that hasn't been picked up. Do you know why?"

"No."

"Neither do I," Quentin admitted. "And neither, I suspect, does the court. Which means somewhere in the greatest bureaucracy ever built, there is an error. An error with your name on it." His eyes gleamed. "In my experience, scribe, errors are doors. And doors open from the inside."

Below us, across the sleeping valley, a light kindled in the temple archive tower. One window, then two. At this hour? Archivists did not burn midnight oil except twice a year, at audit and at inventory, and neither was due for months.

Quentin saw it too. His ears swiveled.

"Hm," he said. "Someone's awake. Someone's nervous. Nervous men in archives usually mean one of two things: they've discovered a problem, or they're destroying the evidence of one." He looked back at me, and his grin was all teeth and no comfort. "Your mother's contract is filed in duplicate, scribe. One copy beneath the mountain. One copy in your temple's vault. If I wanted to know exactly what was promised, by whom, and with what signature—I know which copy I'd go read tonight."

The light in the archive tower flickered. Then, unmistakably, it began to move—window to window, descending, floor by floor, like something searching.

Or like something burning its way down.

"Well?" said the fox at the window. "Are you coming?"
