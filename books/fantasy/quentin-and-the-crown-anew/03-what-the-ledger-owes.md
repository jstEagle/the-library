## 3. What the Ledger Owes

The archives of the Temple of the Ledger opened at the third bell and closed at the ninth, and in all the years Cyrus had served his indenture, he had never once been sent down to them. That was not an honor. The archive was where records went to be forgotten—contracts too old to collect on, debts too tangled to enforce, names that had outlived their usefulness. The living worked upstairs, in the scriptorium, where ink was still wet enough to matter.

He went down at the fourth bell, when the corridors emptied for the midday meal, carrying a stack of folios he had no intention of returning. In his pocket, folded so small it had left a crease like a scar against his thigh, was the page Quentin had shown him: a copy of a copy of a receipt, dated twenty-six years past, recording the sale of one soul, unborn, from a woman named Sera Almeida to a party whose name had been blotted out with a stamp rather than ink. *Received in full settlement,* the stamp said. *Account closed.*

His mother's name. His mother's hand—he knew her writing the way he knew his own heartbeat, the slight forward lean of every letter, as if she were always hurrying toward something she'd never reach.

He had read the page forty times since the fox-spirit had come through his window. Forty times, and each reading peeled back another layer of the man he had thought he was. He was not merely poor. He was not merely indentured. He was *inventory*. Somewhere beneath Mount Vessel, in whatever dark countinghouse the old stories kept, there was a ledger with his name in it, filed between someone's ox and someone else's harvest debt.

*Unless the name is wrong,* Quentin had said, tail curling like a question mark. *Names are how they hold you. Find the original contract. Find out what your mother actually signed.*

The stairwell smelled of cold stone and older dust. At the bottom, the archive stretched away in ranks of shelving taller than two men, iron galleries zigzagging up the walls into gloom. No windows. A dozen lamps burned in niches, each one consecrated so its flame could not spread—a mercy, or a warning. Cyrus had always understood the temple's relationship with fire to be ambivalent. Paper remembered everything. Fire was the only thing the temple feared more than an audit.

He did not light a lamp. He had spent twenty years learning to see in the margins of things, in the half-dark between other people's attention. He moved down the aisles by touch, fingers walking the shelf-edges, reading spines by their ridges and worn corners. Contracts of sale, 40th year of the Wardens' Peace. Contracts of service. Contracts of surety. And there—the narrow case, chained shut, labeled in faded gilt: *PERPETUITIES.*

Perpetual obligations. Debts that could not die. Souls.

The chain's lock was old brass, and old brass yields to patience. He had picked worse locks copying keys for the bursar's office, back when he was young enough to be given errands instead of silence. The chain slithered down like something relieved to go.

Inside: perhaps three hundred documents, each sealed in lead foil stamped with the temple's sigil. He pulled them one by one, checking dates. His mother had died in the spring of his fourteenth year. She had signed whatever she signed before he was born. He worked backward through the years, faster now, heart tapping out a rhythm against his ribs—

And stopped.

Light. Not lamplight. Torchlight, guttering orange at the far end of the aisle, and with it the smell of hot wax and something sharper underneath. Burning paper has a smell Cyrus knew intimately; scribes burned their mistakes daily. But this was different. This was *volumes*.

He pressed himself into the shadow behind the PERPETUITIES case and watched.

The High Scribe stood at a lectern dragged from some office above, a brazier at his elbow, and he was feeding contracts into it one at a time. Not tearing them, not tossing them—feeding them, each page held to the flame until it caught, then laid carefully in the coals to burn completely. He wore no vestments of office. Just a plain gray robe, the kind any clerk might wear, as if he had dressed for anonymity too. As if erasure were a ritual they were both performing, only Cyrus had come to perform it on himself and the High Scribe on everyone else.

Cyrus counted eleven contracts burning before he understood what he was looking at. Then twelve. Thirteen. Each one bore the lead seal of the Perpetuities case. Each one was a soul-debt.

The High Scribe murmured as he worked—not prayer, Cyrus realized, but *reconciliation*. "Received. Received. Received." Matching each burning contract to an entry in a slim ledger he carried under his arm. Writing off the debts. Closing the accounts. Making it so that nothing had ever been sold, because nothing could be produced to prove otherwise.

Cyrus's hands curled into fists so tight his nails bit crescents into his palms.

Twenty years he had copied ledgers. Twenty years of transcribing other people's debts in a fair hand, believing the numbers meant something, believing the temple stood above the valley like a scale held level. And here was the truth, feeding itself to a brazier one page at a time: the temple didn't keep the scales. The temple *was* a party to the trade. It brokered souls. It took its commission. And now that the wards were cracking and the old gods were waking and someone might come asking questions with divine authority behind them, the temple was destroying its own fingerprints.

*Received in full settlement. Account closed.*

They were closing his account. They might already have closed his. Somewhere in that slim ledger was a line item, and somewhere in the brazier was the only proof of what his mother had—

No. Wait.

The original contract would not be here. These were temple copies, temple filings. If his mother had sold his soul to the Undercourt, the true instrument lay beneath Mount Vessel. But there would be a record of the transaction. There would be *his name*, in the High Scribe's reconciliation ledger, or in the master register that the old man would certainly not burn—the register was how the temple proved to itself it had done nothing wrong.

The High Scribe reached the last document in his armful. Held it to the flame. Paused.

Read it.

Cyrus saw the old man's shoulders change—the small collapse of a man reading his own condemnation. The paper caught. The High Scribe watched it burn longer than he needed to, then set it down among the coals and stood very still, the way a man stands when he has just decided something he will spend the rest of his life paying for.

Then he opened the reconciliation ledger, wrote a single line, closed it, and walked away down the aisle without a lamp, without a glance behind, his footsteps fading up the stairwell until the archive was silent except for the tick of cooling ash.

Cyrus waited a hundred heartbeats. Two hundred.

Then he moved.

The brazier still glowed. On the floor beside it, half-buried in ash, lay the corner of a page the fire had missed—the flame had died just short of it, as if the paper had flinched away. He knelt and drew it out with two fingers, blowing ash from the vellum.

Not his contract. Something better. Something worse.

A schedule of commissions. Dates, parties, amounts—and running down the margin in the High Scribe's own hand, the temple's cut of every soul sold through its offices for thirty years. Dozens of names. Hundreds. And there, seventh from the top, in a column dated the year before his birth:

*Almeida, Sera — one soul, unborn — consideration received: remission of 340 marks, medical debts of Vessel-side parish — temple commission: 17 marks.*

Seventeen marks. His whole existence, brokered for seventeen marks, of which the temple had taken its tithe of complicity. His mother hadn't sold him to monsters. She had sold him to *clerks*, and the monsters had simply been the counterparty.

The anger came up through him like water through cracked stone, slow and total, filling every hollow place that twenty years of careful invisibility had carved. He had spent his life learning not to be seen because being seen cost. Well. He was done being affordable.

He took the page. And then, because he was still a scribe, still unable to leave a record incomplete, he did something he had never dared: he dipped a pen in the brazier's soot-black residue and wrote beneath the entry, in a fair hand the temple would recognize anywhere, four words:

*This account remains open.*

* * *

From the private journal of Aldous Vey, High Scribe of the Temple of the Ledger, written by his own hand, in cipher, by lamplight, past midnight:

*I have burned thirty-one perpetuity contracts tonight and I will burn the rest tomorrow, and I want it recorded, even if only to myself, that I hesitated over none of them but one.*

*The Almeida child came down today. I did not see him—I am not certain anyone could see him, which is precisely the problem—but I felt him the way you feel a draft from a door left open somewhere in a sealed house. When I finished, I wrote the reconciliation line for the Almeida file and I confess I wrote it wrong on purpose. I entered the account as open. Unresolved. Collectible.*

*Let the Undercourt come asking. Let them find the ledger says the debt stands. A boy who was sold before his birth will be called below to answer for it, and the court's attention will be on him, and while it is on him it will not be on us. I have spent my life keeping accounts balanced. Tonight I balanced one with a boy's life, and I find the arithmetic sits in my stomach like a swallowed stone.*

*Or perhaps I flatter myself, and it was not strategy at all. Perhaps some part of me, some part I do not audit, wanted the account to remain open. Wanted him to have a door. Gods forgive clerks. We are the only creatures who sin in duplicate.*

*Tomorrow I burn the rest. Tomorrow I report the break-in to the wardens and describe a thief I never saw. Tomorrow the temple is clean.*

*But the Almeida account stays open. Let that be my one dishonesty, entered honestly.*

* * *

Cyrus climbed the stairwell with the stolen page flat against his chest, under his shirt, where his heartbeat could keep it warm. Above him, the temple bells rang the ninth bell, and the sound fell through the stone like water through a sieve.

In his cell, Quentin sat on the windowsill in the shape of a red fox with ink-stained paws, and the godling's eyes caught the moonlight like coins.

"Well?" said Quentin.

Cyrus pulled out the page and laid it on the cot between them. "I found my price."

The fox leaned close. Read. And then, softly, with something that was almost reverence: "Seventeen marks. They priced your whole soul at seventeen marks."

"And the temple took its share." Cyrus heard his own voice come out level, and was surprised by it. Underneath the levelness, something had hardened into a shape he recognized: resolve, setting like poured lead. "Quentin. How many entries are on that page?"

The godling's whiskers twitched. "I stopped counting at two hundred."

"Then I'm not going to the mountain to argue for one soul." Cyrus folded the page along its crease and put it away, next to his skin, where it belonged. "I'm going to argue for all of them. Tell me the way out of the wards."

Outside the window, far across the sleeping valley, Mount Vessel stood black against the stars—and for the first time in living memory, the stars above its peak seemed to be *moving*.
