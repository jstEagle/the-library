## 4. Leaving the Wards

The stolen page burned against his chest like a coal sewn into his shirt.

Cyrus had folded it small and tucked it beneath the linen binding at his sternum, where the temple's ink-stained aprons would never think to look—scribes were searched for pens, not papers. He could feel every crease of it with each heartbeat. *Contract of Sale: one soul, unborn, Almeida line.* His name in a stranger's hand. His mother's mark pressed beside it, shaky as a bird's track in snow.

He had not stopped shaking since the archive. That was three hours ago, or three years; the bell-rows of the Temple of the Ledger all chimed the same note, and time inside them was just another column to be copied.

He moved through the cloister with the walk he had perfected over twenty-two years: shoulders down, eyes lowered, a man shaped like background noise. Around him the evening shift flowed toward Vespers of Accounts, hundreds of grey-robed scribes murmuring figures under their breath like prayers, because here they were the same thing. No one looked at him. Being unseen was a craft, and Cyrus was its master.

Except someone had seen him. The High Scribe had stood in that burning archive with ash on his cuffs and murder settling into his face like sediment, and when their eyes met across the flames, Cyrus had understood with total clarity that by morning there would be a new entry in a ledger somewhere, and it would be his.

*Run now,* Quentin had said—or rather, the fox-shaped nothing that only Cyrus seemed able to see had said it, perched on the archive sill with its tail curled around its feet like a comma awaiting a sentence. *Run tonight. The wards are thin at the western stones. Thin enough for me to whisper through, which means thin enough for you.*

*Whisper through? You're already here.*

*I'm a rumor of myself here. A draft under a door.* The fox's amber eyes had gone strange and distant. *Outside, I am considerably more.*

Now Cyrus crossed the outer courtyard, past the tithe-barns, past the penance-garden where debtors knelt among rows of white stones counting what they owed, and came at last to the wall—and beyond the wall, the ward-stones.

They rose from the earth like teeth. Seven of them, ancient and lichen-eaten, spaced along the valley's western rim, each carved with spirals so worn they were more suggestion than script. The valley folk called them the Grandmother's Fingers and touched them for luck without knowing why. The temple called them Perimeter Assets, Item Seven, and had never once, in all Cyrus's years of copying, explained what they were *for*.

But he knew now. Everyone had always known, in the bone-deep way you know a thing you've never been told: the valley forgot. It forgot magic the way a scarred hand forgets it once had fingers. Prayers went up and hit something soft and dead and came back down as silence. Gods did not answer here because gods could not *survive* here, and the ward-stones were the reason—the great forgetting fence, humming its one flat note, generation after generation.

And the note was going sour. Cyrus could hear it now that he knew to listen: a wavering under the hum, like a singer running out of breath. The old gods were stirring beyond the mountains, and the fence was cracking.

"Cyrus."

His name, spoken aloud, hit him like cold water. In twenty-two years no one in the temple had used it casually; names were currency here, spent carefully, mostly in ledgers. He turned.

Brother Halloway stood at the edge of the courtyard with two others flanking him—temple hunters, though the robes called them Reconcilers, grey hoods and iron-tipped staves and the patient look of men who reconciled things permanently. Halloway had taught him letterforms when he was nine. Halloway had once slipped him an extra ration during the famine winter. Halloway's face now held no recognition of either kindness.

"You have taken temple property," Halloway said. Not unkindly. That was the horror of it—his voice was gentle as a ledger-line. "Return it, and your account remains open. There is always work for a steady hand."

"The property," Cyrus heard himself say, "is my own name."

Something flickered behind Halloway's eyes—regret, maybe, filed and stamped. "Then you understand we must collect."

The Reconcilers moved. They didn't run; running was undignified, and besides, where was there to go? Every child in the valley learned before speech that the world ended at the stones. Cyrus ran anyway.

He ran through the knee-high grass of the rim meadow, lungs tearing, the page against his chest hot as a brand, and behind him the hunters came on at their terrible walking pace, certain as interest accruing. The first ward-stone loomed out of the dark, taller than a man, its spiral carvings catching moonlight like old ice.

Up close, the hum was deafening. And up close, Cyrus could see what twenty-two years of living beside it had never shown him: cracks. Real ones, hairline-fine, spidering up from the stone's base, and through the cracks leaked something that was not light exactly but was the *memory* of light, the idea of color, a shimmer like heat off summer road.

*The westernmost one,* Quentin's voice said, everywhere and nowhere, tight with strain. *It's the oldest. The oldest fences rot first. Put your hand on it and push, and whatever happens—*

*What happens?*

*Everything,* the fox said, and laughed, high and wild. *Everything happens. That's rather the point.*

Boots on grass behind him. The click of an iron staff being grounded. "Cyrus Almeida," Halloway called, using the full name now, spending it like a coin, and Cyrus understood that the name itself was the leash—they would call him back with it, reel him in with it, write him back into the book where he belonged.

He put both palms against the ward-stone.

The hum entered him through his hands. It poured up his arms and filled his skull with that single flat note, the note of a whole life—wake, copy, sleep, wake, copy, don't be noticed, don't be *anything*—and for one vertiginous moment Cyrus felt the entire weight of the valley's forgetting settle onto his shoulders like a wet wool cloak, and he understood that this was how it worked, this was how it had always worked: the wards didn't keep magic out. They kept it *forgotten*, and forgetting needed somewhere to go, and for twenty-two years some portion of it had been poured into him like everyone else, layer upon layer, until he was less a person than an accumulation of erasures.

*No,* he thought, with a fury that surprised him with its size. *No. I want it back. All of it. Everything I wasn't allowed to be.*

He pushed.

The stone cracked like a struck bell.

Sound first—a peal so vast it was less heard than suffered, rolling out across the meadow, up the valley walls, echoing back from Mount Vessel's distant black shoulder. Then light, pouring up through the shattering spirals in ropes and ribbons, gold and green and colors Cyrus had no education for, colors the valley hadn't contained since before the temple was built. Then wind, rising from nowhere, from *everywhere*, grass flattening in a ring around him, and through the widening crack in the world came—

Magic. Actual magic. It came back into the valley like water into a drained lock, like blood into a numb limb, and it came with such force that Cyrus was thrown backward onto the grass, gasping, every hair on his body standing, his teeth buzzing, his heart hammering a rhythm that suddenly had *variations*, sudden grace notes, syncopation, music where there had only ever been the flat note.

Behind him, the Reconcilers had stopped. Their iron staves were glowing cherry-red and steaming, and Brother Halloway stood staring at his own hands with an expression Cyrus had never seen on any temple face: wonder, naked and terrified.

Because the hunters could feel it too. Everyone in the valley could feel it. Twenty miles away, in the penance-garden, debtors were lifting their heads from their counting. Somewhere a prayer was being answered for the second time in living memory, and then a third, and then so many that keeping count became impossible.

"Quentin?" Cyrus said aloud, stupidly, to the empty air.

"Oh," said a voice from above him, rich and layered and delighted, a voice with harmonics in it, "oh, *yes*."

Cyrus looked up.

The fox was still a fox—but no longer the fox of rumors and drafts. This one blazed. Its coat was autumn fire, red-gold shot through with veins of silver light, and it stood nearly the height of a pony atop the broken ward-stone, nine tails fanning out behind it like a hand of cards being triumphantly revealed. Its eyes were molten amber, and they were fixed on Cyrus with an intensity that made him feel, for the first time in his life, entirely and specifically *seen*.

"Quentin," Cyrus breathed. "You're—"

"Magnificent? Yes. Modest? Regrettably also yes." The godling leapt down from the stone, landing without sound, tails swirling. Up close the blaze resolved into detail: whiskers like silver wire, a grin containing far too many teeth for comfort, and around its neck a collar of tiny bells, each one engraved with a word too small to read. "A godling of small justices, at your service, restored to full—well, not full. But *considerable*. Do you know how long I have been a whisper? Three hundred years, Cyrus Almeida. Three hundred years of being a draft under a door while that—" he flicked a tail at the ruined ward-stone, at the light still bleeding from its cracks, "—sucked the world dry."

"It's coming back." Cyrus got to his feet slowly. His legs didn't feel like his legs. Nothing felt like anything he owned; everything felt like something he'd been lent. "All of it?"

"All of it, everywhere, and faster now that you've made a hole in the fence." Quentin circled him, sniffing, tails brushing the grass and leaving brief trails of sparks. "Which brings us to the urgent part of the evening."

"The hunters."

"The hunters, yes, but also—" the godling's ears swiveled east, toward the valley's heart, toward the temple, "—the Ledger knows. The moment the wards broke, every contract in that building woke up. Including yours. Your soul has been notified, little scribe. The Undercourt has been notified. And the Undercourt does not send Reconcilers with iron staves." His grin stayed fixed, but his eyes went serious as a closed account. "It sends collectors."

Far off across the darkened valley, a bell began to toll—not the temple's measured chime but something older, deeper, a sound like a door opening at the bottom of the world. The grass shivered. The light bleeding from the ward-stone bent toward the sound, as if leaning in to listen.

"They know I'm coming," Cyrus said.

"They know you're *coming*," Quentin agreed, hopping onto a boulder, nine tails wrapping around him like a cloak. "Which means the only thing worse than arriving at Mount Vessel expected is arriving late. We have a road ahead of us, scribe—four days to the mountain, if the road behaves, and roads are behaving very strangely tonight." He glanced back once, at the temple lights, at the small figure of Brother Halloway still standing motionless in the meadow with his ruined staff, watching them go without pursuing.

"Goodbye, Brother," Cyrus said quietly. He found, examining himself, that he meant no malice by it. Only: goodbye. A door closing on a room where he had never truly been.

"Well said," Quentin declared. "Sentimental, brief, no fine print. You'll do." The godling sprang down and trotted west, toward the black line of hills and the road beyond, and his voice drifted back bright as struck flint: "Come along, Cyrus Almeida. Let's go see about getting you *unbought*."

Cyrus took one breath—the first air of his life that tasted of anything, pine and rain and something wilder underneath, something that might have been freedom or might have been danger, and smelled remarkably similar—and ran to catch up.

Behind them, far below in the valley, the second ward-stone cracked on its own.

* * *

They walked all night through country Cyrus had never seen, though he had lived within sight of it forever. Beyond the ward-line the world grew strange by degrees: trees that leaned together like conspirators, a stream that ran briefly uphill out of what Quentin called "enthusiasm," moths the size of songbirds orbiting the godling's tails. Cyrus catalogued it all with a scribe's reflex, wanting desperately to write it down, and kept reaching for a pen that wasn't there.

"You'll want paper," Quentin observed, near dawn. "There's a widow two valleys over who owes the temple a debt she doesn't actually owe. Fraudulent interest, compounded cruelly. I intend to fix it, and you—" the fox's grin flashed in the grey light, "—are going to help. Consider it your first case, counselor. The road to Mount Vessel is paved with small debts, and we're going to settle every last one."

Ahead, the sun rose over hills that were, Cyrus realized, watching the light spill gold across them, *glowing*—not metaphorically, not poetically, but actually, faintly, alive.

Somewhere on those hills, something answered his next breath before he took it.

Quentin's ears went flat. "Ah," the godling said. "That's early. That is *much* too early. Walk faster."
