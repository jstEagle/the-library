## 5. The Road of Small Debts

The world outside the wards was louder than Cyrus had imagined. Not in sound—though there was sound, birdsong with actual malice in it, wind that argued with the trees—but in *presence*. Every stone seemed to be paying attention. Every shadow had an opinion.

He walked the old drover's road north with his satchel of stolen pages against his spine and a godling trotting at his heel in the shape of a red fox with too many shadows.

"You're limping," Quentin observed.

"I'm walking carefully."

"You're limping *carefully*, which is worse. Sit." The fox flicked an ear toward a milestone crusted with lichen. "We've made six miles. The mountain is nine days off if we're slow, four if we're not, and you are currently very slow."

Cyrus lowered himself onto the milestone and discovered his feet had been complaining for some time and he simply hadn't been listening. That was the trouble with a life spent being unnoticed: you learned to ignore your own signals along with everyone else's.

Quentin sat on his haunches and regarded the road behind them. "Do you know what I am, precisely? You never asked."

"A fox-spirit. A godling of small justices. You said."

"I said it, but you didn't believe it. Believing things is different from hearing them." He scratched behind one ear with a hind leg, undignified as a dog. "I keep the little accounts. The ones no one else bothers with. A baker shorted a widow by two loaves over a winter. A landlord moved a boundary stone three feet into a neighbor's field while the neighbor was burying her husband. A miller ground his tenant's grain with stones worn smooth so the flour came out half chaff. Nobody prays about those. Prayers want to be about kings and harvests and wars. But someone has to keep the small ledgers, or the world forgets how to be fair in the places where fairness actually lives."

"And you were asleep," Cyrus said. "Like the others. Like all the old gods."

"Like all the old gods." Something moved under the fox's skin, briefly—a shape too large for the body wearing it. "The valley's wards didn't just keep magic out, Cyrus. They kept us quiet. A ward-stone is a gag and a blindfold dressed up as protection. Whoever built them wanted a valley where nothing answered when people called. And a valley where nothing answers learns to stop calling."

Cyrus looked down the road, back the way they'd come, toward the smudge of the Temple towers hidden in the morning haze. Three hundred scribes. Nine hundred years of ledgers. His whole life arranged so neatly around the principle that wanting things was a defect to be corrected.

"So we walk," he said.

"We walk. And on the way—" Quentin's grin was all teeth and something older than teeth, "—we do a little bookkeeping."

* * *

Maren Ostell had buried her husband in March and been cheated by May, which she considered a fair summary of her luck.

It was the miller, Tull. It was always Tull; it had always been Tull, since her grandmother's day. He took her barley to grind and returned flour that poured like sand and baked like regret, because his grinding stones were worn to glass and everyone knew it and no one said it, because Tull's brother-in-law sat on the village council and Tull's cousin held the mortgage on Maren's field, and a widow who complained was a widow who lost her land.

So she said nothing. She kneaded the chaff-grit into her bread and ate it and told herself it was only flour, only hunger, only the ordinary tax of being a woman alone.

She was at her hearth the evening they came—she remembered that afterward, the ordinary miracle of it, bread dough under her knuckles—when a knock sounded at her door, two raps, polite as a neighbor's.

The man on her step was young, travel-worn, with ink stains on his fingers that no amount of road dust could hide. Behind him, in the last light, sat a fox. She would have sworn later that the fox was smiling.

"Maren Ostell?" the man said. "Widow of Aldous Ostell, late of this parish?"

Her hand found the doorframe. "Who's asking?"

"My name is Cyrus. I'm—" He hesitated, and she watched him decide, watched him choose honesty like a man choosing a heavy tool. "I was a debt-scribe at the Temple of the Ledger. I know what Tull's stones weigh. I know what they've weighed for eleven years. I have his grinding records here—the weights in, the weights out, the shortfall, season by season." He patted the satchel at his hip. "Eleven years comes to roughly four hundred pounds of barley that belongs to you."

Maren laughed. It came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "And who'll make him pay? The council? The council eats at Tull's table."

"Not the council." The fox stood, stretched, and became—for one blink, no longer—a tall figure in a coat the color of rust and embers, with eyes like struck flint. Then it was a fox again, and Maren's knees informed her the doorframe was now load-bearing.

"But the old law will," the fox said. Its voice was dry leaves and small bells. "Debt owed to a widow is the oldest kind there is, lady. Older than councils. Older than mortgages. When the gods woke last month, every unpaid small thing in this valley sat up and cleared its throat. We're merely delivering the mail."

Maren stared at them both. Then, because she was her mother's daughter and her mother had taught her that miracles should be offered tea before they were believed, she stepped aside and opened the door.

"Come in," she said. "You can explain it over supper. And if this is a trick, I've a cast-iron pan that settles arguments."

"It isn't a trick," Cyrus said, ducking under the lintel. "But I'd still like to see the pan."

* * *

Tull the miller came to Maren's gate three days later, pale as his worst flour, carrying a sack of barley on his back and a deed of restitution in his fist, witnessed by the reeve's own clerk, who had gone white as whey signing it and would say only that he'd dreamed of scales.

Maren watched from her doorway as Cyrus explained the arithmetic to the assembled gawkers—not just her four hundred pounds, but the same debt itemized for forty other households, forty names read aloud in the square, each one a person who had stopped expecting to be counted. Old Wenna the fishwife. The Hedley brothers, whose orchard wall Tull had let his cattle break one hungry autumn. Names. Just names, spoken clearly, in daylight.

She had forgotten what it did to a person, hearing their own name said without an invoice attached.

That night she packed a bundle without quite deciding to. In the morning she caught them up on the north road, walking with a stick and her husband's old hat, and when Cyrus turned with a question already forming, she cut it off.

"Don't. I've spent thirty years being nobody's business. I'd like to spend a month being part of one." She fell into step beside him. "Besides. Someone has to cook. You two would live on hedge berries and self-pity."

Quentin, trotting ahead, made a sound that was unmistakably laughter.

* * *

They gathered as rivers gather—one thread, then another, then a current.

At a crossroads shrine with its statue's face chiseled away by some pious vandal, they found a spirit bound into the stone—a hearth-god, small and house-shaped, chained there two hundred years ago by a landlord who wanted his tenants praying to nothing rather than to anything that might take their side. Quentin read the binding like a contract (because it was one; everything was), found the clause where the binder had neglected to renew the chain on the feast of the founding, and spoke the lapse aloud. The stone cracked like an egg. What stepped out of it was the size of a child and smelled of woodsmoke and fresh bread, and it bowed to Cyrus first, before Quentin, which made the fox grumble for an hour.

"The unbought soul," the little god said, in a voice like a kettle beginning to sing. "We heard about you at the crossroads. Word travels fast among the forgotten—it's the only post we get."

By the time the mountain showed itself on the horizon—a gray shoulder shrugging out of the clouds, wearing its crown of cloud like a debt uncollected—they were eleven. Maren. Wenna the fishwife, who had walked out of her village with her market basket and never looked back. The hearth-god, whom they called Ember. Two field-spirits, a well-ghost, a boy of perhaps fifteen named Tam who had run from a bond-service contract and attached himself to Maren with the devotion of a burr, and an old shepherd who claimed he'd been praying to the small gods his whole life on the principle that the big ones had never once returned his letters.

They walked in loose formation on the drover's road, and at night they cooked together, and Cyrus—who had spent twenty-six years eating in silence among three hundred silent scribes—found himself laughing at Tam's terrible jokes and arguing with Wenna about the proper way to salt fish and falling asleep to the sound of voices that knew his name.

Not *Cyrus Almeida*, indentured scribe, entry 4471 in the temple rolls. Just Cyrus. It was astonishing how much lighter a name got when no one was keeping score with it.

One night, sitting watch beside Ember's small fire, he said as much to Quentin.

"When I get my soul back—if I get it back—I keep waiting to feel like it'll fix something. Make me into someone. But I think I felt more like a person tonight, eating burnt fish with a ghost, than I ever did in the temple."

"That's because souls aren't furniture," Quentin said. He was lying on his back, all four feet in the air, watching the stars—which were brighter out here, unwarded, unrestrained, burning like a ledger of open accounts. "A soul isn't something you *have*, Cyrus. It's something you *owe*. To other people. The court below thinks a soul is property—that's the whole rotten heart of them. They buy and sell and file, and they've never once understood that what they're trading is just the receipt. The soul itself is the owing. Your mother owed you love and paid it badly, in secret, in the only coin she had. You owe these people on this road the case you're going to argue. And the court—" his eyes gleamed, flint-strike bright, "—the court owes a debt it has been hiding since the day it was founded. We're going to go collect."

"You keep saying that. Hiding since the day it was founded. You know what it is."

"I know what it is." For once the fox's voice held no play at all. "And I know why you need to find it yourself, in their own records, in their own ink. A justice you're told isn't a justice you own. Trust me. I'm a god of small things. I know the difference between being given a truth and earning one."

Beyond the fire, the sleeping company breathed in ragged harmony. Beyond them, Mount Vessel stood against the stars, and near its base, faint as a scar, a light burned that had no business burning—a customs-house lit through the night, doors open, queues of the dead winding down its steps like smoke pouring the wrong direction.

Maren stirred in her sleep and murmured her husband's name.

Cyrus fed the fire another branch and watched the mountain, and felt the road beneath him bending, slowly, inevitably, toward the place where his name would be taken from him—and understood, for the first time, that he was afraid not of losing it,

but of how easily he might let it go.
