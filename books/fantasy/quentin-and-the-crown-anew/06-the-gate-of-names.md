## 6. The Gate of Names

The mountain had been visible for three days before it stopped looking like a mountain.

From the road it was a gray shoulder against a grayer sky, snowless, treeless, its summit lost in cloud that never moved. But as they climbed the last switchbacks, the shape resolved into something else entirely: terraces cut into the rock, each one stacked with buildings, and the buildings stacked with windows, and every window lit with the same pale, patient lamplight that Cyrus had spent his whole life copying ledgers beneath.

"It's a customs-house," he said. "The whole mountain is a customs-house."

"Was there ever any doubt?" Quentin sat on Cyrus's shoulder in his road-shape—a fox no larger than a cat, fur the color of rust and old copper, eyes too bright for anything natural. "Where do you think debts go when they're too large to collect among the living? They're carried up here. Or down, depending on how you look at it. The Undercourt is beneath everything. The mountain is just where the paperwork enters."

Cyrus had imagined gates—portcullises, wardens, something with teeth. What he found instead at the end of the switchback road was a queue.

It stretched from a doorway carved into the mountainside, down a stair, across a stone bridge over a gorge full of slow silver mist, and back up again, coiling like a dropped rope. The people in it did not speak. Some of them were old; some were children; some were dressed in burial clothes gone green with mold, and some wore the plain smocks of indentured scribes, and one man near the front still had a millstone's dust on his hands as though he'd been taken straight from his work.

They were not all alive. Cyrus understood this without being told. The dead stood in line with the living, and the mist below the bridge rose up between the planks and touched their ankles, and they let it.

"The Gate of Names," Quentin said quietly. "Every soul that passes through is entered into the rolls. And to be entered—" the foxling's ears flattened, "—you have to be named."

"I know my name."

"You don't understand what I'm telling you." Quentin turned on his shoulder, and for a moment the godling's small face was very old. "A name given to the court is never returned intact. That's not cruelty. That's bookkeeping. A name is an address. It's how a debt finds you, how a claim follows you through death and out the other side. Once 'Cyrus Almeida' is written in their ledger, then *Cyrus Almeida* belongs to them in every way that word can mean—not your body, maybe, not your breath, but everything the name touches. Your memories of your mother. Your mother's name inside yours. Every kindness anyone ever did under that name. They'll hold the whole of it as collateral."

The queue shifted forward. No one looked at them.

"There has to be another way in," Cyrus said.

"There isn't. There's only the way everyone goes in, which is why almost no one comes out." Quentin leapt down to a boulder, so they were eye to eye. "You could turn around. You've got the page naming you. You could take it to the small gods, to Maren's people, and live free outside the wards and let the court keep what it thinks it owns. It would be a life. Not a bad one."

Cyrus looked at the queue. At the child in burial clothes, standing with the terrible patience of the dead. At the scribe in temple smock—he recognized the stitching, the Temple of the Ledger's own gray wool—and felt something in his chest go hard and bright.

"When I prayed over Tomas," he said, "and something answered—I didn't pray for myself. I prayed that someone would notice him. That his dying would count for something." He met Quentin's eyes. "Every person in that line was noticed by the wrong ledger. I'm not turning around."

Quentin held his gaze a long moment. Then the godling bowed, low, the way one bows to an equal, and Cyrus's throat tightened, because no one had ever bowed to him before.

"Then we do this properly," Quentin said. "Listen carefully, because I'll only say it once. When they ask your name, you give them a true one—but not the one they want. A name is a door, and doors can be small. Give them something true and tiny, and they will write it down, and they will own exactly that and nothing more. Do you understand? Never give them the whole house when they've asked for a keyhole."

"What's true and tiny?"

Quentin smiled, and it was not entirely a comforting smile. "That's between you and whatever you decide to be."

* * *

The customs-house interior was vaster than the mountain should have allowed. The entry hall rose through darkness into darkness, hung with lamps at every height, and the walls—Cyrus made himself look—were shelving. Floor to unseen ceiling, shelf after shelf, and on the shelves, books. Not ledgers. Books bound in every color, some fat, some thin as pamphlets, some so worn their spines had gone soft as cloth.

He knew what they were before the clerk told him. He was a scribe. He knew a library of lives when he stood in one.

The desk nearest the door was manned by a figure that might once have been human. Its skin had the smooth grayness of paper left too long in damp, and where its eyes should have been there were two small brass rings, like the grommets set into ledger pages to keep them from tearing. It wrote without looking down, and its pen never needed dipping.

"Next," it said. Its voice was the sound of a page turning.

Cyrus stepped forward. Behind him, the queue breathed.

"Purpose of entry?"

"I've come to file a petition."

The brass-ringed eyes fixed on him. Somewhere far above, a lamp flickered. "Petitions are filed by petitioners. Petitioners must be entered in the rolls. State your name for the record."

Here it was. He felt the moment the way he'd once felt the ice go out over the millpond as a boy—the crack running ahead of him faster than he could stop. Quentin's warning rang in his skull: *never give them the whole house.*

But he also knew ledgers. He knew that a false entry was worse than none—that courts fed on fraud, that his entire case would rest on the court having no legitimate claim to him. If he lied at the gate, he gave them the loophole first.

So he would not lie. He would simply be precise.

"My name," Cyrus said, "is the son of Sera Almeida."

The pen paused above the page.

"That is not a name. That is a relation."

"It's the truest thing about me," Cyrus said. "Everything else I learned secondhand."

The clerk tilted its head, and the motion made a sound like paper settling. "Names must be singular. Individual. An entry cannot be a relationship."

"Then enter me as what remains when the relationship is removed." His heart was hammering, but his voice stayed level, the voice he used for reading figures aloud—flat, exact, unarguable. "Enter me as unnamed. Enter me as the residual interest of a contract already in your possession. You hold the debt of Sera Almeida's son. I am that debt, walking. Write *the debt itself* if you need something to put on the line."

Silence. The lamps overhead burned steady now, all of them, as though the whole hall were leaning in.

And then, faintly, from the fox-shaped shadow at his heel, Quentin made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled into a cough.

The clerk dipped its pen—it did dip, Cyrus saw, into a well of ink so black it looked like a hole cut in the desk—and wrote. He heard the scratch of it, one line, two, and then the clerk tore a slip from the page and held it out.

On the slip, in ink that seemed to sink into his eyes rather than sit on the paper, was written:

**ENTERED: THE DEBT ITSELF. NO NAME OF RECORD.**

"Hold this," the clerk said. "You will surrender it at the Hall of Petitions. Do not lose it. Unnamed parties who lose their slips become... difficult to process." Something that was almost a smile moved across the gray face. "Difficult things are stored."

Cyrus took the slip. The paper was warm, like something recently alive.

"Through the arch," the clerk said, already turning its brass-ring gaze to the next in line. "Mind the third corridor. It remembers being a throat."

* * *

Beyond the arch, the customs-house opened into corridors lined with those soft-bound books, and clerks moved among the shelves on ladders, pulling volumes, annotating, returning them. Cyrus walked slowly, and Quentin trotted at his ankle, and neither spoke until they'd passed out of earshot of the nearest clerk.

"You magnificent, impossible scribbler," Quentin breathed. "*The debt itself.* You walked past the trap by handing them the bait with the hook still in it. They can't own a name you never gave, and they can't refuse an entry that's technically accurate, because accuracy is the one law even they obey." The godling spun in a circle, tail lashing. "Do you know how long it's been since anyone beat the Gate of Names? Centuries. *Centuries*, Cyrus."

"They still have my mother's contract," Cyrus said. "And they still know who I am, even if the roll doesn't say it. The High Scribe sold me the same way she did. Someone down here signed both pages."

"Yes." Quentin's brightness banked, like a lamp turned low. "Which brings us to the part I didn't want to say at the gate, because gates have ears." He stopped, and looked up, and his voice went quiet enough that Cyrus had to kneel to hear it. "There's a reason the court lets petitions be filed at all. A reason the machinery has a slot shaped exactly like hope. Every petitioner who walks in believing the rules will save them makes the rules stronger. The court doesn't fear arguments, Cyrus. It *eats* them. It has eaten better lawyers than either of us, and their books are on those shelves."

Cyrus looked at the endless soft-spined volumes. Thought of his mother, young and drowning in debt, signing her son's soul because someone kind-seeming explained the terms. Thought of the fine print she'd never been shown.

"Then we won't argue by their rules," he said. "We'll show them a rule they broke first."

Ahead, the corridor forked. To the left, lamplight and the murmur of queues. To the right, a passage marked with a sign in letters of tarnished brass:

**HALL OF PETITIONS — ALL CLAIMS CONSIDERED — NONE WAIVED**

And beneath the arch, waiting, stood a figure in temple gray.

Not a hunter. Worse. The High Scribe himself, holding Cyrus's stolen page in one hand, smiling like a man who has finally found the error in his own ledger.

"You're a long way from the valley," said the High Scribe, "and I've come a long way to correct an entry."
