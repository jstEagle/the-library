## 7. Beneath, the Bureaucracy

The mountain did not open so much as *admit* them.

Cyrus had expected a gate, a door, some hinge of stone. Instead the customs-house simply became the world around him—walls of slate rising on every side, ceilings lost in a haze of lamplight that came from no lamp he could see. The air smelled of ink and cold iron and something older underneath, like the inside of a sealed room that had been waiting a very long time for someone to breathe it.

He was nameless now. He had felt the name leave him at the threshold, peeled away with a clerk's efficiency—a sensation like a splinter drawn from the palm, painless only in retrospect. What remained of him walked forward because walking was all it knew how to do.

"Keep your hands visible," Quentin murmured from his shoulder. The godling rode there as a fox-shaped shadow, small enough to be mistaken for a trick of the light, which was precisely how he preferred to travel here. "And whatever they hand you, read it before you sign. Especially the parts that say you don't have to read it."

"That's not how reading works."

"It's exactly how reading works *here*."

They joined the queue.

Cyrus had spent eleven years among queues—the morning line for bread, the evening line to surrender completed ledgers, the long silent line of scribes waiting to be told whether their indentures would be renewed or sold onward. He had thought he understood queues. He had understood nothing.

This one stretched beyond sight in both directions, folding back on itself through corridors of slate, and it was made of the dead. Not ghosts, not shades—*souls*, rendered visible here where the wards of the living valley could not reach. They looked like people seen through smoked glass: a farmer still wearing his harvest coat, a girl clutching a doll stitched from sackcloth, an old man whose hands were stained the same ledger-ink brown as Cyrus's own. They did not speak. They did not weep. They waited, with the terrible patience of those who had learned that complaint was a line item billed against their balance.

"How many?" Cyrus whispered.

"No one counts," Quentin said. "Counting is work, and work is billable, and no one down here will pay to know the size of what they own." He flicked one ear toward a placard hanging above the nearest wicket: **WINDOW 4,001 — CONTRACT VERIFICATION — ESTIMATED WAIT: 340 YEARS**. "Though I'd wager the number has four zeroes more than anyone wants to say aloud."

The queue moved in increments. Hours passed, or what passed for hours; time beneath Mount Vessel moved like syrup poured uphill. Cyrus learned the rhythm of the place the way he'd once learned the rhythm of the scriptorium: by watching the machinery ignore him.

Souls were called to windows by numbers stenciled on their wrists—numbers Cyrus realized with a slow, sickening turn of his stomach were *account numbers*. At each window sat a clerk. The clerks were not demons. That was somehow worse. They were gray, patient figures with faces like unfinished sketches, and they worked with the same hunched diligence he saw in his own mirror each morning. One of them stamped a document, and the sound echoed down ten corridors like a small, polite execution.

At Window 2,117, a soul was being informed that her contract contained a clause transferring custody of her *descendants' gratitude* to the court, and that gratitude, properly amortized, nearly covered her debt. She nodded. She had been dead two hundred years and she still nodded, because somewhere a temple scribe had taught her that nodding shortened meetings.

Cyrus's hands curled into fists. Quentin pressed a weightless paw against his neck.

"Not yet," the godling said softly. "You're nobody right now. Nobody is the most powerful thing you can be in this building—for about another day. Then the paperwork catches up to us, so let's make sure we're holding better paper when it does."

* * *

They found the Archive of Instruments on the ninth sublevel, guided by Quentin's nose for loopholes—"contracts smell like copper and regret," he explained, "and this whole floor reeks of both"—and by a bribed porter-spirit who accepted payment in the form of a remembered summer afternoon Cyrus hadn't known he was carrying until Quentin showed him how to take it out and offer it.

The archive was a cathedral of shelving. Contracts hung from hooks in their thousands, each one folded into a shape that hurt to look at directly, because a signature was a knot in a person and knots were never meant to be displayed. Clerks moved among the shelves on rolling ladders, retrieving instruments, verifying instruments, and—Cyrus noticed, with the particular attention of a man trained to spot alterations in a fair hand—*correcting* instruments.

He stopped beneath a ladder and watched.

A clerk drew down a contract, unknotted it, laid it flat. From a drawer it took a pen and, in a hand indistinguishable from the original signatory's, added three words to a margin: *freely and knowingly.* It blotted the ink. It refolded the contract along creases worn smooth by centuries of this exact task. It hung the instrument back on its hook and rolled its ladder on to the next.

"They forge the signatures after death," Cyrus said. His voice came out flat, professional, the voice he used for reporting discrepancies. "The person can't object. The witness is dead. The counterparty is the court itself. There's no one left in the transaction who can say *I never wrote that*."

"Now you understand the business model," Quentin said. There was no foxish levity in it. "Every soul down here signed something. Very few of them signed *this*."

* * *

Finding his mother's contract took four hours and cost him another memory—this one of rain on the temple roof, traded to a shelf-spirit for access to the Almeida folio. He told himself the rain had been ordinary rain. He was not sure he believed it anymore.

The folio was thin. That was the first wound: everything his mother had been, reduced to six documents and a dust sleeve.

He took them to a reading alcove and unfolded them one at a time, and Quentin, uncharacteristically, said nothing at all.

The first was a debt schedule. Wren Almeida, widow, weaver, resident of the lower terraces. Debts incurred across nine years: grain advanced during the blight year, at interest recalculated monthly in a manner Cyrus recognized instantly because he had copied such schedules by the thousand—arithmetic designed to grow faster than any labor could repay. The final figure was impossible. It had always been impossible. That was not a flaw in the schedule. It was the design.

The second document was a promissory note. The third, a renewal. The fourth, a notice of default.

The fifth was the contract.

Cyrus read it the way he read everything—twice, slowly, hunting the margins—and the first pass told him nothing he didn't already know: Wren Almeida, in exchange for cancellation of debt, assigns unto the Undercourt the soul of her issue, unborn, to be collected upon said issue's natural death or earlier forfeiture.

His mother had sold him. He had carried that knowledge up the mountain like a stone in his chest, and he set it down now to read the second time, and the second time he saw what grief had kept him from seeing.

There was a signature on the line for the court's representative. It was not a clerk's mark. It bore the seal-stamp of the Temple of the Ledger—his temple—and beneath it, in a fair, careful, familiar hand, the name:

*High Scribe Ondrell Vasse.*

And beside the signature, initialed in the corner the way clerks initialed amendments, a notation in Vasse's own hand: *"Instrument drafted per standard conversion. Subject unaware of assignee's true identity. Advise presentation as debt-forgiveness covenant only."*

*Subject unaware.*

*Advise presentation as—*

Cyrus read the notation eleven times. The words did not soften. They sharpened.

She hadn't gone looking for the Undercourt. A woman of the lower terraces couldn't have found it if she'd tried. She had gone to the temple—the temple, the one place in the valley that was supposed to stand between the poor and the dark—with a debt that could never be repaid, and she had been handed a document and told it was forgiveness, and she had signed it because the alternative was watching her child starve, and the men who drafted it had noted, in their tidy professional way, that she must never be told what she was actually signing.

"She didn't sell me," Cyrus said. The alcove swallowed his voice. "She was *sold*. I was the goods, but she was—" His throat closed. "They used her love as the signature line."

Quentin's tail curled around Cyrus's wrist, feather-light. "Now you know why I brought you down the long way," the godling said quietly. "Anger keeps a man moving. But anger aimed at your mother burns the wrong house down. This—" he tapped the notation with one claw, "—this is the fire worth carrying."

"The High Scribe is burning records," Cyrus said. "Chapter three of my life, remember? He was burning them *before* I ever ran. Because if the temple's complicity surfaces—if it can be proven that the court's local agents forged consent—then every contract routed through the valley is tainted. Every single one."

"Mm." Quentin's ears went up. "And how many souls came through the valley's temples, would you guess?"

Cyrus looked out over the archive: the shelves receding into lamplight haze, the hanging contracts in their tens of thousands, the patient clerks correcting the dead.

"All of them," he said. "The valley fed this court for four hundred years. And every feeding passed through a temple scribe's hand."

* * *

He copied the contract.

It was reflex older than thought—eleven years of indenture had built the habit into his bones—but this time the copying was theft. He reproduced every line, every initial, every damning notation in a fair hand that matched the original stroke for stroke, and then, in the bottom margin, he added a line of his own, in cipher only a Temple-trained scribe could read and only he could prove:

*Original examined beneath Mount Vessel, witnessed by a godling of small justices. Fraud entered into evidence.*

Quentin watched him write it and something in the godling's shadow-shape seemed to settle, like a fox curling up at last beside a fire it had tended a long while.

"You know what happens next," Quentin said. "The moment you file that, you stop being nobody. The court will pull your account number out of the dark and put it up on a board, and every clerk in this mountain will know your name even if you don't."

"I gave my name up at the door."

"You'll get a new one. Courts only ever give you one kind." Quentin smiled, and it was not entirely a kind smile. "They'll call you *petitioner*. And petitioner is the most dangerous word in this entire building—because a petitioner, by the court's own ancient law, cannot be collected upon, detained, or amended until his case is heard."

Cyrus folded the copy into his shirt, against his heart, next to nothing at all.

Somewhere far above them, through nine sublevels of slate, a bell began to ring—not a temple bell, not a funeral bell, but the flat administrative chime of a system registering an anomaly. Lights kindled along the archive aisles, row by row, converging.

"They've noticed the folio's open," Quentin said, hopping to his shoulder. "So. Do we run, scribe—or do we walk to the Office of Petitions like we own the corridor?"

Cyrus looked down at his ink-stained hands—hands that had spent a lifetime copying other people's debts—and closed them into fists.

"We walk," he said. "Slowly. Let them read our handwriting."
