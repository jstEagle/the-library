## 8. The Petition

The Undercourt did not sleep, but it paused. Somewhere in the deep hours between one queue's end and the next queue's beginning, the great brass wheels of the registry slowed, and in that pause Cyrus sat at a clerk's desk with a stolen pen and a blank petition form, and wrote the words that would either save him or unmake him entirely.

The desk was his by right of queue. He had stood in line for six days—six days of gray light that came from nowhere, of air that tasted like copper coins and old paper, of souls shuffling forward with their claim-tickets clutched to their chests like talismans. Six days, and when his number was called he had walked to this desk, this scarred oak slab staffed by a creature that might once have been a man, and he had said:

"I'm not here to settle a debt. I'm here to file a challenge."

The clerk had looked at him with eyes like ledger-lines, horizontal and endless, and had not blinked. "Challenges require Form 9-Vessel. In triplicate."

"I know," Cyrus said. "I've read the code."

He had. Three days in the court's public library—a cavern where the law books grew like stalagmites, dripping new clauses overnight—while Quentin scouted the upper galleries and Maren kept watch at the gate-house above, holding his surrendered name in a locket of river-iron, because a nameless petitioner could still carry *effects*, and effects were property, and property could be searched.

Now the form lay before him, and his hand—his scribe's hand, trained for twenty years in the Temple of the Ledger to write small and straight and invisible—moved across it without trembling.

*Petitioner: [name withheld per Gate protocol].*

That was the first loophole, and it had cost him a night of terror to find. The court required a name on every document; the Gate had taken his name and would not return it intact. But the code, buried in Volume 114, Subsection 3, allowed a petitioner whose name was *held in escrow by the court itself* to file under seal—and anything filed under seal could not be dismissed for incompleteness, because the court could not compel disclosure of what it already possessed without admitting possession.

You could not demand a name from someone you had already robbed. The law, even here, gagged on its own greed.

*Grounds of challenge:* He wrote it out in full, each clause a stone laid across a river.

*1. That the contract dated [seal of the Temple of the Ledger, year of the Gray Frost] purporting to convey the soul of the above-referenced mortal to the Undercourt was executed in exchange for consideration never delivered.*

His mother had signed for a debt-forgiveness that the temple had never granted. He had found the temple's own copy in the burning archive—the High Scribe's fire had missed one drawer, one page, and Quentin's paws had pulled it free while the smoke still rose. The forgiveness column was blank. It had always been blank. She had sold her unborn son for nothing, because the temple told her the ink was dry when the ink had never been mixed.

*2. That the said contract was procured through material misrepresentation by an agent of the court, to wit: the Temple of the Ledger, chartered collection-office of the Undercourt since the Wards were raised.*

That was the deeper cut, the one Quentin had found in the founding records—not the mother's fraud but the court's. The temples were not independent creditors. They were branches. Every valley priest who had ever whispered *the gods are dead, only the Ledger remains* had been reading from a script the Undercourt wrote. The wards that strangled magic were not protection. They were foreclosure.

*3. That a contract obtained by fraud is void ab initio, and no right of collection exists thereunder; and further, that the court, having knowledge of the fraud, is party to it.*

The clerk read the finished form three times. Its eyes moved like abacus beads.

"Under seal," it said at last. "Filed." A stamp descended, black wax, a sigil like a closed eye. "Your challenge enters the docket. Hearing before the Crown Anew will be scheduled at the court's convenience."

"When?"

"The court's convenience," the clerk repeated, and smiled with a mouth that had forgotten how, "is measured in centuries."

Cyrus took the receipt. His hands were steady. Inside, everything was screaming.

* * *

From the Crown's high perspective, the matter was a speck.

The Crown Anew had worn ten thousand shapes since the old gods fell and it inherited their debts. Tonight it wore the shape of a throne room the size of a weather system, and it regarded the sealed petition the way a glacier regards a match.

*A challenge,* it thought, in a voice like tectonic plates turning over. *From a nameless thing. Filed under Section 114.3.*

It knew the section. It had written the section—or rather, its predecessor had, the First Court, the Court Beneath, before the fraud, before the founding, before a stolen soul had been salted into the cornerstone to make the whole edifice stand. The Crown did not remember which soul. That was the trouble with foundations: you built on them and forgot they were there.

But the match had been struck by something else. Something small and red-tailed and laughing, moving through the galleries below, whispering to the minor powers chained in the filing-shafts.

*The fox,* the Crown thought. *The little justice again.*

It dispatched a memo. The memo was stamped, shelved, and lost within four minutes, because the clerk assigned to it had accepted a bribe of one hour's true daylight, paid in advance by a godling with amber eyes.

* * *

"They're going to bury us in procedure," Quentin said. He was sitting on the windowsill of the petitioner's dormitory—a cell with no window, which had never struck anyone as strange until a fox-spirit opened one anyway, out onto a view of the mountain's roots and a sky that shouldn't have existed this far down. "That's how the court kills things. Not with fire. With *dockets*. Your hearing gets scheduled behind eleven thousand others, and we all die of old age waiting."

"Then we make sure it can't schedule anything at all," Cyrus said.

Quentin's ears went up. "Oh," he said, delighted. "*Oh.* You've been reading the nasty parts."

"I lived in a temple," Cyrus said. "I know how bureaucracy fights. You don't beat it with swords. You beat it with *paperwork*."

The plan was simple in the way avalanches are simple. The court ran on quorum: every hearing required the presence of three registered witnesses from the rolls of minor deities, the small gods of thresholds and chimneys and lost buttons, who had signed themselves into the court's service generations ago in exchange for being left alone. For centuries they had attended, silent, stamping whatever they were told to stamp.

But a witness, the code said—Volume 12, the oldest volume, the one grown so deep in the library that no clerk had read it in living memory—could *recuse*. And a recusal required a reason, and a reason had to be *recorded*, and every recorded reason entered the public docket, where any petitioner could read it.

So Quentin went down into the filing-shafts, where the small gods were kept like ledgers themselves—bound to their stations with contracts of attendance—and he did what he had always done, all his long life, on roads and doorsteps and in the margins of other people's stories.

He listened.

He heard the hearth-god who had not felt warmth in nine hundred years. He heard the threshold-spirit made to guard doors she'd never chosen. He heard the little god of honest weights, chained to a court where nothing had ever weighed honestly, weeping rust.

And he asked them each the same question: *"What does the court owe you?"*

Not freedom—that came later, that was Cyrus's argument, that was the great wheel. Just the question. Just the debt, named aloud, written down, filed as a *counterclaim*. Eleven hundred counterclaims in three days, each one forcing a hearing, each hearing requiring witnesses, each witness now entitled to recuse because the docket was *conflicted*, because you could not fairly judge a case about owed debts when you yourself were owed—

The machinery seized.

Cyrus watched it happen from the gallery above the docket-hall: the great brass wheels slowing, stuttering, clerks running with armfuls of forms that multiplied faster than they could be stamped. A hearing for the god of honest weights was scheduled; two witnesses recused citing conflict; a replacement was summoned; the replacement recused too, because she had also filed a counterclaim, because Quentin had gotten to her first. The hearing collapsed. Another was called. Collapsed. The backlog grew like floodwater.

"They can't hold my trial," Cyrus breathed, "because they can't convene a court."

"They can't hold *anyone's* trial," Quentin corrected, tail lashing with joy. "There's a widow up top who'd want you to know this is for her too."

* * *

For two days, it worked.

For two days, the Undercourt drowned in its own procedures, and Cyrus slept for the first time since descending—not peacefully, but deeply, the sleep of a man who has set a fire and cannot yet be burned by it.

On the third day, the Crown stopped playing by its own rules.

He was summoned. Not by docket, not by stamp, not by any instrument the code recognized. A herald simply appeared in the dormitory—tall, faceless, robed in vellum—and said, in a voice like a verdict:

"The Crown Anew waives procedure. The petitioner will be heard. Now."

"That's not legal," Cyrus said.

"No," the herald agreed. "But the Crown wrote the law. It may notice, whenever it wishes, that it is standing above it."

Quentin appeared on the sill, and for the first time since Cyrus had known him, the fox looked afraid. "They're skipping the queue. That means they think the jam is worse than the trial. That means—" his ears flattened "—they think they can win it. Cyrus. Whatever happens in that room, they will offer you something. Something sized exactly to you. Don't—"

"Don't take the deal alone," Cyrus said. "I know."

"You don't know," Quentin said quietly. "Nobody knows until the offer is in their mouth. That's how the court was founded, little scribe. Someone was offered something. And said yes."

The herald waited. The wheels of the court, far below, had gone utterly silent—all eleven thousand counterclaims suspended, all the flooded dockets frozen mid-stamp, the entire vast machine holding its breath around one nameless man with a stolen page in his coat.

Cyrus stood. He touched the locket at Maren's throat upstairs, three miles of stone away, holding the shape of who he used to be. He thought of his mother's blank forgiveness column, of the hearth-god's rust-colored tears, of the widow Maren counting coins that had been shorted her for thirty years.

"Lead on," he said.

And the Crown, in its throne room the size of a weather system, felt the match arrive at last, and prepared to offer it everything it had ever wanted—alone.
