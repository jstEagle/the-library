## 9. Before the Crown Anew

The courtroom of the Undercourt was not a room. It was a wound in the mountain, a cavern so vast that the ceiling was only a rumor of darkness, and the walls were shelved floor to unseen height with ledgers—millions of them, bound in vellum that Cyrus now knew, with a sickness in his stomach, was not the skin of any animal.

His name had been taken at the gate six days ago, or six years; time below moved like water finding its level. He walked into the court as *no one*, and the court had hated it from the first moment. A petitioner without a name could not be indexed. Could not be cross-referenced. Could not be quietly lost in a drawer and forgotten, which was, Quentin had explained with a fox's grin, the court's favorite way of resolving inconvenient cases.

So they had to hear him.

The Crown Anew sat at the far end of the cavern on a dais of black basalt, and it was not one thing but three: three thrones, three shapes, three presences that blurred when he tried to look directly at them, the way a word blurs when you stare too long. The middle throne held something tall and robed in ledger-leather. The left throne held something old and patient, its face a blank page. The right throne—

The right throne was empty.

It had been empty since the trial began, and every clerk and bailiff and soul-clerk's assistant in the cavern glanced at it the way sailors glance at a cloudless horizon that they know is lying.

"Petitioner," said the middle presence, and its voice was the sound of a stamp coming down on wax. "You have no standing. You have no name. You have no claim. Why does this court suffer you to speak?"

Cyrus stood in the well of the cavern with his stolen page in his hand—the contract naming him, the one he'd pulled from the High Scribe's fire—and he felt the fear he had carried all the way down from the valley finally settle into something else. Not calm. Calm was too gentle a word for it. It was the stillness of ink drying.

"Because," he said, "I am the only unbought soul in this room."

* * *

They let him speak because they believed they could not lose. That was the first fraud, though not the one he'd come to expose. The clerks of the Undercourt had spent nine hundred years perfecting procedure until procedure became a fortress, and they looked at a nameless scribe in a temple coat and saw a mouse in a granary.

Quentin sat on Cyrus's shoulder in his small form—a fox no bigger than a cat, red as sealing wax, his tail curled around Cyrus's collarbone like a scarf. To the court he was nothing. A familiar. An affectation. Gods of small justices were beneath the Crown's notice, which was precisely why Quentin had spent the last five days doing what he did best: noticing.

"The contract before you," Cyrus said, holding up the page, "binds the soul of a child conceived but not yet born, sold by his mother, Maren Almeida—" he caught himself; Maren was the widow's name, not his mother's, but grief braided them together now and he let it— "sold by his mother to satisfy a debt of eleven crowns, four shillings, incurred to this court through the Temple of the Ledger."

"Which is lawful," said the middle presence. "Consent was given. Signature was affixed."

"By my mother. Who could not read."

A murmur went through the ranks of clerks, the dry rustle of ten thousand pages turning at once.

"The Temple's agents read the document *to* her," Cyrus continued, and his voice did not shake, because he had rehearsed this in the dark of his cell a thousand times, in the queues, on the Road of Small Debts, over Maren's kitchen table while she wept for her own cheated dead. "They read her a version. I have obtained the version she heard." He lifted a second page. "And I hold the version she signed. They are not the same document. The one she heard forgave the debt upon payment of eleven crowns. The one she signed transferred ownership of her unborn child's soul *in perpetuity*. This is fraud under the court's own statutes, section one, clause one: *no bargain binds that was not understood by both parties.*"

The middle presence was silent for a long moment. When it spoke again, its voice had cooled by degrees. "The agent who deceived her acted without the court's sanction. His crimes are his own."

"Then produce him."

"We cannot. He is dust."

"He is *not*," said Quentin, and for the first time the godling spoke aloud in the court, and every clerk flinched, because a god speaking inside the Undercourt was like a candle lit inside a powder store. "He is right there." The fox's nose pointed—not at the dais, not at the clerks, but at the empty third throne. "Third seat. Been sitting there for nine hundred years. Wearing a crown he stole. Go on, Your Fraudulence. Take off the shape you're wearing and show them what's underneath."

* * *

Here is what Cyrus had learned in the archives, in the burning records, in the fragments the High Scribe had failed to destroy: the Undercourt was not founded by gods. It was founded by a man.

Nine hundred years ago, when the old gods still answered prayers and the valley still sang with magic, there had lived a magistrate named Vessel—Mount Vessel was not a mountain's name but a man's, swollen by worship into geography—who grew tired of justice being free. Free justice, he reasoned, was worth exactly what people paid for it. So he built a court where every mercy had a price, and he wrote its founding charter, and he sealed its authority with the oldest trick in law:

He made the court's power rest on a debt owed *to the court itself*.

Specifically: the charter declared that the Crown's authority derived from the First Contract—the purchase of a soul freely given, witnessed and sealed, the model upon which all subsequent contracts were patterned. If the First Contract was valid, every contract beneath it was valid. Every soul in every queue, every ledger on every shelf, all of it hung from that single signature.

And the First Contract was forged.

Vessel had purchased no one's soul. There had been no seller, no consent, no witness. He had written a contract between himself and a woman who died before the ink dried—or rather, before the ink was even applied; he had written it *after* her death and backdated it, then sworn before the assembled gods that it was genuine. And the gods, young and trusting and bound by their own reverence for law, had accepted it. Had granted the charter. Had withdrawn from the valley when the wards went up, wards paid for with the interest on a lie.

The court's authority rested on a soul it never legally purchased.

And Cyrus—this was the part that had kept him awake in the dark of the customs-house, laughing until Quentin pressed his paw against Cyrus's mouth—Cyrus was the only mortal alive whose unbought soul could carry a petition into the court. Which meant the court's entire existence had been waiting, for nine centuries, for exactly one person: a scribe whose mother never sold him, standing in their courtroom, holding their founding fraud in his hand.

"You cannot prove this," said the middle presence, but the words came slower now, each one weighed before release. "The original charter is sealed. Sealed documents may be opened only by—"

"By an authorized officer of the Crown," Quentin finished sweetly. "Yes. We know. Funny thing about seals, though." The fox hopped down from Cyrus's shoulder and trotted, insolently, across the marble floor of the well, tail high. "They're only as strong as the hands that keep them. And your Keeper of Seals—" he stopped before the front row of clerks, where a small gray figure trembled behind its desk— "has been very generously compensated these last five days. Haven't you, friend? Small justices. I pay in small things. A debt forgiven here. A name returned there. Nothing anyone would miss."

The gray clerk raised its head. With shaking hands, it placed a scroll on its desk and pushed it forward.

The founding charter of the Undercourt. Unsealed.

* * *

What happened next, Cyrus would remember all his life in fragments, the way you remember a storm: in flashes, in sound.

The middle presence rising from its throne, robes unfurling like smoke. The reading of the charter—Cyrus read it himself, his scribe's voice steady and clear, carrying to the highest shelves, reciting clause after clause after clause that described a transaction that had never occurred. The murmur becoming a roar among the queued dead, thousands of souls who had waited centuries in lines suddenly understanding, all at once, that the line itself was the lie. The left throne cracking down the middle like river ice. The bailiffs moving toward Cyrus and being stopped—not by force, but by the small gods, dozens of them, hundreds, godlings of cracked thresholds and returned tools and found coins and mended fences, pouring up out of the seams of the world where Quentin had gathered them, jamming every door, every stamp, every procedural mechanism of the court with the divine equivalent of a bent paperclip.

And above it all, the empty third throne beginning, at last, to fill—to shudder and darken and take shape, as the thing wearing it abandoned its disguise of absence and stood revealed: a man-shaped absence, a magistrate grown vast on nine hundred years of stolen interest, the Founder himself, who had hidden so long inside his own bureaucracy that he had become its middle throne, its left throne, its voice, its stamp, its everything.

"*Enough.*"

The word fell like a guillotine. Silence slammed across the cavern.

The Founder looked down at Cyrus, and Cyrus looked up, and for a moment neither of them was anything but two men—one who had built an empire on a lie, and one who had copied other people's debts for fifteen years and never once been mentioned in a ledger of his own.

"You want your soul, scribe," the Founder said. "That is all this theater has ever been about. So take it. Walk out of here whole. Name restored, contract voided, debt erased. You will be the freest man in the valley. No one will ever own you again." A pause, silk over stone. "Or. Void the First Contract entirely, and every contract falls with it. Every soul in every queue goes free—including yours. But the court dissolves. And understand what that means, little clerk: the court is the only thing standing between the dead and the dark. Nine hundred years of order. Without us, the debt-bound dead do not go home. They go *nowhere*. Is that your justice? Will you spend your one life's single claim to gamble ten million souls on chaos?"

The offer hung in the air, perfectly balanced, perfectly poisoned.

Take back his soul alone. Or give it away, and unmake the court, and trust that what came after was mercy and not oblivion.

Cyrus thought of his mother, who could not read, signing her love away in fine print. Of Maren the widow, who had fed him stew in a house that smelled of rosemary and told him, *you belong wherever you're needed.* Of the queues outside, stretching beyond sight, souls who had been patient for centuries because patience was all they were allowed.

He thought of the empty third throne, and how the court had always had a hole where its heart should be.

"No," Cyrus said.

The Founder tilted its head.

"I don't choose between your options," Cyrus said, and unrolled the stolen page one final time, and dipped his finger—not in ink, but in the blood he had opened along his palm that morning, because some amendments must be written in the author's own hand—"because both of them assume the court survives. Neither does. I'm amending the First Contract. Clause one: the fraud is voided. Clause two: every debt it secured is discharged. And clause three—" his voice rang off the shelf-walls, off the ledgers, off the bones of the mountain— "*the authority of this court reverts to its rightful source: the souls it claimed to own. All of them. Effective immediately.*"

He signed it *no one*.

The charter ignited in his hand with white fire, and the first shelf of ledgers, high on the left wall, burst open—all at once, ten thousand covers springing wide, and out of them rose light, rising like heat-shimmer, like birdsong, like the valley remembering magic.

The Founder screamed, and the scream was the sound of an empire discovering it had never existed.

Quentin leapt onto Cyrus's shoulder, grinning like dawn. "Well," said the fox-god of small justices, as the Crown Anew came apart around them in cascading light, "I hope you know what you've just unleashed."

Above them, through the cracking ceiling of the Undercourt, the first of the dead were already going home.
