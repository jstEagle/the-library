## 10. Everything Unbought

The Crown's offer hung in the air of the great chamber like a coin balanced on its edge.

"Take back your soul," it said, and the voice was every voice—the queue-clerk's, the archivist's, his mother's, his own. "One soul, lawfully reclaimed. The court will honor it. Sign here, and walk out whole."

Below the dais, the queues went on forever. Souls stood in lines that spiraled down into dark the eye could not follow—farmers and midwives and children who had died owing, or been born owing, or been entered into the ledger by clerks who never learned their faces. They had stopped hoping so long ago that hope had become a kind of posture, a way of standing in line.

Cyrus looked at the contract in his hands. His mother's signature at the bottom, small and shaking. *Mara Almeida.* Above it, the founding clause he had spent three days dragging into the light: the Undercourt's authority rested on the first soul it had ever purchased—a soul taken under a seal forged from a god's stolen name. Every contract since had been built on that cracked foundation. Every one of them void, if he said so. If he pressed the claim not for himself but for all of them.

"One soul," the Crown repeated, patient as stone. "Or everything, and nothing left for you. Choose."

Quentin sat on the railing beside him, small and fox-eared, his tail curled around his feet. He did not say *take the deal*. He did not say anything at all. That was the thing about Quentin: he governed small justices, and he knew better than most that no one else could weigh yours.

Cyrus thought about his cell in the Temple of the Ledger. About copying debts by lamplight until his fingers cramped, invisible among hundreds, careful as dust. He had spent his whole life being nothing because being something cost too much.

He thought about Maren, who had followed him down the mountain with her dead husband's IOU folded like a prayer. About the bound spirit they'd freed at the crossroads well, who had wept water instead of tears. About the widow's hand on his shoulder, the strangers who had become a road-full of people who saw him.

"I have a counteroffer," Cyrus said.

The chamber stilled. Even the pens of the clerk-souls stopped scratching.

"You offered me my soul back. I decline." He held up the contract, and his voice did not shake, which surprised him. "I spend it. All of it. Void every contract signed under the forged seal—which is every contract this court has ever enforced. Free them. All of them. The living debtors and the debt-bound dead. Dissolve the liens. Return what was taken."

"That is not how spending works," the Crown said. "A claim is redeemed once. For one holder."

"Then let me amend the instrument." Cyrus set the contract on the dais and took out the scribe's pen he had carried since the temple—the pen that had written ten thousand debts and never once a mercy. "I'm a scribe. Amending instruments is the only prayer I know."

He wrote. And as he wrote, the old gods who had come crowding into the chamber—the small ones, the forgotten ones, the god of threshold-stones and the goddess of second sowings and Quentin's hundred cousins of minor mercy—leaned close, and their attention was ink, and their attention made it binding.

*Let this claim be spent not for the bearer but for all named herein,* he wrote, and the names began to appear on their own, rising up through the page like bubbles through dark water, thousands of them, tens of thousands, every soul in every queue, each name arriving with a small sound like a bell struck underwater.

The Crown watched. It could have refused—it had the power, surely—but power is not the same as right, and the whole architecture of the court had always depended on pretending otherwise. Cyrus wrote to the bottom of the page, and when there was no more room, the page grew more room, because pages do that when gods are watching.

"There," he said. "Filed."

* * *

The dissolution of the Undercourt was not an explosion. It was an unbinding.

It began with the ledgers. Every book in the archive opened itself at once, and the ink lifted off the pages like starlings rising from a winter field—debt after debt after debt peeling free and dissolving into motes of gold that drifted upward through the vaulted dark. The chains on the queue-rails rusted to nothing between one breath and the next. The customs-house gate, which had demanded names for nine hundred years, swung open with a groan like a man waking, and forgot what it was for.

And the dead began to go home.

They went up through the mountain the way water finds its way downhill—through seams and shafts and doors that had been sealed since the wards were raised. Cyrus stood in the great chamber and watched them pass: the farmer who kissed his own calloused hands as if greeting old friends; the midwife who had died owing a fee she'd never actually been charged; the children, who ran, laughing, because children understand freedom faster than the rest of us. Some paused to touch Cyrus's sleeve in passing. None of them knew his name. He had given it away at the gate, and the court had kept it, and now the court was coming apart, and his name was going wherever names go when their owners stop needing them.

He found he didn't mind. A name, he was beginning to understand, was just a ledger entry you carried around inside yourself. There were other ways to be known.

Maren found him at the foot of the dais. She was holding her husband's IOU, or what remained of it—a scrap of blank paper, the debt gone out of it like breath from a mirror.

"It's empty," she said, wonderstruck. "It's just paper. It was always just paper."

"Yes," Cyrus said. "That's the trick of it. That was always the trick."

Quentin appeared on his shoulder, full-sized now outside the valley's starving wards, red-gold and sharp-eyed and smelling faintly of woodsmoke and rain. "You realize," the godling said, "that you've unmade the largest institution in the underworld and your only compensation is a blank piece of paper and my company."

"The paper's worth more."

"Flatterer." But Quentin's ears were soft, and his eyes were not mocking at all. "Come on, scribe. The mountain's emptying. Let's not be under it when the last wall forgets to hold."

* * *

The valley, when Cyrus returned to it, had changed less than he had.

The ward-stones still stood along the ridgeline, but they were only stones now—old boundary markers, moss-covered, honest about what they were. Magic moved through the valley like light through a window newly cleaned: tentative, growing stronger by the day. In the temple, the High Scribe had been arrested by his own clerks, who had discovered, in the reopened archives, exactly what he'd been burning. The Temple of the Ledger was becoming something else. No one had decided what yet. Cyrus intended to have opinions.

He took a cell smaller than his old one, near the eastern wall, where the morning came in early. He had no name to sign to the lease, so he signed with a mark: a small fox curled around a pen. The clerks accepted it. Marks, they were learning, could be covenants too.

Quentin taught him on the long evenings, when the lamps were lit and the valley's people came with their grievances and their hopes.

"A covenant is not a debt," the godling said, pacing the tabletop like a lecturer, tail high. "A debt says: you owe. A covenant says: we agree. A debt survives the people who made it—that's why courts love them. A covenant dies with its makers unless they renew it. Renewal is the whole point. It means you keep choosing each other."

"And if someone breaks it?"

"Then the small gods get involved." Quentin grinned, all teeth. "We're very involved."

So Cyrus wrote new instruments. Marriage covenants that named both parties' rights in equal ink. Tenant agreements where the land owed the farmer as much as the farmer owed the land. A covenant between the valley and the river, renewed each spring with a festival everyone pretended was ancient. A covenant with the dead—not debts owed to them, but promises made to them: that they would be remembered without being billed for it.

His hands cramped some nights, the way they had in the old days. But the cramp felt different now. Before, he had copied other men's words. Now the words were his, and the people who signed beneath them looked him in the eye, and even without a name he was never once mistaken for furniture.

Some nights he climbed to the ridge and sat against a ward-stone and watched the valley glow with its slow-returning magic, and he tried to remember what it had felt like to be Cyrus Almeida, the scribe with the sold soul. It felt like remembering another person's dream. Vivid, but not his.

What was his was simpler. A pen. A fox. A valley full of people who nodded when he passed. A blank piece of paper from the Undercourt, framed above his desk, on which he had eventually written a single line:

*Paid in full.*

* * *

I want it recorded—I am recording it, in the only court that matters now, which is the memory of small things—that I never doubted him.

That is a lie, and I am a godling of small justices, and lying in a record is a small injustice, so: I doubted him. At the dais, with the Crown's offer shining like a coin, I doubted him. I thought, *he has been nothing his whole life; he will take the one thing that makes him something.* I am old enough to know how hunger works in the erased.

But here is what I have learned since, watching him work: erasure does not teach you to want. It teaches you to see what wanting costs. Cyrus had watched a valley run on debt for thirty years. He knew precisely the price of owning things. So when the moment came, he did not reach for the coin. He reached for the pen.

The old gods are waking everywhere now. Great ones too—storm-fathers and hearth-mothers, the loud kind, the kind who make demands. They will want temples. They will want thrones. Some of them will look at the valley and see a population ripe for worship, and they will come with contracts dressed as blessings.

When they come, they will find a scribe with no name and a fox with no throne, and between us we will write something they have not seen in nine hundred years: terms.

He thinks he is finished learning. He is not. Tomorrow a delegation arrives from over the western passes—they have heard about the valley where the dead went home, and they are bringing their own ledgers, and their questions will be harder than any the Undercourt ever asked.

I have already sharpened the pen.

He does not know yet. I can't wait to see his face.
