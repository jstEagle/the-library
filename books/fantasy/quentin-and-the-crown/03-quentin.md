## 3. Quentin

I woke to the smell of cold iron and old money.

That combination should have told me everything I needed to know before I opened my eyes. Cold iron means someone wants you contained. Old money means someone wants you contained *politely*, which is worse, because polite people keep receipts.

The vault was round, windowless, and lit by a single lamp burning behind glass — witch-lamp, the kind that eats its own smoke, the kind you only find in places where fire insurance is a moral question rather than a financial one. I was lying on a cot that had been positioned with mathematical precision equidistant from three iron-bound doors. Somebody had thought about that placement. Somebody had measured.

My side hurt in the deep, structural way that means stitches were involved and the stitcher was competent but angry about it. Someone had also taken my boots, my coat, my knife, and — this was the part that stung — the wax-sealed tube from inside my coat, and left in their place a folded blanket, a pitcher of water, and a small card reading, in beautiful copperplate: *Inventory held pending assessment.*

Assessment. Right. I'd been assessed before. It generally ended with me being appraised at less than I cost to maintain.

"Hello?" I said, because the first rule of waking up in a cage is to establish whether your jailers are conversational. Conversational jailers can be bargained with. Silent ones have already done their arithmetic.

The middle door opened without a sound, which told me it was expensive, and the woman from the threshold came through it carrying a tray.

She set the tray on a stool — bread, cheese, an apple, tea in a cup that would have paid for a month of my lodgings — and stepped back to what I would later learn was her habitual distance: close enough to count your teeth, far enough to dodge if you tried anything.

"You bled on my ledger," she said.

"I apologize."

"Don't. It balanced." She said it flatly, like a joke she'd made so many times it had worn smooth, and then ruined the effect by looking at me the way she'd looked at me on the doorstep — not with pity, not with fear, but with the particular attention of someone reading a column of figures and finding one that wouldn't resolve. "You're awake. Good. Name?"

"Quentin."

"That's not a name, that's a syllable wearing a coat. Full name."

"That *is* the full name." I sat up, which my ribs objected to, and took the tea anyway, because refusing tea from your captor is pride, and pride is for people who can afford it. "It's complicated."

"Everything worth counting is complicated," she said. "I keep books for a living. Try me."

Her name, I already knew, was Mara Pemberton. I knew it because I'd spent the last six days learning everything there was to know about the Gildenholt's counting-house, on the theory that if you're going to die somewhere, you should die somewhere you've researched. Mara Pemberton, hedge-trained, self-taught in the guild's arithmetic, keeper of the impossible ledger. The one clerk in Gildenholt whose entries never needed auditing. There were stories about her in the remnant's safehouses — small stories, the kind told over thin soup: *the Pemberton woman can price anything. The Pemberton woman caught a master trader skimming years off the tithe rolls. The Pemberton woman came out of nowhere and no one knows her people.*

No one knows her people. That phrase again. I'd heard it about myself my whole life.

"The map," I said instead of answering. "Where is it?"

"Vault nine. Under triple seal, behind a ward keyed to my blood, in a room that doesn't officially exist." She folded her arms. "Which brings us back to names. Yours, specifically, and why three men with guild badges and one man with no badge at all are currently standing in Mistress Veyra's office explaining, with increasing volume, how a bleeding courier got past the weighing door on closing bell."

"One man with no badge?"

"Tall. Grey coat. Smiled too much. Veyra sent him away, but he smiled the whole way out, which I'm told is his way of filing a complaint."

Ah. Then they'd found me faster than I'd hoped. I drank my tea and did the sums I'd been avoiding since the doorstep. Six days running. Two safehouses burned. Masters dead — all of them, every hand that had signed my commission, every voice that could vouch for me. A map sewn into my coat lining that redrew itself when I bled near it. And now I was in an iron vault belonging to an institution that had spent a century growing fat on the covenant's corpse, while outside that vault waited a smiling man in grey who did not work for the guild and did not need to.

"You should give the map back," I said. "To whoever asks loudest. It's the safest thing you can do."

"Is it?"

"No." I looked at her. "But it's the safest thing *you* can do. Those aren't always the same sentence."

Something moved behind her eyes — recognition, maybe, of a distinction she'd already drawn herself. People who keep ledgers understand that some debts belong to the house and some to the clerk holding the pen, and confusing them is how clerks end up in vaults.

"They killed your masters," she said. Not a question.

"Yes."

"All of them?"

"All of them." I put the cup down. My hands were steady, which surprised me; grief, I've found, saves its shaking for later, when it can ambush you at a decent hour. "House Aldermere. You won't have heard of them. They were careful to be unheard of. They were what remained of the Seelie court's mortal-side household — the ones who kept faith after the Sundering. Couriers, archivists, oath-keepers. For a hundred years we carried messages between what's left of the hidden folk and the few humans who still honored the old terms. Small things. A blessing traded for a bowl of cream. A memory returned to a village that had sold it in a bad winter. Keeping the account open, my mistress used to say, so that someday it might be settled."

"And now?"

"Now the account has one asset outstanding." I tapped my own chest, where the map had lived against my heart for eleven days. "And every creditor in two worlds knows where it sleeps tonight."

* * *

She didn't leave. I'd expected her to leave. Clerks, in my experience, deliver you to their superiors and wash their hands with soap made from the transaction. But Mara Pemberton pulled the stool closer, sat down across from me with her spine straight as a ledger-line, and said, "Start at the beginning. And don't round the numbers."

So I told her.

I told her about the Sundering, a hundred years gone — though "gone" is the wrong word, because nothing about it had finished. The human lords and the hidden folk had sworn a covenant at a stone in the Sundered Marches: rain for luck, luck for memory, memory for time, each realm holding the other's seasons in trust. Mortal hands and fae hands together, or the whole architecture comes down. And then the mortal signatories decided the arrangement was expensive — covenants usually are; that's what makes them covenants — and broke it, quietly, in a single night, with signatures forged and witnesses bought. The stone went dark. The Marches began to bleed. And both realms discovered that a broken promise between worlds doesn't die; it just starts charging interest.

"The guild," I said, watching her face, "was founded to service that covenant. Did you know that?"

"I suspected."

"Its charter is still written in covenant-law. Every bottled storm, every pawned shadow, every year sold back to some desperate farmer — those are fragments. Splinters of the old covenant, traded piecemeal at ruinous markup because the whole instrument stopped trading a century ago. Your masters aren't merchants, Mara. They're scavengers picking over a wreck, and they've convinced themselves they built the ship."

"I know," she said quietly. "I read the founding charter last night. After I hid you. Before I slept, which I didn't."

"You hid me." I stared at her. "Why? You didn't know me. You knew nothing except that I was bleeding and hunted, and you hid me anyway, in the one building in Gildenholt where hiding something guarantees it will eventually be found, priced, and sold."

"You were dying on my doorstep."

"People die on doorsteps all the time. The world considers it weather."

"Well." She looked at her hands. "The world prices things cheaply that I don't."

I want to record, here, honestly, what I felt in that moment, because it matters to what came after. I felt gratitude — real gratitude, the kind that sits in the chest like a coal. And underneath the gratitude, arriving a half-second later the way rot arrives under paint, I felt guilt. Because I knew what I was carrying. I knew what followed it. And I understood, with the clarity of a man reading his own indictment, that by pressing that sealed tube into her hands I had not thanked her. I had invoiced her.

"Mistress Pemberton," I said, "what exactly do you think is on that map?"

"I think it's a map of the Sundered Marches. I think the seal is Seelie wax, which hasn't been lawfully rendered in a century. And I think—" she paused, and I watched her choose precision over comfort, the way she must choose it forty times a day over columns of figures— "I think it's the reason you're alive and everyone you served is dead."

"Yes."

"So tell me what it does."

I told her that too. All of it. That the Covenant Map wasn't ink on vellum but a contract wearing a map's clothing — that it showed, to blood of the right descent, the path to the covenant-stone where the pact could be reforged. That reforging required two hands, as the original had: one mortal-born, one bound to the other side. That the mortal-born hand had to know the old craft — not guild arithmetic, not contract sorcery, but hedge-craft, hearth-craft, the small binding magic of oaths spoken over bread and salt, the magic the first signatories had carried in their kitchens. That the other hand had to carry fae inheritance openly, unhidden, or the stone wouldn't know it.

And then I stopped talking, because I had arrived at the part where I looked at her and said the thing I'd been circling since the doorstep, and saying it felt like handing her a bill.

"Hedge-craft," I said. "Oaths over salt. Binding words. Where did you train, Mara?"

The silence lasted four full seconds. I counted. Counting is what I do when I'm afraid; it's the family trait I got from no family at all.

"Aldous Crane," she said finally. "Seven years. Until I ran."

I had never heard the name. But I heard what it did to her voice — the way a figure does something to a total — and I filed it, the way you file anything that makes a careful person flinch.

"Then I owe you an apology," I said, "and a warning, and probably a grave. Because the second hand the reforging requires — the mortal hand, the hedge-craft hand — I've been looking for it for eleven days. And it's been sitting in a counting-house in Gildenholt, balancing ledgers, the entire time."

* * *

She laughed. Actually laughed — one short, disbelieving note, like a coin dropped on stone.

"No," she said. "No. I fled that man. I built a life out of being *no one*. Do you understand what that costs? Do you know what I traded—"

"Yes," I said. "Believe me. I'm unclaimed by both sides of a war. I know exactly what anonymity costs, because I've never been able to afford it."

That stopped her. We looked at each other across the tray of untouched bread, two people who had each built a fortress out of being uncounted, and I saw her arrive at the same sum I had: that the map hadn't come to her by accident. Maps like that don't fall into hands. They go where the debt directs them, and debts, my dead mistress always said, have better manners than people — they always find the right address.

"If I refuse?" she asked.

"Then the map stays in vault nine until the smiling man in the grey coat finds a way through Veyra's wards, and he will, because he isn't guild and doesn't negotiate, he collects. And then the covenant stays broken, and the Marches keep bleeding, and in another generation there won't be enough luck left in the borderlands to grow a turnip. Also, separately, everyone who ever touched that map gets audited by things that don't use ledgers." I spread my hands. "Refusing is a choice. It's just not a free one. Nothing is. That's rather the point of the whole instrument."

"And if I agree?"

"Then you become the most hunted person in two worlds, and it will be entirely my fault, and I will spend whatever remains of my life trying to balance that entry." I met her eyes. "I'm telling you this now, plainly, before you decide, because the men who killed my masters were very good at making people decide quickly. I won't do their work for them."

Mara Pemberton stood, walked to the door, and stood with her hand on the iron for a long moment. When she turned around, her face had done the thing I would see it do a hundred times in the months ahead: the fear went down and the arithmetic came up, and what remained was neither brave nor calm but simply *resolved*, which is braver than bravery because it's chosen rather than felt.

"The solstice," she said. "On the doorstep you said the debt comes due at solstice."

"Nineteen days."

"Nineteen days." She nodded slowly. "Then here's what happens next. I'm going to walk out of this vault, and I'm going to find out what the guild knows, and what it wants, and what it will charge us for knowing it. And you are going to stay here, behind my ward, and eat that apple, because you cost me four stitches and I intend to see a return on the investment."

"And then?"

"Then," she said, opening the door, "we're going to talk about terms. Real ones. Between equals." A pause, and the ghost of something that was almost a smile. "I've read enough contracts to know they're all lies, Quentin. It's time somebody wrote an honest one."

The door closed. I lay back on the cot, ribs complaining, and stared at the ceiling of the vault where I had woken as a prisoner and was apparently now a partner, and I thought about my dead mistress's last instruction to me, shouted through smoke: *Find the second hand. Trust the one who counts.*

Trust the one who counts.

I ate the apple. Outside the vault, somewhere above, I heard the muffled bell of the Gildenholt marking the hour, and beneath it — faint, patient, entirely unhurried — the sound of footsteps in the corridor that did not belong to Mara Pemberton, and did not hurry, and did not stop.

They passed. They always pass, the first time.

But I lay awake a long while after, doing sums I didn't like, and every total came out the same: nineteen days, two hands, and a debt I had already, without meaning to, laid on a stranger who had never once been given the choice to refuse it.

The guilt, I realized, was going to keep. Debts always do.
