## 4. What the Guild Knows

*Mara*

Veyra sent for me at the third bell of morning, which in guild time meant she had already been awake for two days and expected everyone else to apologize for it.

I climbed the stairs to her office with my hands steady and my stomach doing arithmetic it didn't like. The counting-house below was already humming—clerks calling out exchange rates on moonlight and memory, an appraiser arguing with a client over whether a grandmother's blessing had appreciated or depreciated since last quarter. Normal sounds. Sounds that said nothing was wrong. I let them settle over me like a coat.

The door to Veyra's office stood open. It always stood open. That was not hospitality; that was theater. Veyra Ashgrave believed a closed door invited speculation, and speculation was the one commodity the Gildenholt never traded in—officially.

"Sit," she said, without looking up from a ledger that was not one of ours. I recognized the binding. Foreign make. Fae-tanned hide, if I wasn't mistaken, which I usually wasn't.

I sat. The chair across from her desk was comfortable in the way expensive things are comfortable when they want something from you.

"You hid a man in Vault Nine last night," she said.

"I inventoried him," I said. "There's a difference. Inventory is temporary."

That got her eyes off the ledger. Veyra was sixty if she was a day and looked carved rather than aged, all planes and patience, silver hair pinned back so severely it seemed less a hairstyle than a sentence being served. Her eyes were the gray of coin worn smooth by too many hands.

"Inventoried," she repeated.

"He arrived bleeding against our threshold at closing bell carrying a sealed map and a debt statement. Under guild charter, section eleven, any asset presented at the weighing door before final bell enters guild custody pending appraisal." I recited it flatly, because flatness was armor and I'd been wearing it since I was nine years old. "He arrived four minutes before final bell."

Something moved at the corner of her mouth. Not quite approval. Approval from Veyra was a full transaction with signatures. This was more like interest accruing.

"And where is this asset now?"

"Asleep. Sedated, bandaged, and behind a ward I paid for out of my own pocket, because the infirmary charges by the hour and he hasn't got an account." I paused. "Neither do I, technically. But you know what I mean."

"I know exactly what you mean, Pemberton. It's why you're still employed." She closed the foreign ledger and laced her fingers over it. "Tell me about the map."

And there it was. The question underneath the question, the way there always was with Veyra. I could have lied. I considered lying. But Veyra Ashgrave had been reading ledgers since before my mother was born, and people who lie to accountants are simply donating information on an installment plan.

"It's wax-sealed. Old seal—I don't recognize the house mark, but the wax has iron filings in it, which means it was meant to keep fae hands off as much as human ones. He called it a covenant map. He said—" I made myself say it plainly, because plainly was how numbers went and numbers didn't flinch. "He said the debt comes due at solstice."

The silence that followed had weight. I felt it settle on my shoulders like wet wool.

Veyra rose, crossed to the window, and looked down at the counting-house floor through the glass panel set into it—a habit of hers, watching the clerks move like pieces on a board she'd designed. When she spoke again, her voice had changed register. Less mistress, more historian. Or confessor.

"Do you know what the Gildenholt was founded to do, Pemberton?"

"Trade in impossible things."

"That's what we do now. What we were *founded* to do was service a contract." She turned. "One hundred and twelve years ago, there was a covenant between the human realm and the hidden folk. A treaty of exchange. They gave us luck, rain, long harvests, the small mercies that don't have names. We gave them—well. It varied. Stories. First fruits. Sometimes names, freely surrendered and freely returned. The point was never the goods. The point was the *flow*. Two worlds trading across a border, each keeping the other honest."

"And then?"

"And then a group of merchants realized that a broken covenant is more profitable than a working one." Her voice stayed level, but I heard the rust under it, old and bitter. "When the flow stops, everything the two worlds used to trade becomes scarce. And scarcity, Pemberton, is the mother of price. Bottled storms. Borrowed years. Pawned shadows. Every miracle on our inventory exists because someone, a century ago, cut the supply line and started selling the reserves."

I sat very still. I am good at sitting still. It was the first thing Crane ever beat into me, and the only lesson of his I've kept out of spite.

"You're telling me the guild broke it."

"I'm telling you the guild's founders broke it. Seven signatories. Their names are in the charter vault under three seals, and I will deny to my grave whatever you think I just implied about whether I've read them." She came back to the desk but didn't sit. "The covenant's been bleeding out for a hundred years. Both worlds. The Marches starve on our side; their side goes strange and hungry in ways your ledgers can't render. And every year the debt compounds, because a broken oath isn't a dead thing, Pemberton. It's an outstanding balance. And balances come due."

"Solstice," I said.

"Solstice," she agreed. "Which brings us to the man in Vault Nine."

Here is what I understood, in that moment, with the terrible clarity I usually reserved for finding errors in other people's sums: Veyra had not sent for me because Quentin was a problem. She'd sent for me because he was an *opportunity*, and she wanted me to appraise it before anyone else did.

"What are you offering?" I asked. Because with Veyra, there was always an offer. She didn't threaten; she quoted.

"Protection. Full guild sanctuary for the courier. Warding, legal cover, medical care, immunity from any claimant—including, should it arise, certain parties who may come asking." Something flickered across her face at that, quick as a miscounted column. She knew about Crane already, then. Of course she did. "In exchange, the map comes into guild custody, and the courier signs on as guild property for the duration of the recovery."

"The duration of the recovery," I repeated.

"Of whatever the map leads to. Reforging a covenant is a venture, Pemberton. Ventures need backing. Backing needs collateral." She slid a document across the desk. "Standard terms. Have your look. You're the only person in this building who actually reads them."

That last part was a compliment, and compliments from Veyra were like coins from a pickpocket—you checked your pockets after.

I read. I read the way she'd taught me to read, in the early years, before I understood that teaching me to find traps was also teaching me they existed: line by line, clause by clause, trusting nothing that sounded generous.

Most of it was standard. Sanctuary, surety, indemnity. And then, page six, subsection four, in type small enough to require squinting:

*...the undersigned party, herein termed the Bearer, together with all instruments, maps, inheritances, claims, and latent attributes held or embodied by the Bearer, shall be deemed held in trust by the Gildenholt for the term of the venture, such trust to include, without limitation, any name, title, or standing recovered, reforged, or revealed in the course thereof...*

Latent attributes. Recovered names. I read it twice to be sure, because I wanted to be wrong.

"Veyra," I said carefully, "if this map leads to a reforged covenant—and if the man carrying it turns out to have some claim or standing on the other side of the border—then per this clause, the guild owns the outcome. Not facilitates. Owns."

"The guild *secures* the outcome," she said smoothly. "Ownership is such an ugly word."

"It's a precise word. Precision is my trade." I set the paper down and looked at her, and for once I didn't flatten my voice. "You're not offering him sanctuary. You're offering to buy him at the bottom of the market, before anyone else knows what he's worth. You've been waiting a hundred years for someone to walk through that door holding the other half of your founders' crime, and now here he is, bleeding on the threshold, and you want me to do the paperwork."

The silence stretched. Veyra studied me the way she studied a suspicious entry—not angry, not offended. Recalculating.

"You could have missed that clause," she said at last. "Most would have."

"I know."

"That's why I wrote it where you'd find it." She picked the document back up, squared its edges. "Understand me, Pemberton. I am not my predecessors. I have no love for what the founders did—but I have obligations. Seven thousand guild members. A building full of assets that become worthless the moment both worlds go to open war over an unpaid century of debt. If the covenant comes due at solstice and nobody holds the paper, the collapse takes this city down with it. I am trying to *manage* an apocalypse within acceptable risk parameters. Forgive me if my instruments are sharp."

"And if managing it means owning the only bridge between two worlds?"

"Then the bridge gets maintained," she said, "which is more than it would get from either side otherwise. Bridges belong to someone, Pemberton. That's why they don't fall down."

I thought about that all the way down the stairs, and I hated that some cold accountant corner of me saw the sense in it. That was the worst thing about prices: once you learn to see them, you see them everywhere, even on the things you love.

*Quentin*

The vault smelled of cedar and cold iron, and waking up in it was like waking inside a locked thought.

I lay still for a while, taking inventory of myself—the way you do when you're not sure yet what shape the day will take. Ribs: cracked, bound, complaining. Left arm: stitched, thanks to someone with small, precise hands. Thirst: considerable. Everything else: present, more or less. More or less was my lifelong condition.

The woman came back at what I guessed was midday, carrying water and bread and an expression like a woman who'd spent the morning arguing with a superior and won on points.

"Mara," she said, by way of re-introduction. "You were delirious last night. In case you said anything embarrassing."

"Define embarrassing."

"You asked if the ceiling was load-bearing and then apologized to it."

"That tracks," I admitted, and drank the water. It was good water. Cold, clean, unclaimed. On the far side of the border, water always wanted something. "How bad is it out there?"

"How bad do you think? The guild knows you're here. My mistress knows what you're carrying—or enough of it. And she's drafted terms for your protection that include a clause giving the Gildenholt ownership of, quote, any name, title, or standing recovered in the course of the venture." She watched my face while she said it, and I knew why. She was checking whether the clause had teeth.

It had teeth. I kept my face still anyway. Old skill. When you grow up belonging to no one in particular, you learn to keep your expressions out of the negotiation.

"So she knows," I said.

"She knows the covenant's broken, that it comes due at solstice, and that you're the walking collateral. She does not know what you are." Mara crouched to my eye level, and up close her eyes were the brown of well-kept leather—worn, practical, harder than they looked. "Which brings us to the question I've been chewing on since dawn. The map responded to your blood last night. I saw it redraw itself. Hedge-craft has a word for things that happen around a person's blood, and most of those words are bad."

I could have lied to her. I'd lied to better interrogators on worse mornings. But she'd hidden me from the enforcers, sedated me with her own supplies, paid for my warding out of her own pocket—and more than that, she'd found the ownership clause. Nobody finds the ownership clause. People spend lifetimes not finding ownership clauses.

"My mother was of the hidden folk," I said. "My father was a ferryman on the Marches. Neither world signed for the result." I turned my left forearm over, where the stitches were. Beneath them, faint as watermark, the skin carried the old shimmer that showed in certain lights—the light of twilight markets, of solstice gates, of vaults warded with cold iron, apparently. "Half-blood. Unclaimed. My name's been held in trust since I was born, by people who are now dead, which means legally speaking I'm a debt with legs."

Mara didn't flinch. I'd expected flinching. Half-fae got you flinching in most company—fear on the human side, contempt on the other.

"A debt with legs," she repeated. "I've audited worse."

Despite everything—the ribs, the vault, the hunters I could feel circling somewhere beyond the wards like weather coming in—I laughed. It hurt. Worth it.

"The men who killed my masters," I said, when I could speak evenly again, "were creditors. Seelie remnant, hardline faction—they call themselves the Reclamation. They believe the covenant was stolen from their side, and they intend to collect the whole arrears at solstice, in full, with interest. Blood interest. The map shows where it can be reforged instead—reforged properly, so neither side collects and both sides pay. I've run it north twice. First time, I lost my escort. Second time, I lost everyone." I looked down at my hands. Courier's hands, scarred, ordinary-looking until the light caught them wrong. "The reforging needs two hands, they told me. One mortal-born. One bound to the other side. I'm the second hand. I've been looking for the first."

"And you walked into a counting-house," Mara said slowly, "and bled on the floor of the one guild in the realm founded on the covenant's corpse."

"I was dying. My criteria were flexible."

She sat back on her heels. I watched her do the thing she clearly did with everything—turn it over, weigh it, find its price. I wondered what I cost. I wondered if I wanted to know.

"Your blood opened the map," she said. "Mine might close it. There are hedge-rites for sealing, if the seal wants a mortal hand. Which means—" she exhaled through her nose, annoyed at her own conclusion, "—means I'm not the clerk in this story anymore. Damn it. I liked being the clerk. Clerks survive."

"Clarks survive," I agreed. "But somebody has to carry the ledger."

"Nobody has ever died saying that to me before," she said, "but I have a feeling you're going to be a first of many."

She gathered the tray, stood, paused at the vault door with her hand on the iron.

"For what it's worth," she said, not turning around, "I refused her terms. The guild's. I told her the fine print smelled like Crane's ink." Now she turned, and there was something in her face I couldn't price—something that hadn't been there yesterday. "So you're not guild property yet. Which means you're nobody's. Which means, as of this morning, you're the freest man in this building. Try not to die of it."

The door shut. The ward hummed.

I lay back against the cedar shelving and thought about what I'd just done—told a stranger the whole shape of me, the half-blood truth I'd kept folded away through two years of running. It should have felt like loss. Secrets are the only currency I've never owed anyone, and I'd just spent a fistful of mine on a hedge-witch clerk with ink-stained fingers and a grudge against fine print.

But it didn't feel like loss. It felt like the first honest entry in a very long ledger.

Then the ward hum changed pitch, and the temperature dropped, and I knew—even before the bells started ringing above us, fast and wrong—that somewhere below, at the weighing door, someone had arrived to make a claim on me. Someone whose claim, unlike Veyra's, came with hounds.

Through the vault door, muffled by stone and iron, I heard Mara's voice, tight and cold as a balanced column:

"He's here, isn't he. Crane."
