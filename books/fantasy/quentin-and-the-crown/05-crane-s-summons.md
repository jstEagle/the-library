## 5. Crane's Summons

The summons came at dawn, which I took as a bad omen. Nothing good has ever arrived in this city before the second bell. Dawn deliveries are for debts, deaths, and tax assessors, and the Gildenholt's morning runner carried all three flavors of trouble folded into a single gray envelope.

*Master Aldous Crane requests audience with the Counting-House regarding an outstanding claim of indenture,* the note read, in handwriting so precise it looked machine-made, each letter standing at attention like a soldier who had never once been permitted to slouch.

I knew that hand. I had spent six years copying it under threat of the strap. Crane believed—correctly, as it happened—that a person's penmanship was a window into their soul, and he had spent those same six years grinding mine into something legible and obedient and nothing like my own. When I fled his house at nineteen, the first thing I did with my freedom was learn to write like myself again: looser, faster, with a slight leftward lean, like a woman walking out a door without looking back.

I held the envelope over the counting-house brazier and watched the ink curl. Then I went to find Veyra, because apparently we were going to receive him anyway.

"He's coming regardless," she said when I asked why we'd invite a man like that past our threshold. "Guild escort, guild seal, guild protection. He filed his claim through proper channels, and the Gildenholt honors proper channels. It's rather the whole point of us."

"The whole point of you," I said, "is impossible things. A man claiming ownership of a living soul is not impossible. It's merely old."

Veyra smiled her ledger-smile, the one she used when a number was about to get worse. "Old things are the most profitable kind."

* * *

I spent the hours before his arrival doing what I always do with dread: converting it into arithmetic. Fear is unmanageable in its raw state—it sloshes around inside you like bilge water—but reduce it to columns and it holds still long enough to be examined. So I sat at my desk and made a list.

Item: Aldous Crane, hedge-witch, licensed practitioner of the minor arts, holder of three apprenticeship bonds currently active, four lapsed, and one escaped (mine; outstanding; he had never stopped counting the years I owed him).

Item: his claim on Quentin. Filed yesterday, per the runner's gossip, citing an indenture drawn up—this was the part that made me put down my pen and stare at the wall—in *the year the covenant broke*. A century-old contract, dusted off like a bottle of wine someone had been saving for exactly this occasion.

Item: how does a man come to hold a century-old indenture on a courier half his apparent age? Either the document was fraudulent, or Quentin's people had signed something once and Crane had bought the paper from whatever estate sold it. Both answers were ugly. Ugly answers usually being the true ones, I prepared for both.

And item, the one I wrote smallest because writing it large would make it real: *he is coming here because of what I did.* I had hidden Quentin in the vaults. I had lied in the ledgers—I was getting better at lying in the ledgers, which is the sort of skill improvement one hopes never to need—and somewhere between the weighing door and the tithe auction, word had crawled back to Crane that his runaway apprentice was entangled with a piece of property he claimed. He wasn't coming for Quentin. Or rather, he was coming for Quentin the way a collector comes for a coin he has discovered sits in another man's pocket next to something *he* lost.

Me. I was the thing he'd lost. And Aldous Crane had never in his life forgiven a loss. He entered every absence in a book and waited, sometimes decades, for the account to come due.

I knew this because I had been an entry in that book.

* * *

He arrived at the fourth bell, under escort of two guild enforcers who looked less like guards than like furniture that had learned to walk. Crane himself came through the weighing door the way weather comes through a broken window—inevitably, and making everything colder.

He hadn't changed. That was the first cruelty of it. Men who traffic in years ought to show the spending, but Crane had spent other people's time for so long that his own had gone untouched, hoarded like coins in a miser's mattress. Same iron-gray beard trimmed to the width of a rule. Same coat, or its identical successor, buttoned to the throat against a warmth he refused to acknowledge on principle. Same eyes, pale as vinegar, that performed a full inventory of any room he entered and priced everything in it, including the people.

Especially the people.

He found me within four seconds. Of course he did. I was behind my desk, which was where I had chosen to be—not hiding, I told myself, *stationed*, though the distinction felt thinner than ledger paper—and his gaze crossed the room, weighed the enforcers, dismissed Veyra's greeting as overhead cost, and settled on me with the particular satisfaction of a man confirming that an asset he'd written off had appreciated after all.

"Mara." He said my name the way he'd said it a thousand times in the old house: correctly, completely, and with no warmth whatsoever, a name pronounced like a figure entered in a column. "You've landed on your feet. The Gildenholt. Ambitious of you."

"Employed," I said. "There's a difference."

"There is," he agreed, "and it's measured in wages, which are taxable, whereas apprentices are capital." He turned to Veyra as if I had already left the conversation, which, in his accounting, I suppose I had. "Mistress Veyra. You hold, knowingly or otherwise, property of mine. A young man, half-blooded, taken in off your threshold some nights ago bleeding. I have the paper. I have the seal. I have, if it becomes necessary, the law."

"You have a claim," Veyra said. "Claims require settlement."

"All things require settlement," Crane said. "That is the one lesson this century has taught me worth keeping." And then, God help me, he sat down across from her and began to bargain.

* * *

I want to describe it accurately, because there is a particular sickness in watching your own history repeat itself in front of you while you sit close enough to take notes. That is literally what I did. I took notes. It was either that or put my hands around his throat, and I have always known which impulse wins in a room where he holds the paper.

He bargained the way he taught me to keep books: coldly, precisely, with every concession pre-priced and every mercy converted to terms. Quentin, he said, was bound by an indenture executed by his mortal parent—a father, presumably, since the fae side came from elsewhere—who had sold the boy's service for a debt of healing. Crane had cured the man of something terminal. The price was the child's labor until majority, and then, per a clause I remembered well because a version of it had governed my own life, *renewal at the master's discretion.*

Renewal at discretion. There it was. The sentence that had hung over my entire adolescence like a blade hung by a hair. You could serve your term perfectly, flawlessly, and still wake on the morning after your majority to find your master had renewed you—for cause, for convenience, for no stated reason at all, because the clause required none. I had fled two days before my own renewal date. I have sometimes wondered whether Crane ever worked out that I understood the arithmetic better than he thought. Whether he knew I ran not from the work but from the fine print.

He certainly suspected I could read it now. Every few minutes his eyes flicked to me while Veyra parried, checking his figures against my face, and I gave him nothing. Blank as an unaudited page. Inside, I was reciting multiplication tables to keep the terror at a manageable volume, because the terror was there, fully intact, six years older and no smaller—the dry mouth, the calculating hands, the way my body remembered being sixteen and learning that the person who fed you also owned the right to beat you, and saw no contradiction between these duties.

But something else was there too, sitting beside the terror like a guest at the same table. Clarity. Cold, hard, almost luxurious clarity. For the first time in my life I was watching Crane operate from the far side of the desk, with power of my own, small but real, and I could see his method whole. Before, I had only ever seen it from underneath, where it looks like weather—pressure, mood, the sudden storms of his temper. From beside, it looks like what it actually is: arithmetic. Everything is arithmetic. His rages were budget overruns. His mercies were investments. His affection, on the rare occasions he manufactured any, was a retention strategy for assets too valuable to lose to flight.

And Quentin—he was bargaining over Quentin the way he had once bargained over me, except now I could watch the numbers move. He opened high: the indenture plus penalties for the years of evasion, plus costs, plus interest compounded annually at a rate he quoted without shame. Veyra countered low. They met somewhere in the middle, as such things do, and the middle was a human being.

"I'll want access," Crane said, in the tone of a man discussing warehouse rights. "The asset will return to my custody, but I'm prepared to leave him registered here for the duration of—" a pause, delicately theatrical, "—whatever enterprise brought him to your door. In exchange, the Gildenholt waives its harboring liability, and I extend to the guild a ten percent preference on all hedge-craft contracts south of the river for five years."

Ten percent. Five years. That was the price of a soul in this room, and both parties at the table considered it a fair opening position.

"And the apprentice?" Veyra asked, and my stomach dropped through the floor, because I knew—knew with the certainty of a woman who has audited enough contracts to smell a rider before it's spoken—that he had saved me for last on purpose. Crane always saved the personal item for last. It kept the counterparty off balance. It had kept *me* off balance for six years.

"What apprentice?" he said pleasantly. "My records show an escaped bond. Outstanding. Eleven years, four months, and—" he consulted a slip of paper he had brought for exactly this moment, because of course he had, "—seventeen days, as of yesterday. With interest, the sum owed comes to a figure I'd be happy to waive. As a gesture. In the spirit of settlement."

Waive. As a gesture. He was offering to forgive my debt—the debt he had invented, inflated, and pursued across six years—in exchange for what, he did not yet say. He didn't need to say it. The unsaid part sat in the room like a third negotiator. He wanted the map. Or the boy delivered compliant. Or me, returned, publicly, walking back through his door of my own feet, because a runaway who returns validates the system that broke her, and there was nothing in this world Aldous Crane valued more than validation of the system.

I set down my pen. I stood up. My legs did the thing they do, the old trembling, and I let them, because hiding it had never once worked and I was tired of paying that particular interest.

"No," I said.

Crane turned. Looked at me. And smiled, and it was the smile I remembered best of all his smiles, the patient one, the one that said *you misunderstand your position, but I can wait.*

"Mara," he said gently, "you aren't party to this negotiation."

"No," I agreed. "I'm collateral. I've been collateral since I was thirteen. But I keep the books of this house, Master Crane, and I am telling you, as the only person in this room who has lived under your paper: the boy isn't yours. The indenture you're holding was executed by a dead man against a child who had no say in it, renewed by clauses written to be unescapable, purchased secondhand from grief. I know the instrument. I *was* the instrument. And I am telling Mistress Veyra—" I turned to her, and my voice did not shake, which surprised us both, "—that whatever she settles today, she should read the renewal clause twice. Because it doesn't end. That's the design. It never ends. Not for me, not for him, not for anyone whose name gets written into it."

Silence. The good kind, the kind where everyone recalculates.

Then Crane, unhurried, gathered his papers, squared their edges against the desk until they aligned like soldiers, and rose. "Sentiment," he said, to Veyra, to the room, to me. "An unsecured loan against future returns. I've extended credit on worse." He paused at the door, and gave me one last look—not anger; anger would have meant I mattered emotionally, and I had learned long ago that I only ever mattered to him numerically. "You have until solstice, mistress. After that, I file in chancery, and chancery does not negotiate. Enjoy the interim."

And then he was through the weighing door, and the air he had displaced slowly warmed again, and I stood at my desk with my hands flat on the wood and shook for a full minute, quietly, like a building settling after a tremor.

* * *

That evening I went down to the vaults. I told myself it was to check the inventory. It was mostly to check on Quentin, though I framed even that in bookkeeping language, because apparently I could not stop translating my own heart into entries.

He was awake. Sitting on a crate of bottled fog, eating bread I'd smuggled down at noon, looking better than a man had any right to look after nearly dying on our doorstep. I told him what had happened upstairs. All of it. I don't know why I told him all of it—there were reasons of operational security, certainly, he needed to know his former owner had found him—but some of it I told because he needed to hear what I'd said in that room, and maybe I needed to hear myself say it outside that room, where it couldn't be repossessed.

"So he thinks he owns you," Quentin said. "Through paper."

"Through paper, through precedent, through eleven years of compound interest on a debt he invented." I sat down on the crate opposite him. "He'll file in chancery at solstice unless something changes the arithmetic."

"Then we change the arithmetic," he said, simply, as if it were easy, as if men like Crane were weather and not men.

I laughed—a short, startled laugh, the kind that escapes before permission. "You sound like me, six years ago, the night before I ran. I had a plan too. It was a good plan. It worked. And I have spent every year since learning that escaping a cage and being free are different line items entirely."

Quentin looked at me for a long moment. "What's the difference?"

"The escape is an event," I said. "Freedom is a practice. Daily. Like prayer, if prayer involved double-entry."

He smiled, and there was something in it that made me want to warn him about something I couldn't yet name. Then he said the thing that rearranged my evening entirely:

"The map moved today. While you were upstairs. I didn't touch it."

I felt the cold arrive ahead of my understanding, the way frost crosses a window. "Moved where?"

"Toward the Marches," he said. "It's redrawing itself. And Mara—it's drawing *him*. There's a mark on it now, a new one, near the southern edge. Your master's route north passes directly through it."

We sat in the lamplight of the vault, two fugitives and a map that had begun making its own decisions, and I did the only thing I knew how to do with news that bad.

I opened a fresh page, and I started keeping score.
