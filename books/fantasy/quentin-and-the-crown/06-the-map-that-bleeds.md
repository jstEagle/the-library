## 6. The Map That Bleeds

I woke to the sound of a quill scratching, and for one merciful moment I didn't remember where I was or what I was running from. Then the cold of the vault floor came up through my back like a creditor's hand, and I remembered everything.

Mara Pemberton sat three feet away with a ledger open across her knees, writing by lamplight with the fierce concentration of a woman defusing something. She'd been at it when I slept, I realized. She'd been keeping watch and keeping accounts at the same time, because apparently that was simply how she was built.

"You're writing about me," I said. My voice came out like gravel in a sack.

"I'm writing about *inventory*," she said, not looking up. "One map, wax-sealed, self-updating. One courier, unconscious, bleeding through his shirt. Both currently unaccounted for on any ledger in this building, which means as far as the Gildenholt is concerned, neither of you exists." She turned the page. "I find that restful."

"Most people would find it terrifying."

"Most people have never read a guild contract all the way through." She finally looked up, and her eyes went straight to my side, where the bandage she'd tied around my ribs had soaked through again. "You're bleeding."

"It's a hobby."

"It's a *problem*." She set down the quill and crossed to me with the brisk, unsentimental efficiency of someone who had treated wounds before, probably her own. "Hold still."

I held still. There was something about being handled by someone who clearly expected nothing from me but cooperation that made argument feel pointless. She peeled back the bandage, clicked her tongue at what she found, and reached for the little witch-kit she'd apparently assembled from vault stores—clean linen, a brown glass bottle, a needle threaded with something that wasn't quite thread.

"This will sting."

"Everything does."

She cleaned the wound without ceremony. It was a long gash below my ribs, courtesy of a hound that hadn't been a dog and a night I'd rather not relive, and it had been weeping for two days in a way wounds shouldn't. Not red, exactly. The blood that came away on her cloth was dark, almost violet at the edges, catching the lamplight wrong.

Mara stopped moving.

I watched her look at it. Watched her mind do whatever it did—sorting, pricing, balancing—and arrive at an answer she didn't like.

"That's not human blood," she said.

"No," I said.

* * *

Here is what I should have told her, in order: that I am half-fae; that my mother was of the hidden folk and my father was a courier who died owing them nothing, which among their kind is practically sainthood; that my name is held in trust by the dead who raised me; that the Seelie remnant took me in because a half-blood can walk both roads and belong to neither, which makes him ideal for carrying things no one else can touch.

Here is what I actually said:

"It's complicated."

Mara tied off the bandage with a yank that suggested her patience for complicated had limits. "Everything is complicated. Blood is simple. It's either yours or it isn't, and that—" she flicked the stained cloth into the brazier, where it flared green for a heartbeat before dying— "isn't yours. Or it isn't only yours."

"The map," I said, because it seemed safer than the truth. "You said it moved."

"It's still moving." She retrieved the map from its hiding place beneath a false bottom in the shelving and unrolled it between us. And there it was—the thing I had been dreading since the moment I first understood what I carried.

The Sundered Marches had never been on this map. That was the point of it, the craft of it: the covenant lands were hidden even from those who sought them, rendered only as a blank space at the northern edge, a white silence where the world had been torn. But now the blank space was shrinking. Ink crawled outward from its borders like frost reversing itself, sketching hills I recognized with a sick lurch—the Ashfall road, the drowned bell-tower at Merrow's Crossing, the black line of the Thornwood. The map was remembering the Marches. And at the center of the redrawn territory, a mark was forming, fine and deliberate, like a signature.

"It started when you arrived," Mara said quietly. "Or when you bled on it. Which was it?"

"Both. Neither." I pressed my palm flat against my knee to stop it shaking. "It responds to fae blood. That's how it navigates. The old couriers called it *following your nose home*." I laughed, and it came out wrong. "Except I've never been home anywhere in my life, so mostly it just follows me."

Mara studied me the way she must study a suspicious entry in a ledger—not with horror, not with pity, but with the particular attention of someone determining whether the numbers add up.

"How much fae?" she asked.

"Half. On my mother's side." The words felt strange in my mouth, too large, like coins I'd never spent. "She died when I was small. My father died before that. The remnant raised me—or housed me. There's a difference."

"And your name?"

The question landed harder than it should have. I looked at the map instead of at her, at the crawling ink, at the mark that was unmistakably, impossibly, *me*.

"Held in trust," I said. "By the dead. It's a fae custom. When someone of the blood dies young, they can leave their name to a child—not given, you understand, just *held*, the way a bank holds collateral. You live nameless until you've earned it back, or until someone gives you one freely, which never happens, because names are the most expensive thing there is." I shrugged, and immediately regretted it, because my ribs objected. "So I go by Quentin. Quentin is a courier's designation. It means *the one who carries*. It's not a name. It's a job description."

Silence. The lamp hissed. Somewhere above us, the counting-house settled and groaned like an old ship.

"So you're telling me," Mara said slowly, "that you are half of a people whose entire economy runs on names and debts, and you have neither."

"When you put it that way—"

"—you're the only person in this building poorer than I am."

I blinked. Then, despite everything—the wound, the hunt, the blank space growing on the map—I laughed. It hurt, and I laughed anyway, and after a moment something that was almost a smile crossed Mara Pemberton's face before she smothered it under professional gravity.

"Don't mistake me for a friend," she said. "I'm assessing risk."

"Of course."

"But if we're going to survive whatever this is—" she tapped the map— "I need to know what you are. All of it. Because right now you're a variable I can't price, and unpriced variables get people killed."

So I told her. Not all at once, and not gracefully—I told it the way you dig a splinter out, in pieces, wincing. I told her about the Seelie remnant, the last court-in-exile, keepers of the broken covenant's terms. About the night they were slaughtered, and how I'd survived because I'd been out making a delivery, and how the killers had left me alive deliberately, because a bearer who runs draws pursuers away from what he runs *toward*. I told her about the map, sealed in wax that only breaks for fae blood, keyed to my veins specifically, useless to anyone who might steal it—which meant that killing me wouldn't help them. They needed me alive and bleeding, on schedule, at solstice.

"They don't want the covenant reforged," I said. "Whoever broke it a century ago has spent a hundred years profiting from the breach. The guild sells bottled storms because the Marches can't make their own rain. Someone pawned the luck of whole villages. Every impossible thing in Veyra's inventory upstairs is a symptom, and the cure puts the Gildenholt out of business."

Mara was quiet for a long time. When she spoke, her voice had gone flat and careful, the voice of someone reading fine print.

"And the reforging needs two hands. One mortal-born. One bound to the other side." Her eyes came up to mine. "That's why you looked at me like that. At the door. When you grabbed my wrist."

"You have hedge-witch training. Old-line. Crane's line, specifically, and Crane's line descends from the original covenant-witnesses. Your hands would work." I hesitated. "I'm sorry. I didn't choose you. The map chose the route, and you were standing on it."

Something moved behind her eyes—hurt, maybe, or its close cousin, anger—but she mastered it the way she mastered everything, filed it somewhere with a label on it.

"Well," she said. "At least someone around here knows what I'm worth."

"That's not—"

"Quentin." She said my false name gently, and somehow that was worse than if she'd said it cruelly. "I spent six years being told what I was worth, and it was always less than what I cost. Don't apologize for pricing me accurately. Just—" she exhaled— "don't lie to me about the number."

* * *

We sat with the map between us while the lamp burned low, and I learned something about watching someone think. Mara thought with her whole body—quill tapping, lips moving faintly around silent arithmetic, one finger tracing lines on the parchment as though she could audit geography.

"The mark in the south," she said at last. "The new one. It appeared today?"

"Veyra says so."

"And your blood is on this map. Has been since the door." She looked up sharply. "Which means the map knows where you are. Does it know where *everything* is? Everything of the blood?"

The question I'd been dreading. I nodded.

"Then whoever holds this map holds a compass pointed at every surviving creature of fae descent within a hundred leagues." She said it without drama, just laying the fact on the table like a coin. "Including you. Including—" her jaw tightened— "whatever Crane sent north. If his route crosses the Marches, and the map marks him..."

"Then the map thinks he matters," I finished. "To the covenant. To the debt coming due." I rubbed my face. "There's something else. Something I haven't said, because saying it makes it real."

Mara waited. She was good at waiting. It occurred to me that she'd probably spent years being interrupted, talked over, priced cheap, and had built this patience out of sheer spite.

"My mother didn't just leave me nameless," I said. "She left me a claim. Half-bloods can't hold fae land—that's law older than the covenant—but a full claim can be *carried* through us, held in trust, passed on. The remnant were going to use mine. The reforging requires a stake in both worlds, and my mother's claim is the last unbroken stake on the fae side." I looked down at my hands, at the violet-dark blood crusted under the nails. "I'm not a courier. I'm a *vessel*. They dressed it up as employment, but I was cargo. The most valuable thing I own is the thing I'm least allowed to touch."

"And the people who slaughtered them—"

"Want the vessel intact and empty. Deliver me to the stone at solstice, drain the claim, reforge nothing, and the breach stays profitable forever." I smiled, and felt how thin it was. "My masters' ghosts trusted me with the most important delivery of the century, and the honest truth, Mara, is that I don't know if I'm carrying it or *am* it."

For once, she didn't answer quickly. The lamp guttered, and in the shifting light her face was all planes and shadow, a woman deciding something.

"Here's what I know about ledgers," she said finally. "An asset that doesn't know its own value gets sold short. Every time. By everyone." She rolled the map closed with brisk, decisive movements. "So we're going to establish value. Yours. Precisely. What you carry, what you cost, what you're owed—and then nobody, not Veyra, not Crane, not the ghosts of two courts, prices you but us."

"*Us*," I repeated.

"Working association," she said, with the tone of someone nailing a clause shut. "Provisional. Revocable. Witnessed by nobody, because everyone who could witness it wants us dead." She stood, gathered her kit, and paused at the edge of the lamplight. "Get some sleep, Quentin-the-designation. Tomorrow I teach you double-entry, and you teach me everything the remnant knew about solstice. We'll balance as we go."

She climbed the stairs, and I lay back on the cold floor of the vault, alone with a map that remembered a home I'd never seen and a body that belonged, piece by piece, to everyone but me.

But under the fear, quieter than the fear, something else had taken root—a small, stubborn, unfamiliar thing.

Nobody had ever offered to keep my books before.

I was still turning that over when I heard it: footsteps above, more than one pair, and Mara's voice pitched low and sharp through the floorboards—*"He's awake? Then tell me why the mistress's enforcers are asking which vault."*

The map, I thought, had already chosen its next route.

And it ran straight through trouble.
