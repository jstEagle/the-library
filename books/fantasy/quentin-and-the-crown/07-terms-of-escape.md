## 7. Terms of Escape

There is a particular kind of arithmetic that only liars and bookkeepers understand, which is that the two professions are separated by nothing but a signature.

I had been a bookkeeper for six years. I had been a liar for exactly one night, and already I was better at it than I'd expected, which said something about my character that I preferred not to examine too closely.

The Gildenholt's tithe auction fell on the last market-day of the month, when the guild opened its counting-house floor to buyers of the strange and desperate. Bottled thunder went under the hammer. Pawned shadows, redeemed or forfeit. Years sold by the season, names held in escrow against debts their owners could never hope to clear. I had kept the ledgers for all of it—every lot, every lien, every sorrow with a serial number—and in six years I had never once altered an entry.

Tonight I would alter forty-one of them.

I sat at my desk in the corner of the counting-house where the lamplight didn't quite reach, and I did what I did best: I made the numbers tell a story that wasn't true.

* * *

The plan, such as it was, had come to me the way most of my plans came—not as inspiration but as reconciliation. Somewhere between the assets column and the liabilities column there was always a gap, and gaps were where things could be moved if you were careful enough to make them look like they'd never moved at all.

Quentin was, on paper, a problem. A man who had collapsed bleeding against our threshold carried no entry in any ledger. Unrecorded persons were worse than liabilities; they were anomalies, and anomalies attracted auditors, and the Gildenholt's auditor was a woman named Mistress Veyra who had eyes like a scale that always found your weight wanting.

So the first forgery was simple, almost gentle. Quentin became a consignment.

Lot 114: *One courier, bound service, provenance Seelie remnant, condition damaged but restorable.* I wrote him into the auction manifest in my own hand, in the same ink, with the same slight rightward slant the guild's registrar used when she was tired, which she always was by the third bell. A consigned lot could be withdrawn by its consignor before the hammer fell. All I needed was a consignor.

That was the second forgery, and this one cost me.

The Gildenholt kept accounts not only in coin. It kept them in obligations, favors, standing—the invisible currency that actually ran the place, because coin was common and obligation was scarce. I had spent six years building a small, careful balance of it. The night porter owed me for covering his daughter's fever-debt interest. Two of the vault-wardens owed me for quiet corrections I'd made to their hours. Even Veyra, in her cold way, owed me something: I had once found an error in a century-old entry that would have cost the guild three years of bottled rain, and I had brought it to her instead of selling the knowledge to a rival house, which I could have done, and which would have set me up for life.

I spent all of it that night. Every favor, every ounce of goodwill, every small credit I'd hoarded like a miser hoards copper. I called in the porter to move a "consigned lot" from the holding cells to the auction staging room, and he asked no questions because he couldn't afford to. I had the wardens reassign the vault watch so that a certain corridor went unobserved for exactly eleven minutes. And I drafted a withdrawal notice over the name of a consignor who did not exist—a hedge-broker out of the Marches, dead these ten years, whose seal I copied from an old indenture in the deep files.

A dead man withdrawing a living man from auction. There was a poetry in it that I chose not to enjoy, because enjoyment makes you careless.

* * *

The third thing I traded wasn't ink at all. It was my record.

Six years without a discrepancy. Six years of being the woman whose columns always balanced, whose word was collateral, who was boring in the way that is actually the highest praise a counting-house can give. When the withdrawal notice surfaced—and it would surface; I was counting on it surfacing—someone would check the hand. Someone would check the seal. Someone would find Mara Pemberton, the meticulous one, the invisible one, had committed fraud against the Gildenholt itself.

They wouldn't prove it easily. I'd made sure of that; I'd written myself deniability into every stroke, alibis into every timestamp. But suspicion doesn't need proof. Suspicion only needs a name to stick to, and mine would stick.

My clean record was worth more than my savings. My savings were worth everything I had. I intended to lose both, and I sat with that knowledge for a long moment before I sealed the last envelope, because I am many unflattering things but I have never been able to lie to myself about a price.

Here is what nobody tells you about stealing your first thing: it isn't the fear that stays with you. It's the lightness. As though some weight you'd been carrying so long you'd mistaken it for your own body had simply been set down.

* * *

I found Quentin in the staging room among the lots, sitting very still between a crate of bottled fog and a jar containing what the manifest optimistically called *one (1) borrowed summer, slightly used.*

"You look terrible," I told him.

"I've been told I'm improving." He was gray around the mouth, and his side was bandaged with the guild's cheap linen, which was honest enough about its purpose and dishonest about its quality. "Tell me we're leaving."

"We're leaving. During the auction, when every eye in the building is on the money." I laid out the route for him the way I'd lay out a ledger: north corridor, unwatched for eleven minutes, then the tithe-gate where carts went in and out with the auction freight. "There will be a cart. There will be a driver who has been paid to see nothing. You will be cargo."

"What are you?"

"Inventory," I said. "Apparently. Lot 114. Try to look valuable."

He almost smiled, and then didn't, and the almost was worse than the failure. "Mara. If they catch you—"

"They won't."

"If they catch you," he repeated, with the stubbornness of a man who had been hunted long enough to know how hunting worked, "what happens?"

I considered lying to him. I was, after all, on a streak. "Forfeiture," I said. "My wages, my savings, my bond. Possibly an indenture of my own, if Veyra's feeling creative. She usually is."

"And you're doing it anyway."

"You're the second hand the reforging needs. I'm apparently the first. That's the whole contract, and I don't leave contracts half-executed. It offends me professionally."

He looked at me for a long moment with those eyes that were the wrong color for either world he belonged to—too pale for mortal, too warm for fae—and said, quietly, "You're going to regret this."

"Probably," I agreed. "But I'll regret it on my own terms, which is more than I've ever managed before. Now be quiet. The bell's about to ring."

* * *

The tithe auction began, as all Gildenholt auctions began, with Veyra.

She stood at the rostrum in her slate-gray silk, and the whole floor hushed the way water hushes before it freezes. She did not raise her voice. She never needed to. "Honored creditors," she said, "honored debtors, honored guests—you know which you are. We open with the seasonal lots."

And they were off. Bottled thunder fetched a scandalous price from a mill-owner's widow who wanted her husband's temper preserved for posterity. A pawned shadow went unredeemed and was struck through in red chalk, and somewhere in the gallery a man made a small sound like a door closing. I stood at my ledger-stand in the corner, recording bids with a steady hand, and inside I was screaming in a way that felt suspiciously like singing.

Eleven minutes. I counted them in entries.

At minute four, the withdrawal notice reached the registrar's table. I watched her frown at it, watch her send for the deep-files clerk, watch the machinery of verification begin to grind exactly as I'd designed it to grind—slowly, toward the dead broker's seal, away from me, buying time with bureaucracy. Bureaucracy is a wonderful animal. It is loyal to no one and therefore can be ridden by anyone.

At minute seven, the porter moved a covered figure on a handcart down the north corridor. At minute nine, the wardens looked elsewhere. At minute eleven, a cart rolled out through the tithe-gate carrying, among thirty crates of legitimate freight, one lot that had never existed at all.

I recorded the final bid of the session in a hand that did not shake, closed my ledger, and allowed myself exactly one breath of triumph.

It lasted until I turned around and found Veyra standing beside my stand.

* * *

"The Pemberton girl," she said, to the room at large, though the room had conveniently emptied. "Walk with me."

We walked. Through the counting-house, past the weighing door, along the gallery above the vaults where the guild's impossible inventory slept in ranked and labeled darkness. Her heels made a sound like a metronome. Mine tried to keep time and failed.

"A curious auction," she said. "A lot withdrawn by a consignor who has been dead since before you were apprenticed. A corridor unwatched by men who owe you small kindnesses. A cart that left heavy and arrived light." She stopped at the railing and looked down into the dark. "You understand that I could have any of this verified by morning."

"Yes, mistress."

"And yet I find I'm not inclined to verify it." She turned those scale-eyes on me, and I understood suddenly that I had been weighed, completely, sometime in the last hour, and that the reading had satisfied her. Which frightened me far more than accusation would have. "Do you know why?"

"No, mistress."

"Because a forger of your quality is worth more free than caught." She said it the way she said everything—as though reading terms aloud. "You altered forty-one entries tonight. I counted forty-two. You missed one."

Cold walked down my spine. "Which one?"

"Doesn't matter. It was well hidden. That's the point." She resumed walking, and I followed, because what else does a debt do but follow its creditor? "You're going north after him. To the Sundered Marches. To the covenant-stone, with the map, to do whatever it is that dying couriers and hedge-witch clerks imagine they can do that a century of guild masters couldn't."

"I don't know what you mean, mistress."

"Mara." She used my given name for the first time in six years, and it landed like a stamp on wax. "I signed the withdrawal notice myself an hour ago. Your dead broker has excellent penmanship. Almost as good as yours."

The gallery was very quiet. Below us, the vaults breathed their cold air upward, full of summers and storms and other people's names.

"Why?" I asked. It was the only word left in me.

Veyra smiled, and it was the first genuine expression I had ever seen her wear, and I wished immediately that she hadn't. "Because the Gildenholt was built to service that covenant, child, and it has grown fat on the ruin of it, and fat things grow slow. Let your two hands go north and do the dangerous part. Find the stone. Learn the terms." She paused at the head of the stairs, silhouetted against the lamplight below. "Whatever they forge there will need an intermediary. A bank. A ledger-keeper both worlds can trust."

"You want a monopoly."

"I want *revenue*," she said, mildly, as if correcting my grammar. "Monopolies are merely how revenue behaves when no one is watching. Go, then. Run your errand. Spend your savings, spend your reputation—I've already spent them for you, in the projections. And when the covenant stands reforged, remember who let you out the door."

She descended the stairs. I stood alone in the gallery with the taste of copper in my mouth, doing the arithmetic I should have done days ago.

Every favor I'd called in. Every warden reassigned, every corridor left open, every seal passed unchallenged. It had gone too smoothly. It had gone smoothly because it had been *allowed*, because I had spent six years learning to move money invisibly through a machine that had been watching me move it the entire time, amused.

I had thought I was stealing Quentin from the Gildenholt.

The Gildenholt had let me. Had perhaps, in its cold ledgered heart, *commissioned* it—using my hands, my record, my savings, spending me like currency toward an end I hadn't agreed to. Veyra hadn't caught a thief. She'd made an investment.

And investments, I knew better than anyone alive, always came due.

I gripped the railing and looked north, through the high windows, toward the dark line of the Marches where the map was waiting, and I made myself one promise, the only kind I'd ever kept: whatever Veyra had projected, whatever she had entered in her books against my name—

I would find the error in her columns. I always did.

Then I went down to pack, and to catch a cart, and to become, for the second time in my life, a runaway.
