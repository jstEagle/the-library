## 8. Road Through Ashfall

**MARA**

The world north of Gildenholt smelled like a hearth that had been left too long.

I had read about ashfall in the guild's ledgers—entries for "weather, bottled (fair)" and "rain, seasonal, one village's worth"—and I had always assumed the entries were quaint. Accounting for weather seemed like accounting for God's moods: technically possible, practically absurd. But walking through it was different. The ash came down soft as snow and gray as debt, sifting into my collar, dulling the color out of everything until the road looked like a page someone had erased and never rewritten.

"Is it always like this?" Quentin asked. He walked like a man who expected the ground to invoice him for the privilege.

"Ledgers say the ashfall started ninety-seven years ago." I kept my eyes on the road. "Same year the covenant broke. Coincidence is a word accountants use when we haven't found the entry yet."

He made a sound that might have been a laugh if laughter weren't so expensive these days. We had been on the road two days, sleeping in ditches with our backs against each other like a pair of spoons nobody wanted, and we had developed the particular rhythm of two people who trusted each other exactly as far as they could throw the other's secrets—which, in my case, was quite far; I have excellent aim.

Ahead of us, the road filled with people.

I saw them before Quentin did—I always see numbers first—and what I saw was this: forty-odd souls moving north with everything they owned, which averaged out to less than nothing once you counted what they owed. Carts pulled by thin oxen. A woman carrying a loom on her back like a turtle shell. Children with the specific silence of children who have learned that questions cost.

"Refugees," Quentin said quietly.

"Displaced," I corrected. "Refugee implies somewhere took them."

The caravan's head man was a broad, gray-bearded farmer named Oskar Vell, though he introduced himself by his debts rather than his name, which told me he'd spent time dealing with the guild. "Three seasons' tax arrears to the Marchmoot," he said, squinting at us with the flat assessment of a man pricing strangers. "A well that went dry the year my daughter stopped remembering her mother's face. And luck. You can't tax luck, but you miss it. Nothing grows right without it. What are you two? Pilgrims? Deserters?"

"Travelers with skills," I said. "She can keep books. I can—" Quentin paused, and I watched him do the arithmetic of what to reveal about himself, arriving at a sum he didn't like. "I can walk fast and ask few questions."

Oskar snorted. "Everyone can walk fast. Nobody asks few questions." He chewed on something—tobacco, or the inside of his own cheek. "Passage costs. Cart space, water from the barrels, a share of the fire. What've you got?"

I opened my satchel and produced the only currency I had ever truly trusted: paper. "Bookkeeping," I said. "Your caravan has forty-one people, nine carts, and I'd wager not one of them knows who owes whom what. Favors rot faster than fruit when nobody writes them down. Give me an evening with your tally-sticks and I'll give you order. Order is worth more than coin out here. Coin you can steal. Order has to be earned back every day."

Oskar looked at me the way men look at a horse that has just done something clever and slightly unnerving. Then he looked at Quentin, who lifted his hands in surrender.

"And him?"

"He carries things," I said. "Heavy things. Awkward things. Things that bite."

One of the cart wheels needed turning, and Quentin turned it. That was how we bought our passage.

* * *

**QUENTIN**

Here is what nobody tells you about being half of two worlds: you belong to neither, and both send collectors.

I learned the shape of the broken covenant from the caravan, because the border folk wear their losses openly, the way city folk wear jewelry. I sat on Oskar Vell's cart between a widow named Brey and her grandson, a boy of perhaps seven who stared at me with the frank hunger of the very young, and I listened.

"The year I was married," Brey said, "it rained on schedule. You could set a christening by the clouds. My mother said the old pact kept the sky honest—the hidden folk owed us weather, see, and we owed them... something else. Something no one says anymore." She lowered her voice as if the ash itself were listening. "When the pact tore, the rain stopped keeping appointments. Now it comes when it likes or not at all, and mostly not."

"My wife," Oskar said from the front, not turning around, "forgot me over three winters. Not all at once. Like a candle burning down. First she forgot small things—where she'd put the scissors, the name of the neighbor's dog. Then bigger ones. Last spring she looked at me across the table like I was a lodger." His hands were steady on the reins. Steady the way things are steady when holding still is the only alternative to shaking. "The cunning-woman said memory drains out of a place when the old bargain dies. Luck, rain, remembering—all one river, and somebody dammed it upstream."

"Who?" I asked, though I knew. I had carried the map of it against my ribs until my blood soaked through the wax seal.

"The lords who signed it and then got greedy," Oskar said. "On both sides of the hedge, from what the stories say. Humans who wanted the fae's goods without paying the fae's price. Fae who decided mortals were livestock." He spat over the wheel. "And the little folk caught in the middle, and us under both their boots."

Brey's grandson tugged my sleeve. "Are you one of them?" he asked. "You've got the look. The too-pretty, wrong-angle look."

"Bran!" Brey hissed.

But I found I couldn't lie to him. Children can smell a lie the way hounds smell fear, and worse, they remember it. "Half," I said. "Half of them. Half of you. All of neither."

The boy considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. "That sounds lonely," he said, and went back to carving a notch into the cart rail, and I sat with his verdict the rest of the afternoon because there was no appealing it.

* * *

**MARA**

That evening, by firelight, I taught Quentin double-entry bookkeeping, and I want it noted for posterity that he was a terrible student.

"It's simple," I said, drawing columns in the dirt with a stick. "Every favor has two sides. When Brey mends Oskar's harness, that's a credit to Brey and a debit to Oskar. Two entries, always balanced. The ledger doesn't care who's generous. It only cares that the sum stays true."

"Why does it need two sides?"

"Because a favor with only one side isn't a favor. It's a theft with good manners." I drew the line firm. "If you give and never record what you're owed, you'll wake up one day empty and unable to say where it went. If you take and never record what you owe, you'll wake up one day owing everything and owning nothing. Balance, Quentin. It's the only honest magic I know."

He crouched over the dirt columns, and I watched him work through it the way he seemed to work through everything—with the wariness of a creature that had been handed poisoned gifts before. "In the courts I grew up around," he said slowly, "debts aren't written. They're *felt*. A favor unpaid becomes a weight on the soul. The fae call it a 'grudge-light.' You can see it glowing in someone who owes too much." He glanced up. "You're telling me humans invented a way to make the grudge-light visible on paper."

"I'm telling you humans invented a way to make it *arguable*. There's a difference." I tapped the dirt. "Show me. Brey gave you half her bread tonight. Enter it."

He frowned, wrote—or scratched—in the left column: *Brey: bread, half loaf.* Then he stopped.

"What goes on the other side?"

"That depends. Do you intend to repay her?"

"Yes."

"Then enter what you'll pay: *Quentin: bread, half loaf, owed.* Now the ledger balances and everyone knows where they stand. But—" I held up a finger, because this was the part that mattered, the part Crane had never understood in thirty years of teaching me his crooked arithmetic, "—here's the secret the guild doesn't put in its primers. A ledger can also record what you *give freely*. No debit. No expectation. Just: *Given: bread, half loaf, no return required.* It balances differently. It balances against nothing. Most bookkeepers say such entries corrupt the accounts." I smiled, and felt my own face do something unfamiliar with it. "I think those are the only entries worth keeping."

Quentin looked at me for a long moment. Firelight does flattering things to most faces, but on his it did honest things instead—the hollows under his cheekbones, the exhaustion, the fear he carried folded up small like a letter he kept rereading.

"You really believe that," he said. "After everything. After Crane. After the guild."

"Belief isn't a mood. It's a practice. I balance toward it daily."

He laughed then—a real one, short and startled out of him, like a coin dropped in an empty room. Around us the caravan settled into sleep, forty-one souls breathing under gray ash that fell without malice and without mercy, and I entered the evening in my head the way I entered everything:

*Debit: one evening's peace, spent on hope.*
*Credit: unknown.*
*Balance: pending.*

* * *

**QUENTIN**

Two nights later, the caravan reached the crossroads shrine at Hollowmere, and I learned what a village looks like when it has been forgotten twice—once by its dead, and once by its gods.

The shrine had been a waystone once, one of the old covenant-markers, I realized, my blood humming in recognition before my mind caught up. The stone stood at the meeting of two roads, carved with interlocking hands, mortal and otherwise, and it had been defaced—not smashed, but *erased*, the fingers chiseled smooth, the pact-symbol ground away with patient, deliberate hatred.

The villagers had tied ribbons to it anyway. Hundreds of them, knotted by desperate hands, each ribbon a prayer: *send rain, send luck, let my mother remember me, let my child be born whole.*

"They still come," Mara said beside me. She had gone very quiet, which for Mara meant the numbers had stopped adding up. "Ninety-seven years of nothing answered, and they still tie ribbons."

"Hope is the worst-run business in the world," I said. "No reserves. No security. Infinite exposure."

"But it never files for bankruptcy." She touched one of the ribbons—faded red, older than either of us—and I watched her jaw set in a way I was beginning to recognize as the prelude to either genius or arson.

"Mara."

"There's an entry missing here, Quentin." Her voice had gone low and fast, the counting-house voice, the one that had made grown guild masters check their own figures twice. "These people have been paying into a broken contract for three generations. Rain they didn't get. Luck that drained away. Memories bleeding out of their heads like a cracked barrel. They've been *debtors* to a covenant that defaulted on *them*. Do you understand what that means?"

"That the terms were never settled," I said slowly. "When the pact broke, the obligations didn't die. They just stopped being honored. On one side."

"On one side." She turned to me, and the firelight caught the ash in her hair and made it look almost like snow. "Which means when you reforge it—if we reforge it—it won't just be sealing a gate. It'll be settling ninety-seven years of arrears. Every dry well. Every forgotten face. Every ribbon on this stone. Someone will owe these people, Quentin, and whoever holds the new covenant decides how they get paid back."

I looked at the waystone, at the smooth-chiseled hands that could no longer hold anything, at the ribbons stirring in a wind that carried no rain.

And that was when I felt it—the pull, faint but unmistakable, from the north. From the Sundered Marches. Something on the other side had noticed the map against my ribs, the way a spider notices a fly testing the edge of its web, and the sensation that slid through me was not menace.

It was *welcome*.

Home, something whispered, in a voice like my mother's and nothing like my mother's. Come home, little half-born. We have been keeping your seat.

I did not tell Mara. Some entries, I thought, watching her untie a fresh ribbon from her sleeve and knot it to the stone with brisk, furious competence—some entries you carry alone until you know whether they're a debt or a trap.

But the whisper stayed with me all night, patient as interest accruing, and by morning the map had begun, very softly, to bleed.
