## 9. The Toll of Names

I have crossed a great many rivers in my life as a courier, and I can report with some authority that most of them are honest. They want you wet, they want you cold, and if they're feeling ambitious, they want you drowned. That's it. That's the whole transaction. A river has never once asked me who I am.

This one did.

We'd been walking north for six days by then, following the map's slow crawl toward the Sundered Marches and the caravan of the displaced that had adopted us somewhere outside Ashfall Hollow. The border country had a look I was coming to know too well—the color gone out of the fields like dye left too long in the wash, wells that gave up mud instead of water, villages where people had stopped saying each other's surnames because half the time they couldn't remember them. The broken covenant didn't rage. It *leaked*. Luck drained out of the world through a crack a century old, and everything downstream of the crack went quietly gray.

Mara kept our accounts the whole way. Not money—we had almost none—but favors. She'd taught me her double-entry system on the second night, scratching columns into the dirt with a stick while the caravan fires smoked around us: what we owed, what we were owed, and—this was the part she insisted on, tapping the stick against the middle line—what we owed *each other*, which she tracked separately and refused to let me argue about.

"You keep giving me things," she said, "and pretending they're nothing. Rations you 'weren't hungry for.' Watches you 'weren't sleeping anyway.' That's not generosity, Quentin, that's bad bookkeeping. Everything goes in the ledger or it doesn't exist."

"It exists," I said.

"Then write it down."

So I did. And every night the middle column grew, and every night I felt something I didn't have a word for yet, watching my debts to her accumulate in her careful hand like a wall being built between me and the empty place where other couriers kept their homes.

The river appeared on the morning of the seventh day, and it was wrong in a way I recognized before I understood.

It ran silver. Not reflective—*silver*, actual metal-bright water moving without sound, without current, from a source we couldn't see to a mouth we couldn't see, cutting the road in two. The caravan stopped well back from it. I watched an old woman make a sign against evil and then, after consideration, make a second, more thorough sign.

"That's not on any map," Mara said beside me. She had the Covenant Map out despite my warnings, wrapped in oilcloth, held at arm's length like something that might bite. "And it wasn't here yesterday. The miller at Ashfall said the ford was three miles east."

"The Marches are bleeding through," I said. "Rivers move when the old law gets close. Old law loves boundaries." I looked at the water and felt the hair rise on my arms, because I knew what kind of boundary this was. "And old law collects tolls."

*

She was waiting at the crossing when we reached the bank, which surprised me less than it should have. Fae toll-keepers are never *found*; they are simply already there, the way weather is there, the way interest is there.

She stood on a flat stone midstream where the water broke around her without touching her—a woman-shaped absence wearing a gown of reeds and river-silt, hair like weed in a current that wasn't blowing. Her face was pleasant. That was the worst part. It was the face of someone about to offer you tea.

"Travelers," she said, and her voice came from under the water and above it at once. "The crossing is open. The crossing is not free."

Mara, practical to the marrow, dug in her coat for coins.

The toll-keeper laughed, and fish scattered downstream from the sound. "Coin. Oh, that's sweet. No, hedge-child. This is an old crossing. It takes the old toll." Her eyes found mine across the water, and I felt them like hooks going in. "It takes a name."

I should explain something about names, since the human side of the covenant spent a century pretending it didn't understand the terms. To the hidden folk, a name isn't a label. It's a handle. It's the thread that ties you to yourself, and anyone holding the thread can pull. Sell your name whole and you become a suggestion of a person, easily led, easily worn. My mother's people traded in them the way the Gildenholt trades in bottled storms—and were, if anything, more scrupulous about it, which tells you something.

My own name was already spoken for. Held in trust by the dead, every scrap of it, locked in the trust of masters who were now ash in Seelie ruins. What I carried day to day was a courier's working name, a thing like a delivery seal—functional, temporary, mine the way a guild badge is yours.

But a toll-keeper doesn't need the whole thread. A fragment will do. A childhood memory with your name woven through it. The way your mother said it when you were small. The sound of it in a lover's mouth. Take enough fragments and you don't have to steal the name—you've simply collected all its pieces, and the person walks away lighter each time until one day he tips over in the wind.

"I'll pay," I said, before Mara could speak.

I felt her go still beside me. I stepped forward onto the shingle, toward the flat stone, and began taking inventory of myself the way I'd been taught: what could be spared, what the mission required, what was surplus.

"My grandfather's voice," I offered. "He called me little wren. I remember the exact pitch of it. You can have the memory entire—I'll keep the fact of him but lose the sound. Fair trade?"

The toll-keeper tilted her head, considering, the way a merchant considers meat.

"And the name the nuns gave me at the waystation, the false one, Tammas. I never used it for anything true. It's barely worn. You could get years of use—"

"Quentin."

Mara's voice. Flat. The counting-house voice, the one she used on guild masters who tried to round numbers in their favor.

"Quentin, stop."

"It's fine," I said. "They're just pieces. I've got plenty. I'm—" and here I made the mistake of smiling, because I genuinely believed it, because I had believed it my whole life, "—I'm mostly spare parts anyway. Half of me belongs to a world that won't claim me and the other half is held in probate by dead men. These fragments aren't doing anything. Better they buy us across than sit in the vault rotting."

The toll-keeper smiled with genuine warmth, and I want to be clear that this was worse than malice. She looked at me the way Veyra looked at inventory. The way Crane, I would learn later, looked at indentures. Everyone in every world agreed on what I was worth, and the agreement was: whatever the market would bear, and the market bore very little.

"No," Mara said.

She walked past me—past me, straight through the space where I was negotiating away my childhood—and planted herself on the shingle facing the toll-keeper, ledger already out of her coat, pencil already behind her ear.

"Madam," she said, "I'd like to discuss terms."

The toll-keeper's smile acquired a flicker of interest. "The mortal negotiates?"

"The mortal *always* negotiates. That's the whole trouble with us." Mara flipped the ledger open against her forearm. "You want a name. Names are collateral, and collateral is a loan structure, and loans have alternatives. So. Alternative one: I pay his toll."

That got both of us. I actually laughed. "Mara, you don't *have* a—"

"Don't I?" She turned and looked at me, and there was something in her face I couldn't read yet, something fierce and sad at once. "Witch-oath, Quentin. Crane made me swear mine when I was thirteen. My name's been bound in service-contract since before I could grow a full signature. It's mortgaged, but it's real, and it's load-bearing, and I am not—" her voice caught, just slightly, and she pushed through it, "—I am not watching you hand out pieces of yourself like samples at a fair because you've decided you're worth less than the crossing."

The river went quiet. Even the fish came back to listen.

"Alternative two," Mara continued, turning back to the toll-keeper, businesslike again as if she hadn't just cracked open in front of a crowd. "A promissory note. Sealed in witch-oath, which you know is binding—it's why your kind stopped lending to my master's line, we default so *creatively*. Terms as follows: the bearer—that's me—undertakes one service, named by the lender, to be rendered within a year and a day, the service to be no greater than the value of one river-crossing for two travelers, and no baser than the lender's dignity permits. Interest accrues. Default forfeits—" she paused, and I watched her do arithmetic behind her eyes, actual arithmetic, weighing her own future against mine, "—forfeits one year of the bearer's luck."

"One year of luck," the toll-keeper repeated. Her reed-hair stirred though the air didn't. "You'd wager a year of your luck. For his name-fragments. Which he offers freely."

"He offers freely because nobody ever taught him not to," Mara said. "That's not freedom, that's a hole in his education. I'm correcting it." She licked her thumb, pressed it to the page. "Do we have terms, or do I start looking for a ford?"

For a long moment the toll-keeper regarded her. Then she looked at me, and her expression shifted into something almost gentle, almost pitying, and she said—not unkindly:

"She sees you clearly, little courier. Do you?"

"I'm starting to suspect not," I said.

"She counts you as worth a year of her luck. Note the exchange rate. Mortals rarely misprice themselves upward." The toll-keeper extended a hand of pale water and silt, and Mara, without hesitation, took it. Where their palms met, the witch-oath flared—a thin green light running up Mara's arm like ivy, sinking into the ink of the ledger, sealing itself. I felt the contract close the way you feel a door shut in a distant room.

The river parted.

Not dramatically—it simply developed a gap, a dry lane of stones from bank to bank, as if the water had remembered an appointment elsewhere. The toll-keeper stepped aside on her stone and inclined her head.

"Cross. And courier—" her voice followed me onto the stones, "—the fragments you offered. I declined them, but I heard them. Grandfathers who call their grandsons little wrens are rarer than you think. Keep the pitch. Some things shouldn't be currency."

I crossed with my throat tight and my hands shaking, and on the far bank I turned around and Mara was still standing on the shingle, finishing the entry in her ledger. I watched her write it. I watched her put my name in the *owed* column.

*

We walked in silence for a mile before I managed it.

"You shouldn't have done that."

"Mm."

"A year of your luck, Mara. You grew up with Crane. Your luck's the only thing that's ever gotten you out of anything, and you staked a year of it on—"

"On you." She said it plainly, the way she entered figures. "Yes."

"Why?" And I hated how the question came out, how raw it was, how much it wasn't really *why* but *how could you possibly*.

She stopped walking. The caravan was ahead of us, a smudge of dust and oxen; the gray fields stretched out grayly; somewhere north, the Sundered Marches waited with their impossible errand. She looked at me with the expression she wore when balancing a column that wouldn't come even.

"Because I watched you stand on that bank," she said, "and auction yourself off piece by piece, cheerfully, like it was a clearance sale, like you'd already done the sums and concluded you were worth more in parts than whole. And I know that arithmetic, Quentin. I *invented* that arithmetic. I spent six years doing it in Crane's house. 'I'm not worth the food I eat, but I'm worth the work, so the work is what I'll sell.' Every night I fell asleep doing that sum, and every morning I woke up and did it again, and the only reason I'm alive is that one day the numbers came out wrong and I ran." Her jaw tightened. "I know exactly what it costs to believe you're spare parts. I paid that bill for years. I will not stand in a riverbed and watch someone else pay it while smiling."

"I had it handled," I said, weakly.

"You had it *priced*." She started walking again. "There's a difference. Handling means you come out the other side still holding all of yourself. Pricing just means you decided in advance what losing pieces would cost, so it wouldn't hurt when it happened." She glanced back at me, and her voice gentled by a degree. "It still hurts, Quentin. It hurts the whole time. You've just gotten good at not entering it in the ledger."

I didn't say anything. There wasn't anything to say that wasn't already written down in her careful hand, in the column she kept for what we owed each other, which had apparently been keeping score of me long before I understood there was a game.

But that night, at the caravan fire, while she slept sitting up with the ledger under her coat like a shield, I took out the map and unwrapped it, and the blood-red lines had crawled another inch north. And beneath the mark for the crossing we'd made, in the margin, in ink I hadn't spilled and couldn't account for, a single new notation had appeared in a spidery fae script I almost recognized:

*One debt recorded. One year of mortal luck, held in trust.*

And below that, smaller, fresher, the ink still wet:

*The hounds have crossed the river. Run.*
