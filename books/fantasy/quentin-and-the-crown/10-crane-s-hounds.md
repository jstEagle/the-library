## 10. Crane's Hounds

I had always assumed that if Aldous Crane ever came for me, it would be with paperwork.

That was how he did everything. Contracts, indentures, the little blue slips he made apprentices sign acknowledging receipt of instruction—*instruction* being a word that covered a great deal of ground, the way a tarp covers a body. Seven years I had flinched at the scratch of his quill. So when the hounds came over the ridge at dusk, all teeth and no documents, my first thought was not *run*. My first thought was: *he's getting sloppy.*

My second thought was to grab Quentin by the collar and haul him off the road.

"Down," I said, which was generous of me, since I was already down and using him as a partial roof.

The first hound hit the spot where we'd been standing and kept going in a spray of gravel, because whatever else you could say about Crane's bindings—and I could say quite a lot; I'd mixed the binding-paste myself, years ago, kneeling on cold flagstones while he read me the inventory of my own shortcomings—they were fast but not clever. Bound things rarely are. A bound thing does exactly what it was told and nothing more, and Crane had told these creatures to run down the runaway Pemberton girl and the thief she traveled with, and they were running us down with the single-minded enthusiasm of a ledger column that only knows one direction.

There were three of them. They looked like hounds the way a forged signature looks like handwriting: right shape, wrong soul. Grey bodies that smoked faintly at the joints where the binding entered them, eyes like coins left too long in a dead man's pocket. I knew their breed. I'd fed their predecessors. Crane called them his *auditors*, and he'd laughed at his own joke every single time, which tells you most of what you need to know about Aldous Crane.

"Mara." Quentin had gone very still under my hand, the particular stillness of someone doing arithmetic with his own mortality. "Those are yours?"

"They're his." I could feel my heart going like a clerk counting under deadline. "Which is worse."

The hounds wheeled at the bottom of the slope and came again. The road here ran along a gorse-choked hillside above a stream, the kind of country where a person could break an ankle in forty directions, and behind us the caravan's fires were a soft orange smudge we'd walked away from an hour ago—too far to reach, too near to abandon without shame.

Quentin got his feet under him. "Can you outrun them?"

"No."

"Can I?"

"You're half-fae and bleeding from a river toll-keeper's idea of hospitality. You can out-*glide* them, maybe, for a mile. Then they'll have your scent and I'll be alone on a hillside with three bound things whose master taught me everything I know about what happens to runaways." I heard my own voice come out flat and even, the voice I used for balancing columns that refused to balance, and something in it surprised me. It didn't sound afraid. It sounded annoyed.

The lead hound came up the slope in a low, smoke-trailing rush, and Quentin moved—not away, but between, arms spread, doing the stupid noble thing people do when they haven't yet learned that nobility is just generosity with bad accounting.

And I stopped thinking like a runaway and started thinking like a bookkeeper.

Because here is the thing about hedge-craft, the real thing, the thing Crane never understood even though he built his whole miserable empire on it: magic is debt. Every working is a promise written into the world, and the world keeps books better than I do. Crane taught me that. He made me memorize it, kneeling on those flagstones, reciting until my voice cracked: *every binding is a contract, every contract has terms, every term can be read.* He thought he was teaching me obedience. He was teaching me literacy. It is the great joke of my life that the man priced me cheaply and then paid, in full, for my education.

A bound hound is a contract wearing teeth. And I had helped write those contracts.

"*Hold,*" I said—not to the hound, but to the binding in it, the way you'd address a clause rather than a creature—and I reached into my sleeve for the little kit I'd carried since Gildenholt: chalk stub, iron pin, a twist of salt, and the strip of parchment I'd been saving for emergencies, which I had privately defined as *anything involving Crane*.

The lead hound stopped. Not gently—it stopped the way a cart stops when the axle snaps, all its momentum collapsing inward—but it stopped, its smoking jaws a hand's width from Quentin's chest, and hung there trembling like a guilty entry awaiting audit.

Quentin looked at it. Looked at me. "How."

"I signed the maintenance schedule for its grandmother." I was already moving, chalk out, dropping to one knee in the dirt beside the frozen beast. "Crane binds in circles. Circles need closing. He closes them himself, always, because he trusts no one else to do it right—which means every circle he's ever drawn uses the same closing mark. The same one. For thirty years." I drew the mark in the dust beneath the hound, quick and exact, the way you sign a name you've signed ten thousand times. "He never varied it. Why would he? Variance is inefficiency."

The second hound crested the ridge. The third was flanking wide through the gorse—I could hear it, a dry rustling like paper being turned.

"What are you doing?" Quentin asked.

"Amending the terms." I pressed the iron pin into the center of the chalk mark, and spoke the words Crane had drilled into me as a child of eleven, the closing formula, the little lock-click at the end of every working: *as written, so witnessed; as witnessed, so owed.* Except I changed one word. One word, the way you change one digit in a column and watch the whole sum come apart.

Instead of *owed to the binder*, I said *owed to the bearer*.

The hound shuddered. The smoke at its joints guttered. And then it turned its coin-pale eyes on me, and there was nothing in them—no love, no gratitude, bound things don't get those—but there was *attention*, which is more than Crane ever gave anything, and it sat down in the road like a dog waiting to be told its job.

"Oh," said Quentin softly. "Oh, that's *appalling*. He's going to be livid."

"He's going to be bankrupt," I said. "Do you understand what I just did? These aren't his dogs anymore. They're mine. By his own grammar. I hold their contracts." I stood, knees aching, hands shaking now that the moment had passed and my body had caught up to what my mind had been too busy to feel. "Every lesson he ever beat into me, every formula, every rule—he wrote them all in a hand I can copy."

The second hound reached us then, and I'll admit the theory went easier than the practice. It didn't stop for my spoken word because I hadn't closed its circle yet, and it knocked me flat into the gorse, which is nature's way of reminding scholars that scholarship has a tuition. I felt its breath—cold, ink-smelling, the breath of a thing that had never eaten anything but orders—and then Quentin had it by the scruff with both hands, and his fae side showed for just a moment, a flicker of something tall and antlered looking out through his face, and the hound hesitated.

Hesitation is a gap in a ledger. I filled it. Chalk, pin, word, amendment. The third came out of the gorse before I'd fully risen, and this one I was ready for: I threw the salt in a line across its path—Crane's own warding, lesson fourteen, *salt is a boundary, boundaries are promises*—and it balked at the line like a clerk at a figure he doesn't trust, and while it stood there doubting itself I amended it too.

Then there were three bound hounds sitting in the road in the last grey light, obedient to me, and two people breathing hard among the gorse, and a silence with a great deal of interest accruing in it.

Quentin sat down where he stood. "You turned his own lessons against him."

"I turned his own *contracts* against him. The lessons were just the delivery mechanism." I wiped chalk dust on my skirt and found my hands were still trembling, so I made fists of them and waited for the trembling to be embarrassed enough to stop. "He used to make me recite the closing formula while he held my wrist over a candle. If I stumbled, the wax dripped. If I was perfect, he said I was showing off." I heard the words come out and was mildly astonished at them; I hadn't planned to say any of that. "I have been perfect at that formula for nine years. In my head. Every night. Waiting."

"For this?"

"For *something*. I didn't know what. That's the worst part, isn't it? He taught me so thoroughly that even my revenge is just homework." I laughed, and it came out wrong, jagged, and Quentin had the grace not to look at me with pity, which I would not have survived. "They'll report back, you know. Whatever's left of them reports to whoever holds their bond. Which is now me. Which means—" I paused, because the implication arrived mid-sentence like a bill collector. "Which means I can listen through them. And Crane knows it. He'll know the instant they don't come home that I've taken them. He'll know exactly what I did, because it's *his trick*, inverted. He invented this theft. I'm just the apprentice who finally turned in her masterpiece."

Quentin was quiet for a moment. Down the hillside the stream muttered to itself about the coming dark. "Is that bad?"

"It's terrible. It's also the first time in my life I've ever held an asset he wants." I looked at the three hounds sitting in the road, patient as unpaid invoices, and felt something settle in my chest that I didn't have a name for yet. Fury, maybe, finished cooling into something usable. All my life Crane's lessons had lived in me like shrapnel, hurting whenever the weather changed. Tonight I'd pulled the first piece out and found it had an edge. "Come on. We need to move. There's a place ahead—the map goes strange there, Quentin, remember, the unmappable stretch—and I want to reach it before he sends something that doesn't have a contract I can amend."

We gathered the hounds with a whistle I invented on the spot and which they obeyed, because obedience was the only language Crane had ever installed in them. They fell in around us, smoke leaking from their seams, and I will confess that walking flanked by my master's own auditors felt like the best entry I had ever posted.

It lasted until the moon rose, and the lead hound lifted its head, and I felt—through the fresh bond, thin as thread—a sensation I can only describe as *a page turning somewhere far away*.

Crane had felt the amendment. And somewhere south, in a house full of candles and ledgers, he had picked up his quill.

"He's writing something new," I said aloud, before I could decide whether to say it. "Something he hasn't taught anyone. Something I won't know how to close."

Quentin glanced at me. "Then we'd better be somewhere he can't follow."

Ahead of us, past the last fold of the hills, the air had begun to do something air should not do—thickening, folding, refusing to hold a straight line—and the map in Quentin's satchel, I would learn later, had already given up on rendering it. The Sundered Marches were close. So, I suspected, was everything else.

* * *

We made four miles before the hounds stopped.

Not slowed—stopped, all three at once, like clocks in a house where the money's run out. They stood in the road with their heads lowered and their smoke blowing sideways in a wind I couldn't feel, and through the bond I tasted what they tasted: a command arriving from the south, written in a hand I knew better than my own, in a formula I had never seen.

New terms. Fresh ink. Crane had spent the evening drafting, and now he served his papers.

The lead hound turned its coin eyes to me, and though it had no voice, the bond carried the sense of it clearly enough, the way a summons carries: *amended in turn. Return to your maker, or be unmade.*

Quentin saw my face. "What is it?"

"He's rewritten them." My mouth had gone dry. "And I don't know the closing mark this time." Behind us, far off, a sound rolled down out of the southern dark—not a howl, nothing so honest. More like a page tearing. "Run."
