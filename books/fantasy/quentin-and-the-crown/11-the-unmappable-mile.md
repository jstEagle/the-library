## 11. The Unmappable Mile

The map had been bleeding for two days.

Not metaphorically—though with fae cartography the distinction is mostly academic. The ink wept from the parchment in thin red threads, pooling in the creases, and every mile north we walked it redrew itself a little further from anything resembling honest geography. Rivers migrated. Hills folded into one another like hands in prayer. A forest that had been east of us at dawn was west of us by noon, and Mara had stopped arguing with it somewhere around the second day, which I took as a sign of either wisdom or surrender.

"It's not broken," she said now, holding it up to the grey light filtering through the birches. "It's *honest*. It's telling us where we are."

"And where are we?"

She turned the map sideways. Then upside down. Then back again, with the particular patience of a woman who balances ledgers for a living and has learned that when the numbers stop making sense, the problem is usually you.

"Here," she said finally, tapping a stretch of parchment that was, quite simply, blank. Not empty—the distinction matters. Empty is nothing drawn. This was nothing *renderable*, a smudge of pale grey where the ink refused to go, as if the map itself had looked at what lay ahead and declined to comment. "The unmappable mile. Every account of the Sundered Marches mentions it. You can't chart ground that hasn't decided to exist yet."

"Wonderful," I said. "And we're walking through it because...?"

"Because the covenant-stone is on the other side, and there's no road around." She rolled the map up with brisk efficiency, the way she did everything, as if the world were a column of figures that would eventually sum if you just kept working. "Besides, you're the one who can feel it. Don't pretend you can't."

I could. That was the trouble.

It had started as a hum at the base of my skull, three days back—a tuning-fork note too low to hear and too persistent to ignore. Now it was a pull, gentle and enormous, like standing on a shore and feeling the tide take an interest in your ankles. The Sundered Marches bled through here; everyone had told us so, the caravan folk with their luckless fields and their children who forgot their grandparents' faces, the toll-keeper at the river who had eyed me with something horribly like recognition. The border between worlds wasn't a wall. It was a wound, and wounds don't hold a line—they seep.

And my mother's blood, apparently, was on the far side of the seam, calling its half-son home.

"Quentin." Mara's voice cut across the hum. "You've gone somewhere. Come back."

"I'm here," I said, which was becoming less true by the hour.

* * *

We made camp at the edge of it, because even Mara—who had crossed half a kingdom on forged ledger entries and sheer stubborn arithmetic—drew a line at walking into unrendered territory after dark. We built a small fire of the ash-birch wood the caravan master had given us, wood that burned cold-blue and gave off a smell like rain on hot stone, and we ate hard bread and harder cheese, and I tried very hard to be a person sitting by a fire rather than a door swinging open.

Mara, naturally, kept accounts.

"Favors owed," she said, producing her little notebook by firelight. She'd taken to carrying it everywhere since the river crossing, since the toll-keeper, since the night she'd watched me offer pieces of myself away like a man paying with his own fingers. "Current balance. To me: one rescue from guild enforcers, one set of dry clothes, one lesson in double-entry bookkeeping, partially completed. To you: one forged escape, one negotiated promissory note, one—" she paused, and the firelight caught something in her face I couldn't read, "—one refusal to let you pay a toll with your name. Which I'm counting as a debt on my side, because it cost me something to do it."

"You didn't have to—"

"Yes, I did. That's rather the point of debts freely carried, isn't it? You don't get to opt out of mine any more than I get to opt out of yours." She licked her thumb and turned a page. "Also outstanding: one caravan passage repaid in labor, one witch-oath sealed and pending, and whatever it is you keep not saying whenever you look north."

I looked north. The trees thinned ahead of us into a grey shimmer, like heat-haze in winter, like the world holding its breath. Beyond it, I could almost see—not with eyes, but with the other inheritance, the one I'd spent twenty-six years pretending was indigestion or nerves or a trick of the light. Meadows that had never known winter. A court lit by no sun. And threaded through all of it, faint as a scent, something that knew me.

"When I was small," I said, before I could decide not to, "I used to dream about a garden. Walled, silver-leaved, always dusk. There was a woman in it who never turned around. I'd call and call and she'd start to turn, and then I'd wake up." I fed a stick to the blue fire. "I thought everyone dreamed like that. It was years before I understood it was a memory wearing a nightgown."

Mara closed the notebook. She didn't say anything, which from Mara is a whole speech.

"My mother held my name in trust," I went on. "That's how they do it, the hidden folk. A name isn't yours until someone gives it to you, and hers were given to me in trust until I came of age or came home, whichever came first. Then the Seelie remnant was slaughtered, and the trust died with them, and now I'm—" I gestured at myself, comprehensively. "This. A courier with a delivery address and no sender. Half of everything and owner of none of it."

"The map knows where the stone is," Mara said slowly. "Does it know where the trust went?"

"I've been afraid to ask it."

"Well." She tucked the notebook away and pulled her cloak tighter. "Tomorrow we walk through a mile of country that doesn't officially exist, toward a stone that can reforge a covenant between worlds, pursued by a hedge-witch with a legal claim on both our hides. I think we're past the point where fear gets a vote." She lay back against her pack. "Sleep. One of us should."

I slept. That was my mistake—or perhaps it was the plan all along, mine or the Marches', and by then I genuinely could not have told you whose.

* * *

The dream-garden was waiting.

Silver leaves, perpetual dusk, the wall worn smooth as sea glass. And this time the woman turned around.

She had my cheekbones. That was the first thing, the stupid domestic detail of it—she had my cheekbones and my left-handed way of tilting her head, and her eyes were the grey-green of deep water over stones, and she smiled at me with such naked relief that something in my chest tore cleanly down the middle.

*There you are,* she said, though her lips didn't move. Her voice arrived the way the hum did, beneath hearing, inside the bone. *Twenty-six years. Do you know how long that is, in the courts? Long enough for three wars and a wedding and the fall of a house. Long enough for me to learn patience, which I never managed in life.*

"You're dead," I said. In dreams you can say these things plainly.

*I am.* No grief in it. Dead things, true things, out here they weigh the same. *And you are half mine, and the Marches are bleeding, and the part of you that belongs beyond the seam has been knocking on the inside of your ribs since you were born. Let it out, Quentin. Let it in. Come through, and I will give you what was held in trust. Your name. Your place. A wall to stand inside that will never be breached.*

The garden brightened around her as she spoke, silver leaves igniting one by one, and oh, it was beautiful, it was *home*, it was every lonesome night of my life rendered in architecture. I felt my feet begin to move. I felt the human part of me—cold toes, chapped lips, the smell of woodsmoke and Mara's terrible cheese—begin to thin like ink in water.

*You'll owe nothing,* my mother whispered. *That's the promise. Belonging without debt. No ledger. No balance. Just a son, come home.*

No ledger.

Some traitorous clerk in the back of my skull sat up at that and cleared his throat.

* * *

I woke—or the part of me that was still Quentin woke—to find myself standing.

The fire had burned to blue ash. Mara was asleep, curled tight as a comma. And I was ten paces into the unmappable mile, walking, barefoot—I had been barefoot, when had I lost my boots?—with the grey shimmer closing over me like water over a dropped coin.

The Marches poured in.

It wasn't pain. That's what I'd never been able to explain to anyone, why the fae-touched can't simply refuse it. It was *relief*. Twenty-six years of being half a thing, and suddenly the halves were joining, the mortal frost melting off the fae fire, and underneath it all a voice like my mother's voice but older, vaster, the voice of the land itself:

*Come. Be counted. Be whole.*

My feet kept moving. Somewhere behind me, distantly, Mara's voice—

"—Quentin. *Quentin.*"

—and I tried to answer and found I had misplaced my mouth along with my boots. The grey shimmer was full of shapes now, meadows and walls and a garden that grew toward me eagerly, hungrily, offering itself room by room. All I had to do was keep walking. All I had to do was stop being a creature with debts.

Then, cutting through the vast sweet hum like a knife through honey:

"One."

Mara's voice. Close now, close enough that I could hear her breath sawing, hear her boots on ground that might not technically support them.

"Two. Three. Four."

She was reciting. And I knew the numbers, gods help me, I knew the entries, because I'd helped her write them.

"Five: one caravan passage, repaid in labor, three days' driving, settled in full. Six: one river toll, paid in witch-oath, value assessed at more than a name, terms pending. Seven: one lesson, double-entry bookkeeping, first principles, delivered. Eight: one pair of boots, borrowed, size large, condition poor—*Quentin, your boots, look at your feet, you're bleeding—*"

I looked at my feet. They were bleeding. Mortal blood, mortal pain, and the pain was an anchor-line, and I grabbed it.

"Nine," Mara said, and her voice cracked and steadied, the way it did over the ledger when the numbers fought her and she won anyway. "Nine: one refusal, at the river, to let a certain fool pay a toll with his own name. Entered against my account. Cost: considerable. Value: not for sale. Ten: one map, carried through ashfall and ambush and one thoroughly illegal auction, by two people, jointly, neither of whom owns it alone."

The garden flickered. My mother's smile wavered at the edges like a reflection in troubled water.

*She is pricing you,* the land whispered. *Everything has a price. Come where nothing is priced.*

"She's not pricing me," I heard myself say—my mouth, returned to me, my voice scraped raw. "She's *counting* me. There's a difference. A price says what you cost. A count says what you're worth." My knees hit the ground. The grey shimmer churned around me, furious, grieving, offering one last vision of silver leaves and a woman with my cheekbones, arms open. "I'm sorry," I told her, or the Marches, or both. "I can't come home to a place where I owe nothing. I'd disappear. I'd be nobody's. I've been nobody's my whole life. I'm done with it."

The shimmer broke like a fever breaking.

* * *

I came back to myself in stages: the cold, the blood, the boots lying neatly side by side ten paces back—Mara must have fetched them, gods, she'd run into the unmappable mile barefoot herself to fetch my boots—and Mara kneeling in front of me with her notebook open and her hand fisted in my collar, holding on like I was a page she refused to let the wind take.

"Eleven," she said, and her voice was wrecked and furious and steady, all three at once. "One rescue, from the Sundered Marches, performed by one Mara Pemberton, payment demanded: none. Payment accepted: none. This entry does not balance, Quentin. It stays open. Forever. Do you understand me?"

"I understand you," I croaked.

"Good." She released my collar, sat back on her heels, and pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes for a moment. When she lowered them, she was Mara again, dry-eyed, precise, terrifying. "New rule. From tonight forward, neither of us sleeps within fifty paces of the seam without waking the other every two hours. Also, you're eating. Also—" she thrust the notebook at me, "—read entry eleven. Read it out loud. I want it witnessed."

I read it. My voice shook on *payment accepted: none*, and I found I couldn't finish the last line, so she finished it for me, quietly, the way you seal an oath.

"*Entered in mutual trust. Carried forward.*"

We sat there in the blue ash-light while the sky went from black to bruise to the pale grey of a dawn that existed on both sides of the seam. My feet left red prints on ground that slowly, grudgingly, agreed to hold them. Ahead, the shimmer was thinner now, and through it—faint, unmistakable—we could both see it: a stone, standing alone in a field of snow that hadn't gotten the message about the season, marked on the bleeding map with a single drop of red that pulsed like a heartbeat.

Solstice was tomorrow.

"Quentin," Mara said, pulling her boots on. "When we cross, and the stone asks what you'll surrender—it's going to ask, the old accounts all agree on that much—you should know your answer beforehand. Because out there, in the grey, you nearly gave it everything. And I don't think the stone will be any gentler than the Marches were."

I looked at the stone. I looked at her—this woman who had walked into a country that doesn't exist to read me my own debts until I remembered having a self.

"I know my answer," I said.

And far off, at the edge of hearing, beneath the earth, something ancient turned over in its sleep and began, softly, to count.
