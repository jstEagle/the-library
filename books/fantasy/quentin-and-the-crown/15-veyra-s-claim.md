## 15. Veyra's Claim

The cellar under Crane's waystation smelled of tallow and old arithmetic.

I mean that literally. Hedge-witches of Crane's particular school mark their stores with sigils that carry a scent — his were all about ownership, and ownership, in my experience, smells like candle grease and the inside of a strongbox. I had spent six years breathing that smell. I knew it the way you know a scar: not because you look at it, but because it's yours.

I was in a cage of it now, technically. A binding circle chalked on the floor, three feet across, tuned to my name — the name he'd given me at purchase, *Mara*, which he liked to say was short for *margin*, because everything in his house existed to be measured against something else. He'd left me there while he went upstairs to draft paperwork. That was the thing about Aldous Crane that no one warned you about: he wasn't dramatic. He was *procedural*. He would ruin your life in triplicate and have you countersign it.

I sat in the circle and did what I always did when trapped: I counted.

Fourteen knots in the floorboard over my head. Two hundred and eleven heartbeats since his boots stopped creaking above me. One enforcer standing guard by the stairs, a big man named Harrow whom I remembered vaguely from guild business — one of Veyra's people, seconded to Crane for the escort, standing very still in the way that hired muscle stands still when it would rather be anywhere else.

And one debt outstanding between me and the world, signed when I was thirteen years old and too frightened to read past the first page.

I had signed myself back into that debt two nights ago, in a twilight market full of things that wanted names, to buy Quentin's passage to the covenant-stone. It had felt, at the time, like the only clean transaction of my life. I had walked into Crane's camp at dawn and said, "Here I am. Honor the exchange." And he had smiled — Crane's smile, the one that meant an entry had posted correctly — and put me in a circle.

He thought he'd won. That was the funny part. The genuinely hilarious part, if I'd been in a mood to laugh, which I was not.

"Your master intends to travel tonight," Harrow said. Not to me, exactly. To the room. To the tallow-scented air. Men like Harrow talk to rooms when they can't decide whether they're talking to themselves.

"Of course he does," I said. "Solstice is tomorrow. Everything comes due at solstice."

"You know a lot about it."

"I keep books." I drew my knees up. "Everything is a book, if you look long enough."

He was quiet for a while. Then, in a lower voice, almost embarrassed by its own existence: "The girl who ran from him. Years back. That was you."

"Six years ago. He tells it differently. In his version I stole from him."

"Did you?"

"No. I took back the only thing that was ever mine, which was me, and even that I had to do badly, at night, in the rain, without shoes." I looked at him. "Why? Are you collecting the story for him?"

Harrow shifted his weight. Big men shifting their weight is a whole language; this particular sentence translated roughly as *no, and I don't know why I asked, and I hate that I asked.*

"He has papers," Harrow said finally. "For the Marches. For after. I carried them from the Gildenholt. I'm not supposed to have read them."

My heart did something unprofessional. "But you did."

"I read everything I carry. Habit. You want to know what you're hauling before someone makes you part of the load." He glanced at the stairs. "You're a ledger-keeper. You'll want to see them."

"I can't," I said, gesturing at the circle around me. "I'm inventory."

"Not yet." And then Harrow did the single most expensive thing I have ever watched a man do, more expensive than any entry in the Gildenholt's impossible catalogue: he stepped forward and dragged his boot through the chalk.

* * *

The binding didn't shatter so much as *sigh* — a sound like a contract being torn along the perforations, which is exactly what it was. The pressure that had been sitting on my chest since dawn lifted, and I stood, and my legs remembered they belonged to a person rather than a line item.

"Why?" I asked Harrow. It seemed important to ask while he was still capable of answering.

He rubbed the back of his neck. "Twelve years I've worked for the Gildenholt. Twelve years, and I told myself we were the honest ones. We weigh things. We price things fair. The fae cheat, the witches cheat, but the ledger doesn't lie." His jaw worked. "Then I read those papers, and I understood that the ledger's been lying so long it doesn't remember the truth. There's a village in the Ashfall called Merrow's Bend. My mother's people. They sold their luck to the guild eight years ago when the rains failed — fair price, honest weighing, all of it. I passed through last month. Half the houses are empty. The luck never came back, because—" he stopped, and started again, quieter. "Because it was never going to come back. It was never *supposed* to. Read the papers."

So I did.

Crane kept his documents in a lacquered case under his bed, because men like him believe locks are a moral position. Harrow got the case; I got the papers open on the table while the candles guttered; and I read, and reading, I began to understand the shape of the thing we had been running toward without ever seeing it whole.

The first document was familiar in structure, because I had written its ancestors: a service agreement. The Gildenholt, party of the first part, would provide logistical support, armed escort, and financial instruments to Aldous Crane, party of the second part, in consideration of which Crane would deliver to the guild — and here my eye snagged, the way an eye snags on a misaligned column —

— *the reforged covenant, executed under terms favorable to the Gildenholt, with the Gildenholt designated sole and perpetual intermediary between the human realm and the hidden folk.*

Sole. Perpetual. Intermediary.

I set the page down. Picked it up again, because surely I'd misread. I don't misread. Misreading is for amateurs and optimists.

Every crossing between the worlds. Every toll, every trade, every name sold back to its owner, every borrowed year and bottled storm — routed through the Gildenholt, stamped and taxed and skimmed, forever. Not the repair of the covenant. The *franchising* of it. Veyra hadn't sent Quentin and me north to mend the breach between realms. She'd sent us north to do the dangerous work of opening it, so that she could stand in the doorway afterward and charge admission on both sides until the sun burned out.

"She let us go," I said aloud. "In chapter and verse, she let us go. Too easily. I knew it was too easy."

"The tithe auction," Harrow said. "The forged entries. She found out within the hour. She always finds out within the hour."

"And she let us run anyway, because we were doing her prospecting for her. Finding the stone. Waking the road. All the risky parts." I laughed, and it came out wrong, a dry cracked thing. "She didn't need to trap us. She just needed to follow us. We've been her survey crew since the day Quentin bled on my counting-room floor."

There was a third document, and this one I read twice, slowly, because it was addressed to Crane and it was in Veyra's own hand, and Veyra's hand is beautiful, copperplate with flourishes, the handwriting of a woman who has never once written anything she didn't mean.

*Re: the hedge-witch.* 

*Her value lies in the second hand. Once the rite is done, she is surplus. See that the Crane indenture is enforced to its full term — forty years, I believe, plus penalties accrued during her flight. The guild will not object. Sentiment is a rounding error, Master Crane, and I have never once rounded in your favor or anyone else's.*

Forty years. Plus penalties. I did the arithmetic the way I do all arithmetic, instantly and helplessly, the gift that had priced me cheap to every person who ever held my contract: I was twenty-six. Forty years took me to sixty-six. Penalties for six years of flight, compounded annually at the rate specified in an indenture I'd signed at thirteen, added another nine.

Seventy-five years old. That was the number Aldous Crane had purchased when he bought my debt in the twilight market. That was what my freedom from him had actually cost, converted back into bondage at the moment of maximum usefulness. Veyra had known. Veyra had *priced* it. Somewhere in the Gildenholt there was a column with my name at the top and my remaining lifespan at the bottom, entered as an asset, depreciating.

I want to tell you that I wept, or raged, or threw something. What I actually did was worse and better: I got angry in the specific, surgical way that has been my only true talent all my life. I read the rest of the file. Every clause. Every contingency. Because if Veyra had built a machine, then the machine had tolerances, and tolerances are where machines fail.

* * *

We left the waystation an hour before midnight, Harrow carrying a lantern he had no intention of lighting and I carrying Crane's entire paper empire folded against my ribs like stolen bread.

The snow had that solstice-eve quality, falling straight down, unhurried, as though the sky were settling accounts. Behind us the waystation windows glowed yellow where Crane worked late over his drafts. Ahead, north, the world went black and white toward the Sundered Marches, and somewhere out there Quentin was walking alone toward a stone that would ask him for everything.

"He'll make the border by dawn if the weather holds," Harrow said. "After that the map takes him. Fae roads don't care about weather."

"I know. I taught him the accounting of favors; he taught me that some debts aren't numbers." I pulled my coat tighter. "What I don't know is how to get there before Crane does. He'll discover the circle broken within the hour. He'll discover the papers gone within two. He travels light and fast and he knows these roads better than I know my own handwriting."

"There's a horse," Harrow said. "Guild mount, stabled at the crossroads inn. Branded. Technically I'm stealing it."

"Technically?"

"I'm also stealing its saddle." He looked down at me, and in the dark his face was hard to read, but his voice wasn't. "Twelve years, Mistress Pemberton. I weighed things fair and told myself that was enough. It isn't. Fair scales in a crooked shop are just crookedness with good manners."

I stopped walking. Snow gathered on my shoulders and I let it, because there was suddenly something in my throat that needed a moment.

"Harrow," I said. "When this is over — if this is over — the new covenant is going to have an open ledger. Every entry visible. Every debt public. And the border villages are going to need people who know both sides of a balance sheet and aren't afraid to say when the scale's been tipped." I stuck out my hand, cold fingers and all. "That's not charity. That's a job offer. Terms negotiable later. I'm in no position to pay wages."

He looked at my hand for a long moment. Then he shook it, once, gravely, like a man closing a contract he intended to honor past the point of sense.

"Wages in luck," he said. "Merrow's Bend could use rain more than coin."

"Rain's coming," I said. "It's in the fine print."

* * *

The horse was where he said, a gray mare with the Gildenholt's compass-rose brand and an opinion about being saddled at midnight. We rode north two abreast until the crossroads, where our routes had to divide: Harrow back south, to cover our tracks and misfile Crane's pursuit orders — a man after my own heart, committing fraud in service of the truth — and me onward, alone, toward the Marches and the stone and whatever Quentin was about to face at sunrise.

At the fork he reined in. "One more thing," he said. "The papers. The intermediary clause. When you get there — when the rite starts — Veyra will have a claim filed. Guild law says a filed claim must be heard before the covenant seals. She'll stand right there at the stone, lawful as morning, and demand the covenant recognize the Gildenholt as sole intermediary, and by the old forms, it will have to consider it."

"So we don't use the old forms."

"You think it's that simple?"

"I think," I said, "that every contract ever written has a flaw, and the flaw is always the same one: the person who wrote it assumed everyone else would play fair while they cheated. Veyra built her claim on guild law. But the covenant is *older* than guild law. Older than the guild. If the reforging demands a debt freely carried by both hands—" I heard it as I said it, the shape of the answer arriving whole, the way answers do when you've been carrying the question long enough — "then it also demands that both hands be *free*. No indenture. No claim. No intermediary standing between. You can't freely carry a debt through a tollbooth, Harrow. Someone's skimming on the way through."

"That's a legal argument."

"That's an *accounting* argument. Worse. Legal arguments can be appealed." I turned the mare north. "Tell your mother's village to watch the sky."

I rode. The snow thinned as the land changed under it — I felt the shift before I saw it, the way you feel a stair that isn't there, the ground remembering it used to be two places. The Sundered Marches opened ahead of me under a sky that couldn't decide on a color, and somewhere in that bruised half-light, a stone waited with a question only two hands could answer.

Behind me, faint on the wind, I thought I heard hooves. Crane, or Veyra's riders, or only the land dreaming its old traffic. It didn't matter. Let them come with their claims and their clauses and their beautiful copperplate greed.

I had read every word of their contract now.

And I knew exactly where it broke.
