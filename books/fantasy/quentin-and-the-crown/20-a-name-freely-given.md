## 20. A Name, Freely Given

The counting-house smelled of ink and candle-wax and, faintly, of the storm we'd bottled back in autumn—the one Veyra had finally released into the guild's cellar as an act of what she called *goodwill* and I called *inventory management*. Three weeks since the solstice. Three weeks since Crane's indenture had come apart in my hands like wet paper, since the covenant had sealed itself through two pairs of hands and one shared debt, since Quentin had stood in the snow and refused a true name offered by his own mother's ghost.

Three weeks of me not sleeping properly, because I'd been doing arithmetic in the dark.

Not guild arithmetic. Personal arithmetic. The kind that doesn't balance.

I sat at my desk with the great ledger open before me—the new one, the open ledger, the one whose existence had made Veyra smile like a woman swallowing vinegar—and I stared at a blank line.

Here is something they don't teach you in any counting-house, hedge-cellar, or fae twilight market: names are entries. Every name in every book I'd ever kept was a line item. *Mara Pemberton, apprentice, valued at eleven years' labor.* *Quentin, courier, held in trust, ownership disputed.* Even the covenant, in its old form, had recorded the hidden folk as *counterparties* and the human realm as *debtor*, as if a whole world could be filed under *outstanding*. I had spent my life believing numbers were honest because people weren't, and here was the flaw in my creed, sitting on the page like a spider: a number is only as honest as the thing it's counting. And a name entered as an asset is a lie, no matter how neatly you rule the column.

Quentin had been entered wrong his entire existence. Held in trust by the dead. Claimed by Crane. Sought by Veyra. Offered a name by a ghost who wanted the world severed in exchange. Everyone who had ever written him down had written him down as *property pending transfer*.

I meant to be the first to write him down correctly.

Which left the question of what, precisely, correct looked like.

* * *

He found me there past midnight, because he always did. He had a courier's instinct for knowing where a thing was needed, and apparently what the counting-house needed at midnight was someone to lean in the doorway and watch me frown at paper.

"You've rewritten that same line four times," he said. "I could see the scratching from the stairs."

"It's a difficult entry."

"Is it mine?"

I didn't answer, which was answer enough. He came around the desk and looked down at the ledger, and I watched him read his own name—his old one, the fragmentary thing, *Quentin, held in trust*—and then look at the space beneath it where I'd ruled a fresh line and left it empty.

"What goes there?" he asked.

"That," I said, "is the problem. I've been trying to solve it for three weeks."

"Three weeks." He pulled up the stool across from me, the one with the wobbly leg I kept meaning to fix and never did, because a counting-house should have at least one imperfection to keep it humble. "You reforge a thousand-year covenant between two worlds, and the entry that keeps you up at night is—"

"Yours. Yes."

He went quiet. Quentin did quiet well; he'd had practice being unclaimed, and unclaimed things learn to take up little space. But lately he'd been taking up more—not loudly, just *presently*, the way a hearth takes up a room without asking permission. He turned the ledger toward himself and studied the empty line as though it might confess.

"Veyra tried to have me entered last week," he said. "Did she tell you? An asset classification. *Bridge, inter-realm, singular.* She wanted it in the master inventory."

"I know. I struck it."

"She said the guild has first claim on anything that crosses between worlds."

"The guild *had* first claim. Then the covenant grew an open-ledger clause, and now her claims have to survive daylight and public reading, and she hates it more than she hated losing the monopoly, which I would not have believed possible." I tapped the struck-through line. "She can classify a bridge. She cannot classify you. You're not infrastructure."

"No," he said softly. "I'm told I'm a counterparty."

"Told by whom?"

"The covenant. It sends receipts now. Did yours not—" He caught my expression and grinned. "Mara Pemberton. You get receipts from a millennium-old treaty and you haven't mentioned it."

"They're addressed to *both hands jointly*. Opening your mail felt presumptuous."

"We reforged a covenant together and you're worried about opening my mail."

"I'm worried about everything. It's my best quality. Ask anyone."

But the grin faded off his face the way frost fades off a window, slow and from the edges in, and he looked back down at the blank line, and I understood—I suddenly, completely understood—that he knew what I was circling. He'd known for three weeks too. Two people who are good at accounts always know when one is being drafted.

"You're going to give me a name," he said.

"If you'll let me."

"My mother offered me one." His voice stayed level, but his hand flattened against the ledger page, pressing the paper flat as though it might buckle under what he was feeling. "A true name. Fae-made. It would have fit me like—like a key fits a lock. Perfectly. Because it would have been *made* for me, by someone who made things to be used." He looked up. "And I said no. And some nights I wake up and wonder if I said no out of wisdom or out of spite, and whether there's a difference, and whether a name refused is a door closed or just a door I'll spend the rest of my life standing outside of."

"And what do you conclude on those nights?"

"That I don't want a key. Keys imply locks. Locks imply owners." He exhaled. "I want whatever it is you're ruling lines for."

So I told him. Because he deserved the whole account, not the summary, and because I had learned—one broken indenture, one reforged covenant, one lifetime of being priced cheaply ago—that the things most worth saying are the ones that cost the speaker most to say.

* * *

"When I ran from Crane," I began, and stopped, and started again, because I had told this story perhaps twice in my life and both times as a joke, and jokes are how cowards pay themselves interest on courage deferred. "When I ran, I took nothing. You know that part. What you don't know is why I took nothing. It wasn't bravery. It was bookkeeping."

He waited. He was very good at waiting. Couriers are.

"Crane taught me that everything has a price, and I believed him, because he'd set mine at eleven years and never once pretended otherwise. So when I fled, I fled *clean*. No stolen goods, no unpaid debts, nothing he could file against me. I walked out of that cellar owning exactly myself and owing exactly nothing, and I told myself for ten years that this was freedom." I laughed, and it came out with edges. "It wasn't freedom. It was a zero balance. I was solvent, Quentin. Solvent is not the same as rich. I trusted numbers because every person who'd ever handled me had rounded me down, and a number never pretends to be more than it is. A number never says it loves you and then sells your apprenticeship to settle a gambling debt."

"Crane gambled?" Quentin asked quietly.

"On rain, the year the covenant's rot dried out the western valleys. He bet against the weather and lost, and settled the loss with three years of my labor, transferred to a moneylender in Corvess, without telling me until the man arrived to collect." I turned my palm up. "That's what a name entered as an asset gets you. That's what *held in trust* means, when trust is spelled t-r-u-s-t but reads *collateral*. So yes. I'm giving you a name. And I am going to be extremely careful about how it's entered, because I have seen every way a name can be owned, and I refuse—*refuse*—to add one more."

"And the name itself?" he said. "Where does it come from? Names aren't minted, Mara. They're grown. Family, godparents, the folk who—"

"The folk who choose you. Yes. I've been reading the covenant's annexes." I pulled the second ledger toward me—the covenant ledger, the living one, the one that wrote back sometimes, which still unsettled me in ways I declined to examine. "The old terms say a name is 'a debt freely carried.' Not given. Carried. The giver carries it—keeps it, honors it, answers for it—and the bearer carries it too, wears it, spends it, makes it mean things its maker never intended. Both hands. Same principle as the reforging." I met his eyes. "There's no precedent for a name with no bloodline behind it and no price attached. We'd be writing new law. Again."

"We're good at that," he said. "It's been about six weeks since either of us did anything with precedent."

* * *

We worked through the small hours, and I will record honestly that the first three attempts were disasters.

Attempt one was too clever. I drafted him a name built from ledger-terms—something with *balance* woven through it, *carried forward*, all my own vocabulary pressed into service—and he read it and said, kindly, "It sounds like a receipt," and he was right, and I burned the draft in the candle rather than let it sulk in a drawer.

Attempt two was too heavy. I reached for weight—old words, border words, the kind of name that comes with hills attached—and he read it and said, "This is a name for someone who already belongs somewhere. It assumes a home I haven't got yet." Also right. Also burned.

Attempt three I didn't show him. I sat with it a long time, this third draft, and finally slid it across the desk face-down and said, "Read it or don't. If you don't, we never speak of it, and I try again tomorrow, and the day after, for as long as it takes. There's no deadline. That's rather the point."

He turned it over.

I had written: **Wren**.

Not Quentin-renamed. Not a replacement. A second name, a freely chosen one, to sit beside the fragment he'd carried since birth—the piece of himself the dead had held in trust, which the covenant had since returned to him, unowned, his at last and no one else's. He could keep Quentin. Quentin had earned keeping; it was the name he'd bled through the Sundered Marches under, the name he'd whispered to a bleeding map, the name I'd first heard gasped against my counting-house floor with the words *the debt comes due at solstice*. But Quentin was a name history had handed him sideways, half-finished, a courier's tag. Wren would be the name nobody had ever required of him. Small bird. Loud song. Lives at the edge of things, in hedgerows and borders, and sings anyway—especially in winter, especially when there's no reason to.

Underneath it I had written the entry itself, and this was the part I'd rewritten four times:

*Borrower: none.*
*Lender: none.*
*Terms: carried forward, both hands, in perpetuity.*
*Consideration exchanged: none. Consideration is the name.*

He read it three times. His finger traced the word *none* each pass, gentle, the way you'd touch something you feared might be a trick.

"There's no consideration," he said. "Every contract needs consideration. That's the first law of—"

"Of contracts, yes. This isn't one. That's the whole point. A contract is two parties protecting themselves from each other. This is—" I gestured vaguely at the air, at the candle, at the ridiculous enormity of what I was trying to say. "This is me deciding that you exist to me outside of exchange. I know that's financially irresponsible. I've decided to live with it."

"Mara."

"Don't make it a moment. I'll cry, and I have a reputation as a flint-hearted auditor to maintain."

"Mara." He said it the way you'd set down something fragile. "My mother's name would have opened doors. Any door in the hidden courts, forever. It would have *cost* me—it would have bound me to her side of the veil, made me hers again, made me *theirs*—but it would have opened doors." He looked at the page. "This opens no doors. This is just... a small brown bird that sings in hedges."

"The best things I know are small brown birds that sing in hedges."

"Why a wren?" he asked, and his voice had gone rough, and I pretended not to notice because that was the gift I could give him alongside the name: the courtesy of not making him perform his feelings for an audience.

"Because on the road north," I said, "the night after Crane's hounds, when you thought I was asleep—you sang. Badly. Some border tune about a wren who out-stubborned the winter. And I lay there thinking, that is the most absurd, stubborn, unkillable sound I have ever heard, and it is coming out of a man who owns nothing, belongs nowhere, and refuses on principle to stop. And I thought: if he ever gets a name of his own choosing, it should be that bird." I shrugged, suddenly embarrassed, staring hard at my beautifully ruled columns. "Also you're small. Relatively. For a courier."

"I'm average height."

"You're wren-height and we both know it."

He laughed—that laugh, the one that had gotten rarer over the hunted months and commoner over the mending weeks, the one that sounded like a door unbarred—and then he did something I hadn't planned for, because I plan for everything and he has spent a year teaching me that the best entries are the ones you can't foresee.

He picked up the pen.

"Not refusing," he said. "Amending. With your leave, partner."

Partner. The word landed in my chest and stayed there, warm, like a coal carried in cupped hands.

"With my leave," I managed.

He added a single line beneath my entry, in his courier's quick slanting hand: *Accepted freely, both hands, witnessed by the covenant, which is watching and taking notes.* And below that, smaller: *First thing ever given to me without a price. Learning how to hold it. Give me time.*

Then he signed it. **Wren**, in his own writing, next to **Wren** in mine. Two hands. One line.

The covenant ledger, beside us, rustled its pages once—approvingly, I chose to believe, though with that document one can never entirely rule out sarcasm—and somewhere in the walls of the Gildenholt, faint and far off, something knocked politely. Twice. Paused. Twice again.

We looked at each other.

"That's the third time this week," Quentin said.

"I know."

"The hidden folk don't knock. They arrive, they bargain, they leave riddles."

"I know that too."

"So why," he said, rising, and there was the courier in him again, the man who ran toward debts coming due instead of away, "are they knocking like neighbors?"

I closed the ledger on our two names, side by side, no debts between them, and reached for my coat.

"Because," I said, "apparently we're the sort of establishment people call on now. Come on, Wren. Let's see who's at the door."

* * *

The knock, when we answered it together, was a sparrow made of river-light, standing on the threshold with a sealed scroll in its beak and an expression of tremendous official gravity, and the seal it bore was the covenant's own twin-hands mark, fresh-melted, which meant only one thing.

New business. First honest season beginning. And the border villages—starved of luck, rain, and memory for a hundred years—were owed their spring.

The sparrow dropped the scroll at my feet, bowed with ironic precision to each of us in turn, and vanished.

Quentin picked up the scroll, broke the seal, and read aloud: "*Audit requested. All border settlements. Bring your own ledger. Signed, both hands.*"

He looked at me. I looked at him. Outside, far to the north, I would have sworn the wind smelled of rain.

"Well, partner," he said. "Shall we go count what the world owes itself?"

I fetched the new ledger. Of course I did. I was, after all, the one who kept the books.
