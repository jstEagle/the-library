## 21. The Open Ledger

*Mara*

The rain came back to Hollowmere on a Tuesday, which pleased me disproportionately, because Tuesday was the day the village had always set aside for mending nets, and there is something deeply satisfying about an entry that balances.

I stood in the doorway of the tithe-barn—no longer a tithe-barn, actually; the villagers had voted to rename it the Rain House, which was either sentiment or spite toward the old covenant's collectors, and I approved of it either way—and watched water fall on ground that hadn't felt it in three generations. An old woman named Bess Corrow put her hand out into it and wept without any particular effort to hide the fact. Beside her, her grandson caught raindrops on his tongue like he'd heard of the practice from a story and wasn't entirely sure it was allowed.

"First honest season," Quentin said beside me. He said it the way he said most things now, quietly, as if loudness might summon a bill.

"First audited season," I corrected. "Honesty is what we're here to verify."

He gave me the look he gave me when my humor went too dry even for him, which was a look I had come to treasure in the way one treasures a hearth: not exciting, but warm, and reliably there.

We had been walking the Marches for six weeks. The Gildenholt's new mandate—the open-ledger clause, written into the reforged covenant in ink that Veyra had signed with the expression of a woman swallowing a lemon whole—required that every exchange between realms be recorded, reviewed, and made available to both parties. Which meant someone had to walk the border villages and audit them. Which meant, because I was the only person alive who could read the covenant's terms without weeping, and Quentin was the only person alive who could knock on a fae door without being turned into something ornamental, that someone was us.

I had expected gratitude. I want to be honest about that, because honesty is the whole point of the exercise now, and I have spent enough of my life lying to myself to know how the entries accumulate. I had expected gratitude, and I got something better and stranger: I got *work*. Villages didn't thank us and send us on our way. They fed us, yes—they fed us extravagantly, in the way of people who remember hunger—but then they sat us down at their tables and produced grievances. Disputes. Questions. A widow in Ashenford who claimed a fae hedgerow-keeper owed her eleven years of good harvests and couldn't produce the original agreement. A miller who had sold his shadow before the covenant broke and wanted to know if the new terms entitled him to a refund with interest.

"They don't want charity," Quentin had observed, somewhere around week two, watching me draft a repayment schedule by firelight while the miller's wife pressed a third helping of stew on us both. "They want to be taken seriously."

"Being taken seriously is the only charity worth having," I said, and he looked at me for a long moment, and I realized I had said something true about myself by accident, and decided not to take it back.

* * *

*Quentin*

The thing nobody tells you about having half a soul in each world is that you spend your whole life being a stranger in both, and then one day you're walking down a muddy lane in the human Marches with a ledger under your arm and a witch at your side, and a farmer's wife calls you "the covenant lad," and you realize you've stopped flinching at doors.

I still checked them. Old habits. But the checking had become ritual rather than survival, the way a sailor keeps counting knots long after the storm has passed.

Mara was arguing with a fae orchard-warden when I found her that afternoon—or rather, the warden was arguing, and Mara was doing the thing she did, which was to stand perfectly still and let the other party exhaust themselves against her patience like weather against a stone wall. The warden was seven feet of hawthorn and grievance, and he had been talking for the better part of an hour.

"—and furthermore, the mortal side has taken *four* baskets of early apples beyond the agreed share, and under the old arrangement such overreach would have cost them a child's laughter for a season—"

"Under the old arrangement," Mara said, "your side took a well's memory in payment for a fence dispute, and the village of Dunlow forgot how to find water for nine years. So let's agree the old arrangements aren't the standard we're aiming for." She opened the ledger. "New terms, section four: overdraws are repaid in kind, at fair value, within one turning of the moon. Four baskets of apples means four baskets of apples come autumn—not laughter, not luck, not names. Apples." She looked up. "Unless you'd prefer to file a formal grievance with the covenant court, in which case I'll need your true name for the record."

The warden went very still. Fae do that when you mention true names; it's the one threat that works on all of them equally. Then he did something I had never seen one do in all my years running errands for the Seelie remnant.

He laughed. It sounded like wind through leaves, which I suppose it was.

"You're enjoying this," he accused her.

"I'm an accountant," Mara said. "This is the happiest I have ever been."

I believed her. That was the remarkable thing—I had known her through ambushes and bargains and a solstice gate opening onto snow, and I had never seen her as content as she was standing in an orchard mud telling a tree-spirit about repayment schedules. Crane had taught her numbers as a leash. She had turned the leash into a bridge. I kept waiting for the part where I understood how a person did that, and I suspected I would be waiting a long time, and I suspected that was fine.

The warden left with a copy of the terms pressed between two leaves of himself—he grew a bark pocket for it, which Mara declared "an excellent filing system"—and after he'd gone, she sat down on a fallen apple tree and let out a breath she'd probably been holding since Dunlow.

"He'll contest the interest clause," she said.

"He absolutely will."

"Good. Contests mean engagement. Engagement means the covenant is working." She rubbed her eyes. "How many villages left?"

"Three on the mortal side. Two courts on ours." I hesitated. "And the crossing at Hollowmere wants to see you specifically. The toll-keeper."

Mara's pen stopped moving.

"The one who wanted your name," she said.

"The same."

* * *

*Mara*

I will admit, freely and for the record, that I had been avoiding thinking about the toll-keeper.

At the river crossing, back when Quentin and I were fugitives with nothing but a bleeding map and a shared talent for trouble, the toll-keeper had demanded a name as passage-price, and Quentin—who had spent his entire existence being told his name belonged to dead masters, to fae creditors, to anyone but himself—had started offering pieces of himself like a man selling organs to pay rent. And I had done the only thing I knew how to do: I had opened a negotiation. A promissory note, sealed in witch-oath, promising the toll-keeper a debt to be named later.

It had seemed brilliant at the time. It had seemed brilliant right up until I remembered that promissory notes mature, and debts to be named later must eventually be named, and I am constitutionally incapable of leaving an open entry unbalanced.

The toll-keeper's house—if you could call it that; it was a structure woven from river-reeds and standing water, and it existed slightly to the left of where your eyes insisted it was—smelled of wet stone and old paper. The keeper herself sat behind a desk that was clearly a tribute to my profession, which should have flattered me and instead set every hedge-witch instinct I owned humming warnings.

"Mara Pemberton," she said. Not a question. "Of the Gildenholt. Co-forger of the covenant. You've been busy."

"Seasonal fluctuations," I said. "You hold a note of mine. I've come to settle it."

"Have you." She drew the note from somewhere inside the desk—which is to say, from somewhere inside the concept of the desk—and laid it flat. My own handwriting stared up at me, younger and more frightened than I remembered it. "A debt to be named later. Do you know what I could have done with this, these past months? I could have called it in as anything. Your voice. Your memory of your mother's face. First claim on whatever name you love best." She tapped the paper. "Instead I held it. Do you know why?"

"Because you're bound by the new covenant's fair-value clauses?" I offered.

"Because I wanted to see what you'd do with freedom." Her eyes were the color of river-bottom, and they missed nothing. "The old world priced everything, little accountant. Names, years, luck, grief. Everything had a tag. And then you and the nameless boy stood on a stone in the snow and invented something the old ledgers had no column for—a debt carried freely, by choice, by both hands—and the whole architecture of pricing shuddered." She slid the note across the desk. "So I'm returning it. Unmarked. Settled by observation alone."

I looked at the note. I looked at her. Every instinct I had learned at Crane's knee screamed that nothing was free, that this was a hook dressed as a gift, that the fine print was hiding somewhere beneath the reeds.

But I had helped write the covenant now. I had read its terms until they lived behind my eyelids. And section one, clause one, the clause we had argued over by candlelight while the solstice gate groaned open—the clause that had cost us everything and given back more—said plainly:

*A debt freely carried cannot be converted. A gift freely given creates no obligation.*

She wasn't hooking me. She was testing the clause. And it had held.

"Then I owe you nothing," I said slowly, "and you gave me a gift anyway."

"Now you understand the mathematics that broke the old world," the toll-keeper said, and smiled with too many teeth, but kindly. "Tell your guild-mistress the hidden folk are watching the open ledger. Some of us are hoping it holds."

* * *

*Quentin*

She came out of the reed-house quiet, which for Mara meant her mind was moving faster than her mouth, which for Mara meant something significant had happened. I had learned to wait. Waiting for Mara was never wasted time; the conclusions, when they arrived, were always worth the freight.

"It held," she said finally. "The clause. She tried to give me something for free and the covenant let her."

"That sounds like good news."

"It's *enormous* news. Quentin, do you understand what that means? For a hundred years, every kindness between the worlds was either a transaction or a trap, because there was no framework for anything else. Now there is. Now a fae can give a gift and a human can accept it and neither of them ends up owning a piece of the other." She laughed—a short, disbelieving sound. "We wrote a loophole for generosity."

"We did," I said, and something in my chest did the thing it had been doing lately whenever she talked about our work as *ours*, which was to go warm and unsteady, like a banked fire remembering it was fire.

We walked back along the river in the long gold light. On the far bank—on the fae side, though the border had gone soft and permeable since the reforging, more seam than wall—a hedgerow-keeper waved at us. Just waved. No demand, no toll, no price. I waved back, and my hand didn't shake, and I noticed that it didn't shake, and I nearly had to sit down on the path from the sheer strangeness of a body that wasn't braced.

For most of my life I had been a courier, which is a polite word for a thing passed between owners. The Seelie remnant had used me. The hunters had chased me. My own mother had offered me a true name like bait on a hook, belonging dangled just out of reach unless I abandoned everything I'd chosen. And then a hedge-witch with a ledger had stood in the snow and refused to let me be bought, and had handed me a name of her own coining—*Quentin Vale*, she'd written it into the partnership page, partner rather than asset—and the astonishing thing, the thing I still hadn't gotten used to, was that it fit. Not because it was grand. Because it was given.

"Mara," I said.

"Hm?"

"The Hollowmere festival. Tomorrow night. The one where they light the Rain House and thank the covenant." I had rehearsed this sentence for two days, which was absurd, because I had once delivered sealed messages through a battlefield without rehearsal. "Bess Corrow asked if we'd sit at the head table. Both of us. By name."

Mara stopped walking.

"By name," she repeated.

"Yours and mine. She said—" I grinned, because I couldn't help it. "She said, 'the accountant and her covenant lad.' Apparently that's what we are now. To them."

I watched her absorb it. Mara Pemberton, who had run away from her master with nothing, who had trusted numbers because people always priced her cheap, who had spent a lifetime making herself small enough to be overlooked—watching her understand that a village full of strangers saw her, by name, as theirs. It was like watching a locked door discover it had been open all along.

"Well," she said at last, and her voice was not entirely steady, and I loved her the more for it. "I suppose we should audit the feast, then. Make sure the portions are fairly distributed."

"Naturally."

"And check the ale accounts."

"Someone has to."

She laughed, properly this time, and took my arm, and we walked toward the lights of Hollowmere coming on one window at a time, and behind us the river ran full and the far bank shimmered with things that were no longer hiding.

That was when the crow landed on the milestone ahead of us.

I say crow because it looked like a crow, but I had spent enough years among the hidden folk to know a messenger-shape when I saw one, and this one had the particular stillness of something carrying words it did not want to deliver. It regarded us with eyes like drops of ink. Then it opened its beak, and spoke in a voice like dry paper:

"A summons. From the deep court. For the co-forgers." A pause. "They say the first honest season has raised a question the covenant does not yet answer. They say—" the crow's feathers ruffled, discomfort made visible— "they say someone has filed a claim on the *next* century. And they want the two of you to hear the terms before the equinox."

Mara and I looked at each other.

"Veyra?" she said.

"No," I said slowly, because I recognized the seal now, wax-dark and star-shaped, pressed into the scroll the crow dropped at our feet. I had seen it once before, on the last letter my mother ever sent. "Worse. It's family."
