## 1. The Taster's Tongue

The broth arrived at dawn, as it always did, in a bowl of glazed river-clay that had been fired in the valley's own kilns and carried up the kitchen stair by a boy who would not meet her eyes.

Freya Whitlock sat alone at the taster's table — a narrow plank of ash set apart from the great workbenches, close enough to the ovens to feel their breath on her back, far enough from everything else to be forgotten. The bowl steamed. Barley, she thought. Leek tops. A thread of saffron gone slightly stale, the cook's economy showing at the seams. And beneath all of it, folded into the warmth like a secret sewn into a hem: something else.

She did not reach for the spoon at once. That was the first lesson they had taught her, when she was nine years old and small enough to sit cross-legged on this same bench with her feet dangling above the flagstones. *Look first. Smell second. Swallow last.* Master Aldous had said it with his hands cupped around hers, guiding her nose over the steam, and she had believed then that he was teaching her to survive. It had taken her another ten years to understand he was teaching her to be useful.

The kitchen moved around her in its morning choreography — scullery boys hauling water, the pastry women arguing over lard in low voices, the great spits already turning with nothing on them but their own momentum. No one looked at her. No one ever looked at her directly, as though her work were a kind of shame they had all agreed not to name. She was the most protected person in Emberhold and the least spoken to, and both of those things were true for the same reason.

Freya lifted the spoon.

The first swallow told her what she needed. She let the broth rest on her tongue — barley first, sweet and soft; then the leek, green and bitter at the root; then the saffron, dusty gold, a little past its prime. And under it, arriving late like a guest who hoped not to be noticed: a numbness. Faint. Cool. It began at the tip of her tongue and spread backward along the roof of her mouth, the way frost creeps across a windowpane — beautiful, if you didn't know what it meant.

*Hemlock extract,* she catalogued, setting the spoon down with a small precise click. *Diluted. Perhaps one part in forty. Introduced after cooking — the heat would have broken it otherwise, so someone added it at the pass, which means someone in the kitchens or someone standing over the kitchens.* A pause in her own mind, dry as ledger paper. *Someone testing me again.*

She took a second swallow. Then a third. Then she drank the bowl down to the glaze, because that was the work, and the work did not care whether she wanted it. The numbness spread to her lips, to her gums, to the soft hollow beneath her tongue where the body keeps its most honest alarms. Her fingertips began to prickle. Somewhere behind her ribs, her heart slowed by a fraction — not dangerously, not yet, just enough that she could feel it choosing each beat with unusual care.

By now she knew every poison the crown's enemies could buy, steal, or brew, and she knew them the way a musician knows scales: not as threats but as vocabulary. Wolfsbane sang high and cold, a bell rung inside the skull. Arsenic was patient, metallic, a slow gray tide. The nightshade family flirted — dryness, flushing, a dilation of the world. And hemlock, poor hemlock, was the coward's choice: cheap, common, detectable in three swallows by anyone trained, which is to say by anyone but the people it was meant for.

Her hands were steady. They were always steady. That was the other thing they had built into her, drop by drop, year by year — the immunities, layered like lacquer, until what would kill a king in an evening only made her sleepy. She flexed her fingers under the table where no one could see and waited for the wave to crest and pass.

It passed. It always passed. That was the point of her.

"Whitlock." Master Aldous stood at the end of the table, a ledger open against his forearm, his spectacles fogged with oven-steam. He was old the way oak is old — bent, grain-hardened, impossible to imagine young. "Report."

"Hemlock," Freya said. "One part in forty, maybe fifty. Added after the fire. The king's portion came from the east cauldron, so whoever dosed it had access to the pass between the third and fourth bells." She recited it flatly, the way she recited everything, and watched him write it down without surprise. "Also your saffron's stale. If you're going to poison the breakfast, at least serve good saffron with it."

Something flickered at the corner of his mouth — the ghost of a smile, quickly strangled. He had never once laughed at her jokes in nineteen years, but he had never once reported them either, and in Emberhold that was practically affection.

"Noted," he said. "You'll take the midday service as well. The Margrave of Hessel is visiting, and His Majesty wants his table watched personally."

"The Margrave." Freya turned her spoon over, studying her reflection in the curve of the glaze — a thin face, dark hair pinned back without ceremony, eyes the color of strong tea. "Is the Margrave likely to poison the king?"

"The Margrave is likely to be poisoned," Aldous said. "His Majesty prefers his rivals healthy until he's finished with them." He closed the ledger. "You'll taste everything that passes his lips too. Both directions."

Both directions. Guard the king from the guest; guard the guest from the king. It was, in its way, the most honest description of her office anyone had ever given her, and she felt the old resentment stir in her chest like a banked coal — familiar, warm, dangerous to touch. She fed it air anyway. It was the only fire she was permitted.

"I'll need the antidote kit refreshed," she said. "My emetic stock is down to two doses, and the charcoal's been cut with chalk again. Someone in the stores is skimming."

"Someone is always skimming the stores." But he wrote that down too, and Freya understood the small ritual for what it was: proof that she noticed things. Proof that she was sharp. Proof, though no one would ever say it aloud, that the crown's investment in her was sound.

They had bought her, dish by dish, sip by sip, since before she could remember. An orphan of the valley with no family name worth speaking, no kin to ask questions, no future except the one being built inside her body one immunity at a time. She was a vault they kept filling. A sword they never intended to draw. And every year the walls of her life grew a little higher, a little smoother, a little more comfortable — better rooms, finer wool, a key of her own to a door that opened only onto other locked doors.

Valued. Contained. She turned the words over like coins and found both of them debased.

* * *

The midday service was a performance, and Freya performed it well.

She walked the long gallery behind the king's steward with her hands folded and her spine straight, tasting from the silver as it passed — the quail in vine leaves, the honeyed carrots, the wine cut with water in the southern fashion. Each dish got its measured swallow, its pause, its small nod. The courtiers watched her do it the way people watch a clock: not seeing it, merely confirming it still runs.

The Margrave of Hessel sat at the king's right hand, a broad man with a laugh like a landslide and rings on six fingers. He ate enormously and drank more, and Freya tasted for him too, standing at his shoulder while he joked with the king about border levies, and she noted — because noting was the whole of her — that the Margrave's cupbearer never once drank from the cup himself, and that the man's boots were stained with road-dust from the north road, though the Margrave had officially come from the south.

Small things. Useless things. Things she collected the way a prisoner collects scratches on a wall: evidence of a world existing outside her cell, even if she could do nothing with any of it.

It was during the cheese course that the numbness came back.

Not hemlock this time. This was subtler — a faint silvery hush across her tongue, almost pleasant, like snow melting on skin. She swallowed carefully, set down the tasting knife, and ran the inventory. Not wolfsbane. Not belladonna. Not anything in her lexicon at all, and her lexicon was the finest in the kingdom, because it had been written in her flesh.

She raised her eyes, very slowly, and looked down the length of the high table.

No one was looking at her. Everyone was looking at her. The king was laughing at something the Margrave had said, and the laughter went on a half-beat too long, and the steward's hand rested on the back of her chair as though it had always rested there, and outside the tall windows the light was doing something strange — thickening, ambering, as though the sun itself had leaned closer to watch.

*What did you just give me?* she thought, at the room, at the crown, at whatever patient hand had seasoned her food for nineteen years. *And why does it taste like the valley?*

Because that was the thing she had never been able to say aloud, the thing she kept below even her resentments: everything in Emberhold tasted faintly of nothing. The valley was magic-dead — everyone knew it, everyone said it with relief, *thank the saints nothing follows us here* — and its food, its water, its very air had a flatness she had learned to read the way sailors read weather. But this hush on her tongue was not the valley's flatness. It was the valley's flatness *concentrated*. Deliberate. As if someone had distilled the absence itself and slipped it into her cup.

Her heart slowed again. Her thoughts went smooth and wide and strangely calm, and she heard herself say, in a voice that sounded like hers but pitched lower, "The course is clean," and saw the steward nod, and understood with perfect distant clarity that she had just lied for the first time in her professional life, and that no one had noticed, because no one ever imagined the taster might have a tongue of her own.

The meal ended. The court emptied. Freya walked back down through the kitchens with the silver hush still melting along her jaw, and paused at the taster's table to collect her shawl, and found that someone had left something there beside her bowl.

A scrap of paper, folded once, unmarked by any seal.

She should have called Aldous. She should have called the guard. Nineteen years of training said *report, report, report*, and her hand was actually moving toward the bell-cord when some older instinct — the one that had learned to catalogue poisons by surviving them — stopped her, unfolded the paper instead, and read:

*Millrace. Tonight. Come alone, or don't come, and we all die with the truth.*

Below the words, pressed into the wax that sealed the fold — no seal, just plain wax, but pressed deep — was the mark of a door.

Freya stood very still in the emptying kitchen, listening to the ovens tick as they cooled, feeling the strange calm drug hum in her blood like a second heartbeat.

Outside, far off toward the millrace, a bell she had never heard before rang once — low, cracked, and old — and stopped.
