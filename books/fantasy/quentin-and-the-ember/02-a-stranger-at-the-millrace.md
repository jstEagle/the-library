## 2. A Stranger at the Millrace

The morning after the numbness came the ache, and Freya had learned to love the ache.

It meant her body was arguing back. It meant the poison in last night's broth — a pale thing distilled from moonshade root, she'd catalogued it as *bitter, then sweet at the edges, like a lie told kindly* — was being beaten down by whatever stubborn chemistry twenty-two years of small doses had built into her blood. She sat on the edge of her narrow bed above the kitchens and let her fingertips throb and thought: *good girl. Keep fighting.*

Outside her window, Emberhold was waking the way it always woke: slow, gray, smelling of wet stone and woodsmoke. The valley held its mist until nearly midday, cradling it against the mountainsides like something precious. Somewhere below, the millrace turned with its patient wooden groan, the sound Freya had slept to every night of her life. She could not remember a time before that sound. She suspected there wasn't one.

She dressed, bound her hair, and went down into the heat of the kitchens, where Master Ollen already had her first tray waiting.

"Barley porridge," he said, not looking up from his ledger. "The king's brother takes breakfast in the west tower today. Cook wants your tongue on everything before it leaves."

Freya lifted the spoon. The porridge smelled of honey and brown butter and, beneath it, faintly, of almonds — too many almonds. She let the spoon rest on her tongue for three heartbeats, then swallowed, then waited, eyes closed, reading her own body like a page.

Tingling at the tip of the tongue. A thinning sensation, as though the taste itself were being peeled away. Not lethal. Not even dangerous, to her. But enough to stop an unguarded throat for an hour, maybe two, if the dose were doubled.

"Bittercress extract," she said. "Fourth time this month. Tell Cook his almond trick is getting lazy, and tell whoever's buying it that the valley apothecary is either careless or complicit."

Ollen's quill scratched. "Noted." He never asked who might be poisoning whom. That was the arrangement. She tasted; he recorded; the crown paid. Questions were not part of her wages.

That was the thing about being the royal taster of Emberhold — everyone knew her name and no one knew her. She was a fixture, like the millrace or the eastern wall. Valuable the way a lock is valuable: useful precisely because it stays in one place. Twenty-two years old, and her whole life measured out in sips and swallows, in the careful geography of her own mouth. She knew the flavor of forty poisons the way other women knew the faces of their cousins.

Some mornings she woke up furious about it. This morning she only felt the fury settle over her like the valley mist, familiar, close, impossible to see through.

*One dish at a time,* her mother used to say. *That's how you survive anything.* Her mother had been a taster too. And her mother's mother. Freya had never thought hard about what that meant, the way you don't think hard about the color of the sky.

She finished the tray — bread, cheese, stewed plums, each bite taken and read and reported — and Ollen dismissed her until the noon service. Which was why she was walking along the millrace with an hour to herself when the world cracked open.

* * *

He came out of the reeds like something the water had rejected.

She heard him first — a wet, dragging sound, a body moving when it should have been still — and turned to find a man on his knees at the edge of the race, half in the shallows, his hands pressed to his belly as if holding himself shut. He wore traveling leathers gone dark with blood. His face was gray as the mist.

Freya's body moved before her mind did. She'd seen injury before — kitchen burns, a carter's crushed hand — but never anything like this, never a man actively dying six feet away from her, and yet her feet carried her forward all the same, skirts hiked, kneeling in the mud without caring.

"Don't—" His hand shot out and caught her wrist with shocking strength. His eyes were the pale green of river glass, and they were not confused, not pleading. They were *urgent*. "Don't call for anyone. Listen. There's no time. They're already on the road."

"You need a surgeon—"

"I need you to *listen*." He coughed, and the sound was terrible, and red came up between his teeth. "You're Whitlock. The taster. I looked for you by name."

Cold walked up her spine. "Who are you?"

"A messenger." He laughed, or tried to, and it broke into another cough. "Two hundred years late, but a messenger." With his free hand he fumbled inside his coat and brought out a bundle wrapped in oilcloth and leather, tied with a cord gone soft with age. He pressed it into her hands and folded her fingers closed over it, both of his bloody hands around hers, as if in prayer.

"The map," he whispered. "It has to be a Whitlock. It can only be a Whitlock. Do you understand? You're the last heir of Vael."

The word went through her like a fishhook.

"Vael," she repeated. "There's no such place."

"There is." His grip tightened. "There is, and it's dying, and you are its heir, and they will kill you for that paper if they catch you holding it. Do you hear me? Not question you. Not arrest you. *Kill you.* The justiciar's riders left Emberford at dawn. Hours behind me. Maybe less."

"Who's *they*? The king?"

His mouth twisted. "The king." He said it strangely, the way you'd say the name of a disease. "Girl — Freya. Your mother was Maerin Whitlock. Her mother was Sefa. Sefa was born across the water, in a country that the histories say never existed. That's what the bargain bought. That's what the valley *is*."

The millrace groaned on behind them, patient as ever. The mist hung in the trees. Everything was exactly as it had been ten minutes ago, and nothing was.

"I don't understand any of this."

"No." He smiled, and it was almost gentle. "You will. The map will teach you. Read the true script — the script under the script. And when the solstice comes—" Another cough took him, longer this time, and when it passed he sagged, and she caught him, and his weight was wrong, too light, a body already halfway to being an object. "When the solstice comes, the door opens. Last door. Don't let them close it forever."

"What door? Whose side are you on? *Whose man are you?*"

"Vael's," he said simply. "Always. Even when Vael forgot itself." His eyes found hers and held them, and in them she saw no madness, which was somehow worse. Madness she could dismiss. This was certainty, purchased at the price of his life. "You taste everything before it reaches a royal mouth. Ask yourself, child — who taught your grandmother's grandmother to do that? Who decided a Whitlock's tongue was worth more than a Whitlock's life? Someone chose that for your family. Someone chose it *for* you, before you were born. Isn't it time you asked why?"

Then his strength went out of him all at once, like a candle pinched between wet fingers.

She knelt there in the mud holding a dead man, the leather-wrapped bundle crushed between their hands, and shouted anyway — shouted for help, for the miller, for anyone — because whatever else he'd been, he was also a bleeding man alone, and some things in her ran deeper than instruction.

No one came quickly. The valley was never quick. By the time the miller's boys arrived, hauling him onto the grass, there was nothing to be done but cover his face.

* * *

They asked her questions within the hour.

Not the miller, not the village reeve — riders. Four of them, in the king's charcoal-and-silver, mud to the girth straps, horses blown hard as if they'd ridden through the night. And at their head a woman in black with a chain of office at her throat and eyes like coins left in a well: cold, round, and fathoms deep.

Justiciar Corvane. Freya had seen her twice in her life, both times at a distance, both times during the solstice ceremonies. Up close she was smaller than rumor made her, and much worse.

"Lady Whitlock." Corvane dismounted herself, which surprised Freya; justiciars, she'd assumed, were carried through life. "You found the body."

"He died in my arms, my lady. I couldn't save him."

"Did you try?" Something flickered in those coin-gray eyes. "I mean no insult. I mean: did you touch him? Take anything from him? Speak with him?"

"He spoke. He was delirious. He said—" *You're the last heir of Vael,* said the voice in her memory, and Freya heard herself answer, smooth as pouring cream, "—he said a name I didn't catch. Some woman. He seemed to be looking for someone."

Corvane watched her for a long moment. Freya had spent her life being watched while she ate; she knew how to keep her face still under scrutiny. But this was different. This was a woman reading her, not her reactions, and Freya suddenly understood that the bundle was a live coal against her ribs, wrapped in wool and prayer, and that if Corvane ordered her searched there was nothing on this earth she could do about it.

"Delirious men often speak names," Corvane said at last. "What did he look like?"

"Tall. Gray hair. River-green eyes. Leathers, no livery."

Something moved behind the justiciar's face — recognition, quickly buried. "And where were you when you found him?"

"Along the race. I walk there on my free hours."

"Alone?"

"Always."

Corvane nodded slowly, and then did the thing that turned Freya's blood to well-water: she smiled. It was a courteous smile, warm at the edges, and it did not reach anywhere near her eyes.

"You've served the crown faithfully, Lady Whitlock. Your family has. Longer than most people know." She stepped closer, and her voice dropped to something almost kind. "Faithful service deserves protection. So I'll give you some advice, freely given. If the dying man gave you anything — a word, a token, a scrap of paper — bring it to me tonight, and no harm will come to you or yours. Refuse, and I'll be forced to conclude that a food-taster has involved herself in matters far above her station." She paused. "And tasters who rise above their station have historically been... retired."

The millrace groaned. The mist hung. Somewhere a cart rattled past on the far road, an ordinary sound in an ordinary morning, and Freya stood very straight and said, "If he gave me anything, my lady, it died with him."

"Of course it did." Corvane mounted. At the turn of the road she looked back once. "Tonight, Lady Whitlock. Before the moon sets. I do so hate to be kept waiting."

The riders went north, toward Emberhold, toward the king's table where Freya would be expected at noon to put her tongue to the royal soup.

She waited until the hoofbeats faded, then walked — slowly, at a strolling pace, a taster taking her exercise — until the bend of the millrace hid her from the road. Then she opened the bundle just wide enough to see what lay inside: a fold of leather, and on it lines drawn in ink that shimmered faintly, like light on deep water, marking mountains she had known all her life and naming them in a language she had never been taught and somehow, terribly, almost understood.

Under the mountains, in smaller letters, in that same half-familiar script, ran a single line:

*The last heir of Vael must choose before the solstice.*

Her hands would not stop shaking. Her small world — the kitchens, the trays, the sips, the safe gray valley — had just shown her its teeth, and the teeth belonged to something enormous.

The moon would set tonight. And Freya Whitlock, who had never in her life gone farther than a day's walk from the millrace, understood that by tomorrow she would be running.
