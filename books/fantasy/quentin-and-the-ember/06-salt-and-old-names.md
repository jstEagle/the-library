## 6. Salt and Old Names

The sea had taken the archive a hundred years ago and never quite finished the job.

We came down to it at low tide, when the drowned library stood half out of the water like a whale's ribcage bleached white by salt and forgetting. Quentin went first, testing each stone with his boot before he let me follow, and I watched him move through the shallows with that careful, economical grace he never wasted on anything — not walking, not fighting, not even kindness. The water was the color of pewter and smelled of iron and rot and something older underneath, something sweet, like flowers left too long in a closed room.

"The tide turns within the hour," he said. "Whatever you're going to find, find it fast."

"Whatever I'm going to find," I said, "you don't want me to find it."

He didn't answer, which was its own kind of answer.

The map had brought us here — or rather, what lived inside the map had. Three nights ago, camped in a gully above the salt marshes, I had held the leather wrapping over our small fire, and the heat had woken something in the ink: lines that moved, that breathed, that rearranged themselves into script I could suddenly read as easily as my own name. *Vael's true script*, Quentin had said, going pale in the firelight. *No one has read it in two hundred years.* And at the bottom of the map, where a cartographer would put his seal, there was instead a single line of instruction:

*Where the sea keeps the books, the heir may read what the crown has burned.*

So here we were, ankle-deep in a drowned country's memory, while the tide counted down behind us.

* * *

The doors had long since rotted away, but the arches remained, and between them the shelves — stone shelves, thank whatever god had built this place, cut from grey granite that the water could erode but not devour. The lower rows were lost entirely, furred with weed and crusted with barnacles, home now to crabs that clattered away from my torchlight like small nervous thoughts. But the upper shelves rose above the high-water line, and on them, sealed in wax and lead and glass, the books survived.

I pulled the first one down and felt my heart stutter.

It wasn't paper inside the casing. It was thin sheets of hammered tin, inscribed with the true script, each letter stamped deep enough to outlast empires. The wax seal bore a device I recognized without knowing how — a cup and a sprig of some flowering herb, crossed — and beneath it, a title:

*Ledger of the Tasters. First Year of the Bargain.*

"Bargain," I said aloud, and my voice sounded strange in the vaulted dark, flattened by salt air. "Quentin. What is a bargain?"

He had stopped at the entrance, standing in the doorway like a man at a graveside who isn't sure the dead want company. "Freya."

"You knew." It wasn't a question. I heard it in my own voice, flat and cold as the water around my ankles. "You've known since the millrace. Since you caught up to me on the road. You keep saying your oath binds you to protect the map's bearer, but you've been protecting me from something, haven't you? Not for me. From me."

The torch hissed. Somewhere deeper in the archive, water dripped with the patience of centuries.

"I swore an oath to the old order," he said finally. "Not the crown. The order that kept the records. I'm disgraced because I wouldn't burn them." He looked at the ledger in my hands. "Read it. You were always going to. Better here than in Corvane's hands."

I opened the ledger.

* * *

The first pages were administrative. Rations. Shipments. Names. I read them the way I read everything — carefully, cataloguing, the habit of a woman whose life depended on noticing what didn't belong — and at first it seemed mundane, the bookkeeping of some coastal province two hundred years dead. Grain sent east. Wool sent east. Then, in the third year of the Bargain, an entry in a different hand, heavier, pressed hard into the metal:

*Item: one (1) child of the blood, female, age six, conveyed to Ember Valley under escort. To be raised in the kitchens. Dosage to begin at weaning and continue per schedule. She is not to know what she is.*

Dosage. The word sat in my stomach like a swallowed stone.

I turned the leaves faster. Year four. Year nine. Year fifteen. A new child every generation, sometimes two, always a child of the blood, always *conveyed*, always *to be raised in the kitchens*. Always the same closing line, repeated across decades in different hands, different inks, the same terrible constancy:

*She is not to know what she is.*

And then, in the forty-first year, an entry that made my hands go still:

*Note on the preparation: the draught does not confer immunity. Nothing confers immunity. The valley itself devours all art, all poison-working included; no taster within it has ever needed protection. The draught's purpose is gentler and crueller: it slows the blood, dulls the memory, keeps the heir placid as a milk-fed calf. We tell her court she builds resistance dish by dish. We tell her she is valued. Valued is not the word. Contained is the word. The crown fears what sleeps in her veins, and so it feeds her poppy and calls it a career.*

The torch trembled in my grip. I steadied it against my shoulder the way I'd steady a ladle, the way I'd been taught, and hated my own hands for their obedience.

Every dish. Every broth I'd swallowed and catalogued, every numbness I'd noted in my lips and fingertips and reported back to the steward with professional pride — I had thought I was building armor. I had thought my body was a fortress under construction, brick by brick, sip by sip. Twenty-three years of it. The numbness after the laced broth hadn't been poison's signature. It had been the drug's. My whole life measured in sips, and the sips weren't training me.

They were keeping me quiet.

"Say it," I whispered. I wasn't sure if I was talking to Quentin or to the ledger or to the drowned dark. "Say it out loud. I need to hear it in a voice that isn't mine."

Quentin came forward then, water streaming off his boots, and took the torch from me because my hands had gone past trembling into something worse. He read the entry himself, his jaw working, and when he spoke his voice was rough as rope.

"You're not a taster, Freya. You're the heir. Every taster in the valley's history has been the heir of Vael — the last living blood of a country the crown erased. They didn't conquer Vael. Your ancestor gave it away. Signed it over in exchange for — " he gestured at the shelves, the sea, the silence — "something. The terms are in the later volumes, if they survived. But the price of the bargain was this: the bloodline lives, but never knows it lives. Drugged, docile, dressed in an apron, and thanked for its service."

"Immunity," I said. The word tasted like the broth. Like ash. "They told me I had a gift."

"They told you a story." He said it gently, which somehow made it unforgivable. "A gift is something given freely. What you have is a leash, and someone's been holding it since before you could walk."

* * *

I don't know how long I stood there. The tide turned; I felt it before I understood it, a cold insistence rising around my calves, the sea coming to reclaim its library shelf by shelf. Quentin was saying my name. I was reading.

Because there was more. There was a final volume, higher on the shelf, its lead casing unbroken, and stamped across it in the true script: *Terms of the Bargain, Complete and Final.* And beside the title, a warning in smaller letters that I read three times before it made sense:

*To be opened by the heir alone, at the door, before the solstice. All other hands: the terms will not suffer you.*

The door. *The door out of the vanished country.* The stranger's dying words came back to me with the taste of blood on them — *before the solstice, before the last of it dies* — and suddenly the map, the archive, the drowned books, all of it snapped into focus like a lens finding its subject. This wasn't a treasure hunt. It was a countdown. Two hundred years of children fed poppy in a kitchen, and somewhere a door waiting for one of us to wake up in time.

The water was at my knees now. Quentin had my arm.

"Freya. The tide."

I let him pull me toward the entrance, but at the threshold I stopped, turned back, and looked at the archive filling with grey water — the ledgers sinking back into the sea's keeping, the record of twenty-three generations of stolen children returning to the dark. Grief should have undone me. For one long moment it nearly did; I felt it rise in me like the tide, huge and salt and indifferent, wanting nothing more than to drag me under and be done with it.

But grief, I discovered, is only sorrow that hasn't found its work yet.

My mother had died in that kitchen. I'd assumed it was her heart, her lungs, the slow wearing-out of a woman who tasted death for a living. Now I knew: she had been dosed from the cradle too, and her mother before her, and hers, back and back and back until the first child of the blood was carried through the mountain pass and told she was being given an honor. We hadn't been servants. We hadn't even been prisoners, really — prisoners know they're imprisoned. We'd been livestock. Kept calm. Kept close. Kept *flavored to the crown's liking*.

The king hadn't feared poison in his food. He'd feared the truth in my blood.

"Quentin." My voice came out level. That surprised me. Underneath the levelness something was burning down to a point, fine and hard as a needle. "The justiciar's riders. When they asked about the map — Corvane didn't send them to retrieve a stolen document, did he. He sent them to retrieve *me*. Or my body. Because if the heir reaches the door before the solstice—"

"—the bargain breaks," Quentin said quietly. "Everything the crown built on Vael's grave comes due. Yes."

"Then we stop being careful."

He searched my face, and whatever he saw there made him nod slowly, once, the way men nod before a battle they intend to win rather than survive.

"There's a fishing village two miles north," he said. "Corvane's outriders will reach it by dawn. If we move now, we can be past them before first light."

"No." I climbed the last steps out of the sea and stood dripping on the shingle, the map dry against my chest despite everything, warm as a live thing. "I mean we stop being careful with *them*. I've spent my whole life swallowing what other people put in my cup." I looked south, toward the capital, toward the figurehead king and the justiciar who served something older than any crown. "It's their turn to drink."

Behind us, the sea closed over the archive without a ripple, keeping its books, keeping its patience.

Ahead of us, the road ran toward the capital — and somewhere along it, moving fast, riding hard, came Corvane, carrying an offer he believed one of us couldn't refuse.
