## 10. One Dish at a Time

The morning after the world changed, Freya woke in a bed that smelled of salt and cedar and did not know her name.

That was the strangest part. Not the light — though the light through the shutters was wrong, gold shot through with green where it had always been plain amber in Emberhold. Not the sounds — gulls instead of kitchen bells, the sea's long exhale instead of the millrace's chatter. It was that the room did not know her. Every room she had ever slept in had known her: the taster's cell with its ledger of poisons scratched into the doorframe, the narrow cot above the kitchens where the ceiling beams sweated grease in summer. This room in Vael's restored palace had been empty for two hundred years, waiting for an heir who never came, and now that she had come it only stared at her, beautiful and blank as a new bowl.

She lay still and took inventory, the way she took inventory of every dish set before her. Body: whole. Tongue: hers again — that was the miracle she kept testing, pressing it to the roof of her mouth like a coin bitten for gold. For twenty-six years something had sat between her tongue and the world, a veil of numbness laid down sip by sip, dose by dose, until she had forgotten there was ever anything underneath. Now the veil was gone. She could taste the air. She could taste the salt on her own lips. Yesterday morning she had licked a drop of broth from her thumb and wept over it like a girl at her first festival, because it tasted of *leek*, of leek entire, leek in every green dimension of itself, and no one in the history of the world had ever been so foolishly happy about a leek.

Beside her, on the floor, Quentin slept sitting up against the wall, sword across his knees even now, even here, even after everything. He had refused the bed. He had refused three beds. He slept the way he did everything else — as if someone might revoke the permission at any moment.

Freya rose quietly and went to the window.

Below, Vael was waking. The city that had been a rumor, a wound in every map, a country erased so thoroughly that its own descendants had forgotten they were descendants — the city stood in the morning light with its towers wearing moss like old velvet and its streets filling with people who had walked out of the valley two days ago and not yet stopped looking over their shoulders. They came slowly, in family clusters, carrying what they carried: hens in wicker baskets, grandmothers in wheelbarrows, a fiddle, a loom's worth of thread, the bones of a house cat wrapped in linen because you do not leave your dead behind twice.

They were choosing. That was the word Corvane had used, spitting it like gristle on the day of his binding — *choosing*, as if it were a disease. The valley's people were choosing their own country, without magic's shelter, without the bargain's anesthesia, and some of them were choosing badly, quarreling in the square about water rights and whose grandfather had owned which orchard when the erasure fell, and Freya found she loved them most in exactly those moments. A kingdom built on a lie had been quiet. This one argued about chickens. She could live in a kingdom that argued about chickens.

A knock. Then, because he never waited for permission he thought she'd already given, Quentin's voice through the door: "There's a delegation."

"Of course there is."

"They've brought food." A pause. "I checked it."

"Did you *taste* it?"

Another pause, longer. "I watched you sleep instead."

She smiled at the door where he couldn't see it. Some things were better rationed.

* * *

The throne room of Vael had been built to hold a court of ten thousand, and it held perhaps four hundred now, standing among pillars furred with two centuries of silence. Sunlight came down through a broken oculus in dusty spears. The throne itself — and this was the detail Freya had loved from the first moment she saw it, back when Vael was still half-ghost — was not a chair at all. It was a table. A long, low table of black oak, worn smooth at one end where generations of Vaelli rulers had evidently sat *among* their people rather than above them, eating with them, drinking what they drank, running whatever risks ran through the common pot.

Her ancestors had been tasters too. That was the truth the drowned archive had given her, bitter as medicine: the first kings and queens of Vael had eaten beside their people by law, sharing every plate, and when the last heir bargained the country away, the crown had kept the custom and hollowed it — turned the shared table into the poisoned chalice, turned the taster into a leash, bred a line of children in a hidden valley and fed them poison one dish at a time so that no true heir could ever rise unnoticed. Her immunity had been a cage built into her blood. Her whole life had been a sentence being served in sips.

Now the four hundred stood before the table-throne, and the oldest among them — Maren of the millrace, who had once been kind to a strange girl who lingered too long watching the water — stepped forward with a cushion, and on the cushion lay a circlet of black iron set with river pearls.

"Freya Whitlock," Maren said, in the old tongue that had come back to all of them the way a name comes back after fever, "last heir of Vael. The table is yours."

The hall held its breath. Somewhere near the back, a hen objected to the suspense.

Freya looked at the circlet. She looked at the table. And she found, examining herself with the same cold attention she gave any dish set before her, that she knew exactly what this was.

"It's poisoned," she said.

Maren blinked. "My lady?"

"Not with hemlock. With need." Freya stepped forward, and her voice carried in the ruined acoustics the way her mother's had carried in the kitchen, the way voices carry when they have spent years learning to be heard over steam and shouting. "You are four hundred people who have lost a country twice — once to a bargain, once to a forgetting. You are frightened, and grief makes us reach for crowns the way drowning people reach for anything. I know, because I reached for one myself, on the solstice night, when I spoke Vael's name and felt the whole erased weight of it come flooding home. I wanted to be the heir. It would have been so easy. It would have tasted like justice."

She lifted the circlet from the cushion. The iron was cold; the pearls were warm, which should have been impossible, and she chose not to wonder about it.

"But I am a taster," she said. "And I am telling you, as a taster tells a king: *do not eat this.* Not yet. Not until you know what's in it. A throne taken in fear will taste of fear for a hundred years. Ask me how I know. Ask the king across the mountains, who has been eating stolen magic for two centuries and calls the numbness peace."

Silence. Then a voice from the crowd — a young man with flour to his elbows, one of the bakers who had crossed with the second wave — called out, "Then who rules? Someone must."

"No one must," Freya said. "Not immediately. Not forever. That is the luxury you have been given, and it cost my life as I knew it, so I suggest you spend it well." She turned the circlet in her hands. "Here is what I offer instead."

* * *

They founded the Order in the autumn, in a low stone hall beside the millrace where Freya had first held a dying stranger's head above the water. It had no banners. It had no oath, or rather its oath was a single sentence, spoken aloud by each member on the day of their taking: *I will tell you what is in the dish.* That was all. Not *I will protect you* — protection curdles into custody; she had lived inside the crown's protection for twenty-six years and knew its flavor intimately. Not *I will serve you* — service answers to power, and power was precisely what the Order would never hold. Only: *I will tell you what is in the dish.* Whatever the dish was. Broth or treaty, marriage contract or coronation cup, the small print of a bargain or the large print of a war.

Tasters and truth-keepers. Quentin had suggested the second word, one evening on the hall's unfinished benches, turning his cup the way he turned everything, as if checking it for flaws. "Because that's what you actually are," he said. "You didn't just taste the poison in the crown. You tasted the lie under it. Anyone can learn to detect arsenic. Detecting a bargain takes—" he gestured vaguely with the cup, "—whatever it is you have."

"A lifetime of being lied to in small doses," Freya said. "Builds tolerance."

"That too."

The Order's first students were valley children, mostly — quick-tongued girls and watchful boys whom the old regime would have marked for the taster's cradle, and Freya taught them the way she wished someone had taught her: that a palate is a form of memory, that attention is a form of courage, that the question *who benefits?* is the sharpest instrument in any kitchen. She taught them to read ledgers the way they read recipes. She taught them that when a ruler offers you bread, you ask who grew the wheat, and when a ruler offers you safety, you ask who is paying, and you keep asking until the answer stops tasting sweet.

Quentin taught the other lessons. How to stand between a threat and its target without becoming the target's jailer. How to break an oath cleanly, before witnesses, when the oath itself goes rotten — he told that story often now, his own disgrace and its unmaking, and each time he told it the shame sat a little lighter on him, like a coat finally hung up by the door. He had been reinstated, formally, by a council that no longer answered to the figurehead king across the mountains. He had declined. He wore no colors at all now, only a plain sword and, at his collar, a small iron pin shaped like a spoon, which made him laugh every time he caught himself in a mirror, and which he refused, absolutely, to stop wearing.

Corvane they had bound, not killed — the terms of the bargain required a living keeper of its memory, and Freya had ruled that the punishment for engineering two centuries of quiet was to be the only man awake in it. He tended the archive now, cataloguing the true histories, alone with every fact he had spent his life burying. Quentin visited him sometimes. Freya did not ask what they said to each other. Some dishes needed to finish cooking in private.

* * *

On the first anniversary of the restoration, the valley sent its answer.

It came as a procession down the old road — the road that had appeared overnight when Vael flooded back into the world, a road that existed on no map because maps had not yet caught up to the truth of things. Two thousand people walked it, and then more, a slow river of the valley folk coming to see the country their great-grandmothers' stories had whispered about. They came wary. They came arguing. They came carrying soil in jars, because you do not entirely trust a resurrection until you have tested it against something that grows.

At the head of them walked an old woman Freya recognized — Hesta, who had run the Emberhold kitchens, who had salted Freya's broths for twenty years without knowing what the salting served. Hesta stopped at the door of the Order's hall, looked up at the carved lintel — *I WILL TELL YOU WHAT IS IN THE DISH* — and laughed until she had to wipe her eyes.

"All those years," she said. "All those years I wondered why you'd take such work, a clever girl like you. Swallowing filth for a wage." She shook her head. "We didn't know what we were feeding you. Any of it. You believe that?"

"I know," Freya said. "That's rather the point of the Order. No one knows what they're feeding anyone. So we taste it, and we say so, and we let people choose."

Hesta looked past her, into the hall, at the long tables where students bent over cups and ledgers, at Quentin in the corner pretending not to hover, at the black oak table-throne that now stood in the middle of the room like any other table, laden with bread and honey and the first honest leeks of the season.

"And the throne?" Hesta asked. "Truly? You'll not sit it?"

"The table does more good than the chair ever did." Freya stepped aside, holding the door. "Come in. Eat first. Decide after. That's the whole philosophy — it fits on a spoon."

That evening the hall was fuller than it had ever been, loud with valley accents and Vaelli ones, with arguments about orchards and water rights and whether the fiddle player knew any songs older than the erasure. Freya moved along the tables with a ladle, tasting as she went, and everything tasted of everything — of salt and cedar and leek entire, of a future with no veil between her tongue and it.

Quentin found her at the end of the night, on the step outside, where the millrace talked to itself in the dark.

"Well?" he said. "How is it?"

She considered the question the way she considered everything now: carefully, completely, with nothing numbed and nothing hidden.

"Bitter," she said. "Sweet. Underdone in places. Needs another year at least." She handed him the ladle, handle-first, the way trust was offered between the two of them, always hilt-first, always. "Taste for yourself."

He did. And somewhere beyond the hills, in a capital that no longer quite believed in its own king, the first rumors were already moving — whispers of a valley that had chosen, of an order that answered to no crown, of a girl with a taster's tongue who could tell a lie from a broth at forty paces. The world was going to come asking. Let it come.

Freya watched the stars come out over her rebuilt, unmagical, stubbornly arguing country, and began, quietly, to plan the menu.
