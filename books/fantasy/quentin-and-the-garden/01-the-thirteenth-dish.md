## 1. The Thirteenth Dish

There were forty-one dishes at the Autumn Concord banquet, and Xanthe Kavanagh tasted every one of them.

She did this standing slightly behind the queen's right shoulder, in the narrow wedge of shadow between the throne's armrest and the great candle-stand, where a person could exist for years without being perceived by anyone who mattered. Nine years of practice had made the wedge hers the way a snail owns its shell. She knew its temperature by season, its acoustics by hour, and precisely how much of her body she could let exist before some visiting duke squinted at her and asked, *And who is that?*

The answer, always, was: nobody. The taster. Don't look at her.

It was, Xanthe reflected, arranging her face into the mild blankness of furniture, the finest armor ever devised. Not steel, not spells — just the profound human unwillingness to notice the woman holding the little silver spoon.

Dish thirty-one was the trouble.

It arrived on a platter of black river-stone, carried by a servant Xanthe didn't recognize, which was itself unusual. She knew the palace servants the way she knew the wedge: by habit and survival. This one was new, or very good at being invisible too, and he set the dish down with a small ceremonial bow that no kitchen hand in Vellamere had performed since the old dynasty.

"River eel," announced the chamberlain, "in sauce of mountain-honey and blackthorn, from the ambassador's own stores."

Ambassador Vess of the Thornfell delegation smiled with all his teeth. He had been smiling like that all evening, in the way of a man watching a slow clock.

Xanthe stepped forward, spoon in hand.

She tasted first with her nose — nine years will teach you that your nose dies for you more often than your tongue does. Honey, yes. Blackthorn, bitter and correct. Underneath it, something faintly mineral, like rain on slate, which might have been the mountain in the honey or might have been nothing.

Then the spoon.

For half a heartbeat, it was only eel, rich and sweet, the kind of thing she'd never afford in ten lifetimes.

Then her tongue went cold. Not numb — *cold*, the specific cold of well-water in deep winter, spreading backward toward her throat, and beneath it a second flavor woke up, shy as a spider under a stone. Bitterroot. Gravemoss. And something else, something she had no name for, that tasted the way a locked door sounds.

*Hemlock-and-worse*, said nine years of instinct, very calmly. *Enough to stop a heart in four minutes. Enough to stop yours in two.*

Xanthe did not die.

This was, she would think later, the strangest part — not the poison, which she knew like an old penance, but the interruption. Because something happened between the spoon and her blood. Something reached into her mouth — there was no other word for it — and took the poison out, the way you'd lift a thorn from a sleeping child's foot. Gently. Carefully. With what she could only describe, absurdly, as *manners*.

The cold receded. The eel was suddenly just eel. And somewhere behind her sternum, a warmth bloomed and faded like a coal breathed on once and left alone.

Xanthe swallowed.

Every eye at the high table was on her, because that was the ritual: taste, pause, count. Three breaths meant safe. She gave them three breaths, then four for theater, and inclined her head.

"The dish is sound," she said.

Her voice came out steady. She was rather proud of that.

Ambassador Vess's smile did not waver, but something behind it shifted, the way water shifts when a fish turns underneath. He looked at her — actually looked, which almost never happened — for one long moment, and then raised his glass to her across the table as if toasting her health specifically.

Xanthe returned to her wedge and stood very still, doing arithmetic.

Either the Thornfell ambassador had tried to poison the Queen of Vellamere at a peace banquet, in front of two hundred witnesses, with a compound that failed — or he hadn't tried at all, and the poison had been meant for someone else entirely, and everyone at that table knew something she didn't.

Both possibilities were bad. One of them was impossible.

She took inventory of herself, discreetly, the way she'd done ten thousand times. Pulse: steady. Hands: steady. Tongue: her own. Whatever had taken the hemlock had left no trace, except —

Except that when she exhaled, her breath fogged. In a warm hall full of candles and two hundred bodies, her breath fogged, once, like midwinter.

Nobody noticed. Nobody ever noticed. That was the whole architecture of her life.

So why, Xanthe wondered, did she feel *seen*?

* * *

The banquet wound down the way banquets do, in a slow landslide of toasts and flattery, and the queen retired at midnight with the Thornfell delegation bowing their way out below. Xanthe followed Her Majesty to the private apartments, tasted the chamomile wine — plain, honest, merely capable of making her sleepy — and was dismissed with the queen's usual distracted kindness.

"You did well tonight, Xanthe."

"Your Majesty is generous."

"I mean it." The queen paused at her door, firelight catching the grey coming in at her temples. She was fifty-one and tired in the bones of it, and she was, Xanthe thought not for the first time, the only person in the palace who bothered to say thank you. "You've kept me alive nine years. Sometimes I forget to say so."

"Then I'll keep reminding you by continuing," Xanthe said, and the queen laughed — a real laugh, brief and unroyal — and shut her door.

Which left Xanthe with three hours until dawn and a poison in her memory that refused to sit still.

She should have gone to her room. She should have slept, risen, reported the anomaly to the Master of Household Security in the flat, deniable language tasters used — *a note of bitterness in the thirty-first dish; recommend inquiry into the ambassador's stores* — and let the machinery of the court grind it into nothing, as it ground everything.

Instead she found herself walking out into the gardens.

It was nearly the feast of First Frost. The gardeners had spent a week wrapping the rose beds in sacking and mulching the borders against the cold, and the whole grounds had that stripped, held-breath look of a place bracing for winter. The sky was clear and hard, the moon a day off full, and Xanthe's boots crunched frost on the gravel as she walked without quite deciding where she was going.

Her feet decided for her.

They took her along the yew walk, past the sundial nobody corrected, through the gap in the wall where the formal gardens gave way to the old ones — the wild tangle at the palace's southern edge that no gardener tended because no charter covered it. And there she stopped, because the wild garden was blooming.

In late autumn. Under frost.

Beans, of all things, climbing a dead trellis in flower and fruit at once. Marigolds open wide as coins. A row of cabbages so fat and smug they looked painted. And over it all hung a smell — warm earth, green growth, the smell of a kitchen garden in high summer — rolling out across the frozen ground like a rumor.

At the center of it, half-swallowed by the abundance, stood a ruined shrine.

Xanthe knew it distantly, the way you know a piece of furniture you've walked past a thousand times. Cracked stone urn on a broken plinth, moss-eaten, its carved face worn to suggestion. Old faith, pre-dynastic, the sort of thing the crown tolerated because demolishing it wasn't worth the effort. She'd passed it for nine years and never once seen anything growing near it. The ground around shrines was traditionally barren; even weeds respected a dead god's privacy.

Now the shrine sat in the middle of a miracle, and the cracked urn — she could see this clearly, even by moonlight, even from twenty paces — was *warm*. Frost lay on everything, silvering every leaf, except in a rough circle around the urn, where the stones were dark and damp and steaming faintly, like a beast's back in cold weather.

Xanthe stood at the edge of the bean rows and felt the same sensation she'd felt at the banquet table: something reaching toward her, gently, carefully, with manners.

"Well," she said aloud, to no one, because speaking made things ordinary. "That's new."

The marigolds nearest her turned. There was no wind. They turned anyway, all together, faces lifting toward her like a crowd of small orange children noticing an adult had entered the room.

Xanthe Kavanagh had survived nine years of professional poisoning by trusting exactly three things: her nose, her discipline, and her invisibility. Her nose told her the air smelled of summer. Her discipline told her to walk away and report nothing, because wonders were more dangerous than hemlock — hemlock only killed you.

And her invisibility, her beautiful armor, her shell —

Her invisibility was failing. She could feel it going, the way you feel a fever starting. Something in the garden had looked at her tonight. Something at the banquet table had reached into her mouth and saved her life without being asked, and things did not do favors for nobody. Things did not turn their flowers toward furniture.

Whatever had intercepted that poison knew her name.

Worse — and this was the thought that finally made her take a step back, crunching frost, breath fogging again in the summer-smelling air — worse, she suspected it had known her name for a long time. Nine years, maybe. As long as she'd been swallowing death in small doses and calling it a living. As long as she'd been walking past this shrine with her eyes politely elsewhere, the way the whole court walked past everything that mattered.

Behind her, up at the palace, a bell rang the third hour.

In front of her, the cracked urn steamed gently in the frost, and Xanthe would have sworn — later, when she was the sort of person who swore such things — that she heard, from inside the stone, very faintly, a voice clearing its throat.

Like someone who had been waiting a very long time to be introduced.
