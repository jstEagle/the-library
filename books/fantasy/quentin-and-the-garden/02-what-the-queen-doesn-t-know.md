## 2. What the Queen Doesn't Know

The queen's private study smelled of beeswax and dried lavender, and Xanthe stood in the middle of it feeling like a fly that had wandered into a cathedral.

This was not where tasters were summoned. Tasters were summoned to kitchens, to banquet halls, to the anteroom behind the dais where a nervous under-steward would hold out a spoon and say, *her majesty's portion, if you please.* Tasters were not summoned to rooms with carpets thick enough to swallow footsteps and windows that looked out on the queen's own rose garden—the real one, the one with walls, not the ornamental wilderness where courtiers went to be seen pretending to be informal.

"Sit, Kavanagh." The queen did not look up from her letters. "You're hovering. It's unsettling. You hover like a man who owes money."

Xanthe sat. The chair was softer than her entire bed, mattress included.

Queen Isolde was fifty-one years old and had the kind of face that made portrait painters despair—not because it was plain, but because it refused to sit still for flattery. She had ruled for nineteen years through two wars, one famine, and four separate assassination attempts that Xanthe knew of personally, having tasted for three of them. She signed her letters with a quick, impatient slash, as though even her own name bored her.

"You were at the Marlowe dinner last week," the queen said.

"I was, your majesty."

"And you tasted the quince tart."

"I did."

The queen set down her pen at last and looked at her. "And you didn't die."

"It was," Xanthe said carefully, "a very good tart."

Something flickered across the queen's face—amusement, quickly banked. "Lord Aldery's cook has been dismissed. He insists the tart was poisoned. He insists it must have been, because Lord Aldery ate a slice at home from the same batch and spent two days in a trance so deep his physician thought him dead."

Xanthe kept her face still. This was the first discipline of her trade: never let the food see you think.

"A trance," she said.

"He woke on the third day with no memory of it and an appetite like a harvest festival. His wife says he wept at breakfast without knowing why." The queen folded her hands. "He is the fourth. Four courtiers in two months, all of them found insensible, all of them waking hungry and hollow-eyed and *strange*. And here is the part that interests me, Kavanagh: none of them died. Not one. Whatever this is, it isn't killing anyone. It's *taking* something."

The fire popped. Somewhere below, the palace went about its business, a hundred small sounds rising through the floor like roots through soil.

"Why tell me?" Xanthe asked. "Your spymaster has men for this. Men with knives and disguises and very small notebooks."

"My spymaster has men who look for *people*," the queen said. "Traitors, smugglers, ambitious second sons. This isn't people. I've had the physicians in, the priests, that dreadful hedge-witch from Copperlane whom I pay entirely too much. They find nothing, because there's nothing to find in a body. Whatever's happening, it happens somewhere else." Her eyes, gray as winter sea, settled on Xanthe with uncomfortable weight. "But you taste things no one else can taste. You've told me before—bitterness under honey, ash in wine. Things my cooks swear aren't there."

"They weren't wrong to swear it."

"No. They were just wrong about whose tongue was broken." The queen leaned forward. "I want you to find out what's happening to my courtiers. Quietly. No reports, no channels—come to me directly. If anyone asks, you're running errands for the stillroom."

"And if I find nothing?"

The queen smiled then, and it transformed her face into something almost young. "Then you'll have earned a very good bottle of wine and my undying gratitude, which is worth slightly less but lasts longer."

It should have been simple to say no. Xanthe had built nine years out of saying nothing, tasting everything, being furniture with a functioning tongue. Invisibility was armor; she'd stitched every seam herself.

But the queen had remembered her name. Had noticed she could taste what others couldn't. Had looked at her—actually *looked*, the way you look at a person and not a utensil—and asked for help.

Nobody had asked Xanthe for help since her mother died.

"I'll need access to the affected households," she said. "Their kitchens, their cellars, their rubbish, if it comes to that."

"You'll have letters of passage. And Kavanagh—" the queen's voice dropped, and for a moment the humor drained out of it entirely, leaving something old and tired underneath, "—whatever you find, however strange it seems, bring it to me before you bring it to anyone else. There are people in this court I trust less than I trust a food-taster, and I want you to understand exactly how damning that is."

"Yes, your majesty."

"Go, then. And take a pear from the bowl on your way out. My gardener's pride, apparently. Tell me if it's any good—I've stopped being able to tell."

Xanthe took the pear. It was perfect, cold-sweet, faintly of honey. She said so, and the queen waved her off with the air of a woman who had long ago stopped believing reports about pears.

She was three corridors away before she realized her hands were shaking—not from fear, or not only from fear. From warmth. From the dangerous, ridiculous warmth of being *useful to someone who saw her.*

Her mother would have said that was how they got you.

* * *

The first three households gave her nothing—or rather, they gave her plenty, and none of it was strange. Lord Aldery's kitchen was spotless and smug about it. Lady Vess's cellar held forty years of wine and a cook who cried when questioned, which turned out to be unrelated. The third courtier, Master Corvin, kept a pantry locked like a treasury, but inside it was only cheese going elegantly over the hill and a quantity of candied violets that Xanthe sampled on principle. All sweet. All safe. All ordinary.

It was the fourth household that broke the pattern, and it broke it in the most boring way imaginable: a delivery ledger.

Lady Merrisole's steward kept meticulous accounts, and while Xanthe pretended to inventory the stillroom, she read six months of entries. Grain from the lowland farms, salt from the eastern pans, oil, wine, wax—all ordinary, all priced fairly, all dull as ditchwater. Then, scattered through the columns like burrs in wool:

*Honeyed figs, preserved. Vendor unnamed. 4 silver.*
*Candied ginger, superior quality. Vendor unnamed. 6 silver.*
*Wine, dark, mountain vintage. Vendor unnamed. 9 silver.*

Every entry marked *unnamed vendor*. Every entry dated within a fortnight of a courtier's trance. And every entry, when she pressed the steward with her letter of passage and her best bored-bureaucrat voice, traced back the same way: bought off a cart, cart came down from the north, cart came from Thornfell.

Thornfell. The word landed in her chest like a stone dropped down a well.

She knew those roads. Not from maps—from childhood. Her mother had grown up in the fold-hamlets on Thornfell's southern skirts, and twice a year they'd walked the smuggler paths together, high sheep-tracks and goat-ways that threaded between the mountain's knees, where the king's taxmen never went and the locals traded wool for salt and gossip for gossip. Her mother had taught her the paths the way other mothers taught prayers: *if you're ever lost, girl, walk downhill until you hear water, then follow it south. The mountain keeps its own doors, but water always remembers the way out.*

Xanthe stood in Lady Merrisole's stillroom holding a jar of honeyed figs, and her mouth went dry.

Because she'd tasted these. Or ones like them. At the Marlowe dinner, under the quince tart's sweetness, there had been a note she'd logged and dismissed—a deep, dark undertaste, like wine gone to velvet, like something grown in shadow and fed on more than sun. She'd assumed it was the mountain vintage in the sauce. Everyone used Thornfell wine; it was cheap and excellent and nobody asked questions.

But the trances hadn't come from the banquet. They'd come from *these*—small deliveries, private purchases, sweet things eaten alone in studies and solariums. Someone was feeding the court delicacies, one courtier at a time, and whatever slept inside the sweetness was drinking something out of them while they dreamed.

She opened the jar. The smell rose up rich and golden, and beneath it—that note again. Dark. Velvet. Patient.

Not poison. That was the thing her tongue understood before her mind caught up. It wasn't poison at all. Poison wanted to hurt. This wanted to *keep*.

Xanthe closed the jar, set it down, and pressed her palm flat against the cool shelf until her hands steadied.

Nine years she had survived by being nobody. Now the queen had given her a name to use and a task that mattered, and both of those gifts pointed straight up a smuggler's road into the black shoulders of Thornfell, toward whatever had woken in the dark there and developed a taste for courtiers.

Her mother's roads. Her mother's mountain. And her mother, dead eleven years now, who had never once in her life let Xanthe eat anything sweet that hadn't passed her own hands first—who had gone pale as whey, once, when a peddler offered the child a honey cake, and paid him double to forget he'd ever climbed this far south.

*Xanthe, love,* her mother had said, gripping her wrist hard enough to bruise, *if anyone from the mountain ever offers you sweets—you run.*

She had thought it was famine-lore. Old women's rules, like never whistle indoors and always leave the last spoonful for the house.

Now she wasn't sure.

Xanthe took the delivery ledger's dates, copied them into memory the way she memorized everything—twice, slowly, tied to images—and left Lady Merrisole's household by the servant's gate as the afternoon light went long and coppery. Behind her, the city settled into evening. Ahead of her, past the northern wall, the Thornfell mountains stood against the sunset, blue-black and patient, wearing their snow like lace collars.

Somewhere up there, on roads she had loved as a child, someone was selling sweetness and collecting something far dearer than silver.

She would have to go and see. And the worst part—the part that kept her awake that night, staring at her narrow ceiling—was that some small, starved corner of her had already started walking.
