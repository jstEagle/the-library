## 3. Quentin of the Cracked Urn

The trail led Xanthe somewhere she had been a hundred times without ever looking.

She had learned, in nine years of tasting other people's food, that the eye is a lazy servant. It shows you what you expect: a wall, a hedge, a rusted gate chained with ivy. It was only because she was now hunting rather than hiding—only because she had spent three nights tracing the trance-touched courtiers back to their deliveries from the Thornfell roads—that she noticed the garden gate at all.

It stood at the end of the kitchen yard, behind the scullery where the palace's least fortunate onions went to die. The gate was iron gone soft with rust, and beyond it lay a garden that should not have existed.

Thornfell roses, blooming in the last grey week of winter. Rosemary grown into a hedge tall enough to hide a standing man. A fig tree—*a fig tree*, in the northern cold—leaning against the old shrine wall as though it had been told to keep quiet and had taken the instruction seriously.

Xanthe stood in the doorway of her own invisibility and felt it crack.

She knew this garden. Every taster knew it, the way every cat knows which rooms are never swept. It was the forgotten corner, the dead plot, the place where the head gardener sent tools to rust and apprentices to reflect on their failures. She had passed its gate perhaps a thousand times. And she had tended it.

That was the part she had never told anyone. Nine years ago, half-dead from her first week of poisons, she had crawled through this gate to be sick in private and found the little shrine—a cracked stone urn on a plinth, a god's name worn to nothing—and something in her, some animal instinct for kinship, had made her come back. With water. With seeds stolen from the queen's own stores. With the small stubborn care of one overlooked creature for another.

She had thought she was gardening. She had not known she was *feeding* someone.

The roses said otherwise. The roses said she had been feeding him for nine years, and he had woken hungry, and the first thing he had done was intercept a poison meant for her throat.

Xanthe let herself through the gate. The hinges did not creak; they sighed, like an old man settling into a chair he'd been told was his.

"Hello?" she said, and then, feeling foolish, "I brought bread."

She had, in fact, brought bread. It was a habit so deep it had become involuntary; she carried bread the way other women carried pins. She set the loaf on the plinth beside the cracked urn and waited.

Nothing happened. The garden breathed around her, green and impossible. Snow sat unmelted on the far wall while frost-ferns unfurled along the path between her feet, and the two weathers coexisted with the uneasy politeness of relatives at a funeral.

Then the urn moved.

Not rolled. Not toppled. It *sat up*, the way a cat sits up when it has decided you are interesting, and out of the crack in its side poured a thin ribbon of light that gathered itself, grumbling, into the shape of a man.

He was not impressive. That was her first thought, and she was ashamed of it, and then she wasn't, because he was clearly thinking the same thing about himself. He was made mostly of shadow and soil, with light showing through him in patches like sun through a hedge. His hands were enormous—gardener's hands, hands built for hauling and planting—and his face kept sliding, as if he couldn't quite remember how faces worked and was trying several on for size.

"You," he said. His voice sounded like water finding a dry channel. "You're the bread one."

"I'm the—" Xanthe stopped. Recalibrated. "Yes. I'm the bread one."

"Nine years." He counted on fingers that had too many joints. "Nine years of bread and water and marigold seeds and once—" his face lit with an ancient grievance, "—once, a *turnip*. You left me a turnip and no explanation."

"It was a good turnip."

"It was an *insult*. Do you know what I am? I am Quentin. God of kitchen gardens. Keeper of the bean-row and the bee-skep. In the old days, the cooks of this palace poured me honey-wine every solstice and sang my name over the first cutting of the year." He gestured at himself, at the light leaking through his ribs. "*This* is what nine years of turnips gets you."

"A turnip is a perfectly respectable offering," Xanthe said, and then caught herself, because she was arguing theology with a shrubbery, and because underneath the argument something in her chest had gone tight and strange. Two forgotten creatures, recognizing each other across a garden wall. She knew exactly what nine years of being nobody's priority did to a person. Apparently it did the same thing to a god.

Quentin squinted at her. His eyes were the brown of turned earth, and they saw more than she liked.

"You're poisoned," he said.

"Not currently."

"*Permanently,*" he corrected. "You're a walking larder of death. Arsenic in your hair. Belladonna in your fingernails. There's a hemlock signature in your blood so old it's practically a fossil." He said it with professional admiration, the way a mason might praise another man's wall. "What *are* you?"

"The queen's taster."

Something changed in his face. The sliding stopped. For a moment he looked very old and very still, the way mountains look when they stop pretending to be hills.

"That explains it," he said softly. "That explains why the court-below's work tastes of you."

The word landed in her stomach like a dropped stone. "What court?"

"The one under Thornfell." He began to pace, and his pacing was odd—he moved like weather moving, appearing at one end of the path and simply *being* at the other. "The old gods. My elders. My betters, they'd tell you, if any of them could still string two thoughts together. They starved when the prayers stopped. Did you know that? Nobody prays anymore. Nobody pours anything out. So they got clever, and cleverness in a starving god looks a great deal like cruelty."

"The trances," Xanthe said. "The courtiers."

"Harvested," Quentin said. "Dreams skimmed off the top, souls weighed and portioned. And the delivery system—" he smiled, and it was not a nice smile, it was the smile of a thing that had watched a great deal from a cracked urn, "—poison. Poison is prayer, did you know that? A poisoner kneels over the cup and thinks of nothing else in the world. Perfect concentration. Perfect devotion. The court-below taught the trade to certain smugglers on the south road, and the smugglers sell to ambitious courtiers, and every cup poured is a little candle lit at their altar."

Xanthe thought of nine years of cups. Nine years of kneeling over wine and thinking of nothing else in the world, because thinking of anything else got tasters killed.

"Oh," she said.

"Yes," said Quentin.

"And mine? The dish at the banquet. The one that should have killed me."

"That was theirs. Court-work, unmistakable—I've tasted its signature on the wind since midwinter. Meant for you, or meant to pass through you to someone worthier of harvest." He came closer, and up close he smelled of rain on warm stone, of cut stems, of the inside of a greenhouse in summer. "But it touched *you* first. And you are so saturated with death that the poison took one look at you and refused. Like pouring new wine into a barrel that already holds the whole sea." He tilted his head. "It didn't kill you because you are, in the most literal sense available to us, already full."

"How comforting."

"I thought so." He settled onto the plinth beside his own urn, folding those impossible hands. "So. Bread-one. Taster. Barrel-of-the-sea. Why does the queen's little death-drinker come walking into my garden asking after gods?"

And there it was—the question she had walked three miles of frozen road to avoid answering, blooming here among the impossible roses.

Because the truth was that she had not come for the queen. She had followed the trail of trances dutifully, yes, she had mapped the smuggler deliveries and marked the names—but when the trail ended at this gate, she had not turned back to report. She had walked in. Because some starving, buried part of her had recognized the shape of the hunger on the other side of the wall.

"I tended you," she said instead. "For nine years. And you never said a word."

"What was I supposed to say? 'Thank you for the turnip'?" But his voice had lost its edge. He looked down at his huge hands. "Gods can't speak first, bread-one. Not to mortals. Old law. Older than the court-below, older than the mountain. We wait to be named, to be addressed, to be *wanted*. Nine years you came through that gate, and nine years I sat in my urn and—" he made a small gesture that contained, badly hidden, an ocean, "—waited."

The tightness in Xanthe's chest cracked open. She thought of her mother, who had fed her through famine by selling pieces of herself she never named. She thought of the queen, who trusted her with every breath. She thought of herself, kneeling in the dark over other people's cups, useful and unseen, waiting for someone to say her name.

"Well," she said, when she could trust her voice. "Consider yourself addressed. Hello, Quentin. I'm Xanthe Kavanagh, and I think the court under Thornfell has my soul in a ledger, and I would very much like to steal it back."

Quentin looked at her for a long moment. Somewhere above them, the fig tree dropped a ripe fruit into the snow, deliberately, like a man setting down his cup before serious talk.

"Now *that*," he said, rising, light spilling through his cracks like dawn through shutters, "is the best offering anyone has brought me in four hundred years."

* * *

They talked until the false weathers traded places—snow winning the wall, frost taking the path. Quentin told her about the court-below in fragments, the way a man describes a house he fled as a child: the ledger-hall where souls hung like hams in a smokehouse; the thirteenth moon, when debts came due; the elder gods too starved to be reasoned with and too proud to die.

"They'll collect you," he said. "A debt written in a mother's hand is the oldest ink there is. No court breaks that kind of contract, and no mortal walks out of the ledger-hall carrying her own name."

"No mortal," Xanthe agreed. "But I've spent nine years learning to survive things that should kill me. It's the one skill I have."

Quentin was quiet then, and his quiet had weight. When he spoke again, he did not look at her.

"There may be a way," he said slowly. "There's a piece of me in that court. Stolen, long ago—my divinity, the true heart of me, sold north by a priest who owed gambling debts. If it's still in their keeping, and if you could reach it..." He turned his earth-brown eyes on her, and in them she saw the terror of a creature who had just remembered wanting things. "It might buy your name back. Or it might burn us both to ash. I genuinely don't know which."

Xanthe looked at the god of kitchen gardens, cracked and leaking light, mad as a March hare and twice as stubborn, and felt the strange fierce tenderness of recognizing her own reflection in something utterly unlike her.

"When do we leave?" she said.
