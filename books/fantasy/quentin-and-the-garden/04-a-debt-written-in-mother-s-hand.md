## 4. A Debt Written in Mother's Hand

The chest had sat under the eaves of her rented room for nine years, and in all that time Xanthe had treated it the way one treats a sleeping dog with an uncertain temper: respectfully, and from a distance.

It was her mother's. It had crossed half the kingdom in a cart, then a boat, then another cart, arriving at her door the winter after the famine ended, smelling of cedar and long roads. Xanthe had opened it exactly once, found a shawl that smelled of rosemary and woodsmoke, and closed it again as though the smell might escape and follow her around the room, asking questions.

Tonight, with the thirteenth moon a bruise on the horizon and a god's cracked urn still warm in her memory, she opened it a second time.

The shawl was still there. She folded it over her knees without deciding to. Beneath it lay the ordinary archaeology of a poor woman's life: a darning egg worn smooth as a river stone; a bundle of letters tied with string, most of them from a cousin in Marrowdale who wrote about weather the way other people wrote about scandal; a wooden spoon with a bite taken out of the handle—Mam's, from the pot she'd never once let anyone else stir; a tin of buttons; a child's shoe, one only, small enough that Xanthe had to look at it for a moment before she understood it had been hers.

And at the very bottom, wrapped in oilcloth, a ledger book.

Not a ledger, she corrected herself, lifting it out. A recipe book. Mam's recipe book—the one she'd kept chained to her hip in the kitchen at Thornfell, the one Xanthe had assumed burned with the cottage. The cover was soft with grease and years. *Nettle soup, when the garden fails. Barley bread, third rising. Honey for the cough, but only the dark honey.*

Xanthe turned the pages slowly. It was like hearing her mother's voice, and she had to stop twice, once at a marginal note—*X. likes it with more honey, greedy thing*—and once at a pressed sprig of something she couldn't name, some flower that had no business growing in a famine year.

Then the handwriting changed.

It was still her mother's hand—the same forward-leaning letters, the same impatient crossbars—but the ink was different, darker, and the words were not words a cook would use. They were words a cook would use only if she were very frightened and very determined and had run out of every other kind of word.

*Received of the undersigned, in consideration of the life of the child Xanthe Kavanagh, who shall eat and thrive through the winter of the black barley, one soul, to be delivered at the turn of the thirteenth moon.*

Below it, a signature. Her mother's. And below that, in a hand so elegant it looked embarrassed to be on the same page, a second signature she didn't recognize, followed by a title: *Steward of Accounts, Court Beneath the Mountain.*

Xanthe read it four times. The words did not rearrange themselves into anything kinder.

*One soul.*

*The child Xanthe Kavanagh.*

*The thirteenth moon.*

She set the book on her knees very carefully, the way she set down dishes she'd already tasted and was waiting to see if they were kind.

"Well," she said to the empty room, because her mother had raised her to say something when the world went strange, even if the something was only *well*. "Well, Mam."

* * *

She had expected, all these years, to feel betrayed. She had rehearsed the feeling in the small hours, the way one rehearses arguments that will never happen. *You let me believe you were just clever with the barley. You let me think we were lucky.* She had a whole speech prepared, polished with use, addressed to a woman nine years dead.

But sitting there with the shawl around her shoulders and the recipe book open on her knees, Xanthe found the speech had rotted out of her. What remained was simpler and much worse.

The winter of the black barley. She remembered it in fragments: the sound the wind made in the empty fields, which was no sound at all; the neighbors' doors staying shut; the way her mother had begun to measure flour with her eyes instead of a cup, which was what starving women did to make the lie of plenty last longer. She remembered being hungry the way children are hungry—loudly, and without understanding—and she remembered, above all, that she had *eaten*. Through the worst of it, when the village went quiet and the priest stopped ringing the bell because ringing it took strength, Xanthe Kavanagh had eaten every day.

She'd thought her mother was a miracle worker. She'd thought it right up until the woman died, and for nine years after, and it had been one of the load-bearing beliefs of her life: *Mam could always find food. Mam could always find a way.*

Mam had found a way. The way had a price, and the price had been signed in the same hand that wrote *X. likes it with more honey, greedy thing.*

That was the part that undid her. Not the selling—gods knew, and Quentin knew, and the court beneath the mountain knew, that desperate people sold things they didn't own. It was that the two hands were the same hand. The woman who'd traded her daughter's soul had also pressed a flower into a recipe book and noted her daughter's greed for honey with something that was unmistakably, unbearably, love.

Xanthe cried the way she did everything: quietly, usefully, with an eye on the door. She cried for the child who'd been fed on credit. She cried for the mother who'd paid. She cried, a little, for herself at nine years old, opening this same chest someday and finding the same book, and closing it, because she hadn't been ready, and some part of her had known.

Then she blew her nose on the shawl, apologized to it, and got to work.

* * *

Because here was the thing about Xanthe Kavanagh, the thing the court had never understood and the queen had never needed to: grief was an ingredient. You could work with grief. You could render it down like fat and use it to grease the machinery of the day.

She read the contract again, and this time she read it like a taster.

*In consideration of the life of the child.* Not the woman. The contract named a child, and a winter, and a moon. It did not say what happened after. It did not say what *collection* meant, whether the soul was taken whole or harvested in installments—and a cold thought slid into place beside that question, smooth as a knife into a joint: nine years of poisonings. Nine years of the court's dishes passing her lips. If the court below fed on what the ledger held, and her name was in the ledger, then every dish she'd tasted had been a payment on account. They hadn't been trying to kill her. They'd been *feeding* her to something.

She thought of the dish at the banquet that should have killed her and hadn't. Something old had intercepted it. Quentin, probably, or whatever Quentin still was. A god of kitchen gardens, stepping between her and a plate.

*Why?* she asked the ceiling. The ceiling, being a ceiling, declined to answer. But Quentin's cracked voice was still in her ear from the shrine, rambling about roots and debts and the old hunger under Thornfell, and one thing he'd said kept surfacing like a cork: *Nothing down there gives anything for free. Not even rescue. Not even love.*

Had her mother known that? Had she stood in a kitchen in a famine year and weighed her daughter's soul against her daughter's body, and chosen the body, because the body was the thing in front of her, the thing she could feed with her own two hands? Of course she had. Xanthe would have done the same. That was the horror and the comfort of it, wound together like the two strands of a rope: her mother's love had been real, and her mother's love had been a transaction, and there had never been any contradiction between those facts, because that was what love looked like when it was poor. It looked like arithmetic. It looked like *I will give the thing I cannot see to keep the thing I can.*

Xanthe closed the book and held it against her chest and let herself, for exactly one minute, be a daughter instead of a taster.

Then the minute ended.

* * *

She spent the rest of the night doing what she did with any dish that might kill her: taking it apart.

The contract had a signature. The signature had a title. *Steward of Accounts.* Accounts meant ledgers. Ledgers meant a hall, a keeper, a system—and systems, Xanthe had learned in nine years of palace kitchens, could be entered. You could not walk through a wall, but you could walk through a door, and every door had a person who was tired of standing near it.

The contract had terms. *The turn of the thirteenth moon.* Which meant the debt had not yet come due, which meant there was still, technically, a negotiation to be had. Debtors could negotiate. Debtors could *travel*. And where did one travel to negotiate with a steward of accounts, if not down?

The contract had a flaw. She found it near dawn, and she laughed out loud, alone, in a cold room, which was the kind of thing her mother would have done too. The consideration—the price paid—was *the child surviving the winter of the black barley.* The child had survived. The consideration was rendered, complete, nine years ago. But the collection clause named no date of enforcement except the moon, and a debt whose terms were fully paid but not yet collected was, in any kitchen in the kingdom, a matter for the person holding the spoon.

Her mother had taught her that. *Whoever stirs the pot, owns the pot.* Mam had never once let anyone else stir.

Xanthe looked at the wooden spoon with the bitten handle, lying in the chest among the buttons and the small dead shoe, and she understood, with a clarity that felt like sunlight through ice, what she had to do.

She was going to go down under the Thornfell mountains, into the court that ate prayers and harvested souls, into the presence of a steward of accounts with an elegant hand and a starving god's patience, and she was going to negotiate for her own soul using her mother's recipe book as her credentials.

And she was not going alone. There was a cracked urn in a ruined shrine, and a half-forgotten god inside it who knew those roads, who knew that court, who had said *nothing down there gives anything for free* with the bitterness of a man who'd been robbed by the invoice.

Xanthe packed the way she did everything: efficiently, invisibly, leaving the room exactly as boring as she'd found it. Shawl, knife, bread, the recipe book wrapped in oilcloth. She hesitated over the wooden spoon.

Then she took it too. Whoever stirs the pot, owns the pot.

At the door she paused, one hand on the latch, and looked back at the chest, at the small shoe, at the shawl-shaped absence on her knees.

"You were a good mother," she told the room, and meant it, and it was the truest and most terrible sentence she'd ever said. "And I'm going to get your money back."

The thirteenth moon was eleven days away. The smuggler's road south started at the edge of the palace gardens—past a certain ruined shrine where, she happened to know, something old was blooming out of season, and waiting, and pretending not to.

Xanthe Kavanagh went out into the dark, stirring the pot.
