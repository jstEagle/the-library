## 5. The Smuggler's Road South

Quentin had not walked anywhere in ninety years.

He had *drifted*, mostly. He had seeped. A god of kitchen gardens does not so much travel as slowly become adjacent to other places, the way damp becomes adjacent to a wall. But when Xanthe Kavanagh came down the garden path at moonrise with a pack on her back and murder in her ledger, Quentin found he had legs again—or something like them—and that they remembered how to be used.

"You're limping," she said.

"I'm *strolling*," said Quentin. "There's a difference. Limping implies urgency."

"Your left side drags."

"My left side has always dragged. It's a feature. Gods of my particular vintage were designed asymmetrically—like jugs." He gestured grandly at the dark hedge ahead of them, and the hedge, out of some residual courtesy, leaned aside an inch. "Behold. The smuggler's road south."

Xanthe looked at the hedge. Then at him. "That's a gooseberry bush."

"It's a *gate* that happens to be embarrassed about it. Keep up."

* * *

The truth—which Quentin intended to keep between himself and his cracked urn for as long as possible—was that the road south was not a road at all but a habit. Smugglers had worn it into the mountain over two hundred years of not wanting to be seen, and things that are worn by wanting-not-to-be-seen take on a flavor. Old gods could taste it. Quentin could taste it the way Xanthe could taste arsenic in almond milk: faintly, professionally, with a shudder.

She could not taste it. That was the problem. She had a taster's tongue for poison and a peasant's map for mountains, and neither would help her when the wraith-toll collectors came drifting up from the court below to check their ledger-lines. So Quentin did what he had sworn off doing for nine decades, which was to care where someone else put her feet.

"Left," he said, for the eleventh time that night.

"I *was* going left."

"You were going left-ish. Left-ish is how people fall off this mountain. There's a family of crows down there that's been living off left-ish travelers since before I was shrunk, and they're fat, Xanthe. Fat crows mean no left-ish."

She adjusted her footing on the scree without complaint, which he was beginning to understand was her way. She rationed everything: water sipped by the thimbleful, bread broken into pieces the size of communion wafers, sleep taken in twenty-minute installments with one hand on her knife. He watched her divide her own safety into portions and serve herself last, always last, and something in him—the part that had once made tomato vines fruit out of sheer spite against frost—curled tight and hurt.

"You know," he said, "you're allowed to drink a whole mouthful. The mountain isn't keeping score."

"The mountain is always keeping score," she said. "Everything keeps score. I've read the paperwork."

"That's grim."

"That's *bookkeeping*." She passed him the waterskin anyway, unasked, and he drank more than his share because she pretended not to notice him doing it, and he pretended not to notice her pretending, and that was the first transaction of many that neither of them entered in any ledger.

* * *

They smelled the toll-collectors before dawn—not smelled, exactly; Quentin tasted them, a cold mineral tang like well-water from a grave—but Xanthe felt it too, in the way her hand went flat against the rock.

"How close?" she whispered.

"Close enough that we stop being clever and start being small." He pulled her down behind a shelf of stone furred with moss—*his* moss, technically, grown from a spore he'd carried in his crack for eighty years, and he'd never been prouder of it than now, when it closed over them like a blanket. "Collectors don't see. They *audit*. They drift along the debt-lines and anything that owes gets a tug. So whatever you do, don't owe anything right now."

"I owe you," she said. "You got me off the mountain path past the sentries."

Quentin's whole being did something undignified. "That's—that's not a debt, that's a *professional courtesy*. Between colleagues. Of the forgotten."

"Invisible," she corrected softly. "We're the invisible."

The collectors came through the pass like a draft under a door. Quentin felt them pass through the very stone they hid against—three shapes of ledger-light and hunger, trailing ink. One paused. Hovered. Tasted the air above their hiding place with the attention of a clerk who has found a column that doesn't sum.

Then it moved on, because Xanthe Kavanagh owed nothing in any book the court kept—yet—and because Quentin, god of kitchen gardens, registered to their senses as roughly as much soul as a turnip.

They waited until the cold taste faded. Xanthe's breath had gone shallow and quick, and Quentin realized she was shaking, and that she was furious about shaking, and that she would rather die than admit either.

"Hey," he said. "Turnip solidarity."

A wet, startled laugh escaped her before she could ration it. He decided it was the finest sound on the mountain, and immediately resolved never to tell her so, because gods who say things like that get their urns cracked further.

* * *

They made camp in a hollow where a shepherd's bothy had fallen in on itself generations ago, and Quentin did the thing he had been not-doing for ninety years: he grew something. Just a little. A patch of cress in the lee of the wall, coaxed up green and peppering in the space of an hour, because a god of kitchen gardens cannot watch a woman eat dry bread crumbs without committing a small act of insubordination against the natural order.

Xanthe stared at the cress. Then at him. Then at the cress.

"You've been sitting in a ruined shrine," she said, "for decades, able to do *that*?"

"Sitting in a shrine and doing are different professions. I've been on leave." He plucked a sprig and handed it over with as much dignity as a man-shaped collection of twigs and stubbornness can manage. "Eat. It's got iron in it. And opinions."

She ate. She ate the whole patch, actually, with the focused efficiency of someone who has spent nine years tasting food she was permitted to swallow only in fear, and Quentin watched her enjoy something for perhaps the first time, and felt a want open in him like a seed casing splitting.

He wanted her to come back tomorrow and eat cress again.

This was, he understood dimly, how it started. This was how gods got diminished—not stolen from all at once, but wanting, wanting, wanting, each want a little door that something hungry could eventually walk through. His divinity hadn't been taken in a night. It had been *spent*, wish by wish, on gardens that fed people who never learned his name.

And here he was, opening doors again. For a poison-taster with a death-wish schedule and eleven days of calendar.

"What are you looking at?" she asked.

"A fool," he said honestly. "Never mind. Sleep your twenty minutes. I'll wake you."

"You don't have to keep watch."

"No," he agreed. "I don't *have* to do anything. That's rather the point of me now." But he kept watch anyway, all four hours while she slept longer than twenty minutes because for once nothing in her body expected the food or the dark or the company to be poisoned, and Quentin sat in the falling-down bothy and let himself want things, quietly, terrified, like a man counting coins he already knew would be stolen.

* * *

On the third day the pass narrowed into the throat of the mountain, and the smell changed. Not the grave-cold of collectors—something older, sweeter, wrong. Honey and hot metal. Xanthe stopped dead.

"That's the smell from the banquet," she said. "The dish that should have killed me."

"Yes," Quentin said, and hated that his voice cracked on it. "Because it wasn't meant for you, not really. Poison's just the envelope. What's inside is addressed to the court below." He looked up at the black seam in the rock ahead, where the smuggler's road went down instead of through, and where the air pulsed faintly, like a throat swallowing. "They're feeding something down there. They've been feeding it for years, and the portions are getting bigger, and in eight days the thirteenth moon comes round and the bill arrives."

"And my name's on it."

"Ink still wet," he said gently. "I'm sorry. I should have told you sooner."

"You told me when it mattered." She rolled her shoulders back the way she did before tasting something lethal—chin up, breath even, self portioned and served last. "Come on, then. How far to the door?"

"Half a day. And Xanthe—" He caught her sleeve, twig-fingers on wool. "When we get there, they'll offer you something. They always do. A trade that sounds like mercy. Whatever it is, whatever shape it wears—" He didn't know how to finish it. He was a god of cress and gooseberries and one beloved cracked urn. He had no dominion over this.

But she looked at him, and something passed between them that no ledger recorded, and she said, "I know. Whoever stirs the pot owns the pot." A pause. "So don't let go of the spoon."

They went down together, the invisible and the turnip, toward the honey-and-hot-metal dark—and behind them, far above, the thirteenth moon rose early over the ridge, one day ahead of every chart Quentin had ever known.

Someone below was hurrying the calendar.

Someone below already knew they were coming.
