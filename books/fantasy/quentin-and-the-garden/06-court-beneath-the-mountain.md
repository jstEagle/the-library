## 6. Court Beneath the Mountain

The trick to passing as a soul-debtor, Quentin had told her, was to look thoroughly miserable.

"You'll want to slouch," he'd said, arranging her collar with fingers that smelled of loam and, faintly, of the honey cakes she'd brought him that morning. "Debtors are never proud. Pride is for people who own things. Slouch like the mountain itself is sitting on your shoulders."

"I've been invisible for nine years," Xanthe said. "Slouching is my native language."

"Ah, but this is a different dialect." He'd tapped her sternum. "Up there, you were nobody. Down here, you're somebody who owes. It's worse. Owning nothing makes you furniture. Owing everything makes you *food*."

She thought about that now, three hours into the dark, following the smuggler's road down through the roots of Thornfell while the walls sweated mineral light and the air grew thick enough to chew. She had dressed the part: her second-worst dress, mended badly on purpose; her mother's shawl, which still smelled of woodsmoke and dried lavender if you held it close enough to lie to yourself. Around her wrist she wore a cord with nine knots in it — Quentin's suggestion, a debtor's tally of years owed. He'd tied them himself, muttering an old blessing over each one, and when he finished the ninth he'd looked at his hands as though they'd betrayed him by remembering how.

"Don't die down there," he'd said at the mouth of the cave. "I've only just gotten used to being noticed."

* * *

The court announced itself long before she saw it.

First came the sound: a low, continuous murmur, like a thousand people praying in their sleep. Then the smell — not rot, exactly, but the sweetish staleness of a temple left shut too long, incense gone stale in the cloth. Then the light, rising up the passage in slow pulses, gold and dim and rhythmic, and it took Xanthe far too long to realize the light was breathing.

The passage opened into a cavern so vast the ceiling vanished into darkness overhead. Stalactites hung like the ribs of some drowned cathedral, and between them, tier upon tier carved into the living rock, sat the court.

They were beautiful the way winter is beautiful. Tall, gray-gold figures with faces like unfinished sculptures — features suggested rather than committed to, as though they'd once been vivid and had been worn smooth by centuries of being looked at wrong. They wore robes of woven shadow. And every one of them, every single one, was thin.

Not gaunt. *Thin* the way a candle flame is thin near its end. Xanthe could see the pulse of light inside them guttering, could see how they sat very still to conserve themselves, the way she rationed bread in a bad month.

A herald-thing with too many joints in its neck beckoned her forward. "Name and nature of debt?"

"Xanthe Kavanagh," she said, and her voice did not shake, because nine years of tasting poison teaches you that your voice is the last thing you get to keep. "Soul-debt. Inherited. I've come to negotiate terms before collection."

The herald consulted a slate it drew from somewhere inside its robes. Its eyes — small, dark, terribly tired — flicked up at her. "Kavanagh. Yes. You're expected." A pause. "You have your mother's chin."

"I wouldn't know."

"No," the herald said softly. "I suppose you wouldn't." It gestured her deeper. "The Ledger-Keeper will see you. Walk slowly. The hungry ones sit near the walls, and they mistake quick movement for generosity."

* * *

She walked slowly.

The hungry ones did sit near the walls — smaller gods, or fragments of gods, huddled in alcoves like beggars at a fire that had gone out generations ago. As she passed, heads turned. Not menacingly. That was the horror of it. They turned the way starving children turn toward the smell of bread, with a hopeless, practiced restraint, having learned long ago that hope was the cruelest course.

One of them, a little god with moss still clinging to one shoulder — a river spirit, maybe, or something that had once loved bridges — whispered as she went by: "Is it spring? Up there? Is it spring yet?"

"It's autumn," Xanthe said.

"Oh." The little god folded its hands. "Autumn's kind, though. Autumn has harvest festivals. People *thank* things, at harvest festivals." It smiled with terrible sweetness. "Thank you for answering. Nobody answers."

Xanthe kept walking, and hated herself for keeping walking, and kept walking anyway.

The Ledger-Hall occupied the cavern's heart: a round chamber floored in polished black stone, lit by a chandelier of glowworms arranged, with a certain grim whimsy, in the shape of a crown. At its center stood a desk of white bone-wood, and behind the desk sat the Ledger-Keeper — a squat, broad god with spectacles made of frozen tears and ink-stained fingers, the only well-fed creature in the entire cavern. He looked, incongruously, like a village schoolmaster, right down to the disapproving set of his mouth.

"Xanthe Kavanagh." He pronounced it carefully, tasting the name. "Sit."

She sat. The chair was warm. Everything down here was warm, she realized — the whole mountain was body-heat, the collective warmth of starving divinity, and the thought made her skin crawl in a way no poison ever had.

"You wish to negotiate." The Ledger-Keeper opened a ledger the size of a paving slab. "I'll be direct, as I am with all debtors: negotiation is a courtesy we extend to make ourselves feel civilized. The debt stands. Thirteenth moon. Collection follows."

"I want to know what collection *means*."

He looked at her over the frozen-tear spectacles. "Most don't ask."

"I taste poison for a living. Curiosity is my occupational disease."

Something flickered across his smooth gray face — amusement, perhaps, or its fossil. He turned the ledger around so she could read it.

"Then understand what you owe," he said. "And whom."

* * *

The ledger's pages were not paper. They were skin — pale, fine-grained, and when Xanthe touched the edge of one, it flinched.

"Prayer-skin," the Keeper said, without apology. "The oldest medium. Words written on faith hold longest." He traced a column with one stained finger. "Your world above believes the old gods died, or left, or were never real — whatever story is fashionable this century. Here is what actually happened. We starved. Slowly, then quickly. Worship thinned to ceremony, ceremony thinned to habit, habit thinned to nothing. A god can live a long time on almost nothing, taster. Almost nothing, for a very long time. But not on *nothing*."

"So you poison people."

"We *feed*," the Keeper said sharply, and for a moment the schoolmaster fell away and something vast and wounded looked out through his face. "Every dish your court's chefs lace, every noble who dines and grows strange and dreamy and devout — do you think trances are accidents? A poisoned soul loosens. It leaks prayer the way a cracked jug leaks wine. We catch what drips. Nine years of state banquets, taster, and this winter we will eat enough to wake fully. Enough to walk back into the world. Enough to make the fields honest and the rivers clean again, whether the world above remembers us or not."

"And the souls themselves? The ones that leak empty?"

The Keeper was quiet a moment. "Some few are collected whole. The deepest debts." He turned pages — hundreds of names, thousands, each in a different hand, each hand more desperate than the last. "We are not cruel, Xanthe Kavanagh. Cruelty wastes. We are *hungry*. There is a difference, and the difference is that cruelty sleeps well."

She should have felt triumphant, she supposed, or vindicated — here was the monster laid bare, the explanation, the enemy. Instead she felt the floor tilt under everything. Because she had watched those courtiers drift through the palace, dreamy and smiling, and she had pitied them. She had not once wondered who was hungry enough to need their dreams.

"The famine," she said quietly. "Thirty years ago. The southern provinces. Children starving in the roads."

"What of it?"

"My mother walked out of it with a living daughter. Everyone said it was luck."

The Keeper turned back several pages. His finger stopped. "Ah," he said gently. "Here."

He rotated the ledger toward her, and there it was.

*Kavanagh, Xanthe.* Her mother's handwriting — she knew it instantly, the cramped, stubborn letters of a woman who'd taught herself to write by copying grain-price notices off tavern doors. The contract beneath it was brief. *One child's survival, guaranteed through famine. Payment: her soul, due at the thirteenth moon of her ninth-and-twentieth year.*

The ink was still wet.

Nine years of contracts in that hall, and hers gleamed fresh as a wound. She stared at it and felt something crack open in her chest that was not quite grief and not quite rage but had the weight of both.

"She bought you bread," the Keeper said. "That was the bargain's shape. Not protection — bread. Every week of the famine, a loaf would appear where none should be. Your mother knew the price from the first day. She signed anyway. She asked only that you never know, so you could grow up unhaunted." He closed the ledger with surprising tenderness. "Mothers are the worst negotiators we deal with. They always overpay."

"She didn't sell me," Xanthe said. Her voice came out strange. "She *bought* me. From you."

"Yes. And now we've come to collect, and you've come to negotiate, and the moon turns in eleven days." The Keeper laced his ink-stained fingers. "So. What does the taster offer?"

* * *

It was later — much later, though time moved strangely under the breathing light — that she saw it.

She'd risen to leave, terms deferred, the Keeper watching her go with an expression she couldn't parse. And as she crossed the Ledger-Hall, past the alcoves of the hungry ones, a small god reached out and caught the hem of her shawl. Not to feed. Just to touch something woven above-ground, something that smelled of lavender and smoke and sky.

"Kind taster," it murmured. "You carry such interesting ghosts."

"Let go, please."

"One moment. One moment only." Its dark eyes fixed on her wrist, on the nine knotted cords. "You wear a god's blessing. Faint. Garden-small. He tied these himself, didn't he? I remember that knot-work. Urn-cracked Quentin. Kitchen-patch Quentin." The little god smiled. "We ate his bright half, you know. Years ago. It's in the vaults with the other stored radiance. Still glowing. Still *warm*."

The hall seemed to tilt.

"Where," Xanthe said.

The hungry god blinked at her tone, and something in its ruined face shifted — recognition, maybe, of what hunger sounds like when it speaks in another's voice.

"In the vaults," it repeated, puzzled. "Behind the ledger-hall. Why do you ask, kind taster? Debtors don't ask about the vaults."

"No," Xanthe agreed, already moving, already counting doors, already doing the arithmetic of eleven days and one stolen god-shard. "But gardeners do."
