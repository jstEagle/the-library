## 7. The Bargain She Refuses

The court beneath the mountain did not shout. That was the worst of it. Xanthe had expected chains, threats, the rattle of bone instruments — some honest ugliness she could brace against. Instead they gave her tea.

It was served in a cup of veined white stone by a herald whose face was a smooth oval, features suggested rather than committed to, like a sketch someone had abandoned halfway through liking it. The tea tasted of honey and, beneath the honey, faintly of iron — the way blood tastes when you've spent nine years learning where it hides in a flavor.

"You'll forgive the hospitality," said the Chancellor of Debts, who presided over the long table like a heron presiding over a pond. He was tall in the way that cave things are tall: too many joints, all of them patient. "We find that negotiations go better when no one is being eaten."

"That's very modern of you," Xanthe said.

"We are nothing if not adaptable." He turned a page of the ledger before him. Her name sat at its head in ink so fresh it shone. *Xanthe Kavanagh. Daughter of Maren Kavanagh. Collected upon the thirteenth moon.* "Now. You came here disguised as a soul-debtor come to negotiate. Let us spare each other the theater of pretending otherwise. You wish to know what your mother bought, and what it costs, and whether there is any version of this evening in which you walk out of here whole."

"Is there?"

"There is always a version." The Chancellor smiled. His teeth were small and even and entirely too numerous, like a mouthful of seeds. "You are a food-taster. Do you understand what an extraordinary thing you are? Nine years of poison, and still walking, still *reading* — you taste intent, we're told. You can tell a jealous cook from a careless one from a murderer across a banquet table. Do you know what that saturation is worth, to beings like us?"

"Beings like you," Xanthe repeated carefully.

The Chancellor gestured, and the hall answered. Torches guttered down to embers, and in the ember-light she saw what she had been refusing to see since she entered: the walls were not carved with figures. They were *made* of figures — old gods rendered in bas-relief, their stone cheeks hollowed, their stone hands pressed to stone stomachs. Starving. They had been starving for centuries, ever since the world above forgot their names and offered its prayers elsewhere.

"The poisonings," the Chancellor said gently, "are not murders. They are meals. Every soul we take above — every courtier who wanders into a trance, every debtor who signs too eagerly — feeds what remains of us. We are not cruel, taster. We are *hungry*, which history will tell you is far more forgivable."

"And my name in your ledger?"

"A debt, honestly incurred. Your mother signed in her own hand; we keep our contracts clean." He turned the page toward her, and there it was — Maren Kavanagh's careful, famine-thin script, the same hand that had written *eat something, even if it's only bread* on a hundred notes tucked into Xanthe's pockets. "But debts can be restructured. That is what I am offering. Walk away owing us nothing, and owe us instead a service."

The tea had gone cold in her hands. She hadn't noticed drinking it. She set it down with great care, as though it might be listening. It probably was.

"What service."

"You have the queen's table. You have her trust, her plate, her private kitchens, her ear when she takes supper alone. And in three days' time she hosts the Vigil Feast, where every soul in the palace drinks from the same cups." The Chancellor's voice stayed warm as banked coals. "One vessel. One harvest. Enough to wake what sleeps in these walls for another age. We would need only your hands, taster. You would not even have to lie to her. Tasters are never asked what's in the dish."

*You would not even have to lie to her.* It was obscene how kindly he phrased it. That was the trap's true craftsmanship — not the offer itself but the shape of it, built exactly to the hollows of her. Void the debt. Save her own soul. Keep Quentin's shard out of reach of people who could unmake him with a ledger line. All she had to do was become, at last and deliberately, the thing she had spent nine years pretending not to be: a weapon aimed at a kind woman's throat.

"I need time," Xanthe said.

"Of course." The Chancellor closed the ledger with a sound like a kiss. "Two days. The thirteenth moon does not negotiate, but I am softer than the moon."

As the herald-thing escorted her up through the tunnels, toward the smuggler's road and the thin grey light of morning, Xanthe caught herself doing arithmetic. Not *should I* — that question had already answered itself somewhere below, quietly, without ceremony. No. The arithmetic was worse. It was *how long do I have, and who do I warn first, and what does Quentin cost me if I fail,* and every sum came out the same: someone she loved, on the wrong side of the equals sign.

* * *

Quentin was waiting at the shrine, and he had, apparently, been busy.

The ruined little garden around his cracked urn had exploded into riotous, unauthorized life. Beans climbing the broken columns. A squash vine attempting to annex the altar. Marigolds shoulder-to-shoulder like a crowd that had heard something was happening and refused to miss it. In the middle of it all stood a god barely taller than the marigolds, dirt to his elbows, looking simultaneously proud and guilty.

"It's called momentum," he said, before she could speak. "I planted one row and then the ground remembered what it was for and simply — went. I couldn't stop it. I didn't especially want to." He squinted at her. "You look like a woman who has been negotiating with herons."

"Something like that." She sank down onto the shrine's low step, and a bean tendril immediately investigated her hair. She let it. "They offered to void my debt."

Quentin went very still, in the way of small creatures deciding whether to freeze or flee. "And the price?"

"My hands. My access. The Vigil Feast, three nights hence. One cup, poured right, and the whole court above becomes a harvest." She laughed, and it came out cracked. "Do you know the truly insulting part? It's a good bargain. By any honest accounting, it's a *good* bargain. One soul — mine — against hundreds. Except the hundreds aren't dying, they're being *eaten*, and the queen —"

"The queen who trusts you."

"The queen who trusts me." Xanthe pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes. "She asked me to investigate the trances. Me. Because I'm observant and invisible and she thought I'd be safe. She's going to drink from those cups believing I've already tasted everything on her table, because that's what I'm *for*. And the only way out of my debt runs straight through her wine."

Quentin crouched in front of her. Up close, his divinity showed in small ways — the exact green of new growth in his irises, the way the air around him smelled faintly of rain on warm soil. When he spoke, his voice had lost its half-mad flutter. Neglect had burned a lot out of him, but apparently not this.

"You're not going to do it."

"No."

"Even though it voids the debt. Even though it might be the only coin that buys back my shard." His jaw tightened. "Even though I am, objectively, a minor garden god of extremely limited portfolio, and you'd be trading a queen for a *turnip specialist*."

A laugh escaped her, wet and unwilling. "You're not a turnip specialist."

"I have strong opinions about turnips, Xanthe. It counts."

"I'm not doing it," she said, "because I have spent nine years surviving by being useful to power, and I know exactly what that costs, and I refuse — I *refuse* — to spend the rest of my life knowing I bought my soul with hers." She looked at him. "That's the whole arithmetic. There isn't more."

For a moment Quentin said nothing. Then, softly, almost afraid of the words: "When I was whole, people made bargains about me constantly. Villages traded my festivals for rain rights. Priests sold my relics. Nobody ever once asked what I wanted, because wanting is such a small thing to give a god." He reached out and took her poison-scarred hand in both of his earth-dark ones. "Thank you for wanting me anyway."

Above the shrine, the sky had begun to bruise toward evening, and the moon — thin, hungry, nearly round — was already showing its face ahead of the dark.

* * *

The trouble with returning to a palace after two days' absence is that palaces notice. Palaces are Quentin's opposite: nothing grows there unseen, and everything is counted.

Xanthe made it as far as the second corridor before Lord Aldous Vane fell into step beside her.

He was the queen's spymaster, and he wore his profession the way other men wore cologne — lightly, expensively, and impossible to ignore once you knew the scent. He was also carrying two cups of mulled wine, one of which he offered her with the easy smile of a man who had absolutely checked it first.

"Lady Kavanagh. Or should I say *Mistress* Kavanagh — forgive me, titles blur. Two days gone. No leave requested. Your lodgings report your bed unslept-in, which for you is practically a confession; you strike me as a woman who sleeps the way soldiers sleep, lightly and out of duty." He sipped. "And now you return smelling of woodsmoke and mountain pine, with dirt under your fingernails that I'd wager my pension is not palace dirt."

"Gardening," Xanthe said. "It's a hobby."

"Mm. And the trances?" His eyes were pleasant and bottomless. "Her Majesty set you to finding their source. Have you found it?"

Every loyalty demanding a different betrayal — she could feel them now, pulling her apart like horses in a team that had stopped agreeing on direction. Tell Vane the truth, and the truth led to a starving subterranean court, a ledger with the queen's own harvest scheduled, and a god in a garden whom Vane's predecessors had helped forget. Lie to Vane, and lie to the queen, and become exactly the creature the Chancellor wanted her to be.

"I've found a thread," she said at last. "I need three more days to pull it."

Vane studied her for a long, silken moment. "Three days brings us to the Vigil Feast."

"Yes."

"How convenient for everyone involved." He drained his wine and smiled. "Then here is my gift to you, Mistress Kavanagh, freely given: I have assigned a man to follow you. He is excellent. You will not see him. Consider him not surveillance but *company* — the road south through Thornfell is dangerous this season, and I would hate for anything to happen to the queen's taster."

He bowed and drifted away, leaving Xanthe standing in the corridor with cold hands and a tail she could not shake, while overhead, through the high windows, the thirteenth moon climbed fat and pale toward fullness — two days early-looking, though moons keep no one's schedule but their own.

Behind her, faintly, from the direction of the kitchens, she heard the first deliveries arriving for the Vigil Feast.

Crate after crate after crate, stamped with the seal of Thornfell.
