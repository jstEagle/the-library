## 8. Thirteenth Moon Rising

Quentin had been a god for a very long time, and in all those centuries he had learned exactly three things with any certainty: that beans always came up crooked no matter how you planted them, that prayers from hungry children carried further than prayers from kings, and that the truth, like compost, was best turned over early before it went septic.

He had been sitting on the truth about his divinity for eleven days now. It had gone thoroughly septic.

Xanthe knelt at the shrine's edge with her sleeves rolled to the elbow, turning the winter-bare soil by hand because she didn't trust the trowel he'd grown for her out of a blackthorn branch. The garden around them bloomed in defiance of the season—marigolds in the frost, rosemary flowering out of turn, a fig tree heavy with fruit that had no business existing this far north and less business existing in midwinter. His power leaked into everything here. It was the only place in the world where he could still be said to exist at all.

"You're staring," Xanthe said without looking up.

"I'm observing."

"You're staring the way you stare when you've done something wrong or are about to." She sat back on her heels and wiped her hands on her apron—the queen's livery apron, still, though she'd taken to wearing it inside-out so the crest wouldn't show. "Which is it?"

"Neither."

"Quentin."

"It's both," he admitted. "It's historically both."

She waited. That was the thing about Xanthe Kavanagh that unsettled him more than any wraith-toll collector ever had: she could wait. Nine years of tasting poison taught a person patience of a very particular flavor. He had watched her sit through silences that would have cracked a lesser mortal like an egg.

The thirteenth moon hung above the Thornfell peaks even now, a pale sliver in the afternoon sky, growing fatter each night. Four days left. Perhaps five, if the court below counted generously, and the court below did not count generously.

"The ledger," Quentin said finally. "When you read it. In the hall beneath the mountain."

"I remember."

"You read your own name. You read what they're doing with the harvest—the prayers, the souls, the slow siphon through every poisoned dish." He picked up a fallen marigold and turned it in his fingers. It was warm. Everything he touched was warm; it was one of the few dignities remaining to him. "You didn't read far enough down the page."

Her hands went still in the soil. "Whose name was under mine?"

"Mine." He said it lightly, the way one says *the weather's turned* or *we're out of bread*. "Or rather—not my name. My shard. What's left of me that isn't—" he gestured at himself, at the cracked urn, at the general thinness of his being, "—this."

* * *

He told it badly, and he knew it, and he told it anyway because the alternative was telling it never.

"A kitchen-garden god isn't much," he began. "I want to be clear about that. I wasn't a war god. I wasn't a hearth god, which would have been respectable. I kept the beans upright and the blight off the cabbages and I made sure the cook's youngest daughter stopped putting salt in the honey cakes, because gods can only do small things and that was mine. Small things, done steadily, for people who mostly forgot to say thank you."

"And then?"

"And then the old gods under the mountain started starving." He let the marigold fall. "This was long ago. Before the famine your mother knew. They'd been sleeping, and something woke them wrong, and they woke *hungry*, and hunger makes theologians of everyone. They couldn't reach the living world directly—too much stone, too many wards, too much forgetting. So they bought. Through intermediaries. Through smuggler roads and middlemen and one very persuasive merchant who came to my shrine dressed as a pilgrim and offered me a bargain I was too diminished to refuse."

"What kind of bargain?"

"He said I was dying anyway. He was right. No one had tended my shrine in forty years. My urn was cracked, my grove cut down for firewood, my name mispronounced by exactly one old woman who then died. He said: give us your divinity—we'll hold it in trust—and we'll see that your valley never knows famine. And I thought—" Quentin laughed, and it came out as the sound wind makes through bean rows, hollow and green. "I thought, what use is a god who can't feed anyone? Better the divinity do some good down there than rot up here with me."

Xanthe had gone very pale. Not taster-pale—that was a professional pallor, controlled, useful. This was personal.

"They fed the valley," she said slowly. "During the famine years. There were villages along the Thornfell foothills that shouldn't have survived, and they did. My village was one of them."

"Yes."

"My mother—"

"Bought your survival," Quentin finished quietly. "From a court that had just enough stolen divinity sloshing around its vaults to make such miracles cheap. Yes. I worked that out some time ago. I'm sorry. I know it doesn't help to know your life was purchased partly with my remains, but there it is. We've been entangled longer than either of us knew."

She stood. She walked to the edge of the shrine, arms crossed, looking at the frost-line where his warmth ended and the real winter began. He gave her the silence. He owed her that much, and more.

"So it's still there," she said at last. "Your divinity. In their vaults. In the ledger."

"In the ledger. Under collateral. Which means—" and here his voice did something traitorous, going rough, "—which means it's leverage. You walk into that hall four nights from now, and instead of begging them to void your debt, you offer them a trade. Their claim on your soul against the return of my shard. A god's divinity is worth a hundred mortal souls to starving things. They'll take it. They may even be grateful."

"And you get restored." She turned back to face him. "Whole. What you were."

"What I was," he agreed, "was a minor deity of legumes. But whole."

"No." She shook her head. "No, you're doing the thing again."

"What thing?"

"The thing where you describe yourself as though you're reading your own obituary and expect me not to notice." She crossed back toward him, and there was that taster's steadiness in her eyes, the look she must have worn over nine years of swallowing death and smiling at the queen. "There's a cost. There's always a cost. Tell me the cost."

* * *

Here is what Quentin had learned about wanting things: it was worse than starving.

For two hundred years he had wanted nothing. Wanting required a shape, and he had gradually lost his—a little each decade, like a shoreline. Then a woman with poison in her blood and grief in her spine had started weeding his ruined shrine on her days off, muttering complaints about his taste in flowers, and the wanting had come back all at once, greedy and enormous, like spring.

He wanted to keep her. That was the whole of it. He wanted four more seasons of her cursing at the bean rows. He wanted to teach her grafting. He wanted to find out whether she hummed when she was happy, because he suspected she did and had never once caught her at it.

And the plan he had just handed her—the plan that saved her soul—might unmake him entirely.

"The shard has been in their vaults for centuries," he said. "Poured out of me, stored, handled by things that eat souls for breakfast. Divinity remembers its shape, but it also remembers what's been done to it. If it's returned to me—if I take it back in—I don't know how much of *me* comes with it. The vessel is small now. The wine is old. Pour old wine into a cracked urn and sometimes the urn holds, and sometimes—" He shrugged. "Sometimes the wine wins. Sometimes what stands up afterward wears my face and keeps my memories and is not, strictly speaking, me. A god-shaped appetite with a gardener's manners."

Xanthe stared at him. "You've known this for eleven days."

"Twelve."

"*Twelve.*"

"I was waiting for the right moment."

"There is no right moment for 'consuming my soul might replace me with a monster,' Quentin!"

"There's a *less wrong* moment," he protested, "and I judged this to be it, since in four days we'll both be dead or harvested and the moment will have expired entirely—"

"Don't you dare be funny about this." Her voice cracked in the middle, which frightened him more than shouting would have. Xanthe did not crack. Xanthe swallowed arsenic and asked politely for seconds. "You let me build a plan around saving you. Every road south, every wraith we dodged, every night I spent memorizing the ledger-hall—I built it around getting *you* back. And you knew. You knew it might not be you that came back."

"Yes," Quentin said. "I did."

"Why?" And there it was, the question underneath, the one she actually meant: *why does your survival matter less to you than mine?*

He considered lying. He was, after all, a god of small things, and lies were among the smallest. But she had fed him honey cakes on the solstice and pulled bindweed out of his flowerbeds and once, half-asleep against the shrine wall, called him *home* in a voice she would deny to her grave. He could not lie to her. It would be like salting the honey cakes.

"Because I spent two hundred years as nothing," he said, "and I had made my peace with it. And then you came along and reminded me what it was to want, and wanting hurt, but it hurt *beautifully*, and I thought: well. If this is the last shape my existence takes, let it be useful. Let it buy something that matters. You talk about your invisibility like it was armor, Xanthe, but I watched you tend a forgotten god's shrine for a year before you knew he was listening. You were never invisible. You were just waiting for someone worth being seen by."

The silence stretched between them, full of bees that should not have been alive in winter and were, because everything near him refused to die properly.

"That's not an answer," she said thickly. "That's a speech."

"I'm a god. Speeches are most of what I have left."

"Well." She wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand, briskly, as though tears were a weed to be pulled. "Then here's mine. I'm not trading your shard for my soul."

"Xanthe—"

"Hear me out. I'm going to steal it."

He blinked. "Steal—"

"You heard me. The ledger-hall. Four nights from now, when the whole court is gathered for the collection and every eye is on the moon-turn. I've already mapped the ward-rot on the eastern stair—you showed me, that first night, remember? You said the old stones remembered you. Well, they're about to get reacquainted." She was pacing now, taster-calm, planter's hands gesturing, and Quentin felt something in his cracked urn lurch that had nothing to do with divinity. "We don't negotiate. We don't trade. We take your shard back, we burn my page, and we leave before the thirteenth moon finishes rising. Both of us. Whole."

"If the shard destroys me—"

"Then it destroys you *free*," she said fiercely, "and not in a vault, and not as their collateral, and not as a bargain chip I spent to save my own skin. Whatever happens, it happens chosen. That's the only currency I trust anymore."

Quentin looked at her—at this small, saturated, stubborn mortal who had survived nine years of poison by being useful and unseen, and who was now proposing to rob a court of starving gods armed with a trowel and a grudge.

"You're going to get us both harvested," he said.

"Probably."

"It's a terrible plan."

"It's the best one I have."

And Quentin—who had kept beans upright through drought and blight, who had done small things steadily for people who never said thank you, who had wanted nothing for two hundred years and now wanted one impossible woman safe in his garden for fifty more—found himself laughing until the marigolds shivered.

"Then we'd better get the details right," he said. "Come here. The eastern stair's ward-rot runs deeper than I showed you, and if we're going to commit deicide-by-trowel, you'll need to know which stones sing and which ones scream."

They bent together over the frost-warmed soil, drawing the mountain's bones in spilled seeds, while overhead—fat and white and patient—the thirteenth moon began, at last, to rise.
