## 9. The Harvest Interrupted

The thirteenth moon rose the color of a bruise, and Xanthe walked into the mountain wearing her own name.

That was the trick of it, she realized as the wraith-toll collectors parted before her at the southern gate. Nine years of being nobody had taught her every disguise worth knowing, and the best one was always the truth, worn slightly wrong. She had come to the court beneath Thornfell not as an intruder but as what the ledger said she was: a debtor, arriving on the appointed night, punctual as poison.

"Xanthe Kavanagh," said the gate-warden, a thing like a man made of pressed ash, consulting a slate that wept condensation. "Collection is at moon's turn. You're early."

"I'm thorough," she said. "It's why I've lived so long."

The warden almost smiled. Almost. Ash-faces couldn't manage it properly; their mouths were made for other work.

They led her down through corridors of black stone veined with something that glowed faintly gold—the stolen divinity, Quentin had told her, worked into the very bones of the court like mortar. He had wanted to come with her. He had argued, in his cracked-urn voice, that two thieves stood better odds than one, and she had argued back that a god walking into the place that ate gods was less a rescue than a delivery.

"You'll wait by the hot spring," she'd said, "and if I'm not back by moon's turn plus one hour, you'll go home and you'll keep the garden alive, and you will *not* do anything noble, because nobility is how you got shrunk to the size of a bad decision in the first place."

He hadn't liked it. He'd kissed her anyway, tasting of loam and green things, and said, "One hour, taster. I'm counting. Gods are very good at counting. It's most of what we do."

Now she counted too—her steps, the torches, the doors—as they brought her deeper, past halls where the starving old gods sat propped like grandfathers at a funeral feast, too proud to admit the table was empty. Their eyes followed her. All of them could smell it on her, she knew. The nine years. The accumulation. To them she must smell like a pantry left unlocked.

The ledger-hall was at the bottom of everything, because important rooms always are. It was smaller than she'd imagined—a round chamber with a domed ceiling painted with thirteen moons, one for each month of the old reckoning, and the thirteenth painted fresh, its silver still wet enough to shine. In the center stood a desk of ironwood, and behind it sat the Ledger-Keeper, who was either a very old woman or a very young crow, depending on which way you looked at her.

And there, on the desk, in a setting like a ring box at a jeweler's: a shard of light no bigger than a thumbnail, humming faintly, the way a tuning fork hums after you strike it against something true.

Quentin. Or the piece of him that had been sold, ninety years ago, to pay for a garden that outlived its worshippers.

"The debtor arrives," said the Ledger-Keeper, without looking up. Her pen scratched. "Name?"

"You know my name. It's in your book."

"It's polite to say it aloud. Names lose their flavor otherwise." She looked up then, and her eyes were the exact brown of dried ink. "You've been eating well, Xanthe Kavanagh. Well—being eaten well. There's a difference. Shall we review your account?"

"I'd rather review his." Xanthe nodded at the shard. "Garden god. Minor portfolio. Kitchen plots, window boxes, the patron saint of things that grow where they shouldn't. You bought his divinity from someone—I never learned who—and you've kept it here ever since, feeding crumbs of it to your starving masters like butter scraped thin over too much bread."

The Ledger-Keeper set down her pen with great care, the way people do when they are deciding whether to be amused.

"And how does a food-taster know so much about our accounts?"

"Because I've tasted your work. Every dish at court that made a lord dream of roots growing through his ribs, every lady who woke speaking in a language older than the kingdom—that was you. A harvest, spoonful by spoonful. Prayers skimmed off the top of sleeping minds. Souls sipped slow, so no one chokes." She took a step closer. The shard's hum grew louder, or perhaps that was her own blood. "I know harvests. I've watched them taken from me for nine years, one poison at a time."

Something moved in the shadows at the chamber's edge—one of the old gods, drawn down from its chair by the scent of her, all hunger and patience. The Ledger-Keeper raised one finger and it stilled, sulking.

"Moon's turn is close," the Keeper said. "Here is what happens now. We collect what your mother sold us. It is a small soul, honestly—a taster's soul, mostly scar tissue—but a debt is a debt, and the book must balance. Unless—" and here her crow-eye glittered, "—you'd prefer the alternative arrangement. The one my lords discussed with you. The queen's cup, the final toast, the harvest of a whole court gathered into one vessel. Your debt voided. Your immunity honored. A position of *taste*, shall we say, rather than mere survival."

"I did consider it," Xanthe said truthfully. "For nearly four minutes. Then I remembered I like the queen, and I dislike you, and those have always been the two poles of my entire personality."

She moved before the Keeper finished frowning.

Nine years of training went into that motion—not swordsmanship, nothing so useful, but the small quick economy of a woman who has spent a decade palping food for danger while smiling at the person across the table. Her hand closed around the shard, and it burned like a coal and sang like a struck bell, and every god in the room turned toward her at once with the unified attention of wolves hearing a whistle.

"Oh," said the Ledger-Keeper softly. "Oh, you foolish little dish."

The ledger opened itself. Pages turned without wind. And Xanthe saw her own name rise up the page like a bubble through dark water, the ink around it gleaming wet, gathering itself, reaching—

She had planned this part last, because it was the part she couldn't rehearse.

"Wait," she said, and her voice cracked only a little, which she counted as a triumph. "Before you take it. Before you write me down. I want to show you what you're buying."

She held up her free hand, palm out, and let it happen.

It had been building for days, since Quentin first told her the truth of what she was: not immune, never immune, just *full*. Nine years of arsenic and nightshade, of henbane and the gray powders with no common name, swallowed in measured doses because a dead taster protects no one. The poisons had nowhere to go, so they had gone everywhere—into her blood, her marrow, the whites of her eyes, the dreams she didn't remember. She was not a woman who survived poison.

She was a woman made of it.

The light came off her skin like heat off a summer road. The nearest god recoiled, then lunged, hunger beating sense—and stopped, mid-lunge, its mouth full of her shadow, and began to scream in a voice like a cellar door. What it had swallowed was working through it, and there was nothing in creation that could digest nine years of Xanthe Kavanagh.

"No," said the Ledger-Keeper, rising at last, ink spilling off her pages like panic. "No, that's not—you were supposed to be *clean*. A clean vessel. That's what the contract—"

"My mother bought my survival," Xanthe said. "Nobody promised you a meal. Read the fine print, Keeper. I hear you're very good with fine print."

Her name came up the page, reached the surface, and she felt it begin to pull—the cold hook behind her navel, the sensation of being *read*—and she laughed, actually laughed, because it tickled, and because terror and laughter have always been neighbors in her chest.

"Take it, then," she said. "Take my soul and swallow it down with everything else I am. Every drop of what this court fed me, concentrated and aged nine years in the cask of my miserable, invisible life. Drink deep, my lords. I'm the richest vintage you'll ever be poisoned by."

The hook pulled. She held on to the shard and let it.

What happened next, she would describe afterward as a ledger catching fire, though Quentin insisted it was more accurate to call it a ledger being *eaten*. The ink of her name met the ink of her account and found, to its horror, that they were the same substance—nine years of harvested death, returned to sender. The page blackened. The black spread, page to page, contract to contract, every bargain written in the court-below's hand unraveling into smoke that smelled, Quentin swore forever after, of burnt toast.

The old gods fled their chairs. The Ledger-Keeper screamed a word in a language with no survivors and stamped her foot, and the stamp itself cracked the thirteenth moon painted overhead, right down the middle.

Xanthe ran.

Up through the gold-veined dark, past empty chairs and abandoned halls, the shard clutched to her chest like a bird with a heartbeat, her body coming apart and reassembling with every step as nine years of poison finally, *finally* found somewhere to go—which was nowhere, which was out, which was leaving her the way floodwater leaves a house: taking the furniture with it.

She burst out of the mountain's mouth into snow and starlight and collapsed face-first beside the hot spring, where a small god in a patched coat knelt in the drifts with his hands already glowing green.

"One hour and eleven minutes," Quentin said, and his voice shook. "You're late, taster."

"Had to burn down a government," she mumbled into the snow. "These things take time."

His hands found the shard and her collarbone in the same moment, and the light went home into him like water into cracked earth, and he gasped—grew—an inch, two inches, years of stolen height returning—and then stopped, still small, still thin, still *him*, restored but rationed, a god on rations.

And Xanthe lay in the snow and felt, with clinical detachment, the last of her immunity guttering out of her like a candle in a draft. No armor left. No arsenal. A body that was, for the first time in nine years, merely a body—mortal, permeable, one bad mushroom away from disaster.

She started laughing and couldn't stop.

"Xanthe." Quentin's face hovered over hers, frightened. "What's wrong? What's happening?"

"Nothing," she wheezed. "Everything. I'm going to have to *chew my food* now, Quentin. Like a normal person. I could die of a bad oyster." She grabbed his coat and pulled him down into the snow beside her, laughing into his shoulder. "Do you understand? Anyone can kill me now. *Anyone.*"

"Yes," he said slowly, and then, because he was beginning to understand her, he smiled—the first whole smile she'd ever seen on him. "So you'll have to choose, every day, whose cooking to trust. How terrible. How *alive*."

Above the mountains, the thirteenth moon slid past its fullness and began, at long last, to wane.

Below them, far beneath the stone, something vast and starving stirred in the wreckage of its ledger, and began—very quietly, very patiently—to count what remained.

But that was tomorrow's problem. Tonight there was snow, and a hot spring, and a god who owed her a garden, and Xanthe Kavanagh closed her unprotected eyes and slept like the dead, which, she reflected drowsily, was now a figure of speech.
