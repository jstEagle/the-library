## 10. Rooted

The poison had left her body slowly, over three days of fever and sweating in a rented room above a chandler's shop, but the fear of it lingered longer. Xanthe kept catching herself reaching for habits that no longer served her: checking the color of her wine, counting the steps between herself and the door, tasting the air of every room for the faint almond ghost of arsenic.

There was nothing to taste anymore. She was, for the first time in nine years, an ordinary woman with an ordinary body that could be killed by a careless cook or a determined enemy or a bad oyster. It should have terrified her. Some mornings it did. But mostly it felt like setting down a load she had carried so long she'd forgotten the shape of her own shoulders without it.

The queen received her alone, in the small morning room where the light came through amber glass and made everything look like something out of an old story. Queen Maren was not wearing her crown. She was feeding crumbs to a one-legged sparrow on the windowsill, which was the sort of thing most people never learned about her, because most people never bothered to look.

"Xanthe." The queen did not turn around. "My spymaster tells me you've been lying to me for weeks. He tells it very dramatically, with maps. I told him to wait, because I wanted to hear it from you."

"Then I'll save us both the trouble of pretending otherwise," Xanthe said. "Yes. I lied. I'm going to tell you all of it, and then I'm going to resign, and you may have me arrested at any point during the telling if it pleases you."

The queen turned then. There were new lines around her eyes that hadn't been there a season ago — grief for the courtiers lost to the trances, Xanthe thought, and the particular exhaustion of a ruler who has learned her kingdom's pantry was stocked with stolen souls.

"I would rather hear it than arrest you," Maren said. "Though I reserve the right to change my mind. Sit down. You look terrible."

"I've been told I look better than I have any right to."

"That was before whatever this is." The queen sat across from her, folded her hands, and waited with the patience of a woman who had raised four children and buried two.

So Xanthe told her. All of it, in order, as plainly as she could: the thirteenth dish that hadn't killed her, the shrine in the garden, Quentin, the contract in her mother's hand, the road south, the court beneath Thornfell, the ledger with her name in wet ink, the bargain she'd refused, and finally the harvest interrupted — nine years of swallowed death offered up like a cup no god could drink from, the ledger burning, the old gods scattering into the deep places to starve or mend or do whatever old gods did when their meal was snatched away.

She left out almost nothing. She left out the way Quentin's hand had felt in hers when they climbed back into daylight, because some things were not the queen's business. She left out how afraid she still was, most nights, of a world without armor.

When she finished, the silence went on long enough that the sparrow finished its crumbs and flew off through a gap in the glass.

"You understand," the queen said at last, "that what you're confessing is treason. You conspired with a foreign power — even if that power was a kitchen god in a cracked urn. You traveled under false pretenses. You destroyed evidence my magistrates will now never be able to study."

"Yes, Majesty."

"And you saved my court. Possibly my kingdom. Certainly my soul, though I didn't know it was on the menu." The queen rubbed her eyes. "I cannot publicly forgive you, Xanthe. If I do, every ambitious fool at court will learn that treason plus success equals pardon, and I'll spend the rest of my reign putting out fires."

"I know. That's why I'm resigning."

"That's why you're *what?*"

Xanthe smiled despite herself. "You heard me. A taster who can be poisoned is worse than useless — she's a liability and a target. And a taster who knows what the court-below's poisons smell like could be useful, I suppose, but only if she's honest, and I've spent nine years being useful instead of honest. I'd like to try the other thing for a while. See how it suits me."

The queen studied her for a long moment. "You know what I find remarkable? Nine years, and I never once wondered about you. You were furniture. Excellent, loyal furniture. And the whole time you were carrying more weight than half my council combined." She rose, went to the window, and stood with her back turned. "There's a cottage on the royal demesne near the Thornfell foothills. Old gamekeeper's house, empty since the last keeper died. The rent is one basket of vegetables a season, delivered to my kitchens. Non-negotiable."

"Majesty—"

"It is not charity. It is severance, and witness protection, and gratitude, all disguised as a land arrangement so that neither of us has to say anything embarrassing. Take it or don't." A pause. "Take it, Xanthe."

"I'll take it," Xanthe said, and found her voice had gone rough. "And Majesty — the vegetables will be excellent. I know someone with a gift for growing things."

"I assumed you did," said the queen, and for just a moment, before Xanthe reached the door, the Queen of the realm smiled like an ordinary tired woman glad to see a friend walking free.

* * *

The shrine looked smaller in spring.

That was unfair, Xanthe corrected herself, hauling the last basket of seed potatoes up the hill path. The shrine hadn't changed. She had. Nine years ago she had crept up here with stolen bread and a guilty conscience, and the ruined little temple had seemed vast and tragic, a monument to forgotten things. Now she saw it plainly: two crumbling walls, a roof of new-hatched thatch (Quentin had opinions about thatch), and a cracked stone urn in the alcove where a god lived, because apparently divinity, once restored, fit comfortably in a vessel the size of a wine jug.

Quentin was kneeling in the dirt of the old garden plot, arguing with a worm.

"Not *there,*" he was saying, in the tone of a man negotiating with a stubborn mule. "That's where the carrots go. You can have the north bed. The north bed is excellent. Damp, shaded, full of everything a worm of discernment could want—" He paused, cocked his head, listened to something Xanthe couldn't hear, and sighed. "Fine. FINE. The carrots can share. But I want it noted that this is extortion."

"He drives a hard bargain?" Xanthe asked, setting down the basket.

"He wants the entire east bed and he doesn't even eat roots. It's the principle of the thing." Quentin looked up at her, and the sight of him still caught somewhere behind her ribs, every time. Restored, he was not the towering radiant god of old stories — he was a lean, sun-browned man with soil permanently under his fingernails and eyes the green of the first brave shoots of March. Small, he'd said, and meant it. Enough divinity to make a garden generous, not enough to make a mountain move. He had wept about it, the first night, and then planted something, because that was how Quentin grieved: by putting seeds in the ground.

"Well?" he said. "Did she take it?"

"The cottage? Yes. One basket of vegetables a season, delivered to the palace kitchens." Xanthe sat down beside him in the warm dirt, not caring about her dress, because dresses were for people with reputations and she had resigned hers along with the job. "I told her the vegetables would be excellent."

"They will be. I've already bribed the worms." He hesitated, turning a seed potato over in his fingers. "Are you certain? About all of it? No immunity, no position, no — armor. Anyone with a grudge and a vial could—"

"Anyone with a grudge and a vial could have gotten me any day these nine years, if they'd known where to aim. The difference is that now I'd see it coming, and I'd have people worth running toward instead of walls worth hiding behind." She bumped her shoulder against his. "Besides. You'll taste everything before I eat it. You're a god of gardens; I assume you can tell a poisoned parsnip at forty paces."

"I can tell a poisoned parsnip at forty paces," he admitted. "It's one of my three miracles. The other two are making beans grow angry and knowing exactly when rain is coming, which sounds useful until you realize I can't do anything about it."

"So we'll carry umbrellas."

"So we'll carry umbrellas." He laughed — really laughed, the sound rolling down the hillside and startling birds out of the hawthorn — and pressed the seed potato into her palm. His hand closed over hers, warm as sun-warmed stone. "Eyes here. This one goes deepest. Everything else we plant today grows around it."

"What is it?"

"First potato of the first garden of the rest of your life. It deserves ceremony." He guided her hands, and together they dug the hole — properly deep, properly wide, the loosened earth dark and smelling of rain and possibility — and set the potato in like a heart being returned to a chest.

Xanthe knelt back and looked at the plot: the straight furrows waiting, the crooked trellis Quentin had built from fallen branches, the shrine standing patient behind them, the whole hillside green with a spring that had come early and stayed late, as if the land itself were leaning in to watch.

Nine years of surviving, and she had called it living because she hadn't known the difference. Survival was a held breath. This — dirt under her nails, a god arguing with worms beside her, a debt paid and a name that belonged to no ledger but her own — this was breathing out.

"Quentin," she said.

"Hm?"

"When the beans grow angry — how angry are we talking?"

He grinned, and the whole hillside seemed to grin with him. "Plant them and find out."

She did.
