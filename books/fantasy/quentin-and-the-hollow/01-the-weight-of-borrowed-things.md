## 1. The Weight of Borrowed Things

The bell above the door of the Charmwright's Rest had a crack in it, so that every customer announcement came out as a small, apologetic stutter—*ding-ding-ding* instead of a clean ring. Ulyana Zhou had never fixed it. A cracked bell, she'd decided years ago, sounded like a shop that couldn't afford trouble, and a shop that couldn't afford trouble was a shop nobody bothered to rob.

This morning it rang for Mrs. Aldery.

Mrs. Aldery was a widow of some eleven months now, which in Vessing was long enough to stop wearing black and start wearing grey, but not so long that anyone expected her to be cheerful. She came in on the second Thursday of every month, always at half past ten, always with the same careful walk of a woman who had learned that grief made people clumsy and refused to be clumsy. She bought nothing. She sold something—or rather, she rented it back.

"Good morning, Mrs. Aldery," Ulyana said, in the tone of a woman selling candles. Because as far as any observer could tell, that's what this was: a candle shop. Beeswax tapers in the window, bundles of dried lavender hanging from the beam, little clay pots of salve labeled in a hand so boring it looked like a clerk's. The sign out front said *Candles & Sundries*, and the sign was not lying, exactly. It simply wasn't telling the whole truth, the way most signs don't.

"Good morning, dear." Mrs. Aldery settled onto the stool by the counter with the sigh of someone sitting down after carrying something heavy up many stairs. "You have it ready?"

"Wrapped and waiting."

Ulyana reached under the counter and brought out a small tin, the kind that once held throat lozenges, now holding a charm no bigger than a thumbnail: a sliver of amber with a thread of gold running through it like a vein. Inside the amber—if you knew how to look, and only two people in Vessing knew how to look—was a memory. Not a metaphor. An actual memory, lifted whole from a living mind and folded into resin until it kept, the way a fly keeps in amber, perfect and slightly terrible.

It was Mrs. Aldery's wedding day. Forty-one years ago, the fourth of June, the church of Saint Vessing-the-Lesser, rain on the windows that everyone pretended was lucky. Her husband Tomas alive and ridiculous in a borrowed coat that didn't fit across the shoulders. The taste of the cake. The way he'd stepped on her hem during the first dance and laughed instead of apologizing, and how she'd loved him more in that moment than in any of the forty years that followed, because laughing at your own mistakes is the whole secret of marriage and he'd known it without ever once saying it aloud.

Ulyana knew all of this because she had taken it from Mrs. Aldery herself, eleven months ago, three days after the funeral, when the widow had come in weeping and asked—not knowing what she was asking—for anything at all to make the hurting stop.

That was the ugly arithmetic of Ulyana's trade. She did not sell charms. She sold *relief*, and relief had to come from somewhere. Grief is heavy; a memory of joy is lighter than grief; so you lift the memory out, and the grief has less to hold onto, and the person can stand up straight again. And then—and this was the part that let Ulyana sleep at night, mostly, in a bed she'd learned to call sufficient—you fold the memory into amber and sell it back to them, so they can hold their own happiness in their palm on the bad days, warm it against their wrist, and feel it flicker.

They just can't quite remember *why* it feels warm.

"It's the good one," Mrs. Aldery said, taking the tin with both hands, the way you take communion or a baby bird. "The one where he laughs."

"The one where he laughs," Ulyana confirmed. "I checked the seal myself."

Mrs. Aldery pressed the amber to the inside of her wrist, below the glove, and went still. Her face did something complicated and private—the kind of expression Ulyana had spent twelve years learning not to watch too closely, because watching closely was how you started caring, and caring was how you ended up on the gallows with your name spelled correctly on the placard.

"There he is," Mrs. Aldery murmured. "There's my foolish boy." Then she blinked, twice, and put the tin away in her reticule, and her face smoothed over into the ordinary polite sorrow of a woman buying candles, and she asked whether Ulyana stocked the short tapers this week or only the dinner-length ones, and Ulyana said both, and they discussed wicks for four minutes with tremendous seriousness, because that was the ritual, that was the price of the arrangement: neither of them ever acknowledged what actually happened in this room.

She paid six coppers. She left. The cracked bell stammered her out.

Ulyana stood behind the counter for a moment, hands flat on the wood, and let herself feel the thing she felt every second Thursday, which was a feeling she had never found a better word for than *the bruise*. Not guilt, exactly. Guilt would have been simpler, and guilt could be confessed, and confession was a luxury. It was more like the ache of lending your coat to someone in winter and standing there in your shirtsleeves pretending you aren't cold, forever, by mutual agreement, because the alternative was admitting that the coat had been theirs all along.

*You're a thief,* she told herself, in the voice she used for such reminders, which was brisk and unimpressed. *A thief with excellent customer service. Now restock the lavender.*

* * *

Vessing did not allow magic. This was printed on the customs notices, preached from the pulpits, carved—Ulyana was fairly sure—into the lintel of the gallows itself, though she'd never gotten close enough to check, and intended to keep it that way. Magic meant the rope. Not prison, not exile, not a fine and a stern letter. Rope. The city had been burned once, a century back, by something that came out of the east wearing a crown, and Vessing had decided with the clarity of the scalded that the safest country was an empty one: no wonders, no wonders' cousins, no clever tricks that might turn out to be wonders wearing hats.

So the Ministry of Forgetting kept its offices in the old customs house by the river, gray-robed clerks who collected rumors the way other ministries collected taxes, and every few months somebody was led up the steps of the Gallows Gate while a herald read out the charges, which were always the same shape: *did practice arts forbidden, did traffic in memories not her own, did conceal the same.* And the crowd watched, because crowds watch, and afterward the baker did extra business, because executions make people hungry in a way they don't like to discuss.

Ulyana had survived twelve years in this city by being aggressively, exhaustingly ordinary. She rose at the same hour. She bought bread from the same baker, who thought her dull. She attended services at the same shrine, where she prayed with visible competence to gods she considered colleagues at best. She complained about the price of coal to the same neighbors, at the same volume, with the same mild frown. When the Ministry clerks came through Lowmarket on their rounds, checking licenses and listening for whispers, they walked past her shop without slowing, because Ulyana Zhou was a candle merchant, and candle merchants were furniture.

Invisibility, she had learned, was not the absence of attention. It was the *management* of it. You gave people exactly enough of yourself to fill their expectations, and you kept the rest folded up small, like letters in a drawer, like memories in amber.

Most days she believed this was wisdom. Some days—Thursdays, usually, around eleven o'clock, after Mrs. Aldery's stool had gone cold—she suspected it was just loneliness with good posture.

* * *

The afternoon brought the routine work. There was always routine work; it was the camouflage and the income both. Mr. Pell wanted his usual order of tapers and also, he mentioned hopefully, "something for the rheumatism," and Ulyana sold him salve that was just mutton fat and mustard seed and worked about as well as prayer, which is to say adequately, since half of medicine is being cared about by a shopkeeper who remembers your name. Two apprentices from the chandlers' guild came in to giggle at the lavender. A boy delivered a crate of beeswax and asked, with the directness of the very young, why her candles smelled like nothing, and Ulyana explained that fine candles were supposed to smell like nothing, and the boy nodded gravely, having learned his first lesson in adult dishonesty.

And then, at the hour when the light through the window went the color of weak tea, an actual customer. Old Sorrel, the night-soil man, who came monthly and always bought the same thing: a charm he called his "quiet one." He never said what it held. Ulyana never asked. Theirs was the purest transaction in the city—two professionals respecting each other's silence—and she treasured it accordingly.

Sorrel counted out his coppers, took his tin, and paused at the door.

"You hear about the fire?" he said. "Two streets over. Ash Lane."

"I heard smoke," Ulyana said carefully. "I haven't heard details."

"Mm." Sorrel turned his hat in his hands, which was what he did instead of talking. "Ministry men were there before the bucket brigade finished. Carrying things out in boxes. Little boxes." He set his hat back on. "Well. Good evening to you."

"Good evening, Sorrel."

He left, and Ulyana stood in her quiet shop and did the math she hated doing, which was the math of proximity. Ash Lane was two streets over. Ministry men, boxes, a fire—those three together had a shape, and the shape was *burning*, and burning was what Vessing did to magic, and magic was what Ulyana did all day, every day, in a shop with a cracked bell, two streets over.

She pulled the shutter closed early. She banked her stove low. She took the stock box from beneath the floorboard—her real stock, the amber, forty-odd slivers of other people's lives—and moved it to the cellar, which was damp and dark and had the advantage of being a place Ministry men rarely volunteered to go, Ministry men being famously fond of dry floors.

Then she sat upstairs with a single candle lit, drinking chicory that had gone bitter, and listened to the city outside.

Vessing at night sounds like a kettle deciding things. Carts, dogs, the river muttering under the bridges, drunks negotiating with lampposts. But tonight, underneath it, from the direction of Ash Lane, there was another sound. Faint. Rhythmic. A crowd noise, the particular low chant-hum of people gathered around something they're afraid to look at directly. And threaded through it, once, unmistakable even at distance, the sound Ulyana had dreaded for twelve years:

A woman's voice, screaming, cut off mid-note.

They say the fires are quick. They say the rope is quicker. Ulyana had no reason to believe either and every reason to know better. What she knew was that the Ministry burned slowly, thoroughly, and with paperwork, and that somewhere on Ash Lane a person was dying for the crime of holding something true inside their head, and that the difference between that person and Ulyana Zhou was approximately two streets and a great deal of caution.

She pinched out the candle. In the dark she sat with her hands over her sternum, where she kept—folded smaller than everything else, wrapped in three layers of forgetting, the one theft she had never committed and therefore the one thing no buyer could ever take from her—the memory of a morning, twelve years ago, with a man whose name she no longer allowed herself to say even in the privacy of her own skull. Dain. Even thinking it felt like spending coin. *Dain.* There. Spent. She'd think about him tomorrow, or never, whichever came first.

The chanting faded toward midnight. The city swallowed its evening the way it always did, completely, without residue, so that by morning Ash Lane would be a street with a scorch mark and no story, and the baker would sell twice as many rolls, and nobody would remember why.

Ulyana went to bed. She slept badly, which was normal, and dreamed of nothing, which was a skill.

* * *

She was up before dawn, as always, opening shutters, lighting the stove, arranging the window display with the fussy precision of a woman who had opinions about symmetry. The day promised to be ordinary. That was the whole design. Ordinary was armor.

At half past nine, the cracked bell rang.

Not the stammer of a regular. Something else—a hesitant ring, ring... ring, the sound of someone small working the handle with effort. Ulyana looked up from her ledger.

Standing in the doorway of the Charmwright's Rest was a child. Perhaps seven years old, perhaps eight, thin as a winter branch, barefoot on her threshold in a city in late autumn, in clothes that had been mended by someone who loved him and outgrown by someone faster. Dust on his feet. Dust, Ulyana noticed with a lurch she couldn't immediately explain, in a pattern that didn't look like road dust. It looked finer. Paler. Like ash, or like salt, or like—

Like something she had seen once, in a memory that wasn't hers, in a dead smuggler's head, twelve years ago, and had filed away as nonsense because nonsense was safer.

The child stood perfectly still. He did not cry. He did not beg. He looked at Ulyana with an expression she had never seen on a customer and rarely on a person: the absolute, unblinking attention of someone who has been looking for a very long time and has finally stopped looking.

And then he spoke.

The words were soft, unhurried, in a language with too many vowels and a grammar that seemed to bend around the meaning rather than carry it. Ulyana understood none of it. She understood, impossibly, almost all of it—a phrase here, a phrase there, rising in her not like translation but like *recognition*, the way a tune rises in you that you've never learned. Words older than kingdoms. Words that matched fragments in stolen heads: the dying smuggler on the salt road, babbling about a door; the mad priest in the debtor's cell, singing to a country that wasn't there.

The child said something that meant, as near as the broken pieces assembled themselves in her mind: *I was told you would keep it.*

Keep what? she wanted to ask. Keep what?

But the child had gone quiet again, and the Ministry's gray robes were two streets closer than they had been yesterday, and the fire on Ash Lane was still smoldering, and Ulyana Zhou—who had built a life out of never opening doors, out of keeping her head down and her ledger clean and her heart folded into a drawer—heard the cracked bell settle behind the boy and understood, with the particular dread of a careful woman watching her carefulness fail:

She was going to open this one anyway.
