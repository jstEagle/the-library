## 2. A Knock at the Hour of Ash

The trouble with living above your own shop, Ulyana Zhou had long ago decided, was that you could never fully leave work behind. The trouble with living below it was the stairs. She had chosen the stairs.

She was counting them now out of habit — fourteen, one of them loose, all of them creaky in a way she'd spent two years and a small fortune teaching them not to be — when the smell reached her. Woodsmoke was ordinary in Vessing in autumn; every chimney had its say. This was different. This had fat in it, and pitch, and under those something sweeter and worse that she refused to name even inside her own head, because naming things gave them weight, and weight was how the Ministry found you.

*Ash Lane,* she thought. *They're burning someone on Ash Lane.*

Two streets over. Close enough that the smoke would gray her windowsills by morning. Far enough, if she stayed very still and very small, that it might as well be happening in another country.

Ulyana was very good at other countries. She kept one folded up inside herself at all times, the way other women kept a handkerchief.

She lit no lamp. That was rule one, though rules one through nine were really all the same rule wearing different coats: *be nothing, want nothing, be seen wanting nothing.* She moved through her dark little rooms by memory, which was appropriate, since memory was her trade, and settled into the chair by the cold hearth with her ledger balanced on her knee. Not the real ledger. The real ledger lived inside her skull, filed among forty-odd stolen lives, cross-indexed and triple-shelved like the world's most dangerous library. The paper ledger was for the Ministry's benefit, should they ever come knocking: a dull, honest record of a dull, honest charm-seller's inventory. *Widow Maren Ostell, one (1) wedding-day charm, purchased gladly, paid in full.* Even the ink looked bored. She'd chosen the ink for that reason.

The wedding charm had gone out that afternoon, and here was the thing about the trade that nobody warned you about, the thing that made it theft even when everyone smiled at the end of it: Maren Ostell had come in weeping because her husband had been dead four years and she could no longer see his face. Grief had eaten it, the way grief does, starting with the mouth and working outward. And Ulyana had sat her down, made her tea, held her hands like a friend — *the tea helps, the hands help, this is not a lie* — and lifted the memory out as gently as lifting an egg from a nest.

Then she'd sold it back to her.

That was the whole trick, and it was a good trick, and some nights Ulyana could almost believe it wasn't monstrous. The widow got her husband's face back, bright as a struck match, sealed in a little glass bead she could hold to her ear like a shell. Ulyana got paid. Nobody died. Compared to what the Ministry did with magic — compared to whatever was happening right now on Ash Lane, to whoever had been careless or desperate or simply unlucky enough to be caught *being* something — selling a woman her own wedding twice was practically charity.

But she always took a copy. One thread of it, spun off before the sealing, kept for herself. Forty lives' worth of threads, coiled in the dark of her, and she told herself it was inventory insurance, quality control, professional habit. She told herself a lot of things. It was easier than telling herself the truth, which was that a woman who has made herself invisible will take company anywhere she can get it, even secondhand, even stolen, even in the dark with no lamp lit while a stranger burns two streets away.

From somewhere beyond the rooftops came a sound she knew too well: the crowd's roar, that awful many-throated *ah*, the sound Vessing made when it watched something die. Then the bells. All of them except hers — the cracked old bell of Lowmarket, silent these twelve years, which was half the reason she'd taken rooms here. A city that couldn't ring its own alarm was a city that had agreed, formally, to look away.

"Be small," she murmured to herself, to the room, to the smoke. "Be nothing."

The fire would burn until the small hours. The ash would fall like filthy snow. By morning the Ministry criers would read the name off the steps of the Assize Hall — *a practitioner of forbidden arts, justly condemned* — and the name would mean nothing to anyone, because that was rather the point of names on those steps. And Ulyana Zhou would open her shop at the usual hour, and sell a homesick dockhand a charm of his mother's kitchen, and be nothing, and be safe.

She was so busy being nothing that she almost didn't hear the knock.

Almost.

It came at the hour of ash — that dead stretch past midnight when the fires have burned down to their red bones and the whole city holds its breath between one day's sins and the next. Three knocks. Small ones. The kind of knocks made by a fist that didn't weigh much, delivered with more politeness than urgency, as though the knocker were sorry to intrude.

Ulyana went absolutely still, ledger sliding forgotten from her knee.

Rule ten: *no visitors after the bells.* Rule eleven: *especially not on burning nights, when anyone who knocks is either a trap or a catastrophe, and both wear the same face at first.*

She sat in her chair and performed stillness like a devotional act. The knock came again. Three more. Patient. Almost apologetic.

*Go away,* she said silently, with tremendous force, in the direction of the stairs. *Whoever you are, whatever you are, go away and be somebody else's catastrophe. I am a boring woman with a boring shop and a ledger full of nothing. There is nothing here worth knocking for.*

The third round of knocks was followed by silence, and the silence was somehow worse, because it had a quality she recognized from her work: the silence of someone waiting to be remembered. She'd heard it in dying men's voices. She'd heard it in her own, once, years ago, through a door very much like this one, on a night she did not keep in any ledger.

"Oh, hang it," Ulyana said aloud, to nobody, in the tone of a woman losing an argument with herself that had never had any suspense in it. "Hang it all."

She took the poker from beside the hearth — not because she expected to use it, but because holding something heavy makes cowardice feel like caution — and climbed her fourteen treacherous stairs, and put her eye to the peephole she'd drilled herself, the size of a peppercorn, angled so that she could see the step without ever being seen from it.

There was a child on her doorstep.

Barefoot. Small — seven, eight years old, hard to tell; children who've walked a long way all shrink to the same size. Hair the color of wet slate, cut badly, possibly by the child. Clothes that had been good once, somewhere else, somewhere where the buttons meant something. No coat. In this cold. And standing oddly, weight on the balls of the feet, the way you stand when the ground beneath you has never quite been trustworthy.

No lantern. No adult lurking at the gate — she checked, twice, the whole street empty as a drained cup. Just the boy, alone, in the falling ash, looking directly at the peppercorn hole as if he knew exactly where her eye was.

Which was impossible. But then, everything about him was arranged wrong, the way a room is wrong after a burglar has been careful. His stillness was too complete. Children fidgeted; it was practically their trade. This one stood like furniture. Like something *placed*.

Ulyana's hand was already on the latch.

She noticed this with genuine academic interest, the way you notice your hand moving toward a stove you know is hot. Some traitor part of her had skipped the entire debate and gone straight to opening. She made it stop. She made herself stand there in the dark of her own stairwell, poker in her fist, heart going like a moth in a jar, and count the reasons not to open the door.

One: burning nights brought informers out like moths, people who earned their bread reporting neighbors, and a strange child on a charm-seller's step was a story that wrote itself.

Two: children could be vessels. Everyone knew the stories. You didn't smuggle magic into a warded house in your own pocket; you sewed it into something innocent and knocked.

Three — and this was the true one, the one underneath, the load-bearing reason — three: she had built twelve years of safety on a single principle, and the principle was *never be the person who opens the door.* Doors were how the careful became the caught. Every story she'd ever collected from the frightened and the fleeing began the same way: *someone knocked, and I opened it, and I was sure, I was so sure—*

The child raised his hand and knocked a fourth time, softly, and then — and this was the moment, this was the exact moment her careful life tipped over — he spoke.

Not to her. To the door, maybe, or to the night, or to himself. Two words, low and clear, in a language that was not any language spoken within five hundred miles of Vessing.

And inside Ulyana Zhou, forty stolen lives turned over in their sleep.

It was not hearing, exactly. It was closer to what happens in a dark cellar when you strike one match: things that had been merely stored become suddenly, horribly *present*. A smuggler named Corvo, dying in her keeping with a gut wound and a secret, gasping words she'd assumed were delirium — *there*, that cadence, that rising turn at the end, like water finding its level. A mad priest she'd robbed in the Fettered Ward, who'd babbled in tongues for an hour and wept the whole time, and whose babble she'd sold cheap because she'd assumed it was worthless. It hadn't been worthless. It had been *this*. The priest's grief and the smuggler's urgency and the widow's wedding and the dockhand's mother's kitchen — all of it stirred, all of it leaned toward the sound of those two words like grass leaning toward sun, and Ulyana stood on her own stairs with her hand pressed flat against her sternum because something in there was *moving*, and things in there were not supposed to move.

The words meant something. That was the worst of it. She didn't know how she knew, but she knew, the way you know a face across a market crowd: the meaning was there, just past the reach of her mind's arm, and her mind's arm was suddenly, desperately short.

The child looked at the peppercorn hole again. He couldn't have seen it. Nobody could see it. He looked anyway, with the terrible patience of the placed thing, and then he said one more word — a longer one, softer, and this time the stirring in her chest had a shape, almost, almost, like—

Like a country. Like the idea of a country, green and specific and impossibly far, the way home feels in a dream you can't finish.

"No," Ulyana said out loud, to herself, firmly. "Absolutely not. Whatever you are, whatever this is, I am a shopkeeper. I sell beads. Beads, and tea, and polite conversation. Go knock on the Ministry's door, they're very fond of openings."

The child tilted his head. Ash settled on his bare shoulders and he didn't brush it off, and that — that small, stupid, human detail, a kid too tired or too frightened or too strange to brush ash off — undid her more efficiently than any magic could have. Magic she could resist. Magic she had resisted, professionally, for twelve years.

But she had also been eight years old once, barefoot somewhere she shouldn't have been, knocking on a door and hoping the person inside was kinder than the night. Somebody had opened. She had never learned to stop being grateful, and gratitude, she had discovered, is a debt that compounds.

"One minute," she heard herself say, in a voice she barely recognized — warm, resigned, already lost. "One minute, and if this is a trap, I want you to know that I will be extremely annoying about it. I'll scream. I'll cry. I'll make a scene the Ministry will write songs about. Are we understood?"

The child, naturally, did not answer. He only stepped back from the door — politely, giving her room — and waited.

Ulyana set down the poker. Picked it back up. Set it down again, finally, with the air of a woman signing something. Then she drew back the bolt — the first bolt, the real bolt, the one she'd oiled monthly for twelve years so that it would never, ever make noise — and opened her door onto the ash-fall and the cold and the small barefoot silence of him.

Cold poured in like a confession. The smell of the fire came with it, sweet and dreadful, and up close the boy smelled of woodsmoke too, and under it something older, something like a riverbed drying, like stone left in sun. His eyes were dark and unhurried and they looked at her face the way scholars look at texts: carefully, hungrily, checking something.

"You shouldn't be out," she said, uselessly, in Vessish, because terror and tenderness both default to the mother tongue. "Where's your — do you have anyone? Anyone at all?"

He considered her. Then he raised one small hand, palm up, in a gesture that was unmistakably a question, and asked her something in that impossible language — soft, urgent, the rising cadence, the water finding its level — and every stolen life in her chest turned over again, and this time one of them answered.

Not her. Something in her. Corvo's dying syllables reaching across twelve years to meet this child's living ones, matching, fitting, clicking together like the two halves of a seal — and through the click, for one vertiginous instant, Ulyana *understood*, the meaning arriving whole and unasked-for, the way a sneeze arrives:

*I was told you would keep it.*

Keep what? she wanted to ask. Keep what?

But the child had gone quiet again, and the Ministry's gray robes were two streets closer than they had been yesterday, and the fire on Ash Lane was still smoldering, and Ulyana Zhou — who had built a life out of never opening doors, out of keeping her head down and her ledger clean and her heart folded into a drawer — heard the cracked bell settle behind the boy and understood, with the particular dread of a careful woman watching her carefulness fail:

She was going to open this one anyway.

She stepped aside, and held the door wide, and the ash drifted in over her threshold like the first snow of a winter she already knew would change everything.
