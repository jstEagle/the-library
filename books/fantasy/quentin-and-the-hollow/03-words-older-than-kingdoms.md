## 3. Words Older Than Kingdoms

The boy slept in the good chair.

Ulyana had given him the good chair because he looked like a person who had never once been offered one, and because her own bed was a loft ladder she didn't trust herself to climb while distracted, and because — she admitted this only to the kettle — she liked watching someone else enjoy something she owned. It was a novel sensation. She'd have to be careful not to make a habit of generosity; it aged badly in Vessing, like milk or honesty.

He slept the way soldiers and cats sleep: lightly, with one ear working. Every time she moved toward the back room he surfaced, watched her with those grey eyes that were too old for his face, and sank again. By the third surfacing she gave up on stealth and made tea instead, loudly, so he could hear where she was.

"Right," she said, setting two cups down. He took his with both hands, which undid her slightly. "Here are the rules of my house. One: you don't touch the shelves. Two: you especially don't touch the *third* shelf. Three: if anyone knocks, you go in the cellar and you don't breathe until I come get you. Breathing is negotiable. Rule three is not."

He looked at her for a long moment. Then he said something.

It was four syllables, soft and round, and they went through Ulyana like a fishhook through silk.

She knew them.

Not the way you know a word — the way you know a smell from childhood, sudden and total and disorienting. The syllables arrived already loaded with meaning, meaning she had no business possessing, meaning that came attached to images: a rope bridge swaying over fog, salt bread broken and shared, a woman's hands stained blue to the wrist with some dye that had no name in any tongue Ulyana spoke on purpose.

"That's—" she said, and stopped, because what it *was* was impossible.

The boy tilted his head. Said it again, patiently, the way you repeat yourself for someone slow. Then he added two more syllables and pointed at the tea.

*Drink,* said the meaning, unbidden, in the back of her skull. *You've gone pale.*

"I have not gone pale," Ulyana told him, with dignity. "I am naturally the color of expensive paper."

He almost smiled. It was the first almost-smile, and she felt it land somewhere under her ribs and decided, firmly, not to think about that either.

* * *

There were eleven hundred and forty memories in Ulyana's stockroom. She knew the number the way a miser knows his coins, and for the same reason: each one had cost her something to take, and every one was a hanging offense if found. They lived in lacquered boxes on the third shelf, wrapped in waxed linen, tagged in her own cipher. Widows' weddings. Sailors' homecomings. A baker's memory of his mother's kitchen, sold to him twice by accident before she caught the error, which was the closest she'd ever come to a scandal.

She did not sell big memories. That was the rule beneath all her other rules. Big memories — first loves, dead children, whole vanished years — carried weight, and weight left tracks, and tracks brought the Ministry of Forgetting to your door with their polite little ledgers and their less polite instruments. Ulyana dealt in charms: small comforts, thin slices, an hour here, an afternoon there. Harmless as candied ginger. Forgettable as — well.

But now she sat on her stockroom floor at two in the morning with a lamp turned low and a sleeping child in the next room, pulling boxes down one by one, hunting a language.

Because here was the thing she could not stop turning over: the boy's words had *meant* things to her. Meaning doesn't travel. You can't catch comprehension off a stranger like a cold. Which meant the meaning was already inside her — filed away in some stolen corner of her head, in a memory she'd taken from someone who had spoken those syllables, or heard them, or died within earshot of them.

And there were only two candidates she could think of.

The smuggler had been dying when she found him, ten years ago, in the reed-shallows south of the Saltmarsh road — stabbed in a dispute over cargo, or possibly over a woman, or possibly, he'd insisted with his last breaths, over *nothing at all, nothing, that's the whole joke of it*. He'd been delirious, and delirious men talk, and Ulyana had been young enough then to think a dying man's memories were fair salvage. She'd lifted the lot: thirty years of running goods along coasts that weren't on any chart she'd ever seen. Most of it was ordinary — bribes, storms, a wife in three ports. But threaded through it, recurring like a fever, had been a word he used for a place he claimed he'd never been.

The priest had been different. Mad Father Oskell, defrocked, drunk, living in a belfry and preaching to pigeons about the country God had erased as punishment — no, no, not erased, the priest would correct himself, wagging a finger at the birds, *redeemed*, taken up out of the world like a stain lifted from cloth, praise be, praise be, and why did everyone look at him like that? Ulyana had bought him wine for a month to keep him talking, then taken everything, partly for curiosity and partly because a mad priest's memories were worthless on the market and she wanted to know what she was carrying. What she'd found was liturgy. Whole prayers in a language with no living speakers, chanted by congregations of faces she couldn't quite see, in streets that kept going grey at the edges whenever she tried to look straight at them.

She found the smuggler's box second from the bottom of the fourth row. Sat cross-legged with it in her lap like a cat, and pressed two fingers to the waxed linen, and let a thread of herself down into the borrowed dark.

The reed-shallows. The sting of the wound. And the voice, slurred with blood-loss, saying it again and again like a rosary:

*"Vessing's safe, Vessing's safe, nobody asks after Vessing — but the Hollow, girl, mind the Hollow, the Hollow is where the maps go to lie—"*

There it was. Four syllables. Soft and round. The same ones the boy had said over his tea.

Her hands were steady. That surprised her. Inside, something was doing a long slow fall, the way the floor does when a ship changes its mind beneath you.

She opened the priest's box next. The liturgy rose up around her, incense-thick, and this time she listened properly, syllable by syllable, matching them against what the child had said, against the shape of his mouth when he'd pointed at the cup. The grammar fit. The music of it fit — that rising lilt at the end of statements, as though every sentence were half a question, as though the whole language were unsure the world would agree to hold still long enough to be described.

The Hollow. The country that had never existed.

Everyone in Vessing knew the story, in the way everyone knows a thing without knowing how they know it: some centuries back, a kingdom called the Hollow had been proven — conclusively, officially, by royal decree and ministerial seal — never to have existed at all. There had never been such a country. The documents claiming otherwise were forgeries. The songs mentioning it were corruptions. Anyone insisting otherwise was confused at best and seditious at worst, and the Ministry of Forgetting maintained a standing offer to help with the confusion. It was, Ulyana had always assumed, one of those old political erasures — a rival dynasty scrubbed from the record, a rebellion un-happened. History was full of them. You didn't grieve for places that had never been. That was rather the point of never having been.

Except a mute child had walked out of the ash-fall tonight speaking its language like a native.

Except the language was alive in her own head, in two dead men's memories, stitched through with bridges and salt bread and blue-stained hands.

Except the priest's congregation had sung it, hundreds strong, in streets that went grey when you looked at them — and Ulyana had always assumed the greying was madness. Now, sitting on her stockroom floor with the lamp guttering, she understood with a lurch that it might have been something much worse than madness. It might have been accuracy.

* * *

She worked until dawn.

It was, she reflected somewhere around the fourth hour, the strangest inventory of her career: a woman burgling her own stockroom for a country. She laid the memories out on the floor in rows like a merchant laying out cloth, and walked among them, and began — tentatively, dreadfully — to count.

The smuggler: thirty years of coastal runs, and at least nine voyages whose destinations he'd recorded in that soft round script of syllables. Waypoints. Landmarks. A lighthouse that leaned. A harbor entered by singing a particular note so the tide-gates would know you.

The priest: forty years of liturgy and pilgrimage, and underneath the piety, geography. Processional routes. Feast days tied to solstices over specific hills. A door — the priest had fixated on a door, prayed to it, wept at it — a door in a hillside that opened for those who carried true memories, and Ulyana had assumed *that* was madness too, and was rapidly revising her assumptions about everything.

And then the others. This was the part that made her sit down on the cold floorboards and stay there a while.

Because once she started listening for it, the language was everywhere in her stock. Not whole — fragments, fossils, single bones of it. A fisherman's memory of his grandfather humming a work-song whose chorus wasn't in any coastal dialect. A noblewoman's recollection of a tapestry in her family's oldest hall, woven with a map that showed a coastline where the charts showed open sea — and the family legend, passed down in whispers, that the tapestry was *wrong on purpose*. A midwife who remembered delivering a baby whose grandmother "came from across the flats, from the place we don't say." Eleven hundred memories, and in nineteen of them — nineteen! — the Hollow leaked through like water through old mortar. Her clients had never noticed. Why would they? A hummed chorus, a wrong map, a place nobody says. Small things. Charms.

But strung together, end to end, the fragments stopped being fragments.

They became a route.

Ulyana stood in the middle of her floor with the lamp burning blue and saw it plainly, the way you see a face in a pattern of stains once someone points it out: the smuggler's leaning lighthouse gave the bearing; the fisherman's song named the flats beyond it; the tapestry drew the coast; the priest's processional roads ran inland from the shore, past hills with feast-day names, to a door in a hillside. Nine sources. Nineteen. A country reassembling itself out of the scraps she'd spent twelve years picking out of other people's heads.

Her inventory of stolen lives was a map. Had been a map all along. She'd been carrying the Hollow in lacquered boxes and selling it by the hour as wedding-day sweetness, and she had never once known.

The vertigo of it was physical. She put a hand flat on the floorboards and breathed.

Because there was a worse thought underneath, and it surfaced now, patient as a drowned man: *the Ministry burns people for less than this.* Not for owning the fragments — the fragments were invisible, deniable, scattered across a hundred lawful transactions. But for *assembling* them. For knowing. Knowledge was the whole crime; it had always been the whole crime. The widow two streets over hadn't burned for magic, whatever the criers said. She'd burned because the archivists found a memory in her house that referenced a thing that had never existed, and the penalty schedule for that was short and public.

And Ulyana had nineteen of them. Plus a child. Plus, she realized, going cold, a child whom the census had no record of — and she knew exactly how thorough the Ministry was about absences, because absence was their favorite subject.

She thought about the smuggler again, dying in the reeds over *nothing at all, that's the whole joke of it.* She'd assumed he'd meant the argument. Standing there among her rows of boxes, she wondered, for the first time in ten years, whether he'd meant himself. Whether he'd run cargo for a country that was being unwritten, and known it, and found it funny in the way doomed men find things funny, and whether the Ministry had found him before his wound did.

She got up. She blew out the lamp, because dawn was leaking grey through the shutters and lamplight at odd hours was the kind of detail neighbors remembered.

In the main room, the boy was awake, sitting in the good chair with his knees drawn up, watching the light come. When she came in he looked at her and said six syllables, gentle, questioning.

*Did you find it?* said the meaning, arriving uninvited in her skull. *Did you find our country?*

"Our country," Ulyana repeated. She crouched down to his level, which was getting to be a habit, and habits with children were dangerous. "Listen to me. I'm going to ask you questions, and you're going to answer however you can, and I'm going to understand more than either of us will like. All right?"

He nodded.

"Where is your door?"

He pointed. Not at the wall, not at the street. He pointed east and slightly north, his arm steady, his eyes certain, at a direction that meant nothing in Vessing and everything in a dead man's memory of a leaning lighthouse.

Ulyana looked at his finger, and then out the shuttered window toward the salt flats she couldn't see, and felt the wire begin to tighten around the whole small careful life she'd built.

Somewhere across the city, she had no doubt, the Ministry's archivists were already asking their quiet questions about a burned widow's neighbors. Somewhere a ledger was being opened, and a column was being ruled, and a space was being left blank for a name.

She had perhaps days. Possibly hours. Certainly not enough time to do what she suddenly, desperately needed to do: walk her stockroom floor again, box by box, and finish drawing the map of a country that had never existed — before the people who proved it never existed came to prove it to her.
