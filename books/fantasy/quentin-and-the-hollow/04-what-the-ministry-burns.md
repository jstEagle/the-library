## 4. What the Ministry Burns

The smoke came first, as it always did.

Ulyana smelled it before she heard the bells — a greasy, sweetish stink that she had learned, over twelve years in Vessing, to distinguish from chimney smoke and tannery smoke and the smoke of the fishmonger's cart when old Bessel fell asleep at his coals again. Witch-smoke had a particular note in it. Something about what they used to start the fire. She had never asked what. She did not want to know which of her neighbors' belongings burned hot enough to make that smell.

She was grinding dried marjoram into her charm-base when the bells began, and her hands went still on the mortar.

"Two streets over," said Maretta from the doorway, because Maretta always knew things before anyone else did, a talent Ulyana suspected involved more listening at windows than any honest woman would admit to. "The cooper's lane. They took the tanner's widow."

"The one who sells candles?"

"The very one. Said she was seen doing something unnatural with a flame." Maretta sniffed. "As if half the women in Lowmarket don't do unnatural things with flames every morning just getting a stove lit."

"And did she?"

Maretta gave her a look over the top of her shawl. "Did she *what*?"

"Do anything unnatural."

"How should I know? I sell buttons, Ulyana. Buttons and thread and an honest opinion now and then." But her eyes flicked, just once, toward the shelf behind the counter where Ulyana's charms sat in their neat rows of little glass vials, each one labeled in careful, boring handwriting: *For restful sleep. For courage at dentists. For forgetting the name of someone who doesn't deserve remembering.* All of them technically magic. All of them technically gallows-worthy. And all of them so small, so domestic, so thoroughly wrapped in the language of folk remedy that even the Ministry's inspectors had learned to sigh and move along. *Harmless charms,* the last inspector had written in his ledger. *Superstition, not sorcery.*

That was the art of it. That had always been the art of it. Make yourself so small, so dull, so precisely unremarkable that the machinery of Vessing slid past you like water around a stone.

"They'll want the crowd worked up first," Maretta went on. "They always do. Nothing burns well on a cold audience." She paused, and lowered her voice in a way that made Ulyana's stomach drop before the words even arrived. "There were soldiers asking after children this morning. Down by the census office. Asking whether anyone had taken in a stray."

"A stray."

"A child-shaped stray. Barefoot, they said. Not on any register." Maretta shrugged with elaborate carelessness. "I told them I hadn't seen anything but pigeons. Pigeons and you, being your usual tedious self."

"Maretta."

"Don't thank me, it makes it awkward." She was already leaving, pulling her shawl tighter. "Keep your shutters closed tonight, love. The whole street will be watching the fire, and the whole street will also be watching each other watching the fire. It's the worst possible night to be interesting."

The door clicked shut behind her.

Ulyana stood in her shop amid the smell of marjoram and candle-wax and, faintly, underneath everything, smoke, and listened to the silence at the back of the house. The specific silence of a cellar containing a child who was trying very hard not to breathe.

*Not on any register.*

She put down the mortar. She walked to the cellar door, opened it, and descended the stairs into the dark, where a small shape sat curled on her oldest blanket between jars of pickled plums and a barrel of rainwater, watching her with enormous dark eyes.

"I'm sorry," she said. "You have to come upstairs. Tonight of all nights, you can't be down here."

The boy — Tam, though he had never told her so; she'd read the name off his lips the second night, when he'd whispered it to himself like a prayer or a warning — tilted his head. He didn't understand Vessing-tongue yet. He understood tone, though. Children always understood tone; it was the first language anyone ever stole.

She held out her hand. After a moment, he took it.

His fingers were cold. His feet were still bare. Twelve days now, and no shoes in the city fit him, because his feet were oddly shaped, longer in the toe than any cobbler's last allowed for, and Ulyana had finally begun cutting down a pair of her own old slippers with scissors and stubbornness. She'd finished them that afternoon. She fetched them now, and knelt, and eased them onto him while he watched her with an expression she couldn't read — gratitude, maybe, or pity, or the patient amusement of someone who knew something about her that she didn't.

"There," she said. "Practically fashionable."

He touched the slipper. Then, carefully, deliberately, he touched her cheek — one finger, light as ash falling — and said something in that language.

That language.

It happened again, the way it had happened every time since the first night: the memories stirred. Not hers. Never hers. The ones in the jars, the ones folded into her charm-stock like letters in a drawer — they turned over in their sleep. A dying smuggler's voice, hoarse and salt-cracked, murmuring syllables that matched the boy's. A mad priest's fevered chanting, three years sealed in glass, suddenly awake and *rhyming*. She felt them press against the inside of their vials like moths against a window.

She had stolen hundreds of memories in her career. She had never once had them steal anything back.

"Not now," she told them, under her breath, and the boy watched her talk to the shelves, and something in his face suggested this was entirely normal where he came from, which was somehow worse.

* * *

By full dark, the crowd had gathered in the cooper's lane, and Ulyana stood among them with her hood up and her heart going like a trapped bird, because Maretta was right — the worst possible night to be interesting — and also because she could not stop herself from looking. She needed to see what the Ministry did. She needed to measure, precisely, how much rope the age allowed a woman who kept illegal magic in glass bottles and an unregistered child in her spare room.

They built the pyre in the square outside the tannery. The widow stood on it already, wrists bound, and she was not screaming, which frightened Ulyana more than screaming would have. Screaming meant panic. Silence meant the widow knew something the crowd didn't.

And then the inquisitor arrived, and Ulyana stopped breathing altogether.

She knew him. Of course she knew him. You did not spend six years as partners in the memory trade — six years of shared safehouses and split takings and one catastrophic, unspoken almost-something that had ended the night the Ministry cracked their ring and she ran and he didn't — without knowing Dain Marrow's walk, his shoulders, the way he held his head slightly tilted as if perpetually listening for a sound no one else could hear.

Except that the man walking toward the pyre was not quite Dain Marrow. This man wore the grey coat of the Ministry of Forgetting. This man's face had been tidied, somehow — smoothed at the edges, like a page copied too many times. And when his gaze swept the crowd, it passed over Ulyana without a single flicker. No recognition. No guilt. Nothing.

Twelve years ago, Dain Marrow had known her better than anyone alive. Now she was a stranger in a hood, and the looking-through-her was so complete, so professionally thorough, that she understood it had been done *to* him. Edited. Like one of her charms. The Ministry hadn't just caught him. They had kept him, and cleaned him, and pointed him at people like her.

The widow looked out over the crowd. Her eyes found Ulyana — found her, specifically, through two hundred faces, the way a compass finds north — and she smiled.

Ulyana's blood went to ice.

Because the widow's lips moved, and though no sound carried across the square, Ulyana read the shapes of the words easily enough, having spent years reading lips through keyholes and across market stalls:

*You still have some of mine.*

The flame went up. The crowd made its noise. Ulyana turned and walked home at exactly the pace of a bored, unremarkable woman, and did not run until she was behind her own door.

* * *

Ulyana did not sleep. She sat at her kitchen table with every lamp lit — a reckless extravagance, but darkness felt worse tonight — and laid out her stock like a general studying a losing battle.

Forty-one vials. Thirty-eight of them ordinary: borrowed wedding days, borrowed griefs, borrowed courage, all ground fine and diluted until they were barely more than suggestion. Safe. Boring. *Superstition, not sorcery.*

Three were not.

The smuggler's fragment — a strip of grey coastline, a door set into a cliff, words spoken in the boy's language. The mad priest's fragment — chanting, and beneath the chanting, weeping, and beneath the weeping, a name repeated like a heartbeat: *the Hollow, the Hollow, the Hollow.* And the third, acquired two years ago from a client who wanted to forget a lover and paid extra to forget fast — a fragment Ulyana had catalogued as *seaside holiday, corrupted, unusable*, because it showed a beach that resolved, the longer you held it, into a shoreline that belonged to no map she had ever seen.

Everyone who remembered the Hollow, the priest's memory insisted, died within the year. Or forgot. Or were forgotten. The priest himself had lasted eleven months after acquiring his obsession. The smuggler, Ulyana now realized with a slow, sick lurch, had died of something the healer called "wasting of unknown origin" seven weeks after she'd lifted the coastline from him.

And the widow with the candles had smiled at her through a crowd and mouthed *you still have some of mine,* and tomorrow morning there would be nothing left of her but smoke and a census line struck through.

"I'm sorry," Ulyana said to the empty kitchen. It was inadequate. It was the only thing she had. Her whole trade was built on the promise that stolen memories were harmless once diluted — that she sold comfort, not consequence. She had told herself that story for twelve years, and it had been a good story, warm as a coat, and tonight the seams were coming apart in her hands.

Above her, a floorboard creaked. Tam, sleepless too, pacing his little room like a small caged animal that had decided, provisionally, that the cage was kindly.

She climbed the stairs and found him at the window, watching the glow over the rooftops where the fire was burning itself out. When she joined him, he pointed at the glow, then at her, then pressed his small hand flat against his own chest, and spoke — softly, clearly, in that language that made her jars whisper in the dark.

She didn't understand the words. She understood the grammar of the gesture perfectly: *they will do this to you too.*

"No," she said, and then, because lying to children is a sin she'd rather not add to her ledger: "Not if I'm careful. And I am *extremely* careful. I'm so careful it's practically a personality."

He considered this. Then he reached up and took her hand, and turned it palm-up, and traced something in the skin with one finger: a shape. A rectangle. A vertical line down the middle.

A door.

The same door from the smuggler's coastline. She was certain of it — certain the way you're certain of a face in a crowd, instantly and without evidence. The boy drew it again, slower, insistent, and then pointed at the floor. At the *ground*. At somewhere far beneath the cobbles and the cellars and the bones of Vessing.

"You want to go home," Ulyana said quietly.

Tam shook his head. He pointed at her chest this time, at the place below her collarbone where a person keeps the thing they're most afraid of, and said one word. Just one. And though she didn't know the language, the word landed in her like a stone dropped down a well, and she heard — impossibly, absurdly, from three years away in a sealed jar downstairs — the mad priest's voice echo it back:

*Home.*

Downstairs, in the dark, every vial on her shelves hummed at once, softly, like a congregation settling into pews.

Ulyana stood very still in the firelight from the window, holding a child's hand, listening to her illegal inventory sing, and understood with perfect clarity that her life as she had built it — small, dull, invisible, safe — was over. The only question left was what she would build from the wreckage, and how long she had before the Ministry's grey coats came up her stairs asking after pigeons.

Somewhere across the city, a bell tolled the hour of ash. Tam leaned his head against her arm, heavy with exhausted trust, and slept.

Ulyana did not sleep. She counted her dangers instead, like coins: a dead widow's accusation. Three vials of a country that didn't exist. A child-shaped hole in the census, sleeping in her spare room. And an inquisitor with a familiar walk and an edited mind, who had looked straight through her tonight — but ledgers, she knew, were patient things, and men who kept them eventually read every line.

Two streets over, the last of the fire collapsed into orange embers, and the wind shifted, and brought the smell of it through the window cracks: sweetish, greasy, final.

Ulyana closed the shutters, and began, in her head, to make a list of everyone she had ever robbed.
