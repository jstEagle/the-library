## 5. The Partner Who Forgot Me

The bell above the door of Whitlock's Curiosities had a crack in it, so that every customer announcement came out as a slightly wounded *ding-dong*, like a bird with a limp. Ulyana had meant to fix it for eleven years. She hadn't, because a cracked bell was a warning system and a disguise at once: it made her shop sound shabby, and shabby shops did not get robbed, and more importantly they did not get *noticed*.

On the morning Inquisitor Dain Marrow pushed the door open and set the bell limping, Ulyana was behind her counter sorting charms into little velvet bags, and she felt the particular cold that starts in the sternum and works outward, the way frost climbs a window.

She knew him instantly. That was the joke of it — the whole cruel architecture of it. He did not know her at all.

"Inquisitor," she said, before he could speak, because greeting a Ministry man first was a small courtesy that sometimes bought you three extra seconds of being treated as a person rather than a problem. "We sell charms. Love knots, luck buttons, memory tinctures — the harmless kind, I assure you. Nothing that would interest the Ministry."

"Everything interests the Ministry," Dain Marrow said mildly. "That's rather our trouble."

He was taller than she remembered, or perhaps she had simply been younger, and everything had been larger then — the streets, the risks, the man himself. His hair had gone grey at the temples in a way that looked deliberate, like he'd ordered it from a catalog. He wore the dove-grey coat of the Ministry of Forgetting, which was cut well enough to be intimidating and plain enough to pretend it wasn't. And his eyes — this was the part that made her want to put both hands flat on the counter to steady herself — his eyes moved over her face the way a man reads a sign in a language he almost knows. Searching. Not finding. Not knowing what he was searching for.

"Tea?" she said, because her mouth had gone ahead of her judgment, as mouths do.

"That would be kind." He settled onto the stool by the counter, unhurried, coat arranged just so. An inquisitor who accepted tea was either harmless or very good at seeming so, and Ulyana had met enough Ministry men to know the second category always traveled in pairs, and there was no one waiting outside. Which meant either he truly was alone, or he was confident enough not to need backup, and confidence in an inquisitor was worse than numbers.

She poured tea into two mismatched cups — hers chipped, his the least chipped of the display stock — and told herself the tremor in her hand was the cold morning.

"I'm conducting inquiries," he said. "Routine ones. There have been irregularities in Lowmarket."

"Lowmarket is mostly irregularities," she said. "It's why people live here. The rents are low and nobody asks what you're recovering from."

A flicker at the corner of his mouth. Almost a smile. She knew that smile; she'd spent years earning it, back when smiles between them were currency they minted themselves, unbacked by any government. Now it surfaced on his face like something washed up on a beach, and he looked at it with faint puzzlement, as if wondering where it had come from.

"You've been here long?" he asked.

"Eleven years."

"And before?"

"Before," she said, "I was elsewhere."

He waited. She sipped her tea. The silence stretched, comfortable on her side and professionally patient on his, and she understood suddenly that this was the game: he was going to ask gentle questions all afternoon, questions soft as wool, and every one of them would be a hook dressed as courtesy. The Ministry had learned long ago that you caught more thieves with kindness than with irons. Irons made people lie beautifully. Kindness made them relax.

"What's your name?" he asked, pen already hovering over a little ledger.

"Liora Vess," she said, giving the name she'd worn so long it had grown roots. "Like the city. My parents were sentimental."

"And you run this shop alone?"

"Alone as the moon."

"No family?"

"A brother in Corvane who writes twice a year and owes me money."

Dain wrote it down. His handwriting was neat, upright, bureaucratic — nothing like the slashing scrawl she remembered, the handwriting of a man who wrote fast because he lived fast, back when they'd split takeout on a rooftop and he'd drafted their next job on the back of a wine label. She watched the pen move and felt a grief so specific it had an address: *he used to write my name like it was a signature on something worth keeping.*

* * *

He asked about charms. Of course he asked about charms. It was the natural question for a shop like hers, and also the only dangerous one, and he asked it gently, leaning forward, interested, a scholar rather than a hunter.

"Memory work," he said. "I understand some charm-sellers deal in it. Little tinctures. A widow buys back the feeling of her wedding day, that sort of thing."

"That sort of thing," Ulyana agreed. "Though I'll confess most of what I sell is theater. Rose oil and a good story. People don't want their memories back, Inquisitor — they want ten minutes of believing someone might want them again. I charge four coppers and let them cry in private. It's honest work, dishonestly labeled."

"That's a remarkably candid answer."

"I find candor disarms people. Also I have nothing to hide." This was a lie delivered with such warmth that she almost believed it herself, and she saw him note it — *candid, warm, no visible fear* — and she thought: *good, note that, note everything except the cellar hatch under the rug.*

Because Tam was down there. Of course Tam was down there. She'd left the boy with bread, a candle stub, and instructions written in three languages, one of which was a language that officially did not exist, and she had spent the entire conversation so far with one ear angled toward the floorboards, listening for thumps, for weeping, for the sound of a child deciding that hiding was boring and doors were interesting.

"The irregularities," Dain said, stirring his tea though it needed no stirring, "concern provenance. Certain charms circulating in this quarter carry impressions that shouldn't exist. Places that were never places. Names that were never names. You'd know nothing of that, naturally."

"Naturally."

"Naturally," he repeated, and there was something in the repetition — a softness, an echo — that snagged on her like a burr. As if the word tasted familiar to him. As if somewhere under the grey coat and the ledger and the twelve years of professional editing, a man who had once been a thief was hearing himself say a word he used to say across card tables while palming coins.

Ulyana set down her cup very slowly.

"May I ask you something, Inquisitor? Impertinent, but the morning's young."

"Impertinence is the tax on tea," he said. "Pay it freely."

"Were you ever in Lowmarket before? Before the Ministry, I mean. Did you ever — I don't know — frequent shops like this one?"

He considered the question with the visible care of a man checking a locked drawer for tripwires. "I don't believe so," he said finally. "My records begin twelve years ago. Before that, there's a gap. Recruitment, they call it. Most of us have them."

*A gap.* Twelve years ago. To the season. Ulyana kept her face pleasant and her breathing even and thought, with a clarity that felt like falling: *they didn't recruit him. They harvested him. They took the years that contained me and filed them under removed.*

She had known this, in theory. She had known since the first time she'd seen him across a market square three years back, in uniform, and he had looked through her the way one looks through weather. But knowing a wound in theory and sitting across a counter watching it breathe are different economies entirely.

"What was your gap like?" she asked, and her voice came out gentler than she intended, gentler than strategy allowed.

"My gap?" He smiled — the beachcombed smile again. "Empty. Everyone says so. Like waking from a nap without dreams. One moment I was a person with a past; the next I was a person with a *file*. Honestly, it doesn't hurt. That's the strange part. You can't miss what isn't missing. It's not a hole. It's smooth."

"Smooth," she said.

"Seamless, really. Very fine work. Whoever does the edits, they're artists." He said it with genuine admiration, the admiration of a craftsman for craft, and Ulyana wanted to turn the counter over. Because she knew the artists too. She knew exactly how seamless it could be. She had sold seams herself, once upon a time, before she'd understood that a seam is just a scar that learned manners.

* * *

He stayed an hour. Maybe more. He asked about her suppliers (wholesalers in Corvane, mostly fictional), her customers (widows, romantics, one magistrate she declined to name), her views on the new licensing laws (supportive, in principle, opposed to the fees). He never raised his voice. He never threatened. Twice he laughed — actually laughed, a short surprised sound, once at her impression of the Corvane wholesaler ("he says 'quality' the way other men say 'help'") — and each time he laughed he looked briefly startled by it, a man discovering a room in his own house he didn't know he had.

And through all of it, Ulyana performed the hardest work of her life, which was to sit across from the love of her life and chat with him about tea.

There were moments. God help her, there were moments. When he reached for the honey and she passed it before he finished asking — a reflex, twelve years old, still living in her hands — and he paused with the honey jar halfway to his cup and frowned at it, at her, at the air between them, the way one frowns at a word that's been on the tip of the tongue all day. When he said "you remind me of someone" and then couldn't finish the sentence, and she said "someone from the file?" and he said "no, the file has no someones," and the look on his face was the look of a man standing at a window painted shut, aware of weather he cannot feel.

When he stood to leave, he did something small and fatal. He picked up her chipped cup — the one she'd been drinking from — to move it aside from his ledger, and he held it a half-second longer than cups require, turning it, finding the chip with his thumb. And Ulyana watched his thumb find the chip and press it, once, the way you'd press a bruise to confirm it's yours, and she thought: *he doesn't know why that cup matters. He doesn't know that he bought it. That we bought it, at a stall on Foundry Lane, because it was cracked and therefore cheap and therefore ours, because we were young and everything cracked was funny to us then.*

"Thank you for the tea, Ms. Vess," he said, returning to his coat like a man putting away a holiday.

"Come again," she said. "The honey keeps."

"I may. Routine inquiries can take weeks." At the door he paused, hand on the frame, and looked back at her — a long look, unprofessional in its length, hungry in a way he clearly didn't have vocabulary for. "Have we met? Before today. Don't answer — it's a foolish question. Everyone says everyone looks familiar. It's the curse of the work."

"The curse of the work," she agreed.

The bell limped behind him. The street took him. And Ulyana stood in the quiet with two cooling cups and the specific, exquisite agony of a woman who has just spent an hour being interviewed by the ghost of her own heart.

* * *

She went down to the cellar as soon as the street was empty. Tam sat on the flour sacks with the candle stub, drawing in the dust with one finger — spirals, mostly, and something angular that might have been writing or might have been a fence. He looked up when she came down the stairs, and his face did the thing it always did now, the thing that was undoing her: relief, immediate and total, as if she were weather he'd been waiting out.

"He's gone," she said. "The grey coat. He's gone."

Tam studied her. Then he pointed at her face, and then at his own, and drew a line down his cheek with one finger. *You were crying,* he said, in the language that didn't exist, and then, patiently, in gesture, because he was learning her limits: *why.*

"I wasn't crying," she said, wiping her cheek with her wrist and discovering he was right. "Allergies. Dust. Old cellars are full of history and it gets everywhere."

He gave her the flat, unconvinced look that children have been giving adults since the invention of lying, and went back to his spirals.

Ulyana sat down on the bottom step. Above her, muffled by floorboards, the cracked bell dinged its lame ding-dong at a customer, and her voice floated down automatically, warm and steady, selling rose oil and good stories. Below, in the dark, a boy from a country that had been traded away like a debt drew spirals in flour dust, and hummed — she noticed it now, she'd been noticing it for days — a tune with six notes in it that she knew, somehow, the way you know a staircase in the dark.

Twelve years ago, she and Dain Marrow had made each other promises in a language with no Ministry in it. Then the Ministry had edited him smooth as lake ice, and she had run, and hidden, and called it safety, and built a small life with a cracked bell, and told herself the ache in it was just the shape of prudence.

Now the ache had a grey coat and excellent manners and drank tea with honey, and would be back, because routine inquiries take weeks.

And downstairs, a mute child hummed a lullaby from a country that officially had never existed — and Ulyana realized, with a lurch like a stair missing in the dark, that she recognized the melody from a memory she had stolen years ago. From a dying smuggler. Who had learned it in a place with a door.

She had an inventory full of ghosts, and one of them, apparently, kept a map.
