## 6. Inventory of Ghosts

The cellar smelled of dust and old paper and, faintly, of the tallow candles Ulyana had stopped lighting down here after the third time she'd nearly set her entire livelihood alight. She worked by lamplight instead, which was its own kind of stubbornness: a thief who feared fire but not the Ministry, hoarding her little glass vials in the dark like a dragon with poor eyesight.

Tam sat on an upturned crate beside her, knees drawn up, watching her work with the grave attention of a boy who had decided that whatever this strange woman did with small bottles was Very Important Business. He hadn't spoken since dawn—not that he spoke much at all—but he'd eaten three eggs and half a loaf, which Ulyana chose to interpret as trust.

"Right," she said, mostly to herself. "Inventory."

Her charm-stock lived in rows of labeled jars and boxes, each memory sealed in glass the way a beekeeper seals honey: carefully, and with respect for what it could do if mishandled. She'd built the collection over twelve years, one careful theft at a time. A widow's wedding morning. A fisherman's last sight of his son before the sea took him—no, she didn't sell that kind; she kept those, out of some superstition she'd never examined too closely. A baker's pride in his first risen loaf. A soldier's memory of home, which was really just a memory of bread, because soldiers mostly remembered bread.

She pulled each jar into the lamplight, held it up, and listened.

That was the trick nobody taught you in the trade: memories had sounds. Held near your ear, a good charm hummed—a low, warm note like a struck bell wrapped in wool. A false one, a fabrication, a thing stitched together from other people's scraps, buzzed. It had taken her years to learn the difference, and longer to learn why it mattered. True things rang. Lies rattled.

Most of her stock rang honestly. The widow's wedding. The baker's loaf. A child's first snowfall, sold three times over by now, still ringing sweet as a coin dropped in a well.

But four jars did something else.

Four jars, when she lifted them, went quiet—and then, underneath the quiet, they *pulled*. Not toward her ear. Toward some direction that wasn't any direction, the way a compass needle pulls north except this needle pulled somewhere that had never been on any map she'd ever seen.

She set them apart on the crate: four small vials, their labels written in her own younger handwriting.

*Smuggler — last words.*
*Priest, mad — the sermon under the hill.*
*Sailor — the coast that isn't there.*
*Old woman, Lowmarket — her grandmother's stories.*

She remembered acquiring each one. That was the trouble with being good at your trade: you remembered everything, because remembering was the whole point of you.

The smuggler had been dying in an alley off Tanner's Row, gut-stabbed over a cargo dispute or a woman or both; men like him rarely distinguished. He'd grabbed her wrist with surprising strength and said, in a language she hadn't recognized, "Take it, take all of it, someone has to keep it," and she'd taken his last hour because a dying man's wish is the closest thing to a contract that can't be broken. His final memory had been of a coastline at dusk, and a door standing alone in a hillside, and words spoken in that same unplaceable tongue.

The mad priest had preached, in his final lucid year, about a country that God had sold. She'd stolen the sermon thinking it would be worth money to collectors of the macabre. It had sat unsold ever since, ringing so strangely that buyers flinched from it.

The sailor and the old woman had been smaller hauls—fragments, edges, a word here, a place-name there. She'd filed them under *curiosities* and forgotten them, the way you forget a splinter until it finds a new way to hurt.

Now she held all four up to the lamp, and the pull in them aligned, all four needles swinging to the same impossible north, and Ulyana felt the cellar floor tilt very slightly beneath her feet.

"They're all the same place," she said aloud.

Tam looked up.

"The smuggler's coast. The priest's country. The sailor's coast-that-isn't." She turned a vial slowly, watching the lamplight swim in the memory inside like a fish in amber. "Different people. Different years. Same door."

Tam slid off the crate. He didn't touch the jars—he seemed to understand, in the way children understand glass, that these were not for him—but he leaned close and breathed on one, fogging it briefly, and inside the fogged glass the memory *shivered*, like a sleeper disturbed.

Ulyana's skin prickled. "Don't do that."

He did it again, deliberately, watching her.

"You're going to be a menace," she told him, and found, to her surprise, that she was smiling. Dread and tenderness, sharing a face. That was going to be the shape of her life now, apparently.

* * *

It was past midnight when she did the arithmetic, and the arithmetic was the worst part.

Because here was the thing she'd been avoiding since the night the child arrived: she knew what happened to people who remembered the Hollow. She'd been keeping the records herself, in her own ledger, without knowing she was keeping them. Every client who'd bought a fragment. Every seller she'd lifted from. She cross-referenced her sales book against her memory of the city—the city's obituaries, its empty chairs, its sudden silences—and the pattern assembled itself with the terrible neatness of a well-made lie.

The smuggler: dead within the year. Well, he'd been dying anyway. But the priest—dead within eight months, quietly, in his sleep, though the parish swore he'd been mending. The sailor: drowned, though he'd retired from the sea two years prior and hadn't gone near open water since. They said he'd walked into the harbor at night, calm as Sunday, fully clothed.

And the old woman from Lowmarket—Ulyana closed her eyes and reached for her name and found—

Nothing.

Not a blank, exactly. Blanks have edges. This was worse: a smoothness, like river stone, where a name used to be. She remembered stealing from her. She remembered the transaction. She remembered the jar. But the woman herself had been sanded away, and when Ulyana checked her sales book, the entry read only *old woman, Lowmarket*, because apparently even twelve-years-ago Ulyana had failed to write the name down, and no one else had ever known it, and so the name was simply—gone. Unmourned, because there was nothing left to mourn with.

Ulyana sat back on her stool and let the cold walk up her spine one vertebra at a time.

Everyone who remembered the Hollow died or forgot within a year. And she had spent the last week handling fragments of it daily. She thought of the smuggler's last words—*someone has to keep it*—and wondered, with a humorlessness that surprised her, whether the old man had known exactly what he was asking. Whether *keep it* had always meant *die holding it*.

"Well," she said to the cellar at large. "That's fine. That's completely fine."

From the crate, Tam made a small sound. It might have been skepticism. Children learned it early, even mute ones.

"I'm being brave," she informed him. "Bravery and denial look identical from the outside. Ask anyone."

* * *

Dawn came grey through the cellar grate, and with it Tam's habit of drawing.

He drew everywhere—on scrap paper, on steam-fogged windows, once, memorably, on the side of Ulyana's cat, who had tolerated it with the weary dignity of a creature who had seen everything. Most of his drawings were ordinary: birds, boats, a dog with too many legs. But this morning, while Ulyana boiled water upstairs and tried not to think about sailors walking calmly into harbors, Tam crouched by the cold hearth and drew in the ash-dust with one deliberate finger.

Ulyana came down with the teapot and stopped on the stairs.

A rectangle. Tall, narrow, drawn with the confidence of someone copying from life rather than imagination. An arch above it. A lintel with markings—not letters, exactly, more like knots, the way you might draw rope if rope were trying to say something. And around the door, radiating outward in careful lines, grass. Long grass, wind-bent, all leaning the same way.

She set the teapot down very slowly, because her hands had developed opinions.

"That's the smuggler's door," she said.

Tam looked up at her, ash to his elbows, and nodded once, businesslike, as if to say: *obviously*. Then he pointed at the door in the dust, and then at himself, and then—he hesitated, and his finger drifted, and landed on her.

"No," Ulyana said. "No, no. I'm not from anywhere. I'm from Vessing, which is barely from anywhere itself. I'm from a rented room and a bad knee and a stock of other people's Tuesdays."

Tam's expression did not change. It was the expression of a boy who had heard many adults say many untrue things and had developed a policy of waiting them out.

She crouched beside the drawing despite herself. Up close, the knot-marks on the lintel stirred something in her—not a memory, nothing so clean, but a resonance, the way a tuning fork answers a note played across the room. The priest's sermon. The smuggler's last hour. Somewhere in her stolen inventory, those marks had been described, and some part of her had memorized them without permission.

"He stood in front of it," she murmured, dredging the smuggler's memory up carefully, the way you lift a sleeping animal. "In the last hour. Grass to his waist. He was crying, and he wasn't allowed to go in. There was someone on the other side—or the idea of someone—and the door wouldn't—" She frowned. "No. That's not right. The door *would*. It always would. It was *he* who couldn't. You had to carry something true to get through, and he'd arrived empty-handed. He'd come back to a country that had already been signed away, and the door knew, and it stood there being a door at a man who could no longer be let in."

Tam touched the ash-drawing's threshold, gently, the way you'd touch a wound.

"And you came through it," Ulyana said. "You're the opposite. You got out." She looked at the boy—at the barefoot, silent, impossible boy eating her eggs and fogging her inventory—and the question she'd been circling for days finally landed. "Why? Why leave, if you could stay?"

Tam considered her for a long moment. Then he spoke—one of his rare sentences, in that old tongue that made her stolen memories sit up like dogs hearing a whistle—and though she understood perhaps a third of it, the third she understood was enough:

*The others are forgetting. Someone has to carry it out.*

Someone has to keep it. The smuggler's dying words, twelve years ago, in another man's mouth, in another alley. Ulyana felt the two sentences click together like a lock accepting its key, and the sound of it was awful.

"It's not a country anymore," she said quietly. "It's a relay. Everyone who remembers becomes a runner, passing it hand to hand until the Ministry catches them or the forgetting does. And now it's caught you"—she pointed at Tam—"and me." Her laugh came out crooked. "I've spent twelve years making myself invisible. Do you know how insulting it is to be found by a *country*?"

Tam patted her hand. It should have been condescending. Somehow it wasn't.

* * *

By full light, she'd made her decision, and the decision frightened her precisely because it felt calm.

She spread her ledger open on the kitchen table and began, methodically, to list every client and contact whose purchased charms might contain Hollow fragments. The widow—yes, the widow, the one whose wedding she'd lifted and sold back twice; there had been something in the grandmother's stories the widow had inherited, a lullaby with wrong-shaped words in it, and Ulyana had taken that too, filed it under curiosities, and the widow was still alive, still buying charms, still—

Still alive. Which meant, by the pattern, she was running out of time.

Ulyana stared at the entry. *Widow, Bellows Lane. Wedding, twice. Grandmother's songs.* And beneath it, in the column where she noted debts and favors, an older notation in faded ink: *owes me nothing; I owe her the truth about the second sale.*

Guilt was a funny thing. It slept for years under invoices and routine, and then a mute child drew a door in your hearth-ash and it woke up starving.

"She'll think I'm mad," Ulyana said. "Or worse, she'll remember the second sale, and then I'll have to apologize, and I am *terrible* at apologizing. I once apologized to a fence for robbing him and he charged me for the apology."

Tam, packing ash-dust drawings into his memory with the seriousness of a scholar, gave her a look that clearly said: *go anyway.*

"Yes, thank you, I'd gathered." She closed the ledger, and her hand rested on its cover a moment longer than necessary. Because here was the other thing, the thing under the thing: if everyone who remembered the Hollow was dying or dissolving, then her shop—her careful, invisible, twelve-years-in-the-making life—was sitting on top of a fault line, and the ground had already started humming. The Ministry burned witches two streets away. Inquisitors asked gentle questions. And somewhere in her cellar, four small vials pulled toward a door that wanted carrying.

Find the door before the forgetting finds us. It wasn't a plan. Plans had steps. This had only a direction—which, she supposed, put it ahead of most of her life's decisions.

She fetched her coat, and her purse, and the smallest of the four vials, tucked where a knife would be if she were the sort of person who carried knives instead of other people's mornings.

"Bellows Lane," she told Tam. "We're going to see a widow about a lullaby. Try to look harmless."

He looked at her.

"Fine. Be yourself."

She locked the door behind them, and did not look back at the cellar, where four small lights waited in the dark, pulling, patiently, toward a country that insisted it had never existed.
