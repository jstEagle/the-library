## 7. The Widow Who Sold Her Wedding Twice

Bellows Lane smelled of tallow and rain that hadn't fallen yet, the particular grey smell of Vessing in the hour before evening, when the lamps were lit by men who walked fast and looked at no one. Ulyana had always liked this street for its honesty. Nothing on Bellows Lane pretended to be anything other than what it was: narrow, soot-stained, one step from respectable and three steps from ruin.

The widow's house was the yellow one with the crooked shutter, third from the corner, where a cat of no fixed allegiance sat on the step like a landlord waiting for rent.

"Wait here," Ulyana told Tam. "Lean against the wall. Look like a boy who is bored, not a boy who is impossible."

He leaned against the wall with the gravity of a small monument. She was beginning to suspect he did this on purpose.

She climbed the two steps and knocked — the little rhythm she used with clients, three quick and one slow, so they'd know it was her and not the Ministry, because the Ministry never knocked in rhythms. The Ministry knocked once, heavily, the way a stone knocks when it falls on you.

Footsteps inside. A pause too long at the door.

Then it opened a hand's width, and Maren Ostrey's face appeared in the gap, older than Ulyana remembered and paler than any face ought to be in lamplight.

"You," said the widow.

"Good evening to you too, Mistress Ostrey. I was in the—"

"I know why you're here." The door opened another inch, which Ulyana noted with professional interest was not an invitation but an ambush of some kind. "You'd better come in before someone sees you standing there looking like *that*."

"Like what?"

"Like a woman who knows things."

Inside, the house was tidy in the way of houses where tidiness has replaced living. Maren cleared a chair of nothing in particular and gestured Ulyana into it, then stood by the cold hearth with her arms folded, as if guarding the ashes from theft.

Ulyana set her purse down carefully. "Maren. I sold you a charm eight months ago. A wedding memory. You came back twice after that, and I told you both times—"

"I know what you told me." Maren's voice cracked along some old seam. "I'm not here about the wedding."

"No?"

"The wedding was lovely, by the way. Second-best purchase of my life." Her mouth did something that wanted to be a smile and got lost on the way. "It's the other thing. The thing that came *with* it."

Ulyana went very still, the way she did when a memory stirred unbidden — except this time it wasn't a memory stirring. It was arithmetic.

"When you lifted the wedding," Maren said, "you took more than the wedding. There was something underneath it. Something old. I didn't notice at first — it just felt like a warm corner in my head, a place I liked to sit. And then—" She stopped. Her hands were shaking, and she looked at them as if they belonged to a stranger who owed her money.

"Maren. Slowly."

"And then the archivists came."

The word dropped into the room like a coin into a well. Ulyana heard it hit bottom.

"Archivists," she repeated.

"That's what they called themselves. Grey coats. Very polite. The tall one had ink on his fingers, actual ink, like a man who writes all day and never sleeps. They said they were conducting a survey of *folk recollections of regional heritage*." Maren laughed, and it was the worst sound Ulyana had heard all week, and she had spent the week listening to Tam speak a dead language in his sleep. "Regional heritage. In Vessing. Where the only heritage anyone's allowed is the price of coal."

"What did they ask you?"

"They asked me about a song." Maren turned to face her fully now, and Ulyana saw that the widow's eyes had the flat shine of a woman who has been crying in a house where crying leaves stains. "A lullaby. They hummed it to me — can you imagine? Two grown men from the Ministry of Forgetting, humming a lullaby in my parlor like a pair of demented uncles — and they asked if I'd ever heard it before. And I said no. Because I hadn't. Not that I knew."

"But you had."

"It was in the warm corner." Maren pressed her knuckles to her mouth for a moment. "When they hummed it, the corner opened. Ulyana, I sang it. I sang the whole thing, every verse, in a language I have never learned, standing in my own parlor, while the tall one wrote it down like he was milking me. And then they thanked me very kindly, gave me a receipt — a *receipt*, for my own head — and left. And ever since, the corner has been getting colder. Every day a little less. Whatever you put in me, it's being taken out again, and I can feel it going, and I don't even know what it *is* that I'm losing, only that it matters more than my wedding ever did, and my wedding was the best day of my life."

The hearth was cold. The cat outside yowled once at the darkening street. Ulyana sat in the borrowed chair and felt the specific, leaden weight of guilt arrive and settle on her shoulders like a coat made of wet sand.

She had lifted Maren Ostrey's wedding eighteen months ago on a damp Tuesday, quick and clean, the way she did everything, and she had skimmed — everyone skims, you can't help it, a memory comes up out of a person trailing its roots — and whatever root had been tangled in that wedding, whatever old song lay coiled beneath the vows, she had packaged it into the charm without looking. Sold it back to the widow as a kindness. Sold her a piece of something else entirely, something that was now killing her by inches in a tidy parlor on Bellows Lane.

Her trade had always been a knife that cut both ways. She'd known that. She'd simply never watched the second edge work.

"Maren," she said quietly. "What I'm about to ask will sound strange."

"Stranger than singing in tongues for the government?"

"Marginally. The thing in your head — the warm corner. Can you give it to me?"

Maren stared at her. "You want to take it *out*?"

"I want to move it somewhere safe. It's not safe in you. Whatever the archivists started, they'll finish, and when it's finished you won't be sick, you'll be *empty* — you'll lose the wedding too, everything near it, everything it touches. I've seen the pattern." She thought of her ledger, the neat columns of the dead and the dimmed. "Everyone who carries a fragment of this thing loses it within the year. Or loses everything around it. I don't know which is worse."

"And if you take it?"

"If I take it, it goes into a vial, and vials are quiet, and quiet things get overlooked." This was a lie, or half of one; the four lights in her cellar were many things, but overlooked was not among them. She pressed on anyway. "And I'll lift the wedding first, properly this time, all of it, and give it back to you whole, so that when the fragment goes, your best day stays. That's the trade. That's all I'm offering, and I swear it's honest, which should frighten you, coming from me."

Maren Ostrey looked at her for a long moment. Outside, the cat gave up on the doorstep and went to terrorize richer households.

"You know what the worst part is?" the widow said at last. "When they hummed that song, part of me was *happy*. Happier than I've been since Aldric died. Like something in me recognized it and said *oh, there you are, we thought you'd drowned.*" She wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand, briskly, angrily. "Do it. Before I talk myself into being sensible."

* * *

The lifting took eleven minutes, and Ulyana did it the old way, hands on either side of the widow's temples, eyes closed, breath slowed to the pace of the other woman's pulse. The wedding came up first, gold and candlelit, Aldric young and nervous with his collar too tight, the cake collapsing, the dancing until dawn — and beneath it, deeper, wrapped around the roots like ivy around a fence post, the song.

It was the lullaby. Of course it was the lullaby. It was the same melody that lived in the smuggler's dying memory and in the mad priest's fevered hymn and, though she had not yet admitted this to herself in daylight, in the half-remembered voice of her own mother, humming over a stove in a kitchen that might never have existed. The melody rose through Maren's wedding like water rising through a floor, and Ulyana followed it down, gently, gently, and found the warm corner.

It was not a corner. It was a window. Small, bright, and looking out onto — she could not see what it looked out onto, only that the light coming through it was the light of a country at evening, and that somewhere beyond the glass a child was being sung to sleep by someone who loved her, and that the glass was cracking, hairline by hairline, every day a little more, because men with ink on their fingers had come with their humming and their receipts.

She drew it out. All of it. The widow shuddered once, sighed like a woman setting down a heavy basket, and slumped in her chair.

Ulyana sealed the fragment into the smallest vial — the fifth light now, she thought grimly, the cellar was becoming a congregation — and then, as promised, poured the wedding back, whole and unskimmed, pressing it home with a care she usually reserved for her own secrets.

Maren woke slowly. Blinked. Looked around her parlor as if confirming it still agreed to exist.

"Aldric's collar was too tight," she said wonderingly. "He wouldn't let anyone fix it. Said his mother had sewn it." She laughed, wet and real this time. "I'd forgotten his mother sewed it. You gave that back?"

"All of it. No corners skipped."

"Then take your horrible little bottle and go." But she caught Ulyana's sleeve at the door, and her grip was strong for a woman who shook. "The archivists. They'll come back. They said they would — said the survey would require *follow-up*. When they do, I won't remember the song, and I won't remember you were here tonight, because I intend to drink enough plum brandy to make sure of it. So whatever it is you're doing — finish it. Quickly."

"There's one more thing," Ulyana said. "The tall one. With the ink. Did he say anything else? Anything at all?"

Maren frowned, dredging. "He asked whether I'd ever been visited by anyone else carrying... he called them *unregistered recollections*. And when I said no, he smiled — polite as Sunday — and said that was fortunate, because the last registered holder had proven difficult to locate, and that they expected to find him through his debts." She paused. "He said a name, actually. He said it to his partner, half under his breath, like a man reading a shopping list. *If the widow doesn't have it, try the Cartographer. Renk always knows where the unwritten places hide.*"

The name went through Ulyana like cold water through cloth.

*Ivo Renk.*

She had stolen a memory from Ivo Renk six years ago — a memory he had begged her, drunk and desperate in a Lowmarket alley, to steal and keep and never sell: a map drawn from the inside of a dream. She had kept it. It was the only item in her inventory she had never priced.

"Ulyana?" Maren said. "You've gone the color of the street."

"I'm fine. Drink your brandy. Forget me thoroughly."

She let herself out into the blue dark of Bellows Lane, where Tam stood exactly where she'd left him, leaning against the wall, watching her with those eyes that held a country.

"We have a name," she told him. "Someone who maps places that don't exist. Which means someone who might map the way *in*."

Tam tilted his head. Then, softly, in that language older than kingdoms, he said a single word — and Ulyana's stolen memories stirred and translated it despite themselves, and the translation landed in her chest like a stone:

*Cartographer.*

He already knew the name. Somewhere in the erased country that had sent him, someone had known it too.

Down the lane, two figures in grey coats turned the corner by the chandler's, walking unhurried, walking with purpose, one of them flexing ink-stained fingers against the cold.

Ulyana took Tam's hand. "Change of plan," she murmured. "We don't go home."

They slipped into the alley behind the chandler's as the knock — one heavy knock, the sound of a stone falling — echoed down Bellows Lane behind them, on the yellow door with the crooked shutter, where a widow was already reaching for the plum brandy.
