## 8. Streets That Remember Being Rivers

The boy woke before dawn, as he always did, and stood at the foot of Ulyana's bed with the patience of a small ghost.

"Ulyana," he said. "The water."

She cracked one eye. "There's no water. There's a washbasin, and you're welcome to it."

"Not that water." He was four days into speaking more than single words now, and each day his voice came easier, like a door learning its hinges. "Under us. It's moving."

Ulyana lay still and listened. Under the floorboards of her shop in Lowmarket, under the cellar where Tam slept on a pallet among her charm-stock, under the foundations of Vessing itself — nothing. The city was built on clay and old bones and the Ministry's good order. There was no water under them.

But she had learned, these past weeks, not to argue with the boy about what he heard.

* * *

They went out early, before the shops opened, when Vessing belonged to the milk-carts and the sweepers and no one else. Ulyana wore her grayest shawl and her most forgettable face, which was a kind of craft in itself; Tam wore boots she'd bought off a cobbler who hadn't asked questions, because a barefoot child drew eyes, and eyes were the one thing she could not afford.

"The oldest quarter," Tam said, tugging her hand southward, toward the river district where the streets predated the kingdom's paving ordinances and the buildings leaned together like conspirators sharing a secret.

"Why there?"

"Because it remembers." He said it simply, the way another child might say *because I'm hungry*, and Ulyana felt the familiar cold slide down her spine. She had stolen memories from dying smugglers and mad priests, from widows and merchants and one archivist who'd wept while she took his childhood from him. She knew what remembering looked like from the inside. She did not love seeing it in a seven-year-old.

They crossed Sable Bridge, and Tam stopped in the middle of it and gripped the railing with both hands.

"What is it?"

"It's wrong," he said, frowning down at the brown water. "The bridge thinks it's newer than it is. It's pretending." He looked up at her, troubled. "Do bridges lie here?"

"In Vessing?" Ulyana managed a smile. "Everything lies here, little one. It's how we stay out of trouble."

But she filed it away, the way she filed everything now: *the bridge thinks it's newer than it is.* A sentence that should have been nonsense and instead sat in her chest like a stone dropped into a well, waiting for the splash.

* * *

The oldest quarter began where the cobbles gave up any pretense of pattern. Here the streets had been laid by people who measured with their feet and their arguments, and the result was a tangle that even the Ministry's surveyors had eventually surrendered to. Laundry lines crossed overhead like bunting left over from some festival nobody remembered. Cats owned everything. The air smelled of frying onions and river mud.

Tam walked it like a man reading a letter from home.

He turned left where Ulyana would have gone right. He paused at a wall and pressed his palm flat against it, listening. He led her down an alley so narrow they had to walk single file, past a door painted over so many times it had become a solid slab of color, and stopped at the end of it, where the alley dead-ended against a brick wall crowned with broken bottles.

"Well," Ulyana said. "That's that, then."

"No." Tam was staring at the wall. "It goes through."

"It very much doesn't, sweetheart."

"It used to." He touched the bricks. "It's sulking. It closed up and it's pretending it never opened." His brow furrowed with genuine distress, the way other children fretted over a toy left in the rain. "Why does everything here pretend?"

And Ulyana, who had spent twelve years perfecting the art of pretending — who had made a fortress of it, a career, a life — had no answer for him at all.

Then she noticed the mortar.

Between the bricks, where the wall met the alley's side walls, the mortar was different. Newer. Someone had filled in this gap, and recently enough that the patch hadn't weathered to match. And below the patch, half-hidden under a century of grime, there was a shape in the brickwork — an arch. A doorway's arch, bricked over, painted over, forgotten over.

Someone had closed a door here and hoped the city would forget it.

Ulyana had seen that trick before. She'd used versions of it herself: a memory scrubbed clean always left a scar, if you knew where to look. Cities were no different. Cities were just memories that had learned to hold still.

"Tam," she said quietly. "How do you know it used to go through?"

He shrugged, the shrug of a child asked to explain breathing. "I walked it."

"When?"

"A long time ago." He held up his hands, measuring something only he could see. "When I was tall."

* * *

They emerged from the alley system near Tanner's Stair, a landmark every Vessing child learned: a grand stone staircase, thirty-one steps, descending from the street level to — according to every map, every guidebook, every grandmother — the old tanners' wharf on the river.

Except the staircase descended to a wall.

It had done so for as long as anyone could remember, and Vessing had long since made its peace with the absurdity. Lovers met on Tanner's Stair. Children sledded down it in winter, risking their necks against the final step. A pie seller had set up shop on the landing decades ago and grown rich on the traffic of people going nowhere. The staircase to nowhere was simply part of the city's charm, the way a scar is part of a face.

Tam stood at the top of it and went very pale.

"That's not right," he whispered. "That's not right, that's not right—"

"Hey. Hey." Ulyana knelt, took his shoulders. Around them, morning pedestrians flowed past without a glance; a child having a moment on Tanner's Stair was not worth noticing. "What's not right? Talk to me."

"There should be water at the bottom." His breath was coming fast. "There should be boats. There should be—" He pressed his fists against his eyes. "I can hear it. Under the steps. It's still there, it didn't leave, it's just *covered*—"

"Tam." She kept her voice low and steady, the voice she'd once used on frightened clients. "Look at me. Whatever you're hearing, it can't hurt you. It's just an echo. Old places make echoes."

But even as she said it, she was doing something she despised herself for: counting steps. Thirty-one, the pie seller had told a customer once, complaining about sledging children. She counted. Twenty-nine.

Twenty-nine steps, descending to a wall.

She looked around wildly, wondering if she'd miscounted, if the light had fooled her, but no — she counted again, twice more, and got twenty-nine both times, and the pie seller's stall sat comfortably on a landing that had no business existing between step twenty-nine and whatever came next.

Two steps had been removed. Or two steps had been *unmade*. And everyone in Vessing strolled across the wound daily without ever feeling it, because the city had agreed, collectively and without discussion, not to notice.

Just as the whole kingdom had agreed not to notice a country that had been erased from every map.

Ulyana sat down heavily on the top step, heedless of appearances for once, and pulled Tam into her side.

"You're not mad," she told him. "I want you to hear that first. You're not mad, and you're not broken, and whatever's leaking into you, we're going to understand it. But I need you to tell me everything you hear. Even the strange things. *Especially* the strange things."

He nodded against her shoulder. Then, muffled: "The pies smell wrong."

She laughed despite herself — a startled, ragged laugh. "The pies?"

"They smell like they remember being better."

* * *

They spent the day following him, and by afternoon Ulyana had stopped doubting and started keeping a list, scratched into a little notebook she'd bought for inventory and repurposed for impossibilities.

A courtyard where Tam insisted there had been a well; the flagstones there were worn in a circle, polished smooth by generations of rope and bucket, though no well appeared on any survey. A lane that bent, according to Tam, the wrong way — and when she checked it against her memory of the district (and her memory, professionally speaking, was excellent), the lane *had* bent differently. She was almost sure. Almost sure in the way that made her want to be sick. A shuttered house whose windows, she realized upon a second look, were bricked up *from the inside*.

And then, as the light went amber and the lamplighters began their rounds, Tam stopped in the middle of Cobbler's Square, planted his feet, and refused to move.

"Here," he said. "Dig here."

"We are not digging in a public square."

"It's under here. It's big. It's *cold*." He stamped his foot on the cobbles, and the sound it made was wrong — a hollow boom, deep and resonant, like striking the lid of a vast empty barrel. Ulyana froze. Several pigeons startled. Nobody else seemed to notice anything amiss.

She knelt and pressed her palm flat against the stones.

Cold rose through them. Not the ordinary coolness of evening stone but a living cold, a moving cold, and beneath it — faint, rhythmic, unmistakable once she let herself hear it — the sound of water flowing. A great deal of water. Flowing under Cobbler's Square, under the oldest quarter, under a city that had exactly one river and it ran nowhere near here.

"Cobbler's Square," she murmured. "Cobbler's. Because cobblers need water for the tanning, and tanners need—" Her mind was running ahead of her now, laying stones across a chasm. "Tam. What was this place called? Before?"

He looked up at her with those too-old eyes. "Riversmeet," he said.

The name landed in her like a key turning in a lock she hadn't known was there. Riversmeet. And somewhere in the vaults of her stolen inventory — she could feel it stirring, a smuggler's dying ramble from years ago, *the three rivers meet at the heart, you can still hear them under the new streets if you know how to listen* — she had carried this truth all along, sold it as a harmless charm or locked away unsold, and never once known what she was holding.

Three rivers. Vessing had one.

Vessing had one river, one bridge, one history, and a staircase missing two steps, and a square that boomed like a drum over hidden water, and a boy who remembered being tall in a country that had never existed.

"Ulyana." Tam tugged her sleeve. His voice had gone small. "Is it my fault? That they covered it up?"

She gathered him in, right there in the square, propriety be hanged, and held him against the gray shawl.

"No, little one. Listen to me. You didn't cover anything up. You're the opposite of covering up." She tilted his chin up. "You're the only thing in this whole city that refuses to forget. Do you know what that makes you?"

He shook his head.

"It makes you the most dangerous person in Vessing," she said gently, "and we need to get you home before anyone dangerous-adjacent notices."

* * *

They took the long way back, along the true river, because Ulyana wanted eyes on the water and the approaches to it. The lamps were lit now. Moth clouds orbited them. On the far bank, the Ministry's customs house glowed with official light, and she kept Tam on the landward side of her body, between her and the buildings, out of the sightlines of anyone on the water.

It was nearly full dark when she saw it.

A poster, freshly pasted to the customs house wall, still shining wet with paste. She crossed the bridge — telling herself it was reconnaissance, telling herself a woman studying a public notice was the most innocent sight in the world — and read it by the light of the nearest lamp.

*A REWARD*, it announced, in the Ministry's tidy, merciless script, *is offered for information regarding a child, approximately six to eight years of age, last seen in the vicinity of Lowmarket. The child is believed mute or simple-minded and may not answer to his name. Citizens are reminded that unregistered persons constitute a matter of state concern.*

Below the text was a drawing.

It was a good drawing. It was a very good drawing. Whoever had made it had worked from description rather than life, and yet they had caught him — the serious eyes, the stubborn mouth, the particular set of his shoulders, as if the artist had understood that this child listened to things under the ground.

Beneath the image, in smaller letters, the line that turned Ulyana's blood to ice water:

*Persons harboring the child will be assumed to be acting in service of proscribed historical revisionism.*

Historical revisionism. The Ministry's phrase for remembering something the Ministry had decided never happened.

They didn't know about her yet. The poster named no shop, no thief, no woman in a gray shawl. They were looking for a lost child, not a criminal — but the moment anyone connected the child to the memory-charms circulating through Lowmarket with their impossible provenance, the two threads would tie themselves, and the knot would have her neck in it.

She walked back across the bridge, slowly, matching her pace to the milk-cart behind her, a woman going home, nothing to see here. Tam waited where she'd left him, in the shadow of a warehouse door, and read her face the way he read everything — completely, and without mercy.

"People are looking for me," he said.

"Yes."

"For the forgetting-people."

"Yes."

He considered this with the gravity of a small judge. Then he reached up and took her hand.

"They won't find me," he said. "I'm hard to find. Where I come from, everyone was hard to find. That was the whole point of us."

And Ulyana squeezed his fingers and smiled for him and walked him home through the lamplit streets, past the staircase that lied about its length, over the square that rang like a drum, through a city that had agreed, all together, in one great conspiracy of silence, to forget the water under its feet —

— and all the way home, beneath the cobbles, beneath the clay, beneath the good orderly silence of Vessing, she could hear it now that she knew to listen: three rivers, running dark and patient under everything, carrying the memory of a drowned country toward a sea that no longer had a name for it.

In the morning, she decided, she would open her inventory and start pulling every fragment with salt-water in it, every scrap of a language older than kingdoms, every charm she'd ever sold that hummed when Tam came near.

She had a map to build. And she had a terrible feeling she already knew who could draw it — a man who mapped places that no longer existed, if the widow's fragment had told her true, and finding him meant walking straight through the part of the city where the Ministry's archivists had been asking their gentle, terrible questions.

The rivers, she thought, could wait one more night.

She only hoped the Ministry could too.
