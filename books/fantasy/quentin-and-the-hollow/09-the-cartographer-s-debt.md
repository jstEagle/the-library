## 9. The Cartographer's Debt

The address Tam had pulled from the widow's fragment was not an address so much as a dare. *Third door past the drowned stair, where the wall leans in to listen.* Vessing's oldest quarter was full of doors that matched that description, and half of them belonged to people who would set dogs on me.

We found it by accident, which is how you find anything in Saltrow. The quarter's streets had been laid out by three different centuries that hated each other, and walking them felt like reading a letter someone had torn up and reassembled wrong. A tanner's alley became a garden wall became a staircase going up into nothing. Tam stopped at every one of these impossibilities and touched them like old friends.

"Here," he said, in his strange half-kingdom tongue, pointing at a door that was painted the exact grey of a sky deciding whether to rain.

"Here," I agreed, because arguing with the boy had proven about as useful as arguing with weather.

The door had no knocker. It had a bell-pull made from what looked suspiciously like a femur, and when I pulled it, somewhere inside something went *clonk* rather than *ding*, as though the bell had died and been stuffed.

After a long while, shuffling. Then a voice through the wood: "If you're from the Ministry, I'm dead, and this house is a memorial."

"I'm from Lowmarket," I said. "I'm looking for the Cartographer."

A pause. "Everyone who looks for the Cartographer is from the Ministry eventually. That's how it works. They let you find me first because it saves them the shoe leather."

"I'm not Ministry," I said. "I'm worse. I'm a client."

Another pause, longer. Then bolts — four of them, each with its own reluctant clack — and the door opened on a man who was mostly beard and elbows. He wore spectacles with one lens cracked and the other lens missing entirely, which struck me as either a philosophical statement or a way of seeing exactly half of everything, and he looked at me with the intact eye like a man appraising a horse he suspected of lying about its age.

"You're the memory-witch of Lowmarket," he said.

"I'm a charm-seller," I said. "Witch implies ambition."

He snorted. Behind him, the room — I could see only a slice of it — was papered floor to ceiling in maps. Not maps of places. Maps of *wrongness*. Streets drawn over other streets. Coastlines that argued with themselves. One map appeared to be of a room, drawn from inside it, which is the sort of thing that makes a person's eyes water if they think about it too long.

"The widow Ossen," he said. "You sold her a wedding-charm two years back. She came here after the archivists visited her, babbling about a door in a salt field. She said the memory hadn't come from her at all. She said it had come from *you*, or through you." His good eye narrowed. "She also said you charged her fairly, which in my experience no honest merchant does."

"Honesty is expensive," I said. "Fairness is cheaper. May I come in? It's cold, and I have a child who speaks a language that officially doesn't exist, and I'd rather say the rest indoors."

That got him. You can refuse a stranger at your door; it's harder to refuse a child standing behind one, small and barefoot and looking up at you with the patient gravity of a cat waiting for a door to be opened. Ivo Renk stepped aside with the air of a man losing an argument he'd already rehearsed losing.

* * *

Inside, the map-room was even better and worse than the slice had promised. Every surface held paper. Maps hung from the ceiling on strings, turning slowly in some draft I couldn't locate, so that walking through the room meant ducking under a slowly rotating archipelago. There was a stove, a kettle, a chair with three legs and a fourth leg improvised from a rolled-up chart of — I squinted — a river that no longer existed. Tam sat down on the floor immediately, cross-legged, in the manner of children and philosophers, and began studying a map of Vessing that had been annotated in red ink with corrections. The corrections were extensive. The map, apparently, was wrong about nearly everything.

"Tea?" Ivo asked, and then answered himself, because it wasn't really a question, it was a ritual. He put the kettle on the stove and turned around. "Now. Tell me why a charm-seller whose entire survival strategy is being forgettable has walked into the one room in Vessing where forgetting goes to die."

So I told him. Not all of it — I kept Tam's name back, and the cellar, and the shape of my own fears — but most of it. The language. The fragments in my stock. The widow's memory of the door in the salt. The pattern of deaths and erasures trailing everyone who'd ever remembered the Hollow. I watched his face while I talked, and I'll tell you what I saw: not surprise. Recognition. The look of a man hearing a diagnosis he'd privately suspected for years and had been drinking heavily about.

When I finished, he poured tea into two cups and a chipped bowl for Tam, and sat down on the three-legged chair with the care of a man negotiating with furniture.

"Do you know what I was, before exile?" he said.

"A scholar," I said. "Royal Cartographic Service. Before they burned your credentials."

"Before they burned my *findings*," he corrected. "The credentials were incidental. I mapped things the crown preferred unmapped. And eleven years ago I petitioned the Archive for access to the pre-kingdom surveys — the oldest ones, the ones from before Vessing was Vessing. Do you know what they told me?"

"That there are none?"

"They told me there had never been any." He smiled without any humor in it whatsoever. "Which is a very specific lie. If the surveys had simply never existed, they'd have said *we don't know*. Bureaucracies love saying we don't know; it costs nothing. But *never existed* — that's an investment. That's a lie you have to maintain. Staff it. Fund it. Someone in the Ministry wakes up every morning and lies about those surveys before breakfast, and has done for longer than I've been alive."

Tam said something soft in the Hollow-tongue, looking up at the rotating maps. Ivo's head turned toward the sound so fast I heard his neck click.

"Say that again," he whispered.

Tam, encouraged by attention, said it again, louder, with the particular relish children bring to being understood.

Ivo looked at me. "Do you know what he said?"

"He said the maps are almost right," I translated. My stolen memories stirred, the way they always did around the boy's speech — fragments rising like silt. "Almost right. The word for *almost* is the same as the word for *mourning*. I think. I'm guessing."

"You're not guessing badly." Ivo took off his spectacles and polished the cracked lens on his sleeve, a nervous habit, though polishing a crack accomplishes nothing, which may have been the point. "Eleven years ago, a smuggler came through Saltrow. Dying. Knife wound, bad one. He babbled for six hours in a language no one knew, and I wrote down every word, because that's what I do, I write down everything, it's why I eat alone at parties." He gestured at a shelf, and I saw a ledger there, fat and cracked, its spine mended with string. "Six hours of a dying man describing a country. Rivers, roads, the names of bells. I spent four years trying to place it. There is no country there. There never was. The coastlines don't admit it. The trade ledgers skip it. And then—" he tapped the ledger, "—then a woman sells a widow a wedding-charm with a salt-door in it, and the widow comes to me, and I finally understand what I've been looking at."

"What have you been looking at?" I asked.

He stood, painfully, joints announcing themselves, and crossed to the largest map on the wall — a map of the kingdom, beautifully drawn, official in every line. And then he took a stick of charcoal from his pocket and drew, freehand, over the empty northeastern interior where the map showed only mountains and the words HERE BE NOTHING (which, incidentally, is not what old maps actually said; Ivo had added it himself, out of spite).

"There," he said.

The shape he drew fit. That was the horror of it. The mountains bent around it. The rivers ran toward it and stopped, like sentences interrupted. The trade roads curved away from it in long polite arcs, the way water curves around a stone. The kingdom's own geography had been edited, and whoever had done the editing had been thorough enough to bend rivers but not thorough enough to hide the bending.

"It was traded away," Ivo said quietly. "Not destroyed. Destroyed leaves ruins, refugees, songs. This left *nothing*, which is much more expensive. Eleven years ago — twelve, perhaps — the kingdom was broke. War debt, famine, a navy it couldn't feed. And there was a creditor across the sea, and the creditor wanted something no nation can sell." He turned to look at me. "It wanted a country. Whole. Existing. And the kingdom had one it could spare — a small one, far away, with no allies, because no one else remembered it either. So they signed a treaty. And in the treaty, the Hollow's existence itself was the payment. Transferred. Assigned. Conveyed, in the legal language, like a shipment of timber."

The stove ticked. One of the hanging maps rotated, with a faint creak, so that its coastline faced the window.

"They sold it," I said. My voice came out flat, which surprised me; I'd expected it to shake. "They sold a country."

"They sold a country the way you sell a memory," Ivo said, and looked at me with terrible gentleness. "You of all people know how it's done. You take it out carefully. You leave the shape behind. You make sure no one misses it, because the ones who miss it start asking questions, and questions are bad for business. The Ministry of Forgetting didn't invent the trade, charm-seller. They're just the new brokers. The kingdom was in the business long before them."

"And the people?" I heard myself ask. "The people who lived there. Where did they go?"

"Some were moved. Most were—" he searched for the word and visibly disliked all of them, "—*amortized*. Paid out over time. Forgotten gradually, so the grief would be gradual too, spread thin enough that no single mourning would draw notice. And a few—" he glanced at Tam, who was drawing in the dust on the floor with one finger, drawing, I realized with a lurch, a door, "—a few were kept. As keys. As anchors. Because a thing erased that completely needs something left inside it to hold the edges down, or the erasure starts eating outward. Which, I suspect, is the anomaly your Ministry friends have noticed. The erasure is hungry now. It's finished with the Hollow and it's begun on the leftovers. Everyone who remembers. Everyone who kept a scrap."

"The deaths," I said. "My clients."

"Your clients," Ivo agreed, "were collateral. The machinery doesn't hate them. It doesn't even see them. That's the worst part, isn't it? It's not malice. It's bookkeeping."

And there it was — the fury he'd promised me without promising it, rising in my chest like floodwater. Not the hot fury of a burning building but the cold kind, the kind that keeps. Twelve years I had worked the small trades. I had lifted a widow's wedding and sold it back to her and called it kindness, and it *was* kindness, next to this. Somewhere in a marble hall, men with clean hands had signed a country out of existence and gone to lunch afterward, and their grandchildren now burned witches two streets over from my shop to keep the arithmetic tidy. I thought of the smuggler, dying in Saltrow, spending his last six hours naming the bells of a country that had been legally converted into debt relief. I thought of my mother, humming a tune I'd never been able to source.

I put my cup down very carefully, because if I put it down carelessly I'd have thrown it.

"You said I owed you," Ivo said, watching me. "You haven't asked what I want in return. That's unlike a merchant."

"I assumed you'd get to it."

"Twelve years ago you stole a memory from a young scholar in the Royal Archive. A memory of his father — a surveyor — describing a coastline that appears on no chart. The scholar reported the theft. The Ministry investigated. Found nothing, because you're good. But the scholar kept reporting it, year after year, kept insisting the memory existed, kept demanding its return, until eventually the Ministry concluded that anyone who insisted so hard on a memory that officially never existed must be unstable, and they revoked his post, and he came here, to Saltrow, to keep insisting alone." He spread his hands. "You never sold it. I checked. For eleven years I've checked every charm-market in three cities. A father's voice, describing a coast. It exists nowhere for sale. Which means somewhere in your hoard, madam, is the last remaining proof that my father was sane."

I closed my eyes. I could feel it — of course I could. Deep in the inventory, in the drawer I never opened, wrapped in oilcloth against the damp: an old man's voice, patient, amused, describing a harbor shaped like a cupped hand, and his boy sitting on the charts so he wouldn't blow away. I had taken it on a dare, my first year, from a mark who barely guarded his own head. I had never sold it because selling it had felt like selling someone's Sunday dinner. Some thefts you commit and some you merely witness.

"It's yours," I said. "Tonight. I'll bring it tomorrow, unwrapped, whole, with interest — I'll sit with you while you take it back, if taking it back is possible."

Ivo Renk sat down again, abruptly, the way men sit when their knees stop consulting them. He took off the broken spectacles and pressed his sleeve to his eyes and pretended, unconvincingly, that he was cleaning them.

"Well," he said, after a moment, in a voice roughened at the edges. "Then we're square, and I am at your service. What do you need from the Cartographer?"

"The door," I said. "The real one. The one in the salt. The widow's fragment showed it, but a fragment's a pebble, not a road. I need the whole map. I need to know how to reach a country that's been subtracted from the world — and I need to know it before the Ministry's bookkeepers finish their audit and come to erase the remainder."

Ivo was already pulling ledgers off shelves, three and four at a time, arms full, beard bristling with purpose. "Then you'd best put the kettle back on," he said. "Because the last surveyor who mapped that border was my father, and everything he knew is about to come home."

Behind him, Tam looked up from the door he'd drawn in the dust — and blew gently across it, the way a child blows a dandelion, and the dust rose, and settled, and the door was still there.

Outside, two streets away and closing, boots were asking questions in the uniform cadence of the Ministry of Forgetting.
