## 10. Duty Wears a Kind Face

The report arrived on Dain Marrow's desk the way most bad news did in the Ministry of Forgetting: neatly, quietly, and already three days late.

It was a single sheet, typed on the good typewriter — the one reserved for matters of provenance — and it said, in the flat bureaucratic language he had spent nine years learning to speak like a native:

*Anomaly. Charms circulating in Lowmarket district bearing memory-signatures inconsistent with registered stock. Provenance unverifiable. Recommend investigation.*

He read it twice, then a third time with his finger under the words, as though the finger might catch one of them lying.

There was nothing strange about an anomaly. Vessing produced anomalies the way the river produced mud — constantly, seasonally, and in quantities no one wanted to think about too closely. What was strange was the *signature*. He'd read the attached analysis four times now, and every time he came away with the same itch between his shoulder blades.

Memory had a texture. That was the first thing they taught you at the Academy, before they taught you anything useful: stolen memories were like stolen coats. They never quite fit the new owner. The seams showed. A charm cut from a widow's wedding would carry the widow's hands, the widow's fear, the widow's particular way of noticing light. Ministry analysts could read those seams the way tailors read stitching.

The charms moving through Lowmarket had seams, all right. But the seams were *kind*. Whoever was doing the cutting took pains to smooth them — left the seller's grief out of the wedding, kept the dead mother's voice but not the dying. It was craftsmanship. It was almost *courtesy*.

Dain had spent nine years hunting thieves who treated other people's heads like quarries. Courtesy was new.

"You're frowning at that paper like it insulted your mother," said Wren, from the next desk over. She was eating an apple with the focused hostility she brought to all meals, having decided sometime around her thirtieth year that enjoyment was a security risk.

"It's an anomaly," Dain said.

"They're always anomalies. Last month it was the man who kept buying back his own nightmares. Month before, the choir that remembered a verse nobody wrote." She crunched. "You'll go to Lowmarket, you'll drink terrible coffee, you'll come back with a name and a headache."

"Yes," Dain said. And then, because the itch between his shoulder blades had opinions: "Wren. Have you ever handled a charm that felt — polite?"

She stopped chewing. Considered. "Once. Years ago. Before your time." Her face did something complicated and then smoothed itself, the way faces did in this building; there were doors in this Ministry that swung both ways, and everyone learned early to keep their expressions shut. "A charm of a harbor morning. Beautifully cut. I reported it, obviously." She resumed eating. "The analyst who reviewed it retired the following spring. Very suddenly. Very contentedly, they said."

"Contentedly."

"He smiled a great deal and remembered nothing about harbors." She waved the apple core at him. "Go find your thief, Marrow. Just don't bring anything home you can't account for."

* * *

Lowmarket smelled the way it always had, which was to say of fish, rain, frying onions, and the particular desperation of people trying to sell things before the tax assessors finished their tea.

Dain wore plain clothes. This was standard for preliminary inquiries — the uniform made people remember you, and remembering was, after all, the raw material of his trade. He bought coffee from a cart that had possibly been brewing since the last reign, and he asked questions the way he'd been trained to: gently, sideways, as though he were merely curious rather than professionally dangerous.

*"Charms? Oh, aye, everyone buys charms, sir. My wife's got one — says it's her grandmother's kitchen. Smells of bread, apparently."*

*"Where from?"*

*A shrug. A stall number. A stall that, when he reached it, sold buttons.*

He worked the market for two days. On the second day, an old woman selling candles leaned across her table, lowered her voice, and said: "You want the shop on Candlemaker's Row. The narrow one. But mind how you go in, young man — she's a kind one, that woman. Whatever you're wanting, be kind back."

"I'm not wanting anything," Dain said. "I'm asking questions."

"That's what I said," the old woman replied serenely, and went back to her candles.

Candlemaker's Row. He knew the street. Everyone in Vessing knew it vaguely, the way you knew a tune without recalling where you'd heard it — narrow, crooked, leaning buildings that seemed to be conferring with each other overhead. He walked its length once, noting exits, noting the drainpipe that would bear a climber's weight, noting the small shop with the hand-painted sign:

**WHITLOCK & DAUGHTER — CHARMED GOODS, HONESTLY SOURCED**

*Honestly sourced.* He stood in front of the sign for longer than a man conducting an inquiry should stand in front of a sign. Something about it snagged in him, some thread catching on a nail in a room he couldn't picture. *Whitlock.* The name sat in his mouth like a word from a language he'd studied and forgotten.

And underneath the name, smaller, in different paint, older paint: **& DAUGHTER**, where someone had carefully painted over what might once have been another word entirely. He crouched. Tilted his head. There — beneath the careful grey, the ghost of letters. Not *daughter*. Something shorter. Something that started with a—

"Dain?"

He turned around so fast he nearly lost his footing on the crooked cobbles.

She was standing in the shop doorway with a broom in her hands and flour on one cheekbone, a small dark-haired woman with a face that hit him somewhere below the ribs like a dropped book — no pain yet, just the shock of impact, the certainty that something had fallen and he'd have to pick it up and read it.

"Do I know you?" he said.

Her expression flickered. It was the strangest flicker he'd ever seen — not confusion, not recognition, but something *between* them, something that looked like grief wearing politeness as a coat two sizes too large.

"No," she said, after a moment. "I don't believe we've met. You have the look of a man who's lost, though. Can I help you find something?"

*Yes,* thought some traitorous part of him. *Apparently I've been looking for something for years and only just noticed.*

"A charm," he said aloud, because that was the job, because duty was a thing you held onto when everything else got slippery. "For a friend. Something — peaceful."

Something peaceful. He heard himself say it and winced internally. Nine years an inquisitor and his cover story sounded like a greeting card.

But she smiled — a real smile, tired at the edges — and stepped back to let him in. "Come in, then. Mind the step. It minds everybody."

* * *

The shop was warm and cluttered and smelled of lavender and candlewax and, underneath it, something faintly metallic that his training identified before his manners could suppress the identification: *memory-stock*. Fresh-cut. Carefully stored.

He catalogued without meaning to. It was a habit, the way other men drummed fingers. Shelves of little glass vials, each labeled in a neat, even hand. Charms of weather and kitchens and first snows. Nothing illegal on display — everything here was licensed, harmless, the sort of thing widows bought to hear a dead voice again within the letter of the law. And yet the seams of the arrangement, the *courtesy* of it, matched the analysis on his desk exactly.

"You have a good eye for stock," he said, watching her wrap a vial of birdsong in paper.

"My father taught me." A beat. "Most things, anyway."

"And the rest?"

"The rest I taught myself." She handed him the parcel. "That'll be four coppers. For your friend."

"For my friend," he agreed, and paid, and stayed, because leaving felt like abandoning a sentence halfway through. "Do you make them all yourself? The charms?"

"Some. Some I source." Her eyes met his, level and brown and — he searched for the word and found, absurdly, *familiar*, which was impossible, because he had never seen this woman in his life. "Sourcing's the honest part, actually. People sell their hard memories to me and I keep them safe. Better than keeping them yourself, sometimes. Some things weigh less in glass."

"Some things," Dain said slowly, "shouldn't be sold at all."

"No," she agreed softly. "Those are the ones I keep."

The silence that followed had weight. He felt it settle on his shoulders like a familiar coat — and there it was again, that image, coats and seams and fitting, circling him like a bird he couldn't shoot down.

"You have me at a disadvantage," he said. "You know I'm—"

"A customer," she said pleasantly. "Yes. We get all sorts."

He laughed. It startled him, the laugh; it came up from somewhere unguarded, somewhere he hadn't visited in years. "Right. A customer." He tucked the parcel inside his coat. At the door he paused, hand on the frame, and heard himself say: "The paint. Over your sign. There was another word there once, wasn't there?"

Behind him, very quietly, Ulyana Whitlock set down her wrapping paper.

"There's a word under everything," she said, "if you scrape hard enough. Most people are happier not scraping."

* * *

That night, in his rooms above the Ministry annexe, Dain did something he had never done in nine years of service.

He opened his own file.

Every inquisitor had one. It was supposed to be sealed — *the investigator must remain uninvestigated*, ran the doctrine, because a man who knew his own edits could reverse-engineer anyone's — but Dain had picked locks that were considerably better defended than the records office, and guilt had a way of making keys unnecessary.

His file was thin. That was the first wrong thing. Inquisitors' files were supposed to be thick — psychological evaluations, loyalty reviews, incident reports going back to the Academy. His began twelve years ago with a single intake form and then jumped, clean as a cliff edge, to his commissioning.

Twelve years ago. The gap was eleven months wide.

He sat with the file open on his knees while the candle burned down, and he did what the Ministry had trained him never to do: he looked directly at the hole.

Eleven months. He knew certain facts around it, the way you know the shape of a missing tooth with your tongue. He knew he had come to Vessing at twenty-three with no money and an excellent memory. He knew he had spent some portion of those years — doing what? The intake form said *laborer, docks*. But his hands, when he looked at them honestly, had calluses in the wrong places for dock work: fine calluses, precise ones, the kind that came from delicate work. From picking small things. From handling glass vials, maybe.

From handling glass vials.

He set the file down. His heart was behaving badly.

There was more. There was a page near the end — a routine annual review, eight years back — where the evaluator had written: *Subject displays anomalous affective response to stimuli associated with the Lowmarket district. Subject cannot account for response. Recommend continued observation.* And beneath that, in a supervisor's hand: *Response is benign. Do not flag.*

*Benign.* Someone had found his unexplained feelings and decided they were safe to leave in place. Like leaving furniture in a house you'd emptied of everything else.

Dain got up and walked to the window. Below, Vessing lay under its usual haze of lamplight and river fog, and somewhere in it — two miles east, maybe less — a woman with flour on her cheekbone had said *there's a word under everything* in a voice that made his chest ache like an old injury in cold weather.

He tried the exercise they taught first-year analysts. Free association. Say the word, write the first thing that comes.

*Lowmarket.* — *Rain on canvas awnings. Laughing. Running across wet cobbles with someone, both of you drunk on nothing, on youth, on the specific joy of being fast and unseen—*

He stopped writing. His pen had pressed hard enough to tear the paper.

He had never run across wet cobbles with anyone. He was quite sure of that. He was sure of very few things tonight.

* * *

In the morning, he wrote his first report.

He sat at his desk with Wren crunching apples beside him and the good typewriter waiting, and he composed the truth in his head first, the way you rehearse a confession: *Anomaly traced to shop on Candlemaker's Row, trading as Whitlock & Daughter. Proprietor displays advanced cutting technique consistent with unlicensed memory-work. Proprietor's manner suggests prior acquaintance with this investigator. Investigator notes an eleven-month gap in own records. Requesting full forensic audit of—*

He looked at the words for a long time.

Then he thought about the old woman with the candles. *Be kind back.* He thought about the supervisor's note: *benign, do not flag.* He thought about the way she'd said *those are the ones I keep*, and the way the whole Ministry around him hummed like a hive built out of other people's emptied heads, and he thought — with a clarity that frightened him more than any anomaly ever had — *if I write the truth, they will not investigate her. They will simply take. Everything. The way they took eleven months from me and called it administrative tidiness.*

He typed instead:

*Preliminary inquiry complete. Anomaly attributable to unlicensed but low-harm practitioner operating in Lowmarket. Technique crude, output minimal, no evidence of organized trafficking. Subject appears to be elderly hobbyist. Recommend monitoring only. No further action warranted.*

*Elderly hobbyist.* He stared at the lie and felt it sit in his stomach like swallowed stone. Nine years of perfect service, and his first falsified report described a small dark-haired woman of perhaps thirty as *elderly*.

"You're making that face again," Wren said, not looking up.

"What face?"

"The one where you've eaten something that disagrees with you and you're deciding whether to blame the food or yourself." She glanced over, apple poised. "Everything all right, Marrow?"

"Fine," he said. "Report's filed. Dead end."

"Mm." She bit the apple, chewed, swallowed, and said, with terrible mildness: "Funny. The provenance clerk mentioned you bought a charm yesterday. From Candlemaker's Row."

The room got very quiet, or perhaps just Dain did.

"Routine cover," he said.

"Of course." She returned to her apple. Then, as he rose to leave: "Marrow. If you ever do find something you can't account for—" she paused, choosing words with unusual care, "—some of us keep a bottle in the bottom drawer for exactly that occasion. No reports required."

He looked at her. Eight years of shared desks, and he realized he knew almost nothing about Wren except that she ate apples like executions and had once, briefly, handled a charm of a harbor morning.

"Noted," he said.

* * *

He went back to Candlemaker's Row that evening. Officially, he told himself, to maintain surveillance. Unofficially, he had already bought a second charm he didn't need — a vial labeled *summer rain on a tin roof* — and he caught himself standing outside her door at dusk like a suitor in a bad ballad.

Through the window he watched her close up shop. She moved efficiently, surely, snuffing lamps, stacking vials — and then she paused, holding one small charm up to the last light, and her face did the thing again: that flicker, that grief-in-politeness, that look of a woman talking to someone who wasn't there.

To someone who *used* to be there.

*Who did you lose?* he thought. *Or worse — who did I—*

The thought broke off cleanly, the way thoughts did when they approached the hole in him. He stood in the gathering dark with his useless charm and his falsified report and his eleven missing months, and understood, with the calm of a man watching floodwater reach the doorstep, that he was cracking. That duty — the thing he had built himself out of, beam by beam, because there had been nothing else to build with — was no longer load-bearing. That somewhere under the paint of him there was another word, and it started with a *U*, and it was getting louder.

Inside the shop, Ulyana blew out the final lamp.

And Dain Marrow turned up his collar, walked out of Candlemaker's Row, and went home to do the most dangerous thing an inquisitor can do: he sharpened his lockpicks, and began, methodically, to reconstruct the man he had been before the Ministry made him.
