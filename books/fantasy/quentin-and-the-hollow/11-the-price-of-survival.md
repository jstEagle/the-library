## 11. The Price of Survival

The smuggler's name was Ferro Vask, and he smelled like pickled herring and bad decisions, which was how Ulyana knew he was exactly the man she needed.

He sat across the table from her in the back room of the Drowned Lantern, turning his cup in slow circles without drinking from it. He had the look of a man who had already counted the exits twice and found them all disappointing.

"You want passage to the border ruins," he said. "Nobody wants passage to the border ruins. The border ruins are where passage goes to die."

"I want passage," Ulyana said, "and I want it quiet."

"Quiet costs."

"Everything costs. That's the whole religion of this city."

Ferro grinned at that, showing teeth stained the color of weak tea. "You're not what I expected, Mistress Lark. The woman who sent me to you said you were a charm-seller. Little bottles of other people's happiness. Weddings and first snows and such."

"That's the shop." Ulyana folded her hands on the table, keeping them still because she had learned long ago that hands betrayed people. "This is the back room. Different inventory."

"And what's in your inventory tonight?"

She had spent three days deciding this. Three days of lying awake in the narrow bed above her shop while Tam slept in the cellar among the jars, breathing the slow breath of a child who trusted her, which was somehow worse than if he'd cried. Three days of taking down charms and putting them back, taking down memories and weighing them like a miser weighing coins, looking for anything large enough, true enough, to buy what she needed.

Her stock wouldn't do. The widow's wedding was lovely but small; grief had worn its edges soft. The mad priest's fragments were true but shattered, worth less than the trouble of carrying them. Everything she had stolen from other people came pre-diluted by distance — she hadn't lived those moments, only lifted them, and buyers could feel the difference through the glass the way you can feel a counterfeit coin.

A buyer paying for passage to the border ruins would want something real. Something with weight. Something that had actually happened to someone.

There was exactly one thing in her possession that qualified.

"A memory," she said. "Mine. Large and true."

Ferro's grin faded into something more professional. "Whose?"

"Does it matter?"

"It matters for price. A stranger's joy is worth a meal. Your own—" He whistled low. "Your own is worth a ship."

"Not a ship. Passage. Two seats, one of them small, and no questions at any checkpoint between here and the salt flats."

"No questions is expensive."

"So am I."

* * *

She went home and took down the jar.

It sat on the highest shelf of her workroom, behind the row of mislabeled charms she kept there as camouflage, and it had not been touched in twelve years. It didn't need to be. Memory keeps better than milk, better than wine, better than most promises. Inside the glass, the morning swam like a goldfish: green-gold light, the smell of bread, a laugh she would have known anywhere even now, even blindfolded, even underwater.

Dain Marrow, twenty-four years old, sitting on the roof of a warehouse in Lowmarket with his shirt unbuttoned one button too far and his boots off because he claimed boots were a form of tyranny. The sun coming up over the rooftops the color of apricots. His hair doing something ridiculous in the wind. The two of them splitting a loaf they'd stolen from their own employer, which Dain had insisted made it not theft but *internal redistribution*, and Ulyana had laughed so hard she'd nearly fallen off the roof, and he'd caught her wrist without even looking up, the way he always caught things without seeming to watch for them.

That morning. That specific, stupid, perfect morning, when neither of them had known yet that he would become an inquisitor and she would become a ghost, when the future was still a country they assumed they'd both be living in.

Twelve years she had kept it. Not sold it, never sold it, though there had been months — bad months, hungry months — when selling it would have fed her for a season. She had told herself it was principle. A thief who steals her own past is a thief with nothing left. But principles are just prices we haven't been offered yet, and here was Ferro Vask, smelling of herring, offering.

She carried the jar downstairs to the cellar, where Tam was drawing in the dust again.

He drew the door every day. Sometimes twice. It was always the same door — tall, arched, with a keystone carved in a shape that wasn't quite a knot and wasn't quite an eye — and he drew it with the concentration of a monk illuminating scripture, his small finger dragging lines through the dirt floor while the lantern threw his shadow huge against the wall. Today he had added something new: figures. Two of them, small, hand in hand, standing before the door.

He looked up at her and pointed at the figures, then at her, then at himself. Then he said something in that old language, soft and urgent, and one of the words snagged on a hook deep in her stolen inventory — *vessha*, or something near it — and a dead smuggler's voice rose unbidden in her skull: *the door remembers who comes to it whole.*

"I know," Ulyana said, though she didn't, quite. "We're going. I'm arranging it."

Tam studied her with those too-old eyes, and then he did something he had never done before. He reached out and touched the hem of her sleeve, very lightly, the way you touch something you're afraid will vanish.

Children shouldn't know about vanishing. Someone had taught him early.

"We'll both go through," she said, and heard her own voice lie, gently, the way you lie to children and to yourself: smoothly, with conviction, hoping the truth won't notice until it's too late to matter.

* * *

The sale happened two nights later, in the same back room, with the same herring smell and a second man present — Ferro's partner, a broad silent woman called Maret who handled the actual extraction, since Ferro, it turned out, was the talker and Maret was the knife.

"There's a risk," Maret said, setting out her tools on the scarred wood: a shallow copper dish, a needle thin as an eyelash, a stoppered vial of something that caught the lamplight like oil. "Taking from your own head is different from taking from a client's. Clients feel it as a loss. You'll feel it as an absence. Like a tooth gone. Your tongue keeps going back to the gap."

"I've pulled my own teeth before," Ulyana said. "It's not so bad once the root's out."

Maret looked at her for a long moment with the flat appraisal of someone who has heard every kind of bravado and believes none of it. "Which memory?"

Ulyana set the jar on the table between them.

Maret uncorked it, and the morning came out into the room like warmth from an oven. Green-gold light played across the ceiling beams. Somewhere impossibly close, bread. And a laugh — Dain's laugh, young, unguarded, the laugh of a man who had never yet edited anyone out of existence — and Maret flinched, just slightly, as if the sound had brushed her cheek.

"This is yours?" she asked quietly.

"It's mine."

"What is it?"

Ulyana almost said *nothing*. Almost said *an old morning, nothing important*. But Maret was holding her past in an open dish, and some debts deserve accurate accounting.

"A morning," she said. "With someone I used to know. We stole a loaf of bread and ate it on a roof and thought we were clever. That's all it is. That's everything it is."

Maret nodded slowly, the way one nods at a funeral. Then she began.

The extraction itself was strangely gentle. Ulyana had expected pain — she had performed extractions, she knew what the needle did, the little cold hook that finds the memory's root and draws — but instead there was only a pulling sensation behind her eyes, like a tide going out, and the room growing brighter and simpler as the morning left it. She watched the green-gold light gather in the copper dish. She watched the bread-smell thin and fade. She watched the laugh fold itself up small and pour into the waiting vial, and she braced for the grief to hit her like a wave.

It didn't.

That was the frightening part. She sat there afterward, tongue going back to the gap, probing the place where the morning had been, and found — smoothness. Acceptance. A faint ache, yes, like weather coming, but no wave, no drowning. She knew, intellectually, that the memory had mattered. She could see the shape of its importance the way you can see the shape of a missing tooth. But the matter of it, the blood and bread and wind of it, was gone, and what remained felt oddly like reading a letter about someone else's love affair.

"Well?" Ferro asked. "How do you feel?"

"Lighter," Ulyana said, and meant it, and hated meaning it.

Maret corked the vial and held it up to the lamp. Inside, the morning glowed like a firefly in a bottle. "Passage for two," she said. "Salt road, no checkpoints, no questions. Be at the tannery gate Thursday before dawn. Ask for the woman with the grey mule." She paused, vial still raised. "For what it's worth — whoever he was, he laughed like the world was going to be kind to him."

"It wasn't," Ulyana said.

* * *

She should have felt devastated. She catalogued her own feelings that night with the methodical attention she gave to mislabeled stock, looking for devastation the way she'd once looked for Hollow fragments, and found instead a terrible, functional calm. She ate supper. She checked the locks. She answered Tam's wordless questions with reassuring nonsense. She slept — actually slept, deeply, dreamlessly — for the first time in weeks.

And in the morning she understood why it frightened her more than the sale ever could.

Because the memory hadn't hurt to lose. Which meant it had already been dying. Twelve years of keeping it on a shelf, of taking it down to look at and putting it back, of loving it at a careful distance the way you love a photograph — she had been embalming it, not living it. The morning with Dain on the roof had stopped being a thing that happened to her and become a thing she owned, and things you own can be sold with nothing more than a wince.

Survival, she thought, watching Tam draw his door in the dust, doesn't take from you all at once. It takes the way a river takes a bank. Slowly. Politely. So gradually you call the erosion peace.

But there was one thing the calm couldn't explain away, one splinter working deeper the more she probed it. When Maret had drawn the memory out — in those last seconds, as the laugh poured into the vial — Ulyana had seen, for half a heartbeat, something else riding inside the morning like a fish inside clear water. A face she hadn't noticed before. Not Dain's. Her own, younger, looking at him across the stolen loaf with an expression she recognized now only because she'd seen it on clients' faces a hundred times, lifting it out of their heads and labeling it for sale:

The expression of a person who does not yet know they are happy, and so wastes none of it being afraid.

She had loved him. Of course she had loved him. She had known that the way you know your own address. But seeing it — seeing herself see him — had done something the loss hadn't. It had left a mark the extraction missed, a residue too fine for Maret's needle, clinging to the walls of the gap like dust after furniture's gone.

And the residue whispered, in no voice at all: *you didn't sell a memory. You sold the last copy of a person. The one who existed before he learned to edit.*

"Ulyana." Tam tugged her sleeve. He spoke rarely, and when he did it was in that old tongue, but lately a few Vessing words had begun surfacing in him like stones in a thawing field. This time he managed one, pointing at the tannery district beyond the window, where the first grey light of Thursday was just touching the roofs.

"Mule," he said.

"Yes." She shouldered her pack and took up the lantern. "Grey mule. Before dawn."

He took her hand — the child who touched nothing voluntarily, whose skin remembered things his mouth couldn't say — and together they climbed the cellar stairs toward the door, and the border, and whatever waited on the salt flats where a country that had never existed was waiting to be found.

Behind them, on the workroom shelf, in a vial bound for a buyer she would never meet, a young man laughed on a rooftop, and the world was still kind to him, and would be forever, because forever was all that was left.
