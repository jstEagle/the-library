## 12. Border of the Unwritten

*Ulyana*

The salt flats began three days out of Vessing, and by the second morning of them Ulyana had learned two things: that the horizon was a liar, and that Tam could walk farther than any child had a right to.

"You could ride," she said, nodding at the mule. The mule's name was Turnip, because Ivo had named him, and Ivo named everything after food he missed from his student days in the capital. "Turnip doesn't mind. He's told me so. At length."

Tam shook his head and kept walking. He'd been speaking more every day since they'd left the city — whole sentences now, though half of them were in that old tongue that made the air feel thicker when he used it. He saved his Vessing words like coins he wasn't sure would still be accepted.

"Suit yourself," Ulyana said. "More room on the mule for the cheese."

The cheese was also Ivo's doing. He'd packed for their journey the way scholars pack: meticulously, generously, and with an unshakable belief that hard cheese solves most problems. Ulyana had tried to tell him that Ministry riders solve problems too, and rather faster, but Ivo had only adjusted his spectacles and said, "Then we shall want to be well-fed when we're caught," which was either gallows humor or genuine planning, and with Ivo it was never possible to know which.

She thought about Ivo now, a day behind them with the cart and the decoy papers, taking the long southern road alone so that anyone following would see a fat scholar traveling to a conference that did not exist. It had been his idea. He'd been terribly pleased with it.

"He'll be fine," she told Tam, who hadn't asked. "He's the sort of man the world forgets to bother."

Tam looked up at her with those grey eyes — eyes the color of the flats themselves — and said something soft in the old language. She caught maybe a third of it through the stolen fragments rattling around her head. *Safe*, possibly. *Follows*, possibly. Or possibly *the salt remembers your feet*, which was such a Hollow thing to say that she decided not to ask.

The ruins announced themselves before they appeared. First the ground changed underfoot — salt giving way to a black stone that drank the heat instead of throwing it back. Then the light changed, going thin and honest, the way light does in places where nothing has bothered to lie for a long time. Then, rising out of the shimmer like the spine of a drowned animal, the border wall of a country that had never officially existed.

It ran north and south as far as she could see, this wall, or what remained of one. Twelve feet high once, perhaps; now mostly waist-high rubble stitched together by stubborn grass. And set into it at intervals — she counted eleven before she stopped counting — were stones unlike the rest. Taller than a man. Smooth as river ice. Each one carved over its whole face in writing that hurt to look at directly, the way a lamp hurts after dark.

"The treaty-stones," she murmured. "Ivo's maps put the main one here." She checked the scrap of vellum against the horizon, then against the wall, then against the horizon again, because the two refused to agree. "Which means it should be... there. Where the wall bends."

The wall did not bend. The wall ran straight until it didn't run at all, and where it stopped, there was a gap, and in the gap stood a single stone taller than all the others, black as wet slate, humming.

Not humming, exactly. Ulyana had spent twelve years handling other people's memories, and she knew the sound memory made when it was alive — a faint pressure behind the ears, like standing near a struck bell that hasn't finished deciding whether to ring. The stone was full of that sound. Full to bursting.

"Tam," she said carefully. "We look first. We do not touch. Touching is how people end up as cautionary tales."

But the boy was already walking toward it, and his walk was wrong — not the careful, hungry-child walk she'd come to know, but something older, surer, the walk of someone returning to a house he'd been told burned down. His bare feet left prints in the black dust, and the prints stayed bright somehow, pale against the dark, like the ground was remembering each step a moment after he took it.

"Tam—"

He touched the stone.

* * *

*Tam*

The stone knew him.

That was the first thing, the truest thing, the thing he would carry forward from this moment no matter what came after: *the stone knew him.* Not the way the Ministry knew people — as names in ledgers, as taxes owed, as bodies to be counted or burned. The stone knew him the way his mother's hands had known him, before. Before the grey. Before the long walking. Before the silence that had filled his mouth for three years like water fills a boot.

Warmth went up his arm. Not fire-warmth. Hearth-warmth. The warmth of bread and of dogs sleeping and of a voice singing low in a language that was his, that had always been his, that lived in him the way marrow lives in bone even when the bone forgets why it is hollow.

And the stone *answered*. He felt it answering, felt the carvings wake under his palm, line by line, letter by letter, each one lighting from within like coals breathed upon. The words were names. He understood that without understanding how. Thousands of names. Hundreds of thousands. Every person who had ever loved the Hollow enough to be remembered by it, pressed into the black stone like flowers pressed into a book, waiting — all these years, all these grey years — waiting for someone to open the book.

*Little key,* the stone said, in the voice of everyone at once, which is to say in no voice at all, only a feeling, vast and gentle. *You came back.*

"I brought someone," Tam said. His own voice surprised him — steady, unafraid. "She can't hear you yet. But she will."

*She is almost one of us,* the stone said. *Almost. There is a door in her that was locked from the outside. Someone locked it. Someone kind.*

Tam frowned. He looked back at Ulyana, standing ten paces off with her hand shading her eyes and her whole body tense as a drawn wire, and he saw what the stone meant. There was a shape to her, now that he looked properly — a shape like a country seen through fog, with borders missing, with a piece torn away. She was missing something enormous. She had been missing it so long she'd built her whole self around the hole, the way a tree grows around a fence wire.

"She gave something up once," Tam said slowly. "Didn't she. To get out."

*The door takes tolls,* the stone said. *All doors do. Hers was very expensive.*

Behind them, far across the salt, something glinted.

Tam turned. The shimmer out there wasn't heat-shimmer. Heat-shimmer wavered. This glittered, hard and rhythmic, like a thousand small mirrors all turning at once, and beneath it, faint as a heartbeat, came the sound of hooves.

*They are coming,* the stone said, and its warmth flickered, just slightly, like a candle in a draft. *The ones who signed us away. They keep excellent records, little key. Records are how they erased us, and records are how they hunt us. You must run now.*

"They'll follow us," Tam said. "Wherever we go."

*Yes.*

"Unless they stop following us."

The stone was quiet. In the quiet, Tam heard the hooves more clearly, and heard, underneath them, another sound — a cart, laboring, coming fast along the southern road. A fat man shouting at a mule.

*Ivo Renk,* the stone said, and something moved through its vastness that might have been sorrow. *He carries our map. He has carried it faithfully for thirty years, a man mapping a country that punished him for believing in it. Little key — some doors are held open by sacrifice. That is not a cruelty. That is simply the price of doors, and it is always paid willingly, or it does not count.*

"No," Tam said.

*Run,* said the stone, gently, and pushed him — not his body, but something deeper than his body — toward Ulyana, toward the gap in the wall, toward the grey line on the horizon where the world stopped pretending and the Hollow began.

Tam ran. But he looked back. Looking back was the first thing the Hollow had ever taught him, and he was not going to unlearn it now.

* * *

*Ulyana*

"Riders," Ulyana said. "Of course riders. Why would anything in my life ever be simple riders-free travel?"

She hauled Tam behind the tallest stretch of ruined wall and pressed her eye to a gap in the stones. Six horses, maybe seven, fanned out across the salt in the practiced spread of people who had done this many times and enjoyed it. Grey cloaks. The Ministry rode in grey because, Dain had once told her, drunk, in another life, *grey is the color of forgetting, and we wear our work.* She'd laughed then. She was not laughing now.

Seven riders. One mule. One scholar. Two fugitives, one of whom was currently glowing faintly, which was not ideal.

"Tam, listen to me." She crouched to his height and took his shoulders. "When I say run, you run for the gap in the wall, the tall stone, and you don't stop, and you don't look back, and you especially don't glow. Can you do that?"

Tam's jaw set in a way that was becoming familiar and that she already dreaded. "Ivo's coming," he said. "On the south road. Fast."

"I know. Ivo is drawing them off. That was the plan."

"It wasn't the plan. He's *making* it the plan." Tam's eyes were too old again, stone-old. "Ulyana. They'll take him."

"And if he blunders in here flapping his arms, they'll take all of us, and then nobody writes down what happened to anybody, and the Hollow stays erased forever, and Ivo's cheese dies unmourned." She heard her own voice go brittle and hated it. "He knew the risk. He *volunteered*. He said—" her throat closed briefly around it, "—he said someone had to hold the net open while the fish got through. His words. Terrible metaphor. Very Ivo."

The hoofbeats were closer. Close enough now that she could hear the riders calling to one another, crisp and bored, the way men sound when the quarry is a formality. *"Tracks by the third milestone — two sets, small and smaller — fan out, Kessel takes the wall—"*

And then, cutting across the flat like a trumpet through a funeral:

*"HO THERE! GENTLEMEN! EXCELLENT NEWS! YOU'VE FOUND ME!"*

Ulyana's heart did something complicated.

Ivo Renk came over the last rise in a cart pulled by a lathered, furious Turnip, waving both arms, his coat flying open, his spectacles flashing, looking for all the world like a man arriving late to his own coronation. "I am Ivo Renk! Cartographer! Scholar! *Delighted* to make your acquaintance! I have documents! I have *credentials!* I have a complaint about the state of these roads that I intend to file with whatever ministry handles complaints!"

The riders wheeled toward him. For one beautiful moment, all seven of them forgot about the wall.

"Go," Ulyana whispered. "Now. Run."

Tam ran. She ran beside him, which was against her own instructions and she did it anyway, because if the stone in the gap was going to do something, she wanted to be close enough to shove him through it. The black stone loomed up, still lit from within, its thousand names burning softly, and Tam reached it and turned and grabbed her hand —

and the stone *recognized her hand*, she felt it, a shock like cold water and like being called by her true name, and there was no time to wonder about that because behind them Ivo's voice rose to its full professorial register:

"—and furthermore, sir, if you examine my papers you will observe that I am traveling to the Congress of Cartographic Sciences in Pell's Crossing, which begins Thursday, and if you detain me you will be delaying the production of the new Imperial Road Atlas, for which I am *solely responsible*, and I wonder, Kessel, is it? — I wonder how the Ministry feels about delays to the *Imperial Road Atlas*—"

He was magnificent. He was ridiculous. He was buying them every second he could with the only currency he had, which was himself.

One rider didn't take the bait. Ulyana saw him — young, sharp-faced, too clever for the grey cloak he wore — swing wide of the little drama on the road and put his horse at the wall at a gallop, coming straight for the gap, straight for them, and she had time to think *not like this, not now, we are so close*—

—and then Ivo Renk did the bravest and stupidest thing she had ever seen a scholar do.

He turned the cart. He stood up in it, arms spread wide like a man greeting a sunrise, and drove Turnip directly into the rider's path, bellowing, "*THE ATLAS, SIR! THINK OF THE ATLAS!*"

Horse and cart met with a crack of wood and a scream that might have been Turnip or might have been the rider or might, Ulyana thought wildly, have been Ivo, still arguing. The sharp-faced rider went down. The cart went over. And six Ministry riders converged on the wreckage, dismounting, shouting, and among the tangle of wheel and cloak and scattered papers — papers, dozens of them, Ivo's life's work, blank maps and impossible maps and maps of places that no longer existed, scattering across the salt like enormous pale leaves —

among it all, Ivo Renk sat up, adjusted his cracked spectacles, looked directly at the gap in the wall, found Ulyana's eyes across four hundred yards of flat, and *tipped his hat.*

Then they pulled him to his feet and bound his hands, and he went on talking the entire time. She couldn't hear the words anymore. She could hear the tone: indignant, pedantic, utterly unbroken. As they led him away he walked with his chin up, a fat man in a ruined coat being marched to whatever the Ministry did with scholars, and the last thing she saw him do was glance down at the scattered maps and say something that made the nearest guard kick him, and he *laughed*.

"Ulyana." Tam's hand tightened on hers. His voice was very small. "He's gone."

"He's not gone." Her voice came out rougher than she intended. "Gone is a thing people decide. He's *taken*, which is temporary, and we are going to write that down somewhere permanent." She wiped her face with the heel of her hand, briskly, businesslike, the way you do when crying is inefficient. "Come on. The stone wants us through. Let's not insult it by dawdling."

They turned to the treaty-stone together, and the stone opened — not a door appearing, but a door *admitting*, the black surface going soft as dusk, the thousand names rearranging themselves into an archway, and beyond it, impossibly, green.

Green. After days of salt. Grass and hills and a river-light, and a road leading down into a country that every map in the world insisted was empty.

Tam stepped toward it without hesitation. Of course he did. It was his.

Ulyana stood at the threshold one moment longer, looking back at the salt flats, at the tiny knot of grey riders dwindling toward the horizon with their prisoner, at the scattered white maps beginning to bleach in the sun.

"Hold on, Ivo," she said quietly. "I'm coming back for you. I'm terrible at debts."

Then she took Tam's hand, and they stepped through, and the stone closed behind them like a book whose reader had finally returned.

Somewhere on the other side, faint and getting fainter, a child's voice was singing in a language older than kingdoms — and Ulyana realized, with a jolt that had nothing to do with fear, that she knew the next verse.
