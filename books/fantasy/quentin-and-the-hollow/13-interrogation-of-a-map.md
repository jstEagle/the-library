## 13. Interrogation of a Map

The cell had one window, and the window had been bricked over so thoroughly that it was less a window now than a rumor of one. Ivo Renk decided within the first hour that he would name it. He called it Gerald.

"You're smiling," said the inquisitor across the table. He was a broad man with a voice like a filing cabinet being closed politely. "I've interrogated smugglers, poisoners, and one man who ate a tax ledger. None of them smiled."

"I'm not any of those things," Ivo said. "I'm a cartographer. Smiling is part of the profession. You spend thirty years drawing coastlines that move when you're not looking, you develop either humor or madness, and the Ministry doesn't hire madmen."

"We arrested you at a treaty-stone in the company of a fugitive memory-thief and an undocumented child."

"Allegedly," Ivo said warmly. "And I'd dispute *fugitive*. She was walking briskly. There's a legal difference. Ask anyone from the salt country."

The inquisitor—his name was Vess, which Ivo found deliciously ironic and intended to use later—opened a ledger. "Let's begin simply. Where is the Hollow?"

"Ah," said Ivo, and folded his hands like a man settling in for a long evening with a good book. "What a question. Let's start with what we mean by *where*."

* * *

Two days' ride behind him, or perhaps three—the salt flats made liars of distance—Ulyana Zhou walked with her hood up and her boots wrapped in sacking to keep the crust from shredding them, and tried to remember why she had ever thought this plan had a middle part that worked.

Tam walked beside her, barefoot on the salt because he claimed—he wrote it in the dust with his finger, since his words came slowly and only in that old tongue she half-knew—that the salt didn't hurt him. It didn't seem to. The crystals parted around his small feet the way water parts around a prow, which Ulyana chose not to think about too hard, because thinking about it too hard meant admitting the child was less a child than a hinge, and hinges get broken by people like the ones chasing them.

"How far," she said, and then translated herself into gesture: two fingers walking across her palm.

Tam considered. He held up seven fingers, then folded down four, then looked apologetic about the whole business.

"Three days if the weather's kind." The weather was not going to be kind. The sky out here was the color of an old bruise going yellow at the edges, and the wind came off the flats carrying grit and a sound like someone whispering just below the threshold of hearing. Ulyana had heard that whisper before—in the dying smuggler's stolen memory, in the mad priest's. The flats remembered being the bottom of a sea that no map admitted to, and they were bitter about it.

She checked the bundle at her back for the hundredth time. Water, dried fish, the charm-stock she hadn't dared sell, and Ivo Renk's last gift to her: a single sheet of paper covered in his cramped, joyful handwriting. *Follow the whisper. When it gets loud enough to argue with, stop. You'll be standing where the door used to mind being found.*

"He'll hold them," she told Tam, more to hear a human voice than out of certainty. "He's impossible to hold. He once argued a border commission into redrawing itself out of embarrassment."

Tam patted her hand. Then he stopped, crouched, and pressed his palm flat against the salt.

Ulyana felt it a heartbeat later—a tremor underfoot, faint as a heartbeat through a wall. Far behind them, toward the border ruins, something went off. Not thunder. Thunder has an ending. This rolled on and on, flattening itself against the horizon.

"That's not us," she whispered. "That's not near us."

Tam looked up at her with eyes that were far too old for his face, and said one word in the old tongue. She knew enough of it now—three days of lessons in the saddle, the boy patient as a stone—to catch its shape: *they are asking him questions.*

* * *

"They are asking me questions," Ivo agreed pleasantly. "You've asked the same one eleven times. I admire your commitment. In cartography we call that surveying by repetition. It's how you find errors."

Vess's knuckles whitened around his pen. "Where. Is. The Hollow."

"Wonderful. Twelfth time. Let's do this properly." Ivo stood, because there was a patch of floor he'd mentally designated as chalkboard, and gestured at it as though diagrams hung there. "Question one: where is anything? Location, gentlemen—" there were two other Ministry men taking notes, and he'd named them privately as Sullen and Sullener— "is a relationship between objects. If I ask where Vessing is, you say 'north of the Marrow delta.' But the delta moved two miles east after the flood of the old king's forty-first year, which means every map printed before then is lying, politely, with great confidence. So: where is the Hollow? I can only answer relative to things. And here's my difficulty—"

He leaned forward, delighted.

"—the Hollow isn't relative to anything. That's rather the point of it. Asking me where the Hollow is located is like asking where Tuesday lives. You could burn me on a slow fire and I still couldn't give you coordinates, because coordinates are exactly what it hasn't got. It's the only honest country in the world. Every other nation lies about its borders. The Hollow refuses to have any."

"The treaty records say otherwise."

"Do they?" Ivo sat back down, folding himself into the chair with the contentment of a cat. "Do show me. I'd love to see the treaty. I've spent nine years trying to read it and it keeps—how shall I put this—sliding. Words go missing off the page. Not torn out. *Missing*, the way a word goes missing when you try to say a name you've been told to forget. Whoever drafted that document built forgetting into the ink. Magnificent work. I'd weep with envy if I weren't slightly terrified."

Sullen cleared his throat. "The prisoner is stalling."

"The prisoner," Ivo said, "is giving the finest geography lecture this Ministry has ever received free of charge, and you should be taking notes instead of glaring. Now. Since 'where' is off the table, let's try 'when,' which is much more interesting—"

* * *

The whisper got louder on the second day, exactly as Ivo had promised, and by noon it was indeed arguing.

It wasn't words. It was older than words, or younger—it was the sound a place makes when it's being erased and hasn't finished complaining. Ulyana walked into it and felt her stolen memories stir like leaves in wind. The smuggler's fragment: *white salt, white door, sing low or it won't know you.* The priest's: *the sea left but the door stayed, doors always stay, that's the tragedy of doors.*

"Sing low," she murmured, and Tam looked at her sharply, and then he began to hum.

It was the lullaby. Of course it was. He'd hummed it in the cellar in Vessing, in the cart, in the ruins; it seemed to be the one thing the erasure couldn't take from him, maybe because he *was* the thing the erasure couldn't take. His voice was small and cracked and absolutely certain, and the whispering flats quieted around it the way a room quiets around a sleeping baby.

Ulyana joined in without deciding to. Her voice found a harmony line she had no memory of learning, and the wrongness of that—knowing a song you never learned, in a language you never spoke—sat in her chest like a swallowed coal. She kept singing anyway. Some knowledge you don't need provenance for.

They walked until the light went the color of honey and the salt under their feet changed. Not gradually. One step was salt, glittering and dead, and the next step rang—actually *rang*, faintly, like porcelain—and Tam stopped and pointed.

Ahead, maybe half a mile, the flats rose into a low ridge, and in the ridge was a shape.

A doorway. Free-standing, white as bone, no wall attached to either side, standing alone in the middle of nowhere with the sunset pouring through its empty frame.

Ulyana's legs nearly gave out. Not from fear. From recognition—some deep, unaccountable recognition, the way you recognize a house from a dream. She had never seen this door. She was sure she had never seen this door. And some part of her, some part older than her certainty, was already home.

"Not yet," she told Tam, whose feet were already moving. "Not in the dark. We camp. We eat. Tomorrow, we knock."

Tam gave her a look of pure six-year-old outrage at the concept of *waiting*, and she laughed for the first time in three days, and somewhere very far behind them a man in a cell was doing the same thing for entirely different reasons.

* * *

"—and that concludes the lesson on temporal borders," Ivo said. "Questions?"

Vess had stopped writing an hour ago. He was staring at Ivo with the expression of a man who has caught himself enjoying something forbidden. "You realize none of this answers our questions."

"All of it answers your questions. Just not the ones you asked. That's the trouble with interrogations—you lot come in with your questions pre-written, and reality declines to follow the agenda." Ivo stretched until his spine popped. Three days in this cell and they'd fed him twice, which was fine; he'd done fieldwork on less. What was less fine was the growing silence from the corridor, the sense of the building holding its breath. Something was happening elsewhere in the Ministry. Riders leaving, he'd wager. Fast ones, heading for the salt flats.

Good. That meant Ulyana was ahead. That meant the plan was working, insofar as a plan assembled in a cartographer's attic from tea leaves and stubbornness could be said to *work*.

"So," he said brightly. "Shall we revisit 'where'? Because I've thought of a riddle that will make it much clearer."

Vess sat down heavily. "I will regret this."

"Undoubtedly. Here it is." Ivo held up one finger. "A country signs itself away in a treaty. The treaty is written in ink that forgets. The signatories forget signing. The maps forget drawing. But—" he leaned in, savoring it— "the *salt* remembers being seabed. The *door* remembers being a door. Memory, gentlemen, is like water: you can dam it, divert it, sell it by the cupful, but it finds the low ground eventually. So my riddle is this: when a whole country is forgotten, where does all that remembering *go*?"

Silence. Sullener's pen had stopped.

"It doesn't evaporate," Ivo said softly, all the performance gone out of his voice now, and what remained was the scholar, the exile, the man who had spent nine years loving a place the world had been robbed of. "It pools. It waits in the low places. In salt. In children. In lullabies nobody remembers learning. Your Ministry burned the maps and called it peace, but you didn't destroy a country—you *buried it alive*, and everything buried pushes up. That's where the Hollow is. It's wherever the remembering gathers. And right now, tonight, it is gathering very fast, in a place you cannot reach in time, and the only person in this building who knows exactly where—"

He spread his hands, smiling Gerald's smile at the bricked-over window.

"—is a map. And you can't torture a map into lying, Minister. A map's whole virtue is that it tells the truth whether you want it or not."

Vess stood. For a long moment he said nothing. Then, quietly, almost gently: "The riders left at dawn. Fourteen of them. They'll reach the flats tomorrow midday."

Ivo's stomach dropped through the floor and kept going.

"If your friends reach their door," Vess continued, gathering his papers, "they'll want a head start. I count cells at midnight. I miscount sometimes. I'm told the eastern drainage tunnel floods in rain, and it looks like rain." He paused at the door. "I was born in Lowmarket, Renk. Before the Ministry posted me. I remember a shop that sold charms. I remember a woman in the window. I was told I remember nothing of the kind." He didn't turn around. "Tell your thief the Ministry's arithmetic is worse than its geography."

The door shut. Ivo sat alone with Gerald and the sound of distant thunder that was not thunder, and did the fastest calculations of his life.

Fourteen riders. One flooded tunnel. Two days of walking ahead of them, if the flats were kind, and the flats had never once been kind in recorded history.

"Well," he said to the bricked-over window, to the pooled dark beyond it, to a country that technically did not exist. "Best start swimming."

He began, quietly, to hum a lullaby he had no memory of learning, and waited for midnight, and the rain.
