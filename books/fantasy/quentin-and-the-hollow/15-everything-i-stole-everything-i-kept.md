## 15. Everything I Stole, Everything I Kept

The door did not care that she was crying.

That was the first thing Ulyana understood about it, standing in the salt wind with Tam's small hand buried in hers: the door was not cruel, and it was not kind. It was honest, which was worse. It stood in the middle of the flats like a doorway someone had set up in a field for no reason at all—no wall around it, no roof above it, just a frame of grey wood gone silver with age, and through it nothing but more salt flat, shimmering.

Except the nothing behind it was a *listening* nothing. She could feel it attending to her the way a moneylender attends to a debtor's footsteps.

"Again," Tam said. His voice had grown stronger every day since the border ruins, as if each mile of salt they'd crossed had been a spoonful of broth fed to him from some invisible hand. "Tell it again."

"I've told it everything I have." Her voice came out scraped. "Twice."

"Then you missed something." He said it without accusation, the way children say true things. He let go of her hand and walked to the doorframe and put his palm flat against the wood, and the wood *warmed*—she saw it happen, saw the silver grain flush faintly gold under his fingers like a banked fire remembering itself. The door liked him. The door was starving for him. Somewhere inside that listening nothing, a whole country was holding its breath over a mute child who now would not stop speaking.

"Tam," she said carefully, "what does it feel like?"

"Like when you're almost asleep," he said, "and someone says your name."

*And what does it feel like to me?* she thought. It felt like standing outside a house where a party was happening, holding a gift she couldn't find in her pockets no matter how many times she checked them.

She checked her pockets again anyway. Metaphorically speaking. This was the work of the last hour: sitting down in the salt with her skirts spread out like a merchant's tarp, closing her eyes, and ransacking herself.

Because here was the ugly arithmetic of it. The door admitted those who carried a true memory of the Hollow. Not bought ones—she had tried offering it the smuggler's dying recollection of white towers, the mad priest's fragment about singing stones, even the widow's purchased scrap of a wedding held under two moons. The door took each offering, turned it over like a jeweler assessing paste, and gave it back. *Borrowed goods,* its silence seemed to say. *We know stolen stock when we see it.* Tam carried thousands of true memories—he was, Ivo had said before the riders took him, less a boy than an archive wearing a boy—and so the door all but purred at his touch. Ulyana carried twelve years of inventory and not one grain of the country herself.

Or so she had believed.

But there had been a moment—that was why she kept searching—the moment on the flats when Tam had started humming. A tune without words, wandering and sweet, the kind of melody that sounds like it's remembering something. And Ulyana's mouth had moved before her mind caught up, and she had harmonized. Three notes. Perfectly. In a mode she had never learned.

She had felt it in her throat like a fishhook.

So now she sat in the salt and went through herself shelf by shelf, the way she'd taught apprentices—back when having apprentices was a thing a person could do—in the old days of the trade. *Start at the front of the shop,* she told herself. *Work backward.*

The front shelves were easy and useless. Yesterday's breakfast. The weight of Tam asleep against her shoulder on the cart. Dain Marrow's face in her doorway three weeks ago, asking gentle questions with professionally emptied eyes—no, she flinched away from that shelf; it was stocked too densely, and every jar on it was sharp. The morning she'd sold her own memory of him to buy their passage. That one was strange to hold now, like touching a scar that didn't hurt: she knew the transaction had happened, could see the buyer's ledger in her mind's eye, but the memory itself was gone, sold, and what remained was only the receipt. Grief with the grief removed. It should have been a mercy. It felt like a room with the furniture taken out but the outlines still printed on the floorboards in dust.

*Keep working backward.*

Her first theft, aged nineteen: lifting a customs officer's memory of his mother's kitchen and selling it to a homesick dockworker. Her training under old Sefa Voss, who smelled of cloves and had hands like a surgeon and rules like scripture—*never take what you can't carry, never sell what you can't account for, and never, girl, ever steal from yourself.* Sefa had said that last one looking at her strangely, Ulyana remembered now, the way you look at someone standing on ice you suspect is thinner than they think.

Backward still. Sixteen. Fourteen. The tenement rooms, the hunger that had a shape and a schedule, her mother—

Her mother.

Ulyana stopped.

Here was the thing about her childhood: it was thin. She had always known this and had never examined it, because examining it felt like pressing on a bruise to check whether it was real. Other people's childhoods, the ones she stole for a living, were crowded things—rooms full of siblings and dogs and summers, attics stuffed with junk and joy. Hers was a sparsely furnished flat. Mother, yes. The lullaby—there it was, the lullaby, she could hear it if she listened sideways, humming along in a language she'd assumed was just her mother being odd, her mother who sang songs from nowhere and claimed they were from her own mother, who was dead, whose name changed depending on how much wine was in the telling. Her mother who had flinched at maps. Her mother who had once, drunk and weeping after a funeral Ulyana was never told the details of, gripped her daughter's wrists and said: *If anyone ever asks where you're from, say Vessing. Say you were born in Vessing. Promise me. Promise me like it's money.*

She had promised. She had been seven.

And then—here was the shelf she had somehow never catalogued, hidden at the back of the shop behind the winter coats, dusty and unpriced—then there was the gap. Between her eighth year and her eleventh, Ulyana Zhou had three years of childhood that were not missing exactly. They were *smooth*. Like a river stone. Like something that had been handled so much it lost its features. She had a vague sense of a gray house and a woman who was not her mother and lessons in penmanship, and underneath the smoothness, when she pressed, a sensation like standing on a bridge over very deep water.

Thieves know what stolen goods look like. Thieves know what a fence's paperwork looks like. Ulyana had spent her whole life reading the provenance of other people's memories, and she had never once read her own.

"Oh," she said aloud, to the salt, to the door, to nobody. "Oh, you *idiot*."

Tam looked up from the doorframe. "What?"

"I didn't lose my childhood." She was laughing, or something adjacent to laughing, with tears going down her face while she did it, which is a thing laughter and grief do together when they've known each other long enough. "I didn't *lose* it. Someone took it. Someone took it and—" and the rest arrived all at once, the whole terrible elegant joke of her life arriving in a single delivery, "—and *sold it*. My own mother sold my memories of the Hollow to keep me forgettable enough to survive. Or the Ministry took them and she ran. Either way—" She pressed her palms against her eyes. "Either way, somewhere out there, or somewhere *in here*, my childhood exists as merchandise. I have been in the trade for twenty years and I never thought to check the market for my own life."

"Would it be there?" Tam asked. "After all this time?"

Memories didn't rot. That was the first law of the trade. They faded at the edges, they yellowed like paper, but a true memory, properly kept, outlasted its owner. Somewhere, possibly, a buyer or a buyer's heir or a Ministry vault held a little girl running through streets that officially did not exist, singing a song in a language that officially had never been spoken.

It didn't matter. She couldn't get to any vault. What she could do was the thing Sefa had forbidden, the one rule, the deepest rule:

Steal from yourself.

Not steal, precisely. *Recover.* Somewhere beneath the smooth river-stone years, beneath the gray house and the penmanship lessons, the original goods might still be cached—because fences were lazy, because erasure was cheaper than extraction, because whoever had done this to her had likely smoothed over the surface rather than digging out the foundation. You didn't demolish a wall to take a painting. You hung another painting over it.

Ulyana knelt in the salt, closed her eyes, and went to the back of her own shop.

She found the gray house first. Walked into it, in the way of memory-work, room by room. Penmanship lessons—a woman with cold hands praising her loops. Meals eaten in silence. A bed she was locked into at night, gently, kindly, the lock explained as being *for her protection*, and the explanation repeated so many times it wore a groove. And behind the groove, behind the repetition, the way a thief finds the safe behind the painting: pressure. Something heavy leaned against this memory from the other side. Something had been pushed in front of it and the pushing had left marks.

She pushed back.

The gray house resisted. Of course it did—it wasn't a wall, it was a *seal*, and seals were made to be stronger than the goods they guarded. But seals were made by people, and people got tired, and this seal had been holding for thirty years against a mind that spent every day of those thirty years learning exactly how memories were built and therefore exactly how they came apart.

She found the seam on the third try. There was always a seam. Sefa used to say God made memories with seams because God had a sense of humor and it was mostly at thieves' expense.

Ulyana put her whole self against the seam and pulled.

* * *

Salt. Wind. A harbor with no ships in it, because the ships had all been requisitioned, and her mother saying *don't look at the water, look at me.* Fireflies in a jar that weren't fireflies, they were *sparks of the boundary*, her mother said, whatever that meant, and seven-year-old Ulyana hadn't cared what anything meant because the sparks glowed green-gold and if you held the jar up to your ear you could hear—singing. Faint. A whole country's worth of singing, coming through the glass like sound through a wall.

Her grandmother's house, the one with the crooked chimney, the one that stood in a town called—she knew the name, she *knew* it, it rose in her like a bubble through deep water and broke the surface—called Vellichor Row, in a city called Ammerhast, in a country called—

The Hollow.

The word didn't hurt to think. That was the miracle of it. The Hollow, the Hollow, the Hollow, three times like knocking on a door, and nothing in her flinched, because these memories had been sealed away *from the inside of her*, protected, not erased—her mother hadn't let them be destroyed, she'd hidden them, hidden them so well that even the Ministry's census-burners and map-eaters had never found them, a smuggling job worthy of the finest thief in Vessing, and the finest thief in Vessing had been a mother with a seven-year-old daughter and a jar of boundary-sparks and one boat leaving on the last night.

More came, faster now, the seal giving way all along its length like ice going out on a river:

Her grandmother's kitchen, smelling of caraway. The two moons over the rooftops—one white, one pale green, everyone in Ammerhast complaining cheerfully about the green one making everyone look seasick at festivals. Her grandfather's map shop, where the maps showed the Hollow in confident ink, rivers and roads and a coastline, because *of course* they did, how else would you draw a country? The neighbors. The names. Maren who kept bees. Old Tull who argued with the well. Children playing a game with knucklebones and rhymes in the Hollow tongue, and Ulyana—small Ulyana, gap-toothed, furious, magnificent—winning, because she always won, some things about a person are load-bearing.

And her mother, young then, singing the lullaby while she packed a single bag, and stopping in the middle of the verse to cry without noise, the way you learn to cry when you live in a country that has just been signed away at a table in a capital far away by men who needed its existence to pay a debt.

*We're going on a trip,* her mother had said. *A long trip. And when we come back—*

They had never come back. Because on the far side of the border, in the gray house, in the country that existed, the price of safety had been forgetting, and her mother had paid it twice—once for the girl, sealing the goods deep where no census-taker would dig, and once for herself, drinking down the official forgetting like medicine, until she was a woman who hummed a tune she didn't know and flinched at maps and couldn't say why.

*Exile was the theft,* Ulyana thought, kneeling in the salt with tears freezing on her cheeks. *I've been in exile my whole life. From my own country. From my own self. And the thief who did it loved me.*

She opened her eyes.

Tam was crouched in front of her, watching with an expression she recognized from mirrors: the careful face of a child waiting to find out how bad the news is.

"I remember," she said. Her voice broke on the second word and she let it. "Tam. I remember. I'm from there. I'm *from* the Hollow. Ammerhast, Vellichor Row, my grandfather sold maps—" She laughed, wet and disbelieving. "My family sold *maps*. We drew countries for a living and then the world decided ours wasn't one anymore."

"The door," Tam said, and tugged her sleeve, urgent now, pointing.

The doorframe was burning.

Not with fire—with light, gold rising through the silver grain like dawn coming up through fog, and the air inside the frame, which had shown nothing but shimmering salt-flat all this time, was showing something else. Grass. A road. Two moons hanging in a bright afternoon sky, the green one faint as a watermark. And a smell came through, impossible, ridiculous, devastating: woodsmoke and caraway and rain on warm stone.

The smell of her grandmother's street.

Ulyana got up off her knees. Her legs shook. She took one step toward the door and the light *brightened*, leaning toward her like a plant toward sun, and she heard—not with her ears, with the place behind her sternum where the lullaby lived—a voice like many voices, like a congregation, like a country clearing its throat after thirty years of silence:

*Welcome home, Ulyana of Ammerhast. Daughter of Ilse. Granddaughter of the mapmaker. Your account is open. Your key is cut. Come in out of the wind.*

"It knows me," she whispered. "Tam, it knows my *name*."

"Of course it does." Tam smiled, and for a moment he looked older than any child, older than the salt and the sky, like something ancient wearing a boy as a coat. "You were always one of us. You just didn't have the receipts."

She reached for the doorframe.

Her fingers were an inch from the wood when the light stuttered.

Just once. Just for a heartbeat, the gold guttered like a candle in a draft, and through the frame Ulyana saw the grass-road flicker grey at its edges, saw one of the moons blink out and come back, and heard beneath the welcome-hum a sound she knew intimately from her trade: the sound of a memory running low. Running out.

Behind her, far across the salt, a line of dust rose against the horizon. Riders. Coming fast.

"Hold the door open," Ulyana said, already turning, already calculating distances the way she once calculated escape routes out of paying customers' houses. "Whatever happens—whatever you hear—hold it open, Tam. We're going home. Both of us. Tonight."

The riders crested the ridge.
