## 16. Inside the Erased

Tam had never been anywhere that was forgetting itself before.

He knew what it looked like when a person forgot. He had watched it happen to the smuggler whose last hours Ulyana kept in a little glass bottle — no, that wasn't right, he hadn't watched that, he had *been* that, or part of him had been, the way you could be a raindrop and also be the river. Memory worked like that inside him. He carried thousands of lives the way other children carried pebbles in their pockets, and most days he could not tell which of them were his.

But a country forgetting — that was new. That was enormous. It felt like standing inside a mouth while it lost the word for *tongue*.

The door had closed behind them with a sound like a promise being kept, and then there was the Hollow.

It was evening there, or something that remembered evening. The sky was the color of the inside of a shell, and it went on much farther than skies usually bothered to. Streets unrolled from the threshold in long pale ribbons, cobbled with stones that had been loved so thoroughly they had gone smooth as bread crusts. Lamplighters were moving down the lanes on ladders, lighting lamps that did not need lighting, because the light was the only thing holding some of the corners up.

"Stay close," Ulyana said, and her voice came out strange, flattened at the edges, like a coin rubbed too many times.

Tam stayed close. He always stayed close. But he also listened, because listening was what he was for.

The city was whispering.

Not people-whispering. *Street*-whispering. He put his palm flat against a wall as they passed and felt it: a low murmur of names, of market-day prices, of somebody's grandmother's recipe for plum brandy, all of it going soft and grey at the borders like a letter left out in the rain. Where the grey reached, things stopped being quite themselves. A fountain stood in a square, but its water fell upward, uncertain which way down was anymore. A cat sat on a windowsill watching them with eyes like two holes cut in the world — not because the cat was frightening, but because the cat had forgotten its own color and was embarrassed about it.

*Hello,* Tam thought at it, in the old tongue, the one that lived in his chest like a second heartbeat.

The cat's ears pricked. Its eyes filled back in — brown, then gold, then brown again — and it yawned enormously, showing its opinion of the whole situation.

Ulyana didn't hear any of it. She walked like a woman walking through someone else's dream, touching doorframes, counting windows under her breath. She had almost nothing of the Hollow in her, Tam knew. Just one small song, buried so deep even she couldn't find it. He wanted to tell her it was there. He didn't know how yet. Words were still arriving in him one crate at a time.

They followed the lamplighters. There didn't seem to be anything else to follow.

* * *

The people came out when the lamps were lit.

They came out slowly, the way furniture comes out of a house that has been shut up for winter, and Tam understood immediately that this was not rudeness. They were *spending*. Every step into the open cost them something. An old man leaned in his doorway and his doorway leaned back, and between them they held each other up by sheer familiarity. A woman was watering a garden that grew memories instead of flowers — Tam could see them, little glassy bulbs nodding on their stems, each one glowing faintly with somebody's summer afternoon.

"Visitors," the old man said, and the word wobbled in his mouth like a tooth. "We haven't had visitors since—" He stopped. Thought. His brow creased and uncreased. "Since a while. A good while. Since the treaty." He said *treaty* the way you'd say a curse, carefully, spitting distance away.

"You know why we've come," Ulyana said.

"No," said the old man pleasantly. "But we're very glad you've come, whatever it is. Come in. Sit. You look hungry, and we have soup."

"We have soup," agreed three other voices, from three different doorways, in perfect unison, and Tam got the distinct feeling that the soup was less a food than an idea everyone was sharing around to keep it alive.

Ulyana's hand found Tam's shoulder. Her fingers were cold. "How do you have soup?" she asked. "Your country was—"

"Traded away," the old man said. "Yes. Signed off like a debt at a funeral. We know. We were there, mostly." He smiled, and the smile was the saddest thing Tam had ever seen, sadder than the grey streets, because it was *practiced*. "You can't kill a country by paperwork, miss. You can only make it forget itself slowly. And we are not entirely without resources." He tapped his temple. "Borrowed recollection. We trade among ourselves. I have my neighbor's childhood; she has my wedding day. Between us we manage a sort of general past. It's patchwork, but it's warm."

"That's terrible," Ulyana whispered.

"It's hospitality," the old man said, with dignity. "Come in. Soup."

And that was how Tam learned that the Hollow's last inhabitants had turned memory-sharing into a kind of dinner party, and that the dinner party had been going on for twelve years, and that nobody had been allowed to leave the table.

* * *

The house they were brought to was large and crooked and full of light that came from nowhere in particular. Inside, perhaps forty people sat around a long table, and every place setting had a candle, and every candle burned a different color, and Tam realized with a jolt that the candles were memories too — set alight just to be seen, just to prove they still existed somewhere outside someone's head.

A woman at the head of the table rose. She was small and round and fierce-eyed, with flour on her hands despite the fact that, as far as Tam could tell, nothing in the Hollow had been baked in years.

"I am Maret Voss," she announced. "I am — let me see — I am currently the mayor, the schoolteacher, and the entire choir. The actual holders of those offices forgot themselves in the spring, poor souls, and their jobs came to me, as jobs do here. Sit. Eat. You must be the key."

The word landed in Tam's chest like a stone dropped down a well.

"The key," Ulyana repeated flatly.

"The child," Maret Voss said, looking straight at Tam with eyes that were kind and hungry in exactly equal measure. "Born of the erasure, carrying the country's whole voice. We felt him come through the door like feeling the sun come through a window. Didn't we?" she asked the table, and the table murmured agreement, and several candles flickered brighter, and Tam understood that they had been waiting for him the way a lock waits.

"He's a child," Ulyana said. "Not a key. Not a tool. He's seven."

"He's our country," Maret Voss said gently. "Or the last living piece of one. There's a difference, miss, though I grant it's a fine one."

Tam tugged Ulyana's sleeve and spoke — his words came slow, in the old tongue, and he watched her frown as half of it reached her: *"Ask them what anchoring means."*

She asked.

Maret Voss's face did something complicated, the way weather does.

"Ah," she said. "Straight to business. Well. The country is going grey at the edges — you'll have seen. Every year the grey takes more. In another year it takes the center. In two, there is no Hollow at all, only a blank spot on a map and a treaty nobody remembers signing. Unless." She spread her floury hands. "Unless the country is re-anchored. Given a heart again. A living heart, rooted here, beating in time with everything we are. The key can do that. The key was made for that — not made, grown, if you like, the way a pearl grows around a wound. The erasure made him out of itself. He can hold us. All of us. Forever."

"And the cost," Ulyana said. It wasn't a question. Tam admired that about her. She always asked the price first, having spent her life on both sides of the transaction.

Maret Voss looked at her for a long moment. Then, softly:

"A life freely given. Anchored here. Always here. The key becomes the country, and the country keeps the key. He would live — oh, longer than you'd think, countries are patient — but he would never leave. Never walk beyond that door. Never see your grey city, your markets, your sky that ends where it should." Her voice caught, just slightly, on the hospitality of it. "He would be loved. You cannot imagine how loved. Forty of us, and every street, and every stone, pouring everything we have into him so that he can pour it back. He would want for nothing except elsewhere."

The silence after that was very long.

Tam looked at the table. At the colored candles. At the old man who had lent out his wedding day and the woman growing summers in her garden. They were all looking at him, and their looking was the warmest thing he had ever felt, and that was exactly what frightened him. It was a trap built entirely out of kindness. No bars. No chains. Just forty people who needed him so much that their need had become a room with no doors.

*They love me already,* he thought, surprised. *They've loved me since the door. That's how they say please.*

Ulyana had gone white. Her hand was on his shoulder again, gripping now. "No," she said. "Absolutely not. Find another way. There's always another way."

"There isn't," said Maret Voss, with terrible gentleness, "but please, have some soup while we discuss it."

* * *

Later — after the soup, which tasted like somebody's memory of soup, which was somehow better than soup — Tam slipped out into the crooked garden while the adults argued in low voices.

The grey was closer here. He could see it at the far edge of the garden, a softness in the air, like the world giving up on describing itself. A swing hung from a tree, and the tree was forgetting how to be a tree, its leaves going translucent at the tips. Beyond the grey, Tam could feel the Hollow's edges straining — a whole country pressing its palms against the inside of its own skin, asking to be let stay.

He knelt and pressed both hands to the earth.

The earth spoke to him. Not in words. In *inventory*: the smell of bread from four hundred bakeries, the sound of a river that used to run where Vessing's canal now pretended to, the name of every dog that had ever been good. It was so much. It was so much, and it was leaking out of the world at the seams, and he was the jar it had been poured into, and they were asking him to be the shelf the jar sat on forever.

*I could,* he thought. And found, to his own surprise, that it was true. He could. He loved them already, these borrowed-memory strangers with their soup and their candles. That was the trap and the truth of it both.

But then he thought of Ulyana. Of her shop that smelled of dust and lavender, of the way she counted coins twice and lied about prices badly, of the night she opened a door she should never have opened because a barefoot child was standing behind it. If he anchored himself here, she would walk out alone. She would carry him in her pockets the way he carried everyone else — a memory, a charm, a weight.

And he thought, further, of the man in her stolen mornings. The one whose name she never said aloud but whose laugh lived in her like a splinter. Tam had seen him from the inside of her kept things. Dain. Even the Hollow knew that name; the door had hummed it when they passed.

There were footsteps behind him. Maret Voss, come to stand beside him in the failing light, flour still on her hands.

"It's a hard thing we ask," she said quietly. "I won't pretend it isn't. But child — do you know what we are? We are everyone who was ever told they didn't exist. We are the proof that they lied. If we go grey, the lie wins forever, and worse — no one will ever even know there was a lie." She knelt beside him, joints cracking, and looked at the grey with him. "You are the only 'no' left to say to it."

Tam looked at the swing. At the translucent leaves. At the town behind them, lit with forty candles' worth of stubbornness.

Then, at the edge of the garden, where the grey thinned the world to gauze, he saw something that made his breath stop.

Light. Far off, beyond the border, out in the salt flats — torchlight. Many torches. Moving fast, in formation, the way men move when they are ordered and afraid.

And behind them, smaller, alone, riding like the wind itself had been insulted and meant to file a complaint — one more rider, wearing no colors at all, leaning over his horse's neck as if the horse were the only thing between him and something he could not bear to lose.

Tam did not know how he knew. He simply knew, the way he knew all the things in all the pockets of all the people he carried.

*Dain.*

He scrambled up and ran for the house, shouting in the old tongue, words tumbling out of him faster than they had ever come — and Ulyana met him in the doorway, and whatever she saw in his face made her go still as glass.

"They've found us," he managed, in her language, his first whole sentence in it. "All of them. And one of them is yours."
