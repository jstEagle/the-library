## 17. The Hollow's Last Bargain

The council of the Hollow met in a bakery.

That was the first thing Ulyana could not get over. Twelve years of imagining the erased country as something grand and terrible—a nation of ghosts, a parliament of the forgotten—and instead she was standing in a low-ceilinged room that smelled of yeast and cardamom, watching seven elderly people argue about chairs while a kettle went cold on a stove that was only half there.

The stove was the problem. Half of it existed, in the way that things existed here: solid where someone remembered it, grey and suggestion-shaped where they didn't. The left burner held the kettle. The right burner had been forgotten by everyone who'd ever used it, and so it had gone out of the world, and now the kettle sat slightly askew, leaning toward existence like a drunk toward a lamppost.

"Sit," said the eldest of them, a woman with flour to her elbows and eyes the color of tea gone cold. "You've been walking for days. Arguing can happen sitting down."

"I'm fine standing."

"You're swaying standing." The woman—her name was Maret, and she baked bread from a recipe no one outside this room could remember, which meant the bread itself was one of the last true things the Hollow still produced—pulled out a chair for her. The chair wobbled on legs that were thinner than chairs' legs ought to be. Everything here was thin. Not the people—the people were sturdy, stubborn, alive—but the *stuff* of the place. Walls you could almost see through if you didn't look directly. A window whose view of an orchard flickered when the last man who remembered the orchard dozed off in his chair.

Tam sat down immediately, because Tam was eight and had better sense than she did. He took bread. He ate it with the solemnity of a boy who understood, in some pre-verbal way, that eating a country's bread was how you told a country you intended to stay a while.

Ulyana remained standing. Old habit. In Vessing, you never sat in a room until you knew where the exits were.

She knew where the exits were here too. That was the trouble. There was exactly one, and it was a door made of other people's love, and it was starving.

* * *

They let her sleep first. That was the second thing she couldn't get over. She had expected demands, bargains struck at knifepoint, the terrible hospitality of myth. Instead Maret fed her soup, showed her to a room above the bakery with a bed that held its shape only because Maret's late husband had slept in it for forty years, and said, "In the morning. Nothing is decided at night except mistakes."

Ulyana slept anyway, because her body had stopped taking her opinion on the matter somewhere around the salt flats.

She dreamed of Vessing. Of the shop, the little jars, the widow Ostrova coming in every month to buy back a memory of her own wedding, paying good coin each time because the charm wore off and she preferred the charm to the truth—the truth being that her husband had died two winters ago and the wedding was forty-one years behind her. Ulyana had never told her. It wasn't kindness, exactly. It was commerce wearing kindness's coat. But lying awake in the grey hours before dawn, in a country that did not exist, she found herself wondering whether the widow would rather have the grief than the rental.

Then she wondered why she was wondering that, and got up.

* * *

The council convened around the half-stove at midmorning. Seven of them, plus Tam, plus her. The seventh member was a man named Pell who spent most meetings staring out the flickering orchard window, keeping it alive through sheer attention, and contributed nothing else, and nobody minded, because his contribution was the orchard.

"You've seen the edges," Maret began, without preamble. "Coming in. Tell me what you saw."

"Grey," Ulyana said. "Streets going soft at the borders. Like wet ink."

"Wet ink." Maret nodded as if Ulyana had paid her a compliment. "That's generous. Last spring it was more like breath on glass. We are running out of country, thief. Not quickly—we have gotten very good at slow—but certainly. Every year the map of us gets smaller, and every year fewer people remember enough of us to hold the middle in place." She turned her teacup in its saucer. The saucer was real. The cup was mostly not; Ulyana could see the table through its rim. "A country is not land. Land doesn't care. A country is agreement. Enough people agreeing, hard enough, for long enough. And we are running out of agreers."

"So anchor him." Ulyana kept her voice flat. She had known this was coming since the door recognized Tam. Since before that, honestly—since the smuggler's dying memories, since the priest's mad ones, since the child appeared at her door speaking a language older than kingdoms. "He's the key. You said it yourselves. He carries thousands of memories of this place—he's a living archive. Anchor him here and the Hollow holds."

Maret looked at her for a long moment with those cold-tea eyes, and Ulyana understood that the woman was being kind, and hated her for it.

"That is one path," Maret said gently. "Yes."

"And the other?"

"The other is you."

The kettle chose that moment to come briefly into full existence—someone in the next room must have remembered it fondly—and then sagged again. Nobody moved to pour tea. There was, Ulyana suspected, a question of whether pouring was possible.

"I'm not from here," she said. "I'm a thief from Vessing. I steal memories and sell them back to people as charms. I am possibly the least anchored person in any world."

"You were born under a lullaby your mother sang in our tongue," said Pell, without turning from the window. "The door knew you. Doors don't make mistakes; doors are the most honest architecture there is. You're from here, girl. You're just from here *badly*."

"But that's not what we're asking," Maret cut in, and her voice changed—not gentler, but plainer, the voice of a woman who had decided that cruelty delivered cleanly was kinder than cruelty wrapped in wool. "The door out of the Hollow—the one you came in through, the one that stands open because ten thousand people loved this place enough to hold it ajar—that door runs on memory. True memory. Given freely. It has been running on dregs for twelve years, on whatever fragments wander in, and it is nearly empty, and when it closes, it closes forever, and everything inside it goes with it. Including the boy. Including us. Including, eventually, the forgetting that leaks out of us into your world and eats anyone who remembers."

"We know about the eating," Ulyana said. "We ran from it across the flats."

"Then you know it isn't malice. It's hunger. An unpaid debt collects interest wherever it can." Maret set down the ghost-cup. "The door needs feeding. A great deal, all at once, or the boy anchors here and stays forever. Those are the choices. I won't dress them up."

"Feed it what?"

And Maret looked at her, and Pell finally turned from his orchard, and the whole room went quiet in the particular way rooms go quiet when everyone present already knows the answer and is waiting to see how long it takes you.

"Your memories," Maret said, "of the person you love most."

* * *

Ulyana laughed.

It came out wrong—too loud, too sharp, bouncing off walls that weren't entirely sure they wanted to be walls—and Tam looked up from his bread with alarm, and she put a hand over her mouth like a child caught swearing.

"No," she said. "No, you've got the wrong woman. I don't—" She stopped. Started again, more carefully, the way she walked on ice. "I'm a memory-thief. Do you understand what that means? I don't keep things. Keeping things is the one trade I never learned. I have sold weddings, funerals, first kisses, mothers' faces. My stock is other people's lives. What I've kept of my own wouldn't fill a thimble, and none of it is—"

"His name is Dain," said the youngest councilor, a round-faced man named Osk who kept the census of the Hollow, such as it was—a ledger written in pencil, because ink was too permanent for their current circumstances.

The room tilted.

"How—" Her voice came out very small. "How do you know that name? I never said that name. I haven't said that name in twelve years. I don't say it. It's not—I don't—"

"He's here," Osk said simply. "Or rather, the echoes are. When you sell a memory, thief, where do you think it goes? You lift it out of a head and press it into a charm, and the charm sells, and the buyer wears it down like soap, and the sloughs off into the world. Memory doesn't die. It diffuses. And this country—this country is the lowest ground in creation. Everything forgotten drains downhill to us. Every charm you ever sold, every memory you ever stole and traded away—it all seeps here eventually, the way water finds a cellar." He tapped his pencil-ledger. "Your Dain is all over my books. Fragments. Echoes. A morning in a kitchen, twelve years ago—you sold that one at the border town, yes? To a carter who wanted to feel loved once before he died? It's here. Most of it. Worn at the edges, but here. A man laughing with flour on his hands. A man saying your name in a tone he never used for anyone else. A man choosing, over and over, in a hundred small ways, to be yours."

"Stop," Ulyana said.

"A man who is, right now, at this moment, according to the drift of things—because news travels strangely here, downhill like everything else—stealing a ledger from his own Ministry and riding for the salt flats to find you." Osk said it without drama, the way you'd report weather. "Duty is a heavy horse, but love rides lighter."

"*Stop.*"

She hadn't meant to shout. Tam had gone white. Maret had risen halfway out of her chair. And Ulyana stood in the middle of the bakery of a dead country with her heart going like a trapped bird, understanding suddenly, completely, the shape of the trap, and it was beautiful, and it was cruel, and it was precisely calibrated, and she might have admired it if it hadn't been aimed at her.

Because here was the thing. Here was the whole rotten, gorgeous thing:

She had spent twelve years telling herself she had nothing left to lose. That was the thief's comfort, the small life's consolation prize: strip yourself down far enough and nothing can be taken from you. She had no family, no partner, no past worth the name, one friend who'd become an inquisitor who no longer remembered her—which was a loss, yes, but a finished one, a wound long since scabbed into scar tissue.

Except it wasn't finished. It had never been finished. Because the memories were gone from her head, but they weren't gone from the world—they were downstream, pooled here, in the cellar of creation, and the Hollow was looking at her across a half-real kitchen table and saying: *we know what you kept. You kept it by pretending you hadn't. Now give it up properly.*

"They're not mine anymore," she said. "I sold the morning. I sold pieces over the years—small ones, I thought they were small ones. Whatever echoes drifted here aren't mine to give. They're yours. Finders keepers."

"Echoes return to their source when called," Maret said. "You know this. It's the first law of your trade, isn't it? Why you never sell anything twice from the same head—the second copy is always weaker, because the first call pulls strength home." She folded her floury hands. "Call them home, and they're yours, entire. Every morning. Every laugh. Every year of him that you've been carrying around like a stone in your shoe, telling yourself it was just a pebble. Call them home and feed them to the door, and the boy walks free, and the door holds long enough for the rest of us to matter out our ending with some dignity. Or leave him, and he anchors, and he grows old here holding up a country with his childhood, and you walk out alone knowing you chose your thimble-full of self over a living boy."

"There has to be another—"

"There isn't," said Pell, from the window, quietly, to the orchard. "We've had twelve years to look. There isn't."

* * *

They gave her the afternoon. The Hollow was polite that way. Terrible hospitality: every horror served with bread.

Ulyana walked the streets that remembered being rivers—no, that was Vessing; here the streets remembered being *streets*, and were losing the argument—and tried to think like a thief instead of a woman, because thieves made better decisions. Thieves asked: what's the price, what's the alternative, what's the exit.

Price: Dain. All of him. Not the scar-tissue version she'd lived with—the whole recovered hoard, every echo called home, every morning and every laugh, and then poured out through a starving door like wine through a cracked jug. And not just given—she understood that too, with a professional's clarity—*spent*. Memories fed to a door didn't sit politely in its hinges. They burned. She would walk out of the Hollow unable to remember why she was crying.

Alternative: Tam. Eight years old. Anchored to a greying country like a fence post set in sand, growing up among the kindly dying, holding up the sky with his small self until the sky won, because it always won, because the Hollow had been losing for twelve years and a child's shoulders were not a foundation.

Exit: there wasn't one. Pell had checked.

She found Tam sitting on the bakery steps, drawing in the dust with a stick. Doors, of course. He drew doors the way other children drew horses. This one had a keyhole shaped like a lullaby.

"If I stay," he said—in his own tongue, the old tongue, which after weeks of travel she now understood nearly whole, the way you understand a song you've heard your whole life without knowing its words—"will you go?"

"Yes."

"Will you remember me?"

The question went through her like a hook through silk. Because that was the fine print, wasn't it. The price was the person she loved most. Did the person she loved most include a barefoot boy who'd arrived at her door on the night of a burning? Was love ranked? Could you pay a debt in one currency when you owed in several?

She crouched beside him. "Listen to me. Whatever happens tomorrow—whatever I do—you walk through that door, and you live somewhere real, somewhere with maps, and you eat bread that doesn't flicker, and you grow up tall and stubborn and impossible. That's the whole assignment. That's all of it. Can you do that for me?"

He considered, the way he considered everything, with the gravity of a much older soul in a borrowed body. Then he leaned against her side, briefly, the way a cat permits affection, and went back to his drawing.

Which was answer enough, and also no answer at all.

* * *

Evening came down on the Hollow like a lid. The stars here were unreliable—half of them were only remembered stars—and Ulyana stood in the bakery doorway watching the uncertain sky and doing sums no thief should ever have to do.

Twelve years ago she had chosen survival over love, and told herself it was strategy. She had walked out of a life that included Dain Marrow because staying meant the gallows for both of them, and one of them escaping was better arithmetic than neither. She had built a small life on that arithmetic and called it safety, and called the safety wisdom, and called the wisdom peace, and it had taken a mute child and a dying country to show her the invoice underneath: she had simply deferred the payment, and interest had accrued, and now the bill had come due in the one currency she'd sworn she didn't carry.

Survival always costs something you can't get back. She'd known that since her first theft. She'd just never expected to be the thing on the scales.

"Maret says you'll decide at dawn," said Osk, appearing beside her with two cups—one real, one nearly not—and handing her the real one. "For what it's worth, and it isn't worth much: the council voted this afternoon. If you refuse, we won't take the boy by force. We're not the Ministry. We'll just lose. But we wanted you to know the choice is genuinely yours. A stolen sacrifice anchors nothing. The door can tell."

"How comforting. A fair bargain."

"It's the only kind we have left." He sipped his ghost-tea, which apparently worked well enough. "One more thing, since you look like a woman collecting information instead of sleeping. The echoes—the Dain-echoes. When you call them home, you'll feel all of it at once. Twelve years of it, un-diluted. People assume the pain is the worst part." He shook his head. "It isn't. The worst part is the morning. The one you sold. It's worse than you remember, because you remember it worn smooth, and it comes home to you with all its edges on."

"Thank you," Ulyana said, "for that."

"You're welcome. It seemed crueler to let you be surprised."

He shuffled off, and Ulyana stood alone in the doorway of a bakery at the end of the world, holding a warm cup in a cooling country, and let herself—for exactly one minute, timed by a heartbeat she trusted—think about Dain Marrow's face when the door closed between them.

Except she couldn't picture it. That was the joke. That was the whole sick joke of it. She'd sold the face years ago along with everything else, and what remained was a shape, a warmth, a name she never said aloud—and the name was already rising in her throat like water finding its level, answering a call that hadn't even been made yet.

Far to the east, beyond the grey edge of everything, something was riding through the night toward the salt flats. She couldn't know that. But the Hollow knew it, and the Hollow leaked, and so she knew it the way you know weather in your bad knee: *he's coming, he's coming, he chose.*

"Duty's a heavy horse," she murmured to the unreliable stars, "but love rides lighter. Let's see which one of us falls first."

Behind her, in the dark of the bakery, the door at the end of the hall—the ordinary wooden one, the one that led to the street—began, very softly, to hum.
