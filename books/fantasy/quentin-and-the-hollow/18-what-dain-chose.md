## 18. What Dain Chose

The trouble with a well-kept ledger, Dain Marrow had learned over eleven years of service to the Ministry of Forgetting, was that it remembered everything you asked it to and nothing you needed.

He sat in his office on the fourth floor of the Ministry's Vessing annex, lamplight pooling on his desk like something spilled, and turned the pages of his own case file for the third time that night. *Investigation: unlicensed memory-charms, Lowmarket district. Subject: female, middle years, trade name "Whitlock."* His own handwriting stared back at him, neat and dutiful and wrong.

Because there were gaps in the file. Not redactions — he knew what redactions looked like; he'd written enough of them. These were gaps the way a pulled tooth leaves a gap: a shape of absence so specific it was almost a presence. Page fourteen ended mid-sentence about a warehouse raid twelve years ago and resumed three lines later with a note in someone else's hand: *Subject escaped. Pursuit discontinued.*

Pursuit discontinued. He had read those four words perhaps two hundred times in his career, always with relief, never once with curiosity. Tonight they itched like a wool collar.

And beneath them, in the margin, in handwriting that was *almost* his own but not quite — a little faster, a little more slanted, as if written by a man who hadn't yet learned to be careful — someone had drawn a small bird.

Dain did not draw birds. Dain drew boxes and arrows and the occasional very competent circle around important names. A man who had spent eleven years making himself into an instrument did not doodle songbirds in Ministry files.

Unless, said a voice in his head that sounded suspiciously young, unless he used to.

He pressed his thumb against the ink. It was old. Older than his promotion, older than his oath, older — though he had no way of proving this — than the particular smooth blankness where other men kept their twenties.

"Late night, Inquisitor?"

Corven stood in the doorway, one of the junior archivists, holding a cup of tea he had clearly been sent to deliver and just as clearly intended to drink himself. He was twenty-three and earnest and reminded Dain of a puppy that had been given a uniform.

"The Whitlock file," Corven said, nodding at it. "Any progress? The Deputy wants—"

"The Deputy," Dain said, "wants a great many things, most of which are mutually exclusive."

Corven laughed, then caught himself, because laughing at the Deputy was technically a flogging offense and everyone under thirty lived in constant low-grade terror of the Ministry's sense of humor. "There's, ah. There's also the matter of the salt flats, sir. The riders report the fugitive woman and the child crossed the border stones at dusk. Renk's been taken for questioning. They're asking whether to dispatch a second cohort."

Dain looked at the file. At the little bird.

"Tell them," he heard himself say, "that the flats are treacherous after dark and the cohort should wait for dawn."

"It isn't treacherous after dark, sir. I've been. It's completely flat."

"That," said Dain Marrow, "is exactly the sort of thing a flat would want you to believe."

* * *

He did not sleep. He told himself this was standard procedure for an active investigation, which was true, and also that he was not doing anything unusual, which was becoming less true by the hour.

What he did instead was what he did best: he made lists.

*Item.* The memory-thief of Lowmarket operated with a precision suggesting formal training. Formal training in theft existed in Vessing in exactly one place: the old Ferrow gang, dissolved twelve years ago, its members scattered or hanged.

*Item.* The Ferrow gang's known members included one D. Marrow, whose file began abruptly at age nineteen with an apprenticeship to a bookbinder and no explanation of how a bookbinder's apprentice came to be recruited into — he checked — yes, into the *Ministry itself*, fast-tracked, sponsored by an archivist named Hessel who had since retired to the coast and who, when Dain had visited him last spring, had looked at him with an expression Dain had filed under *senility* and was now forced to re-file under *pity*.

*Item.* Hessel had said, at that visit, gripping Dain's wrist harder than an old man should be able to: *"You were better before, boy."* And when Dain had asked, better at what? Hessel had wept. Just briefly. Then apologized and offered him pickled herring.

*Item.* Pickled herring was not an apology food. It was a distraction food. Someone had taught Dain that. He could not remember who, and the not-remembering had a texture now, a grain, like running his tongue over a missing tooth.

*Item.* The woman called Whitlock. He had interviewed her twice. Both times she had served him tea in a chipped blue cup and answered every question with the bland perfection of a woman reciting a shopping list, and both times he had left with the peculiar sensation of having forgotten to ask the only question that mattered. Both times, walking away from her shop, he had experienced a feeling he could only describe as homesickness, which was absurd, because he had never been inside her shop before in his life.

Except that the second time, he had reached for the door handle and found it worn smooth in a way that suggested a great deal of use, and his hand had fit it the way a key fits a lock it was cut for, and he had stood there for four full seconds like an idiot before knocking.

*Item.* Four seconds is a long time to stand outside a door.

He wrote all of this down in his private notebook, the one he kept for observations too small for official reports, and then he sat back and looked at the list as a whole, the way you look at a map, and the map resolved.

Not all at once. Maps don't work that way. But coastlines appeared. A river here. A woman with quick hands and a laugh she tried to hide behind her sleeve. A rooftop in summer. The word *partner*, spoken not as a job title but as a vow. A door, blue, with a crooked latch he had promised to fix and never had.

"Oh," said Dain Marrow, alone in his office at three in the morning, to nobody, in the voice of a man discovering water is wet. "Oh, you absolute *fool*."

The Ministry had not merely edited his memories of her. It had edited his memories of *himself* — of the version of him that existed alongside her — and installed, in the vacant lot where a life used to be, a career. Neat, productive, load-bearing. He had lived eleven years in a house built on the demolition site of his own heart and called it home.

And now the woman who had built that demolished house with him was running for the salt flats with a stolen child while the Ministry rode her down, and his orders — his duty, his purpose, the entire architecture of his rebuilt self — were to bring her in.

Dain looked at the little bird in the margin of page fourteen.

Then he got up, took his coat off its hook, and went to do the first honest thing he had done in twelve years: commit treason.

* * *

The treaty-ledger lived in the Deep Archive, sub-level three, behind a door that required two keys and a signature. Dain had one key, half of the second, and a signature he was about to spend the way a gambler spends his last coin.

Getting down there at four in the morning required passing Corven, who was still at his desk, still nursing the tea he'd failed to deliver, and who looked up with the doomed hopefulness of a man who has been waiting hours for permission to go to bed.

"Inquisitor! Did you need—"

"Corven." Dain set a coin on the desk. Not a bribe — Corven wouldn't know how to take one — but a token, the kind you give a witness so he remembers being one. "In about an hour, an alarm is going to sound. When it does, you will recall that I stopped by your desk tonight looking pale and unwell, complained of pains in my chest, and insisted on going down to the Archive alone to fetch my heart medicine."

"I... will?"

"You have a gift for recollection. It's why I picked you for the archive rotation." This was even true. "Also, if anyone asks whether I seemed traitorous, you may honestly say I seemed ill. Both things will be accurate."

Corven blinked at him with the expression of a puppy who has just been handed a live grenade and told it's a ball. "Sir, are you—"

"Heart medicine," Dain said firmly, and went down the stairs.

The Deep Archive smelled of dust and cold stone and the particular institutional despair of ten thousand documents nobody was allowed to read. He signed the access book — *D. Marrow, Inquisitor, retrieving case materials, official business* — and let the lie sit there in fresh ink, the last clean entry he would ever make in a Ministry ledger.

The treaty-ledger was where the rumors said it was: bound in grey leather, unmarked, shelved among customs records from the reign of the second king, on the theory that the best place to hide a crime against geography was in paperwork about tariffs.

He carried it to the reading table and opened it, and read, and felt the floor tilt gently beneath his chair.

It was all there. The Hollow — named, mapped, populated, *real* — ceded in the Treaty of Ashfall not conquered, not destroyed, but *traded*: its territory, its people, and — clause nine, initialled by three chancellors whose portraits hung downstairs in the Hall of Honor — its *existence*. Payment rendered to the signatory powers in the form of the country's own memory, extracted wholesale by the Ministry's founding archivists and distributed as currency. A nation spent like coin. Every charm of forgetting sold in Vessing's markets, every tax levied on remembering, traced back to a signature on this page.

Clause eleven, in a cramped later hand: *Erasure deemed irreversible. Residual rememberers subject to standard procedure.*

Standard procedure. He knew what standard procedure was. He had administered it. There was a widow in Copperlane whose wedding he had — no. That wasn't his. That was the thief's work. Except the thief sold things *back*.

He closed the ledger. His hands were steady, which surprised him. Somewhere under the eleven years of careful instrument-making, apparently, there was still a man who could hold a heavy thing without shaking.

"Well," he said to the empty archive, in the tone of a man addressing a jury he did not respect. "I find for the plaintiff."

* * *

The horse was the hardest part, in the end, and not for any reason the Ministry would have predicted.

The stables kept six mounts ready for night riders, and Dain took the second-best — the best belonged to Deputy Chancellor Ovist, and taking it would be *spiteful*, and whatever else he was tonight, he refused to be spiteful — and led it out through the yard where, eleven years ago, according to a gap in his memory shaped exactly like a summer evening, he had possibly taught someone to whistle.

He was three streets from the annex when the alarm bells began, earlier than expected, which meant Corven had either followed instructions with remarkable efficiency or panicked and pulled the lever himself. Either way: good lad. Dain put his heels to the horse.

They came after him within the quarter hour. Of course they did. He had trained half of them personally. He knew their habits the way he knew his own — knew that Brennick pushed his horses too hard on the straight and lost them on the turns, knew that Sergeant Hale would rather follow a sure trail than a fast one, knew that whoever commanded the pursuit tonight would send riders to the river gate first because that was what *he* would have done, which is why he went south, through the tannery district, where the smell covered hoofprints and the streets were too narrow for formation riding.

Behind him, Vessing burned its lamps against the dark and pretended, as it had pretended for twelve years, that nothing had ever been taken from it.

Ahead of him, somewhere past the city's edge, past the farmlands going grey at their borders, past everything the maps agreed on, lay the salt flats and a woman he had loved and could not remember loving, carrying a child who spoke the language of a country that officially did not exist, toward a door that was not a door.

His duty, eleven years deep and load-bearing, said *turn back*.

But duty, he was beginning to understand, had been translated for him. The original text — the one in the margin, the little bird, the worn door handle, the word *partner* — said something else entirely. And Dain Marrow, who had spent his whole reconstructed life obeying the letter of the law, found that the self he couldn't remember being had left him one final order, written in a hand faster and more slanted than his own:

*Ride.*

So he rode.

* * *

Dawn found him on the flats, and the flats found him wanting.

There was nothing out here. That was the point of salt flats, presumably — nature's way of demonstrating what the Ministry achieved by paperwork. White crust to the horizon, cracked into polygons like the floor of some abandoned kitchen, the sky enormous and indifferent above it. His horse's hooves rang oddly on the ground, as if the land itself were hollow — and he flinched at the word, at how it arrived in his mind already loaded with meaning he shouldn't possess.

The pursuit was behind him. He could see them without turning: six riders strung across the white in a line he himself had invented, drilled into them during a training exercise whose purpose he could no longer recall. They weren't gaining. Salt was hard on horses and Brennick, bless his predictable heart, had already lamed one.

What troubled Dain was not the riders behind him.

It was the light ahead.

The eastern horizon had gone strange — not sunrise-colored but *absent*-colored, a band of grey-white where the world seemed to have been erased down to the primer, and within that band, impossibly, he could see shapes. Hills that the maps denied. A line of trees that cast shadows in a direction the sun wasn't. And at the center of it, small with distance but unmistakable, two figures on foot: one slight, one small, walking side by side toward a point in the air where the light bent like heat-haze.

The woman. The child. Walking into a country that did not exist, and the country, apparently, disagreeing.

Dain stood in his stirrups. Behind him, someone shouted his name — his rank-name, *Marrow*, the name the Ministry had given the thing it built out of his ruins — and ahead of him, faint across the salt, he heard something else. Singing. A lullaby in a language he didn't know, and yet some buried part of him, some slanted-handed, bird-drawing, door-latch-promising part, knew it perfectly, and was harmonizing.

He gave the horse its head and rode toward the singing, the treaty-ledger banging against his chest like a second heartbeat, six former colleagues thundering at his back, and the door — whatever it was, wherever it stood — beginning, very slowly, to open.
