## 20. After the Forgetting

The first thing Ulyana noticed about the country that did exist was that the bread was terrible.

She said as much to the baker, a broad woman named Marta who ran the shop below the rooms they'd taken above it, and Marta laughed so hard she had to sit down on a sack of flour.

"Terrible!" the woman wheezed. "You hear that, Pieter? The new lodger says my bread is *terrible*." She wiped her eyes and pressed a warm roll into Ulyana's hands, unasked, unpurchased, the way people did things here — freely, casually, without weighing what it might cost them later. "Eat it anyway. It's honest bread. It's never lied to anyone."

Ulyana bit into it. It was dense and slightly burnt on one side and tasted of nothing in particular, and she found, absurdly, that her throat went tight.

Honest bread. Never lied to anyone.

"You're crying," Tam observed from the doorway. He was nine now, or looked it; the Hollow had let him keep the years it had borrowed, plus interest. He had grown three inches since the salt flats and had developed an opinion about everything, most of it delivered in the flat tone of a customs inspector. "You cry at bread now."

"I do not cry at bread." She wiped her eyes with her wrist. "There was ash in the air."

"There is no ash in the air."

"There was ash in the *bread*, then."

Marta looked between them with the delighted confusion of a woman who had decided months ago that these two were family and would not be taking further questions on the matter. "You should come to the wedding Saturday," she said. "My niece Anka. Marrying the cooper's boy. There'll be dancing."

"We'll come," said Tam, before Ulyana could develop a policy position on weddings.

"We'll come," Ulyana agreed, because arguing with either of them felt like arguing with weather.

* * *

The country they had woken into was called Ostrava-on-the-Lethe, which was not its real name but was the name on the maps, and after everything, Ulyana had decided she liked a country that admitted its names were negotiable. It sat on the far side of mountains she had no memory of crossing, in a valley where the fog came down every morning like a polite houseguest and left by ten. The Ministry of Forgetting did not exist here. Memory was simply — memory. People kept theirs carelessly, in letters and photograph boxes and arguments at dinner tables, spilling it everywhere, wasting it, hoarding nothing.

The first weeks, Ulyana had flinched at every uniform, every knock, every official-sounding word. She had learned, slowly, to stop. It was like setting down a weight she'd carried so long she'd mistaken it for a spine.

Ivo's papers had arrived by post six days after they crossed — no note, just an envelope containing two identity documents so perfectly forged that they were almost certainly real, because Ivo Renk had never in his life done anything halfway. *Ulyana Zhou, licensed charmwright, registered under the Artisans' Compact.* And beneath it, in his cramped scholar's hand, one line:

*They released me in the confusion. I told them nothing but riddles. They hated the riddles. Consider us even, and consider yourself owed nothing, which is the only kind of debt worth having. — I.R.*

P.S. *The child draws excellent maps. Encourage him.*

Tam did draw excellent maps. He drew them constantly, on scraps, on margins, on the steamed-up window over the wash basin: streets that bent like question marks, staircases going nowhere, a canal sleeping under cobbles. Vessing, from memory — the city that had tried to forget him, rendered faithfully by the boy who couldn't. He never drew the Hollow itself. When Ulyana asked why, once, he shrugged and said, "It doesn't need me to remember it anymore. It has itself back." Then he added, with terrible gentleness, "Do you remember it?"

"No," she said.

"Do you remember—" he started, and stopped, and she watched him decide, again, not to finish the sentence. He did this perhaps twice a week. She had learned to recognize the shape of the unfinished question without learning what it was. It sat in the air sometimes like a bird that had landed and thought better of it.

"Remember what?" she asked, as she always asked.

"Nothing," he said, as he always said. "Pass the jam."

* * *

Her shop opened in the third month. A charm-shop, legitimate, licensed, boring — exactly the life she had spent twelve years pretending to have in Vessing, except that here the pretense required no pretense at all. She sold charms the honest way: little stitched tokens for luck in labor, for calm at funerals, for courage before difficult conversations. No memories in any of them. She had retired from thieving the way a drowning woman retires from water.

Business was slow and then less slow. Marta bought a charm against burnt bread, ironically, and reported that it worked, which Ulyana suspected was the placebo doing honest labor. A fisherman bought eleven charms for eleven grandchildren. An old man came in every Tuesday to buy nothing and talk about his late wife, and Ulyana listened, and found that listening to other people's grief fit inside her like a key she didn't know she'd lost.

But there were things. Small things. Wrong-sized things.

She cried at strangers' weddings.

Not politely — not the single decorative tear of a woman moved by love. She cried the way you cry when something enormous passes through you at speed and takes part of the wall with it on the way out. At Anka's wedding, when the cooper's boy lifted his bride's veil, Ulyana made a sound she hadn't authorized, and Marta's sister had to bring her water, and Tam put his small hand on her arm and said nothing, which was somehow worse than if he'd said the unfinished thing.

"It's happy crying," she explained to Marta afterward, blowing her nose.

"Of course it is," said Marta, who clearly believed otherwise and was too kind to say so.

It happened at the next wedding too. And the one after. She began to dread invitations the way she'd once dreaded Ministry seals. She tried standing at the back. She tried arriving late, missing the veil-lifting. Nothing helped. Once she cried during the *toasts*, which was humiliating beyond all previous thresholds, because the best man had only said something about how the groom used to sleepwalk, and there was nothing in that, *nothing*, except that the groom's mother had laughed and covered her mouth and said "he still does, you know," and something about that — the mother knowing, the keeping of the small useless knowledge, the love of it — had gone through Ulyana like a lance.

"What is wrong with me?" she asked Tam that night, over supper.

He considered his potatoes with great seriousness. "Nothing's wrong with you."

"People don't weep at toast jokes."

"Our people might."

"We don't have a people. We have a lease."

He smiled at that, one of his rare full smiles, and then it faded into the careful look, the look that meant a question had landed and been escorted out the back. "Maybe," he said slowly, "you're crying for something you can't see. Like a draft under a door. You feel the cold without finding the gap."

"That's the most depressing thing you've ever said, and you once described death as 'the long quiet.'"

"I've been reading poetry. Ivo sends books."

"He sends you *maps*. He sends me *bills*."

"The bills are also maps," said Tam, "if you think about it."

* * *

Autumn came down the valley in gold increments. The shop did well enough. Ulyana learned the names of neighbors, learned which ones gossiped (all of them), learned that honesty had a texture she'd never known in Vessing — grainy, imperfect, like Marta's bread. She slept whole nights through. She stopped checking the door latch twice. She caught herself humming while she worked, some tuneless thing, and didn't examine it, because examining things had been her profession and she was on holiday from herself.

And yet.

Some mornings she woke with wet eyes and no reason, the way you wake from a dream that has already packed and left. Some evenings, walking home past the lit windows of other people's houses, she felt a grief so specific it had edges, and she would stop in the street and try to find its shape — a person? a place? a morning? — and it would dissolve under attention like frost under a thumb. She kept a ledger, out of old habit, and in it she recorded the episodes the way she'd once recorded inventory: *Tuesday. Wept at bread. Wednesday. Wept at nothing, duration unknown, cause unknown. Friday. Felt watched by kindness.*

Once, sorting a drawer of ribbon, she found a length of grey-blue silk she had no memory of buying. Her hands knew it, though. Her hands went still around it, and her breath went strange, and for a full minute she stood in her own shop as if someone else were wearing her.

Then Tam called down that the kettle was on, and the minute passed, and she sold the ribbon the next day to a woman making a christening gown, and did not watch it leave.

* * *

In December, a letter came from Ivo. It contained, as usual, no sentiment whatsoever and therefore, as usual, more sentiment than most love letters.

*The Ministry of Forgetting has fallen. You will want details; I will give you none, except these: the archives burned themselves, the inquisitors scattered, and the treaty-ledgers were seized by persons unknown during the chaos. Persons very much known to me, actually, though I am sworn to vagueness. The erasure of the Hollow has been formally declared illegal by three governments who spent decades pretending it had never happened, which tells you everything about governments and nothing new.*

*The Hollow is returning to the maps. Slowly. Cartographers are calling it "the rediscovery of the age." I am calling it Tuesday.*

*I am well. I have a chair now. Chairs matter more than people admit.*

*Give the boy my regards and tell him the eastern coastline he drew from memory matches the survey within half a league, which is frankly showing off.*

*— I.R.*

Ulyana read it twice. Tam read it four times and then went to the window and stood there a long while, looking east, toward mountains and whatever lay past them.

"They'll rebuild it," he said finally. "The Hollow. With everyone's memories coming home to it." He turned. His face was doing something complicated. "The door held, in the end. Because of what you—"

He stopped. The unfinished question again, or its cousin.

"Because of what I what?" Ulyana asked, drying a cup.

A silence. Tam looked at her across the small warm kitchen, and Ulyana saw — clearly, suddenly, the way you see a face in firelight — that he was deciding whether to give her something back. Whatever it was, it was large. She could tell by how carefully he wasn't holding it.

"Because of what you paid," he said at last, quietly. "That's all. That's the whole sentence."

"Well." She set the cup in the rack. Her chest ached, suddenly, specifically, edge-shaped. "Whatever it was, I can't miss what I don't remember. That's the trick of it. That's the whole trick of it."

"Yes," said Tam. "That's the whole trick of it." And he smiled at her, and it was the saddest smile she had ever seen on a child's face, and neither of them said anything else, because the kettle was boiling and the bread needed turning and the country that existed went on existing, indifferent and golden, around the small hole in the middle of their kitchen table where a fourth setting would have gone, if either of them had known there should be one.

* * *

Winter deepened. The shop did well. On the last market day before the solstice, Ulyana set out her stock in the cold morning light — luck-charms, calm-charms, courage for difficult conversations — and paused over the last tray, the unsold oddments, the things she kept out of some instinct she no longer questioned.

At the bottom of the tray sat a single charm she had never priced. Grey-blue silk, stitched shut, unlabeled. Her own handwriting was on the little paper tag, though she had no memory of writing it, in a hand slightly younger than the one she used now:

*do not sell*

She turned it over twice. It weighed almost nothing. It hummed, faintly, the way a struck bell hums after the sound seems finished — and underneath the hum, so faint she might have invented it, was a warmth like morning light through a window, like laughter in another room, like a voice saying something kind that she couldn't quite—

"Madam?" said a customer.

She looked up. A man stood at the counter of her small warm shop, travel-worn, hat in his hands, with the particular posture of someone who has come a very long way to buy something he does not need.

"Good morning," said Ulyana Zhou, charmwright, honestly, to the stranger. "What can I get you?"
