## 21. The Man Who Bought Back Nothing

The Ministry took Dain Marrow's rank the way a tide takes a sandcastle: slowly at first, then all at once, and without any particular malice.

There was a hearing. There were forms. There was a man from Internal Rectification who spoke to him in the gentle tone one uses for horses and grieving widows, explaining that Inquisitor Marrow's recent conduct—desertion of post, theft of Ministry property, consorting with persons of erased provenance—had been noted, weighed, and found deserving not of the gallows but of something worse: a quiet desk in the Records Annex, third sub-basement, where the light came from lamps because windows were considered a security risk.

"You'll be cataloguing," the man said. "Bound volumes. Mostly tax ledgers from the reign of the second Aldric. Very little excitement."

Dain had nodded as though this were a punishment he feared.

What he did not surrender—what they could not take, because officially it did not exist—was the treaty-ledger. He had stolen it from the Vault of Instruments in the chaos after Ivo Renk's release, wrapped it in his own cloak like a colicky infant, and carried it out through three checkpoints by the simple expedient of looking bored. Boredom, he had learned over twelve years of inquisitorial work, was the most effective disguise in Vessing. Nobody stopped a bored man. A bored man was already invisible to himself.

The ledger now lived under the floorboard of his rented room in Lowmarket, beneath a loose plank he'd once pried up to hide contraband brandy back when hiding things from the Ministry had been a hobby rather than a vocation. Some nights he lay awake listening to the building settle and thought about the weight of it—one book, thin enough to slip inside a coat, containing the signatures that had unmade an entire country. Eleven names. Eleven men who had sat in a warm room and sold a place out of existence, and then gone home to their dinners.

He had memorized the names. It seemed the least he could do, since no one else would ever be permitted to remember them.

* * *

The shop appeared on Cordwainer Lane in the spring, wedged between a cobbler who specialized in boots for feet that didn't exist anymore and a tea merchant whose stock was entirely rancid but who refused, on principle, to admit it.

It was called *Borrowed Things*, which made Dain stop in the street the first time he saw the sign, because he knew that phrase. He didn't know why he knew it. That was the trouble with him these days—half his interior life was knowing things without knowing how, like a man walking through a house in the dark and bruising his hip on furniture someone had moved.

He stood across the lane for a long while, hands in his pockets, telling himself he was merely curious. The woman behind the counter was dark-haired, quick-moving, with ink stains on her fingers and a way of arranging her wares that suggested she cared about them more than she'd ever admit. She laughed at something a customer said—a short, startled laugh, as if laughter surprised her every time—and something in Dain's chest turned over like a key in a lock that opened onto nothing.

He went home. He told himself he would not go back.

He went back four days later.

* * *

The bell above the door was cracked and gave a sound like a cough. She looked up from her ledger—her actual ledger, ink and paper—and smiled the smile of a shopkeeper greeting a stranger, which was exactly what he was, which was exactly the problem.

"Good morning," she said. "Looking for anything particular?"

"I don't know," Dain said honestly. "What do you sell?"

"Charms." She gestured at the shelves: small glass vials, each holding a curl of pale luminescence like smoke that had decided to behave. "Memories, mostly. Harmless ones. A widow's wedding day. A fisherman's last good catch. The smell of bread from a bakery that closed twenty years ago. Nothing dangerous, nothing true enough to hurt anyone."

"And people buy those?"

"People buy everything, if you wrap it nicely." Her eyes crinkled. "Grief is very popular. Nostalgia outsells it two to one, but grief pays better."

"That's grim."

"That's retail." She tilted her head. "You're not from this street. Cordwainer Lane doesn't get many men who stand in doorways like they're expecting the room to confess something."

"Do I look like an inquisitor?"

"No," she said, and there was a flicker—there and gone, quick as a fish under ice—"you look like a man who used to be something and isn't sure what yet."

Dain laughed. It came out rougher than he intended. "That's the most accurate reading anyone's given me in months."

"We aim to please. Or at least to move inventory." She spread her hands. "So. A memory? I can recommend something gentle. First snowfall. A dog you owned as a child—I have several dogs, all excellent. One man's memory of falling asleep in a hayloft during a rainstorm. That one's very popular with insomniacs."

"I don't sleep badly," Dain said. "I sleep too well. I sleep through things I shouldn't."

"Then perhaps something that wakes you up." She studied him a moment longer than commerce required. "You're certain we haven't met? You have one of those faces."

"Everyone says that."

"Everyone says that about everyone. But I mean it specifically." She frowned slightly, and Dain held very still, the way he'd learned to hold still when a suspect was circling close to something true. Then the frown passed, and she shrugged, and the moment was gone. "Probably the tea next door. His fumes are hallucinogenic. What'll it be?"

"The hayloft," Dain said, because it was the cheapest thing on the shelf and because buying something expensive felt like a lie he wasn't prepared to maintain.

She wrapped the vial in brown paper with quick, competent movements. "One copper. Instructions are on the label: uncork it before sleep, hold it loosely, don't grip. Memories spook easily."

"Like horses."

"Exactly like horses." She handed it over, and their fingers touched briefly on the paper, and neither of them mentioned it, and both of them noticed.

* * *

He came back the following week. And the week after. He developed a routine so transparent that even he found it embarrassing: he would invent a pretext—the charm hadn't taken, or he'd lost the instructions, or he simply needed to know whether she'd gotten new stock—and she would receive each pretext with perfect courtesy and visible amusement, the way one receives a child's explanation for why the biscuit tin is empty.

"You again," she said on his fourth visit, not unkindly.

"Me again."

"You know, most customers manage to make their charms last longer than six days."

"This one wore off quickly."

"It's a hayloft, not a love potion." She leaned on the counter. "All right. Out with it. Why does a man keep buying memories he clearly doesn't use? I've seen the vials in your coat pocket when you think I'm not looking. You carry them around like talismans and never open them."

Dain looked down at the counter. The truth was that he did carry them. All five of them, lined up in his breast pocket like small pale eggs. He told himself it was evidence-gathering habit—he had been an investigator for twelve years; one collected things—but the honest answer was simpler and more humiliating: they were hers. Her handwriting was on the labels. Her thumb had pressed the cork into each one. Carrying them was the closest thing he had to a conversation.

"I'm not using them," he admitted. "I just... like having them."

She was quiet for a moment. When she spoke again, the amusement had softened into something more careful. "That's either the strangest thing a customer has ever said to me, or the saddest. I haven't decided."

"Could be both."

"Could be both," she agreed. "Look—Marrow, isn't it? You never told me your name, but the cobbler gossips."

"Dain."

"Dain." She tried it out, and something crossed her face—brief, unreadable, gone. "Well, Dain. Next time you come in, try actually opening one. That's what they're for. Hoarding borrowed things is worse than not having them at all. Trust me. Professional opinion."

* * *

He opened the hayloft charm that night, sitting on the edge of his narrow bed with the lamp turned low.

It was exactly as advertised: rain on a roof of thatch, the sweet dusty dark, a boy's contentment so complete it barely qualified as consciousness. It was lovely. It was also, he realized as it settled into him, not quite anonymous. There was a quality to the memory's edges—a way the boy in it noticed small things, catalogued them automatically, the reflex of someone who would grow up to search other people's rooms for a living—that felt uncomfortably familiar.

He wondered, not for the first time, whose memories filled her shelves. He wondered whether she had ever sold one of her own. He wondered why the question mattered to him so much, why it sat in his chest like a stone he'd swallowed.

Under the floorboard, the treaty-ledger waited. He had read it so many times he could recite the clauses in his sleep—which, given his sleeping habits, he possibly did. *Article Seven: the signatory powers agree that the territory herein described shall be regarded as uninhabited, unnavigable, and unhistoried, and that all instruments, records, and recollections pertaining thereto shall be surrendered for dissolution.* Recollections. They had dissolved recollections. Whole country-fuls of them, fed into whatever engine the Ministry kept for such purposes, and the eleven signatories had signed their names beneath it in ink that was probably expensive.

And at the bottom, in a marginal hand, smaller than the rest: a note about compensation. *The displaced population, if any, to be resettled at the Crown's discretion.* If any. As if the people of a nation were a rounding error.

Dain had spent months being angry about the ledger. Lately the anger had been giving way to something quieter and less useful, which was that every time he looked at Ulyana Zhou's shop—her name was on the lease, the cobbler had told him that too—he felt like a man reading a letter addressed to someone he used to be.

* * *

It was nearly summer when he finally understood what he was going to do, and the understanding arrived, appropriately, in the middle of the night, sitting up in bed with the taste of a stranger's childhood dog still fading from his mouth.

Some debts survive even when the memory of them doesn't. He didn't know where the thought came from—it arrived whole, like a bird through a window—but he knew it was true, and he knew what it demanded.

In the morning he pried up the floorboard, took out the ledger, and wrapped it in brown paper. Then he sat with it on his knees for a long time, because once he left it on her counter, it would be the most dangerous object in Vessing, and she would be its most endangered keeper, and she didn't even know his name meant anything.

But she had a cellar. He'd noticed the trapdoor behind the counter weeks ago, and noticed, too, how her eyes went to it whenever the Ministry's criers passed in the street. Whatever she was keeping down there—or whoever—she was already living dangerously. She deserved to know what kind of danger, and whose fault it was, and that at least one man had tried to put it right.

* * *

The bell coughed. She looked up, and this time her smile came slower, and he realized she had been watching the door for him and hated herself for it, and he loved her for hating herself for it, and none of this showed on his face because twelve years of inquisitorial training dies hard.

"Let me guess," she said. "The hayloft leaked."

"The hayloft was fine." He set the brown-paper parcel on the counter. "This is for you. Not a purchase. A delivery."

Her eyebrows rose. She turned the parcel over once, weighing it. "I don't accept gifts from strangers. Shopkeeper's rule. Strangers who give gifts want something, and I like to know the price before I owe it."

"The price is already paid," Dain said. "By other people. A long time ago." He hesitated. "Open it somewhere private. Read the first page, and if you decide it's nothing—if it's nonsense, if it's madness—burn it and forget me. I won't come back. But if it's what I think it is..."

"What do you think it is?" she asked quietly.

He looked at her—at the ink on her fingers, at the small scar on her jaw he'd been noticing for months without knowing why it made his chest ache, at the door behind her that led down to a cellar where a child was almost certainly listening—and he said the only true thing he had.

"I think you know more than you remember. And I think somebody owes you an apology eleven names long."

For a moment she didn't move. Then, slowly, she reached out and rested her hand flat on the parcel—not picking it up, just holding it down, as if it might fly away.

"Dain," she said. "Why do I feel like I've been waiting for you?"

"I don't know," he said, and his voice caught on the words like a sleeve on a nail. "But I've been waiting for you my whole life, and until eight months ago I didn't know yours."

Behind the counter, faintly, from under the floor, a child began to hum.

Ulyana went still. So did Dain. Neither of them moved, and the tune drifted up through the boards—simple, old, in a language that had officially never existed—and Dain watched the color leave her face and return again, watched her lips part around a harmony she didn't know she knew, and thought: *there it is. There's the door.*

"Ulyana," he said gently. "I think you'd better open the parcel."
