## 22. A Lullaby in an Unknown Tongue

The shop on Cordwainer Lane had a new sign, and the sign had a new name: *Zhou & Tam, Restorations.* Ulyana had argued for something less conspicuous—*Fine Charms, Nothing Suspicious* had been her suggestion—but Tam had insisted, in his careful, half-remembered way, that a shop should be honest about what it did.

"We don't restore anything," she'd said.

"We do," he'd said, with the terrible certainty of a nine-year-old who has carried a country inside himself and lived to talk about it. "You just don't know it yet."

That had been four months ago. She still didn't know it yet. But she was beginning to suspect.

The morning was the kind Vessing almost never gave away for free: pale gold light through the front window, dust motes turning like slow dancers, the smell of beeswax and old paper. Ulyana sat behind the counter doing accounts, which is to say she sat behind the counter holding a pen over a ledger and thinking about nothing in particular, which was a luxury she had only recently learned to afford.

Her memory was a house with some rooms closed. She knew this the way you know a house has a locked door—you feel the draft under it. There were gaps. She'd catalogued them without alarm, the way you catalogue a chipped teacup: *the year I was twelve, mostly gone; a woman's face I can't place; a stretch of my twenties with holes in it like moth-eaten wool.* The healers in this country—the real country, the one that existed on maps—had told her gently that grief sometimes eats its own shape out of a person. She had accepted this explanation because the alternative was to sit down in the middle of Cordwainer Lane and weep, and she had a business to run.

She cried at odd moments now. That was the strangest part. At weddings, mostly—she'd been to three since they'd arrived, all strangers' weddings, all attended because Tam liked the music—and each time, somewhere around the vows, her eyes would simply open their floodgates and she would stand there in her good coat sobbing into her sleeve while brides she'd never met patted her arm with bewildered kindness.

"Happy tears?" one groom had asked.

"I'm sure they are," she'd said, and meant it, and understood nothing.

* *

It happened on a Tuesday, the way most important things do, disguised as an ordinary afternoon.

Tam was in the back room sorting charms—he'd developed a talent for it, an instinct for which memory sat in which glass vial, which was either charming or ominous depending on how much wine Ulyana had had—and he was humming.

She noticed it before she consciously heard it. Her hands stopped moving. Her pen lifted off the page. Something in her chest turned over like a key in a lock that she hadn't known was there.

The tune was simple. Six notes, then a pause, then six more, the second phrase answering the first the way a wave answers the one before it. It wasn't a song from anywhere she knew—not Vessing's tavern music, not the hymns the Ministry tolerated, not the border ballads the cart-drivers sang. It was older than all of those. It sounded, if a tune could sound like anything, the way a word sounds when you've forgotten it but your mouth hasn't.

And then—without deciding to, without any part of her giving permission—Ulyana began to hum the answer.

The harmony came up out of her like water finding a crack in a floor. A third below his melody, sliding where it needed to slide, landing where it needed to land, and when the phrase ended she opened her mouth and *words* came out, actual words, in a language made of soft consonants and long vowels, a language she had never studied, never heard, never—

She stopped. The silence rang.

Tam stood in the doorway of the back room with a vial of somebody's first snowfall cradled in both hands, looking at her the way you look at a door that has just opened onto a room you were sure was a wall.

"You know it," he said.

"I don't know it." Her voice came out strange. "I've never—I don't speak—" She gestured at her own mouth as though it belonged to someone else. "What did I just say?"

"It's the third verse." He came forward slowly, setting the vial down with the exaggerated care of a child handling something holy. "The one about the river going home. You said the river goes home even when the banks forget it." He frowned, working at it. "That's what it means. That's always what it means. Every time."

"The banks forget it," Ulyana repeated. The words sat in her ear like a stone dropped down a well, falling and falling and never quite hitting bottom.

"Where did you learn it?"

"Nowhere." And then, because lying to Tam had become impossible somewhere between the salt flats and the door: "I don't remember learning it. There's a lot I don't remember learning."

He considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. Then he nodded once, decisively, the way he did when he'd decided something was true whether or not it made sense.

"That's how the Hollow works," he said. "Things stay even after they're taken. The taking isn't perfect. Nothing's perfect." He picked his vial back up. "Not even forgetting."

He went back to his sorting, humming, and after a moment—helplessly, gratefully, frightened half to death—Ulyana harmonized again.

* *

*

That night, after the shop was shuttered and Tam was asleep in the loft bed he'd claimed as his sovereign territory, Ulyana went through her unsold stock.

This was a ritual she performed monthly, partly for inventory, partly for something she couldn't have named if you'd asked her. Each charm sat in its little glass prison with its label in her own handwriting: *widow's wedding, gentle; smuggler's last sunrise, bittersweet; soldier's mother's kitchen, warm, sells well in winter.* Twelve years of other people's lives, catalogued and priced. In this new country she'd begun, quietly, returning them—writing letters, matching vials to names, sending off pieces of people to people. It was slow work. It felt like the only honest thing she'd ever done.

She was two shelves from the bottom when she found the charm that stopped her heart.

It was unremarkable to look at: a squat vial, stoppered with wax, the memory inside it glowing that faint amber that meant *morning*. But the label—

The label was in her own handwriting. Of course it was; all the labels were. But this one was different. This one was pressed so hard into the paper that the pen had nearly torn through, the ink thick and slightly smeared, as though the hand that wrote it had been shaking. Two words:

***do not sell.***

No price. No description. No date. Just those two words, written by her, in a hand she recognized and could not place the mood of—urgent, fierce, the handwriting of someone standing guard.

Ulyana sat down on her stool. The shop was very quiet. Somewhere upstairs, Tam turned over in his sleep.

She knew every charm in her stock. She had stolen every memory in it personally; she remembered the taking of each one the way a carpenter remembers his joints. Except. Except there were rooms closed in her own house, weren't there? Moth-eaten years. Holes in the wool. And a label written in fear, guarding a thing she could not remember stealing, from buyers she could not remember refusing.

*Do not sell.*

"Well," she said aloud, to the empty shop, in the tone of a woman stepping off a ledge. "You did say not to."

She broke the seal.

* *

Morning. Not this morning—an older one, twelve years deep, wearing the particular gold light of a city she no longer lived in. She was younger in it. She knew that the way you know things in dreams, absolutely and without evidence. Younger, and laughing at something, and there was a man—

A man sitting on the edge of a rooftop with his boots dangling over the street, holding two cups of terrible coffee, complaining about the coffee with the elaborate, theatrical despair of someone who has already drunk it every day for a year and will drink it every day forever. Dark hair going grey too early at the temples. A scar through one eyebrow he refused to explain. He handed her a cup and she took it and the whole memory was suffused with the specific, unbearable warmth of a person who knows your silences and doesn't try to fill them.

"You're smiling," he said. "Stop it. It undermines my complaint."

"I'll smile if I want to, Dain."

*Dain.* The name went through her like a bell struck in an empty church—one clear note, and then the trembling, and then nothing, and then the ache where the sound had been.

The memory was small. That was the thing that undid her. It wasn't a grand passion, wasn't a wedding or a war or a deathbed. It was ten minutes on a rooftop with bad coffee, two thieves on a morning off, her feet cold on the tiles, his shoulder warm against hers, the city waking up below them with all its crimes and charms. At one point he said something—she couldn't hear what, the memory fraying at its edges like cloth rubbed thin—and she laughed so hard she spilled coffee down her sleeve, and he laughed too, and the sun came up properly, and that was all. That was everything. That was the whole of it: a morning, ordinary, kept.

*Do not sell,* her own hand had written, and now she understood the shaking ink. Now she understood why some part of her, twelve years ago, had looked at this small amber morning and known—with the knowledge that lives deeper than memory, in the marrow, in the lullaby places—that this one was not inventory. This one was hers.

But she didn't remember him. That was the cruelty of it. She held the memory and it held her, and she loved him in it, loved him with the full force of whatever she had been—and when the vial emptied and the shop came back around her, cold and candlelit, she reached for his face and found a smooth blank wall where a man should be. She knew the name Dain the way you know a word in a language you used to speak. She knew that she had loved him. She could not remember loving him. The feeling survived; the reason for it had been sold, spent, fed into a door she had no memory of approaching.

She was crying. She realized it only when a tear hit the counter and darkened the wood beside the empty vial.

"Happy tears?" she asked herself, out loud, the way a groom had once asked her. And then, because it was true and because no one was listening: "I'm sure they are."

* *

*

She might have sat there until dawn if the bell above the shop door hadn't chimed.

Vessing—or rather, this country, whose name she now lived in and whose name her tongue still stumbled over—kept honest hours, and no honest customer came at midnight. Ulyana wiped her face with the brisk incompetence of a woman who has done this many times recently, stood, and opened the door.

A man stood on the step. Rain-wet, travel-stained, with a week of road on him and a certain hollowed-out look that she recognized from mirrors. He was perhaps forty. Grey coming in early at the temples. A scar through one eyebrow.

Something in her chest turned over. The key again. The lock again. The draft under the closed door.

"Sorry to call so late," he said. His voice was rough and careful, the voice of a man who has rehearsed a speech and abandoned it on the doorstep. "I saw the sign. Zhou and Tam. Restorations." He almost smiled. "I've been walking past it for three days trying to think of a pretext."

"We sell charms," Ulyana said. "Memory charms. Harmless ones. Mostly."

"I don't need a charm." He hesitated. Rain dripped off the awning between them. "I need to return something. I've been carrying it a long way, and it isn't mine, and I don't—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "I don't actually know what it is. Or why it matters to me. Or why I rode three hundred miles through weather like this instead of throwing it in a river, which would have been the sensible thing." He reached into his coat and drew out a heavy book, wrapped in oilcloth, bound in grey leather gone soft with handling. "But it matters. I know that the way you know your own name. Do you understand that? Knowing something without knowing it?"

Ulyana looked at the book. The treaty-ledger. She didn't know how she knew that either—only that the words arrived fully formed, *the treaty-ledger*, like a lullaby rising out of nowhere, like a harmony she'd never been taught.

"I understand," she said.

"There's a name in it," the man said. "Mine, I think—it's signed in a lot of places, reports, requisitions, things I don't remember writing. Dain Marrow." He watched her face as he said it, and whatever he saw there made him go very still. "And there's another name. Written in the margins, over and over, in different inks, different years. Like someone kept needing to write it down to keep it true." His voice cracked, just barely, just enough. "*Ulyana Zhou.*"

The rain fell. The lamp in her window buzzed. Upstairs, in his sleep, Tam began—faintly, muffled through the floorboards—to hum six notes, and pause, and six notes more.

And Ulyana Whitlock-Zhou, memory-thief, keeper of a shop called Restorations, stood in her own doorway and harmonized with a sleeping child in a language she didn't know she knew, while a stranger with familiar eyes waited on her step, holding a book full of her name.

"I think," she said slowly, "you'd better come in. I'll make coffee. It'll be terrible."

His face did something complicated—grief and hope fighting to a draw—and he stepped across the threshold, out of the rain, into the small warm shop where nothing was ever entirely lost.

"I know," he said. "I'm counting on it."
