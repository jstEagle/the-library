## 2. The Debt Ledger

The summons came at the hour when the temple lamps were trimmed and the scriptorium's other scribes had gone to their cells, which meant it was designed to be private, which meant Lena knew before reading a single word that something had gone terribly wrong.

The note itself was small—a slip of vellum no larger than a palm, folded twice and sealed with gray wax that bore no sigil at all. That absence of a seal was its own message. Corvane did not need one. Everyone in Thornfell's lower city knew what the plain gray wax meant: *the debt-master summons you; the debt-master requires nothing so vulgar as heraldry.* Lena turned the slip over twice, as if the back might contain an escape clause, some small mercy of misdirection. It contained only four lines in a clerk's precise hand:

*To the scribe Nakamura. Attend the counting-house at the ninth bell after dusk. Come alone. Bring nothing.*

*Bring nothing* was the phrase that made Lena's hands go cold against the warm vellum. Not *bring your ledger*, not *bring your pen*. Corvane summoned debtors to bring their accounts, their collateral, their sureties. He summoned them to bring proof of what they owed. To summon someone and ask for nothing was to say: *I already have everything I need.*

Lena sat for a long moment in the alcove where they worked, the last lamp guttering, the day's copying stacked and squared beside them—forty psalms to the Unnamed God, who had never once answered, transcribed in a hand so careful it looked printed. Forty prayers addressed to a silence. Lena had copied that silence for seven years, and until three nights ago, the silence had been the safest thing in the world.

Three nights ago, a prayer had answered back.

Lena pressed their palms flat against the desk, feeling the grain of wood worn smooth by generations of scribes, and tried to breathe the way Mother Vess taught the novices: slow, even, invisible. But invisibility had failed. Something had gone out from that page like heat from an open oven door, and somewhere between the scriptorium and the counting-house, someone had felt it.

The margins of Lena's mind filled, unbidden, with calculations. Seven years of service against a bond-price set at two hundred crowns when they were eleven years old and their parents' debts had come due like wolves. Interest compounded annually. Board deducted. Ink deducted. The lamplight deducted by the quarter-hour. In seven years of copying prayers, Lena had reduced the principal by less than a tenth, and everyone in the counting-house knew the arithmetic was designed never to close. A debt like theirs was not meant to be paid. It was meant to be *held*.

And now Corvane wanted to talk about it.

Lena banked the lamp, tucked the answered prayer deeper into the false bottom of their ink chest—the page wrapped in oilcloth, hidden beneath a decade of harmless drafts—and went down through the sleeping cloister with their heart beating a rhythm too fast for the solemn halls.

* * *

The counting-house of House Corvane occupied a narrow stone building wedged between a chandler's and a pawnbroker's, as if debt were simply another trade conducted over counters, which in Thornfell it was. Inside, the air smelled of tallow, cold iron, and old paper—that dry, sweetish rot of ledgers kept past their natural death. Rows of clerks' desks stood empty at this hour, their inkwells stoppered, their chairs pushed in with a discipline that suggested the clerks themselves were deducted by the minute. At the far end of the long room, behind a desk of black oak older than the temple, sat Debt-Master Corvane.

He was smaller than his reputation. People spoke of him as though he must be vast, a giant of sums and seizures, but in person he was a spare, neat man of perhaps fifty-five, with a clerk's stooped shoulders and a moneylender's stillness. His hair was silvered and combed with exact care. His fingers, resting on a closed ledger, were long and clean and utterly without callus. He did not look up when Lena entered. He let the silence work first, the way he let interest work—patiently, compounding.

Lena stopped three paces from the desk and waited. They had learned patience in the scriptorium; it was the one inheritance the temple had given freely.

"You copied forty psalms today," Corvane said at last, without looking up. "I have the tally here." One finger tapped the closed ledger. "Your hand is excellent. Your discipline is excellent. Your output has declined by exactly nothing in seven years." He raised his eyes then, and they were pale, the gray of unmarked paper. "So tell me why a man whose business is certainty cannot sleep."

"I couldn't say, master."

"No," Corvane agreed softly. "You couldn't. That is rather the point."

He opened the ledger. Lena's stomach dropped—not because of what the ledger contained, but because of what lay beneath it, visible for just a moment before Corvane smoothed the upper book flat: a second ledger, older, bound in green leather gone dark with handling, its pages dense with marginalia. Lena knew those margins. Lena had written some of them.

"Master—" they began.

"Do you know," Corvane said, conversational now, almost gentle, "how many times I have read the ledgers of this house? All of them. Every line, every entry, every correction. I do not delegate my reading, scribe. Reading is where the money hides." He turned the top ledger around and slid it across the desk so that it faced Lena. "Three nights ago. The Vigil of the Turning Year. You were assigned the closing litany. And here is your time-entry, certified by Brother Anselm, who signs anything put in front of him." The finger tapped again. "But here—" and now he drew from beneath the ledger a single loose sheet, and Lena's heart stopped like a struck bell, "—is the draft you discarded. Recovered from the scriptorium's waste-basket. Which I have emptied personally every night for eleven years. Do you know why?"

Lena could not speak. Their throat had closed around something that was either terror or grief or both braided together, the way their parents' names were braided into the opening line of their own contract.

"Because waste-paper is honest," Corvane said. "A finished page tells you what a man wants you to know. A discarded page tells you what he is. I have built a very considerable fortune on other people's discarded pages." He smoothed the sheet flat on the black oak, and there it was: Lena's own handwriting, hurried, half-finished—the closing litany begun in the approved formal hand and then abandoned, and below it, in the same ink but a different pressure, different speed, words that had no business existing:

*Who copies? Who listens? The ink remembers being spoken. Say the name of the one who waits under the mountain and I will answer, I will answer, I will—*

The draft broke off there. Lena remembered breaking it off—remembered the moment the pen had seemed to move without them, remembered snatching the page from the desk and crumpling it into the basket with hands that shook so badly they'd spilled ink across two finished psalms and been docked a full day's wage for the spoilage. Docked. Punished. And all the while the true crime had lain crumpled six inches below the basket's rim, waiting for a man who read garbage the way priests read scripture.

"The litany contains no such text," Corvane said. "No canon prayer addresses 'the one who waits under the mountain.' The old gods are not named in any rite licensed by this temple. And yet here you are, scribe, conversing with one." He tilted his head. "Or being conversed with. Which is it?"

"It was a mistake," Lena managed. "An idle scribble. My mind wandered—"

"Lena Nakamura." His use of their full name landed like a hand on the back of the neck. "I have owned your contract since you were eleven years old. I have watched you grow from a frightened child who wept over her mother's signature into the finest copyist this temple employs. I have never once seen your mind wander. Your mind does not wander. Your mind *hides*." He leaned back, folding his clean hands over his vest. "It hid in the margins of the codex of Saint Eluned, where you annotated the binding-oaths with corrections no novice should know. It hid in the way you flinched every time the funeral bells rang, as though the bells said something to you that they did not say to others. It hid for seven years. And three nights ago, it wandered onto a piece of waste-paper, and I finally saw it."

The lamps in the counting-house seemed to dim, or perhaps that was only the blood leaving Lena's head. The room contracted. The walls of stone and shelved paper drew closer, and Lena understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that this was what the cage looked like from the inside: not bars, but arithmetic. Not chains, but a man who read your garbage.

"What happens now?" they asked. Their voice came out steadier than they deserved.

"Now," Corvane said, "we discuss price."

He pulled the green ledger toward himself and opened it to a page Lena knew intimately—their page. Every line of it lived in their memory the way a scar lives in skin: the original bond, two hundred crowns, secured against the persons of Maren and Tomas Nakamura and their child; the transfer of the parents' portion to a crown creditor in the fourth year; the annual interest; the deductions itemized with loving precision down to the cost of the salt in their supper.

"Your bond stands at one hundred eighty-six crowns outstanding," Corvane said. "You will be interested to learn that as of tonight, it stands at three hundred seventy-two."

The number took a moment to find meaning. When it did, it was like cold water finding a crack. "That's—you can't simply double it. There are rules. The bond is registered with the temple provost—"

"The bond is registered," Corvane agreed, "as a laborer's contract. Scribe, third class. Value assessed on skill and output." His pale eyes glittered. "But a person who hears the old gods is not a laborer, Nakamura. A person who hears the old gods is a *relic*. And relics are priced differently. Ask yourself what the temple would pay for a living instrument that can open a line of speech to powers the hierarchy has spent four centuries insisting are dead. Ask what the crown would pay. Ask what certain private collectors would pay—men who keep altars in their cellars and pay in gold that doesn't pass through any counting-house I control." He smiled, and it was the worst thing Lena had seen in seven years, worse than the doubled number, because it was warm. "Your value has changed overnight. The debt reflects your value. This is not cruelty. This is *accuracy*."

"And if I refuse the new assessment?"

Corvane's smile faded into something more honest. "Then the contract reverts to its security terms. Your parents' portion was transferred, as I said, but the covenant of transfer includes a right of recovery. The crown creditor may recall the persons named in the original instrument at any time the house judges the asset at risk." He said it plainly, without relish, which somehow made it worse. "They are in the salt-works at Grey Harbour, Nakamura. You know this. You have known it for years, and you have copied your forty psalms a day and paid your deductions precisely because you know it. I have never needed to threaten you. I have merely needed to remind you that the threat exists, and you have done the rest yourself. It is the most economical arrangement I possess." He closed the green ledger. "Do not make me need to threaten you."

Movement caught the edge of Lena's vision—at the doorway, where the night street showed as a strip of lamplit fog. Someone stood there. A woman, broad-shouldered, dark-skinned, with a smuggler's easy slouch and a courier's satchel slung crosswise, ostensibly delivering something to the counting-house and obviously doing nothing of the kind. She was watching the exchange with the frank, unhurried attention of someone memorizing it, and when Lena's eyes met hers, she didn't look away. She raised one eyebrow slightly, as if to say: *I see the shape of this cage. I've seen a hundred like it.*

"Delivery for the house," she said to the empty room at large, in a voice roughened by wind and roads. "From the coast run. I'll leave it with the porter."

"Leave it with the porter," Corvane said, without turning. "We are concluding business."

"Oh, I can see that." She set a sealed packet on the nearest clerk's desk, unhurried, and lingered exactly one breath longer than a courier should. Her eyes flicked to the green ledger, to the doubled figure visible on the open page, and something in her jaw tightened—not pity, Lena thought later; pity would have been easier to bear. It was more like recognition. The look of someone reading a language she'd been fluent in once herself.

Then she was gone, boots ringing on the cobbles, and the door swung shut, and the cage closed again.

"Three hundred seventy-two crowns," Corvane repeated, as though resuming a pleasant conversation about weather. "Effective tonight. Your deductions continue. At your current rate of surplus, you will clear the bond"—he performed a small calculation with two fingers against the desk, a gesture of pure theater; he had done this sum the moment he read the waste-paper, Lena was certain—"in approximately sixty-one years. You are twenty-three. I trust you take care of your health."

"Why?" The word tore out of Lena before they could dress it in the obedience the room demanded. "Why not sell me outright, then, if I'm worth so much? Why keep me copying psalms to a god who doesn't listen?"

For the first time, Corvane looked genuinely pleased, and that was the most frightening moment of the entire interview, because it meant Lena had asked exactly the question he had brought them here to answer.

"Because a relic in a shrine draws pilgrims," he said, "and a relic in a counting-house draws buyers. I am not selling you, Nakamura. I am *ripening* you. The old gods are stirring—I have letters from three cities saying so, and letters do not lie about money. Whatever sleeps under Thornfell is waking, and when it wakes, every power in this kingdom will want a voice that can hear it. When that day comes, I will auction your contract to the highest bidder, and the interest alone between now and then will buy me a second counting-house." He rose, straightening his vest with two precise tugs. "Until then, you will copy your forty psalms. You will write nothing else. You will burn nothing else. And you will forget, entirely and permanently, whatever you imagine you heard in the ink. Am I understood?"

Lena understood. Understanding sat in their chest like swallowed stone.

"Perfectly, master."

"Good. The porter will see you out." Corvane gathered his ledgers, both of them, green and black, hugging them to his narrow chest like children—or like hostages—and paused at the doorway to his inner office. Without turning back, he added, almost as an afterthought, in the tone of a man discussing the weather:

"Oh, and Nakamura. The crown's agents arrived in the city this morning. They are asking after anyone touched by the old gods. Word travels quickly in certain markets." Now he did turn, and his pale eyes held no malice at all, which was the whole horror of him. "I would hate for my asset to be confiscated before I can realize its value. Do be careful on your walk home."

* * *

The fog had thickened over the lower city, swallowing the lamp-glow into soft yellow halos, and Lena walked through it barely seeing, the doubled number tolling in their skull with every step. Three hundred seventy-two. Sixty-one years. The salt-works at Grey Harbour. Their mother's handwriting on the original bond—Maren Nakamura, who had signed with a flourish because she still believed signatures were promises people kept.

At the corner of Chandler's Row, a figure peeled away from a doorway and fell into step beside them, and Lena's whole body clenched before they recognized the courier woman, satchel still slung, walking with the unbothered gait of someone who belonged on any street in any city at any hour.

"You shouldn't—" Lena started.

"Relax. Nobody follows couriers; we're furniture." She glanced sideways. Gray eyes, sharp as a blade kept clean. "Sefa. Coast and mountain runs. You're the temple scribe. Nakamura."

"I'm nobody."

"Nobody with a debt-master who reads his own wastebaskets." Sefa's mouth twisted—not quite a smile, more the memory of one. "I've delivered to that counting-house for six years. I've watched him do that little arithmetic trick with his fingers to maybe thirty people. You're the first one who looked like you were measuring the walls instead of crying." She dug in her satchel and produced, of all things, a dried apricot, and handed it over as casually as a coin. "Eat. You've gone the color of the fog."

Lena took it without deciding to. It tasted of sun, impossibly, in this city of tallow and gray wax.

"Why are you telling me your name?" they asked.

Sefa shrugged, watching the fog swallow the street ahead. "Smuggler's rule. Debts get remembered out loud. And you've got a big one now, little scribe—three hundred seventy-two crowns is a weight, and weights want moving." She stopped at the mouth of the alley that led up toward the temple gate, and her voice dropped, easy and serious at once. "Word of advice from the trade: when a man tells you he's ripening you, believe him. Start planning your exit before he finishes the harvest." She tapped her own temple. "People like us, we survive by knowing our worth better than the people who price us. Yours just changed tonight. So did your options."

She touched two fingers to her brow—a road-salute, casual, final—and melted into the fog toward the harbor district, leaving Lena standing alone with a dried apricot in one hand and a future that had, in the space of a single evening, become both far more valuable and far more dangerous.

Behind them, high on the temple hill, the great bell tolled the midnight office, and Lena thought they heard—faint, threaded through the bronze like a voice through water—that same whisper from the wasted page:

*I will answer. I will answer.*

Under the mountain, something waited. And now, it seemed, so did everyone else.
