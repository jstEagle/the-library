## 3. The King Who Forgot His Daughter

The exile-brand on Quentin's wrist ached whenever he came within a mile of the palace, which was how he knew, before he saw it, that something in Thornhold had grown worse.

He told himself it was only scar tissue remembering its making—the iron pressed to his skin in the courtyard below, seven years ago now, while the assembled keepers of the Oath watched him kneel and did nothing. *You have spoken falsely against your king,* the High Keeper had said. *You have called him worn.* And Quentin had looked up through the smoke of the censers at Aldric's face on the balcony—Aldric, who had once pulled him out of a frozen river when they were boys, who had laughed so hard at his own coronation feast that he'd knocked over the wine—and he had said, because it was true and he would not unsay it: *Something is wearing him. I swear it by every oath I hold.*

For that truth, the brand. For that truth, exile. Seven years of walking the border roads with his keeper's sword sold for bread and his name struck from the rolls, until even he sometimes wondered whether he had dreamed the whole thing—whether the thing behind the king's eyes had been only grief, only age, only a father mourning a dead queen too long.

Then the brand began to ache again this autumn, sharper than it had in years, like a dog that scents its master coming home. And Quentin came down out of the hill country with frost in his beard and no plan at all except that he could not stay away any longer.

* * *

Thornfell in late autumn smelled of woodsmoke and wet stone and, beneath it all, the river—the same grey-green river that had nearly drowned him at nine years old, that Aldric had hauled him from with arms far too thin for the work. The city had not changed. That was the terrible part. The banners still hung from the eaves in the royal blue and silver; the fishwives still shouted their prices along the quay; children still dared each other onto the ice-thin edges of the fountain in Coronation Square. Life went on in Thornfell as it always had, dense and stubborn and loud, and above it all the palace sat on its crag like a patient animal, watching.

Quentin entered the city the way he had learned to enter cities during his exile: hood up, beard roughened with ash to dull its color, shoulders rounded so he took less space. A big man made small. It galled him every time. He had spent thirty years learning to stand straight—to be an oath-keeper, one of the hundred sworn swords whose word was iron throughout the Nine Valleys—and now he moved through his own capital like a thief in his mother's house.

But there were ways in. There had always been ways in, for a boy who grew up in the palace kitchens' shadow, and exile had taught him patience if nothing else. The laundresses' gate on the north side took deliveries at dusk. The gatekeeper was new—a young man, soft around the eyes, who counted carts but not faces. Quentin slipped into the train of a wool merchant's porters, carried two bales further than he needed to, and set them down inside the outer ward as though he belonged there, because for twenty years he had.

His heart was hammering. Not from fear, exactly. From proximity. He was close enough now to feel it—that wrongness he had named *worn* seven years ago and been branded for naming. It was like standing near a fire that gave off cold instead of heat. Like a hearth that drew warmth out of the room rather than into it. He had never found better words for it. The old books said the first kings of Thornfell had kept priests whose whole office was to watch the crown and its wearer, to make sure what sat upon the throne was still a man. Those offices had been dissolved three generations back. Everyone agreed they were superstition.

Everyone, Quentin thought grimly, except whoever or whatever had needed them dissolved.

* * *

He did not go looking for the king. He went looking for the princess.

This was the part of the plan—or the absence of plan—that shamed him most, and he turned it over in his mind as he waited out the changing of the guard in the disused chapel of Saint Vessa, where dust lay on the pews like snow no one bothered to sweep. Isolde was nineteen now. He had held her at her christening; he had taught her, briefly, in the year her mother died, how to hold a practice sword without breaking her own thumb. She had been a fierce, bright, grieving child, and then she had become a careful, silent young woman, and Quentin had watched that transformation happen from across a border he was forbidden to cross, hearing about it secondhand in smugglers' taverns: *the princess keeps to herself; the princess doesn't laugh anymore; the princess asked a visiting scholar about old coronation rites, can you imagine.*

That last piece of gossip had followed him for three hundred miles. It had brought him here.

Because either Isolde suspected what he suspected—in which case she might be the only ally in Thornfell with the standing to act—or she was already lost to it, already hollowed, already another empty vessel walking the palace halls. And he would not know which until he saw her.

The chapel bell tolled vespers. Through the arrow-slit window, Quentin watched the light go copper and then grey over the inner court, and he saw her come.

She crossed the court alone. No ladies, no guards—two guards trailed at a distance, but they stopped at the colonnade, and she went on without them, and no one objected, and that itself was a kind of answer. In seven years, Quentin thought, the court had developed customs around the king's affliction the way a body develops customs around a wound: don't touch it, don't look at it directly, keep the dressing changed. The princess visited her father alone. The guards did not follow. Everyone pretended this was normal.

She walked slowly. That was the detail that undid him. Nineteen years old, and she crossed her father's courtyard the way penitents cross the nave of a cathedral—head slightly bowed, hands folded, as though approaching something holy and dangerous at once. As though she had rehearsed this walk so many times that the rehearsal had eaten whatever spontaneous daughter used to run these stones.

Quentin left the chapel and followed the cloister walk that overlooked the inner garden, keeping to the shadows of the columns, and below him, through the bare branches of the winter pear trees, he watched the King of Thornfell sit in his chair by the dry fountain and fail to recognize his daughter.

* * *

It should be said that Aldric looked well. That was the horror of it, and Quentin understood now why the court had stopped whispering, why the physicians pronounced him sound, why the realm slept. The man in the chair was broad through the chest still, silver at the temples, dressed richly in winter furs, his hands resting on the arms of his chair with a king's easy weight. His face was Aldric's face, unlined by anything worse than age. If you passed him in a corridor you would think: *there is a man at peace.*

But peace is not the same as presence. Quentin had spent seven years among the hollowed-out—the war-broken, the grief-mad, the ones the brand of exile seemed to collect like driftwood—and he knew the difference between a man resting and a man absent. Aldric's eyes were open and pointed at the world, but nothing arrived behind them. They were windows with the shutters painted on.

Isolde knelt at his feet. Quentin heard her voice carry up through the cold air, pitched carefully low, the way you speak to a spooked animal.

"Father. It's me. It's Isolde."

"Of course it is." The king smiled. The smile was warm and perfect and reached nothing. "And how is my daughter today?"

The words landed on the garden path between them like dropped glass. Quentin saw Isolde's shoulders take the blow—he saw it happen, the small collapse and reassembly, practiced a thousand times—and when she spoke again her voice had not wavered at all, and he marveled at the strength of that, and hated everything that had made such strength necessary.

"I'm well, Father. I've come to sit with you. May I?"

"You may always sit with me." Aldric gestured graciously to the bench beside his chair, the way he might gesture to a petitioning burgher. "Tell me, child—your face is familiar. Have we met? At some feast? You have your mother's chin."

"My mother," Isolde said, "was Queen Maren."

"Queen Maren." The king nodded slowly, tasting the name. "Yes. Yes, I remember Maren. A fine woman. Gone too soon." His brow furrowed, and for one moment—one flickering, human moment—something moved behind the painted shutters, some vast slow thing turning over in the deep water of him, and his hand rose halfway toward his daughter's bowed head and stopped there, trembling, as though it had forgotten mid-gesture what it was reaching for. "Gone too soon," he repeated, softer. "Why does my chest hurt when I say her name?"

"Because you loved her," Isolde whispered.

"Do I?" The king considered this with genuine, gentle curiosity. "I suppose I must have. I can't recall it."

And that was the sentence, Quentin thought, that should have brought the whole court running with torches and blades. *I can't recall it.* A man does not misplace love. Grief fades, memory blurs at the edges, but the shape of having loved someone stays burned into a person the way a brand stays burned into skin. What sat in that chair remembered facts the way a ledger remembers sums—but the sum of Queen Maren, the living total of her, was gone, and gone cleanly, and gone without pain, and that cleanliness was the tell. Real forgetting hurts. This was not forgetting. This was feeding.

The brand on Quentin's wrist burned like a live coal. He pressed it flat against the stone balustrade and did not move.

Below, Isolde had begun, quietly, to do what she evidently did every visit: she fed her father himself. She told him stories. Small ones, domestic ones, chosen with the precision of a surgeon choosing instruments.

"You flew a kite with me once," she said. "When I was six. On the east meadow. You made it yourself, out of banner-cloth, because I cried that the other girls had kites and I didn't. You wouldn't let anyone help. You got the tail tangled in a tree and climbed after it and tore your sleeve, and Mother was furious, and you laughed until you couldn't breathe."

The king listened with polite attention, nodding at intervals, the way one listens to a pleasant ballad in a language one almost speaks. "What a charming story," he said at last. "Did I? How kind of me."

"It's *you*, Father. It's the truest thing I know about you."

"Is it?" He smiled that warm, perfect, vacant smile. "Then I'm glad someone remembers it. One of us ought to."

Isolde's composure cracked. Only for a heartbeat—only enough for Quentin, watching from the dark of the cloister, to see what lived underneath the princess's care: not patience. Rage. Grief compressed for seven years into a diamond hard enough to cut. Her hands closed in her lap; her jaw set; and when she lifted her head her eyes were wet and blazing and fixed on her father's face with an intensity that would have frightened him, Quentin thought, if anything in that face could still be frightened.

"What are you?" she said. Very quietly. Almost tenderly. "What are you, and what have you done with him?"

The king tilted his head. And for a moment—for three heartbeats, four—the smile stayed exactly where it was, but the face behind it changed. Quentin had no other word for it. The muscles didn't move; the expression didn't alter; and yet the meaning of the face inverted entirely, the way a mask looks different when you realize it's a mask. Something regarded the princess from behind her father's eyes. Something old and patient and interested.

"Careful, little kite," the king said pleasantly.

Isolde went white. She stood—too fast, her knees unsteady—and for a moment Quentin thought she would flee, and she did not; she gathered herself with visible, magnificent effort, curtsied to the thing in her father's chair as though it were her father, and said, in a voice restored to perfect courtly calm:

"Thank you for receiving me, Your Majesty. I'll come again next week, as always."

"As always," the king agreed warmly. "Give my love to my daughter."

* * *

Quentin did not remember deciding to follow her. He simply found himself moving, down the cloister stairs, across the colonnaded walk, catching up to her at the door to the queen's old apartments—which she still used, he noted, refusing the newer wing—as the guards at the corridor's end looked elsewhere with the studied blindness of men paid to see nothing.

"Your Highness."

She spun. He had meant to be gentle and instead he had startled her badly, and he saw her hand go to her belt where a knife wasn't, and he raised both palms and stepped back into the light so she could see his face.

Recognition moved through her expression in stages: confusion, disbelief, and then something raw and complicated that he had no right to expect and received anyway.

"Ser Quentin," she breathed. "They said you were dead."

"They say a lot of things." He glanced down the corridor. The guards had drifted further off; the palace was winding itself into evening; the lamps were being lit by servants who moved quickly and looked at no one. "We shouldn't talk here."

"No," she agreed. "We shouldn't." But she didn't move, and neither did he, and in the lamplight he watched her study him—the exile-brand just visible under his pushed-back sleeve, the ash-greyed beard, the poverty of him—and he watched her decide something. When she spoke, her voice was very quiet and very steady.

"You said it first. Seven years ago. Before the council, before everyone. You said something was *wearing* him." Her eyes were bright and hard. "Everyone called you mad. My father—" she stopped; corrected herself; the correction cost her visibly—"the *king* wanted you exiled rather than executed, and no one understood why, and I have spent seven years understanding why. Because some part of him, underneath, knew you were right. Didn't he."

"Yes," Quentin said. "I believe he did."

"And you've come back." Not a question. She stepped closer, and up close he could see the sleeplessness carved under her eyes, the ink stains on her fingers—ink, on a princess's hands, hours of secret writing—and the fragment of banner-cloth, faded blue and silver, tied around her wrist like a relic. "You've come back because it's getting worse. Because the valleys are going quiet. Because people are starting to forget things they shouldn't forget." Her voice did not rise at all, and somehow that was louder than shouting. "So have I. Come back, I mean. To this question. Every single day for seven years."

"Your Highness—"

"Don't call me that." Fiercely, a whisper. "Not here. Not about this. Here I'm just the girl who visits a stranger in her father's chair every week and tells him stories he'll never keep." Her breath shuddered once, and stilled. "You were branded for telling the truth, Ser Quentin. I have been careful for seven years, and careful has bought me nothing but more careful. So." She lifted her chin, and for a moment she was six years old on the east meadow, furious and fearless under a torn-banner kite. "Tell me what you know. Tell me what it is. And then tell me what you intend to do about it—because I am done watching, and if you have a plan that ends with that thing off my father's throne, then I don't care if it's treason, madness, or heresy. I am in."

Above them, through the high window, the last light failed over the crag, and somewhere in the palace a bell rang the hour, and Quentin felt the brand on his wrist pulse once—like a second heartbeat, like something answering—and he understood that there was no walking away now, not for either of them.

"There's an old rite," he said slowly. "Older than the crown. The first oath was sworn somewhere in the mountains, and what was sworn can be unsworn—if we can find where, and if we can reach it, and if we can get a king out from under a spirit old enough to have eaten half its own history." He paused. "It will need help. The kind of help that doesn't ask questions. The kind that moves through the passes unseen."

Something flickered at the corner of Isolde's mouth—not quite a smile; the ghost of one, the first he'd seen on her. "Smugglers," she said.

"Smugglers," Quentin agreed.

"Then we'd best find one," said the Princess of Thornfell, "before it eats the rest of us."
