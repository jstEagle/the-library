## 4. Roads Through Thornfell

The mule was named Bishop because he had a habit of looking down his nose at people, and Sefa had learned long ago that the best way to survive a lie was to give it a name everyone could believe.

"Keep your head down when we pass the tollhouse," she said, not turning in the saddle. Behind her, on a second mule that answered to the dignified name of Turnip, the disgraced oath-keeper of Thornfell sat wrapped in a dyer's smock two sizes too big, hood up, hands gloved to hide the brand that circled his wrist like a shackle made of fire. "And if anyone asks, you're my cousin Aldous, who took a fever as a child and hasn't been right since. Drool a little if you have to. You look like a man who could drool convincingly."

"I am an anointed keeper of the High Oath," Quentin said, with the particular dignity of a man whose voice was muffled by wool.

"You're a wanted man in a smock the color of eggplant," Sefa said. "Eggplant, Quentin. I want you to sit with that. I paid four coppers for that smock, and the dyer threw it in free because nobody else would take it."

He muttered something that might have been a prayer or might have been a curse. With oath-keepers it was often hard to tell the difference; they prayed like they were filing grievances.

Sefa let him sulk and turned her attention to the pass.

Thornfell in autumn was a country pretending to be gentle. The lower slopes wore their gold and rust like festival clothes, birches blazing against grey stone, and the air had that clean cold edge that made a person feel briefly honest. But Sefa knew what lived under the prettiness: switchbacks that crumbled without warning, weather that changed its mind like a debtor changing his story, and wardens—crown wardens now, which was worse—who had started asking questions at every crossing since the old gods woke. Questions were how it always began. First they asked where you were going. Then they asked who you were. Then they stopped asking and just took.

She'd been running these roads since she was nine years old, first behind her mother's shoulder, then beside her, then alone after the fever winter took half the camp and the other half scattered like seed. The passes were the only parent she had left, and like any parent they were moody, demanding, and occasionally tried to kill her. She loved them anyway.

"Bishop," she murmured, and the mule's ears swiveled back toward her, acknowledging. Good. He'd been skittish since the last ford, and a skittish mule on a cliff road was a sermon on mortality nobody needed twice.

They climbed through the morning, the road narrowing until the drop on their left was less a drop than a philosophical concept—namely, the concept of how quickly a person could stop being a person. Below, the Vell river ran thin and bright as wire. Above, the first snows already clung to the high shoulders of the mountains, and Sefa found herself watching them longer than she meant to. Snow this early meant a hard winter. A hard winter meant hungry passes, hungry villages, hungry prices—and hungry people did desperate arithmetic with other people's lives.

"So," she said, partly to break the silence and partly because she'd been chewing on it since dawn. "The scribe."

Behind her, Quentin shifted. "What scribe?"

"Don't do that." She clicked her tongue at Bishop. "You've been doing that all morning. The 'what scribe' face. You know exactly what scribe. The one from the Ashen Temple scriptorium. The one the whole river trade is whispering about." She glanced back over her shoulder. Under the hood, his jaw had gone tight. "That one."

"Gossip," Quentin said.

"Sefa's Second Law of the Passes: gossip moves faster than soldiers and lies lighter than gold." She held up fingers. "First Law is never trust a bridge you haven't insulted personally, but we'll get to that. Point is, I hear things. It's my job. And what I hear is that there's a debt-scribe in the temple city who copies prayers for the ledger-masters, and somewhere in the last season, the prayers started copying *back*."

The wind came up the valley then, carrying the smell of pine resin and distant woodsmoke, and for a moment neither of them spoke.

"They say," Sefa went on, quieter, because some stories deserved it even secondhand, "that the scribe writes out petitions to gods nobody's worshipped properly in three hundred years, dead names, dry ink, the whole sad routine. And one day the ink answers. Writes back. In the margins, maybe, or under the lines—I've heard both versions. And this scribe, instead of running screaming into the river like any sensible person, hides the page and goes on copying." She shook her head. "Seven years, some say. Seven years hearing the dead and saying nothing."

"Do you believe it?" Quentin asked. His voice had changed. Less grievance, more hunger. That was the thing about the man: he wore exile like a hair shirt, but underneath it he still believed in something, which in Sefa's professional experience was either the most dangerous or the most useful quality a passenger could have.

"I believe the story's traveling faster than the truth usually does," she said. "Which means there's a coal under the smoke. And I believe—" she hesitated, then decided to spend it, because he'd paid honestly and honest passengers deserved honest goods, "—I believe my mother heard a story once, before the fever winter, about temple children who could hear the dead. Old blood, she called it. Blood from before the contracts. The crown used to buy those children. Or burn them. Depends which century."

"And now?"

"Now the old gods are waking up, the king's gone strange, and the wardens are asking questions at every crossing." She spread her hands, reins and all. "You tell me what happens to a temple scribe who hears the dead, when the crown comes looking for exactly that."

Quentin was quiet for a long stretch of road. When he finally spoke, it was low. "I accused the king of being worn. Of being *occupied*. They exiled me for it, stripped my oath, branded me a liar." His gloved hand flexed on the pommel. "If that scribe is real—if the dead are speaking and someone can hear them—then I wasn't lying. And everything I lost might be worth something again."

"There it is," Sefa said.

"There what is?"

"The reason you hired me. Not the shrine. Not the mountain. *Vindication.*" She grinned over her shoulder. "Don't be ashamed. Half my cargo is people carrying the same thing. It packs easy, travels well, and sells for nothing anywhere, which is why they carry it themselves."

"You are an infuriating woman."

"My mother said the same, usually while throwing things. It's hereditary." She nudged Bishop around a fallen slab of granite furred with moss. "But here's what I actually think, since you asked so charmingly. I think a person who spends seven years listening to ghosts and hiding it—that's not a freak. That's a prisoner keeping contraband hope under their tongue. And I think if the crown gets to them first, we'll never know what the dead were trying to say. So." She shrugged elaborately. "Maybe we get there first. If the price is right."

"You're going to charge me extra for a rescue."

"I'm going to charge you extra for *ambition*. Rescue is included in the base rate. Ambition costs."

* * *

They took the midday meal in the shadow of the Shepherd's Tooth, a rock spire that leaned over the trail like a disapproving elder, and Sefa broke out the flatbread and hard cheese and the little jar of honeyed figs she saved for bribes and bad days, and couldn't have told you which this was.

Quentin ate like a man who'd been taught manners by a monastery and hunger by the road, which is to say neatly and too fast. Up close, in the flat white light, she could see what the exile had done to him. He was younger than his greying temples suggested—forty, maybe, no more—and there was a carefulness to his movements, a man perpetually braced for a door to slam. The brand peeked above his glove when he reached for the bread: a circle broken by a slash, the mark of a sworn word retracted. She'd seen keepers with that mark before. They all had the same look, like dogs that had been told, gently and forever, that they were no longer good.

"Ask it," Quentin said suddenly.

"Ask what?"

"Whatever you've been circling since the tollhouse. You have a face like a merchant counting coins. Ask."

Sefa chewed, considered, and spent another coin of honesty. "Fine. Why does an exiled oath-keeper care about a scribe? You said vindication. I don't fully buy it. Vindication doesn't get men in eggplant smocks over the Thornfell passes in the first snow week. Something else is pulling you."

For a moment she thought he wouldn't answer. Then he looked out at the valley—the little terraced farms, the smoke rising straight in the still air, a church bell somewhere below ringing noon with the fat, contented confidence of a bell that had never been questioned—and his face did something complicated.

"When I was sworn," he said, "I kept the king's roads. All of them. Every mile from the capital to the salt coast. Do you know what I learned on those roads?"

"That they're badly maintained?"

"That the kingdom isn't the king." He said it simply, like a man setting down something heavy. "It's the toll-keepers and the millers and the women who walk three miles to argue about a boundary stone. It's the smugglers who move grain through a blockade so a village doesn't starve, breaking the law and keeping the faith while the king's own granaries rot full." He looked at her, and there was no accusation in it, which somehow made it worse. Better. Both. "I failed my oath. I saw the king wearing something that wasn't the king, and I said so too late and too quietly, and a girl in the palace—a child—paid for my caution. So no, mistress Sefa, I don't care about the scribe for vindication. Or not only. I care because if the dead are speaking, then someone is finally telling the truth in this kingdom, and I would very much like to stand next to them when they say it."

The wind moved through the pines. Somewhere upslope, a ptarmigan complained about its entire life.

"Well," Sefa said at last, and cleared her throat, annoyed to find it needed clearing. "That was almost worth the figs."

* * *

The trouble started, as trouble reliably did, at the tollhouse.

It squatted at the junction of the high road and the river path, a stone box with a plank across the road and a brazier in front of it, and this time there were three wardens instead of the usual one, and they weren't checking cargo. They were checking faces. One of them held a paper, and even at fifty yards Sefa could see the wax seal at its bottom, red as a wound.

"Crown business," she murmured. "Cousin Aldous. Fever. Drool if needed."

"I remember."

"Also, new plan. We're not cousins anymore. We're pilgrims. You're taking me to the shrine at Saint Verity's Ford because I made a vow for my mother's soul and I'm weepy about it. Can you be weepy?"

"I once wept in front of the High Council."

"Perfect. Method acting."

She swung down before the plank, leading both mules, letting her hips do the work of a woman with nowhere to be and nothing to hide, and smiled at the senior warden with the specific warmth of someone about to cost him time.

"Pilgrims?" the warden said. He was young, with the unfortunate earnestness of a man who still believed his job meant something. "Road's closed past the Tooth. Crown writ. We're looking for a fugitive."

"A fugitive! On *this* road?" Sefa pressed a hand to her chest. "Officer, we're going to Saint Verity's. My mother—" her voice caught, beautifully, "—my mother passed at midsummer, and I vowed a candle at the Ford shrine, and my cousin here is escorting me because I'm not strong since the grieving. Is it the tall one you're wanting? Because he's simple, poor lamb, been simple since the fever—"

"I'm simple," Quentin agreed, from beneath the hood, in a voice of profound and drooling serenity.

The young warden looked at him. Quentin looked back with the empty, mild patience of a cow in rain. It was, Sefa conceded privately, a masterwork. Seven years of temple corridors had clearly taught the man to be invisible in plain sight.

"It's not a man we're wanting," the warden said, and unrolled the paper. "It's a scribe. Out of the Ashen Temple. Small, dark-haired, answers to—" he squinted, "—no name given. Wanted for consorting with forbidden spirits. Crown pays thirty crowns for word of them. Fifty for the scribe delivered."

Something went cold and quiet in Sefa's chest, and she kept smiling, because that was the job.

"A scribe," she repeated. "Consorting. My goodness. What does that even mean?"

"Writes to things that aren't gods anymore," the second warden said, spitting to the side. "Temple's being cleaned out next week. Archives and all. King's orders." He lowered his voice into the conspiratorial register of small men with news. "They say the old ones are waking. Whole valleys forgetting things, up north. My sister wrote me that her husband's village buried a man and nobody could recall his name to carve the stone. *Nobody.*"

"Terrible," Sefa said, and meant it, and hated meaning it, because it confirmed the third thing she'd heard on the river docks last month and hadn't wanted to be true.

The young warden waved them through with the distracted air of a man already thinking about his sister's letter. "Go with the gods, pilgrim. Whichever ones are left."

"Oh, officer," Sefa said, gathering the reins, "they're all left. That's rather the problem, isn't it."

She didn't breathe properly until the tollhouse was a smudge behind them and the road bent north into the high country, where the pines grew twisted and the silence had teeth.

"Next week," Quentin said quietly. "They're burning the archives next week."

"Cleaning them out. Burning's a kind of cleaning, if you're the crown." Sefa stared up the trail, doing sums. The shrine Quentin wanted lay eight days north through country that was about to fill with wardens hunting a nameless scribe. The scribe herself sat in a scriptorium a week south, about to lose every book she'd ever hidden in. And between those two facts, like a coin balanced on its edge, stood Sefa's crew, her debts, her mother's memory, and the increasingly loud opinion of her own conscience.

"You're thinking of turning around," Quentin said.

"I'm thinking that I run cargo, not crusades." She said it automatically, the way she said *the rate is the rate* and *no names on the mountain*, the litany that kept her alive and alone and fine. "I'm thinking that fifty crowns is a lot of money, and I'm thinking that a person who hears the dead is either the most dangerous thing in Thornfell or the most valuable, and I'm thinking I don't like the crown deciding which."

"But?"

"But nothing. Shut up. I'm thinking." She pulled Bishop to a halt at the overlook where the whole northern range showed itself, white-capped and indifferent, the shrine's peak just visible as a grey finger against the sky. Below, far south, a thread of smoke rose from the direction of the temple city—too big for a chimney, too steady for a signal fire. Archive smoke. Already. They'd lied to the warden's face about the schedule, or the schedule had moved, or the crown had simply stopped pretending there was a schedule.

Or—her stomach turned—the smoke wasn't the archive at all. There was a scriptorium attached to it. Scriptoria had people in them. People who copied prayers that answered back.

"Turnip," she said, and the second mule flicked an ear. "Bishop. New heading."

"We're going south." Quentin had seen the smoke too. His voice had gone strange—hope and dread braided together, the sound of a man watching his vindication possibly die on a hillside. "Sefa. The roads south are crawling. You said yourself—"

"I know what I said." She wheeled the mules with her knees, already cataloguing the route: the drover's cut behind the Tooth, the ford at Miller's Bend where the bridge owed her a favor, the charcoal-burners' paths that didn't exist on any crown map because she'd helped make sure of it. "Here's how this works, keeper, and listen close, because I'll only say it once. Smugglers have a code older than your oaths and kept better than your kings'. We honor debts. We don't sell passengers. And we don't leave people behind to burn just because rescuing them is inconvenient." She kicked Bishop into motion, downhill, south, toward the smoke. "Besides, I owe a candle at Saint Verity's, and I figure the fastest way to earn the coin is to go steal a miracle from under the crown's nose."

"And if the scribe doesn't want saving?"

"Then we'll be two idiots in an eggplant smock who died for nothing, and honestly, Quentin, at our age? There are worse endings." She grinned into the wind, and found, to her considerable irritation, that she meant it. "Ride. We're burning daylight, and somebody down there is about to need us."

Far to the south, the smoke thickened, and the mountain wind carried, faintly, the smell of burning paper—the smell, Sefa thought grimly, of a kingdom trying to forget something, and a scribe who had seven years of remembering to answer for.
