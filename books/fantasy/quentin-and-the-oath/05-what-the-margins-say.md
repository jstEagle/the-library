## 5. What the Margins Say

The forbidden codex smelled of cedar smoke and old fear.

Lena had learned to read a book's history the way other scribes read its text—the way a spine cracked too often at one place meant someone had returned to those pages again and again; the way candle-wax freckles on a cover told of midnight reading by someone who could not risk a lamp; the way certain books were bound in covers too plain for their contents, deliberately dressed in poverty so no curious eye would linger. This one was dressed like a ledger of grain shipments from a decade no one remembered, and it lied about itself with every stitch of its binding.

It had come up from the under-croft three days ago in a crate of genuine ledgers, sent for recopying because the temple's accounting required duplicates of everything—debt, tithe, penance, prayer. Everything in Thornfell was written twice, as if repetition could make any of it true. Lena had been sorting the crate by hand when their fingers found the false spine, and something in the dried ink beneath their fingertips had *shivered*.

Not the voice. Not yet. Just a shiver, like a sleeper turning over.

Lena should have reported it. There was a procedure for anomalous texts—a slip filed with Sub-Prior Edevane, the crate sealed, the book carried away to a room on the third floor that no one spoke about and from which no book ever returned. Lena had filled out such slips before, back when they were new and obedient, back before they understood that the third-floor room was where the temple buried its questions.

Instead they had slipped the codex beneath their cloak, beneath the loose fall of their scribal robe, and carried it to their cell like a stolen coal cupped against the wind.

Now, three nights later, by the light of a stub of tallow candle shielded behind a screen of folded vellum, Lena opened it again.

* * *

The cell was narrow enough that Lena could touch both walls with outstretched elbows. A cot, a stool, a writing board chained to the wall—temple furniture, temple rules, temple life measured out in cells and bells for seven years. The window was a slit, high and thin, and through it Thornfell showed itself in slices: the black shoulder of the mountain, the terraced roofs of the lower town, and beyond both, far off, the pale gleam of the royal road winding toward the capital where King Aldric sat his throne and did not, if the rumors were true, quite sit it anymore.

Lena did not let themselves think about the rumors. Rumors were how scribes disappeared.

The codex lay open across their knees. Its true contents were heretical in the ordinary way—invocations to gods whose names had been struck from the liturgy two centuries past, prayers addressed to powers the Concord of Ashes had declared never to have existed at all. Lena copied such things without believing them; belief was not part of a scribe's wage. But it was not the main text that made their hands unsteady tonight.

It was the margins.

Someone had written in the margins of this book a long time ago—not glosses, not corrections, but a record. The handwriting changed down the years, page by page, generation by generation: a heavy clerical hand giving way to a cramped, hurried one, giving way to another, each inheriting the book from the last like a relay baton passed in the dark. And threaded through all of them, recurring across decades like a refrain, were variations of the same words:

*The crown eats. The crown remembers what it eats. What it eats is lost.*

*There is one rite. One only. We wrote it here so it would survive us.*

*The Oath of Unbinding.*

Lena traced the phrase with one fingertip, careful not to touch ink to ink. Their gift lived in their skin—in the pads of the fingers, in the palms, in the strange sympathy between their blood and anything that had ever been written or spoken with feeling enough to leave a residue. Other people read words. Lena *heard* them, faintly, the way you might hear rain through a roof: not the words themselves but the pressure of them, the weight of whoever had set them down. Most writing was quiet. Ledgers murmured. Prayers, even unanswered ones, hummed like struck bowls.

The marginalia in this book *spoke*.

Not aloud. Never aloud, except—except once, three nights ago, in the scriptorium, in the answered prayer. Lena pushed that memory down again, the way they had pushed it down every day since, and failed again, the way they always failed. A voice in the dried ink. A voice that had said *I hear you* in return. Seven years of copying prayers into silence, seven years of hiding what they could do in the margins of honest documents, and the first time the world had ever answered back, it had answered in a scriptorium full of witnesses-in-waiting.

They had hidden the page. Folded it small and sewn it into the hem of their spare robe, where it lay now like a swallowed secret. If Corvane found it—

No. They would not think about Corvane either. Corvane was tomorrow's problem, and tomorrow arrived on its own schedule regardless.

Tonight there was only the book, and the margin-writers, and the rite.

* * *

The Oath of Unbinding was described across eleven pages of margin, in fragments, in the voices of at least four different hands. Lena read it the way an archaeologist reads a shattered pot—assembling, guessing, grieving the missing pieces.

The first hand, the heavy clerical one, wrote with the confidence of a man who believed he was recording fact:

*The spirit in the crown is not a god. It is what remains when a man refuses to die. It cannot be killed because it has already given up everything killable. It can only be starved, and it can only be starved by feeding it.*

That sentence stopped Lena cold. They read it three times. *Fed.* Fed what?

The second hand, cramped and hurried, answered two pages later—and the ink here felt different under Lena's fingers, felt *afraid*, the residue of terror soaked so deep into the strokes that Lena's palm prickled as though plunged in icy water:

*A complete life. That is the price. Not a memory, not a year, not a grief—all of it, every hour from first breath to last, poured out willingly. The rite fails if the giver holds anything back. The crown takes nothing; that is the horror of it. It only accepts. Someone must stand before it and give it everything they are, freely, and be emptied.*

*We searched for another way for forty years. There is none. Vesh swore there was none, and Vesh knew more than any of us.*

Vesh. The name surfaced again further along, in the third hand—a woman's writing, fine and precise, dated by internal evidence to perhaps a century past:

*Old Vesh's binding still holds in the mountain, they say, though the priests who remember him are dust. He gave himself to hold the first oath together when the crown was forged. If the crown stirs, the mountain will know before we do. Listen for the mountain.*

And then, near the end, in a fourth hand that shook so badly the letters barely held their shapes:

*It is awake. I felt it wake. The king's eyes have gone wrong. God help us, it is awake, and we are the last who know the rite, and I am dying, and I am writing this in the only place the wardens will never look—in the margins of a lie.*

After that, the margins went silent. Two hundred years of blank space. The book had waited in the dark under the temple, holding its dead writers' words, waiting for eyes.

Lena sat very still, the candle guttering, and felt the shape of the thing settle over them like snowfall.

A possessing spirit. In the crown. Feeding on memories—on whole lives, willingly surrendered, because it could take nothing otherwise. And the king's eyes gone wrong, and the villages in the northern valleys that had stopped answering letters, and the soldiers home on furlough who stared at their own mothers like strangers—

The rumors were not rumors. The margins were history. The margins were the only honest document in Thornfell.

And somewhere in the mountain above the town, something called Vesh was bound, listening, the last priest of the old gods holding shut a door that had just begun to creak.

* * *

"You're reading it wrong."

Lena's heart slammed against their ribs. The candle flame bent sideways though there was no draft.

The voice came from everywhere and nowhere—from the codex, from the walls, from the ink-stained skin of Lena's own hands. It was dry as autumn leaves and old as the cedar smell of the book, and it was not the voice from the answered prayer. That voice had been young, startled, curious. This one was ancient, and tired, and it enunciated each word like a man laying stones.

"You're reading it wrong," it said again. "You read with your eyes. Read with what you are."

"I—" Lena's whisper cracked. They pressed their palms flat against the codex, and the world tilted. "Who are you?"

"Later." Impatience moved through the words like wind through wheat. "There is no time for names. Listen. You heard me three nights ago. You heard me when no one else in that stone box could. So hear me now, scribe, and hear me *entirely*."

Lena gripped the edge of the writing board until their knuckles whitened. "What are you?"

"The warning you need before the name I'll give you after. The crown is coming."

The words dropped into the cell like stones into a well. Lena heard them fall and fall and never land.

"It knows what sleeps in your archives," the voice continued. "Two hundred years ago it burned what it could reach and missed this book by the width of a coward's conscience. It has not forgotten. It does not forget—that is the whole obscenity of it. It keeps everything it eats, perfectly, forever. And it has begun to feed openly now, which means it is strong, which means it is *hunting*. Every text that speaks of the old contracts. Every rite of unbinding. Every mind that remembers them." A pause, and when the voice resumed it was quieter, almost gentle, which was somehow worse. "Every ear that hears the dead. Do you understand me, scribe? Your gift is not a secret anymore. It rang through that scriptorium like a bell. Things that hunt memories can hear a bell."

Lena's mouth had gone dry as the ink. "The archives," they managed. "When?"

"Soon. Days. Perhaps less. The debt-masters will sell what the crown wants—they always do; it is the oldest trade in the world, flesh for favor. And you—" the voice seemed to lean closer, filling the cell, pressing against Lena's skin from the inside, "—you are a text, little scribe. Did you never realize? You are a document that walks and breathes and hears. Everything you have ever heard the dead say is written in you. When the crown comes for the archives, it will come for *you*."

The candle chose that moment to drown in its own wax, and darkness took the cell whole.

In the dark, Lena sat with the codex in their lap and their own heartbeat loud as siege drums, and thought, absurdly, of the margins. Of four dead hands passing a truth down two centuries of silence so that someone—anyone—might find it in time. They had spent seven years hiding themselves in margins, writing their gift into the edges of honest documents where no auditor would look. They had never once considered that they might *be* the margin—that the whole world was a main text of kings and debts and ledgers, and people like Lena lived in the white space at the edge of the page, unnoticed, unread, until some greater hand decided to erase them.

"There is a shrine," the voice said in the darkness, softer now. "In the mountain. Where the first oath was sworn. If it comes to the rite—if it comes to the pouring-out—it must be done there, where the oath is strong enough to hold the channel open. Remember that. Whatever else you lose, remember that."

"I can't remember anything," Lena whispered. "That's the point of me. I hide things. I'm good at hiding things."

"Hiding is not forgetting." The voice was fading now, drawing back down into whatever deep place it lived, and its last words came like a tide going out. "Hide yourself well, scribe. Hide the rite in your bones. And when the soldiers come—and they will come—run toward the mountain, not away from it. Everything you need is above you. Everything hunting you is below."

Then silence, total, the silence of a book closing.

Lena sat in the dark until their eyes adjusted, until the window-slit showed its thin slice of mountain black against sky blacker. Then, with hands that shook only a little less than they had an hour ago, they did what a scribe does. They took fresh vellum, and by the light of a relit wick no larger than a moth, they copied the Oath of Unbinding entire—every fragment, every shaking hand, every gap where the margin-writers' knowledge had failed—in a cipher of their own devising, disguised as a prayer commentary, disguised as boredom itself.

They hid it in the lining of their writing-board case, beside the folded page from the scriptorium, beside seven years of secrets.

Then they blew out the light and lay on the cot and listened to the temple breathe around them—the snores of the brothers, the settling of old stone, the distant bell tolling the small hours—and tried not to hear, underneath it all, faint and far away, the sound of the mountain. Listening back.

Three days later, a runner in livery the color of the crown arrived at the temple gate, and Debt-master Corvane sent for Lena Nakamura before the man had finished dismounting.
