## 6. Sold to the Crown

The summons came at the hour of second ink, when the scriptorium's light was thinnest and every scribe's hand ached in the same place, the joint of the thumb where the pen rests its whole life long.

"Lena Nakamura." The novice who delivered it would not meet their eyes. "Debt-master Corvane requires you in the counting room. Now."

Lena set down their pen with exaggerated care, as if slowness could make the summons less real. Around them, forty heads stayed bent over forty pages, but they felt the attention like heat—the way everyone in Thornfell's Great Temple had learned to listen without looking, to know things sideways, to survive by pretending not to see. They had done it themselves for seven years. Now they were the thing being not-seen.

*He knows,* said the voice that lived sometimes in dried ink and sometimes, lately, in the marrow of Lena's own bones. It was Vesh's voice, though Lena did not yet have a name for it—only a presence, old and dry and watchful, like wind through a sealed room. *He has known since the prayer answered you back.*

Lena walked the colonnade with their hands folded and their breath counted, four in, six out, the way they'd taught themselves to breathe through beatings as a child. The temple corridors were beautiful in the way of all places built by people who never had to live in them: vaulted, gold-veined, cold. Seven years Lena had copied prayers here for the glory of gods who never answered anyone but them, and every copper of their wages had gone, and would always go, into Corvane's ledger against a debt inherited from parents whose faces they could barely afford to remember.

The counting room smelled of tallow and vinegar and old paper. Corvane sat behind his desk like a spider that had grown tired of webs and graduated to furniture. He was not a large man. He had the smallness of men who have made themselves dense with other people's obligations, compacted over decades into something heavy and hard to move.

"Sit."

Lena sat. The chair was deliberately lower than the visitor's chair across the room; they had noticed this years ago and hated it quietly ever since.

"You copied the Litany of Unanswered Voices on the ninth of this month," Corvane said. He opened a ledger—Lena knew that ledger, had seen its cousins a hundred times, the great bound volumes in which every scribe's output was logged page by page. "Page two hundred eleven. You will recite the final verse."

Lena's mouth went dry. The Litany was long, its verses interchangeable, its final line a formula every temple child memorized before they could write: *We pour our voices into the dark, and the dark keeps them kindly.* Safe words. Dead words. Words no god had ever troubled itself to answer.

"Recite it," Corvane said again, "as you wrote it."

There it was. Not the litany—the deviation. The three words Lena had added in a moment of exhaustion and something worse than exhaustion, something like hope, when the ink had seemed to shimmer under their pen and they had written, without deciding to: *and the dark answers back.*

They could lie. They had considered lying the whole walk down the colonnade, rehearsing denials the way other people rehearsed prayers. But Corvane had been buying and selling human beings for thirty years, and men like him could smell a lie the way dogs smelled fear, and besides—

Besides, the voice was listening. And some stubborn, suicidal scrap of Lena was tired of hiding.

"We pour our voices into the dark," Lena said, "and the dark keeps them kindly." A pause. Corvane's eyes, small and wet as currants, did not blink. "And the dark answers back."

Corvane closed the ledger with a soft, terrible gentleness.

"Do you know what I paid for your contract?" he asked. "Fourteen silver marks, when you were nine. Your mother signed you over with a steady hand, I'll give her that. Steadier than most." He laced his fingers. "In seven years you have cost me nothing and earned me a modest return. Competent copyist. No vices. No family worth bribing. I considered you one of my duller assets."

"I'm glad to have bored you, debt-master."

Something flickered across his face—not anger. Appetite. That was worse.

"Three days ago," he said, "a prayer left this scriptorium that should not exist. A dead woman's voice came back through the drying ink and spoke to the woman who copied it. She is a laundress in the Lower Ward, and she told her sister, and her sister told a man who sells candles to the palace, and by yesterday evening there were royal agents in my outer office asking very precise questions about which of my scribes might be *touched*." He leaned forward. "I told them I would inquire discreetly. Then I came up here and read page two hundred eleven."

Lena's heart was doing something painful and arrhythmic. "The laundress—"

"Is not your concern. You are my concern." Corvane opened a different ledger, a slim black one Lena had never seen, and turned it around. There was Lena's name, and beneath it, in fresh ink still glossy: a number. Lena read it twice because the first time refused to mean anything.

"That's—" Their voice cracked. "That's triple."

"Doubled, then doubled again overnight. You've become interesting, Nakamura. Interesting is expensive." Corvane tapped the number with one blunt finger. "Here is what happens now. The crown's agents are purchasing contracts—quietly, above market—for anyone the old gods have marked. You are marked. I am selling. This afternoon you will sign a transfer of bond, and you will go with them, and my ledger will finally show black ink where your name sits."

"Where do they take us?"

"No one asks that."

"Where do they *take us?*" Louder now, and the voice in Lena's blood stirred, a cold current rising through their chest, and the lamp flames in the counting room all leaned sideways at once, just slightly, like grass under wind.

Corvane noticed. Lena watched him notice—the tiny recalibration behind his eyes, the merchant reassessing his goods. For one heartbeat he looked almost afraid.

Then he smiled, and it was the worst thing Lena had seen all year.

"There," he said softly. "Do that in front of them and perhaps they'll pay me a fourth time."

* * *

They gave Lena an hour to gather their allotment, which meant an hour to sit on their cot in the scribes' dormitory holding the only possessions they owned: a spare robe, a bone stylus worn smooth by someone else's hand before theirs, and a bundle of hidden pages sewn into the lining of their sleeve.

Not the answered prayer—that page they had burned within the hour of writing it, watching the words curl and go gray, feeling the voice withdraw from the ash like a tide going out. These were older pages. Seven years of margins. Every whispered fragment the dead had pressed into Lena's ink while the ledgers' official text went on praising silent gods: a grandmother's recipe for fever-wine, a soldier's last letter home unsent for sixty years, a child's counting song from a village that no longer appeared on any map. Proof, if anyone had ever cared to look, that the old gods of Thornfell had never stopped speaking. They had simply run out of people willing to listen.

*You cannot carry those,* Vesh's voice said—it had been speaking more freely since the night of the answered prayer, gathering in Lena like water rising in a well. *If they search you—*

"They'll search me," Lena murmured into their collar, lips barely moving. "So I'll hide them somewhere they won't think to search until I'm gone."

*Gone where?*

"I don't know yet. Out. Anywhere out."

*Child.* The voice was patient in the way of mountains. *You have never in your life been out.*

Lena almost laughed, and the laugh turned into something else halfway up their throat, and they had to press their knuckles to their mouth for a moment. Because it was true. They had entered the temple at nine years old and they had not passed its gates since. They knew the scriptorium better than they knew their own face. The smell of size and lampblack, the particular creak of the third stair, the way Brother Anselm hummed off-key when he thought no one could hear—all of it was stitched into them more tightly than any memory of their mother. And in one hour it would be sold.

A knock at the dormitory door. Two guards in temple gray, and behind them a stranger: tall, gray-cloaked, wearing no livery but carrying the kind of clean, expensive sword that announced its owner without help. The agent's face was pleasant and completely empty, like a room swept for visitors.

"The transfer is prepared," the agent said. "Sign, and come."

* * *

But Lena had spent seven years learning this building the way prisoners learn walls, and there was one thing about it the debt-masters had never bothered to learn because it wasn't in any ledger: the scriptorium's archive had a hidden stair.

It was not grand history—a smuggler's passage cut during some old tax war, bricked at both ends, half-forgotten even by the rats. But the upper end opened behind the codex shelves in the restricted stacks, behind a shelf-end carved with a scribe's blessing that Lena had rubbed smooth over the years with a cloth and oil until the hinge gave silently. They had found it in their second year, fleeing a beating, and had kept it secret ever since the way you keep a knife sewn into your coat: not because you plan to use it, but because knowing it exists is the only thing that lets you sleep.

They could not reach it now. The agents were walking them through the public corridors, one ahead, one behind, and the transfer document waited downstairs with Corvane's seal already drying on it.

So Lena did the only thing left. In the corridor outside the counting room, where the lamps burned low and the stone drank sound, they let the voice rise.

*I can't fight them,* they said inside their own skull, hands shaking. *I'm not brave. I'm not anything. I'm a copyist.*

*You are a door,* said Vesh. *Doors do not fight. Doors open. Now—listen to the building. Tell me where the old air moves.*

Lena closed their eyes. This was the part of their gift they had never dared examine, the part that felt least like hearing and most like falling: the sense of a place as a body, its corridors as veins, its sealed spaces as held breath. The temple was old, and old things remembered their own shapes.

"The archive," they breathed. "Behind the stacks. The stair."

*Then give them a reason to look elsewhere.*

The nearest lamp guttered. Down the corridor, another flared green and died. Somewhere below, a shutter banged though there was no wind—and then, faintly, rising through the floor stones, a sound that made both guards go rigid: singing. Thin, many-voiced, coming from the direction of the crypts. A dead woman's voice and a dead man's voice and a dead child's voice, braided together, singing a counting song from a village that no longer existed.

Lena had never pushed that hard before. Blood ran from their nose, warm over their lip, and their knees buckled, and the guards—gods forgive them, they were only men—broke. One ran toward the crypt stairs with his sword drawn against nothing. The other grabbed Lena's arm, then let go as if the skin burned him, crossed himself with the wrong hand, and followed.

Lena ran the other way.

* * *

The archive door was locked, but locks were for people with time. Lena's stylus, ground thin against stone these seven years in idle moments, took the mechanism apart in forty seconds that felt like forty years. Inside, the restricted stacks rose into darkness, smelling of dust and leather and cold incense—seven years of stolen moments spent among these very shelves, reading forbidden marginalia by shielded candlelight, and now they were running through them blind.

Behind the carved shelf-end, the stair breathed out air that tasted of stone and long dark. Lena wedged the bundle of margin-pages deep into the gap beneath the lowest step, wrapped in oilcloth, and told themselves they would come back. Told themselves it firmly, the way you tell children things.

Then the light changed.

Orange light, flickering, climbing the high windows of the archive's far gallery. Smoke threaded the smell of dust. Shouting below—many voices, orders given in the flat cadence of soldiers who have been told a thing is necessary and intend to complete it.

They were burning the archive.

Lena stood frozen on the hidden stair's first step with the hatch open above them, and watched through the shelf-gaps as fire took the west range. Seven years. Forty scribes. Ten thousand ledgers, and the codices, and the marginalia of three centuries—every whisper the dead had ever pressed into ink, every record of every debt and prayer and ordinary life in Thornfell, going up in a roar that sounded almost warm, almost like applause.

Brother Anselm's humming. The third stair's creak. The exact brown of the good iron-gall ink. None of it had ever belonged to Lena—they had known that, they had always known that, a bonded scribe owns nothing, not even the hours of their own hands—but knowing a cage is a cage does not stop it from being home.

They were crying as they climbed. They noted it distantly, the way they noted everything, cataloguing even this: *fear, grief, smoke inhalation, a bruise forming on the left shin where the step caught me.* The stair was narrow and steep and pitch-dark after the first turn, and the voice steadied them when their breath wouldn't.

*Left hand to the wall. Count the steps. There are ninety-one. I counted them once, long ago, when this rock was younger and so was I.*

"Ninety-one steps," Lena whispered, and climbed, and counted, and the count was the only thing keeping them from screaming.

At step sixty the heat reached them, breathing up the shaft like a living thing, and at step seventy-four something above shifted—dust sifting down through cracks in the old masonry, the mountain of the temple settling as its western bones burned. At step eighty-eight Lena's legs gave out and they climbed the last three on hands and knees.

The exit was a crawl-space behind the root cellar of the pilgrims' hospice, choked with rubble and frost-killed ivy. Lena came out into evening air and snow-mud and lay there gasping, smoke-sick, bleeding from the nose, wearing a scribe's robe with a bundle of forbidden pages buried under a burning building and a name that had just been sold.

Above the temple wall, the fire painted the clouds the color of the ink they used for debts.

*Where now?* Vesh asked, gently.

Lena didn't know. That was the truth of it, plain and total: they did not know a single road out of this city. They had never been past the gate. The world beyond Thornfell was a rumor, a margin-note, a blank page.

But the crown's agents would discover within the hour that their purchase had escaped, and Corvane would tear the city apart rather than lose triple-price, and staying anywhere near the temple was dying slowly with extra steps.

So Lena ran—down the hospice lane, into the Lower Ward, into the labyrinth of wet streets and candle-shops and fish-stink where the temple's authority thinned like cheap wine. They ran with no plan except *out*, and no map except the dead, and somewhere in the third alley they heard, cutting through the crowd-noise, a voice speaking in a doorway over the smell of frying onions:

"—the scribe who hears 'em, that's the tale. Crown's paying for touched folk now, and there's a smuggler runs the north passes, Sefa her name, owes no man nothing but pays every debt twice—"

Lena stopped. Turned. The speaker was a fishwife, talking to a neighbor, gossiping the way gossip travels: half fact, half fog.

*Sefa,* said Vesh, tasting the name. *Smugglers keep secrets better than priests. And they know the roads.*

Lena had no money. Lena had no weapon. Lena had a bone stylus, a robe that smelled of smoke, and a head full of voices the crown wanted badly enough to burn a library for.

It would have to be enough. It had always, so far, been enough.

They turned toward the north gate, and began, for the first time in their life, to leave home.
