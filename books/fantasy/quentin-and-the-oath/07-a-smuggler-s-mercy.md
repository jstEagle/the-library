## 7. A Smuggler's Mercy

The storm came down the pass like a debt collector—no warning, no negotiation, just white teeth closing over the road.

Sefa Vane had crossed the Thornfell passes in every month the gods allowed and a few they didn't, and she had learned long ago that the mountain did not care how good your horse was or how cleverly you'd bribed the toll-wardens at Grey Ford. The mountain cared about one thing only: whether you respected it enough to turn back. Most people didn't. Most people died of that.

She hunched in her oilskin at the mouth of the switchback below Crag's Tooth and squinted into the murk, doing sums she didn't like. The caravan was three hours behind her, camped in the lee of the old watchtower with Bram grumbling over the fires and Quentin sitting his horse like a man who'd forgotten how to relax but hadn't yet remembered how to fall off. She'd ridden ahead to check the snow-bridges on the high route—the one that shaved two days off the journey to the lowlands, if the bridges held, which this year they might not.

They didn't. The first one had slumped into a gray slurry, and the second was a lurching suggestion of ice over black air. So it would be the long way round, the cold way, the way where travelers froze to death with pleasing regularity and left their boots for whoever came after.

Sefa turned her mare, Kestrel, back toward the watchtower—and that was when she saw it.

A shape in the snow. Not a drift; drifts lay smooth. This was a crumple, an interruption, a thing that had been walking and had stopped walking. It lay maybe forty paces off the road, half-buried already, and it was the color of the temple-gray robes that Sefa had spent her whole life avoiding.

"Idiot," she said aloud, to the shape, to herself, to the weather. "Absolute idiot."

Because here was the arithmetic of the pass, the smuggler's arithmetic, the one they taught you before they taught you lock-picking: a body in the snow is either a corpse or a trap. Bandits used frozen pilgrims as bait sometimes, propping them up along the roads, waiting for soft-hearted fools to dismount. Sefa had never been soft-hearted. It was practically her defining feature. Her mother had said so at the funeral, loudly, while going through Sefa's pockets.

But the shape moved.

It was small, the movement—a twitch of one hand, fingers curling against the snow like a man trying to hold onto something already gone. And there was something else, something that made Sefa's skin prickle under her layers: the snow around the figure had *melted*. Not much. A hand-span circle, maybe, wet and dark against the white, steaming faintly, as though the body were warmer than it had any right to be. As though something inside it were burning its own fuel to stay alive and had nearly run out.

Sefa sat Kestrel still for ten full heartbeats, weighing it. Then she swore with the fluency of a woman who'd grown up in salt-town dockyards, dismounted, and went to see what the mountain had spat out.

* * *

Up close, the shape resolved into a person—young, narrow, wrapped in a temple scribe's cloak that had been fine once and was now stiff with ice. The hood had fallen back. The face beneath was sharp-boned and blue-lipped, lashes furred with frost, and there was ink on the fingers. Ink everywhere, actually—stained deep into the cuticles, a smear across one cheekbone like a bruise made of midnight. Even half-dead, even frozen, the scribe's hand was curled protectively around something clutched to their chest: a leather folio, bound shut with cord, held the way other people held children or knives.

Sefa peeled back the cord with numb fingers and looked inside despite herself. Pages. Dozens of pages, covered edge to edge in cramped script—but not temple script, not the clean formal rows she'd seen on tax writs and indulgences. This writing crawled up the margins, sideways, upside-down, crowded into every empty space like it was afraid of silence. Some of it wasn't writing at all. Some of it looked like someone had tried to write down sounds.

"Right," Sefa said quietly. "You're trouble."

The scribe's eyes opened. They were dark, unfocused, swimming up from somewhere very far away, and for a moment they fixed on Sefa's face with an expression of such pure, naked terror that Sefa felt it land in her own chest like a stone dropped down a well. The lips moved. Frost crackled. No sound came out—or almost none. Sefa leaned close, because she was a fool, and caught a thread of a whisper:

"...didn't... sell... please..."

And that—that did it. That was the moment Sefa Vane, who had left a man handcuffed to a buoy rather than share her rum, who charged her own crew interest on borrowed gloves, looked at a stranger's freezing face and heard the word *sell*, and understood everything she needed to understand.

Somebody had sold this one. Somebody with ledgers and seals and the law behind them. And now somebody else wanted them badly enough that they'd fled into a killing storm rather than be taken.

Sefa had opinions about people who sold other people. They were not polite opinions. They had gotten her exiled from two ports and one family.

"Can you ride?" she asked.

The scribe blinked slowly. Snow settled in the hollows of their eyes.

"No, then," Sefa sighed. "Nobody can. Doesn't matter." She hauled the folio free—it came reluctantly, the scribe's grip fighting her even through unconsciousness, and she ended up tucking it inside her own coat rather than waste strength arguing with a dying person about stationery. Then she got her arms under the scribe's shoulders, heaved, and dumped the body across Kestrel's saddle like a sack of wet grain.

Kestrel gave her a look of profound equine grievance.

"Don't start," Sefa told her. "You're the one who has to carry them."

* * *

The watchtower materialized out of the white like a bad memory: a broken tooth of old imperial masonry, roofless, its lower vaults still dry enough to matter. Sefa smelled the fire before she saw it—woodsmoke and rendered fat and Bram's stew, which was less a dish than a geological event. She came through the archway leading Kestrel, and the scribe draped over the saddle like laundry, and the conversation stopped the way conversations do when a smuggler walks in carrying a problem.

Quentin stood first. He always stood first; it was the oath-keeper in him, some trained reflex that made him rise whenever anything arrived, as if arriving things required witnessing. His exile-brand showed at his collar, a dull red weal like a burn that had never decided to heal. "What is that?"

"That," Sefa said, sliding the scribe down onto the driest bedroll by the fire, "is a temple scribe I found dying in the snow. Which is strange, because temple scribes are meant to be warm, fed, and indoors copying prayers nobody reads."

Bram emerged from behind his cookpot. He was a slab of a man gone soft at the edges, with forearms like ship's cable and a beard that had opinions of its own, and he looked at the scribe the way he looked at underdone meat—with professional disappointment. "Frostbite's in the fingers," he rumbled. "Maybe the toes. Get the wet clothes off, wrap them, don't rub. And keep that—" he pointed a ladle at the folio poking from Sefa's coat, "—away from the fire. Paper burns."

"You checked the frostbite but not the identity?" Sefa asked.

"I checked what matters." Bram went back to his pot.

Pell, their youngest, a wiry ex-pickpocket with a laugh like breaking glass, crouched by the scribe and peered. "Temple robes. Thornfell colors. Hey—isn't there a bounty? Warden at the last town was asking about a runaway bond-servant. Scribbler type. Price on their head, Corvane's seal and all."

"There's a price on everyone's head," Sefa said. "Mine's higher than theirs and I'm standing right here."

"It's different," Pell insisted. "Bond-servants are *contracted*. You take one, that's theft of property. Wardens treat it different. Treat *us* different."

"They'd treat us different anyway," Sefa said. "We're smugglers. It's in the name."

Quentin had knelt beside the scribe. He'd pulled off one glove and was holding his bare hand above the frozen fingers—not touching, just hovering, and Sefa saw him mouthing something, the old keeper's words, the ones they used for the dying and the sworn. When he looked up, his face had that set quality it got, jaw like a drawn bow.

"This is the one," he said. "The scribe who hears the dead."

"The what?" Pell's eyebrows climbed.

"The story on the passes." Quentin's voice was flat, careful, the voice of a man reporting facts he didn't enjoy. "In the temple towns. A ledger-keeper whose marginalia answers back. Whose prayers get answered *by the page*. The wardens are calling it contagion. The crown's agents are calling it heresy. Either way, they want the scribe found." He paused. "I heard it from a carter at Grey Ford. He thought it was funny."

Sefa looked down at the unconscious face—at the ink-stained fingers, the frost-furred lashes, the cheekbone smeared with midnight—and felt the arithmetic shift under her feet. A runaway bond-servant was a fine and a scolding. A scribe touched by the old gods, hunted by the crown itself, was a different animal entirely. That wasn't cargo. That was a lit fuse with legs.

"We should leave them," Pell said—not cruelly, just practically, the way you'd say *we should reef the sail*. "Leave them by the fire with food and a blanket. Whoever's hunting finds them alive, collects their bounty, everybody's happy except the scribe."

"And if the fire goes out?" Bram asked mildly, without turning from his pot.

"Then the mountain does what mountains do."

"The mountain," Bram said, "already tried. Someone argued with it." He nodded at the melted circle of snow Sefa had described, at the impossible warmth that had kept a body alive past every reasonable hour. "I've cooked for thirty years on these roads. People freezing to death stop shivering, then stop being warm. They don't burn hotter than the snow. Whatever's keeping this one alive, it's spending something to do it." He finally turned, ladle in hand, and looked at Sefa. "So. Captain. Vote."

That was the code, and everyone knew it. Sefa ran her crew by vote, because captains who ruled by decree ended up dead in their beds or worse, kings. Big calls went to the whole company, and the cook voted last, because Bram's vote was worth more than anyone's and everyone knew that too. He'd voted no on the Merrow Point job and saved six lives. He'd voted yes on taking Quentin in, an exile with a brand and enemies, and so far that bet was paying.

"Arguments," Sefa said, and held up her hand, ticking them off. "One: taking this scribe makes us thieves in the eyes of every warden between here and the sea. Two: whatever's hunting them is hunting with royal money, which means it doesn't quit. Three: we know nothing about them except that they're trouble, they're cursed or blessed or both, and they carry a book full of writing that looks like it's screaming." She paused. "Counter-arguments."

"They'll die otherwise," Quentin said simply.

"They might die anyway."

"Then they die among people instead of alone in a snowdrift." Quentin's brand caught the firelight. "I know what it is to be thrown away by an institution. I know what it costs." He didn't elaborate. He never elaborated. "I say we take them."

"They slow us down," Pell said. "Two days to the lowlands becomes four, maybe five, hauling a half-frozen—"

"Then we go slower," Bram said.

Pell threw up their hands. "Bram, you vote *last*, that's the rule—"

"I'm not voting yet. I'm pre-butting your argument." Bram returned to his stew. From the pot he added, "Also, whoever it is will need feeding for a week before they can walk, and I'm not wasting a week of my cooking on a corpse in the morning. So either we save them or we decide right now, and I'd rather decide right now than let them die while we debate. Debating's colder than snow."

Sefa crouched by the scribe. Up close, in the firelight, she could see more: the chapped hands of someone who wrote for a living and was paid nothing for it, the collar worn thin from years of the same robe, the small calluses of a person who had never once in their life been given anything freely. There was a scar on the wrist, old, neat—the kind bonds-houses branded small so the property wouldn't be marred for resale.

Something in Sefa's chest did a thing she didn't authorize.

Her mother had sold her, too. Not with contracts and wardens—with a smile and a fishing boat and a lie about opportunity, and Sefa had clawed her way out of that hold by fifteen and sworn the only oath she'd ever kept: *no one gets sold while I'm watching.* She'd broken that oath twice, in hard years, and she still dreamed about it.

"Here's my vote," Sefa said. "We take them. We feed them, thaw them, and when they can talk, they tell us exactly what they are and exactly who wants them. If the answer's ugly, we leave them somewhere defensible with supplies and a map. Nobody sells anybody. Not on my pass."

"Your pass," Pell muttered. "You found it first, so it's yours. Fine. But I'm recording my objection for posterity."

"Noted for posterity," Sefa said. "Bram?"

Bram took his time. He always took his time; it drove Pell mad and Sefa suspected he enjoyed it. He tasted the stew, considered it, added salt, tasted again. Then he looked at the scribe—the small, frozen, ink-stained stranger who had burned themselves nearly to nothing rather than be caught—and something moved behind the gruffness, brief as a fish under ice.

"Aye," Bram said. "We keep them."

And that was that. Bram's yes settled arguments the way ballast settles a boat. Pell groaned theatrically and went to see to the horses, which was how Pell agreed to things. Quentin began, quietly, methodically, stripping the frozen outer robe away and wrapping the scribe in every dry blanket the camp owned, murmuring the keeper's warming-blessing under his breath like a man apologizing for the state of the world.

Sefa dug the folio out of her coat and weighed it in her hand. Heavy, for paper. She told herself she wasn't going to open it again. She opened it.

By firelight, the marginalia moved. Not literally—she checked, twice—but the effect of it, hundreds of tiny voices crammed into the edges of official prayers, pressing inward, crowding toward the center of each page as if trying to get into the text itself. Trying to be read. Trying, she thought with a chill that had nothing to do with the pass, to be *heard*.

On the innermost page, in fresher ink, in a hand shakier than the rest, someone had written a single line in the bottom margin:

*If you are reading this, I am sorry. They followed me out of the city.*

Sefa closed the book. Outside, the wind changed direction, and for a moment—just a moment—she thought she heard, threaded through it, something that was not wind at all. Something farther down the mountain. Something with the cadence of many hooves and the patience of ledgers.

"Bram," she said, not looking up from the fire. "How fast can you break camp?"

He was already kicking dirt over the stew.
