## 9. Names Are Rations

The cave mouth framed a slice of sky gone the color of old pewter, and beneath it the fire cracked and spat like something alive and disagreeable. Sefa had built it small—smuggler's habit, she'd said, a flame you could smother in one breath—and so they all huddled close, shoulders nearly touching, six people pretending warmth was a thing you could share by proximity alone.

Lena sat with their hands wrapped around a tin cup of Bram's barley tea, which tasted faintly of smoke and more faintly of hope, and watched the light move across the faces of people who had, three days ago, been strangers.

Not strangers anymore. That was the strange part. Sefa had pulled them half-dead off the pass, and Quentin had carried them down through the snow on his back without being asked twice, and now here they all were, breathing the same thin mountain air, bound by nothing but a debt Sefa insisted didn't exist. *You don't owe me,* she'd said. *I owed somebody once, and nobody came. So I came.*

Lena still didn't know what to do with that kind of arithmetic.

Across the fire, Isolde sat with her hood down for the first time since they'd left the free-town road behind. Without it she looked younger—still carrying herself like someone who had been taught to sit straight by tutors with rods hidden in their sleeves, but younger. The fragment of parchment she kept in an oilskin wrap against her breastbone caught the firelight when she moved, a pale sliver of her father's handwriting that she touched the way other people touched relics.

Quentin was sharpening his sword. He was always sharpening his sword; Lena suspected it was less maintenance than prayer. The exile-brand on his wrist showed dark between his cuff and glove, a ring of scarred flesh shaped like a broken circle, and every few strokes of the whetstone his eyes would drift to it and then away, as if he were checking whether it had decided to forgive him yet.

Bram cooked. Bram always cooked. He stirred the stew pot with the same expression he brought to everything—a sort of granite patience, weathered down at the edges into something almost gentle—and he fed them the way he did everything else: without commentary, without expectation, in portions large enough to be an argument against despair.

And Vesh—

Vesh was quiet tonight. Quieter than usual. Lena felt him the way they always did now, a pressure behind the eyes, a voice that lived not in their ears but somewhere deeper, in the blood and bone where the old gods had once written their contracts. Since the shrine vision, since he had shown them the mountain and the first oath and the price waiting at the end of it, Vesh had spoken less and listened more. Tonight Lena could feel him listening to the fire, to the wind, to the six heartbeats around it, with the attention of a man counting coins he already knew wouldn't be enough.

*Lena.* His voice arrived like sediment settling. *Tell them.*

*I don't know how.*

*You told me once that you spent seven years writing prayers no one answered. You know how to say hard things to people who aren't listening. These ones are listening.*

That was true, and it was worse. It was much easier to speak into silence.

Lena set down the cup. The tin made a small sound against stone, and somehow that small sound silenced the whole cave—Sefa's low humming, Quentin's whetstone, even the wind at the mouth seemed to lean in.

"I need to tell you what I learned," Lena said. "From Vesh. About what the crown actually does. Not the stories—the stories say the king is mad, or cursed, or that his mind went soft with age. It's none of those."

Bram stopped stirring.

"The crown eats," Lena said. "It eats memory. That's what it feeds on. That's what it has *always* fed on, since the day it was forged. Every year Aldric has worn it, it has taken from him—first the small things, the things no one would notice. A servant's name. The taste of a particular wine. Then larger things. And when it had stripped him down to the studs, when there wasn't enough left of *him* to keep it fed—" Lena's hands tightened around each other. "It started reaching further. Through him. Outward."

"Reaching how?" Sefa asked. She had gone very still, the way she went still before a switchback turn, before a warden's patrol, before anything that might require running.

"It's a spirit bound into gold," Lena said. "Vesh showed me the forging. It was made to hold the old gods' contracts—to make them obey. But a chain only works if the hand holding it is stronger than the thing chained. And the hand holding this one got hungry." They swallowed. "Memory isn't just in your head. That's what I thought, before—all those years copying prayers, I thought the dead clung to their memories the way they clung to their bones. But memory is in everything. In names. In graves. In songs. In the way a village plants its fields because its grandmother's grandmother planted them that way. All of it is remembering. All of it can be eaten."

The fire popped. No one spoke.

"Villages forget their dead," Lena said quietly. "That's where it starts, out in the country. Someone dies, and the grief is—wrong. Too shallow. People go to the funeral and come home and within a season they've stopped visiting the grave, and then they stop knowing whose grave it is, and then the marker gets used for a fencepost. Not because they're cruel. Because the *remembering* has been taken out of them, scooped out like the middle of an apple. The crown feeds slowly, carefully, in places far from the capital where no one will notice a little less love in the world."

Isolde's hand had risen unconsciously to the oilskin at her chest. "The western valleys," she said. Her voice came out flat, careful, the voice of someone handling something with edges. "There were petitions. Three years ago. Villages in the west petitioning the crown for—relief, aid, I don't even remember what, because the harvests failed and no one could explain why the fields went fallow. Father—" She stopped. Corrected herself. "The king dismissed them. Laughed, I think. Or the thing wearing him laughed." Her jaw worked. "I thought they were being difficult. I thought they wanted money."

"They wanted their grandmothers back," Lena said. "They just didn't know it. You can't petition for what you don't know you've lost. That's the cruelest part. When the crown takes a memory, it takes the *knowing it's gone*, too. The hole seals itself over smooth. You can't miss what you can't remember existing."

Quentin had stopped sharpening. The whetstone hung motionless in his hand. "Soldiers," he said, and his voice was rough. "That's why the garrisons—"

"That's why the garrisons forget why they fight," Lena finished. "Yes. Vesh showed me. A soldier stands watch on a wall in Thornfell, and the crown reaches into him through the king's authority—it's woven into every oath of service, every enlistment vow, because the oaths all route through the crown, that was the design—and it drinks. Not everything. Just enough. The reason. The face of the person he's fighting for. The letter home he never sent. He keeps his discipline, his training, his orders. He becomes very good at fighting and very empty about why. And an army that doesn't remember what it loves—" Lena looked at Quentin. "An army like that will do anything it's ordered to. Burn a temple. Clear a valley. Hunt a scribe across three provinces. There's nothing in them that says *no*, because the parts that would say no have been poured out like dregs."

Quentin set the whetstone down with exaggerated care, the way a man does when he doesn't trust his own hands. "At the garrison," he said, "before my exile. There was a sergeant. Oren. Twenty years' service. He used to keep a drawing of his daughter folded inside his gauntlet—showed it to every new recruit, told the same story about her every time, how she'd lost a tooth the week he shipped out and he'd missed it." Quentin stared into the fire. "In my last year there, I asked after the drawing. He looked at me like I was mad. Said he'd never had children. Said it kindly, but—" His throat moved. "He said it *kindly*. Like I was the confused one. And I believed him. Gods help me, for a moment I believed him, because he believed himself, because the hole was smooth and sealed and there was nothing left in him that knew to ache."

The silence after that had weight, texture, temperature. Bram put another log on the fire, and the sparks went up toward the cave roof like a brief orange snowfall in reverse.

"So it's not just the king," Sefa said at last. She was doing sums; Lena could see her doing them, the smuggler's ledger-mind turning pages. "It's not one madman in a palace. It's a—" She gestured with her cup, spilling a little tea. "It's a leak. A slow drain under the whole kingdom, and everyone downstream is standing in less water every year and calling the new level normal."

"Yes," Lena said. "And it's getting faster. Vesh says the crown is starving. Aldric is nearly empty—there's almost nothing left of him to feed it, just enough to keep the body walking and the mouth talking. So it's begun taking bigger bites, further afield. Whole valleys, not single villages. Vesh showed me—" Lena hesitated. This was the part they had turned over and over in the night, the part that made sleep impossible. "He showed me a map. In my head. Places going dark, one by one, like lamps going out. The west, mostly. The hill country. And here." They looked around the fire. "Thornfell. We're inside the reach now. The passes we came through—there are shepherd steadings up there. How many did we see?"

Sefa's face changed. Lena watched the arithmetic finish behind her eyes.

"None," she said slowly. "We saw smoke at two steadies on the way up, both cold. I marked it as the season—shepherds go down to the low valleys before the deep snow." She paused. "Except Marta's folk don't. The Okell shepherds winter high, always have, they're famous for it, they brag about it, they—" She stopped. Set the cup down. "Their dogs would have barked us off the trail anyway. Okell dogs bark at everything. There weren't any dogs."

"There weren't any dogs," Lena repeated softly, "because the last person who remembered feeding them is a woman who no longer remembers she has dogs. Or the dogs themselves have gone blank—animals remember too, thinner, but they do—and the crown drinks wherever the remembering pools."

Bram spoke for the first time. His voice was low gravel, unhurried, which somehow made it worse. "My mother's village," he said. "Dunmarrow. Up the Kelst water, two days east of here." He was looking at the fire, but Lena suspected he wasn't seeing it. "I send word every year. Harvest festival, I send a barrel of salt fish and a letter. Last year the answer came back short. I figured Ma's eyes were going, or the priest who writes for her was lazy." His big hands lay open on his knees, palms up, as if waiting to catch something. "The letter said, *Who is this?*"

Nobody breathed.

"I laughed," Bram said. "I laughed, because what else do you do. I wrote back, told her who I was, her son, Bram, gone twenty years down the mountain roads, cooking for smugglers, nothing shameful in it. Never heard back. Told myself the priest died, the letter got lost, the roads were bad." He finally looked up, and his eyes in the firelight were terrible in their calm. "You're telling me it's none of those things."

Lena couldn't lie to that face. "I'm telling you it might be none of those things. Dunmarrow is in the dark zone on Vesh's map. I'm sorry. I'm so sorry—I should have said something sooner, I didn't know how to—"

"No," Bram said. "You told it right. Waiting was right." He picked up the stirring spoon, put it down again, picked it up. A man needing something to hold. "If it's true, we'll know tomorrow. Dunmarrow's not far off our road."

"We're not going to Dunmarrow," Sefa said quickly. "Bram. We have Isolde, we have the fragment, we have a schedule, we have wardens on every—"

"I know what we have." Bram's voice didn't rise. It did something worse; it went quieter. "I'm saying we'll know. Passing knowledge of whether my mother knows my name costs us half a day. Half a day, Sefa."

"And if she looks at you like a stranger?" Sefa asked, and her voice cracked on the last word, and Lena understood suddenly that Sefa wasn't arguing about schedules at all. "What then? What do we do, Bram? There's no rite for getting it back. There's no blade you can swing at it. If she looks at you like a stranger, you carry that over the passes for the rest of this run, and it sits on the sled next to everything else, and it slows us down, and slow gets us killed."

"Then I'll carry it," Bram said. "I've carried heavier."

Quentin cleared his throat. "For what it's worth," he said, "the crown's reach thins with distance from the capital, but it pools along the roads. Along the *oaths*. Every courier route, every tax road, every garrison trail—that's how it travels, riding the vows sworn along them. Dunmarrow sits near the old salt road. If it's dark, it won't be long since it went dark. Weeks, maybe." He glanced at Lena. "Could she still—could there be anything left?"

Lena reached inward, tentatively, the way you probe a bruise. *Vesh?*

A long pause. Then, like a man unfolding a worn map: *The Kelst valley went dim within the season. But dim is not dark. There are embers. Old women are stubborn fuel, scribe. Tell the cook that.*

"There may be embers," Lena said aloud. "Memories the crown hasn't reached yet. Deep ones. But Bram—" They hesitated, hating the shape of what came next. "If we go, and she's forgotten you, seeing you won't bring it back. Nothing brings it back. That's the whole horror of it. You'd be a stranger at her door bringing salt fish."

"Then I'll be a stranger who brings salt fish," Bram said simply. "She raised me to believe strangers get fed. Least I can do is be a well-fed stranger."

Something in Sefa's shoulders came down half an inch. She looked around the fire—at Quentin, at Isolde, at Lena—and Lena watched her do the thing she must have done a hundred times on a hundred runs: weigh the cargo against the crew, the schedule against the soul, and find, as she always seemed to find, some crooked honest balance.

"Half a day," she said at last. "One look through the village, quiet, no fires, no names shouted in the street. If it's clean, we take an hour. If it's dark—" She rubbed her face. "If it's dark, we see exactly how dark, and then we move, because every hour we spend grieving is an hour the wardens gain on us, and I will not lose Isolde to Hale's net because we lingered." She pointed the spoon at Bram. "And you cook double rations tonight, because nobody walks into a place like that on an empty stomach. That's not negotiable."

Bram nodded once, gravely, as if accepting terms of surrender, and reached for the salt.

Isolde had not spoken through all of it. Now she drew the oilskin packet from her shirt and held it in both hands, and the firelight moved on her face like water. "Three years ago," she said, "I rode through the western valleys with my father's progress. Before. There was a woman in one of the villages—an old weaver—who grabbed my stirrup as we passed. She said, *Princess, they're forgetting my husband. Everyone is forgetting my husband. I remember him, but I'm seventy-one, and when I go, who will remember him?* And I gave her a coin." Isolde's voice was steady in the way of things balanced on a knife edge. "I gave her a coin and rode on, and I told myself she was mad, and I forgot her by evening." She looked up. "I forgot her *by evening*. Do you understand? I was in the carriage with him. With the thing wearing him. It was drinking right past me, close enough to touch, and I never felt a thing except that the countryside seemed dull."

"Dull," Lena echoed. "Yes. That's how it feels from outside. Like a room where a lamp's gone out and no one remembers there was ever a lamp."

"Then we're not fighting a monster," Isolde said. "Monsters announce themselves. We're fighting a—" She searched. "*Dimming*. A dimming with a crown on it."

"A dimming with a crown on it," Quentin agreed grimly, "that commands ten thousand soldiers and a network of wardens and, as of last week, a debt-master with our descriptions."

"Don't," Sefa said. "Don't say Corvane's name after dark, it's bad luck."

"He sold my parents' contract," Lena said quietly. "To the crown. Years ago. I found out on the road. Which means my parents—" The words caught. Lena made them keep going; seven years of practice at saying hard things. "Which means my parents may be in one of these dark places. Or they may be feeding it directly, in whatever cellar the crown keeps its purchased lives. Either way, every valley we pass through, I'm looking at faces now. Looking for them. Knowing I wouldn't know them if I found them." They looked into the fire. "The crown doesn't just eat the past. It eats the future. It eats reunions that haven't happened yet. Somewhere in a dark village there's a door my mother opens every morning, and neither of us will ever—"

*Breathe,* Vesh said, gentle as falling ash. *Grief is ration too, scribe. Don't pour it all out here. Save some for the road.*

Lena breathed. Around the fire, no one spoke, but Sefa's hand found Lena's shoulder and stayed there, heavy and warm and unembarrassed, and that was its own kind of sentence.

It was late when the talk finally wound down. Bram handed out bowls of stew—double rations, as promised, thick with barley and the last of the dried mushrooms—and they ate in a silence that was not empty but full, the way a held breath is full. The fire burned lower. Outside, the wind had shifted, coming down from the heights with a smell like iron and coming snow.

Lena washed their bowl in the snowmelt pot and sat back down, and that was when they felt it—Vesh rising in them like a tide, urgent now, all his long patience burned away in a moment.

*Ask the smuggler about the Kelst road,* he said. *Ask her about the bridge at Dunmarrow. Ask her when she last crossed it.*

"Sefa," Lena said aloud. "When did you last cross the bridge at Dunmarrow? The Kelst crossing."

Sefa frowned. "Two years ago, maybe three. Why?"

"What was carved on the bridge posts?"

"Carved?" She laughed shortly. "Nothing. It's a plank bridge over a mill race. What kind of question is—"

"It's not a plank bridge," Lena said. Their own voice sounded distant to them, borrowed, Vesh's certainty moving through it. "Vesh walked it once, in the days when priests still walked anywhere. It's a stone bridge with the old god-marks cut into the parapets—contract-marks, blessing-marks, a hundred years of travelers' votive scratches. It's one of the anchor-points of the whole valley's remembering. If it's a plank bridge now, then either the stone bridge fell—which bridges like that don't do—or it was never remembered at all, and the crown took it so thoroughly that even the stones in your head are wrong."

Sefa stared at them. The firelight made her eyes very bright and very still.

"You're telling me," she said slowly, "that I can't trust my own map of a road I've walked my whole life."

"I'm telling you," Lena said, "that none of us can. Not anymore. Not anywhere the crown has reached. Our memories aren't ours alone—they're shared, threaded through everyone we ever walked a road with, and it's drinking from the shared wells." They looked around the circle of faces, one by one, committing them deliberately, fiercely, to whatever private vault still belonged to them. Sefa's crooked grin. Quentin's scarred knuckles. Isolde's straight spine. Bram's hands, huge and careful around a spoon. "So starting tonight, I want us to do something. Every night, at the fire. Each of us says one true thing we remember—one specific thing, a detail, a name, a smell—and the rest of us repeat it back. We keep each other's memories the way you'd keep coin in separate purses. If one of us goes blank, the others carry it. That's all the defense we have. Names are rations now. We ration them together."

Silence. Then Bram, gruffly, into his bowl: "My mother's kitchen smelled of juniper smoke and burnt honey. She burned the honey every single morning of my childhood and swore it was intentional."

"Burnt honey and juniper," Sefa repeated obediently. "Intentional."

Quentin: "My first oath-keeper's boots squeaked on marble. Left boot only. Drove the whole court mad."

"Left boot only," Isolde echoed, and almost smiled.

"My father flew kites," Isolde said. "Badly. He always crashed them and blamed the wind." Her voice caught on the last word, and four voices caught it for her, repeating it back into the dark: *badly, always crashed them, blamed the wind.*

Then they all looked at Lena.

Lena opened their mouth. Closed it. Reached inward for a memory to offer and found, for one vertiginous heartbeat, that the well they drew from was also the well Vesh lived in, and the boundaries had gone strange, and they could not immediately locate a single memory that was certainly, provably, only theirs—

*Here,* Vesh said quietly, and pressed something into their hands like a gift across a table. *This one is yours. Keep it.*

"The scriptorium," Lena said. "The temple scriptorium, third window from the north wall. On winter afternoons the light came through at an angle that turned the dust gold, and the ink froze in the well if you didn't warm the stone, and I used to press my palms flat against the sill just to feel something warm that wasn't mine to copy." They swallowed. "Gold dust-light. Frozen ink. Warm sill."

"Gold dust-light," the crew said back to them, four voices in the dark of the cave, "frozen ink, warm sill."

And it should have been comforting, and it was, but underneath the comfort Lena felt Vesh withdraw slightly, thoughtfully, the way a man steps back from a wall to check whether it's bearing weight—and when he spoke again, it was low, and it was aimed at Lena alone:

*Embers in the Kelst valley, scribe. But embers mean someone has been tending them. And tending means someone there still remembers more than they should. Go carefully tomorrow. Sometimes the crown leaves one bright thing in a dark valley on purpose—*

*a lure, a snare, a watching eye.*

*And sometimes, rarely, it leaves a witness.*

Outside, far down the eastern slopes, where Dunmarrow slept or didn't, a dog began—or did not begin—to bark.
