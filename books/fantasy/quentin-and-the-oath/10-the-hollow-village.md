## 10. The Hollow Village

Bram smelled the village before he saw it.

That was the thing about a place you'd grown up in—your nose knew it first. Woodsmoke and wet peat and the sour-sweet reek of the tannery ditches that his father had cursed every day of his life. Underneath all of it, the cold iron tang of the mountain streams that came down off the Thornfell spine in spring, carrying snowmelt and the occasional unlucky marmot. Thirty years gone from Hollowmere, and one breath of the wind off the eastern pass and Bram was eight years old again, barefoot in the mud, chasing his brother Tam through the barley rows while their mother shouted at them both to mind the seedlings.

He stopped walking so abruptly that Sefa nearly collided with his back.

"What is it?" she asked, low, her hand already drifting toward the knife at her hip. That was Sefa. Everything was either an ambush or an opportunity, and usually both.

"Nothing." His voice came out rough as gravel. He cleared his throat. "It's nothing. It's just—" He gestured ahead, down through the thinning pines to where the valley opened up like a cupped hand. "I know this place."

The caravan had been three days out of the switchbacks where the wardens had bled them, and Sefa's wound had turned the color of storm clouds, and they'd needed somewhere to resupply that wasn't a warden-town. Lena had been quiet since the ambush—not frightened-quiet, but listening-quiet, the way they got when the ink-voice was talking to them. Quentin rode with his hood up and his brand hidden, and Isolde wore a servant's cloak over her fine bones and said almost nothing at all. A miserable little company of ghosts, Bram thought. And now the road had delivered him home without asking his leave.

"Hollowmere," he said, and the name sat strangely on his tongue, like a boot he'd outgrown.

Sefa looked at him for a long moment. He could see her doing the arithmetic—the detour, the risk, the wound that needed rest, the fact that Bram had never once, in eleven years of hauling cargo and dodging tolls together, mentioned where he came from. She didn't ask why. That was the smugglers' code, unwritten and unspoken: you didn't dig in another person's pack, and you didn't dig in another person's past. But he watched her weigh it anyway, her dark eyes flicking to the valley, to the smoke rising thin and gray from the chimneys below.

"We need feed for the mules," she said finally. "And I need two days flat on my back before this hole in me finishes the job the wardens started. If your village has a barn we can rent quiet, I'll take it."

"It'll have a barn," Bram said. "Old Man Ferrow's barn. Biggest roof in the valley." Then, because the words were already climbing out of him and he couldn't stop them: "My mother used to sell him eggs."

* * *

They came down into Hollowmere in the late afternoon light, when the shadows of the pines lay long across the fields like spilled ink, and Bram understood within the first hundred yards that something was wrong.

It wasn't obvious. That was what made his skin crawl. The village looked like itself—the same crooked lanes, the same stone walls furred with moss, the same geese strutting with their idiot dignity along the millpond's edge. The barley was in, the woodpiles were stacked, the smithy hammer rang its familiar rhythm off the hillsides. Life going on. But there was a quality to it, a hollowness underneath, the way a drum sounds different when the skin's been split. People moved through their own streets like sleepwalkers who'd learned to fake being awake. A woman hung washing with her eyes fixed on nothing. Two men repaired a fence post together and did not speak, not once, not even to argue about how to set it, and Bram had never in his life known two Hollowmere men to repair anything without arguing.

A child sat alone on the churchyard wall, maybe six years old, swinging her legs, watching the road. As the caravan passed, she looked up at them with a bright, open, friendly expression—and Bram saw that there was no recognition in it at all. Not the wariness children gave strangers. Just nothing. An empty room with the door standing open.

"Lena," Sefa murmured, and Bram heard it too, because he'd started hearing it himself: the scribe had gone rigid in the saddle, their breath coming fast and shallow, their eyes moving from face to face down the lane like a man counting corpses.

"They can't—" Lena's voice cracked. They pressed a hand flat against their chest, over the ledger they always carried against their ribs. "There are names here. Dozens of names. Written in the margins of everything, under everything, and none of them are attached to anyone. It's like walking through a house where all the portraits have been taken down and the nails are still in the walls."

"Speak plain, scribe," Bram growled, though some part of him already knew, had known since the churchyard wall, since the silent fence-menders. Some part of him had been bracing for it since the wind brought him the smell of home.

"The crown," Lena whispered. "It's been feeding here."

* * *

Old Man Ferrow's barn still stood, and Ferrow—or a man with Ferrow's bent back and liver-spotted hands—still owned it, and he rented it to them for coin and asked no questions, which was unlike Ferrow, who in Bram's memory had once interrogated a traveling tinker for half a day over the provenance of a copper kettle. He counted the coins twice, thanked them with a smile that never reached anywhere near his eyes, and shuffled back toward his house without a single word about the weather, the crops, or the state of the king's roads, which by Bram's reckoning meant the man had spoken perhaps ten words total, where the Ferrow of old would have spent a hundred.

Bram stood in the barn doorway after the others had seen to the mules, and looked down the lane toward the cottage at the end of it, the one with the blue door his father had painted the summer Bram turned nine, because his mother had said once, only once, laughing, that she'd always wanted a blue door, and his father had gotten up before dawn the next morning with the paint pot and said nothing about it ever again.

His mother was hanging washing in the yard.

She was smaller than he remembered. That was the first shock—that she'd shrunk, that time had done this to her without asking his permission, bent her spine into a question mark and thinned her arms until the sleeves of her dress hung loose around wrists he knew had once kneaded enough dough to feed six. Her hair was white now, fully white, where his memory kept it iron-gray. But she moved the same. That same economy, that same unhurried competence, pinning each garment with two strokes, no wasted motion, the rhythm of a woman who had done ten thousand days of laundry and would do ten thousand more if the world let her.

Bram crossed the lane. His legs felt like someone else's legs. Twenty feet away, fifteen, ten, close enough to see the flour still dusting her forearms, close enough to smell the lye soap that had cracked his knuckles every winter of his childhood—

"Mara," he said. Her name. His mother's name, spoken aloud for the first time in thirty years, and it came out steady, gods be praised, it came out almost gentle.

She turned. Looked at him. Smiled the polite, vacant smile of a woman greeting a stranger at her gate.

"Good evening to you," she said. "Are you selling something? We've little use for peddlers, but if it's needles, I'm always short of needles."

The words went into Bram like a blade between the ribs, sliding clean, no resistance, the way the sharpest wounds go.

"Mara," he said again, and his voice betrayed him this time, cracked down the middle. "Mara, it's Bram. It's Bramble. Your son."

Her smile stayed fixed, pleasant, patient—the smile you gave a madman or a drunk—and behind her eyes he watched her search for him. He actually watched it happen, watched something move behind her face like a fish under ice, circling, circling, finding nothing. No hook to catch. No shape named Bramble anywhere in the water.

"I'm sorry," she said kindly. "You must have me confused with someone else. My boys—" and here something flickered, some ghost of feeling crossing her face like a cloud shadow, grief with no memory attached to it, mourning for a loss she could no longer locate— "my boys went away. I don't recall where. Isn't that strange? I know I had boys. There's a sweater in the chest upstairs that I keep meaning to give away, and I can't, because my hands say it belongs to someone, but I couldn't tell you who. Isn't that strange?" She laughed, a small bewildered sound. "Isn't that strange?"

Bram made a noise. He would never be able to say afterward what kind of noise it was. Somewhere behind him he heard Lena's sharp intake of breath, and Sefa saying his name—*Bram*—and none of it reached him. He was looking at the sweater-hands, the flour-dusted wrists, the blue door, and the whole architecture of his childhood was collapsing inward like a burned roof.

"You made barley soup," he heard himself say. Foolish words. Desperate words. "On Sundays. You put in more pepper than the recipe said because Father liked it hot and you liked winning arguments with him. You sang when you baked. Badly. You sang *The Miller's Lament* and you got the third verse wrong every single time and Father corrected you every single time and you told him the song was better wrong." His voice was shaking apart. "You held my hand when Tam died. You held it all night. You didn't sleep. You just held it and said *we're still here, we're still here*, over and over, like a prayer, like—"

"Sir." Her voice had changed. Wariness now, and beneath the wariness, fear—the animal fear of a woman cornered by a weeping stranger in her own yard. "Sir, I think you should go. I think—I think you should go now."

"Bram." Sefa's hand on his arm. Firm. "Bram, look at me. Come away."

"She's my mother," he said. He hadn't cried since Tam died, thirty-one years ago, and he'd sworn it then, sworn it on the grave with his fists in the cold dirt, and now the oath broke like rotten rope. "Sefa, that's my mother, and she doesn't know my face. She doesn't know my *name*. The crown ate it. It ate thirty years of me out of her head and left the sweater and left the blue door and left—" his voice failed— "left *nothing*."

"I know," Sefa said quietly. "I know. Come away, you great fool. You're frightening her."

And she was frightened—he could see it, could see his own mother afraid of him, and that was the worst cut of all, deeper than the forgetting, because he had become a thing to be feared in the yard where he'd learned to walk. He let Sefa turn him. He let Quentin take his other arm, the disgraced oath-keeper steadying him like a brother, and he walked away from the blue door with his mother's polite voice following him across the yard:

"Good evening to you. Mind the geese, they bite."

* * *

He didn't go back to the barn. He walked out past the millpond, past the tannery ditches, up the slope into the barley field where he and Tam had run wild, and he sat down in the stubble with his back against the old stone wall, and the sun went down over Thornfell, and he let himself come apart the way a man can only do when no one who loves him is watching.

Except they were watching. Of course they were.

He heard them coming—Sefa's uneven gait, favoring the wounded side; Quentin's measured tread; the soft double-step of Lena, who still moved like someone apologizing for taking up space. They settled around him in the stubble without a word, the four of them, sitting in the ruins of his boyhood while the sky went orange and then violet and then the deep bruised black that comes early in mountain valleys.

For a long time nobody spoke. The stars came out. Somewhere down in the village a dog barked twice and gave up.

"My brother's buried under the rowan tree," Bram said at last, to the darkness, because it was easier than saying it to their faces. "Behind the church. Tam. Died of the sweating sickness when he was eleven and I was twelve. I dug the grave myself. My father held the lantern and my mother held me up, because my legs wouldn't hold me." He swallowed. "I went and looked today. While the rest of you were seeing to the mules. The rowan's still there. Bigger now. And there's a new stone next to it—my father's. Dead nine years, the mason's mark says. She buried him alone. She stood at that grave alone, because whoever she'd have had beside her, she couldn't remember why they mattered."

Lena made a small sound. In the starlight Bram could just make out their face, pale and stricken, and he realized with distant surprise that the scribe was crying—for him, for a gruff old cook they'd known barely a season.

"There's worse," Lena said softly. "Bram... there's worse, and I have to tell you, because you deserve the truth of it. What the crown takes, it doesn't store. It eats. Your mother isn't holding those memories somewhere behind a locked door. They're gone. Burned as fuel. Every Sunday soup, every lullaby, every time she held your hand—they're ash inside that thing's belly, and no rite, no god, no prayer of mine will ever bring them back to her."

Bram nodded slowly. He had known. He had known since the churchyard wall.

"But here's what I keep turning over," Lena went on, and their voice gained a strange fierce steadiness, the voice of a person reading from a page only they could see. "The margins. When I listen to the dead, I hear what's written underneath things. And underneath your mother's emptiness, there's—there's residue. Grief with no object. Love with no name attached. Her hands remember the sweater. Her heart remembers that it lost something enormous. The crown took the memories, Bram, but it couldn't take what the memories *built*. She is still the woman who loved you. She just can't find the door to it."

"That's a cold comfort, scribe."

"Yes," Lena said simply. "It is. Most true things are."

Quentin spoke for the first time, his deep voice quiet in the dark. "When we unbind the crown—if we unbind it—no one gets those memories back. You understand that. Isolde understands it about her father. Understanding it and accepting it are different labors, and the second one is longer." A pause. "But there will be no more villages like this one. Whatever else happens. That's worth the walk."

"And what about her?" Bram asked. His voice came out raw. "What happens to her? An old woman alone in a house with a blue door, grieving a son she can't name, waiting for boys who went away and aren't coming back because they were never—"

He stopped. Because an answer had arrived, unbidden, terrible and obvious, rising in him like water into a well shaft.

"I could stay," he said.

Silence in the barley stubble.

"I could stay," he said again, testing it. "Cook for her. Chop her wood. Tell her I'm a hired man, some traveler down on his luck, work for my supper. She'd take me in—she took in every stray in the valley when I was a boy, she fed every beggar between here and the pass. I could sit across the table from her every night for whatever years the gods grant her, and she'd never know, and I'd know, and maybe that's enough. Maybe that has to be enough."

"No," Sefa said.

"Sefa—"

"*No.*" Her voice cracked like a whip, and he turned to look at her, and in the starlight her face was fierce and wet-eyed and furious. "You'd do that? You'd spend her last years as a stranger in your own house, eating your own mother's cooking under a false name, dying by inches every time she called you 'sir'? You'd bury her as her hired man?" She grabbed his shoulder, her grip strong despite the wound. "Bram Okafor-of-Hollowmere, you listen to me. I've run cargoes past every toll and tax in seven provinces, and I'll tell you the one law I've never broken: you don't pay a debt with your whole self. That's not payment, that's surrender. This crown has already taken her memory of you. Don't you dare hand it the rest."

"So I just ride away," Bram said bitterly. "Again. Leave her grieving in that yard."

"You ride away," Sefa said, "and you finish what we started. Because there are a hundred villages like this one, Bram, and a thousand mothers, and the only thing standing between them and that empty yard is a scribe who hears the dead and a band of fools stubborn enough to walk them to a mountain shrine." Her grip tightened. "You want to honor her? Honor the woman who taught you to feed strays. Get up. Walk south. Win."

The stars wheeled overhead. Down in the valley, the blue door was a dark rectangle in the moonlight. Bram sat with his grief the way he sat with everything—solidly, heavily, like a man built to bear loads—and felt the truth of her words settle into him the way stones settle into a wall, one by one, load-bearing.

"She'd have liked you," he said finally, to Sefa. "My mother. Terrible judge of character. Would've fed you till you burst."

"Everyone likes me eventually," Sefa said. "It's a curse."

Lena laughed—a wet, broken laugh, but a laugh—and Quentin put one heavy hand on Bram's shoulder and left it there, and they sat a while longer in the stubble of the field where two brothers had once run wild, four strangers who had become, without anyone signing anything, a family.

* * *

They left at dawn.

Bram rose before the others, as he always did, and got the fire going, and set out bread and hard cheese and the last of the honey, and while the kettle heated he stood in the barn doorway and looked down the lane one last time.

His mother was in her garden already, wrapped in a shawl against the morning chill, kneeling in the dirt among the cabbages. Working. Always working. And as he watched, she paused, and lifted her head, and looked directly at the barn—at him, though she couldn't possibly have seen him in the shadow of the doorway—and her hand drifted up to press against her breastbone, the way a person does when something aches and they cannot say why.

Then she shook her head, puzzled at herself, and went back to the cabbages.

Bram shouldered his pack. Behind him, Lena appeared in the doorway, quiet as a margin note, and stood beside him looking down at the small figure in the garden.

"When this is done," Lena said, "when the crown is broken and the kingdom starts putting itself back together—I'm going to write her story down. Everything you can remember. Every soup, every song sung wrong on purpose. All of it, in ink that listens. So that somewhere, in the record of the world, there's proof that Mara of Hollowmere loved her sons beyond all measure." They glanced up at him, eyes red-rimmed and resolute. "The crown can eat memory. It can't eat testimony. Not if we keep it."

Bram looked at the scribe for a long moment—this small, frightened, stubborn creature the crew had pulled half-frozen off a mountainside, who owed them nothing and was offering everything.

"You do that," he said roughly, "and I'll bake for you till the day I die."

From the road, Sefa's voice rang out, impatient and warm: "Are you two coming, or are we founding a monastery?"

Bram took one last look at the blue door. Then he turned his back on Hollowmere, and followed his family south, toward the capital, toward the crown, toward whatever price the mountain shrine would ask—and behind them, faint on the morning wind, the smell of woodsmoke and wet peat and home receded mile by mile, carried forward instead in the only vessel the crown could never touch: the telling, and the telling again, and the ones who refused to forget.
