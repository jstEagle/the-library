## 11. Isolde's Bargain

The palace had a smell that Isolde had stopped noticing years ago—beeswax and cold stone and the faint sweetness of the censers that burned in the throne room from dawn until the king retired, which was to say, until the thing wearing her father went wherever it went when it was not being king. Tonight she noticed it again, sharply, the way you notice a room only when you are about to leave it forever.

She stood at the window of her apartments on the third floor of the east wing, watching the lamps of the lower city gutter in a wind that smelled of coming snow. Below, past the walls, past the tanners' quarter with its perpetual haze of smoke, the Salt Road wound away north toward the mountains. Toward Thornfell. Toward whatever was left of the old gods.

Behind her, on the writing desk, lay a letter she had written eleven times and burned ten of them.

*To the one called Sefa,* the eleventh draft began, *whose crew is said to move anything through anything for the right price—*

She had torn that one up too. Not because it was wrong, but because it was too careful, and careful letters got people killed. She had learned that much in the last year, watching her father's court rearrange itself around an absence shaped like a man. The ministers who asked too many questions were gone—retired suddenly, promoted to distant governorships, or simply not seen at morning council anymore. The guards who had served her father for twenty years had been rotated out in favor of young men with blank faces and new uniforms. Even the kitchen staff had changed twice.

Only Isolde remained, because Isolde was useful. A princess was proof of continuity. A princess smiling at banquets was worth more than a hundred soldiers, because a princess smiling meant nothing was wrong.

So she smiled. She had gotten very good at it.

Three nights ago, at the autumn feast, her father had taken her hand across the table—his fingers cool and dry as paper—and looked at her with eyes that were her father's brown and yet not his brown at all, and he had said, "You have your mother's chin," which was true, and then he had said, "What was her name?" which was not something her father could ever have asked, because her mother had been dead nine years and Aldric of Thornfell had wept at her grave every autumn of those nine years until two winters ago, when the weeping stopped all at once, like a door closing.

Isolde had smiled and said, "Queen Maren, Father," and watched him nod slowly, filing the name away the way a man files away a number he will need once and never think of again.

That was when she had known—not suspected, not feared, but *known*, with the flat certainty of someone reading their own death warrant—that the thing on the throne was not her father, and that her father, if he still existed anywhere, existed somewhere beneath it, buried alive.

And that was when she had begun to plan.

* * *

The fragment lived in a place no thief would look and no minister would dare: sewn into the lining of her mother's old riding cloak, which hung in Isolde's wardrobe behind three seasons of ceremonial gowns, unworn since the funeral. Queen Maren had been a northern woman, married up out of the hill clans, and she had never fully trusted the palace or its priests. She kept things. Small things, hidden things—a pressed flower from her wedding garland, a lock of Isolde's infant hair, and this: a strip of vellum, older than Isolde herself, covered in a cramped hand that Isolde now knew better than her own signature.

It was not the whole oath. It was a fragment—the opening invocation, perhaps a tenth of the rite that had bound the crown to the kingdom and the kingdom to the gods, copied out in her father's younger hand. Isolde knew it was his because she had spent her childhood watching him write, and because the fragment bore the small tell she would have recognized anywhere: the way he crossed his *t*s with a single impatient stroke, like a man slashing through a knot.

He had copied it before the coronation. Before the crown. Which meant he had read the rite entire, understood what it demanded, and gone ahead anyway—or been made to go ahead, by men whose names Isolde had spent a year learning. Chancellor Veyran. High Priest Ossler. And the debt-masters of the capital, whose ledgers, her father's old steward had drunkenly confessed before his own convenient retirement, had financed the forging of the crown itself.

A crown built on debt. It figured. Everything in this kingdom ran on debt; why should its holiest object be different?

But the fragment mattered, and Isolde understood why, even if she did not understand everything. Because the words on it were *true* words—the coronation as it should have been spoken, before whoever forged the crown had cut the old gods' names out of the invocation and replaced them with silence. She had compared it, over months of terrified midnight work, against the official liturgy in the palace chapel, and the differences were small and enormous: where the official rite said *and the crown shall be fed*, the fragment said *and the crown shall be given freely, and take nothing that is not offered*. Where the official rite named no power above the king, the fragment invoked *the mountain that remembers, the river that forgives, the hearth that holds*.

Someone had edited the oath. Someone had turned a covenant into a leash. And whatever wore her father had been leashed into him by the altered version—or perhaps had *written* the alteration, though that thought kept Isolde awake even now.

Either way, the fragment was evidence. The fragment was a key. And keys were useless locked in a wardrobe in a palace full of things that ate memories.

Which brought her, eleven drafts later, to the twelfth letter, and to the problem of Sefa.

* * *

She had done her research the way she did everything now—quietly, through channels that could not be traced back to her. The smugglers of the Salt Road were not romantic figures; they were freight-runners and debt-dodgers and occasionally worse, and most of them would sell a princess to the wardens inside an hour for the price of a good horse. But there were stories. A crew that moved goods through the Thornfell passes in winter, when the warden patrols pulled back to their forts. A crew that had once carried a whole village's grain stores past a tax blockade without losing a sack, and refused payment beyond costs because, the story went, "the village had already paid enough." A cook named Bram who had grown up in the hollow country and a captain named Sefa who kept her word the way other people kept knives—close, sharp, and rarely drawn twice.

Most importantly: a crew that had recently taken on two passengers of its own. A disgraced oath-keeper with a brand on his face, and a temple scribe with a price on their head, both of whom had vanished from the capital's records within days of each other, which meant either remarkable coincidence or remarkable smuggling.

If they had saved those two, they might save her. If they had sold those two, they would sell her, and she would know within the hour and be dead within the day. There was no way to be sure except to stand in front of them and find out, and that was the part of the plan Isolde hated—hated with a cold, clarifying hatred—the part where she put her life and her father's last hope into the hands of strangers and trusted to their character.

Her tutors had taught her statecraft. None of them had ever mentioned that statecraft eventually came down to this: standing in a smoky back room, betting everything on whether a stranger's eyes were kind.

* * *

The meeting place was a chandler's shop off Tannery Row, chosen by the smugglers themselves, which Isolde took as a good sign—they had picked the ground, which meant they were confident, which meant they probably intended to leave the meeting alive themselves. She went dressed as a merchant's daughter in wool dyed the dull brown of respectability, with her hair braided tight and ash rubbed into the gold of it, and she brought no guards, because guards were a confession, and only one companion: Serah, her maid since childhood, who had guessed everything months ago and asked nothing, and who waited outside with the horses and a knife she hopefully would not need to use.

The chandler's shop smelled of tallow and smoke. Behind the counter, a broad woman with grey-shot black hair and a face like a closed door was trimming wicks with a small sharp blade, and she did not look up when Isolde entered, and did not stop trimming when Isolde said, "I'm told you carry freight north."

"Freight's seasonal," the woman said. "Roads are bad."

"I'm told your roads are never bad."

"Then you've been told stories." The blade paused. Now she looked up, and her eyes were dark and quick and entirely unfooled. "Stories get people hanged. You want to be careful who you tell them to."

"My name doesn't matter," Isolde said. "But I'll give you a truth to weigh me by: I know you're carrying a scribe and an oath-keeper. I know they went north through the passes three weeks ago. And I know the wardens have a bounty notice with my seal on it—my seal, which means someone at court wants them found, which means someone at court knows what they're carrying, which means I know something too, or I wouldn't be here."

The woman set down the blade. Slowly. The way a person sets down a blade when they are deciding whether to pick up a bigger one.

"You're her," she said. "The princess."

"I'm a customer," Isolde said, "with a difficult shipment."

For a long moment the shop was silent except for the tick of cooling wax. Then the woman laughed—one short, hard note, without much humor in it but some, buried deep, like a coin at the bottom of a well.

"A difficult shipment," she repeated. "That's one word for it. Sit down, highness. This is going to take longer than trimming wicks."

* * *

Her name was Sefa, and she poured tea that tasted of pine resin and honey, and she listened to Isolde's proposal with an expression that gave away precisely nothing, which Isolde recognized as a professional skill and envied bitterly.

"The sum I can pay is large," Isolde finished. "Not treasury-large—I don't control the treasury—but large. My mother's jewels, land deeds in the hill country, and coin I've been moving out of the palace a purse at a time for eight months. Enough to buy your crew new lives somewhere the crown's writ doesn't run, if that's what you wanted. What I need is passage north, quietly, to the mountain shrine at Thornfell, and protection on the road. That's all."

"That's never all," Sefa said. "Passage and protection for a princess is a war in a box. The moment the court notices you're gone—and they'll notice within a day, because I'd bet my ship they watch you closer than you think—every warden between here and the capital border turns north. We'd be smuggling the one cargo in the world they can't afford to lose." She sipped her tea. "So before I say yes or no, I want to know what you're actually carrying. Not the jewels. Not the coin. What is it you think is so important that it's worth turning my crew into traitors?"

Isolde had prepared for this question. She had prepared answers, deflections, partial truths arranged in descending order of disclosure. She opened her mouth to deliver the first of them—

—and found, instead, that she was tired. Tired in a way that had nothing to do with sleep. Eighteen months of smiling at a thing that wore her father's face, eighteen months of hiding fragments in cloak linings and counting coins in the dark, eighteen months of being the only person in a palace of hundreds who knew, and the weight of it chose that moment, in a tallow-scented shop on Tannery Row, to come due.

She reached into her coat and drew out the strip of vellum, still smelling faintly of cedar from its hiding place, and laid it on the table between them.

"This," she said. "It's a piece of the coronation oath. The real one. My father wrote it out before he was crowned—before the crown was remade, before the rite was changed. Look at it. Compare it to the liturgy they use now. Where the new rite says the crown shall be *fed*, this says it shall be *given to freely*. Where the new rite leaves the old gods unnamed, this names them. Someone cut the heart out of the oath, Captain, and stitched the skin back over it, and whatever sits on my father's throne came in through the cut."

Sefa looked at the vellum for a long time. She didn't touch it.

"And your father," she said at last. "Where does he figure in this? Alive or dead?"

And there it was—the question under all the questions, the one Isolde had not said aloud to any living soul, because saying it made it real, and because the answer was the loneliest sentence in the world:

"I don't know," Isolde said. Her voice held. She was proud of that, later. "I think he's underneath it. I think the thing wearing him keeps him the way a miser keeps coins—buried, hoarded, spending nothing. I think he's been aware this whole time, every feast, every council, every time it used his mouth to ask me my mother's name. And I think there's a rite—maybe the one this fragment belongs to, maybe something older—that can unbind it and give him back. I don't know that either. But I know the shrine at Thornfell is where the first oath was sworn, and I know that everyone who might help me is either dead, bought, or too frightened to breathe the word 'old gods' indoors. So I came to the people who aren't afraid of the crown because they've spent their whole lives dodging it."

She stopped. The shop was very quiet.

"That's the truth," she said. "All of it. You asked what I'm carrying. I'm carrying a piece of paper and a maybe. That's the whole cargo."

Sefa sat back. For the first time, something moved behind the closed door of her face—not warmth exactly, but recognition. The look of a woman hearing a story she had heard before, in another voice, on another night, from a scribe with ink-stained hands and nowhere left to run.

"You know," she said, "three weeks ago I had a passenger just like you. Different clothes, same eyes. Somebody who'd spent years being careful in a place that eats careful people, and finally decided careful wasn't working. I told them the roads were bad too." She turned her cup slowly on its saucer. "Here's my problem, highness. My crew voted on taking them, and it wasn't unanimous, and I don't vote alone. So I can't promise you yes tonight. What I can promise is that I'll put it to them straight—your paper, your maybe, your face—and whatever they decide, that's the decision. No bargaining after. Can you live with that?"

"No," Isolde said honestly. "But I can accept it."

Something flickered at the corner of Sefa's mouth. Almost a smile. "Honest, at least. Your father's girl, then, whatever else is true." She stood, and the interview was plainly over. "Chandler's back door, tomorrow, an hour past dawn. Traveling clothes, nothing that shines, and leave the jewels—coin only, small denominations. And highness—" she paused at the counter, picking up her wick-trimming blade again, not looking at Isolde, "—the scribe I told you about. They hear the dead. The dead talk to them. I don't know what you believe, but if half of what they've said round our fire is true, then your paper and your maybe might be worth more than you know. Might be worth exactly what the mountain's waiting for."

Isolde stood. Her knees were steadier than she expected.

"Then I'll see you at dawn," she said.

At the door she paused, one hand on the frame, and looked back at the woman trimming wicks in the lamplight, and asked the question she had been carrying since the feast, since the grave, since the first night her father's eyes had come back wrong:

"If it were your father under that crown," she said, "would you go?"

Sefa's blade stilled.

"Mine died owing money to men like the ones who built that thing," she said. "So I can't say. But I've got a crew that's family by choice, not blood, and I know what I'd do if something wore one of them." She resumed trimming. A curl of wick fell away. "Go home, highness. Sleep if you can. Dawn comes early, and the pass comes harder."

* * *

Serah was waiting with the horses in the alley, breath steaming, and neither of them spoke until the chandler's district had fallen away behind them and the palace rose ahead, lit and golden and terrible against the darkening sky.

"Well?" Serah asked.

"They'll decide tomorrow," Isolde said. "Their whole crew decides. It's how they do things—it's honest, I think. Honestly is rare enough that I'm inclined to trust it."

"And if they say no?"

Isolde watched the palace windows slide closer, each one a small bright cell. Somewhere in that building, in the dark at the center of the light, the thing wearing her father was doing whatever it did at night—feeding, perhaps, or listening, or simply waiting with the patience of something that had already eaten a kingdom and intended to eat the rest.

"Then I go north alone," she said, "with a piece of paper and a maybe. It's more than he has."

They passed the gate. The guards saluted her, blank-faced and correct, and she smiled at them—the smile, the practiced one, the one worth a hundred soldiers—and rode on into the courtyard of the only prison she had ever known, carrying her freedom folded small in a coat lining, over her heart, where the next dawn would either begin her father's rescue or end her own.

Above the east wing, clouds were closing over the stars. Snow by morning, the stable boys always said when the sky did that. Snow by morning, and hard roads north.

Isolde dismounted, handed off her reins, and walked toward her rooms with her spine straight and her hand pressed flat against her coat, against the small stiff rectangle of vellum, like a pilgrim touching a relic, like a daughter touching a wound.

Dawn, she thought. One more dawn in the cage. Then the mountain.
