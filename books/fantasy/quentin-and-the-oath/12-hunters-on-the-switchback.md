## 12. Hunters on the Switchback

The road above Ravenmere was a lie the mountain told travelers.

Sefa had been telling it that way for eleven years—*a road*, the maps said, *a proper trade route with gravel and culverts and room enough for two carts to pass*. What it actually was, once you climbed past the second waterfall, was a shelf of stone barely wider than the caravan's axles, cut into the cliff face in a series of hairpin turns that the locals called the Switchbacks and everyone else called, when they thought no one was listening, the Widow's Teeth. Below the shelf, six hundred feet of air, then the white roar of the Ravenmere falls smashing itself against black rock. Above the shelf, another six hundred feet of granite leaning out over the road like a drunk uncle at a wedding.

It was, in Sefa's professional opinion, the finest place in Thornfell to be ambushed, which was why she'd spent the last three miles watching it the way a gambler watches another player's hands.

"You're doing the thing," Bram said from beside her, walking at the lead mule's shoulder. He said it around a mouthful of dried apple, because Bram ate constantly when he was nervous, and Bram had been nervous since dawn.

"What thing."

"The thing where your eyes go flat and you start counting exits nobody else can see." He swallowed. "There's an exit behind us and one over the cliff. That's the arithmetic. Stop making the face."

"There's also," Sefa said, "the turn ahead. The long one, past the shrine-cairn, where the road dog-legs back under that overhang." She nodded at it—the pale scar of stone jutting from the cliff wall, deep enough to swallow twenty feet of road in permanent shadow. Snowmelt had stained it green-black down the years so that it hung there like a curtain half-drawn. "If I were a warden captain with orders to take us alive, that's where I'd stand. Shade for my archers, high ground, and the only way past is straight through their teeth."

Bram looked at the overhang. Looked at her. Chewed.

"So we don't go through it," he said.

"We have to go through it. There's no other road down this face until Coldharbor, and Coldharbor's two days back the way we came, and by now Corvane's bounty has reached every tavern between here and the coast." She kept her voice easy, pitched for him alone, though she was aware—she was always aware—of the others strung out along the line behind them. Quentin bringing up the rear with his hand never far from the oath-keeper's iron at his belt. Isolde riding the second cart with her hood up and her princess posture beaten down into something that almost passed for a merchant's daughter, if you didn't look at her hands. And Lena.

Lena walked in the middle of the column, as far from both edges as the road allowed, and they were not looking at the overhang at all. They were looking at the cairn.

That was the thing about Lena Nakamura that Sefa had learned in the weeks since the pass, since the snow and the fire and Bram's grudging *aye*: the scribe saw everything sideways. Everyone else on this road was watching the obvious danger, the shadowed kill-zone, the classic trap. Lena was staring at a pile of stacked stones that some pious traveler had raised to a dead god forty years ago, and their expression was the one they got when the ink talked to them—which meant, in Sefa's experience, that the day was about to get worse.

She drifted back along the line, casual, a woman stretching her legs.

"Talk to me, scribe."

Lena didn't startle. They'd stopped startling weeks ago; Sefa counted that among her quiet victories. "The cairn's wrong," Lena said quietly. Their voice always dropped when the gift was on them, went thin and careful, like someone speaking in a sickroom. "The stones are stacked the old way—prayer-stones, offering-stones. But somebody's moved the top three. Recently. Within days." Their grey eyes flicked up. "And there's a thread tied around the fourth stone. Red wool. It wasn't there when we passed going north."

Sefa looked. A thread, fine as spider silk, red as a wound, knotted around the base of the cairn and running—she followed it with her eyes, and her stomach dropped—running along the ground, along the very lip of the road, up into the scree above the overhang. A trip-line. No, worse than a trip-line. She'd seen warden-work before, in the bad years after the border wars, and this was signal-craft: pull the thread from cover and something would fall, or sound, or burn, and every man hidden in that shade would know exactly where the caravan stood.

"They know we're coming back this way," she said.

"They knew yesterday," Lena said. "The knot's weathered. Someone's been waiting since before the storm broke."

Sefa did the arithmetic again, fast and ugly. Wardens on the switchback, above and below, holding the only road. A signal-line rigged to spring the trap at the moment of maximum vulnerability—the moment the carts were committed to the turn, wheels locked, nowhere to run but off the edge of the world. Captain Hale's work, if she had to guess. The man had a reputation for patience, and patience on a cliff road was worth fifty swords.

Behind her, the lead mule chose that moment to plant its feet and refuse the turn entirely, ears flat, nostrils working at a scent the wind carried down from the scree. Horse. Men. Fear-sweat.

"Well," Sefa said. "They've been made."

* * *

She gathered them in the lee of the cart, quick and low, the way she'd done a hundred times in a hundred bad places, and watched the plan form and fail and reform across four faces.

"Options," she said. "One: we fight through. Six of us, unknown numbers of wardens, archers with the high ground, and our backs to a six-hundred-foot drop. Two: we turn around, retreat to Coldharbor, and let the crown's net close behind us while we starve politely in a border town full of informers. Three—" she jerked her chin at the cliff wall rising sheer above the road— "we find a way off this road that isn't the road."

"There isn't one," Quentin said. His voice had the flat certainty of a man who'd marched these passes in the king's colors, back when he still wore them. "I've patrolled the Switchbacks. The cliff's unbroken for a mile either direction. That's why the wardens hold it. One road, one gate."

"The king's roads are drawn by men who ride them at a walk," Lena said.

Everyone looked at them. Lena had gone pale—that particular bloodless look they got when the voices pressed close—but their eyes were clear, fixed on the cliff face above the overhang, reading it the way they read margins: looking for what was written between the lines.

"When I was copying toll-ledgers for Corvane, third year of my bond, there was a dispute about the Ravenmere road-tolls," Lena said. "Two merchants swore they'd never used the king's road at all, and the toll-master wanted proof. So I copied the old survey. The pre-coronation survey." They swallowed. "Before Aldric's grandfather smoothed the Switchbacks for his armies, there was another way down this face. A smugglers' turn. The goat-paths. The survey called it the Kestrel Stair—it starts above the overhang, where the cliff breaks at a fault-line, and drops through the scree to the valley floor. Half a day instead of two hours. Steep enough to kill a loaded mule. But it exists, because the surveyor drew it, and the toll-master made me copy every word twice."

"That was seven years ago," Bram said. "Paths change. Rockfalls. Winters."

"It exists," Lena repeated, and there was something underneath the words, something that made Safa's skin prickle—the tone of a person relaying, not remembering. "The fault-line hasn't moved. Stone doesn't forget."

"Stone doesn't forget," Sefa echoed softly. "But scribes do, sometimes, whether a thing's real or only written down." She held Lena's gaze. "You're certain?"

"I'm certain it's in the ledger." A beat. "And I'm certain the ink wants us to take it."

Quentin's jaw tightened. He hated the ink-voice; Sefa had watched him hate it for weeks, watched him flinch every time Lena spoke in that borrowed cadence, as if the dead were reaching through the living to steer them. But he looked at the overhang, where the shadows lay thick and patient, and then at Isolde, who carried a scrap of coronation oath worth more than the whole caravan, and whatever he hated lost the argument.

"How do we reach the stair without passing the overhang?" he asked. "It's above it, you said. We'd have to climb past their position."

"We don't climb past it," Sefa said, and felt the old cold pleasure rise in her chest, the smuggler's joy, the moment the impossible map turns in your hands and shows you a door. "We make them think we're coming through. Bram takes the lead cart and the loudest mule and makes noise at the turn—lots of noise, swearing, whip-cracks, the whole performance. Quentin stands visible on the road behind him, iron out, playing rearguard. Every eye in that shadow goes to the noise." She turned. "Meanwhile, the rest of us unload the second cart and take the goods up the talus slope, there—" she pointed to the fan of broken stone below the fault-line, gray and loose and steep— "and we find the scribe's stair. When they spring their trap on an empty cart, we're already ghosts."

"And if there's no stair?" Isolde asked. Her voice was steady but her knuckles were white on the cart rail. "If the ledger lied, or the path fell, we're stranded on a scree slope in full view with all our goods and no road."

Sefa grinned at her, and knew the grin was wolfish, and let it be. "Then we die honest, Highness. But ledgers don't lie. Debt-masters see to that." She clapped her hands once, soft. "Move."

* * *

The next hour passed in the particular silence of people trying very hard not to die loudly.

Sefa went first up the talus, testing each step, distributing weight the way you distribute it on loose stone—flat-footed, never on your heels, moving when the wind moved so the rattle of scree hid your own. Behind her came Lena, who climbed badly, gracelessly, with the stiff terror of someone whose body had spent seven years indoors, but who climbed anyway, without one word of complaint, pausing only once to press a palm flat against the rock and murmur something too low to catch. Isolde followed, and Sefa revised her opinion of the princess upward by a full grade: the girl had clearly done this before, somewhere in her gilded childhood, and did it with a economy of motion that smelled like exile training, or escape rehearsals, or both.

The fault-line revealed itself exactly as promised—a vertical seam in the cliff where the stone had cracked and healed crooked, leaving a staircase of tilted slabs zigzagging up through the break. At its foot, half-buried in scree, someone had piled a marker: three stones, old lichen-eaten, stacked in the same prayer-fashion as the cairn below.

Lena touched them and shivered. "Older than the road," they whispered. "The priests used this way. Before the kings closed the mountains." A pause, and then, in that other voice, thin as paper: "*Climb while the light holds.*"

"Climb while the light holds," Sefa repeated aloud, briskly, because the crew took instruction better from her than from ghosts. "You heard the management. Up."

Below them, right on schedule, hell broke loose.

It started with Bram's performance—a bellowed curse, a whip-crack, the mule screaming—and then the overhang bloomed with movement, dark shapes detaching from darker shadow, and a voice amplified by cupped hands shouting the crown's justice in the name of King Aldric, halt in the name of King Aldric, and then the first arrow, loosed wild at a cart that was, by then, already empty except for a very smug cook who had rolled himself into the ditch the moment the shouting started.

Sefa allowed herself one glance down. The wardens poured onto the road—eight, ten, twelve of them, more than she'd guessed, Hale ran a tight net—and swarmed the lead cart, and found nothing in it but flour and fury, and began, with the slow dawning she could read even at distance, to look *up*.

"Less admiring the view," she said. "More climbing."

They climbed. The Kestrel Stair was everything the survey promised and worse—slabs tilted at angles that argued with the hips, handholds crumbled to gravel, a stretch near the top where the seam narrowed and they had to pass the packs up hand to hand, breath smoking, hearts slamming. Sefa went last, counting bodies, counting packs, counting the seconds since the shouting below had changed pitch.

The pitch changed just as Isolde hauled herself over the final lip.

A horn. Not the wardens' short signal-horn—a long note, deeper, blown from somewhere down-valley, and answered a moment later by another from the heights above the road. Sefa froze with her fingers hooked over the rim of the cliff, and understood, with the clarity that comes only when it's too late to be useful, what she was hearing.

Not one net. Two. The road-block below was the hammer. Something else was the anvil, waiting higher up the pass, and the horn-call meant the anvil had just been told the mice were in the walls.

"Off the rim," she snapped. "All of you, away from the edge, *now*—"

The crossbow bolt took her in the side as she turned.

* * *

Pain, later, would arrive in installments. In the moment there was only the impact—a fist of iron punching under her ribs, left side, below the arm—and the strange floating shock of watching her own legs decide not to hold her. The scree came up fast. She heard someone scream her name, heard it doubled and trebled as if the mountain were throwing the sound back, and then hands were on her, dragging her back from the rim, behind the tilted slab that formed the stair's last shelter, and the world contracted to sky and stone and faces.

Bram's face, arrived impossibly fast, enormous, furious. "Where. Where is it. Talk to me, Sef."

"Left," she managed. "Under the—ribs. Don't pull it." Because she could feel it there, a hard wrong weight that shifted when she breathed, and she'd seen enough wounds to know the bolt was the only thing keeping her from bleeding out on a mountaintop. "Leave it. Bind around it."

Bram bound around it. His hands, which shook when he carved roast fowl, did not shake now; they moved with a terrible competence, cutting cloth, packing the wound's edges, wrapping tight enough to make her gasp and then apologize and then wrap tighter. Quentin crouched at the slab's edge with his iron drawn, watching the rim, watching the scree field above them where the second horn had sounded, watching everything, his jaw set in the shape of a man composing a report to a god about how badly the day was going.

And Lena knelt in the blood, and put both hands over the wound, and did not pray.

Sefa felt it happen—there was no other word for it. A warmth that wasn't warmth, a pressure that wasn't touch, something pouring through the scribe's palms into the torn meat of her side like water finding the level. The pain didn't vanish. But it stepped back, the way a crowd steps back from a nobleman, and beneath it she felt the bleeding slow, felt the torn edges of herself grow quiet.

"Lena." Her voice came out frayed. "What are you—"

"Hush," Lena said. Their eyes were unfocused, seeing the rim of the world and something beyond it. "He says hold still. The one who shot you—he's sorry. He was aiming for Quentin." A pause, and their face went strange. "He says the wardens above aren't coming down the stair. They're being recalled. All of them. Down to the road." Another pause, longer, and when Lena spoke again their voice had dropped into that cadence that made Quentin flinch, the voice that was not quite their own:

"*The net is drawing closed around something else. Hurry. There is little time, and the mountain remembers the way, but the mountain cannot carry her.*"

The warmth withdrew. Lena sagged, caught themselves on one hand, blinked down at Sefa as if surfacing from deep water. Blood had soaked through their sleeve—not hers. Nosebleed. The gift always took its toll in red.

"Sefa," Lena said, in their own voice again, ragged and young. "Are you—did that work? I've never—I only ever listened, I never tried to—"

"You sewed me shut with a ghost's hands," Sefa said. "Ask me again when I'm not bleeding." She tested her weight against Bram's shoulder, and the world swam and steadied, swam and steadied. Bad. Walking-wounded bad, not dying bad. "Someone tell me what the dead man meant. Recalled to the road. Drawn around something else. What else is on this mountain?"

No one answered. Then Isolde, standing at the rim with the wind pulling her hood back, went very still in a way Sefa had learned to read like weather.

"Smoke," the princess said. "Down-valley. Past the falls." She turned, and her face had gone the color of the snowmelt. "That's the road to the capital. And those horns weren't hunting us." Her voice dropped to a whisper. "They're mustering. The crown isn't sending wardens after a smuggler's caravan anymore. It's moving an army—and we're standing on the only stair that looks down on the road it has to take."

Sefa laughed, and it hurt, and she laughed anyway, because eleven years of smuggling had taught her that the alternative to laughing at news like that was lying down.

"Well then," she said, gripping Bram's shoulder, testing the leg, the side, the stubborn animal of her body. "Somebody help me up. If we're going to outrun an army, I want a head start—and I want to know what in the nine hells the crown thinks it's marching on."
