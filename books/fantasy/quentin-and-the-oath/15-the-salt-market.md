## 15. The Salt Market

The free-town of Brackish Hollow announced itself the way all free-towns did: by smell. Salt and tar and fish gone just past their best, layered over woodsmoke and the particular sourness of too many people trading in too small a space. Sefa had been coming here since she was nine years old, when her mother first put a crate in her arms and told her to smile at the harbormaster and lie about what was inside it.

She came down the gangplank with salt spray drying on her cheeks and a list in her head longer than her arm. Rice. Lamp oil. Boot grease—Quentin's boots were eating themselves alive, the man walked like he was trying to punish the ground. Dried fish, onions, that dark honey from the terraced hives up-valley that Bram swore by and would sooner die than admit he liked anything.

Behind her, the crew assembled on the dock like a problem looking for somewhere to happen.

Bram was easy. Bram was always easy—a big man in a plain coat, moving with the unhurried patience of someone who'd spent thirty years convincing vegetables to behave. He'd pass for a carter anywhere between here and the coast, because that's exactly what he'd been before Sefa's mother fished him out of a debt-gang and discovered he could cook like a prayer answered.

Quentin was harder. The exile-brand on his throat had faded to something like an old scar, but old scars still drew eyes in towns where warden patrols walked in pairs. She'd wrapped him in a fishmonger's smock and told him to carry crates and look stupid. He was very good at carrying crates. He was working on looking stupid.

Lena was hardest of all. There was something about Lena that made people look twice—not the face, which was ordinary enough, scribe-pale and ink-stained at the cuticles, but the way they held still. Like a bird deciding whether the cat had seen it. Sefa had dressed them in a smuggler's leathers and a hood and told them to walk like they belonged to her crew, and Lena had studied Sefa's own gait for a full minute before mimicking it so precisely it was unsettling.

And Isolde—

Isolde was the one who couldn't be disguised at all. You could put a princess in rough wool and scrub the court off her hands, but you couldn't scrub out the way she stood, spine straight as a mast, or the way she looked at people—directly, frankly, the way nobody in a free-town ever looked at anybody. Sefa had solved it the only way available: she'd given Isolde the most boring job on the ship. Inventory. Below decks. Where nobody could see her posture.

"Rules," Sefa said now, low, turning to face them on the dock. Gulls screamed overhead. "We're strangers. We met on the road out of Marrowdale, we're running salt and lamp oil south, we don't know each other's mothers. Quentin, you're my cousin's husband, which is why you're doing manual labor. Lena, you don't talk unless I talk to you first. And if anyone asks where you learned your letters—"

"I'm a temple-trained scribe fallen on hard times," Lena said. "It's even true."

"It's *too* true. Say it bored. Everything true sounds like a lie when you say it proud." She jabbed a finger at each of them. "Wardens are walking pairs today. I counted four already from the deck. That means someone's posted paper. We get what we need, we're back aboard within two hours, and nobody buys anything interesting."

"What counts as interesting?" Quentin asked.

"If you have to ask, don't buy it."

* * *

Brackish Hollow's market square was built around a salt-pond the color of dirty pewter, ringed with stalls whose awnings had faded to every possible shade of once-red. Sefa worked it the way she always did, a slow spiral from the outer stalls inward, trading gossip for prices, prices for gossip. By the third stall she had the shape of it.

Corvane had posted paper. Not just here—in every free-town down the coast, which meant he'd paid the Harbormen's Compact good silver, which meant he wanted this quiet and thorough rather than loud and legal. The bounty wasn't for a name. It was for a description: a scribe, young, dark-haired, ink-marked fingers, traveling with armed companions, possibly in company of a woman of noble bearing. Reward sufficient to buy a fishing boat outright. Or a house. Or ten years of not being poor.

Sefa bought her onions, haggled the fishwife down out of habit, and kept her face pleasant and empty.

By the fifth stall she had more. Wardens weren't just walking pairs; they were asking questions. Not the usual questions about cargo and tariffs, either. They were asking whether anyone had come through who seemed *watched*. Whether anyone flinched at bells. Whether anyone's hands smelled of ink.

That last one was Corvane's touch. Sefa knew it the way you know a signature. He'd spent years buying contracts on children with clever hands, and now he was hunting one specific clever-handed child across half a kingdom, and he'd taught the wardens his tells.

She found Quentin at the boot-grease stall, holding a tin of grease and radiating the tension of a man performing casualness.

"Wardens stopped me," he murmured, not looking at her. "Asked where I was born."

"And you said?"

"Marrowdale. Which is true, which is the only reason I'm not in irons." His jaw worked. "They asked if I'd seen a scribe. Young. Nervous-looking."

"What did you tell them?"

"That everyone young looks nervous to me lately." He set the tin down, picked it up again. "Sefa. Two hours is too long. This town's a net and we're standing in the middle of it."

"We need the oil and the rice. We need the honey or Bram will sulk for a week, and a sulking Bram is worse than wardens." But she was already recalculating, cutting the spiral short, steering toward the chandler's near the pond's edge where the oil barrels stood in rows. "Get the grease. Meet me at the chandler's. Walk slow. Stupid men walk fast."

* * *

It went wrong at the honey stall, because of course it did. Nothing in Sefa's experience had ever gone wrong at the boot-grease stall or the oil barrels. It was always the thing you didn't need, the luxury, the small human want that undid you.

Lena had broken rule three without meaning to. Sefa saw it happen from twenty feet away: a stall-keeper's little girl, maybe seven, gap-toothed, sitting on a crate behind her mother's honey jars, staring at Lena with the frank fascination children reserve for anyone who looks like a story. And Lena—kind, doomed, gentle Lena—smiled back and said something. Sefa couldn't hear it over the market noise. Probably nothing. Probably *your mother's honey smells wonderful*, or some other harmless kindness.

But the girl lit up like a lamp, and she didn't stop watching after that. Children never did. They followed interesting things the way gulls followed gutting-tables.

Sefa finished paying the chandler, signed nothing, and turned—and saw the girl tugging her mother's sleeve, pointing, saying something urgent and excited into the woman's ear. Saw the woman glance over. Saw the woman's gaze snag on Lena's ink-stained fingers, then lift, sharpening, going from merchant-bored to merchant-calculating in the space of a breath.

Then the woman looked past Lena, toward the pair of wardens standing at the well.

Sefa moved. Not fast—fast was death—but with purpose, closing the distance, arriving at the honey stall with a smile and coin already in hand.

"Two jars of the dark valley honey," she said loudly. "The good stuff, not the stuff you show tourists."

The woman's eyes flicked between her and Lena. "You together?"

"Crew. Salt run south." She set the coins down, added another. The woman didn't reach for them.

"That scribe?" the woman asked, quietly. "Ink on the fingers?"

"Scribe? No, that's—" Sefa's mind raced through lies and found them all too slow. The girl was still pointing. A warden had turned away from the well and was scanning the crowd, and his partner was drifting toward the honey stall with the loose, patient walk of a man following a thread.

"The paper says reward enough for a boat," the woman said. Her voice wasn't hostile. It was tired. That was worse. "I've got three kids and a leaky hull. You understand."

"I understand," Sefa said, "that the paper also says *possibly in company of a woman of noble bearing*, and the man with me is my cousin's husband, and there's no noblewoman here, so what you've got is a rumor worth nothing and a fine for wasting warden time worth plenty." She was bluffing with everything she had and the woman could probably see straight through it, and then—

"I'll take the honey," said a new voice, cool and clear, "and the girl can have this, for being helpful."

Isolde.

Sefa's stomach dropped through the dock planks. Isolde had come up from the ship—against every instruction, drawn by some instinct or simply unable to stay below decks while her people walked into a net—and she was standing at the honey stall in her rough wool with her terrible unmistakable posture, and she was sliding a ring off her finger.

Not just any ring. Sefa knew gold weight when she saw it, and she knew sigils better, and even half-turned she could see the crest pressed into the band: the crowned stag of House Aldric. Royal signet. The kind of ring that got whole families executed for possessing it—or rewarded beyond dreaming for reporting it.

The honey-woman went very still.

"That's—" the girl began.

"A gift," Isolde said, "from a traveler who appreciates good honey and honest children. Sell it if you like. Melt it if you like. But if you sell it, sell it far from here, because men will ask where you got it, and the answer 'a kind stranger' keeps you safe, and the answer 'the woman at the honey stall' does not." She closed the girl's small fingers around the ring. "Do you understand, little one?"

The girl nodded, eyes enormous.

The woman understood better. Sefa watched it land—the arithmetic behind her eyes, the boat-reward against the royal-ring risk, greed losing to fear in a fair fight. She took the coins for the honey at last, and wrapped the jars, and said nothing at all as the wardens drifted past close enough to touch, their thread-of-a-thread attention sliding off toward the fish stalls where nothing waited.

* * *

They didn't run. Running was confession. They walked—slow, stupid, bored—back along the pond's edge, four travelers with oil and honey and boot grease, and only when the gangplank was under their feet and the *Kestrel's* deck rose around them did Sefa allow herself to breathe like a living creature.

Bram met them at the rail. He looked at the four of them, at the honey, at Isolde's bare hand, and his eyebrows climbed a quarter inch, which for Bram was a full tantrum.

"Don't ask," Sefa said.

"I wasn't going to." He took the honey jars. "Stew tonight. The good kind."

"You're the reason any of us stay sane, you know that?"

"I know," Bram said, and carried the jars below.

Quentin cast off lines with mechanical precision. Lena stood at the stern rail, hands folded, watching Brackish Hollow shrink. Sefa came to stand beside them as the town slid astern, its salt-pond flashing pewter in the afternoon light.

"She was going to sell you," Sefa said quietly. "The honey-woman. For about six seconds, she was going to sell you, and then a princess gave a child a king's ring to make it not happen." She shook her head. "That ring fed her family for a year, maybe. Instead it bought us an hour's silence and a story that'll spread from here to the coast by spring. *A stranger gave a girl a royal signet.* Wardens will hear that tale. Corvane will hear that tale."

"I know," Lena said. Their voice was thin. "I smiled at a child and nearly got us all caged. I keep—I keep forgetting that I'm not a person anymore. I'm a price."

Sefa looked at them—at the ink-stained fingers gripping the rail, at the stillness that wasn't calm but endurance—and felt something in her chest do the thing it always did around this crew, the thing she'd sworn off after her mother died, the thing that made debts personal.

"Listen to me, scribe." She turned, made Lena look at her. "On this ship, you're not a price. You're the reason a hollow-eyed oath-keeper sleeps at night instead of pacing. You're the reason Bram cooks like there's a tomorrow. You're cargo I chose, which makes you mine, and I don't sell mine." She jerked her chin toward the shrinking town. "That woman back there isn't evil. She's poor and scared, and Corvane builds his nets out of exactly that. So we stay ahead of the nets. All right?"

"All right," Lena whispered.

From the bow, Isolde watched the coastline unspool, her bare finger curled in her fist. When Sefa joined her at the rail, the princess spoke without turning.

"My mother wore that ring at my father's coronation," Isolde said. "It's the last thing I had of hers that he ever touched." A pause. The wind pulled at her hair. "It was worth it. Don't tell me it wasn't worth it."

Sefa weighed the truth and found it heavy but honest. "It was worth it," she said. "And it cost us. Both things are true, and that's the whole job." She squinted ahead, where the coast bent north toward the mountain passes, toward the shrine none of them could see yet. "Rest while you can, Highness. Word travels faster than ships, and I'd bet my keel that ring's already being talked about in a tavern somewhere—and that somewhere, a man with a ledger-book is listening."

Far behind them, Brackish Hollow's bell rang the hour, thin and bright over the water, and Sefa found herself counting the rings like a debt coming due.
