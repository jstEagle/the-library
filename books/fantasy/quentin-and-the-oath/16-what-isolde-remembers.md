## 16. What Isolde Remembers

The fire had burned down to its red heart by the time Isolde finally spoke.

They had made camp in the lee of a granite shoulder called the Shepherd's Tooth, a spur of rock that broke the wind off the high passes and gave them a pocket of stillness in a country that had forgotten how to be still. Sefa had set the watches. Bram had produced, from some bottomless reserve of his cook's pack, a stew of salt pork and mountain onions that smelled like every good winter Isolde had never actually had. And now the crew sat around the coals in that particular silence that follows a hard day's road—the silence of people who have decided, for one evening, not to be afraid out loud.

Isolde sat apart from them, as she had sat apart from everyone for three years, and turned the oilcloth packet over in her hands.

She had carried it against her ribs since the night she left the palace sewn into her riding coat. Through the warden checkpoints and the frozen switchbacks, through the ambush on the cliff road where Sefa had taken a blade meant for her, through all of it, she had touched it a hundred times a day without opening it, the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth. Tonight, watching Lena turn their hands toward the fire—those ink-stained hands that had heard her father's name in the margins of a dead priest's writing—she found she could not keep it closed any longer.

"I want to show you something," she said. "And I need you to understand what it costs me to do it."

The crew went quiet. Even the wind seemed to lean in.

* * *

She unwrapped the oilcloth with the care of a surgeon, folding back each layer as though the thing inside might startle and vanish. What emerged was a strip of parchment no longer than her forearm, cracked along one edge, stained at the corner with something brown and old that she had never let herself identify too closely. The writing on it was small and slanted, the hand of a man who wrote quickly because he thought quickly, letters leaning forward like runners at a starting line.

"This is a fragment," she said. "Of the coronation oath. The true one—not the version they read aloud in the cathedral, not the pretty words the priests composed for the crowds. This is what my father actually swore, alone, in the shrine beneath the palace, the night before he was crowned. He copied it out himself afterward. He kept it hidden. I don't think even my mother knew it existed."

Quentin leaned forward first. Of course he did; he was an oath-keeper, and this was scripture to him, or worse than scripture—a relic of the rite that had failed. His exile-brand caught the firelight as he moved, the raw scar tissue on his jaw shining like old wax.

"May I?" he asked.

Isolde hesitated, then passed it to him. Watching another person's fingers touch it was harder than she had expected. She had to fold her hands in her lap to keep from snatching it back.

Quentin read it silently, his lips moving. Then he read it again, slower, and she saw the color drain slowly out of his face, the way water drains from a lock when the sluice opens.

"This is a binding," he said quietly. "This is a *contract*. Not an oath of service—an oath of *containment*." He looked up at her. "Your father knew. Before he ever put the crown on, he knew what it was."

"He suspected," Isolde said. "He wasn't certain. That's why he copied it—to compare it against what the priests gave him. And when he found they didn't match..." She stopped. Her throat had closed around the next words, and she had to work them loose like a knot in wet rope. "When he found they didn't match, he came to find me. It was the last time he was entirely my father."

* * *

The kite had been blue.

That was the detail that always came first, unbidden, the way grief arranges itself around the smallest things: not the dying words but the color of a ribbon, not the last embrace but the smell of someone's hair. The kite had been blue, the deep bruised blue of evening sky, with a tail of white paper ribbons that her mother had cut from the backing of an old painting because proper kite-tail paper was beneath the dignity of the royal household and her mother had never once cared about dignity when her daughter wanted something.

Isolde had been nine years old.

It was autumn, the last warm week before the coronation, and the palace had gone strange around them—servants whispering in corners, priests arriving in caravans, her father's study filling day by day with documents sealed in black wax. She had been forbidden the west wing, forbidden the crypts, forbidden to ask questions. So when her father appeared at her schoolroom door in plain clothes, no crown, no guards, holding a bundle of silk and bamboo behind his back like a boy smuggling sweets, she had known immediately that something enormous was happening, because her father only broke rules when the rules were about to break him.

"The princess," he had said gravely, "is required by ancient law to master the winds of Thornfell before winter. It is a sacred duty. There will be running."

"There will be *running*?" she had said, scandalized. Princesses did not run. Princesses were walked, formally, from room to room.

"Terrible amounts of running," her father said. "Possibly falling. Almost certainly laughing. Are you prepared?"

They had gone out past the orchards, past the boundary stones, up the long grass slope above the river valley where the wind came off the mountains in long steady breaths, and Aldric—King Aldric, who in two days would kneel beneath a crown forged by men whose names had been scraped from history—had shown his daughter how to feel the wind with her face before trusting it with the kite. How to pay out line slowly, patiently, the way you'd feed a frightened animal. How to read the gusts in the bending of the grass, so that the kite's tugging became a language and she could answer it.

"You're fighting it," he'd said, wrapping his arms around hers to guide them, his chin resting on top of her head. He had smelled of cedar shaving soap and ink. "A kite isn't a thing you conquer, little star. It's a thing you *agree* with. You hold on, and you let go, both at once, and neither of you lets the other fall. That's the whole secret. That's—" and here he had paused, and she had felt rather than seen the change in him, some thought passing through him like a shadow through a window, "—that's most things, really."

The kite climbed until it was a blue fleck trembling at the edge of sight, and Isolde stood with the line humming in her fists and her father's hands over hers, and she remembered thinking, with the absolute certainty available only to children, that nothing bad could ever happen while she could feel his heartbeat against her back.

Then he had knelt down beside her in the grass, and taken the line gently from her hands, and wound it slow, bringing the kite home in wide spirals, and while it descended he talked to her in a low voice, not looking at her, looking at the kite, as if it were easier to tell the truth to something flying.

"Isolde. In two days I'm going to become king. Properly king, crowned and sworn." The kite dipped, caught, steadied. "There are things about the crown that no one will explain to me honestly. Things the priests are... careful about. So I've done something they didn't expect. I found the old words. The real ones. And I've written them down, and I've hidden them, and I need you to remember that I've hidden them, even if you never learn where. Can you do that? Can you carry a secret that heavy?"

"Why can't you just refuse the crown?" she had asked, nine years old and practical.

Something crossed her father's face then that she had spent twenty years learning to name. It was fear—but not fear for himself. It was the fear of a man standing on a bridge he believes is rotten, knowing there are people behind him who must cross anyway.

"Because if I don't take it," he said, "they'll find someone who won't look for the real words. Someone who'll swear whatever they put in front of him and never ask why the priests' version leaves out the part where the crown makes promises *back*." He smiled, and it was the kite-flying smile, warm and crooked, but there was frost under it now. "Better a king who goes in with his eyes open. Even if the eyes don't stay open long."

The kite landed softly in the grass between them, wings folded, tame and tired. Her father picked it up and held it out to her.

"When you're older," he said, "if I ever seem far away—if I ever look at you like a stranger—you come find this memory. Fly it again in your head. Because I promise you, little star, wherever I am inside myself, however deep they bury me, the part of me that taught you this is the part they cannot reach. Hold the line. Let it pull. Don't let go, and don't fight it. Both at once."

Two days later he wore the crown, and the man who came out from under it had her mother's name wrong within the month, and Isolde's within the year.

* * *

The fire had burned lower. Somewhere beyond the rocks, Bram's mule shifted and stamped, and the sound was enormous in the silence.

Isolde realized her cheeks were wet only when the cold air found them. She did not wipe them away. She had spent three years being the composed princess, the careful fugitive, the woman who hired smugglers with a level voice and paid in silence—and tonight, sitting in the dirt with a cook and a scribe and a branded oath-keeper and a woman who ran cargo like other people ran rivers, she found she had run out of composition entirely.

"That's what I remember," she said. "That's the last clear one. Everything after is fog and ceremony and my father's face going gradually, politely blank, like a portrait left out in the rain." She looked at the fragment in Quentin's hands. "He hid the original somewhere in the palace. I've searched for six years and never found it. But this copy—he gave it to me himself, the morning of the coronation. Tucked it in my coat the way you'd tuck in a child's blanket, and kissed my forehead, and said, 'Hold the line.' Nothing else. He walked into the shrine smiling."

Lena spoke for the first time. Their voice was quiet, roughened by the cold, and it carried the particular weight of someone who has spent seven years listening to dead things and knows how much lives in a living voice.

"The crown feeds on memory," Lena said. "We know that now. It takes the oldest first—it eats from the roots, the childhoods, the names learned earliest. That's why the villages forget their dead before they forget their harvests. That's why your father lost you before he lost his own reflection." They hesitated, and Isolde watched them choose honesty over comfort, the way she was learning this whole strange crew chose it, daily, like a discipline. "If the spirit keeps what it takes—and Vesh says it does, that everything eaten is stored inside it, hoarded like a dragon's gold—then somewhere inside that thing wearing your father's face..."

"...is the man who taught me to fly a kite," Isolde finished.

No one said anything. The fire popped. High above, a star slid free of the peaks and fell, and no one wished on it, because they had all stopped believing wishes worked and started believing in stranger machinery.

"It's not just a weapon, then," Quentin said slowly. He was still holding the fragment, and his thumb had come to rest, unconsciously, on the slanted handwriting, the way a man rests his hand on a friend's shoulder. "What you're carrying. It's not just the counter-words that can bind the spirit. It's proof. Proof that Aldric fought. Proof that he knew, and walked in anyway, and left his daughter a thread to follow." He looked up, and the firelight made the exile-brand look almost healed, almost like an ordinary scar. "I accused that man of being worn. In front of the court. I said the king was hollow. And he was—but I never once considered that he might have chosen the hollowing. That he might have walked into it *on purpose*, to buy time, to leave a door open." His voice cracked on the last word, and he bowed his head over the parchment. "Gods forgive me. I exiled myself for telling half a truth."

"He never blamed you," Isolde said. "I read the court records. When you spoke, he—the thing in him—went very still. But underneath, in the way his hands moved, in the half-second before the guards took you... I think some part of him was *glad*. Someone had finally said it out loud. Someone had finally seen the cage." She managed something that was almost a smile. "You weren't disgraced, Quentin. You were the first witness. Witnesses get treated badly. That's how you know they matter."

Sefa snorted softly from across the fire. "In my experience witnesses get thrown off cliffs. But fine. Poetic version's nicer." She was turning something over in her own hands—a whetstone, working it along a knife she did not need to sharpen, the fidget of a woman processing feelings she'd deny owning. "So the plan's the same. Shrine, trials, unbinding. Only now we know the king left us a map and a message, and the princess here has been carrying the map stitched to her ribs for three years like a madwoman." She glanced at Isolde, and her voice dropped its irony entirely, which from Sefa was more intimate than tears. "Why didn't you show us sooner?"

"Because showing it means admitting it might burn," Isolde said simply. "As long as it stayed folded, it was still a hope. Open it in front of people, and it becomes a plan, and plans fail, and then the last thing my father ever trusted me with is ash on a mountainside." She looked around the circle—at Lena's patient dark eyes, at Bram's grizzled nod, at Sefa's knife going still, at Quentin cradling the fragment like a sleeping bird. "But I watched you all walk into a warden ambush for a stranger's contract. I watched Bram vote yes for a half-frozen scribe he'd never met. So I'm done protecting my hope by keeping it locked away. My father held the line alone for twenty years. I don't intend to make the same mistake. Whatever comes—we carry this together now."

Bram cleared his throat, a sound like a landslide deciding to be polite. "Kite story," he rumbled. "Tell it proper sometime. The whole thing. With the running." A pause. "My girl used to fly one off the mill roof. Before." He did not say *before what*. No one needed him to. The hollow villages had taken enough names that the word *before* had grown teeth.

"I will," Isolde promised. "Every detail. The ribbons, the falling, the terrible laughing."

"Good." Bram nodded once, decisively, and began gathering the stew bowls, and that was that; in Bram's economy, a promise accepted was a thing already built.

* * *

Later, when the others had rolled into their bedrolls and the watch had changed, Isolde sat awake with the fragment restored to its oilcloth, pressed flat against her ribs where it had lived so long she sometimes thought it had grown there.

Quentin had copied it out twice by firelight—one copy for Lena to study, one for the shrine, "in case yours burns," he'd said, and she had loved him a little for the practicality of it, the way grief loves anything that treats it as survivable. Lena had pored over the counter-words until sleep took them mid-syllable, pen still in hand, and Sefa had covered them with a blanket without comment, and Isolde had understood that she was watching a family assemble itself out of strangers and shared danger, the way rivers assemble themselves out of rain.

Her father had flown alone into the storm with his eyes open. She had a crew.

She was almost asleep when Lena stirred by the coals and sat up sharply, one hand pressed flat against their sternum, eyes wide and unfocused in the way Isolde had learned meant the voice in their blood was speaking.

"What is it?" Isolde whispered. "What's wrong?"

Lena's gaze found hers across the dead fire, and in the starlight their face had gone pale as the paper ribbons of a blue kite.

"The ink-voice," they breathed. "Vesh. He says we're being followed—not wardens this time. He says it's someone who knew our parents' contracts." A pause, and then, quieter, with dread settling into the words like sediment: "He says the debt-master Corvane has reached the pass. And he's brought soldiers."
