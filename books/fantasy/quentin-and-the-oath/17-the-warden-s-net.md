## 17. The Warden's Net

The trap was not sprung in the dark, as Lena had always imagined traps would be. It came at midday, on a road so ordinary it might have been any road in Thornfell—a rutted track between hedgerows gone gold with autumn, the smell of woodsmoke from a farmstead they never reached.

Sefa had taught them to read roads the way Lena read margins: for what was written between the lines. So when the birds went quiet—not startled, but *absent*—Lena felt the page turn before anyone else did.

"Wardens," they said quietly. "Ahead and behind. The hedges are wrong."

Sefa's head came up like a hawk's. "Wrong how?"

"No droppings. No nests. Someone's walked these lines every day for a week, teaching the birds to leave." Lena's hands were already moving, tucking the codex deeper into their coat, feeling the ink-voice stir against their ribs like a second heartbeat. *They have been patient,* Vesh murmured. *Patience is a warden's first weapon.*

Quentin's hand went to the sword he no longer wore openly. Isolde, hooded and unremarkable in her traveling wool, went very still in the way of someone who had spent her whole life being looked at and had learned exactly how to stop.

It didn't matter. The net closed anyway.

They came out of the hedgerows and the ditches and the farm lane all at once—twelve of them in grey-and-iron, faces wrapped, crossbows leveled with the unhurried competence of men who had done this a hundred times. Not soldiers. Wardens. Crown-sworn, oath-bound, answerable to no magistrate. The kind of men who made people disappear into ledgers.

"On your knees," said the one in front, and his voice was tired rather than cruel, which somehow made it worse. "All of you. Hands where I can see them."

* * *

They knelt in the dust of the road because there was nowhere else to be. Lena kept their eyes down and their breathing even and listened—to boots, to the creak of crossbow stocks, to the small human sounds beneath the discipline. A man coughing. Another shifting his weight off a blistered foot. And underneath it all, faint as writing pressed through from the page below, the dead.

There were always dead near wardens. People who had asked questions. People who had run. Their voices pooled around the grey-and-iron like oil around a lamp, and Lena had learned long ago not to flinch when the dead spoke, because flinching was how secrets got noticed.

But one voice was louder than the rest, and closer, and it wasn't dead at all.

"Which one is the scribe?"

The question came from the farm lane, unhurried footsteps on packed earth, and the wardens parted the way water parts around a keel. Lena risked a glance up.

Warden-Captain Hale was not what the stories promised. The stories said he was a butcher with a ledger, that he'd cleared the Vale of Merrow in a season, that he could smell old-god touch on a person like smoke on cloth. What knelt into view beside Lena was a broad, gray-stubbled man in his fifties with a warden's cloak gone soft at the shoulders and eyes the color of rainwater over slate—eyes that were, Lena realized with a lurch, *tired in a way that had nothing to do with the road.*

Hale stopped in front of them. He didn't draw a weapon. He drew a small book instead, thumbed it open, and read aloud in a flat clerk's voice:

"Lena Nakamura. Indentured scribe, Temple of Saint Verren, scriptorium second-class. Contract sold to crown service by debt-master Corvane, price paid in full, transfer registered—" he turned the page "—eleven days ago. You've been running eleven days."

"Has it only been eleven days," Sefa said pleasantly from beside them, "it feels like a season."

"Sefa Vane." Hale didn't look at her. "Smuggling charges pending in four jurisdictions. I'll get to you."

"You're very kind."

"I'm very busy." Hale crouched then, slowly, until he was level with Lena, and studied their face the way a man studies a lock before deciding whether to pick it or break the door. Up close, Lena could see the fine tremor in his left hand where it held the book. Could see the ink smudge on his thumb—he wrote his own reports, then. Could see, most unsettling of all, that he was looking at them not with hatred or even duty, but with something that looked almost like *hope*, and hope in an enemy's face was the most frightening thing Lena had seen in weeks.

"They say you hear the dead," Hale said.

"I copy prayers," Lena said. "That's all."

"The dead don't answer copied prayers."

"No," Lena agreed. "They usually don't."

Something moved behind the captain's eyes—there and gone, a fish under ice. He stood, and his voice went hard again, pitched for his men. "Bind the smugglers. The girl too." A pause; Isolde's hood had slipped just enough. "The *other* girl especially. Gag her if she speaks above a whisper—I'll know why later."

*He doesn't know,* Lena thought, heart slamming. *He has Isolde in irons and he doesn't know who she is.* Or he suspected and hadn't decided yet whether knowing would be worth the trouble. Either way, the princess said nothing, chin high, letting herself be bound like cargo, and Lena loved her a little for it.

They marched them off the road into the farmyard of the empty stead—the family fled weeks ago, the door hanging open, a child's wooden horse still sitting on the step as though it expected to be picked up—and put them against the barn wall in a row. Crossbows stayed level. The afternoon sun lay warm on the boards, indifferent.

And that was when Lena heard the boy.

Not a ghost. A living voice, thin and young, coming from inside the farmhouse, where two wardens had been posted. At first Lena took it for a captive—but no, the tone was wrong, easy, complaining the way children complain about supper. Then one of the wardens answered, gentle, exasperated, fond:

"Tamsin, we are *not* stopping to fish. Get back in the cart."

*Tamsin.* But the voice that answered was a boy's voice, bright and indignant: "You *promised*, Da! You said after the Merrow road we'd—"

The sentence stopped. Not cut off—*stopped*, the way a candle stops when the wick's burned through. Silence stretched. Then the boy's voice again, smaller, puzzled: "...Da? Why are you looking at me like that?"

Lena's blood went cold.

They knew that silence. They had heard it in villages along the pass, in the way mothers hesitated over cradles, in the way old men called their wives by the wrong names and laughed it off. The crown fed, and it fed nearest to hand, and a warden who carried the king's writ carried the king's hunger home with him like fleas.

Hale had begun forgetting his son.

* * *

They worked on Lena alone, at first, in the barn's cool interior while the others waited roped outside under guard. Hale sat across a feed trough from them, the little book open, a stub of pencil moving.

"Where is the shrine."

"I don't know what you mean."

"The mountain shrine. The first-oath place. Your companions have been asking after mountain passes since the salt market. My informers are cheap but they are numerous." Pencil scratch. "Where is the shrine."

"If I knew, why would I tell a man who serves the thing wearing the king's face?"

Hale's pencil stopped. For a moment the barn was very quiet—just wind in the loose shingles, and the far-off sound of Tamsin's boy asking his father, again, why he was looking at him like that.

"Do you know," Hale said at last, conversationally, "what the worst part of my work is? It isn't the killing. Killing is quick and you sleep after. It's the *records*. Every person I take, I write them down. Name, trade, kin, last known words. Eleven years of it. Forty-one books like this one." He tapped the cover. "I used to think that was mercy. That as long as someone was written down, they couldn't be wholly lost."

"And now?"

"Now I opened my own first book last month." His voice didn't change at all, which was how Lena knew it cost him everything. "Read about an arrest I made in the year the plague came north. A woman named Maren Hale, taken for harbor-side preaching. And the report says she had a son, age six, who witnessed the arrest and cried." He looked up, and the rainwater eyes were dry and terrible. "Captain. I don't remember my son crying. I remember the woman's face. I remember the weather. I remember the smell of the harbor. I have read that page twenty times and the boy will not come back into my head."

*Tam,* whispered the dead of the Merrow road, all together, a chorus of pity. *His name is Tam.*

"He's fourteen now," Hale said. "He rides with me because I won't leave him in the capital, not with the feeding getting worse there. And yesterday, on the road, he told me a story about a kite. Said I bought it for him when he was seven, off a peddler, blue paper with a painted heron on it. He remembers the string cutting his fingers. He remembers me laughing." Hale closed the book. "I remember nothing. There is a hole in me shaped like a kite, Captain Nakamura, and I know it is a hole only because my son fell into it and looked up at me waiting for me to catch him."

Lena's throat ached. This was the trap, they understood suddenly—not the crossbows, not the ropes. The trap was that Hale was not a monster. The crown's cleverest servants were the ones it had already begun to eat, men fighting for the thing that was devouring them because they could no longer remember why they might refuse.

"You can't fight it with ledgers," Lena said softly. "Writing down what you've lost doesn't give it back. Believe me. I've spent seven years copying prayers into a book no god reads."

"Then what?" And there it was again—that awful hope, naked now, stripped of rank. "You people are running toward the mountain. Toward the shrine. Everyone touched by the old gods runs the same direction, like iron filings to a lodestone. Tell me you have something better than a ledger. Tell me there's a rite, a name, a *word*—"

"There is a name," Lena said, before wisdom could stop them.

Vesh stirred, alarmed. *Child, what are you doing.*

*Bargaining,* Lena answered silently. *The only coin I have.*

Hale went very still. "Say that again."

"The crown-spirit has a true name. Everything bound has a name—it's how binding works. The old gods weren't worshipped, Captain, they were *contracted*, and contracts have terms, and terms can be read." Lena held his gaze. "There's a relic in the temple archive at Verren—or what's left of it. A seal-stone from the forging. If I touch it, I can read the name off it. I'm the only one who can. And once the name is spoken aloud in the right place, the thing in the crown can be *unbound*. Stripped out of your king. Sent wherever bound things go."

Outside, a warden shifted; a crossbow stock creaked. Inside, Hale sat with his hands flat on the table like a man steadying himself on a deck.

"What do you want for it."

"My friends walk free. All four. Ropes off, horses back, no pursuit, no report to the capital. You tell your superiors you found nothing but smugglers and let them go for lack of evidence." Lena's heart was hammering and they made their voice stay level. "In exchange, I go with you to Verren. Willingly. Under your guard. I read the seal-stone, I give you the name, and you can do whatever your orders say to do with me after."

"That's a fool's bargain," Hale said. "You're offering yourself to the crown's custody."

"I'm offering myself to *you*," Lena said. "There's a difference. I don't think you'd deliver me to the palace alive once you understood what the palace does with people like me. I think you'd find your orders had become... flexible." They leaned forward. "You want your son's name back, Captain. I can't promise that. But I can promise you this: the thing eating your memory has a name, and names are how you fight things. Ask any warden. You've built eleven years of mercy on that principle."

The silence went on so long that Lena counted the shingles rattling in the wind. Forty-one. Forty-two.

"You understand," Hale said finally, heavily, "that if this is a trick—if you lead me to Verren and there's no stone, no name—I won't be able to protect you from my own men. And if it's *not* a trick, if you actually can speak that name..." He rubbed his face with the ink-smudged hand. "Then you're more dangerous than every smuggler and pretender I've hunted in eleven years combined, and the palace will want you dead the moment you're done being useful. You're buying your friends' freedom with your own life."

"Yes," Lena said simply. "That's what it costs. I've been told the price of things my whole life. This is the first time I've set one myself."

Something cracked in the captain's face then—not a smile, nothing so whole. Just a fracture, a seam letting light through. He stood, pushed open the barn door, and said in his flat command voice:

"Cut them loose. All of them. Insufficient evidence—smuggler trash and a runaway scribe, not worth the paperwork." And then, quieter, only for Lena, as the ropes came off Sefa's wrists and Quentin's and Isolde's: "Verren. Three days. Bring nothing you can't burn."

* * *

They rode away from the farmyard in a silence that lasted a full mile, until the hedges gave way to open moor and Sefa finally exploded.

"*What in the nine hells was that?*" She wheeled her horse alongside Lena's. "You handed yourself to the warden-captain of Thornfell! Alone! On a maybe!"

"On a strong maybe," Lena said. "The seal-stone exists. Vesh remembers it."

*I remember it,* the ink-voice confirmed grimly, loud enough now that Lena felt it press behind their eyes. *And I remember that reading a bound thing's true name is not like reading a ledger, little scribe. Names bite. When you speak his, he will hear yours.*

"Then it's good I have three days to decide what mine is," Lena said aloud, and Sefa stared at them, and Quentin—riding quiet on Isolde's other side—gave Lena a long look that held neither approval nor blame, only the grave attention of a man watching someone keep an oath they hadn't formally sworn yet.

"You could have let him hang us," Isolde said at last, softly. "Any of us. He'd have believed whatever story kept you safest. Instead you bought four strangers with your own neck." Her hood was back now; the late sun caught the stubborn line of her jaw. "Why?"

Lena watched the moor roll past, gold and endless, and thought of a hole in a man shaped like a kite. Thought of a mother's face remembered and a son's tears forgotten. Thought of seven years of margins, of every name they'd ever copied for no one, waiting.

"Because someone should get to choose the price," they said. "Just once. Even if it kills me."

Behind them, far off on the road, faint as writing pressed through from another page, a boy's voice called out happily to his father—and the father's answer, when it came, was half a heartbeat too slow, like a man reaching for a word that wasn't where he'd left it.

Three days to Verren. Three days to learn a name that bites.

Lena touched the codex through their coat, felt Vesh coil tight as a drawn wire, and rode on.
