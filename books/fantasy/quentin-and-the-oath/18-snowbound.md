## 18. Snowbound

The storm came down on them like a debt collector—sudden, total, and indifferent to what they owed.

Bram smelled it an hour before the sky split. Twenty years of cooking over open fires in every pass between Thornfell and the salt flats had given him a nose for weather the way other men had a nose for coin, and what he smelled was wet stone and old snow and the particular iron tang of a sky about to empty itself. He said nothing at first. He just shifted his pack higher on his shoulders, lengthened his stride until he was walking beside Sefa at the head of the line, and grunted.

She glanced at him. She always knew what his grunts meant; that was the trouble with twenty years of sharing a fire.

"How long?"

"Before dark. Maybe less."

"Shelter?"

He squinted up the switchback ahead of them, where the trail clung to the mountain's flank like a scar that hadn't finished healing. "There's a cave. Old smuggler cache, two bends past the frozen falls. If it hasn't fallen in."

"And if it has?"

"Then we dig," Bram said, "and we complain about it, which is half as warm but twice as honest."

Sefa laughed—that short, sharp bark of hers that never quite reached her eyes when she was counting risks—and turned back to call the order down the line. Quentin tightened Isolde's cloak at the throat with the fussy care of a man who had once been paid to protect royalty and had apparently never stopped, royalty or no. Lena walked near the middle of the column, hood up, hands tucked into their sleeves, watching the mountain the way they watched everything: like a page they were trying to read before someone snatched it away.

The storm gave them forty minutes. It was generous by mountain standards and cruel by every other measure. The first flakes came sideways, then the wind came behind them, and then the world simply closed, white pressing against white, until Bram was navigating by memory and the pull of the rope he'd tied from his own belt to Sefa's, counting paces to the frozen falls the way he'd counted them at nineteen, the first time old Maro had dragged him over this pass with a stolen bolt of silk and a mouth full of lies.

Two bends past the falls. There.

The cave mouth was exactly where his memory had left it—a black seam in the rock, half-hidden behind a curtain of ice, big enough for six people and a mule if nobody was proud about elbow room. The mule, a sour-faced animal named Turnip whom Bram privately suspected of holding grudges across incarnations, expressed an opinion about the arrangement and was overruled.

Inside, the cold changed its character rather than its mind. It went from a knife to a weight. Bram got a fire going in the old ring of stones—Maro's ring, still there after all these years, the same flat rocks some dead smuggler had carried up this mountain one by one because a fire set directly on cave stone cracks the stone, and a cracked stone floor means a collapsed roof, and a collapsed roof means your bones stay on this mountain keeping company with the ice—and the small yellow light pushed back just enough dark to make the place livable.

They settled the way travelers settle, which is to say the way animals do: backs to the rock, feet toward the fire, each person claiming a territory roughly the size of a grave and defending it with elbows. Quentin took the wall nearest the entrance, because of course he did; the man slept like a drawn blade even now, especially now. Isolde curled beside him, pretending she wasn't cold, fooling no one. Sefa sat cross-legged with her maps, though there was nothing to map in a whiteout and she knew it; she read them anyway, running her thumb along contour lines like a woman counting rosary beads. Lena sat closest to the fire, hands stretched toward it, and Bram noticed—they had been noticing for days, the way you notice a limp or a lie—that the scribe hadn't spoken above a whisper since morning.

Turnip complained. The storm howled. The fire cracked and spat.

It was Sefa who broke first, because Sefa could endure anything except silence longer than a watch.

"Well," she said, to no one and everyone, "if we're going to die on this mountain, I'd rather do it properly fed. Bram."

"I heard you the first time."

"You didn't say anything."

"I was composing my refusal."

He made the stew anyway. Of course he made the stew. Dried lamb, roots he'd hoarded from the last free-town like a dragon guarding particularly unglamorous treasure, barley, and the last of the good pepper he'd been saving for a day that felt like it deserved it. A storm-trapped crew facing a warden-hunted road into a city full of forgetting—that day qualified. He cooked the way he prayed, which was wordlessly and with his whole attention, and when he ladled it out into six bowls (five bowls and the dented tin cup that had belonged to Maro and that Sefa drank from on principle), the smell alone improved the cave by several degrees.

They ate. The storm screamed. And somewhere between the second bowl and the third, the evening did what evenings around fires do—it softened.

* * *

It started, as these things often did, with an argument.

"The kite story is not true," Sefa announced, apropos of nothing, jabbing her spoon toward Quentin. "Nobody learns to fly a kite off a palace roof. That's a nursemaid's tale."

"It happened," Isolde said, with the mild steel that was apparently hereditary in the royal line.

"On a *roof*."

"The south tower. My father said the wind was truer there." Isolde's voice did something complicated on the word *father*, a door opening and shutting very fast, and Bram saw Quentin's hand move—an inch, no more—toward hers before stopping, the way a man touches a hot thing and remembers himself. "He held the string and told me the trick wasn't pulling. It was letting out line. You can't fight the wind. You give it slack and take it back, give and take, until the kite trusts you."

"That's not how kites work," Sefa said. "That's not how *anything* works."

"It's how my father worked," Isolde said quietly, "before."

Silence took the cave again, but it was a different silence now—heavier, more careful, the kind of silence that has a person standing in the middle of it. Bram stirred the coals. He was not a talker; he had built a whole life on the understanding that words were like salt, best used sparingly and never wasted on things that would keep. But he knew stories. Every cook knows stories. People tell cooks things they'd never tell priests, because priests are listening for sins and cooks are only listening for hunger.

"First winter I ran with Sefa's crew," Bram said, to the fire more than to anyone, "we got caught in weather worse than this. Up past Greywater, in the old watch-station. Three days snowed in, five of us, and the food for two."

Quentin looked up. So did Lena. Even Sefa paused over her cup.

"Old Maro—she ran the crew before Sefa—she dealt it out fair, same portions every meal, and on the second night Veyra, who was our scout then, started telling stories. Not true ones. Big ones. Lies so large they needed their own weather. She'd fought a river-pirate queen single-handed. She'd supped with the Fox of the Fenlands. Every night the stories got bigger, and here's the thing—" Bram pointed his spoon at the circle, a gesture he'd learned from Maro and that Sefa had inherited along with the maps and the temper. "Here's the thing. Nobody got hungry. I don't mean the food lasted. I mean the *hunger* didn't come. You put five scared people in a hole in the ground with three days of food and you'll have fighting by dawn. You put five people in a hole with three days of food and a liar like Veyra, and they walk out friends."

"A parable about lying," Quentin said. His mouth twitched. "From an oath-keeper."

"An ex-oath-keeper," Sefa corrected.

"An oath-keeper," Quentin said, and there was something in his voice that made Sefa raise an eyebrow and let it go, "who is learning that oaths can be kept crooked and still be kept."

Bram shrugged. "I'm saying stories are rations too. Same as bread. You ration them out fair, everybody gets through the night."

He looked at Lena then. He hadn't meant to; the look just went where it went, the way water finds the low ground. The scribe was sitting with their bowl cradled in both hands, empty now, and the firelight was doing something unkind to their face—showing every hollow, every year of temple dust and copied prayers. They looked, Bram thought, like a ledger page someone had written on too hard, the words pressed clean through to the wood beneath.

And Lena, feeling the look—or maybe hearing something in it, because that was the other thing about the scribe, the thing that had stopped making Bram's skin crawl and started making him sad—they looked up, and met his eyes, and said:

"I had a mother who sang off-key."

The words came out plain as a price chalked on a slate. No drama in them. That was somehow worse.

"She couldn't carry a tune in a bucket. My father used to say the gods gave her the words and gave the tune to somebody else, and they'd laugh, and she'd throw a cushion at him, and start over. Off-key. Every night. She sang while she mended, she sang while she cooked, she sang when the debt-collectors came to the door, which—" Lena's hands tightened on the bowl. "Which she thought frightened them off. It didn't. Nothing frightened them. But she sang anyway."

Nobody moved. Even the fire seemed to lean in.

"They sold the contract when I was nine," Lena said. "My parents'. Not mine—I was too young to be worth a whole bond, so they kept me and sold them north, to the aqueduct works past the border. Two contracts, one price, and the price covered what my father owed plus interest plus the fee for the assessor plus—" the scribe's voice stayed level, terribly level, the voice of someone reading figures aloud, "plus the cost of the ink and vellum for the sale itself. I know because I've seen the entry. Corvane keeps meticulous records. I copied adjacent pages for seven years. I never found theirs. I used to think that meant they might still be alive. Now I think it means the page was copied into another book, in another hand, and the hand that wrote it didn't know what it was writing either."

"Lena," Quentin began.

"I'm not finished." Plain as arithmetic. "My father whittled. Little birds, mostly. He'd sell them at market for coppers. The last thing he gave me was a bird with a chipped wing, and he said, *it flies crooked but it flies*, and I didn't understand what he meant until years later, and by then I'd lost the bird in a ledger-room sweep, they burned everything personal in the scriptorium twice a year, did you know that? Twice a year. Cleanliness of mind, Corvane called it. So I don't have the bird. I don't have the song. I have—" and here, for the first time, the level voice bent, just slightly, like a reed in wind, "I have the fact of them. Two entries' worth of absence. And I have spent seven years copying prayers to gods who don't answer, and I think—I think some part of me believed that if I was very precise, if I copied every letter perfectly, the precision itself would be a kind of prayer, and somebody, somewhere, would look at the margins and find my parents written there, because I wrote them there, in the only place I was allowed to write anything. In the margins. Where nobody reads."

The cave was very quiet. Outside, the storm threw itself against the mountain like something that wanted in, and inside, six people and a mule sat in a ring of firelight with one bowl's worth of grief sitting among them, steaming.

It was Bram who moved. He was closest to the supplies, and he was not a talker, and so he did the only thing he knew: he reached into his pack, past the roots and the salt-cloth, and brought out the small cloth-wrapped bundle he carried at the bottom of everything, under the spare flint, where it had ridden for eleven years, and unwrapped it, and set it on the stone between himself and Lena.

A wooden bird. Carved pine, worn smooth by a decade of a large rough thumb worrying it in the quiet hours. One wing chipped.

"My daughter's," he said. "She carved it herself. Badly. She was six." He cleared his throat, which had developed an obstruction. "She died of lung-fever the winter after. Her mother went two springs later. Same fever, slower about it. I kept the bird because throwing it in the river felt like losing her twice, and I am not a man who can afford to lose things twice. I've got no cleverness about grief, scribe. No words worth the salt they're seasoned with. But I know what it is to hold the fact of someone and nothing else. Fact of them, and no song."

Lena looked at the bird. Their hand came out—slowly, the way you reach toward a fire you've been told will burn you—and hovered over the chipped wing without touching.

"*It flies crooked but it flies*," they whispered.

"Aye," Bram said. "That's the whole of the wisdom I've got. Things fly crooked. They still fly. Crews included."

Sefa was looking at the bird with an expression Bram had seen perhaps four times in twenty years, the expression she wore when the maps failed and she had to navigate by something else. Quentin had taken Isolde's hand properly now, and neither of them was pretending otherwise. Even Turnip had gone silent, which the mule had never once done voluntarily and which Bram chose to interpret as respect.

"So," Sefa said at last, and her voice was rougher than usual, and she didn't try to sand it smooth. "Stories instead of secrets. That's the rule tonight?"

"That's the rule," Bram said.

"Then I've got one. Greywater, the watch-station, the night Veyra claimed she'd danced with the Duke of Saltmarsh's ghost—"

"—that one was TRUE," Bram said, scandalized. "I was THERE."

"—and I say she made it up, and I say the proof is that the ghost allegedly asked her to dance AGAIN the following spring, and ghosts don't repeat themselves, everyone knows—"

"Ghosts absolutely repeat themselves," Lena said faintly, and the laugh that went around the cave was wet and ragged and real, and it broke the night open the way a thaw breaks a river—not gently, but moving.

They traded stories until the fire burned low. Quentin told one about his first posting, guarding a granary from nothing but his own boredom, and how he'd befriended the rats rather than kill them, and Isolde laughed until she had to put her cup down. Isolde told one about sneaking into the kitchens at eight years old and being taught to roll dough by a cook who treated her like any other clumsy child, and Bram listened to that one with particular attention and decided, privately, that whoever that cook had been, they'd done the kingdom more service than half the lords. Sefa told the Greywater stories, all of them, the true ones and the enormous ones, and refused to say which was which. Bram himself told the one about Maro out-drinking a customs officer and then selling him his own boots back at markup, and even Quentin laughed at that, a startled bark like a man discovering a muscle he'd forgotten he owned.

And somewhere past midnight, when the fire had gone to embers and the storm had spent its first fury and settled into the long steady roar of a mountain winter doing its work, Lena spoke again. Quietly. Almost to the coals.

"Bram. Your daughter's name?"

"Edda."

"Edda." The scribe said it carefully, the way they copied a rare word—giving it its full weight, its exact letters. "I'll remember it. That's the one thing I'm good for now, I think. Remembering. What the margins say. What the songs sounded like, even off-key. What the names were." A pause. "Someone should keep the record. Since the crown eats the ledgers."

"Then we'll keep it together," Sefa said, from her bedroll, eyes already closing. "Whole crew. Division of labor. I'll remember the roads, Bram remembers the food, Quentin remembers the rules so we know which ones we're breaking, Isolde remembers the palace, and you—" she yawned hugely, "—you remember us. All of us. That's the job now. First job you've ever had that comes with a family attached."

Lena didn't answer. But Bram, who slept light and woke often and saw most of what the fire chose to show him, saw the scribe's lips move in the ember-light, shaping names silently into the dark—Edda, and others, a whole litany of the lost, filed away in the safest vault on the mountain, which was not the shrine they were walking toward and not the archives burning behind them, but the stubborn, crooked, flying heart of one tired scribe who had finally found a page worth writing on.

* * *

Bram woke before dawn, as he always did, to check the fire and the weather and the mule, in that order.

The storm had broken. Through the cave mouth, the sky showed hard and clean and blue-black, scattered with the last stars, and the snow lay deep and wind-carved on the trail below—deep enough to slow them, shallow enough to cross. Good traveling, if you didn't mind cold. He minded cold. He minded it professionally.

He was banking the coals for the morning meal when he heard it: a sound from the pass below, thin on the frozen air. Not wind. Wind didn't fall into rhythm.

Hoofbeats. Many of them, moving in formation, coming up the southern switchback with the disciplined cadence of men who rode for pay rather than love of it.

Bram stood very still, one hand on the cave's icy lip, and watched the grey light below, and counted, and did not like his count.

Then he turned back into the cave, where his family lay sleeping in a ring of ash, and put his boot gently against Sefa's shoulder.

"Wake up," he said quietly. "Riders on the pass. And bring the bird—we're leaving it somewhere warmer than this."
