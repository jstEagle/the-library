## 20. Corvane's Offer

The snow had stopped an hour before Corvane found them, and Lena would spend the rest of their life wishing it hadn't. Fresh snow meant fresh tracks, and fresh tracks meant that the debt-master's outriders had followed them up the switchbacks like hounds on a blood-trail—patient, unhurried, certain. Sefa had chosen the campsite well: a hollow beneath a granite overhang where the fire's light couldn't be seen from the road and the wind funneled away eastward instead of down into the tents. But well-chosen ground was no armor against a man who had spent seven years learning every weakness Lena possessed.

Lena heard him before anyone else did.

It wasn't the crunch of boots on crusted snow—that came later, polite and deliberate, timed so that the sentry would have time to announce him rather than raise an alarm. It was the ink. Lena had been mending a page of Bram's old recipe book by firelight, copying out a stew recipe that had been water-stained past reading, and the ink in Lena's travel pot went still. Not dried-still. *Listening*-still. The way it did when something dead or hungry or both came near.

"Corvane," Lena said quietly, to no one, to everyone.

Bram looked up from his whetstone. Sefa's hand drifted to her knife with the unhurried ease of a woman who had already decided how she'd use it. Quentin rose, oath-brand hand pressed flat against his chest as though he could feel the shape of what approached through the scar tissue.

"He's alone," Lena said. The ink told them that much—the dead left traces in everything they'd touched, and Corvane carried his ghosts like coins in a purse, jingling. "He wants to talk."

"Wardens don't talk," Sefa said.

"He's not here for the wardens." Lena set down the pen. Their hands were steady, which surprised them. Seven years of dread had apparently worn grooves deep enough that even this slid along them smoothly. "He's here for me."

* * *

Corvane came into the firelight wrapped in a traveling cloak of good grey wool, fur at the collar, boots that had never known a day of honest mud. He was smaller than Lena remembered—or perhaps Lena had simply grown. In the scriptorium he had seemed a fixture of the building itself, part of the architecture of debt, as permanent and pitiless as load-bearing stone. Here in the open air, under a sky salted with stars, he looked like what he was: a soft man in fine clothes who had traveled a long way in cold weather because there was money in it.

Two of his outriders waited at the edge of the camp, just beyond the ring of firelight, hands visible and empty. A courtesy. A message: *this is a negotiation, not a seizure.*

"Master Nakamura." He inclined his head with the precise degree of respect owed to property of considerable value. "You've been difficult to find."

"I wasn't hiding," Lena said. "I was walking."

"A distinction without a difference, I'm afraid." He glanced around the camp with the appraising eye of a man inventorying assets—Sefa's knives, Quentin's brand, Isolde half-shadowed at the back of the hollow with her hood up, though of course he knew exactly who she was; the bounty notices had made sure of that. "May I sit? The ride up was punishing."

"No," said Sefa.

"Yes," said Corvane, and sat anyway, on a supply crate that Bram pointedly did not offer him. He arranged his cloak with a fussy little motion that Lena knew intimately—the same motion he'd used settling into the chair across from debtors in his counting room, the one that said the discomfort in this conversation belonged entirely to the person opposite him. "I'll be brief. I know you're a busy company."

"We're listening," Quentin said. His voice had the flat calm he used when his hands wanted to do something else. "Briefly."

Corvane smiled at him without warmth. "Oath-keeper. Or former oath-keeper, I should say. What a curious crew you've collected, Nakamura. An exile, a smuggler, a cook with a warrant in three provinces—" Bram grunted, unbothered, "—and Her Highness Princess Isolde, traveling incognito with a fragment of the coronation oath sewn into her lining. You must forgive me if I skip the pretense that I don't know why you're all here. The crown has been very generous with information lately. It values its secrets less than it used to. It prefers to trade them."

Lena felt the cold move through them that had nothing to do with the mountain air. *It trades its secrets.* The thing wearing Aldric's crown, giving away pieces of the kingdom's memory like a merchant selling off stock before the shop burns.

"What do you want, Corvane?"

"Want is such a strong word." He produced a document from inside his cloak—a single sheet, heavy vellum, sealed twice—and laid it on his knee without offering it. "I prefer *settle*. Your contract, Nakamura. Seven years of interest, penalties, the surcharge we applied after your little episode with the answering prayer. It has grown, I regret to say, into a sum you could not pay in three lifetimes of honest copying. That was rather the design."

"I know it was the design."

"Of course you do. You were always quick." Something almost like fondness crossed his face, and it was worse than cruelty. "Here is what I carry. The crown will forgive the whole of it. Every copper. The ledger page burned before your eyes, if you like—I'll bring the book myself and let you strike the line. Full and unconditional release. You walk away owing nothing to anyone. Free."

The word landed in Lena's chest like a struck bell. *Free.* They had built entire days out of imagining it—in the margins of ledgers, in the dark after lights-out, they had written *free* in tiny letters until the letters stopped meaning anything. And now here it was, offered on vellum, sealed twice, held out over the snow like bait over a trapline.

"And the price," Lena said. Their voice came out level. Seven years of practice at keeping their voice level in front of this man. "What does the crown get?"

"You know what it gets." Corvane spread his hands, reasonable, gentle, the voice he used when explaining arithmetic to people whose children he owned. "The princess. Delivered to the royal wardens at Thornfell Pass, unharmed, within the week. That's all. No one dies. No one fights. The fragment she carries goes back to the archive where it belongs, the succession crisis resolves itself quietly, and you"—he tapped the vellum once, lightly, lovingly—"walk out of this story owing nothing to anybody. Clean paper, Nakamura. Do you know how rare clean paper is? Most people die owing something."

The silence stretched. The fire popped. Somewhere above them, wind moved through the pines with a sound like a long exhale.

Lena looked at the vellum. Then at Isolde, who had come forward into the light without being asked, hood back now, her chin lifted in the particular way of someone who had decided to meet her own price standing up. She didn't look afraid. She looked like she was memorizing Lena's face, the way people memorize faces they expect to lose.

"Well?" Corvane asked softly. "You've dreamed about this moment for seven years. I've watched you dream it. Don't tell me the reality disappoints."

"It does, actually," Lena said.

His smile flickered.

"You want to know what I dreamed, Master Corvane? All those nights in your scriptorium?" Lena stood. There was no plan in it, no strategy—just seven years rising up through them like floodwater finding its level. "I dreamed of walking out of your building with my own name in my mouth. Not traded for. Not bought back at someone else's cost. *Walked out.* You're not offering me freedom. You're offering me a bigger cage with the door painted to look like sky. Because that's what freedom purchased with Isolde's life would be—it would be you, forever, sitting in the middle of every choice I ever made afterward, holding the receipt."

"The princess is going to be taken regardless," Corvane said, and for the first time the silk thinned enough to show the wire underneath. "The wardens are a day behind me. If not through you, then through force. You're not saving her by refusing. You're only choosing to stay poor."

"Then I'll choose to stay poor."

"You'll choose to stay *owned*—"

"No." Lena heard their own voice crack, and didn't care. "No, that's the thing you've never understood, in seven years of owning my hours. Debt is a number. It lives on paper. Paper burns. But what I am—what I can hear, what the old gods woke in me—that isn't on any ledger you keep. You can't sell what I am because you never owned it. You owned my mornings. You owned my wrists while they held the pen. That's all you ever had."

Something moved behind Corvane's eyes—calculation, always calculation—and then he sighed, and folded his hands, and did the thing Lena would remember for the rest of their life, however long or short that life turned out to be.

He leaned back. And he smiled. And he said:

"Do you want to know how your parents' debt was settled?"

* * *

The words went through the camp like a blade through wet canvas.

Sefa had gone very still. Quentin had turned fully toward Corvane, and the oath-brand on his hand had begun, faintly, to glow—not with power but with attention, the way it did when an oath was being tested nearby. Even the fire seemed to lean in.

Lena did not sit down. Sitting would have meant falling.

"My parents are dead," Lena said. "Sold south when I was nine. The fever took them. That's what the letter said. That's what *you* told the temple when you took custody of me."

"The fever took them," Corvane agreed pleasantly. "In the second year. Both of them, within a month of each other, at the Crownmere work-farms. Very sad. But their *contract*, Nakamura—their debt, the balance outstanding against their names—that didn't die. Debts don't die. Debts transfer." He tilted his head. "And contracts touching the old gods, well. Those became very valuable indeed about six years ago. When the crown began to wake up hungry."

The world narrowed. The fire, the snow, the watching faces—all of it receded to the edges, and there was only Corvane's voice, smooth as ledger-vellum, dealing the cards one at a time.

"I sold it," he said. "Your parents' contract. Transferred the balance to the crown's own accounts, at a considerable premium, eight years ago—before you ever copied that first answered prayer, before anyone knew what you were. I didn't sell it because I knew. I sold it because the crown pays best, and because debts owed to the crown are collected by methods I don't have to witness." He shrugged, a small, tidy motion. "Do you understand what I'm telling you? The crown has been collecting on your family for years. Not your labor. Your *line*. There's a reason the spirit in that crown noticed you the moment you touched the margins, Nakamura. It wasn't discovery. It was *repossession*. You were always theirs. I merely held the paper."

The sound Lena made was not a word. It came from somewhere below language, below the seven years of level voice and steady hands, from the nine-year-old who had been handed a pen instead of a funeral and told to copy prayers for the god who'd eaten their parents' future.

They crossed the space between them in three steps.

Quentin moved too—he was faster—but Sefa's arm came up across his chest, and later, much later, he would say he never knew why he obeyed it. Bram caught Lena's shoulder, not to stop them, just to slow them, and it was enough. Lena stood over Corvane with their fist half-raised and shaking, and Corvane looked up at them without fear, because men like Corvane could not conceive of violence that wasn't transactional.

"Go on," he said quietly. "Strike me. My outriders will kill half of you, the wardens will take the rest, and the princess still goes to the crown. Nothing changes except that you'll have spent your one free act on my face." He paused. "Or. Sit down. Listen to the last thing I have to say. It's the truth, and I suspect you've had very little of it from anyone."

Lena's fist opened. Slowly. Finger by finger, the way a hand unclenches when it decides, deliberately, that it refuses to hold what it's been given.

"Say it."

"The crown doesn't want the princess." Corvane rose, brushing snow-damp from his cloak, recovering his composure piece by piece like a man redressing a shop window after a storm. "The crown wants *you*. The princess is leverage because leverage is cheaper than force. The fragment she carries, the shrine, Vesh, the whole ragged pilgrimage of it—you are the key to all of it, and the crown knows it, because the crown helped write the lock." He straightened his collar. "So here is my final offer, freely given, no vellum required: walk away from all of them tonight. Take the north road. I will tell the wardens you went south. You will owe nothing—to me, to the crown, to anyone—because I will simply lose track of you. Some debts are cheapest when the debtor vanishes. Consider it the only honest service I have ever rendered you."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then in approximately one day, warden-captain Hale arrives with forty men, and I watch him take everything you love, and I write it all down in a ledger, and I balance the column." He buttoned his cloak. "I am very good at balancing columns, Nakamura. It's the only prayer I know."

He walked out of the firelight. His outriders fell in behind him. For a long moment the only sounds were the wind and the fire and, distantly, the horses turning on the frozen road.

* * *

No one spoke for a while.

Isolde crouched and fed another branch to the flames, and the light climbed, and Lena became aware gradually that they were sitting down—they didn't remember sitting—and that Bram had put a bowl of something hot in their hands without asking, the way he did everything, as though feeding people were not kindness but simply the correct order of the universe.

"He sold them," Lena said finally. Their voice sounded far away to their own ears. "Before I ever—before any of it. He sold my parents to the thing that ate them. He's been holding the paper on my grief for eight years and charging me interest."

"Yes," said Quentin.

"I should have hit him."

"Yes," said Sefa, and Bram nodded solemnly, and despite everything—despite the ash taste in their mouth and the cold that had gotten under their ribs and would live there now—something in Lena's chest cracked sideways, and it might have been a laugh.

"But you didn't," Quentin said. He crouched in front of them, forearms on his knees, close enough that the faint glow of the cooling brand lit the lines of his face. "You opened your hand. Do you know what that was? I've seen men swear oaths with less discipline than that. You chose, right there, in front of him—you chose who you're going to be. He wanted you to swing. A swinging debtor is just a debtor with bruises. But someone who opens their hand—" he shook his head slowly, "—that's someone the crown can't price."

"The crown can price anything," Lena said. "That's the whole problem."

"Then we stop letting it count us." Isolde spoke from across the fire, quiet and clear. "My father taught me that a king's first duty is to know the worth of things truly. Whatever wears him now knows only the *cost* of things. There's a difference, and it's the whole difference, and I think you just showed it to Corvane tonight whether he understood it or not."

Lena looked down at the bowl in their hands. Stew. Bram's stew, heavy with root vegetables, steam curling up into the cold. And underneath the grief and the rage, underneath the eight years of a lie told in a letter, something small and stubborn stirred—an ember-thing, banked but alive.

Because here was the truth Corvane had handed them along with the poison, without realizing it: he had needed to come himself. Not a letter. Not agents. *Him.* The great balancing machine of Lena's entire childhood had ridden two days into freezing mountains because the numbers had stopped working, because for the first time in seven years the column wouldn't balance, because Lena Nakamura had become something that could not be entered in a ledger.

He had offered them freedom and called it a cage. He had confessed his worst crime and called it leverage. And he had left with neither.

"He'll be back with Hale," Sefa said. She was cleaning her nails with her knife, casual as Sunday, but her eyes were on the dark road. "A day, maybe less. Forty men."

"Then we break camp tonight," Quentin said.

"We can't outrun forty men on these roads," Bram rumbled.

"We don't outrun them." Sefa grinned, and it was the grin she wore entering harbors she wasn't supposed to be in. "We *out-turn* them. There's a route off this pass the wardens don't have on their maps because it isn't on any map. Old smuggler's turn, drops through the scree to the river valley. Narrow, ugly, and absolutely fatal to anyone who learned geography from a ledger." She looked at Lena. "Well, scribe? You're the one who reads everything. Feel like making some margin notes of our own?"

Lena drank the stew down to the bottom. Warmth, real warmth, spread out from the center of them—not the false warmth of Corvane's vellum promises but the plain, unledgered heat of food given freely by people who owed them nothing.

They stood, and looked once more down the dark road where Corvane had gone, and spoke the eulogy for the last seven years of their life:

"Burn the contract," Lena said. "If he comes again—if any of them come again—we burn the paper first and argue after."

Sefa laughed. Bram shouldered his pack. And high on the mountain above them, faint but unmistakable, the ink in Lena's travel pot began—very softly—to sing.
