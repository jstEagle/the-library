## 22. Trials of the First Priests

The shrine gate closed behind them with a sound like a book shutting.

Sefa felt it in her teeth more than her ears—a soft, final click that traveled down through the stone floor and up through the soles of her boots. She turned once, out of habit, to check the way back. There was no way back. The gate had become wall, seamless and old, its carved surface already dimming as if the mountain were swallowing the light of it.

"Well," said Bram, behind her. "That's one door I'd rather not have opened."

"You voted to come," Sefa said.

"I voted yes because you looked like you needed someone sensible along." He shifted his pack on his shoulders. "I'm beginning to question my judgment."

She didn't answer him, because answering would have meant admitting she was questioning it too. Instead she looked ahead, down the corridor the gate had revealed: a passage of pale stone, wide enough for four abreast, lit by nothing they could name. The walls gave off a faint luminescence, like moonlight trapped in milk. It had been waiting a long time, that light. It made her feel watched in a way that wasn't hostile—only patient. The patience of something that had waited nine hundred years and could wait nine hundred more.

Quentin came up beside her, hand resting near the knife at his hip out of old habit rather than any real expectation of use. The exile-brand on his forearm had cooled since he'd sworn himself at the gate—she'd seen him touch it twice on the walk up the mountain, disbelieving, like a man poking at a tooth that had finally stopped aching. "No dust," he said quietly.

"What?"

"No dust. Look." He crouched and ran a finger along the floor's edge. "Nine centuries sealed, and there's not a grain of grit on this stone. Either the mountain cleans house, or—" He glanced at Lena, walking a few paces back with Isolde, their head tilted in that listening way Sefa had learned to read by now. "Or someone's been expecting us."

"Vesh said the trials test the party," Lena murmured. They hadn't stopped walking; their voice drifted forward like smoke. "It didn't say what happens to those who fail."

"Cheerful as ever," Bram grunted.

Isolde said nothing at all. She walked with her hood back and her chin up, the fragment-oath wrapped in oilcloth against her chest, and Sefa caught herself studying the princess the way she studied weather over a pass—looking for the change that would come, trying to guess when. Isolde had been a passenger for eleven days. Somewhere between the salt market and the switchback ambush, she had stopped being one. Sefa wasn't sure she liked what the princess was becoming. She liked even less how much it reminded her of herself, ten years ago, learning that blood bought less than loyalty and cost more.

The corridor ended at a door.

Not a grand one—that was the first surprise. It was a plain slab of grey stone, shoulder-high, set into the wall without frame or hinge or handle. No carving, no inscription. Just stone, and above it, cut shallow into the rock, a single line of the old script that Lena translated before anyone asked:

*Speak what is true, and pass.*

"A riddle door," Quentin said. "I hate riddle doors."

"It's not a riddle." Lena stepped closer, and Sefa saw their fingers twitch—the scribe's reflex, reaching for a pen that wasn't there. "Riddles want cleverness. This wants honesty. There's no trick to find."

"Then it's worse than a riddle," Bram muttered.

Sefa stepped up first, because she was captain, because someone had to go first, and because she had spent twenty years making a profession of knowing which doors to knock on. She put her palm flat against the stone. It was warm. Not sun-warm—blood-warm, skin-warm, the temperature of a living thing at rest.

"Open," she said.

Nothing.

"The truth," Lena said gently. "It wants a truth spoken aloud."

Sefa kept her hand on the stone and thought about all the truths she owned. There were hundreds, thousands, the small currency of a life lived outside the law: the names of every pass through Thornfell, the weight of every bribe, the location of every cache from here to the coast. Those were easy. Those were inventory.

But the door was warm under her palm, and it did not feel like it wanted inventory.

"My name isn't Sefa," she heard herself say.

Behind her, the crew went very still.

"Sefa Dorrin died in a customs raid off Greywater when I was nineteen. I took her papers because mine were worth a hanging and hers were worth a fine. I've been her for twenty years. Her mother wrote to me for six years before she passed, and I answered every letter as her daughter, because telling an old woman the truth would have killed her faster than the sea did." Her voice came out level, which surprised her. "I don't know who I was before. That's the truth. That's the truest thing I have."

The door opened. Silently, inward, revealing a short passage and then another chamber beyond—and on its surface, where her palm had rested, a faint warmth lingered like a handprint fading from glass.

Nobody spoke. Then Bram clapped her once on the shoulder, hard enough to stagger her, and said, "Twenty years and you never told *me*?" and there was laughter buried somewhere in the outrage, and Sefa laughed too, rough and startled, and the moment broke the way ice breaks on a spring river—all at once, and none the worse for it.

"I'll tell you everything over ale," she said. "When we're rich."

"When we're *alive*," Bram corrected. "Rich is a rumor."

Quentin went next. He stood before the second door—it had appeared where the first passage ended, same grey stone, same shallow script—and was silent for a long time. Long enough that Sefa started counting in her head and got past two hundred.

"I swore an oath to protect King Aldric," he said at last, to the stone, to the dark, to whatever listened. "And I broke it. Not by raising a hand against him. By staying silent when I should have spoken. By telling myself the crown was heavy and every king changes, when I knew—I *knew*—that the man wearing it was being eaten alive. I chose my reputation over my oath. That's the truth." His jaw worked. "I have never said it aloud before. Not even to myself."

The door opened. And Sefa, watching him walk through it with his shoulders squared and his eyes wet and refusing to blink, thought: *there goes the most honest man I know, and it took a magic door to get it out of him.* She resolved, privately, to make sure he never regretted saying it.

Lena went third, and their truth was quiet, almost inaudible: "I hear the dead, and part of me has always been glad of it, because the dead are the only ones who ever talked to me like I mattered." The door opened before they finished speaking, as if it had heard enough—as if the wanting itself, confessed, was the truth.

Isolde stood before the last door with her spine straight and her hands folded, a princess to the bone, and Sefa braced for something formal, something rehearsed.

"I am glad my father forgot me," Isolde said. "Because the man who taught me to fly kites would be heartbroken by what I've become, and I couldn't bear to see it in his eyes."

The silence after that was the worst kind—not shocked, but recognized. Every one of them had a version of that sentence folded away somewhere. The door opened, and no one met anyone else's eye as they passed through, and somehow that was its own kind of closeness.

* * *

The bridge was the second trial, and it was worse.

They came out of the door-chambers into a vast vertical space—a chasm in the mountain's bones, so deep that dropping a pebble produced no sound of landing, only a long diminishing whisper, like a word being forgotten. Across it stretched a span of stone perhaps forty feet long and no wider than a coffin laid flat. It looked solid. It looked, Sefa thought grimly, exactly as solid as things always looked right before they killed you.

Carved above the far side: *No one crosses alone.*

"Right," Bram said. "So we go one at a time, quick and light—"

He stepped onto the bridge and the bridge ceased to exist.

Not collapsed—*ceased*. One moment there was stone under his boot; the next there was open air, and Bram pitched forward with a bellow, and Sefa lunged and caught his collar and hauled him back onto solid ground with a strength she didn't know fear could lend her. For a heartbeat they stood tangled together, his heart hammering against her arm like a fist on a door.

"It's not a bridge," Lena said faintly. Their eyes had gone distant, unfocused—the ink-voice look. "It's a *weighing*. Vesh says—" They pressed their fingers to their temple. "The bridge holds only shared weight. It measures what you carry alone versus what you carry together. Cross it solo and it isn't there. Cross it carrying each other and—"

"And it holds," Quentin finished. He was looking at the gap where the bridge had been, and Sefa saw him doing the arithmetic: five people, forty feet of nothing, and a crossing that required them to be, quite literally, each other's burden.

"So we link arms," Isolde said. "Like crossing a flooded ford."

"Like crossing a flooded ford," Sefa agreed, "over a bottomless hole, while trusting four people I've known between two months and twenty years." She rolled her shoulders. "Bram with me. Heaviest pair in the middle. Quentin and Isolde behind us. Lena—"

"Lena in the middle too," Quentin said. "Lightest. If anyone slips, they fall *onto* us, not past us."

It was good tactics and Sefa hated that she hadn't thought of it first. She linked arms with Bram, his bulk solid and warm against her side, and felt Lena take her other arm, thin fingers gripping hard. Behind them, Quentin and Isolde completed the chain. Five people, one shape.

"Step together," Sefa said. "On my count. And nobody looks down. Look at me, look at Bram, look at the far side. There is no down."

There was, of course, a down. She felt it the whole way across—a vast cold attention rising out of the chasm like breath from a well, the mountain's own hunger or indifference, she couldn't tell which. But the bridge held. It held under their shared weight the way a rope holds under a team hauling together: not because any single strand was strong, but because none of them pulled alone. Halfway across, Bram's boot skidded on frost-slick stone and the whole chain lurched, and Sefa felt four grips tighten on her at once—Lena's fingers digging into her arm, Bram's weight swinging into her shoulder, the drag and counterweight of Quentin and Isolde behind—and the bridge *held*, hummed almost, a low note in the stone like approval, and they stumbled the rest of the way across in a single stumbling organism and collapsed onto the far ledge in a heap of arms and packs and ragged breathing.

For a moment nobody moved. Then Bram, flat on his back, staring at the ceiling of the cavern, began to laugh. It rolled out of him in great helpless waves until Sefa was laughing too, and Isolde—prim, careful Isolde—had both hands over her mouth and her shoulders shaking, and even Quentin wore something that on another face might have been called a grin.

"Never again," Bram wheezed.

"Again on the way out, probably," Sefa said.

"Don't," said everyone, in unison, and that—more than the bridge, more than the doors—felt like the trial being passed.

* * *

The third chamber had no door at all. They simply walked out of the bridge-chamber's far passage and into a darkness that swallowed the luminous stone, that swallowed lantern-light, that swallowed, Sefa suspected, anything you brought into it except yourself.

A voice—not Vesh's dry whisper, but something older, layered, many-voiced, like a choir heard through a mountain—spoke from everywhere and nowhere:

*The last trial is yours alone. Each will walk it singly. Each will meet what they least wish to meet. Come out the far side, all of you, and the shrine is open.*

"And if we don't?" Sefa called into the black.

The dark did not answer. Dark places rarely did.

They argued briefly about order—Sefa insisted on going last, captain's privilege, so she'd know if the others needed pulling out—but the dark apparently had opinions, because when Lena stepped forward to test the boundary, the darkness simply took them. No sound, no struggle. One moment Lena stood at the edge; the next the space where they'd been was empty air and the others were shouting, and then, from somewhere impossibly far away, Lena's voice floated back: *"It's all right! I'm—I'm somewhere. Keep going!"*

One by one, the dark collected them. Sefa watched Isolde go with her chin high and her fists clenched; watched Quentin pause at the edge and murmur something that might have been a prayer or an oath; watched Bram crack his knuckles, spit ceremonially into the blackness, and stride in like a man entering a tavern he intended to drink dry.

Then she was alone, and the dark came for her.

It wasn't falling. It was *being poured*—her sense of direction dissolved, her body felt distant, and then the darkness arranged itself into a place she knew.

Greywater harbor. Rain. Nineteen years old, standing on a dock that smelled of tar and herring, watching a customs sloop board a smuggler's boat. Watching, through the rain, a girl her own age dragged to the rail—dark hair, a scar through one eyebrow, laughing even then, even being captured, some joke on her lips that Sefa would never hear.

Sefa Dorrin. The real one. Dead three days later of fever in a customs cell, according to the records Sefa had eventually paid a clerk to lose.

*I could have warned her.* The thought arrived in the dark's own voice, gentle and terrible. *You were on that dock. You saw the sloop coming. You could have shouted, could have run the flag up, could have done anything—and instead you calculated. You watched her ship die and thought: there are papers on that boat. Papers with a clean name. You let her be taken because her ruin was your opportunity.*

"That's not—" Sefa's voice cracked in the blackness. "I didn't know she'd die. Nobody knew she'd—"

*You didn't check.* The dark was patient. *You could have checked. For twenty years you could have found her grave, paid for a stone, written her mother one true letter. You chose the kind lie over the true one, every year, six years running, and called it mercy.*

Sefa stood in the rain that wasn't rain and let it hit her. Because it was true. All of it, true, and the worst memories are never the ones that lie to you—they're the ones that don't. She had built a life on Sefa Dorrin's name and told herself the story gently: that she'd inherited it, that fate handed it over, that the dead girl would have laughed and shared a drink and maybe, given the chance, given her blessing.

Maybe she would have. Sefa would never know, because she had never once, in twenty years, dared to imagine asking.

"All right," she said finally, to the dark, to the rain, to the girl on the dock. "All right. You're right. I owe you a grave, and a stone, and the truth. I'll pay it. First free town, first honest priest, I swear it on the only name I actually own—which is the one I'm making, right now, out of what's left."

The rain stopped.

The dark thinned like fog at sunrise, and Sefa walked out of it onto warm stone, into lamplight—or the shrine's version of it, that milk-moon glow—and found the others.

All of them were there. That was the first thing she counted, the captain's reflex: four bodies, four chests moving. Bram sat against the wall with his head in his huge hands. Quentin stood rigid, staring at nothing, his brand-arm bare—he'd pushed the sleeve up, she realized, deliberately, facing the old burn. Isolde knelt with her forehead nearly touching the floor, and her eyes, when she lifted them, were red-rimmed and absolutely steady.

Lena sat apart, hugging their knees, and when Sefa crossed to them, they looked up with an expression she'd never seen on that careful face: stripped. Unguarded. A person with all their shutters open.

"What did it show you?" Sefa asked quietly.

Lena opened their mouth. Closed it. Then, in a voice like paper tearing: "My parents. The day they were sold. Except in it, I *followed* them. I hid in the debt-wagon and I watched where they were taken, and I could have—there was a moment, a whole minute, when I could have run to them, and I didn't, because I was afraid Corvane's men would catch me too." Their hands tightened on their shins. "But that never happened. I never followed. I never knew. The dark *made* it—showed me the worst memory I could have, the one I'll never get to have or undo. It showed me the choice I never got to make, and made me watch myself make it wrong."

Sefa lowered herself to the stone beside them. Around them, the others drifted close without seeming to decide to—Bram lifting his head, Quentin turning, Isolde rising. None of them spoke. None of them needed to. Twenty minutes ago the dark had flayed each of them individually, alone, in the place where no one could help—and now they sat in a loose circle on the floor of a nine-hundred-year-old shrine, flayed and raw and *together*, and Sefa understood suddenly that this had been the trial all along. Not the doors. Not the bridge. Not even the dark.

The trial was whether, after seeing each other's worst, they would still sit in a circle.

They sat in a circle.

At the far end of the chamber, stone ground against stone. A final doorway swung inward, spilling out air that smelled of snowmelt and cedar and something older underneath—incense, maybe, or memory itself. Beyond it, Sefa could just make out the glint of a vast hall, pillars receding into gloom, and at its center a raised dais holding a shape she couldn't resolve: an altar, or a throne, or a book the size of a table.

And standing before it, translucent as heat-shimmer, a figure in a priest's rotted robes turned toward them with eyes like banked coals.

"You are slower than the last ones," said Vesh, last priest of the old gods. "But you are more. Come in. We have very little time—and there is something you must hear about the price, before your courage decides to volunteer what your sense should refuse."
