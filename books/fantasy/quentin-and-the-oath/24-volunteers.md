## 24. Volunteers

The shrine's antechamber held its silence like a held breath. Torches of pale stone-light burned without smoke along the walls, and the first oath—carved into the floor in letters older than any kingdom—glowed faintly beneath their feet, as if the mountain itself were listening.

Vesh had finished speaking. The price was named. A complete life, freely given. Every memory poured out through the vessel who could hear the dead, until the crown drank itself full and cracked.

Lena stood very still in the center of the chamber, hands folded, the way they always stood when the debt-masters read numbers aloud that decided the shape of their life.

It was Quentin who broke first.

* * *

He waited until the others had drifted apart—to the edges of the chamber, to the narrow stair, anywhere a man could pretend he needed to be alone. Then he crossed the stone floor to where Lena sat on a fallen pillar, sharpening a knife they didn't need, because their hands wanted something to do.

"I've been thinking," Quentin said.

"That's dangerous."

"Lena." He crouched so their eyes were level. The exile-brand on his forearm caught the stone-light, the old burn shaped like a broken circle. "I failed one king already. I stood in a throne room and watched him change and I said nothing loud enough, long enough. My whole life since has been the interest paid on that debt." He turned his arm over, showing the brand. "This is what a broken oath looks like. But an oath can be re-sworn differently—you told me that yourself. So here it is. Let me pour myself out instead. A keeper's life. Twenty years of oaths kept and broken, every face I've guarded, every failure. It's a full life. It's *mine*. And I'm offering it freely, which Vesh said matters more than anything."

Lena set down the knife. For a moment they only looked at him, and Quentin saw something in their face that made his chest ache—the same look they'd worn in the scriptorium, he imagined, copying prayers for masters who never once said thank you.

"Vesh already refused you," Lena said quietly. "Before you even asked me. Didn't they?"

Quentin's jaw tightened. "They said the rite needs a hearer. Someone who can carry memory out through the first oath. Someone the dead will follow."

"And how many people do you know," Lena said, "who hear the dead?"

"You could teach someone. There must be—"

"There aren't." Their voice stayed gentle, which was worse than anger. "Quentin. I have spent seven years being told what my gift was worth to other people. Do not make this the last thing it's worth to you too."

He knelt there a long moment, the brand burning cold against his skin, and then he did the only thing left to him. He reached out and took Lena's hand, rough palm over ink-stained fingers.

"Then hear my counter-oath," he said. "Whatever happens in that throne room—I will not let them forget you. Not one face. Not one name. I'll spend the rest of my life telling your story if I have to carve it into every wall between here and the sea."

Lena's breath caught. They squeezed his hand once, hard, and said nothing at all, because some promises are too large for thanks.

* * *

Sefa found her chance at dusk, when the shrine's light dimmed to a deep amber and the crew gathered around Bram's cookfire in the outer court. She waited until Lena went to refill the water skins at the spring that sang somewhere behind the shrine wall, and she followed, casual as a woman stretching her legs.

The spring pooled in a basin of black stone. Lena knelt at its edge, and Sefa leaned against the archway with her arms crossed, watching the water move.

"You know," she said, "in twenty years of running cargo, I've learned one rule that's never failed me. Never let the most valuable thing in your hold take the risk. You guard it. You put it in the deepest part of the ship, behind the false bulkhead, under the false floor. You let everything else be lost first."

Lena glanced up, water dripping from their fingers. "Is that what I am? Cargo?"

"Don't be stupid. You know exactly what you are." Sefa pushed off the wall and came to crouch beside them, close enough that the spring's mist beaded on both their faces. "You're the reason any of us are still alive. The switchback—that was you. The warden's net—that was you reading Hale true. Half my crew would be dead or forgotten twice over if you hadn't heard what nobody else could hear." She flicked water at them, almost playful, almost not. "So here's my offer, scribe. I've got a smuggler's memory. Twenty years of passes and codes and faces and debts honored. Every route through Thornfell, every name on every ledger I ever burned. That's a rich life. That's a *full* hold. Pour me out instead."

"Sefa—"

"The rite wants willing. I'm willing. Ask your ghost."

"Vesh says—" Lena stopped. Started again. "The memories have to pass *through* the hearer. Through me. Into the crown. Whoever gives them doesn't touch the crown directly. I'm the channel. I'm the only channel."

For the first time since Lena had known her, Sefa had nothing quick to say. The mist settled. Somewhere above, a nightbird called across the mountain.

"So it has to be you," she said finally. Flat. Like reading a bill of lading for goods she couldn't refuse to carry.

"It has to be me."

Sefa looked at the water a long time. When she spoke again, her voice had gone low and rough, stripped of all its wry armor. "I found you half-frozen on a mountainside and I argued about it. Bram voted last and his yes settled it, and I have thanked every god I don't believe in every day since that we didn't leave you there." She stood, brushing off her knees, and offered Lena a hand up. "So understand me, little scribe. If you pour yourself out tomorrow, I am not going to let this crew become a story about grief. We're going to tell you who you are until you're sick of hearing it. Every road. Every bad joke. Every time Bram salted the stew wrong. You're going to wake up drowning in us."

Lena took her hand and rose. Their eyes were bright and wet in the spring-light.

"That," they said, "is the kindest threat anyone's ever made me."

* * *

Bram didn't argue. Arguing wasn't in him. He cooked.

That night he made the stew—the good one, with the smoked pork and the dark beans and the three kinds of pepper he hoarded like treasure—and he filled a bowl and carried it himself to where Lena sat mending a strap by the fire, and he set it in their hands and sat down beside them with a groan that meant he intended to stay a while.

"Eat," he said.

"Bram, I—"

"Eat first. Then listen."

Lena ate. The stew was perfect, and that was somehow the worst part, because they knew why he'd made it.

When the bowl was empty, Bram took it back, set it down in the dirt, and folded his enormous hands. Firelight moved over a face like a cliffside—weathered, seamed, patient.

"Hollowbrook," he said. "My village. You walked through it with us. You saw what the crown leaves behind. My mother looked at me and saw a stranger, and do you know what the worst of it was?" His voice didn't break; it just got quieter, which from Bram was louder than breaking. "She was polite to me. Offered me bread. Treated me like a traveler, because that's what I was to her—a kind man passing through, nothing more. Forty years of her, gone, and she was *polite*."

"Bram—"

"I know what Vesh said. I know about the hearer. I'm not asking you to break the rite." He turned and looked at Lena straight on, and his eyes were wet and unashamed. "I'm asking you to know what you'd be taking from us. Because I grew up learning that the worst thing you can lose isn't your life. It's the people who remember your life. My mother's still breathing in Hollowbrook, and she's already gone. Don't make us stand around a fire someday trying to remember why we loved you. I couldn't bear it twice."

Lena set down the bowl. They reached over and laid their hand on Bram's forearm, on the old burn scar from a galley stove he'd never explained.

"Then remember harder," they whispered. "Remember enough for both of us."

Bram made a sound low in his chest, half laugh, half grief, and pulled them into a hug that smelled of woodsmoke and pepper and home, and held on until the fire burned low.

* * *

Isolde came last, and she came as a princess, which meant she came with terms.

She found Lena at dawn in the shrine's eastern gallery, where the first morning light fell through a crack in the mountain roof and struck the carved oath on the floor, making the ancient letters blaze gold. Lena stood at the edge of the light like a person warming themselves at a fire that might be taken away.

"My father taught me to fly a kite," Isolde said, without preamble. "On the palace roof, before the crown changed him. I was seven. The string cut my palms and I didn't care, because he was laughing—really laughing, not the court laugh. I have carried that memory through everything. Through the years of watching him eat our names. Through hiring smugglers and betraying my own guards." She stepped into the light beside Lena, and the gold caught them both. "It is the truest thing I own. So I am prepared to bargain with you, scribe, as one keeper of precious things to another."

"There's nothing to bargain with. The rite is the rite."

"Then hear my terms anyway." Isolde's chin lifted—the old reflex, the throne-room spine—but her voice softened. "If you give yourself to that crown, I will rebuild what it breaks. The contracts will become covenants. The ledgers will burn. No child in my kingdom will ever again be sold to cover a parent's debt, and no scribe will ever copy prayers into margins because no one would listen to them aloud." She paused. "And when you wake—if you wake—I will personally see that you want for nothing, though I suspect you'll want for very little, because these people"—she gestured back toward the outer court, toward the smell of Bram's breakfast and the sound of Sefa arguing with the wind—"these impossible people will have already given you everything."

Lena stared at her. "You've been planning this speech."

"Since the cave," Isolde admitted. "Since you told us about your parents, and I realized you have spent your entire life paying for other people's bargains." She extended her hand, formal as a treaty. "So. Do we have terms?"

Lena looked at the offered hand. Then, slowly, they shook their head and stepped forward and embraced her instead—awkward, unpracticed, real. Isolde stiffened, then melted, then held on.

"No terms," Lena murmured against her shoulder. "Just do it. All of it. Promise me you'll do all of it."

"On my father's name," Isolde said. "His *true* name. The one I'm going to give back to him."

* * *

They gathered at midday in the antechamber, all five of them plus a ghost, and Vesh's presence pressed against the walls like weather.

It was Sefa who said it aloud, arms crossed, staring at the glowing oath on the floor. "So let's say it plain, since we're all thinking it. Each of us offered. Each of us refused. The rite needs a hearer, and there's one hearer, and she's standing right there pretending to study the floor."

"I'm not pretending," Lena said.

"You absolutely are."

Quentin stepped up beside them. Bram loomed on their other side. Isolde took their hand. And Lena stood in the middle of it, surrounded, cornered by love, and felt something they hadn't felt since childhood—not the cage, not the ledger, but the terrifying weight of being *wanted*.

"We go tomorrow," they said. "At first light. Down through the god-tunnels, into the palace dark." They looked around at each face in turn, memorizing—no, they corrected themselves, *re*-memorizing, the way you press flowers you already know will fade. "And whatever happens in that throne room, I need you all to swear me one thing. Not that you'll stop me. Not that you'll save me. Just this: when it's done, when the crown cracks—someone tells me the story. All of it. Even the ugly parts. Especially Sefa's ugly parts."

"Oh, there are *so* many," Sefa said, and her voice cracked on the joke.

It was Vesh who ended it—or rather, it was the mountain. Deep beneath them, far to the south, a tremor ran through the stone floor, faint but unmistakable, and the carved oath flickered like a candle in wind.

Lena's head snapped up. The voice in their blood went taut as wire.

"What is it?" Quentin demanded. "What's wrong?"

Lena pressed a hand flat against the glowing letters, listening with more than their ears. When they spoke, their voice had gone strange and distant, doubled with another's.

"The crown knows we're coming. It's begun feeding early—it's pulling memory from the capital districts right now, whole streets at once, faster than it's ever fed." Their eyes widened. "And there are soldiers on the northern pass. A column of them. Wardens, flying the crown's colors, marching for the shrine."

"To stop us?" Isolde breathed.

"No." Lena lifted their head, and wonder warred with fear in their face. "Someone's holding the pass *against* them. Someone's bought us time." They blinked, focusing, coming back to themselves. "It's Hale. Warden-captain Hale and what's left of his honor. He's turned his troops around, and the crown's reinforcements are coming, and he means to stand in the road."

Silence fell over the antechamber, five people and a ghost, each understanding what it cost a man to turn his sword against the crown he'd served—what it cost a father who could no longer remember his son's face.

Quentin was the first to move, reaching for his sword-belt, the exile-brand flaring bright on his arm.

"Then we don't waste the time he's bleeding to buy us," he said. "Get your gear. We march for the capital tonight."
