## 25. Hale's Choice

The pass was called the Shepherd's Tooth, and it had killed better soldiers than Warden-Captain Dain Hale intended to bury in it.

He stood at its mouth as the sun came up behind him, throwing his shadow and the shadows of his sixty warden-soldiers long across the snow, and he tried to remember why he had come.

No. That wasn't true. He remembered why he had come. He had the orders sewn into his sleeve like a man who no longer trusted his own skull to keep them: *Intercept the fugitives at the Shepherd's Tooth. The scribe is to be taken alive. The others may be dealt with as the crown sees fit.* He could recite the words in his sleep. He had recited them in his sleep, waking with his mouth moving in the dark of the tent while his men pretended not to hear.

What he could not remember — what he had gone looking for at three o'clock that morning, digging through his kit by candlelight with hands that would not stop shaking — was his son's face.

Tam. His son was Tam. He knew that the way a man knows the answer to a sum he can no longer work: because it was written down. Because Tam himself had written it, years ago, in a letter that lived folded inside Hale's breastplate, soft as cloth now from handling. *Father — I got your parcel. Mother says hello. Come home for Founding Day if they'll let you. — Tam.* Eleven words in a boy's careful hand. Not one word about what he looked like.

Hale had stood in the candlelight turning the letter over as though the face might have soaked through from the other side. He knew Tam had been eight when he'd last seen him. Eight, and missing a front tooth, and afraid of geese — those facts remained, fixed and strange, like items on an inventory of someone else's house. But the face above them, the face that should have anchored all of it, had gone the way of frost off a windowpane. He could see the outline of where it had been. He could not see it.

And he knew exactly when it had left him, too. That was the worst of it. He knew the day, the hour, almost the minute: the night in the free-town salt market when he had leaned close over the table of a frightened scribe and read, in the margins of a smuggled codex, a name written in ink that seemed to move. He had felt something *lift* out of him then. A cold little tug behind the eyes, delicate as a pickpocket. At the time he had told himself it was exhaustion.

He had spent eleven days since telling himself many things.

* * *

"Captain." Sergeant Vess crunched up beside him, breath steaming, and followed his gaze up the switchbacks where the road climbed white and narrow toward the notch of sky. "Scouts say they camped last night below the third turn. They'll be in the Teeth by midmorning. Six of them, plus the princess."

"Plus whatever the crown sent after them," Hale said.

Vess said nothing, which was its own confirmation. They both had seen the second column on the far ridge yesterday evening — crown regulars, two hundred strong at least, with the black-and-gold banners of the King's Own flying at their head. Not wardens. Wardens were tax-men and tracker-men; wardens dragged debtors out of attics and guarded granaries. What had come over the eastern passes wearing the king's colors moved like nothing Hale had drilled with in twenty years of service. It moved *quietly*. Two hundred men in mail, and the snow made no sound under their boots.

"They'll want us to soften the fugitives first," Vess said finally. "Soften them, hold them, hand them over. Then the King's Own comes through our line like we're a gate left open."

"I know what they want."

"And what are we going to do, Captain?"

It was a fair question, asked in the flat voice of a soldier who already suspects the answer and wants to hear whether his officer has lost his mind or merely his nerve. Hale did not answer immediately. Below them the valley fell away in white terraces, the river under its ice a dull silver seam, and somewhere down that valley — three days' ride south and east, past two rivers and a burned tollhouse — there was a farmhouse where a woman named Maren had once been married to him, and a boy who was fourteen now, fourteen as of the spring, who might be tall by now, who had probably grown out of the face his father could no longer find.

Two hundred crown regulars were coming to take a scribe alive and deal with the rest as the crown saw fit. And Hale had begun, in the last eleven days, to assemble the pieces of what that meant, the way a drunk assembles the memory of the night before.

The villages going blank along the coronation road — he'd ridden through one in autumn where the innkeeper's wife had smiled at her own children like strangers at a fair. He'd written it off as fever. The archives burning in Thornfell city, ordered from the palace itself, the smoke standing over the rooftops for two days like a flag. The old priest-shrines desecrated, the waymarkers pulled up. The king who no longer appeared in public except at dusk, veiled, flanked by men who never spoke. The wardens' own rolls, rewritten this winter in a new clerk's hand, debts transferred, contracts bought up — including, he now understood with a sickness that arrived fresh every morning, the contracts of anyone who had ever been touched by the old gods. Anyone who had ever heard something in the quiet.

Including, very possibly, a warden-captain who had heard a name whisper up out of a margin and felt something small and precious lifted out of him in payment.

They weren't hunting the fugitives. Or rather — they were, but that was a errand, not the war. The war was against remembering itself.

"Captain?" Vess prompted.

Hale took out the letter. He did it without deciding to, the way a tongue finds a broken tooth. He unfolded it there in the wind, and Vess, decent man, looked away and studied the switchbacks as though the fate of the kingdom hung on counting them.

"Tam," Hale read aloud, though he didn't need to; the words were carved into him deeper than anything the crown could dig out. "*Come home for Founding Day if they'll let you.*" He folded it away. "I've forgotten my son's face, Sergeant."

Vess went very still.

"Not dead. Not dulled. Gone — scooped out, clean as marrow from a bone, and I know the hour it happened and I know who holds it now, because I've seen what the crown pays for memories and I've seen what it does with them. There is a thing on the throne eating this country one life at a time, and it ate a piece of me in a salt market, and it will eat the rest unless somebody stops it. And the somebody, Vess, appears to be six smugglers, a disgraced oath-keeper, and a scribe who talks to the dead." He laughed — a short, ugly sound that the wind took. "Gods forgive me. That's the army we've got."

"And the one we don't?"

"The King's Own reaches this pass by tomorrow's dusk." He turned to face his sergeant fully. "Sixty of us against two hundred won't win anything. But a pass doesn't need to be won. It needs to be *held*, and held passes only need to cost more than they're worth. If we stand at the narrows and make them pay for every yard — buy them a day, two days — the fugitives reach the shrine country ahead of the hunt, and the King's Own arrives late and angry and bleeding."

"You're asking the company to die for a smuggler's cargo."

"I'm asking them to choose," Hale said. "That's more than the crown ever asked any of us. More than it ever let us do."

* * *

He gathered them at the narrows an hour later, where the rock walls pinched the road to four men abreast and the old shepherd cairns stood like a congregation. Sixty faces, chapped red with cold, watching him. He had led some of them for a decade. He had buried nine. He had carried Private Osk's body down from the Greymire himself, and sung at Corporal Anthe's wedding, and taught half the young ones to gamble honestly and the other half to cheat well.

"Some of you have noticed I've been odd lately," he began, and a ripple went through them — not laughter, but near kin to it. "Odder. Some of you have heard me say your names wrong. Some of you watched me stand in the supply tent this morning unable to tell Vess which of the mules is Sorrel, and Sorrel has been our lead mule for six years." Silence now, total, the kind soldiers give a man when they understand he is saying true things. "Here is what I can tell you. Something is feeding on this kingdom. It wears the king's crown and it eats what people are made of — their dead, their loves, their reasons. It took something of mine eleven days ago, and I would give my right arm to have it back, and I cannot have it back, and neither can any of you get back whatever it has already quietly taken from you. Think hard. You'll find the holes."

He let them think. He watched the horror move through the ranks like weather, man after man going pale as the search came up empty — Rellik suddenly mouthing a name he couldn't finish; young Piet staring at his own hands as though they belonged to a stranger; Vess closing his eyes and opening them again with everything in them rearranged.

"So here is the choice, and I mean the word. The King's Own is coming up this road behind us with orders to take a scribe alive and deal with everyone else as the crown sees fit. We were sent to soften the ground for them. We can do that. We can stand aside, or even help, and most of us will probably live out the year, and we will go on losing pieces of ourselves at markets and musters until there is nothing left that answers to our names. Or we can hold this pass. Against them. Buy the fugitives their head start. Make the crown pay through the nose for the privilege of starving us." He drew a slow breath. "I'm not ordering it. An order would bind you to my ruin, and I won't spend you like coin. Those who want to fall back to Thornfell and report me for treason — go now, with my blessing and my thanks for every mile. Those who stay—"

Nobody moved.

That was the thing he would carry, whatever else was taken from him, however much they hollowed him out: nobody moved. Sixty warden-soldiers stood in the freezing narrows of the Shepherd's Tooth, and not one boot shifted, and after a moment Sergeant Vess stepped forward and planted his spear in the snowdrift like a fencepost and said, "Well. Somebody ought to teach the King's Own how a proper ambush is set," and the whole line broke — not apart, but open, into the grim cheer of men who have decided how they want to be spent.

Hale had to turn away and pretend to study the ridge. His eyes were doing something he didn't have time for.

* * *

They worked through the morning, and it was the best morning's soldiering Hale had done in years, because for the first time since the salt market he was building instead of eroding. They rolled the cairns into choke-points. They cut the snow bridges that wouldn't bear armored weight and disguised the cuts. Vess, who had grown up goat-herding these same rocks before the wardens took him, found the goat-tracks that let thirty men vanish upward into the scree and leave the road apparently empty. By noon they had turned the narrows into a throat with teeth on both sides, and Hale stood in the middle of it feeling the strangest thing: purpose, arriving late but arriving whole, like a letter forwarded through a dozen hands.

The fugitives came through at midafternoon.

He saw them first through Vess's spyglass: six figures and a seventh wrapped in a plain traveler's cloak that fooled nobody, picking their way up the third switchback with the wary spacing of people used to being hunted. The big cook walked rear guard with a cleaver on his hip like a sidearm. The smuggler woman ranged ahead like she owned the mountain. And between them, small and hooded, moving with a scribe's bad knees and a scribe's stubbornness, the one the whole kingdom was being bent around.

Hale lowered the glass. His heart was going hard. This was the moment his orders had pointed at for eleven days — *take the scribe alive* — and here was the scribe, close enough to seize, and every reason he had for wanting to obey that order lay folded against his chest in a boy's handwriting, and none of them survived the reading.

He stepped out onto the road where they could see him. Behind and above, sixty spears stayed hidden; he'd given that instruction too, and meant to keep it until the last possible moment, because trust was a currency he had no right to still be spending and intended to spend carefully anyway.

The smuggler woman's knife was out before he'd finished raising his empty hands. "Wardens," she said to her people, without taking her eyes off him. "Everyone act surprised."

"Warden-Captain Dain Hale," he said. "Thornfell Third Company. I was sent to intercept you." He saw the cook shift his grip, saw the oath-keeper — he recognized Quentin Ashvane from the exile rolls, the brand visible even at distance — place himself between the soldiers' sightline and the princess without appearing to. Good instincts, the disgraced man. "I am not going to do it. The King's Own is coming up this road behind me — two hundred regulars, by tomorrow's dusk, maybe sooner. My company means to hold the narrows against them. It will buy you a day, perhaps two. After that, this pass belongs to whoever's still breathing on it, and I can't promise it will be us."

Silence, except the wind.

"Why?" It was the scribe who spoke — Lena Nakamura, voice low and roughened by cold, hood thrown back enough to show a face younger than the trouble attached to it. "You hunted us. In the salt market you—" The scribe stopped. Hale watched understanding arrive, watched it land like a blow. "You're one of the holed ones. Something's missing from you."

"My son's face," Hale said simply. "Eleven days ago. I've decided I'd rather lose everything else than let them keep the rest of him." He looked at the small fierce circle of them — the smuggler with her knife still bare, the oath-keeper with his ruined honor, the princess pretending so hard to be a servant, the cook, the others, and the scribe at the center like a lit wick. "I don't know what you're carrying to that shrine. I know it's the only weapon anybody's forged in twenty years that the thing on the throne is actually afraid of. So. Pass is yours, such as it is. Water's cached at the fourth cairn, west side — we won't need it where we're standing. And if any of you live to write about this—"

He stopped. Swallowed something.

"—write down your people's faces," he finished. "All of them. Every angle. Names underneath. Trust nothing to memory. Memory is where it feeds."

The smuggler woman put her knife away, which from her, he suspected, was roughly a knighthood. "Warden-Captain," she said, "when this is done — if it's done — come find us. We know people who owe us favors, and some of them are the kind that get things *back*."

"There's nothing gets a face back," Hale said.

"Maybe not," said the scribe quietly. "But I've heard the dead talking in the margins, captain, and they say nothing that was truly loved stays gone forever. It just waits for someone to say its name right."

Then they were moving, up and around the switchbacks, smaller and smaller against the white, and Hale stood in the road watching them go with his hand flat over his breastplate and the letter inside it, and he thought: *hold, Tam. Hold the door. Your father is holding a door too.*

* * *

They came at dusk the next day, earlier than promised — the King's Own, two hundred strong, black and gold against the snow, and their column made no more sound on the ice than it had on the far ridge. Their commander rode forward alone under a banner Hale had never seen before: not the king's stag, but a closed eye worked in gold thread. He reined in at the mouth of the narrows and looked up at the warden line with an expression of mild, administrative disappointment, like a man arriving to collect a debt already itemized.

"Warden-Captain Hale. Stand your company aside. The crown requires passage."

"The crown," Hale called down from the rocks, "requires a great many things it hasn't paid for."

A pause. Somewhere behind the commander, two hundred men shifted, and the snow said nothing under them, and Hale felt the hair rise on his neck at the sheer wrongness of it.

"Your orders were to detain the fugitives."

"My orders were written by a thing that eats people." He set his spear-butt against stone. "I've decided to stop taking its correspondence. Vess!"

"Aye, Captain!"

"Hold the pass."

Sixty spears came up out of the snowdrifts on both walls at once, and the narrows rang with the sound, and far to the north — beyond two ridges and a frozen river and a shrine older than any kingdom — a scribe with an emptied-out future was climbing toward the first oath with a day's grace bought in iron and blood.

Hale drew his sword and waited for the dark to come up the road.
