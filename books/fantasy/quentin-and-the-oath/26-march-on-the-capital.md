## 26. March on the Capital

The road down from the high passes was the color of old bone, and Quentin could not stop counting the things that were missing from it.

He had learned the trick of it years ago, in his first season as an oath-keeper: when you ride through a country, you read it the way a scribe reads a page. The hedgerows tell you who owns the fields. The wayfarers' shrines tell you what the people fear. The state of the bridges tells you whether the crown is awake or asleep. Thornfell had always been a well-kept book — not a kind one, but an honest one, its margins full of small devotions, its sentences running straight from mountain to sea.

Now the book was being eaten from the inside.

They passed the first abandoned shrine two hours after dawn. It was a little thing, knee-high, built into the shoulder of the road the way such shrines had been built for six hundred years — a hollow stone with a bowl for oil and a niche for a carved name. Every traveler was supposed to touch the niche and speak the name of the god within. Quentin knew the rite; every child in Thornfell knew the rite, even the ones who no longer believed in anything but bread.

The niche was empty. Not broken. Empty — the carving smoothed away as if the stone had never held a face at all, worn to blankness by no weather he could name. The oil bowl still held rainwater and a dead moth.

"Vesh says that one was called Omma-of-the-Turnings," Lena said quietly beside him. They rode close, their grey mare matching his roan stride for stride; they had grown good at riding without seeming to think about it, which was its own kind of miracle given where they'd started. "Guardian of crossroads and second chances. The villagers fed her on the first day of autumn."

"And now?"

"Now there's a smooth place in a rock." Lena's hands tightened on the reins. In the week since the shrine gate, since the trials, since Vesh had named the price in words none of them could unsay, they had developed a habit of speaking about the crown-spirit's feeding the way other people discussed weather — flatly, precisely, because the alternative was screaming. "The crown doesn't kill the gods, Quentin. It eats the memory of them. First out of the stones, then out of the books, then out of the people. What's left is—" they gestured at the blank niche— "*that*. A hole shaped like forgetting."

Behind them, the rest of the company strung out along the switchback in the loose formation Sefa preferred — never bunched, never strung too far apart, the wagons and riders spaced like beads on a cord. Bram drove the cook-wagon with his usual scowl, though Quentin had noticed the cook kept glancing north, back toward the hollow village they had passed three days ago, back toward the mother who had looked at her own son with polite, empty courtesy and offered him bread because he seemed hungry. Isolde rode at the rear in her third disguise of the journey, this one a merchant widow's greys, the fragment-oath sewn flat against her ribs where no search would find it. And Sefa ranged ahead, a dark speck against the bright morning, reading the road the way only she could.

Quentin read it too, and did not like the sentence it made.

* * *

They came down into the river valley at midday, and that was where they found the first village that had forgotten itself.

It wasn't ruined. That was the horror of it. The roofs were whole, the wells were clean, washing hung on lines in gardens gone slightly wild. Chickens scratched in the lane. Smoke rose from chimneys. Everything was exactly where a living village should be — except that when Sefa walked into the square with her hands open and her voice easy, calling the greeting every smuggler used to announce themselves as traders and not raiders, the people who gathered simply stood there.

There were perhaps thirty of them. Farmers, mostly, and a smith, and a woman with flour to her elbows who might have been a baker or might have been anyone at all. They weren't hostile. They weren't frightened, even, and that was worse. They looked at the armed strangers in their square with mild, patient attention, the way you look at weather.

"Good day," Sefa tried again. "We're looking to trade. Salt, iron, cloth."

A man near the front — broad, sunburnt, wearing a carpenter's leather apron — considered this for a long moment. "Trade," he said, as if tasting the word. "Yes. That's a thing we do. Did." He frowned faintly, like a man reaching for a tool that wasn't on its peg. "I don't recall what we take for it."

"You take coin," said Isolde, stepping forward, and Quentin heard the princess under the widow's greys, heard the voice trained in courts to carry. "Silver and copper. You've done it your whole life."

"Have I?" The carpenter smiled apologetically. It was not an unkind smile. It was the smile of someone who has decided the stranger means no harm and cannot quite remember why they would have needed to decide that. "You'll forgive me. I seem to have lost the thread of things. We all have, a bit." He gestured vaguely at his neighbors, and the neighbors nodded, mild as cattle. "It comes and goes. Mostly goes."

Quentin dismounted slowly. He had seen battlefields. He had seen plague-towns, fire-seasons, the aftermath of the border war where the dead lay in rows and the crows grew fat enough to barely flap. None of it had prepared him for a healthy village with nothing wrong except that it was empty behind the eyes — not emptied by violence, not emptied by grief, but *unwritten*, like pages scraped clean for reuse.

"What's this village called?" he asked.

Silence. Thirty faces turned toward him, and in thirty pairs of eyes there was nothing but honest effort, the visible strain of people searching for something that should have lived right at the front of them.

"The sign," said the baker woman at last, brightly, grasping at it. "There's a sign. On the inn." Her face fell. "I can't read it anymore. None of us can. The letters just—" she made a small, helpless motion with her floured hands, "—slide off."

Lena made a sound beside him, very small. Quentin glanced over and saw them standing rigid, both hands pressed flat against their own sternum, as if holding something in — or something up. Their lips were moving without sound. Talking to Vesh, Quentin realized. Listening and answering both.

"Lena," he said quietly. "What is it?"

"They're not mad," Lena whispered. "That's what everyone will say, when the reports go south. That it's a sickness of the mind, that these poor souls are simple, that it happens sometimes in isolated places." Their voice shook with a controlled fury Quentin had never heard from them before; seven years of copying prayers had taught them to keep fury folded small. "But Vesh says the pattern is exact. This happened before. Six hundred years ago, when the crown was forged. Whole valleys going quiet. And the crown learns faster each time it feeds. It's not eating this village randomly — it's practicing. Refining. Learning how to take a community's memory of itself without killing the body, because bodies are useful. Bodies pay taxes. Bodies hold walls."

"Then where's it feeding from?" Sefa asked, all business, though her knuckles were white on her sword-hilt. "The capital's four days' ride. Can it reach this far?"

"It reached this far a week ago," Lena said. "This is old feeding. Look at the garden weeds. Look at the washing." They pointed at the lines strung between the houses, shirts and sheets drying in the wind. "Someone washed those yesterday. Someone who doesn't know why they do it anymore, only that hands remember the shape of the work after everything else is gone." Their voice cracked on the last word. "The hands remember. That's all that's left. The hands remember."

Bram came up from the wagon, and without a word began unloading food — hard cheese, dried apples, the dark bread he baked at dawn every morning without ever being asked to. He set it on the well-head in front of the villagers, roughly, refusing to look at them.

"Eat," he grunted. "Whatever else you've lost, you still got stomachs."

The baker woman looked at the food, and then at Bram, and something flickered across her face — not recognition, nothing so grand as that, but the ghost of recognition, a shape moving behind frosted glass. "You cook," she said wonderingly. "I can tell. You've got cook's hands."

Bram's jaw worked. "Aye," he managed. "I've got cook's hands."

They left the food and did not stay. There was nothing to stay for; there was no one left in the village to help. As they rode out, Quentin looked back once, and saw the villagers sharing the bread among themselves with careful fairness, with the deep ingrained manners of people who had forgotten everything except kindness — and even that, he understood, they hadn't really forgotten. Kindness lived deeper than memory. It lived in the hands, in the body, in whatever floor of a person the crown's teeth had not yet reached.

*Or maybe,* he thought grimly, *it lives in the places the crown doesn't bother eating, because kindness isn't power, and the crown only eats power.*

* * *

They camped that night in a smugglers' hollow Sefa knew — a fold of hills above the river road, screened by old thorn trees, with a cold spring and a fire-ring some long-dead outlaw had mortared so neatly it still held. While Bram cooked, Sefa spread her maps on a wagon tailboard and weighted the corners with stones, and the company gathered around the little flame of a shielded lantern.

"Four days to the capital," Sefa said, tracing the route. "Three if we push, five if we're careful. Here's the problem. Careful routes are all through country like today's village. Fast routes go through the warden stations." She tapped a cluster of ink-marks along the river road. "And here's the bigger problem. Hale."

"Hale let us go," Isolde said. "In the net, on the pass. He had us, and he took Lena's bargain instead."

"He had us and he *hesitated*," Sefa corrected. "Hesitation buys hours, not loyalty. And everything's changed since then." She looked up, and in the lantern light her face was stripped of its usual wry warmth, down to the hard practical thing beneath. "Word travels fast on the rivers, faster than horses. The capital's districts are waking up not knowing their names. Not all of them — pockets, so far. The Docks, they say, and Tanner's Row, and the whole of the Old Quarter where the palace doesn't bother looking. The wardens aren't quelling it. They can't. You can't arrest forgetting. So what does a warden-captain do with an army when the enemy can't be fought?"

"He looks for someone to blame," Quentin said.

"He looks for orders," Sefa agreed. "And the only order coming out of that palace is the king's voice saying all is well, the old gods are stirring and traitors are helping them, and any soul touched by the old ways is to be taken for questioning." She let that sit in the firelight. "Questioning by the crown, mind. By the thing that eats. Every prisoner it takes isn't a prisoner, it's a meal. It's using the wardens as *fishing nets*."

The silence around the fire had a texture to it, dense and awful. Quentin became aware of how loud the river was below them, how indifferent, running silver under the stars toward the capital as if nothing upstream of it had changed in six hundred years.

"So we don't go by the roads," Isolde said at last. Everyone turned to her. She sat with her arms around her knees, the firelight making her look younger than she was and older than she'd ever planned to be. "My father — *Aldric*, my father — used to walk me through the oldest parts of the palace when I was small. Before. And he told me once, the way you tell a child a secret, that the palace was built on top of something older. That the founders didn't clear the hill, they just built over it. There are tunnels underneath. God-tunnels, he called them. From when the hill was a shrine." She hesitated. "He laughed when he told me. He said the kings had been sleeping on top of the gods for nine generations and it was the best joke in Thornfell that none of them knew it."

"The god-tunnels," Lena breathed. "Vesh — Vesh knows those tunnels. Vesh *made* half of them." Their eyes had gone distant, listening inward to the voice bound in their blood. When they spoke again it was with the strange doubled cadence Quentin had learned to recognize, their own words braided with something older moving beneath. "The shrine hill and the palace hill were sisters. The first priests cut passages between them so the contracts could be renewed in secret if the kings ever turned. The kings never turned. But the passages are still there. Sealed, not destroyed. Stone remembers doors."

"Then that's our road," Sefa said. "Under the wards, under the gates, under the fishing nets. We walk into the palace the way the old priests would have — from below, from the part of the world the crown forgot it owned." A thin, fierce grin crossed her face. "Smuggler's route. Six hundred years early."

"There's a condition," Lena said quietly. All the doubled cadence had drained out of their voice, leaving only weariness, and beneath the weariness something Quentin recognized because he carried its twin in his own chest: dread, worn smooth by handling. "Everyone here knows the price already. I won't make speeches about it. But I want one thing understood before we're under the walls, where arguments are harder." They looked around the fire, meeting each face — Sefa's, Isolde's, Bram's, his own. "When we get there, when the moment comes, nobody volunteers. Nobody argues with me about it. The rite needs a hearer of the dead to pour the memories out, and I am the only hearer of the dead on this hill, and I have known since the shrine gate that this road ends with me kneeling in front of that crown. So let's spend our strength on the walking, not on the grieving. Grieve after. If there's anyone left to grieve."

"No," Bram said.

It was the first time Quentin had heard the cook use that voice — not the gruff complaint he wore like an apron, but something from far underneath, tectonic. Everyone turned.

"No," Bram said again, slower. "You don't get to pre-grieve us, girl. That's not how a crew works. I've sailed with captains who talked like that — decided their deaths in advance so nobody could argue them out of it. Every one of 'em died anyway, and we grieved twice: once on the road and once at the grave. You want to spare us? Then leave room for hope. Leave room for us to find a door you haven't seen. Vesh is six hundred years old and even Vesh doesn't know everything — you said so yourself, the night on the ice. So we walk. We fight. We get under those walls. And when the moment comes, if there's another way, we *take* it, and you don't get a vote, because you gave yours away too cheap." His voice roughened. "Nobody on this crew comes cheap. Not to me."

For a long moment Lena stared at him. Then something in their shoulders came down — not surrender, Quentin thought, but relief, the terrible relief of being argued with, of being worth arguing with. "All right," they whispered. "All right, Bram. We look for doors."

Sefa cleared her throat briskly, before the moment could become unbearable. "Doors. Tunnels. Three days' hard march. Watch rotations as normal — Quentin, you've first watch, and Bram, I want double rations tomorrow, we'll need the legs." She rolled up the map. "And one more thing. Tonight, while we still have quiet — everybody sleep touching something you mean to remember. A name, a face, a smell. Write it on the inside of your skull. Because come tomorrow we ride into a city where people are waking up strangers to themselves, and I'll be damned if any of us joins them."

Quentin took first watch, as assigned, and sat with his back against the mortar of the old outlaw's fire-ring while the company slept in the thorn-sheltered dark. Above him the stars wheeled, patient and ancient, and he found himself doing what oath-keepers do on watches: reciting. Not the formal oaths — those had burned him raw enough lately, and been re-sworn, and burned cooler each time, the exile-brand on his forearm fading month by month toward something almost gentle. No. He recited the small inventory. The things worth keeping.

*Isolde's laugh, when she forgets to be a princess.* *Sefa humming off-key at the oars.* *Bram's stew, the one with the river-fennel.* *Lena's face the moment the shrine gate opened — terrified and lit from inside, like a person watching the sun rise after swearing it never would.*

He recited them like scripture, because that was what they were. The crown ate memories to stay alive. Very well. He would build memories too stubborn to die — not locked away, not hidden, but *carried*, shared nightly around fires, sworn aloud, stitched into other people until no single hunger could swallow them. Let the crown eat a kingdom's silence. It would choke on this crew's noise.

Near dawn, Lena emerged from the blankets and sat beside him without a word, and they watched the light come up over the eastern hills together. Far off, past the river bends, past the last blue line of ridges, the capital lay under its morning haze — a smudge of smoke and towers that had ruled Quentin's entire life and broken most of it.

Even at this distance, he thought he could see it: a shimmer over the city, faint as heat-haze, wrong as a bruise. The crown, feeding. Growing bolder as it felt itself approached, or perhaps simply growing hungrier, the way starving things do.

"Districts waking up not knowing their names," Lena murmured, echoing Sefa. "Do you think it can feel us coming?"

"I think," Quentin said, standing and rolling the stiffness out of his shoulders, "that six hundred years ago it was a person who took power so it would never be hurt again. And I think there is nothing in this world more afraid than a thing like that, when it hears footsteps on the stairs it thought it had bricked over." He looked down at them, and found himself smiling — tired, grim, real. "Let it be afraid. We're bringing it company."

Below them, the river ran on toward the capital, silver and indifferent and unstoppable, and they broke camp and followed it down.
