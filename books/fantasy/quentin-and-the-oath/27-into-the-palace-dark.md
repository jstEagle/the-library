## 27. Into the Palace Dark

The tunnels had a smell Sefa never forgot, once she'd learned it: wet stone and old tallow and something underneath both, like the memory of incense that had burned out centuries ago. She stood at the mouth of the first one now—a crack in the hillside above the river gorge, half-hidden by a curtain of dead bramble—and breathed it in, and felt the years peel back.

"Smells like a grave," Bram said behind her. He said it without complaint, the way he said most things, shifting his pack of iron pans on his shoulder as though he might need to cook a meal in there.

"It smells like money," Sefa corrected. "Every route nobody else knows is money. This one's been sitting under their noses for three hundred years."

"Not quite." Isolde crouched beside her at the entrance, hood back despite the cold, her breath fogging in the grey light. In her gloved hands she held a scrap of vellum no bigger than a prayer card, covered in a child's careful handwriting. "Two hundred and forty. My grandfather's journals mention the god-tunnels. The old kings used them to visit the shrines beneath the palace without being seen—back when visiting the shrines was something a king needed to do quietly." Her mouth twisted. "Before it became something a king needed to do never."

"And you found this how?" Quentin asked. He stood a little apart from the rest of them, as he always did, his exile-brand wrapped even here where no one would see it. "Princesses don't read smugglers' maps."

"Princesses read everything they're not supposed to," Isolde said. "It's the only education we're given that's worth anything." She smoothed the vellum against her knee. "My father drew this when he was nine years old. There's a whole folio of them—maps of everywhere in the palace a boy could hide. The servants' stair, the roof over the great hall, the drains. And these." She tapped the little drawing. "He labeled it *the long dark*. He wrote underneath: *Mama says the gods used to live down here before we built the house on top of them.*"

Sefa looked at the child's map, at the confident ink lines running down through the palace foundations into the bedrock, and felt the hair rise on her arms. Not fear, exactly. Something older than fear. The particular shiver of standing on ground that remembered being something else.

"Well," she said, and pulled her scarf up over her nose. "Let's go trespass in a god's cellar."

* * *

The first hour was easy, which Sefa distrusted immediately.

Easy passages were how smugglers died. A corridor with good footing and dry walls meant either that nobody used it—which meant nobody would hear you if you screamed—or that somebody used it constantly, and you simply hadn't met them yet. She called the halt twice in the first mile just to listen, and both times the silence that came back was so complete she could hear her own heart working, and Lena's breathing, quick and shallow, two places behind her.

Lena hadn't spoken since they'd entered. Sefa kept glancing back at them—at the scribe's pale face catching the lamplight, at the way their fingers kept moving against their thigh, tracing letters on nothing. Writing in the air. They did it when the voices got loud, Sefa had learned. Grounding themselves in the shape of words.

"You hearing anything?" she asked quietly, the third time she paused.

Lena's eyes came up, unfocused, then focused. "Echoes. Old ones. Prayers, mostly—it's like walking through a room where someone read aloud for a thousand years and the walls soaked it up." They swallowed. "But there's something else. Underneath. Like... a pulse. Very slow. Very deep." Their gaze dropped to the floor, to the worn channel cut down the center of the passage, smooth as a gutter. "I think these tunnels were made to carry something. Water, or voice, or—" they hesitated— "*attention*. I think the prayers flowed downhill toward the shrine, and the answers flowed back up."

"To the palace," Quentin said grimly.

"To whoever sat on the throne," Lena said. "That's what frightens me. This isn't a secret way *into* the palace. It's the root system. The palace grew out of it, the way a mushroom grows out of mycelium. We're not sneaking into a building. We're climbing up inside the thing that feeds it."

Bram snorted. "Cheerful lot, you scholars."

"Walk," Sefa said, but her hand found her knife-hilt and stayed there.

They went on. The passage descended in long ramps, the stone underfoot changing character as they went—rough-hewn at first, then dressed, then carved, the walls suddenly blooming with relief-work that the lamplight turned into a procession of moving shadows. Figures with raised hands. Figures kneeling. Figures whose faces had been deliberately chiseled away, every one, so that a thousand years of worshippers had processed past a wall of anonymous devotion.

Isolde stopped before one panel and held her lamp close. The carved figure there was larger than the others, robed, crowned—but the crown was wrong. Too many points. Too many mouths, if you let your eye slide along the grooves the wrong way.

"That's not a king," Sefa said.

"No," Isolde said softly. "I don't think it is." She copied the image with a charcoal stub onto the edge of her father's map, quick and precise, and Sefa watched her do it and thought: *she's been preparing for this her whole life without knowing it. Nine years old, drawing maps of hiding places. A girl building escape routes out of a house that was already eating her family.*

"Keep moving," Sefa said again, gentler this time.

* * *

The trouble started at the third junction, where the child's map ended and the mountain began making its own decisions.

A collapse had taken the left passage sometime in the last century—centuries of rubble fanned across the floor, and above it, wedged in the broken ceiling, a slab of carved stone hanging by nothing Sefa wanted to think about. The right-hand passage was intact but narrow, a slot barely wide enough for Bram's shoulders, exhaling air that moved. Moving air meant an opening somewhere. Moving air also meant something might be using the tunnel as a throat.

"We go right," Sefa decided. "Single file. Nobody talks."

They went right. The slot narrowed further, then further, until Bram had to turn sideways and shuffle, his breath whistling, his pans scraping sparks off the stone. Sefa went last-but-one, with Lena ahead of her, and it was Lena who stopped first.

"Sefa." Their voice came out flat and strange. "There's someone in the wall."

"Don't say things like that in a slot I can't turn around in."

"No—I mean it. There's a hollow. Behind this stone. And there's—" Lena pressed their palm flat against the rock, and their face changed, went through several changes, grief and wonder fighting for the same ground. "There's a body. Old. Sealed in. Someone walled themselves in here alive, I think, or—no. No, they were *put* here. With offerings. There are bowls. There's writing all over the inside of the hollow, hundreds of names, and the last one is still warm, Sefa, the last name is still—"

"Lena." Sefa reached forward and gripped their shoulder. "Look at me."

Lena looked. Their eyes were wet and wide and very far away.

"Can you walk past it?"

A long moment. Then Lena nodded, once, and put their hand back on the guide-rope, and walked. But as they squeezed past the sealed hollow, they spoke—not loudly, not to any of them, but to the stone itself, in a whisper Sefa caught only because the slot forced them shoulder to shoulder:

"I'll carry it out. Whatever name you were waiting for someone to say. I'll say it outside."

And the air in the tunnel warmed, just slightly, just for a step or two, like a door opening somewhere onto a lit room.

Sefa didn't ask. Some debts you logged; some you just carried.

* * *

They came up out of the slot into a chamber that stopped Bram mid-shuffle and made even Sefa forget, for three full seconds, that she was the one responsible for getting everyone out alive.

It was round, and vast, and its ceiling vanished upward into darkness that their lamps couldn't reach. Pillars stood around the rim in a ring—twelve of them, each carved as a figure holding up the roof, and each figure's face had been chiseled away like the ones in the corridor. But between the pillars, the walls were covered floor to vault in writing. Tens of thousands of lines of it, in scripts Sefa half-recognized from Lena's margins and scripts no one living could read, layered over each other like sediment, like tide-marks, generation after generation of hands.

In the center of the floor lay a well. Not a water-well—a shaft, maybe four feet across, ringed with a low curb of black stone, descending into a dark that ate lamplight whole.

"The shrine," Isolde whispered. "This is directly beneath the throne room. My grandfather's journal says the coronation oath was *lowered*—the new king knelt over this shaft and swore down into it."

"Swore to what?" Quentin asked.

"To whatever answered," Isolde said.

Lena had drifted toward the well. Sefa watched them lean over the curb, watch their face catch the lamplight and lose it, and felt the pulse Lena had described earlier begin to make itself felt in her own chest—not heard, exactly, but *pressured*, like a change in altitude. The dark below the shaft wasn't empty. The dark below the shaft was *attentive*.

"Lena," she said. "Step back from the hole."

"They can hear me," Lena said. Their voice had gone soft with awe. "Not the crown—the *old* ones. What's left of them. They've been down here so long, listening through the stone, and no one has sworn anything true in this room for two hundred years, and they're—" Lena laughed, a cracked, astonished sound. "Sefa, they're *hopeful*. It's horrible. It's the most horrible thing I've ever felt. They still think someone's coming."

"Someone is coming," Quentin said quietly. "We are."

"Yes," Lena said. "But not for them. Not yet." They stepped back from the well, and their hand found Sefa's sleeve and held it a moment—just steadying, just human. "The passage up continues past the far pillar. That's the way to the undercroft below the throne room. But Sefa—" and now their voice dropped, urgent, "—the crown knows we're here. It's known since the sealed man. It's been *listening through him*. It eats memories, and that poor soul in the wall has been down here for centuries being slowly drained, and everything he knew about these tunnels, the crown has known too. It's not wondering where we'll come up. It's choosing where to meet us."

Sefa stood very still, doing the arithmetic she hated: every advantage bought with the certainty it was already spent.

"Then we don't come up where it expects," she said. "Isolde. Your father's maps. The folio. Was there another way into the undercroft? Something a nine-year-old would know that a king's guard wouldn't?"

Isolde was already unrolling the vellum scraps, her fingers steady, her eyes bright and hard. "The kitchen drains," she said. "He drew them because that's where he hid when he'd stolen honey. There's a service shaft behind the ovens—too small for soldiers in armor, barely big enough for us, and it lets out directly behind the wine cellar, thirty feet from the undercroft stair." She looked up, and for a moment the princess fell away entirely and something younger and fiercer looked out—her father's daughter, the boy who'd drawn maps of hiding places, grown up and come home to use them. "He showed me that shaft himself. Years ago. Before. He said, *a ruler should always know how to leave her own house.*"

"He wasn't wrong," Sefa said. "All right. Ovens it is. Bram—you're the widest thing in this crew and the only one who fights with cookware, so you're rear guard, and if anything comes down that main passage behind us, you introduce it to your pans."

"Aye," Bram said, and unslung them.

They crossed the chamber in single file, hugging the pillar ring, giving the well its distance. And as Sefa passed the last pillar, she felt it—one thread of attention sliding off her shoulders like a cold finger drawn along the spine, unhurried, almost courteous. Somewhere above them, behind thirty feet of stone and ritual and stolen memory, the thing wearing Aldric's crown turned its head toward the dark beneath its feet.

It knew. Of course it knew.

But knowing a road and guarding it were different trades, and Sefa had spent her whole life proving that the second one beat the first.

"Move," she said softly. "Past tense, everyone. By dawn we're ghosts in its own house."

And twelve faceless kings held up the roof in silence as the smugglers slipped out of the god-tunnels and up into the dark beneath the throne of Thornfell.
