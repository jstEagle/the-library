## 28. Aldric, Beneath

The tunnels beneath the palace were older than the palace itself, and Isolde had known that the moment Sefa's lantern touched the first wall. The stones down here were not cut; they were grown, veined with pale mineral like the inside of a shell, and they hummed faintly under her palm—a hum she had felt only once before in her life, at her father's coronation, when she was six years old and pressed against the barricade to watch him pass. She had thought then that the hum was bells. It had not been bells.

It had been the old gods, still bound beneath the mountain of the city, still singing their contracted songs into the foundations of everything her family had built on top of them.

"Stay close," Sefa murmured, though there was no one else to stay close to. They had left the others at the tunnel mouth—Quentin and Lena below with Vesh's voice rising through the stone, Bram holding the entrance with a cook's cleaver and a face that promised murder to anything wearing a warden's uniform. Isolde had argued to come this far alone. Sefa had argued harder that no one went anywhere alone in smuggler country. In the end they had compromised: Sefa would walk her to the door, whatever door it turned out to be, and then Isolde would go into the dark by herself, because the dark, apparently, only opened for blood.

That was what Lena had told her, back at the fire, in that quiet voice that always sounded like someone reading aloud from a book they weren't sure they were allowed to touch. *The crown is a lock, and locks are made of oaths, and your blood is in the oath he swore when he took the throne.* Isolde hadn't understood all of it. She understood enough. She understood that somewhere beneath her feet, in whatever country the spirit had built out of her father's stolen years, there was a man who did not know his daughter's name—and that his name for her, if any part of him still held anything, might be the only key that fit.

The passage narrowed. Sefa lifted the lantern high, and the light found a threshold where no threshold should have been: a seam in the living stone, hairline-fine, running floor to ceiling in a shape that was almost—but not quite—a doorway. Around its edges, carved so shallowly you would miss it unless the light raked sideways, ran a line of script in the old tongue. Isolde couldn't read it. But she knew it. She had seen those same characters a hundred times in her childhood, inked at the bottom of state documents in her father's careful hand, the signature-line of every covenant the crown renewed each spring.

*The king swears, and the land endures.*

"He signed himself in," Sefa said softly. "When they crowned him. He walked in through a door like this one, and something walked out wearing his face." The smuggler's voice was steady, but her knuckles were white around the lantern's handle. "I've moved contraband past worse doors than this. Never past one that eats people."

"It doesn't eat," Isolde said. "It forgets. That's worse."

She drew her knife—not for fighting; she doubted steel meant anything here—and opened the pad of her thumb along the edge. Blood welled, black in the lantern-light. She pressed it to the seam.

The stone drank. There was no other word for it. The blood vanished into the mineral veins as water into drought-cracked earth, and the hum beneath her feet changed pitch—rose, caught, held—and the seam widened with a sound like a long-held breath finally released.

Beyond it was not a corridor. Beyond it was winter.

* * *

She stepped through into falling snow that never landed.

That was the first thing her body understood before her mind caught up: flakes drifting all around her, thick as a blizzard, and not one of them touching the ground, or her skin, or anything at all. They hung in the air like a memory of snow rather than snow itself, and when she looked up she saw no sky—only a pale grey vastness going up forever, featureless, the color of paper left too long in the sun.

The ground beneath her boots was flagstone. A courtyard. She knew this courtyard. She had played in it. The fountain was still there at its center, dry now, its basin cracked, its bronze mermaid green with a decay that shouldn't have been possible on bronze. The colonnade along the east side stood with half its columns missing—not fallen, just *absent*, their spaces filled with more of that endless grey. And beyond the colonnade, where the palace's west wing should have risen three stories of honey-colored stone, there was nothing. Not ruins. Nothing. The world simply stopped, the way a sentence stops when the speaker forgets what they were saying.

"This is his mind," Isolde whispered, and the snow swallowed her voice without echo.

Not all of it. This was what remained of his mind—the last room still furnished, still lit, still defended. Everything else the thing upstairs had already stripped and carried away and burned for warmth. Her father's inner life, reduced to a single courtyard in an endless winter, and even here the walls were going.

She walked toward the fountain. Her footsteps made no sound. Of course they didn't. Sound required a listener, and there was no one left here to listen except—

There.

He was sitting on the edge of the dry fountain, and for one terrible heartbeat she thought she was looking at the thing from the throne room again, because the figure wore her father's face and her father's posture and the grey wool traveling cloak he'd worn the autumn she turned nine. Then he moved, and the difference broke her heart open: the thing on the throne moved like a puppet remembering strings. This man moved like a tired old soldier. He was shivering. The thing upstairs had never once shivered.

"Papa?"

He didn't look up. His eyes were fixed on his hands, which were cupped together in his lap as though holding something small and invisible, something he was afraid to let the wind take. His lips were moving. Counting, maybe. Or praying. Or repeating something over and over to keep it alive, the way you cup a match-flame in a gale.

Isolde crossed the courtyard slowly, the way you approach a wounded animal, or a sleeping bear, or a god. Up close she could see what the winter had done to him. He was thinner than the man who had waved to crowds. His beard had gone white at the chin. There was a hollowed look around his eyes that she recognized from the debt-slaves in Thornfell's markets, from Lena's ledger-thin wrists—from everyone she had ever watched being consumed by something larger than themselves.

But his hands stayed cupped around that nothing, and his lips kept moving, and she leaned close enough to hear.

"—and you run, little bird, and I hold the string, and the wind does the rest—"

Her knees nearly gave.

*The kite.*

* * *

She had been seven. Maybe eight. It was the spring after her mother died, the year the whole court tiptoed around the king as though grief were a contagion, and Aldric had looked at his solemn little daughter in her mourning black and decided, against the advice of every steward and physician in the palace, that what the girl needed was not another tutor.

He had taken her to the high meadow behind the summer lodge, the one that overlooked the valley where the shrine-mountain stood blue and distant on the horizon. He had spent the entire previous night, she learned later, in his study with lengths of silk and split bamboo and a pot of glue, because he refused to buy her a kite when he could make her one. It had been lopsided. One wing was longer than the other. It flew crooked and dragged left and crashed four times in the tall grass, and each time it crashed, her father laughed—not the court laugh, the real one, the ugly snorting one her mother used to scold him for—and hauled the string in hand over hand, and said, "Again, little bird. The wind hasn't finished with us yet."

And on the fifth try it had caught. It had climbed and climbed until it was a red diamond pinned against the blue, tugging at the line like something alive, and Isolde had held the spool with both hands and felt the wind pull back, and her father had crouched behind her with his big hands over hers and said, quietly, right against her ear:

"There. Do you feel it? It wants to fly away, and the string is the only reason it stays. That's not cruelty, Issa. That's how we keep the things we love. You don't cage them. You hold the line, and you feel them pull, and you let them know you're holding on."

She had been seven years old and had not understood him. She understood him now, standing in the frozen ruin of his mind, watching him cup his empty hands around the only thing the devourer upstairs had failed to take.

Because the crown ate memories—it ate them the way a fire eats wood, greedily, indiscriminately, everything fed into the same flame. But some memories are not stored in the head. Some are stored in the hands. In the muscle-memory of a knot tied ten thousand times, in the callus left by a string pulled taut, in the way a body remembers crouching behind a child and covering her small fingers with its own. The spirit could strip a man's history, his name, his loves, his griefs—but it could not reach into his hands and unlearn the shape of a kite-line.

So that was what Aldric had kept. Not the memory itself, perhaps—maybe even that was gone, maybe he didn't know anymore whose hands these were or why they remembered—but the feeling underneath the memory. The pull. The holding-on.

"Papa." She knelt in front of him, on the frozen flags, heedless of the cold that wasn't quite cold. "Papa, it's Issa. It's Isolde."

His lips stopped moving. His eyes came up—slowly, with enormous effort, like a man lifting weights—and they were her father's eyes, grey-green, creased at the corners, and utterly empty of recognition. He looked at her the way you look at weather. At something happening to you that you cannot name.

"Issa," he repeated. Not her name. Just the sound, tasting it, finding nothing attached to it. His brow furrowed—the ghost of confusion, the ghost of effort. "Issa... runs. Little bird. Runs, and I..."

"And you hold the string," Isolde said, and her voice cracked clean in half. "You hold the string, Papa. You always hold the string."

Something moved behind his eyes. Faint. A candle in a cathedral, a mile away, in a hurricane. But it moved.

"The wind—" he said. His voice was rust and dust. "The wind does the rest."

"Yes."

"But I—" His cupped hands trembled. He stared down into them, into the invisible kite only he could see, and the terror that crossed his face was the oldest terror there is, the terror of the man waking in the dark who cannot remember where he put his own soul. "I lost it. I lost the—I dropped the—the string went slack, and something *took*—"

"It didn't take it." Isolde reached out, slowly, and laid her hands over his cupped ones—over the nothing he was guarding, the phantom kite, the last warm thing in his frozen kingdom. His hands were ice. She wrapped hers around them anyway. "Nothing took it. Look at me. Look at my hands, Papa. Feel? Someone's holding the line. I'm holding the line. You taught me how."

He stared at her. The emptiness in his eyes fought with something—fought hard, the way a drowning man fights, without technique and without hope and without stopping. His lips shaped sounds that wouldn't become words. The snow fell and fell and never landed, and somewhere far above them, in the world of thrones and wardens and burning archives, the thing wearing his crown was feeding, growing, reaching—

And Aldric, beneath it all, in the last unfrozen corner of himself, began very quietly to cry.

* * *

They stayed like that a long time. How long, Isolde couldn't say; time in this place was snowfall, directionless, endless. She knelt on the flags with her father's frozen hands between hers and told him the story, over and over, because she had learned from Lena—she had learned from watching a scribe copy prayers into margins until the prayers started answering—that repetition is a kind of magic, that a thing spoken enough times stops being words and becomes structure, becomes load-bearing.

"The lopsided kite. Red silk, Papa, you stole the silk from Mother's good sewing chest and she haunted you for a month. One wing longer than the other. It flew crooked. It crashed four times and you laughed that awful laugh, and on the fifth try it caught the wind, and I held the spool, and you put your hands over mine and you said—" her voice steadied; she made it steady, the way Quentin had taught her that courage is not a feeling but a decision made hourly— "*you don't cage them. You hold the line.*"

Each time she told it, the courtyard changed. She didn't notice at first. The snow kept falling, but it fell slower. The grey void beyond the colonnade receded—one column returning, then another, like teeth regrowing in a ruined jaw. Color bled back into things: the faded heraldic banner over the archway remembered it was gold and green; the cracked fountain basin knit itself closed, drop by drop, and then, impossibly, water began to move in it, thin and silver and singing.

And her father's eyes—each telling, they focused a little more. Each telling, the emptiness retreated another inch from the shore of him.

By the fourth or fifth telling, he was mouthing the words along with her. By the sixth, he was saying them aloud, rust-voiced, halting, but *saying* them: "Red silk. Your mother's sewing chest. Crashed four times." He frowned. "Five. It flew on the fifth."

"On the fifth," Isolde agreed, laughing and crying at once, which she suspected was simply what hope felt like when it had been locked away too long.

"Issa." He said it again, deliberately this time, testing the weight of it. His brow creased—not the blank confusion from before but the fierce concentration of a man hauling on a rope. "Issa. My—my daughter. My daughter has—" He struggled. The word was on the far side of a wall. "Dark hair. Stubborn. Rides like a—like a—"

"Like a smuggler," Isolde said. "You'd be horrified. I've been learning all sorts of things you'd be horrified by."

A flicker. The corner of his mouth. Gods, it was barely anything, a twitch of muscle, and it was the most beautiful thing she had ever seen, because it was *his*, the specific crooked asymmetry of her father's almost-smile, resurrected from the dead.

"The crown," he said suddenly, and the almost-smile died, and his grip crushed down on her hands with sudden desperate strength. "Issa, listen, the crown, it's not—it was never a gift, they lied to us, the founders lied, it's a *mouth*, it's always been a mouth, and I opened my— I let it—" His breath hitched. The courtyard flickered around them; a column dissolved and reformed; the fountain-water stuttered. "It starts slow. It takes the bad days first. You don't even miss them. And then it takes the good ones, and you think, I'll write them down, I'll keep a list, and then it takes the list, and then it takes the *wanting* to keep a list, and then—"

"Papa. Breathe."

"—then there's nothing left that minds," he finished, in a whisper. "That's how it wins. Not the eating. The not-minding."

Isolde's throat was tight. "But you minded," she said. "You're minding right now. You kept the kite. It couldn't take the kite."

Aldric looked down at his cupped hands—at the space between them where, for him, Isolde realized with a jolt, there really was something: a spool, a line, a red diamond riding a thermal above a summer meadow thirty years gone. His hands shook. When he spoke again, his voice had changed—steadier, deeper, the voice that had commanded armies and held a fractious court in the palm of one hand.

"A string," he said. "Every mind needs a string. Something it holds that holds it back." He raised his eyes to hers, and they were clear now, clear as the day of the meadow, and burning. "Mine held. Barely. Held by a thread the width of a kite-line, and no thanks to anyone in that palace." His jaw set. "Where is it? Where is the thing that wears me?"

"Upstairs. On the throne. We came for it—there are people, Papa, good people, they found a rite, an unbinding, there's a scribe who can hear the dead and an oath-keeper who broke his word to protect you and a smuggler crew who owe us nothing and—"

"Smugglers," her father said, and the almost-smile returned, wider this time. "Your mother would have adored them." Then the smile faded into something graver. "Listen to me, Issa, while I am still—while this holds. It will not hold. Every minute I spend awake, it spends feeding, and it will feel me stirring, it will come down here, it will pour the winter back in. So hear me now." He gripped her wrists. "The rite. The unbinding. Whatever it costs—and it will cost, nothing that fights a devourer comes free—you tell that scribe of yours: do not fight it with hate. Hate is a flavor it has tasted for centuries. Hate feeds it. What starves it—" his voice dropped, and the whole courtyard seemed to lean in to listen— "is being *known*. It has eaten ten thousand lives and been mourned by none of them. Make it be seen. Make it be pitied. Nothing unmakes a monster faster than someone kneeling beside it and saying *I know what was done to you*."

Isolde stared at him. "How do you know that?"

"Because I can feel it," Aldric said quietly. "From in here. All these years, wearing me, pouring me out—I feel what it feels. And underneath all that hunger, Issa, underneath everything, it is so *lonely*." His eyes glistened. "Loneliest thing in the world. It ate a kingdom and there's no one left inside it who remembers its name either."

Above them—or in every direction at once, because this place had no honest geometry—the grey vastness shuddered. The snow stopped mid-fall, every flake hanging frozen, and a sound rolled through the courtyard like a door being forced somewhere very far away. The fountain-water went still. Aldric's head snapped up, and Isolde saw the fear cross his face and saw him master it, the old soldier, the kite-holder, refusing to let go of the line.

"It knows you're here," he said. "Go, little bird. Climb. Tell your scribe what I told you. And Issa—" he caught her hand as she rose, and pressed something into it, closing her fingers over it with both of his trembling ones. It was small, and warm—impossibly, defiantly warm in all that winter—and when she opened her hand she saw a scrap of red silk, frayed at the edges, torn from a lopsided wing thirty years ago. "Hold the line," he said. "Whatever happens up there. However bad it gets. Hold the line, and feel me pulling."

The courtyard was dissolving around them now, columns vanishing, the fountain draining into grey, the winter rushing back in like a tide. Isolde backed toward the seam in the stone with the silk clutched to her chest, and her father stood in the failing light of his own mind, straight-backed in his grey cloak, and did not look afraid.

"Papa!" she called, one last time, as the dark took him. "What do I tell them your name is? For the rite—they'll need your true name!"

And from the heart of the collapsing winter, her father smiled his crooked smile and answered:

"Tell them the man beneath the crown remembers his daughter. That's name enough."

Then the seam closed, and the humming stone went silent, and Isolde stood alone in the god-tunnel with a scrap of red silk in her fist and tears freezing on her cheeks—and somewhere far above, through miles of rock and marble and stolen memory, she heard the throne room doors begin to open.
