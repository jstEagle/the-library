## 34. The Blank Page

Bram had cooked for armies, for smugglers, for a princess in hiding, and once—for three days straight—for a funeral wake where the dead man had been his only friend. He knew how to feed people through grief. He did not know how to feed them through this.

He stood in the doorway of the sickroom at the shrine with a bowl of barley stew going cold in his hands, and he watched Lena open their eyes.

It happened slowly, the way thaw happens on a mountain—first a flutter of lashes, then a narrowing against the light, then the long, bewildered blink of someone surfacing from very deep water. Lena's gaze moved across the ceiling beams, down the stone wall where someone had hung the shrine's old banners to dry, and finally landed on Bram's face.

There was nothing in it. Not fear, not recognition, not even the polite blankness of a stranger deciding you're harmless. Just an openness so total it made Bram's chest hurt, like looking into a room that had been swept clean down to the floorboards.

"Hello," Lena said. Their voice was rough as a rusted hinge. They wet their lips and tried again. "Hello. Where—"

The bowl nearly went over when Bram set it on the bedside table. "Easy," he said, and was surprised at how steady his own voice came out, because inside he was screaming. "Easy now. You've been asleep a long while."

"How long?" And then, before Bram could answer, the question underneath it, the one that mattered: "Who am I?"

* * *

Sefa arrived first, because Sefa always arrived first when it mattered, boots half-laced and hair still tied up in the rag she slept in. Quentin followed, moving like a man walking into wind. Isolde came last and stood by the door with her hands folded, the way she'd learned to stand in palaces—present, contained, watching.

They had talked about this. Gods, they'd talked about this for six days while Lena lay empty-eyed among the shards of the crown, breathing shallow and even, untroubled by any dream at all. Vesh's last whisper before the mountain went quiet had been a warning shaped like mercy: *What is poured out does not pour back. But memory has more doors than one. Speak to them. Speak often. What love keeps, love can give.*

So they had agreed on the order of things. Sefa would do the talking, because Sefa could make anything sound survivable. Quentin would be the anchor, because oaths were his trade and truth-telling his craft. Isolde would stay back, because a princess's face was a thing that frightened people who didn't know her. And Bram—

Bram would cook. It wasn't much. It was what he had.

"My name is Sefa," she said now, dragging a stool to the bedside and sitting on it backward, arms folded across its back, close enough to touch and not crowding. "You're in the shrine on Thornfell mountain. The big one. You know—the one with all the doors that argue."

Lena blinked. "Doors that argue?"

"Long story." Sefa glanced at Quentin. "We'll get there."

"You were hurt," Quentin said quietly. "Not your body. Something else. You gave something away—a great deal of something—to save a great many people. Including me. Including her." A nod toward Isolde. "Including a king."

"A king," Lena repeated. Their brow creased, and for one wild second Bram thought—*it's coming back, it's coming back*—but the crease smoothed out again, and Lena said, apologetically, "I don't know why that word feels heavy. Like I should know which king."

"You should," Quentin said, and his voice caught on it. He cleared his throat. "You will. That's the arrangement we've made with the world. You will again, bit by bit, and until then—" He knelt, actually knelt, the exile-brand scar pale on his forearm where his sleeve had ridden up, and Bram saw Lena's eyes catch on it. "Until then, we'll tell you. Everything we can. As many times as you need."

"I don't understand," Lena said. And then their face changed—not to memory, never to memory anymore, but to something adjacent: the instinct of a person who has spent their whole life reading rooms. "I don't understand, but everyone here is grieving, and I think—I think it's about me. Am I dying?"

"No," Bram said, too fast, too loud. The word cracked off the stone walls.

Sefa put her hand over his wrist without looking at him. "No," she said, gentler. "You're not dying. You're just—new. Today's your birthday, if you want to look at it that way."

"That's a terrible thing to say to someone," Lena said.

"It really is," Sefa agreed. "I say terrible things when I'm scared. Ask anyone. Ask—well." She stopped. The joke had walked her straight off a cliff, and everyone in the room watched her fall. "Ask yourself, usually. You always had something to say about my mouth."

Something flickered behind Lena's eyes. Not remembering. Reaching. Bram had seen a man grasp at a rope thrown across a chasm once, and the expression was exactly the same—the fingers closing on air.

"I'm sorry," Lena whispered. "I can't—there's nothing. There's nothing there. It's like being asked to read a page that hasn't been written on yet."

And Bram, who had buried a wife and a son and every dream he'd carried at twenty, who had thought himself finished with weeping years ago, had to turn and face the window so that the newest person in the world wouldn't have to watch an old man cry over them.

* * *

They told it in pieces, the way you'd feed a fire too small to take a whole log.

Sefa took the roads: "You came to us half-frozen on the Kestrel Pass, midwinter, wearing a temple scribe's robe and no boots worth the name. I found you because my mule refused to walk past the snowdrift you were buried in, and Bram says the mule has better judgment than me, which is slander, but in this case accurate."

Quentin took the cause: "There was a spirit wearing the king's crown. It ate memories to live. Whole villages forgot their own children. You learned how to unbind it—an old rite, older than the kingdom—and the rite demanded everything you had. Every memory. Freely given." His jaw worked. "You gave it freely. Nobody made you. I want you to know that. Whatever you learn about yourself in the coming days, learn this first: nobody made you, and you did not hesitate."

Isolde took the smallest pieces, the ones that wouldn't wound: the kite, the color of the sky over Thornfell in autumn, the name of the mule (Duchess, and she was insufferable about it). She spoke carefully, formally at first, and then less so, because Lena kept asking questions—small, hungry questions—and Isolde had never in her life been able to resist a person who genuinely wanted to know.

And Bram took the food. That was the plan, anyway. In practice, Bram took the silences.

It was in a silence, somewhere past midday, that Lena looked at him properly for the first time—at his hands, flour-scarred and burn-speckled, wrapped around the refilled bowl they hadn't touched—and said, "Did I know you? Before?"

"Aye."

"Were we friends?"

Bram considered lying. Considered softening it. But Quentin had set the shape of the day, and the shape was truth, so he said: "You hated my cooking for the first month. Said my stew tasted like 'regret and boot leather.' Then you learned I salted it light on purpose because of Sefa's heart, and after that you used to come sit in my kitchen while I worked, and copy out recipes into your ledger margins like they were prayers. You called it 'the only scripture that ends in eating.'"

Lena's hand rose halfway to their mouth and stopped. "That sounds like someone I'd like to be."

"You are that someone," Bram said gruffly. "She's just sleeping. You're what's left standing guard till she wakes up."

"That's not how it works," Lena said, and there was the first edge of anguish, the first time all day they'd let themselves feel the size of the loss instead of circling it. "You must see that. All of you keep talking as though I'm a door you can unlock if you find the right key, and I'm telling you—there's nothing behind it. I'm a blank page. I don't remember my name. I don't remember a mother or a father or a single road. I don't remember saving anyone. You're mourning someone, and I'm sorry, but I am not her. I'm whatever's left."

The room went very still.

It was Quentin who moved. He crossed to the writing desk in the corner—the one they'd carried up from the valley, the one that still bore scorch-marks from a burning archive—and came back with a journal bound in green leather, worn soft at the corners. He set it in Lena's lap.

"Your margins," he said. "Every book you ever copied, you wrote in the margins. Little notes. Corrections. Arguments with the text." His voice dropped. "When the crown emptied you, it took what was in your head. It couldn't take what you'd written down. We've been gathering them for six days. This is the first volume."

Lena looked down at the journal the way you'd look at a stranger's coffin. Then, slowly, they opened it.

The handwriting was small and fierce, slanting hard to the right, ink-blotched where the writer had been in a hurry. Marginal notes crowded the copied prayers: *this god's name is misspelled in every ledger since the third dynasty, someone should be ashamed.* A pressed sprig of some alpine flower between two pages. A recipe for Bram's stew with a correction in the corner—*more thyme, less regret.* A sketch of Duchess the mule, deeply unflattering, captioned *she knows what she did.*

And on the flyleaf, in careful letters, as though written for exactly this day:

*Lena Nakamura. If you are reading this and do not know why: you gave away everything you had, and it was worth it, and the people reading it to you will tell you true. Trust them. Especially the angry one. Especially the cook.*

Lena's breath hitched. Their thumb moved over the ink like it was warm.

"This is me," they whispered. "This isn't remembering. But it's—" They searched for the word. "It's evidence. That I was somebody."

"You're somebody now," Sefa said. "The rest is paperwork."

* * *

Evening came down the mountain blue and slow. Bram put supper on the table outside the sickroom door—stew, real stew, thyme and all—and they ate together in a loose ring, because Vesh's whisper had been clear: *do not leave the new one alone, not for weeks, not in the dark hours.* So Lena sat among them, propped in blankets, holding the green journal like a shield, listening to the crew talk around and to them.

It was awkward. Of course it was awkward. Sefa kept almost-referencing shared jokes and swerving at the last moment; Isolde kept catching herself using court cadences and stripping them out; Quentin watched Lena's face like a man reading weather. Twice, Lena excused themselves to be sick from sheer exhaustion of soul, and twice, Bram heard someone pacing outside the privy door so they wouldn't be alone, pretending badly to be interested in the corridor.

But there were good moments too. Lena laughed—one short, startled bark—when Sefa reenacted the ambush on the switchback with bread rolls for soldiers. Lena asked Quentin what an oath-keeper actually *did*, and listened with such grave attention that Quentin, who hadn't spoken of his order without bitterness in seven years, talked until the candles guttered. Lena tasted the stew, frowned, and said, "There should be more thyme," and Bram had to leave the table for a moment on the pretext of checking the fire.

It was near midnight when Lena finally said it, small and direct, in the hush before sleep.

"The person I was. Do you want her back? Or do you want—me? Because I've been thinking all day"—and Bram noticed, with a cook's eye for appetite, that thinking clearly seemed to be returning faster than anything else—"that you might be waiting for someone who isn't coming back. And I'd rather know now."

Nobody answered immediately. The fire popped.

"I want both," Quentin said at last. "I want her back because she earned her rest. And I want you because you're here, and you asked the question, and asking it was brave."

"We're not waiting for a replacement," Sefa said. "We're keeping her seat warm. That's different."

Isolde said simply, "My father forgot me completely, and I would have given anything for one person who stayed and told him who he was. Let me be that person for you. It's the easiest vow I've ever made."

Bram said nothing. He just ladled a second helping into Lena's bowl without asking, which in the language of the crew meant more than any of it.

Lena looked around the ring of faces—the smuggler, the oath-keeper, the princess, the cook—and their eyes filled, and they didn't wipe them away.

"I don't remember any of you," they said. "But I think I trust you. Is that strange? To trust people on evidence alone?"

"Not strange," Bram rumbled. "That's how trust works. Memory just gets the credit."

* * *

Later, when the others had gone to their beds and only the banked fire kept watch, Bram checked on Lena one last time. They were asleep with the green journal open on their chest, one hand resting flat on the page, the way you'd rest a hand on the flank of a horse that had carried you far.

On the desk beside the bed lay the quill and the inkpot Isolde had left, and beneath them—a fresh sheet of paper, blank, laid out square.

Bram stood looking at it a long time. A blank page, in a scriptorium man's sickroom, placed where a sleeping hand could reach it. He didn't know whether Lena had set it out before sleeping or whether Isolde had, and it didn't matter. It said what it said: *the story isn't over. Write the next line.*

He was still standing there when he heard the scratch of a quill, and turned, and saw Lena awake—awake and sitting up, eyes fever-bright in the dark, writing by the light of the coals with the fierce right-slanting hand from the journal, copying something, or composing it, he couldn't tell. Writing as if their life depended on it. Writing as if the ink itself might answer.

"Lena?" Bram said softly, using the name for the first time aloud. "What are you doing?"

The quill stopped. Lena looked up at him with an expression he'd never seen on that new, swept-clean face—wonder, and under the wonder, a thread of fear.

"I heard something," they whispered. "In the ink. It's faint—it's so faint—but Bram, I heard it."
