## 35. Relearning Lena

### I. Sefa

The mountain did not care that Lena had forgotten it.

That was the first thing Sefa taught them, on the third morning after the waking, when she hauled them out of Bram's warm kitchen and pointed at the grey shoulder of the pass above the free-town. "Roads don't hold grudges," she said. "People do. Mountains just are. So we start there."

Lena had looked at her with those new eyes—wide, polite, terrified in a way that was worse than the old terror because it had no name—and said, "I'm sorry, I don't—"

"Don't apologize." Sefa had said it too sharply and regretted it instantly. She softened her voice the way she softened rope before a knot. "You didn't lose anything you were careless with. You gave it away. There's a difference, and the difference matters."

So they walked.

Sefa walked them up the goat-track behind the town first, the easy one, the one even drunk smugglers could find in the dark. Lena stopped every twenty paces to touch things: lichen like spilled ash, a cairn of stacked stones, the cold breath coming off a snowmelt stream. They touched everything as if the world might be a language they'd once been fluent in.

"Do you remember any of this?" Sefa asked, not unkindly.

"No." A pause. Then, quieter: "But my hands know where the rocks are loose."

Sefa grinned despite herself. "That's more than most pilgrims ever learn."

They went higher each week. The switchbacks above the tree line, where the wind came off the icefields with a sound like someone breathing through gritted teeth. The shelf-road along the ravine where a misstep meant a hundred feet of nothing. Sefa talked the whole way, filling the silence the way she filled a hold with cargo—efficiently, without wasted space.

"This turn, you cut wide, because the scree slides in spring. This ledge, you trust—the old gods' masons built it, and their work holds better than anything men have laid since. This cave—" she ducked under an overhang, "—is where I hid from a warden patrol for two days eating candle wax, so if you ever need to disappear, remember the candle wax trick. It's terrible but it works."

Lena laughed. It startled both of them. It was the first laugh since the waking, thin and rusty, but real.

"You're laughing," Sefa said. "You don't know why."

"I know it's funny," Lena said slowly, feeling for the words like fingers in dark water. "Hiding. Eating candles. I can't remember you telling me this before, but I think—I think you would tell it exactly this way. Like it's a story you've polished."

"Forty times," Sefa admitted. "Mostly to make Bram groan."

And that was the thing Sefa learned, walking the roads again with a person who was and wasn't the scribe she'd pulled half-frozen off the pass two years ago: memory wasn't only what lived in the skull. It lived in the body, in the habits, in the way Lena still stepped over thresholds left-foot-first, still flinched at the smell of burning paper, still counted stones underfoot the way counters count coins. The crown had taken the ledger of Lena's life. But the handwriting remained.

On the shelf-road, wind screaming, Lena turned into the gale without being told, leaned the correct three degrees into it, and kept walking.

"There," Sefa shouted over the wind, and couldn't say more, because her throat had gone tight. *There you are.*

### II. Quentin

Quentin read to Lena every evening.

He had claimed the duty without discussion, the way he once claimed night watches—by simply taking the first one and never giving it back. He sat by the fire in Bram's kitchen or, in warmer weather, on the step outside the room Isolde had arranged for Lena in the regent's guesthouse, and he opened the journal.

It had come back to them in a crate of salvaged temple records, water-stained and half-burned at one corner, and Quentin had nearly wept over it before he'd read a single page. Lena's handwriting. Small, slanted, crowded into margins the way Lena crowded everything—prayers copied in the spaces between ledgers, observations crammed sideways, upside down, in ink colors that changed mid-sentence when the pot ran dry.

"It's yours," he'd said, offering it. "All of it."

Lena had held it like something hot. "I can't read this. It's my hand and I can't read it."

"So I'll read it to you," Quentin said. "Until you can."

Some nights it was agony. He read entries about Corvane—the clipped, furious notes of a person cataloguing their own cage—and watched Lena listen to their own rage from outside it, puzzled, like hearing a stranger argue in another room. He read the entry about the answered prayer, the voice in the dried ink, and Lena pressed a hand flat against their sternum as though checking whether something was still beating in there.

"The voice," Lena said. "Vesh. Did I love him?"

Quentin considered. "I think you argued with him constantly. And trusted him completely. I think those were the same thing for you."

"That sounds right," Lena said, and smiled faintly at nothing.

Other nights it was worse than agony. He read the entry from the night before the pouring-out—Lena's last entry, the ink blotted where something had fallen on the page—and his voice failed him twice before he got through it.

*If anyone reads this: I chose. Write that somewhere I can't lose it. I chose, and I would choose it again, and the choosing is mine even if nothing else is.*

Lena listened to their own last words with their head bowed. When Quentin finished, the fire cracked, and neither spoke for a long time.

"Did I write anything else?" Lena finally asked. "After. When I woke up empty."

"You couldn't hold a pen for weeks. Your hands shook." Quentin hesitated. "But Bram wrote for you. Every day. What you ate, what made you flinch, what made you smile. He calls it your ledger now. He keeps it in the kitchen."

"He does?" Something moved across Lena's face—not memory, but its shadow, its promise. "Why?"

"Because he's Bram," Quentin said. "And because someone told him once that people are worth writing down."

He read on, night after night, and slowly the journal stopped being a stranger's diary. Lena began finishing sentences he trailed off on. Began correcting him—"no, wait, that word is *thornfell*, not *thornfield*, I always got it wrong until Vesh shouted at me"—began laughing at their own jokes before he reached the punchline, which meant the joke had landed somewhere deeper than recall.

One evening, near midsummer, Lena took the book from his hands gently and read a page aloud themselves. Their voice wobbled. Their hand shook. But the words came out in order, and when they finished they looked up with tears running freely and said, "I remembered how to read my own name."

Quentin, who had sworn oaths to kings and broken them, who had carried an exile-brand for seven years, found that this small thing undid him more thoroughly than any trial of the shrine had. He bowed his head over the fire so Lena wouldn't see, and swore—quietly, newly, in the way he was learning to swear now, freely and without ceremony—that he would keep reading until every page was home.

### III. Isolde

Isolde brought boxes.

She arrived at the guesthouse on a grey morning with four of them, dust-sheeted and heavy, and a cartload of archivists' apologies behind them. The royal archive had spent the winter being reassembled from the ashes of the temple burnings—char-flaked pages sorted by candlelight, fragments matched like the world's saddest puzzle—and what they had recovered of the Thornfell scriptorium's marginalia filled these four crates and no more.

"I had them leave the ledgers," Isolde said, setting the first box on Lena's table. "Corvane's ledgers. The debt records. I thought—" she paused, honest enough to admit uncertainty even to herself, "—I thought you might want them burned. Or kept. I couldn't decide which was kinder, so I brought both options and no opinion."

Lena lifted the lid. Inside lay years of bondage in neat columns, and interleaved among them, crammed into every margin, the secret life: prayers, warnings, the dead's small gossip, Vesh's whispers transcribed in a hand that grew steadier page by page.

Lena touched the margin-writing the way one touches a scar. "This is me."

"This is you," Isolde agreed. "The parts the crown couldn't reach."

They worked through the crates together over the following weeks, and Isolde discovered that this—sitting cross-legged on the floor sorting fragments—was the only time Lena's new emptiness seemed to recede. Here was evidence. Here was proof, in Lena's own ink, that the person who had poured themselves out had existed, had suffered, had hoped, had been *funny*, occasionally, in a dry marginal way that made Lena bark laughter at pages written by a stranger wearing their own history.

"Look at this one," Lena said one afternoon, holding a scrap. "'Corvane counts his rings while he talks. Six rings, zero souls.'" A pause. "Did I write that?"

"You did," Isolde said. "Sefa has it embroidered on a cushion now. She claims it's a warning to visiting officials. Bram claims it's slander. They argue about it weekly."

Lena laughed, then went quiet, thumb moving over the faded ink. "Do you think any of it will come back? Really come back? Not just—knowing about it. Remembering."

Isolde thought of her father, who remembered one thing: a kite, a hill, a girl shrieking with joy beneath it. One thread of a whole life, and he clung to it like a man clinging to a spar in open sea, and it was enough. It had to be enough.

"I think memory isn't a storehouse," she said carefully. "I used to think it was—a room with everything in it, and the crown broke down the door and stole the furniture. But watching my father, I think it's more like... a garden. Some things were dug up. Some things will regrow from roots nobody knew were there. And some things—" she touched the scrap in Lena's hand, "—other people planted for you, and tended, and they grow whether or not you remember planting them."

"That's very regal," Lena said.

"It's very tired," Isolde said, and Lena laughed again, and Isolde privately added the moment to her own garden, to be kept.

Near the bottom of the last crate, they found a fragment Isolde hadn't expected: a single page in Aldric's hand—the true coronation oath, the fragment Isolde had carried through mountains and wardens and war—annotated in the margin in Lena's smaller script. Two hands on one page, separated by years and catastrophe, meeting again in a box of ashes.

Lena stared at it a long time.

"What did I write?" Isolde asked.

"'The oath binds the speaker, not the spoken-for,'" Lena read slowly. "'A king who swears for his people has already misunderstood the words.'" They looked up. "I wrote that? Before all this?"

"Years before," Isolde said. "You saw it before any of us. My father probably never read your margins." Her throat tightened. "I'm reading them now. The whole kingdom is going to read them, if I have anything to say about it. Covenants, not chains. Your notes are going to be required study for every crowned head in Thornfell for the next hundred years."

"Vesh would be insufferable about that," Lena said, and then stopped, hand going to their chest, eyes widening.

"What?" Isolde leaned forward. "What is it?"

"I—" Lena frowned, groping. "Nothing. A feeling. Like almost sneezing. I felt like I *knew* that I'd annoyed him, specifically, on purpose, many times." The frown deepened, then eased into something wondering. "It passed. But it was there."

Isolde wrote it down. That was the other thing she'd learned from the archivists: write everything down. Someday, she promised silently, someday the garden would bloom so thick that fragments like this would stop mattering. Until then, they kept records. They kept each other.

### IV. Lena

Some things returned. Some things did not.

The mountain paths came back in pieces, arriving in the body before the mind: feet finding footholds, lungs knowing the altitude's price, hands remembering knots. Sefa cheered each return like a birth.

Their own journal came back as recognition without ownership—they could read it now, could anticipate it, could finish their own sentences—but the *living* of it stayed sealed behind glass. Lena knew they had loved the scriptorium's dusty light. They could not feel it.

Bram's stew, they learned, they had always loved; that needed no recovery at all. Taste, apparently, ran deeper than crowns could dig. Bram took enormous satisfaction in this. "Spirit ate your whole life," he grumbled, ladling a fourth helping, "and couldn't stomach my cooking. Shows what spirits know."

Their parents' faces did not come back. Not in the journal, not in the margins, not in dreams. Lena grieved them a second time—one grief for the loss itself, long ago, and a newer, stranger grief for the loss of the grief, the theft of even the ache. Quentin sat with them through that one without speaking, because some nights reading was the wrong tool, and presence was the right one.

And then, on an evening in late summer, alone at the table with a fresh sheet of paper and the new covenant text Isolde had asked them to copy—for the official record, in a steady hand, and everyone had pretended it wasn't symbolic, and everyone had known it was—Lena dipped the pen and wrote the first line.

The ink dried. And something stirred in it. Faint. Young. Curious, the way a bird is curious at a window.

Not Vesh. Vesh was gone into the mountain's freed stone, dissolved into gratitude with the rest of the old contracts. This was something else. Something that had perhaps always lived in ink and prayer and listening, waiting for an ear like theirs—an ear that had been emptied out and refilled with other people's voices, and was therefore, somehow, more roomy than before.

*Hello,* the ink said, in a whisper like wet paper.

Lena set down the pen very carefully. Their heart was pounding, but it was not the old pounding of dread. It was something closer to the feeling of standing at a trailhead Sefa had described all winter: unknown country, but a road, definitely a road, leading in.

"Hello," Lena said aloud, to a page, alone, smiling like a fool. "My name is Lena Nakamura. I'm told I hear the dead."

The ink rustled, delighted, and began to answer.
