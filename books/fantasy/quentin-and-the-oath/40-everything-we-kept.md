## 40. Everything We Kept

The mountain did not care that it was an anniversary. It rose the same as it always had, gray-shouldered and patient, its summit lost in a haze of cloud that never quite resolved into weather or sky. The path up to the shrine was the same path, too—switchbacks cut by hands long dead, stones worn smooth in their centers by centuries of pilgrim feet, then abandoned for two hundred years, then walked again now by exactly six people who had no business being pilgrims at all.

Lena counted them without meaning to. It was a habit now, this counting. Sefa out front, finding the trail with her whole body the way she always did, reading stone the way Lena once read ink. Bram behind her, red-faced and grumbling about the grade, carrying more food than any five people could eat because Bram had never once in his life believed there would be enough. Quentin with his hand resting easy on the pommel of a sword he hadn't drawn in a year, his pale scar visible below his sleeve where he'd stopped bothering to hide it. Isolde, who climbed like someone who had been kept indoors too long and was making up for it, her cheeks wind-burned and her laugh carrying down the switchbacks when Sefa said something obscene about the mountain's parentage.

And behind them all, Vesh—or what remained of Vesh, which was a voice, which was a warmth in the blood, which was sometimes nothing at all.

Lena brought up the rear. They had learned not to mind the rear. The rear was where you could see everyone you loved arranged against the world, and Lena had spent enough years looking at the backs of people leaving to find that they preferred this view above all others.

"You're doing it again," Sefa called back, not turning around.

"Doing what?"

"Counting us."

"I'm not counting you."

"You're absolutely counting us. You get this look. Like a merchant tallying stock she's afraid will be stolen." Sefa hopped a gap in the trail, landed light, and finally glanced over her shoulder, grinning. "Nobody's stealing us, little scribe. We're paid in full."

*Paid in full.* The words landed somewhere old and tender. Lena's own debt-ledger had burned with all the others a year ago; they had stood in the ash of the archive annex in Thornfell and felt the first memory come back—not the day they saved a kingdom, never that, but something smaller: firelight, stew, Bram's voice saying *eat or I'll feed you myself*. Freedom had arrived quietly, the way most true things do.

They climbed.

* * *

The shrine gate still bore Quentin's oath in its stone. Not visibly—the words had sunk into the rock the way ink sinks into paper, gone but structurally present, part of the door now. When they reached it, Quentin put his palm flat against the stone the way he always did, and the gate recognized him, and opened without ceremony, groaning on hinges that should have rusted shut decades ago but refused, out of some stubbornness of purpose, to die.

Inside, the great hall was exactly as they had left it. Sunlight fell through the broken roof in slanted columns. Dust motes turned slowly, like a congregation that had forgotten how to pray but remembered how to gather. The three trials stood silent in their corners: the truth-door, the weight-bridge, the dark. They were dormant now. Their work was done. But Lena still gave them a wide berth, the way one gives space to a sleeping dog that has already bitten you once.

Bram went straight to the old hearth and began building a fire, because Bram's answer to every situation, sacred or profane, was fire and food. Within the hour the hall smelled of woodsmoke and onions and the particular spice blend he claimed was a family secret from Hollowbrook, though the village that had been Hollowbrook remembered nothing of him, and so the recipe belonged to no one now except whoever ate it.

They ate. That was the ritual, really. Not the climbing, not the gate, not even the anniversary itself. The eating together, at the shrine where everything had been given away, was the ritual. Last year Isolde had stood at the altar-stone and read aloud the new covenant—the old contracts rewritten, gods and mortals bound by gratitude instead of chains—and they had all sworn small oaths over Bram's stew, ridiculous oaths, *I swear to always take the last roll without asking*, and the mountain had not objected, because the mountain, Vesh said, had always understood that the small oaths are the ones that hold.

This year, Isolde brought news instead of ceremony.

"The eastern temples are copying the covenants," she said, tearing bread. "Not transcribing—copying. There's a difference. The old scriptoriums used to copy prayers to own them. Now they copy them to share them. And—" she hesitated, glancing at Lena with a carefulness that Lena both appreciated and resented, "—they're asking after you. The scribe who heard the dead. Some of them want to learn."

Lena looked down at their bowl. Steam curled up, blurring the world.

"They can learn," Lena said. "The gift isn't mine to hoard. It never was. That was the whole lesson, wasn't it?" A pause. "Though I don't remember learning it."

The table went quiet in the way it always did when Lena said things like that—not a grief-quiet anymore, not after a year, but a careful quiet, the quiet of people setting down something fragile.

"You remember what matters," Quentin said.

"I don't, though. That's the thing." Lena set down the spoon. "Quentin, I know you told me about the throne room. I know the shape of it. I know I knelt before the crown and poured out everything, and I know the crown cracked, and I know Aldric wept your name—no, Isolde's name—I know all of it the way you know a story you've been read many times. But I wasn't there. I'm a scribe who copied someone else's manuscript of my own life." They smiled, and it was mostly a real smile. "Except the margins. I kept the margins."

"The margins," Sefa said.

"Firelight. Stew. The pass where you got wounded saving me. Bram voting last. The kite." Lena looked at Isolde. "I don't remember flying it. But I remember that it matters to you, and somehow that's the same thing."

Isolde's eyes were bright. She had learned, this past year, to let them be bright. Her father sat in a garden in the capital these days, remembering slowly, imperfectly, a man reassembled from other people's devotion—he remembered the kite best of all, because Isolde anchored him with it, and because some memories, freely given, root deeper than anything taken.

"That," Isolde said, "is why we come here every year. So you never have to be the only one who remembers."

"I know." Lena's throat tightened. "That's the strange mercy of it. I lost everything I was. And what grew back is—you. All of you. I'm made of borrowed memories now, and every single one was given, not taken." They laughed softly. "Vesh says that's the oldest magic there is."

Everyone looked, instinctively, toward the place where Vesh lived, which was nowhere, which was Lena's blood, which was the warmth that stirred now, faint and dry as old paper:

*They are correct. Tell the loud one her stew opinions are not covenant law.*

"Bram," Lena relayed, "Vesh says your spice blend is not divinely sanctioned."

"Vesh," Bram said, pointing his spoon at Lena's chest, "can kiss my divinely sanctioned arse."

The hall filled with laughter, and the dust motes swirled, and outside the sun moved westward over a kingdom that, piece by piece, was learning to keep its memories honest.

* * *

Near dusk, Lena walked alone to the altar-stone.

Not alone, quite. Vesh came, the way Vesh always came—a presence like a hand on the shoulder from inside. And the others let them go, because they had learned that Lena needed this: one hour a year, standing where they had emptied themselves, touching the place where they had ended and begun.

The stone was warm. It was always warm. Lena pressed both palms flat against it and closed their eyes.

"Do you ever miss it?" they asked Vesh. "Being whole. Being bound to the mountain with a purpose."

*I was bound in chains, little scribe. You were bound in love. Do not confuse the two.*

"But you stayed anyway. After the contracts dissolved. You could have gone wherever dead priests go. You stayed in my blood."

A long silence. Wind through the broken roof. Somewhere below, Sefa's voice rose in argument with Bram about the proper way to bank a fire, and Quentin's low chuckle threaded through it, and Isolde said something that made them all laugh again.

*That,* Vesh said at last, *is why.*

Lena opened their eyes. The sunset had reached the hall, gilding the pillars, catching the dust motes until they burned like slow golden sparks. And there—at the edge of vision, in the column of light nearest the altar—something moved. Not a mote. Something younger. Curious. It drifted the way a child drifts toward a window, and Lena felt it notice them, felt its attention like a fingertip touched to the surface of water.

It had been coming for months now, in the new ink, in the margins of the covenants Lena copied. Not Vesh. Something else. Something born, perhaps, from the gratitude that had replaced the old contracts—from two hundred years of prayers finally answered, finally freed, finally becoming something new.

It had never spoken. Until tonight.

*Hello,* it said, in a voice like wet ink drying, like a page opening, like the very first word of a very long story. *You're the one who listens.*

Lena's breath caught. Below, their family's laughter rose up the mountain, carried on the evening air, every voice distinct, every voice known.

"Yes," Lena said to the young light. "I am. Would you like to tell me something?"

And the new voice—shy, wondering, impossibly young—began to speak.
