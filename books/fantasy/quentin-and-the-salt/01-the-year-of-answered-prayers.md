## 1. The Year of Answered Prayers

The gulls woke Coral before the bells did, the way they always did, wheeling above his rooftop in a grey spiral and filling the dark with their opinions. *Cold,* they said. *Cold, cold, the water is cold, the fish have gone deep, the man on the roof is sad.* He pulled his blanket over his head and told them, without opening his mouth, that he was not sad, he was sleeping, and they should mind their own business.

They laughed at him. Gulls always laughed.

That was the trouble with Greyharbor: a man could hide anything here except from the birds. The city sat salt-locked between the mountain and the sea like a coin pressed into wax, its streets crusted white, its gutters running brine instead of rainwater. Five years ago the Salt Edict had gone up on every doorpost — *MAGIC IS OUTLAWED; THE GODS ARE SILENT; THE LAW IS ENOUGH* — and the people of Greyharbor had done what coastal people always do with edicts: they had read them, nodded, and quietly gone on living. But Coral Voss had never been able to go on living quietly. He had simply learned to make it look that way.

By day he sold charms. Not *charms*, of course — nothing so incriminating. He sold *keepsakes*. A knotted cord to hang over a cradle, "for luck." A pinch of grey salt sewn into blue cloth, "against bad dreams." A smooth stone from the tideline, "so your nets come home heavy." He said these things with a shrug and a smile, the way a man talks about weather, and the women of the Saltmarket bought them and paid too much and told themselves it was only superstition, harmless as knocking wood. And the charms worked. That was the part Coral tried very hard not to think about. He would knot the cord and murmur something under his breath — not words, exactly, more like a shape made of wanting — and by evening the baby slept, or the fever broke, or the nets came in so heavy the fishermen laughed until they cried.

He did not call it magic. Magic was outlawed, and magic was what the priests of the Law had burned old Tam Broadstairs for, three winters back, in the fish-oil square where everyone could watch. What Coral did was small. Small enough to be invisible. Small enough, he told himself each night, to be nothing at all.

* * *

The widow Brann came to his stall on the fourth morning of the strangeness, while the fog still hung in the streets like unwrung laundry.

She was a big woman, weathered to the color of her own doorframe, and she had buried her husband Halvard nine days ago after the sea gave him back drowned. Coral had sold her a mourning-knot at the funeral — black thread, three loops, a wordless wish that her grief might find somewhere soft to land. He remembered because she had paid with a fish she'd smoked herself, and it had been excellent.

"Voss," she said, planting both hands on his stall's edge. "I need to say something, and I need you to listen, and then I need you to tell me I'm mad."

"That's usually how it goes," Coral said. "Go on."

"I prayed." She glanced left and right, lowered her voice to almost nothing. "Not to the Law. Not to any of it. I went down to the tide-pools past the breakwater, where Halvard used to mend his lines, and I just — talked. Out loud. Like a fool. I said, *bring him back to me, even a piece of him, even his voice, I don't care what it costs.*"

Coral's hands went still over the cords he was pretending to sort. Around them the market roared on — haggling, gull-cries, the bell of the tide-bell tower marking the ebb — but for Coral the sound seemed to come from very far away, through water.

"And?" he said. His voice came out careful, flat, a door held shut.

"The next morning there was a gull on my sill." Widow Brann's eyes were wet and fierce. "A grey-backed gull, old one, scarred beak. And when it looked at me, Voss, I heard him. Halvard. Not words. Just — *him*. The way he hummed when he mended nets. The way he breathed when he slept. It sat there an hour and then it flew off toward the mountain, and I swear on his grave that bird carried my husband inside it like a lantern carries light."

The fog pressed close. Somewhere a child was laughing. Coral found that his mouth had gone dry as the salt crusts on the seawall.

"What do you want me to say?" he managed.

"I want you to say I'm mad."

"You're mad," Coral said gently. "Grief does strange things. Buy a dream-salt packet. Half price, for widows."

She bought one, because that was the ritual of it, and shuffled off into the fog. And Coral stood behind his stall with his heart hammering like a trapped bird, because he knew — he *knew* — that she was not mad.

Because the gulls had been talking differently all week.

* * *

He went to them that night, as he always did, climbing the crooked stair to his rooftop with a heel of bread and the last of the smoked fish the widow had given him. The city lay below in its usual dark, lamp-lit and law-abiding, and above it the mountain stood against the stars like a closed fist. Nobody went to the mountain. The mountain belonged to the Undercourt, if it belonged to anyone, and the Undercourt was a story mothers told to keep children away from mine-shafts: beautiful voices in the deep places, doors that opened for the desperate, prices no ledger could hold.

Coral had never believed in the Undercourt. He believed in gulls, and tides, and the small warm weight of a charm working in a stranger's pocket.

"*Here,*" he called softly, and they came — thirty of them, forty, settling on the roof ridges and the chimney stacks, folding their wings, turning their flat gold eyes toward him. This was the other half of his life, the half that existed nowhere but here: five years of nights spent talking to birds, telling them the news of the day, asking after the shoals, complaining about his neighbors. They answered. Not in words — gulls don't have words — but in pictures and hungers and long slow feelings, the way dreams talk. It was the only place Coral felt entirely himself, which is to say, entirely hidden.

But tonight they were restless. Tonight they kept looking east, toward the mountain, and their minds came to him tasting of iron and incense.

"*What is it?*" he asked, breaking the fish into pieces. "*You've been strange all week. Since the storm.*"

The oldest gull — the scarred-beaked one, he realized, the widow's gull, though he hadn't known that yet, wouldn't know it until later — hopped onto the roof ridge beside him. And into Coral's mind poured something that was not a gull's thought at all. It was vast and slow and tasted of deep water and older names, and it said, in the way the tide says things:

*Something under the mountain has begun to eat the silence. And the silence is bleeding.*

Coral sat down hard on the cold shingles.

Around him the flock stirred, uneasy, and beneath their unease he felt the other thing, the thing he had been refusing to feel for days: the charms working *too* well. The fever-charm last week that had pulled a child back from the brink so completely the physician had crossed himself. The luck-cord that had turned a doomed boat around a reef no pilot could name. Small magics, his magics, five years of them seeping into the city like rain into chalk — and now, suddenly, the chalk was drinking.

His small lies had lit a fuse. Something ancient had felt the warmth of them through the mountain's stone, the way a sleeper feels sunlight, and had begun to wake — and it was not alone. The gulls' minds flickered with glimpses: lights in the deep vaults, a dragon's fire guttering somewhere in the dark, souls moving in cages like fish in barrels.

And threaded through all of it, faint as a name spoken once across a harbor:

*a girl's voice. Wren's voice. Calling his name.*

Coral's hands shook so badly he dropped the fish. Wren had been dead six years. Wren's debts had been sold to brokers who sold to buyers whose names no one knew. He had told himself the story of her death so many times it had worn smooth as sea glass.

The scarred gull looked at him with its gold eyes, and inside those eyes something patient and enormous waited, and Coral understood that the year of hiding was over. The gods were waking. His sister's voice was under the mountain. And someone — the widow, the gull, whatever watched from the deep — had already seen him.

Above Greyharbor, far up the black flank of the mountain, a light kindled that was not a lamp, not a star, not anything the Law had a word for.

It pulsed twice, like a heartbeat learning its rhythm again.

Then it went out.
