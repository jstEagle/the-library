## 2. What the Salt Remembers

The gull came at low tide, three days after the widow Brannock's prayer, and it did not land so much as collapse onto Coral's roof, wings splayed like a broken compass.

He knew every gull in Greyharbor by their voices — the sharp-tempered ones who squabbled over the fish-guts barrels, the young ones who begged, the old ones who said nothing at all. This was none of them. It was grey as unwashed linen, and when it opened its beak, what came out was not a cry.

*"Coral Voss."*

His name in a voice like a hearth going cold. He dropped the charm-basket he'd been weaving and stood very still on his own rooftop, among the drying nets and the gull-droppings and the small dishonest life he had built, listening to the impossible.

"Who's there?" he whispered, because five years of hiding taught him that even miracles should be conducted quietly.

*"Come to the breakwater. Come alone. I have not the strength to say this twice."*

* * *

The dragon lay half-submerged where the breakwater met the sea, and Coral understood at once that no one else could see her. Fishermen walked the stones not ten paces from her flank, hauling crab-pots, complaining about prices, and their eyes slid past her as though she were weather. Only Coral saw the vast grey-gold body, barnacled like an abandoned ship, her ribs rising and falling in breaths so slow they might have been tides.

Her eye, when it turned to him, was the color of deep amber held up to dying light.

"You're Vess," he said. The name arrived in his mouth unbidden, the way old names do, dredged up from somewhere below memory.

"I am. And you are the last of the beast-speakers in Greyharbor, though you've spent five years pretending otherwise. Don't look so stricken. I've watched you talk to my gulls since before your sister died."

Coral's throat closed. "Wren—"

"Is why I called you." Vess's head lowered until her breath stirred his hair, warm and smelling of salt and something older, like stone that remembered being lava. "Listen well, because I am dying, boy, and what I have to say does not fit in a dying creature comfortably. The gods of Greyharbor are not silent. They are *fed upon.*"

The waves broke against the breakwater. Somewhere behind him, the city went on — cart wheels, a fishwife's argument, the bell at the harbor-master's tower. All of it suddenly sounding thin, like music played through a wall.

"There is a court beneath the mountain," Vess said. "Older than the mountain, if truth be told. When the gods withdrew — when they chose silence rather than war — the court saw an opening and crawled into it. Divinity cannot be made, Coral. But it can be *stolen*, soul by soul, and burned like coal in a furnace built to mimic a sun. Every answered prayer in this city these past weeks has been answered by them. Every miracle is a lie wearing a god's coat."

"The widow's prayer," Coral breathed.

"And yours. Your little charms, your small mercies — you thought you were working alone. You were striking sparks. And the court has been gathering every spark you made, hoarding them, waiting for enough light to pass for dawn." Her eyelid drooped, heavy as a closing door. "They buy souls, boy. From debt-brokers. From desperate men. From anyone willing to sell what they don't believe they possess. Do you understand what I'm telling you?"

He did. He had understood since the second sentence, and some part of him had already begun to scream.

"Wren's debt," he said. His voice sounded far away, belonging to someone else. "When she died. The broker — Halloran. He settled her accounts after. I never asked how a dock-girl with nothing paid off what she owed."

"Souls keep," Vess said gently. "That is the horror of it. They do not fade. They wait in vaults of glass and grief, burning slowly, feeding a court of beautiful starving things who want to be worshipped without ever having earned it. Your sister has been screaming in the dark beneath the mountain for six years, and no one heard her because everyone forgot the old names — except one woman."

* * *

Maren's hut sat where the salt marsh gave way to shingle, a crooked black shape against the grey water, and it smelled of iodine and smoke and something underneath both that made Coral's teeth ache — the smell, he realized, of true things.

She was older than any story about her, small and bent as driftwood, with eyes the pale blue of very old ice. She didn't greet him. She held out her palm, filled with coarse grey salt, and said:

"Taste."

"It'll just taste of salt."

"Everything tastes of salt if you're deaf," Maren said. "Taste it and listen."

He took a grain between finger and thumb, set it on his tongue. Salt — but under the salt, layered like sediment, like pages pressed together: a name. Not a word so much as a weight, a shape his mouth wanted to make. He nearly choked.

"That's—" 

"A god's name. One of nine. They're in the tide-salt, always have been. Salt remembers everything it has touched, and the sea has touched everything." She closed her fist. "The gods wrote themselves into the brine when they went silent, so that someone might someday read them again. I've spent forty years learning to read. Now I'm the last page standing between those names and oblivion, and my hands shake too badly to write."

"Why tell me?"

Maren looked at him for a long time. Outside, the light was failing; the marsh grass whispered its dry gossip.

"Because the dragon told me you'd come. Because your sister's soul is in that vault, and grief is the only fuel that burns hot enough to carry the names down through the dark. And because—" her voice roughened, "—because I once bargained with the court myself, boy, and I know exactly how it ends."

* * *

For seven nights, Maren taught him.

She spread salt across her table in patterns — spirals, ladders, the branching shapes of lungs and lightning — and showed him how each configuration held a name, how the names interlocked like gears, how a single true name spoken aloud in the right place could unmake a lock that had stood since before the mountain had a top.

"The court will offer you anything," she said, on the fourth night, pressing his fingers into a spiral of crystals until he felt the name hum in his knuckles. "That's not a threat. That's simply what they do. They are hollowed-out vessels, and they fill themselves with whatever walks through their door. Power. Forgiveness. Your sister's freedom. They'll offer it all, and every word will be true, and every gift will cost more than the thing itself."

"What did they offer you?"

Maren was quiet a long while. The lamp guttered. In its light her face was all shadow and bone.

"They offered me the gods' names whole. Every one, carved into my memory forever, so I'd never need salt or paper or another living teacher. I'd have been the most knowing witch in a thousand years." She laughed, a dry sound like reeds breaking. "I said yes, Coral. Of course I said yes. And the names they gave me were real — and they ate the rest of me to make room. I remember the gods perfectly now. Everything else comes and goes like weather. My mother's face. My children's names. Whether I had children." She looked at her own trembling hands. "The court doesn't steal from you, boy. It trades. It just always knows what a thing is worth better than you do."

* * *

On the seventh night, Coral climbed to the breakwater one last time.

Vess was smaller now — he could see the shoreline behind her flank where before there had only been scale and gold. She was burning herself down to make one final gift, and they both knew it.

"When I die," she said, "my fire goes with me. All but one ember. I can hold that ember long enough to light your way down, and not one moment longer. Whatever happens beneath the mountain, Coral Voss — whatever they offer, whatever you see, however loud your sister's voice cries out of that cage — you must move fast, and trust the salt, and trust no bargain."

"And if moving fast isn't enough?"

"Then be enough anyway." The great amber eye regarded him, and in it he saw reflected a small stooped man holding a basket of charms, five years of small lies, a lifetime of hiding — and something else, something the reflection couldn't contain. "Your sister sold her soul to save your shop, you know. Did you ever wonder why the debts vanished? She heard you talking to the gulls, boy. She believed in you when you couldn't believe in yourself. Grief is not a wound. It's an inheritance. Spend it well."

Vess exhaled — a long, slow tide of a breath — and from somewhere deep in her chest, an ember rose, drifting up through the dark like a falling star reversed.

It hung in the air before him, small and stubborn and impossibly bright.

"Go," said the dragon. "And when the fire gutters — run toward the light, not away from it."

* * *

In her hut above the marsh, Maren felt the ember kindle and knew the boy was descending. She poured a circle of salt around her table, all nine names arranged in their ancient order, and began to speak them — not for magic, but for remembrance, so the names would be warm in her mouth when he needed them passed on.

Halfway through the third name, she stopped.

The salt at the edge of her circle had gone black.

Something beneath the mountain had noticed the light coming down, and it knew her handwriting when it saw it.

"*Oh, child,*" she murmured, pressing her shaking palms flat against the darkened grains, as if she could reach through the earth itself. "*Whatever they've promised you — promise yourself faster.*"
