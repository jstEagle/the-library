## 3. The Court Beneath

The dragon's fire did not light the way so much as remember it.

Coral had expected flame — a torch's honest yellow, shadows thrown sharp against stone. What Vess gave him instead was older than that: a low amber glow that moved like memory, pooling in hollows, sliding along walls as if the mountain itself were being recalled into being around them. Where the light passed, Coral saw carvings. Doors. Stairways worn concave by feet that had stopped climbing centuries ago.

*Deeper,* Vess said, and her voice was smoke now, thinner each hour. *The court keeps its heart below the waterline. They wanted to be beneath even the tide's notice.*

They descended through the drowned quarter of the mountain — halls where salt water stood knee-deep and perfectly still, black as poured tar, holding their reflections with terrible fidelity. Coral kept his eyes on the fire-light and not on the water. In the reflections, he noticed, his own face was always a half-second behind him. He decided not to think about what that meant.

"How much farther?" he whispered.

*As far as it needs to be.* A guttering pulse, almost like laughter. *You still smell of gulls, beast-speaker. The vaults will know you before you see them. Everything down here is hungry, and hunger has a nose.*

He thought of Wren. Five years since the debt-broker's men had come for her — not her body, which they'd left cooling on the floorboards of their mother's house, but the rest of her, scooped out and carted away in a lead-lined box like herring. Sold to settle a debt of eleven crowns. He had been nineteen and too frightened to follow. He had spent five years being too frightened to follow.

Not tonight.

* * *

The soul-vaults announced themselves by silence.

Above, the mountain had murmured constantly — water ticking against stone, the deep groan of settling rock, Vess's labored breathing. Here, nothing. The quiet pressed on Coral's eardrums like depth. And threaded through it, faint as a hairline crack in glass, came a sound he knew from his childhood, from the nights Wren couldn't sleep: humming. Hundreds of voices, all slightly out of tune with one another, all humming the same half-remembered lullaby.

His chest seized. He knew that tune. Their mother had sung it off-key every winter solstice, and Wren had inherited the wrongness of it faithfully.

The vault door was not locked. It didn't need to be. It was a slab of grey stone carved with a single word in the old script Maren had taught him from the tide-salt — *ENOUGH*, it said, though whether it was warning or promise he couldn't tell. Beyond it, rows upon rows of cages receded into the dark, each one small as a birdcage, each one glowing with a soft interior light that shifted color as he passed. Grief-green. Rage-red. One that flickered a helpless, hopeful gold.

And there — third row, at eye height, exactly where he'd somehow known it would be — a cage burning the particular blue-grey of a winter sea.

"Wren," he said, and his voice cracked the vault's silence like ice going out.

The light inside flared. Pressed itself against the bars. And the humming faltered, stumbled, and reformed into a word:

*Coral?*

Five years. Her voice hadn't changed. That was the cruelest thing — souls apparently did not age, did not grow calluses or learn new songs. She sounded exactly as she had the night the debt-broker's men kicked in the door, and she said his name the way she'd said it then, half question, half accusation.

"I'm here." His hands found the cage. The metal was warm, blood-warm, and it pulsed under his palms like something alive trying to remember how. "I'm getting you out."

*You shouldn't have come.* But the light leaned into his hands anyway, starving for warmth. *Coral, you shouldn't—*

"They're already here," said a voice like honey poured over a blade. "Isn't that lovely."

* * *

The Undercourt did not walk so much as arrive, the way weather arrives.

There were nine of them, or perhaps one wearing nine shapes — tall figures robed in woven shadow, faces beautiful in the way that drowning is beautiful, all soft edges and blue light. They filled the vault without displacing anything. The soul-cages dimmed respectfully as they passed, the way candles bow toward a draft.

"You are the beast-speaker," said the one at the center. Its face was Wren's face, aged ten years, kind and tired and wise. "We have watched you, Coral Voss. Five years of charms sold for coppers. Five years of whispering to gulls in the dark because you were afraid to whisper anywhere else." It smiled with his sister's mouth. "So much power, hoarded so humbly. Do you know what humility looks like, from down here? It looks like waste."

Coral stepped between the court and Wren's cage. His back found the cold bars. "I've come for my sister."

"And you shall have her." The court spread its many hands, gracious as a host unveiling a feast. "That was never in question. We are not thieves, beast-speaker. We are *bankers*. Everything here was purchased honestly — grief, mostly, and desperation, and eleven-crown debts. Your sister's freedom costs nothing we mind giving." The beautiful faces leaned closer, all of them, all at once. "It is the *interest* we've come to discuss."

"Name it."

"Maren Salt-tongue. The witch who remembers." The court's voices braided together, harmonizing into something almost tender. "She carries the true names of the waking gods in her head like coins in a thief's purse. Give her to us — her name, her location, her habits, her *trust* — and we will open every cage in this room. Not just your sister. All of them. Four hundred souls, freed before dawn." A pause, delicate as a held breath. "And more. You have felt it, haven't you? Since the prayers began answering. The way your little magics have grown teeth. You lit that fuse yourself, Coral Voss — every charm, every whispered word to the birds. You are already becoming. We offer only to skip the ugly middle part. Godhood, freely given. No burning-out. No cost. Your sister at your right hand, alive again, whole again, and the two of you set above Greyharbor like a sun and its moon."

The temptation was not subtle. That was what frightened him — that he could see every joint and wire of it, could name the trick, and still feel it working on him like tide on a sandcastle. He thought of Wren laughing. He thought of walking through Greyharbor unhidden, unafraid, the widow Brannagh kneeling to him in the street. He thought of never again having to pretend, in front of anyone, that he was small.

He thought of Maren, who had looked at him across a table strewn with salt and said, *The gods went silent because everyone who remembered their names sold out. Someone has to be the last stubborn fool, lad. Might as well be us.*

"No," Coral said.

The court sighed — nine mouths, one disappointment.

"We hoped you'd say yes," it said, almost fondly. "But no was always the more profitable answer."

The vault went dark.

* * *

Not fully dark — Vess's ember still glowed in Coral's fist, a dying coal no bigger than a thumbnail, and by its miserable light he ran.

The soul-cages screamed as he passed, hundreds of voices waking at once, and he understood with sick clarity that the screaming was the point: the court was using them as alarm bells, tracking him by the wavefront of his own guilt. Behind him, the beautiful shapes flowed through the aisles without hurrying, unhurried the way a rising tide is unhurried, certain that everything above the waterline belongs to it eventually.

*Coral!* Wren's voice, torn thin with distance. *Coral, don't stop, don't—*

A cage snatched from the shelf ahead of him — not by a hand, by the dark itself — and swung into his path, blazing gold, that helpless hopeful color, and inside it he heard a voice he recognized from the market, the fish-hawker who'd died last spring, crying out *help me, help me* in a tone engineered to stop him mid-stride. It nearly worked. His feet tangled. His heart broke a little, hearing it.

"It's bait," he told himself, and Vess's ember flared once, agreeing, and he shouldered past.

The court's voices came from everywhere now, patient and loving. *You cannot carry four hundred souls, beast-speaker. You cannot even carry one. Look at your little light. Look how tired it is. Everything ends; we merely end things efficiently—*

"The names," Coral gasped, running, clutching the ember, the salt-pouch at his belt heavy as an anchor. Maren's voice in his memory: *Say the true name of a thing and it must answer. Say enough true names in one place and everything must answer at once.* The vault door ahead, the word ENOUGH carved above it. Beyond it, the long climb, the drowned halls, the black reflecting water.

Behind him, the court stopped pretending to be patient.

The dark itself reached out — he felt it close around his ankle like a hand of cold silt — and ahead of him, between him and the door, Wren's cage rose slowly from its shelf, drifting into the aisle like a lantern lowered into a well, blue-grey light spilling across the stones.

*Coral,* his sister said gently, from very far away. *Run.*

He looked at the cage. He looked at the door. He put his hand into the salt-pouch and began, shaking, to speak the first true name.
