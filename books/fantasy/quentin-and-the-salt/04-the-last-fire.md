## 4. The Last Fire

The vault breathed around them like a living thing.

Coral stood at its heart, in a chamber the size of Greyharbor's fish-market, and everywhere he looked there were cages — not iron, not wood, but woven light, each one holding a soul the way a lantern holds a moth. Thousands of them. Tens of thousands. They hung from the ceiling in slow constellations, drifted through the air like snow that had forgotten which way was down, pooled in the corners in shimmering drifts. And every one of them sang. Not with mouths. With the small, ceaseless hum of what they had been: mothers, masons, fishermen, children, thieves, priests. Five years of answered prayers, Coral understood now, had been nothing but this — the Undercourt spending stolen lives like coin, tossing scraps of divinity upward through the mountain's stone to buy the faith of a desperate city.

*Look what we have made,* the court had said, in a hundred beautiful voices. *Look what we could make of you.*

He had said no. He was still saying no. His voice was gone hoarse with it.

Beside him, low to the ground, Vess lay dying.

The dragon had carried him down through six miles of dark on wings that barely beat anymore, her fire guttering in her throat like a candle in a doorway. She was old beyond old — older than the outlawry, older than the silence of the gods, maybe older than the mountain itself — and she had spent the last of her strength getting him here because, she said, someone ought to witness the end properly. Now her scales had gone the grey of sea-weathered slate, and the light inside her came in fits and starts, and when she spoke it was less a voice than a warmth against his ear.

*Coral Voss.* Her name for him settled into his bones like a hand on a shoulder. *The salt. Quickly. Before they gather their courage.*

The Undercourt had withdrawn after his refusal — not defeated, he knew, but regrouping, the way a wave draws back before it decides how hard to hit. He could feel them above and around, pressing against the vault's boundaries like faces against glass. Beautiful. Hollow. Starving. He had seen their eyes up close, seen the places where their splendor had been eaten away from the inside by everything they'd swallowed, and he understood at last why the old gods had gone silent rather than become this.

His hands shook as he drew out the pouch at his belt. Maren's salt — tide-salt, gathered from the nine remembered shores, each grain carrying a true name folded into it like a letter never sent. She had taught him them one by one, in her hut that smelled of kelp and smoke, her cracked voice reciting names that predated the city, predated the law, predated the lie that magic belonged to anyone at all.

*You'll only get one chance to say them all,* she had warned. *And it will cost you what you love most.*

He had thought she meant Wren. He had been willing to pay it.

He knelt and poured the salt out onto the vault's floor in a long line, and then another crossing it, and then began the ring. The grains caught Vess's failing light and glimmered like a harbor at dusk. Around him the soul-cages stirred, pressing closer, drawn to something they recognized — the sound of their own names being remembered.

Above, the pressure increased. The court had found its courage.

*Little thief,* they said, all of them at once, and the chamber rang with it. *You would unmake heaven to spite us?*

"Not heaven," Coral said. "A prison. There's a difference, and you've forgotten it."

*We were gods once.*

"You were *people* once." His voice cracked, and he let it. "That's better."

He closed his eyes and began the names.

They came up out of him like water from a well — *Ossuin, who is the turning of the tide; Brannoc, who is the weight of the deep; Ilenne, who is the first grey light; Maren-of-the-Nine-Shores, who remembers* — and with each name the salt line blazed, and the vault shuddered, and somewhere far above the mountain groaned on its roots. The court screamed. It was not a beautiful sound anymore. All their loveliness burned away in that cry and left only the hunger underneath, vast and ancient and suddenly, terribly afraid.

*Stop,* they said. *Please. We will give you anything—*

"You already offered me everything," Coral said, and kept naming.

The last name was the hardest. Maren had saved it for last on purpose, and now, kneeling in the burning ring with his gift pouring out of him like blood from an opened vein, he understood why. Because the final name was not a god's name at all.

It was Wren's.

Not the soul-cage label the court had stamped her with — *asset, holding, fuel* — but her true name, the one only family used, the one she'd made him swear never to tell the debt-brokers so they couldn't sell it twice. *Wren Voss, who is the laugh in the kitchen; Wren Voss, who is the song under breath; Wren Voss, who is loved, who is loved, who is loved.*

The cage nearest him shattered like frost off a window.

And out of it rose his sister.

Not whole — souls don't come back whole after five years in a lantern — but *her*, unmistakably her, a brightness shaped like a girl of nineteen who had once dared him to eat a raw oyster and laughed until she cried when he did. She didn't speak. Souls past the veil rarely do. But she turned toward him, and the brightness bent in a way that he knew, that he would know anywhere, in any dark, in any world —

— the way she used to look at him across the dinner table when their mother said something foolish, the shared look, the *you-and-me* look —

— and Coral broke.

He kept naming anyway. That was the terrible arithmetic of it: grief did not stop the work, and the work did not stop the grief. He named every god and the salt took every god, and the cages burst one after another after another, a storm of released light, ten thousand souls rising through the breaking ceiling of the vault like a reversed snowfall, going home to whatever waits for the dead when no one is hoarding them.

And his gift went with them.

He felt it leave — the gull-speech, the charm-warmth, the quiet constant whisper of the world's living attention that had been his since childhood and his secret for five years. It poured out of him through the names, spent entirely, and did not refill, and as it drained he heard the world go silent in a way he hadn't known since he was small. No gull-voices. No salt-whispers. Just a man kneeling in a ruined vault, alone in the ordinary dark.

Vess's flame chose that moment to die.

It did not gutter out. It *gave* — one last great exhalation of gold that swept the chamber, caught the rising souls mid-flight and lit them like a migration of stars, and then folded inward, gently, the way a hand closes. When Coral turned, the dragon was already stone. Had perhaps always been stone, at the end, wearing the memory of fire.

The mountain exhaled.

He felt it through the floor — a long, shuddering release, six miles of stolen divinity collapsing inward on the hollow things that had hoarded it, the Undercourt folding down into the small dark space they had always truly occupied. No scream this time. Just a sigh, almost relieved, as if something that had been holding its breath for centuries finally let it go.

Then: silence. Real silence. And far above, faint as memory, the sound of gulls.

---

Coral climbed for a day and a night, or what felt like it, up through Vess's tunnel with his hands bleeding and his body empty. Twice he slept without meaning to. Twice he woke and kept climbing. There was no fire to guide him now, no gift to listen with. There was just the next handhold, and the next, which was, he supposed, how most people lived their whole lives.

He surfaced into grey dawn.

Greyharbor lay below him in the early light, roofs silvered with salt-frost, the harbor breathing its slow tide-breath, chimneys just beginning to wake. Ordinary. Utterly ordinary. He stood on the mountainside and looked at it and felt — nothing supernatural at all. Just love. Just the plain, unborrowed, unmagical love of a man for the ugly, stubborn, beautiful place that had made him.

They were waiting at the foot of the trail.

He saw them from a distance and his stomach dropped — the old fear, five years deep, the terror of being *seen*. But they weren't soldiers. They weren't the Godwardens or the debt-brokers or the magistrates. They were fishermen and fishwives, dockhands and net-menders, the widow Marta whose prayer had started everything, old boys from the salt-market, children perched on the seawall. Word had traveled, the way word does in a city that has spent five years feeling its prayers land like coins in a well and wondering why.

Marta stepped forward first. She looked at him a long moment — at the salt-crusted clothes, the bleeding hands, the hollowed-out face of a man who had given away the only precious thing he owned.

"You're the charm-seller," she said. "Voss. The one who talks to birds."

"I was," Coral said. His voice was rough as rope. "I'm not much of anything now."

The widow smiled, and it was not a kind smile exactly, but it was a true one. "Good," she said. "We've got enough of much-of-somethings. What we need is neighbors."

And that was how it happened — no proclamation, no pardon, no miracle. Old Tam from the docks clasped his shoulder hard enough to bruise. Someone pressed a cup of hot barley-tea into his hands. A girl of about seven looked up at him with the frank, appraising stare of the young and asked, "Did it hurt?"

"Everything," Coral said honestly.

"Was it worth it?"

He looked back at the mountain, at the thin thread of smoke still rising from its crown where a false heaven had come down. He looked at the city, waking, ordinary, free. He looked at his own hands, which would never again hear the gulls say good morning.

"Yeah," he said. "It was."

* * *

From her hut at the tideline, Maren felt the change come in on the morning tide.

She was very old, and she had stopped pretending otherwise years ago, and these days she mostly sat by her window and watched the water and argued cheerfully with the ghosts of everyone she'd ever known. So she was awake when the sea did something it had not done in three hundred years.

It said thank you.

Not in words. In salt. Every grain on her windowsill, every crystal in her stores, every fleck drying on the rocks outside — they all turned, all at once, toward the mountain, the way flowers turn toward sun. And in that turning she read the news the way she read everything: Ossuin freed. Brannoc freed. Ilenne walking the first grey light again, unhurried, unowned. The nine shores remembering themselves. And threaded through it all, fading even as she felt it, a small bright signature she knew intimately — the mark of a beast-speaker's gift, spent down to nothing, given wholly, gladly, gone.

Maren laughed until she cried, which at her age took some doing.

"Well," she said to the empty room, to the ghosts, to the gods coming home like gulls at dusk. "So the boy did it after all."

She got up, creaking, and put the kettle on. There would be visitors soon — there always were, now that the world had remembered its own name — and the first of them, she suspected, would be a hollow-eyed young man with salt in his hair and no magic left, looking for someone who might teach him what a person was for, now that the wonders were done.

She'd wondered the same thing once, sixty years ago, when her own gift burned out saving something smaller and no less important.

She had found out.

She put out two cups.
