## 1. The Night the Stars Went Out

The night the stars went out, Vesper Blackwood was mending a fence.

He would remember that afterward — the absurd smallness of it. The splinter in his thumb, the smell of cut pine and cold ash drifting down from the kilns, the way Hollowmere's single lamp-moon hung fat and yellow above the eastern ridge like a coin nailed to a ceiling. He had lived nineteen years beneath that coin. He had never once thought to question why there was nothing else up there. No other lights. No slow wheel of distant fires. Just the lamp-moon, and the dark, and the wards humming along the valley's rim like a held breath.

Maerlin had told him once, when he was small enough to still ask questions: *The sky is shy here, boy. The valley keeps us safe from it.*

Vesper had believed him. That was the cruelest part, he would think later. Not the wards, not the theft, not even the dying — but how easily he had believed.

The fence post slipped from his hands when the first ward failed.

There was no sound, exactly. There was a *wrongness* — a note dropping out of a chord he hadn't known he was hearing his whole life. The hum along the ridgeline stuttered, guttered, and died, and every hair on Vesper's arms rose as though the air itself had flinched. Down in the village, dogs began to howl. The lamp-moon flickered once, twice, like a candle in a draft —

— and went out.

Not dimmed. *Extinguished.* And with it went the whole painted ceiling of Hollowmere's sky, the soft glow the valley had always worn at night, and what was left underneath was not darkness but *blackness*, an absolute and starving black, and for one heartbeat Vesper saw what no soul in Hollowmere had seen in three hundred years:

Stars. Thousands of them. Burning through the torn veil like eyes opening.

Then those too began to go out — snuffed west to east, a tide of extinguishing rolling across heaven — and Vesper dropped his hammer and ran.

* * *

Maerlin's cottage sat at the valley's edge, closest to the border stones, because Maerlin had never been afraid of the border. Vesper took the sheep path at a dead sprint, lungs full of pine-smoke and terror, and burst through the door without knocking because knocking belonged to a world that still existed.

The old man lay on the floor before his hearth, and the hearth was burning *wrong* — flames standing straight and silver, casting no heat. Maerlin's right hand was extended toward Vesper, palm up, and in it something shone. Not firelight. Not lamplight. A sigil, seared into the skin of his palm, a knot of interlocking lines that moved when Vesper looked at it directly, turning like a key in a lock made of light.

"Boy." Maerlin's voice was gravel and blood. "Come here. Come *here*."

Vesper fell to his knees beside him. The old man's skin was gray as valley stone, and where the sigil's light touched his wrist, his veins showed silver beneath, branching like frost on glass. "What's happening? The moon went out — the wards —"

"The wards are dead." Maerlin laughed, and it cost him; he coughed red onto his beard. "I killed them. Took me forty years to find the courage. They were never protection, Vesper. They were bars. We stole the sky to end a war, and we called the theft *safety*, and our grandparents' grandparents called it safety, and generations have lived and died inside a locked box and thanked their jailers for the quiet."

"Stolen — stolen *what*?"

"This." Maerlin seized Vesper's hand with a strength that should have been impossible, and pressed his own burning palm against it. "The Sigil of the Returned Sky. The keystone of the old war. Hollowmere tore it out of heaven, and heaven has been blind and bleeding ever since, and now the wards fail and everything that was paused comes due."

The pain was instant and total. It was not like being burned; it was like being *written*. Vesper felt the sigil sink through his palm, through muscle and bone, threading up his arm in silver filaments, and he screamed, and Maerlin held on, whispering through the boy's screams:

"Carry it home. Do you hear me? Carry it home. Up past the lowlands, past the Ashfen, to Skyhold — the old observatory on the Spine. The sigil belongs to the sky, and only a bearer can return it, and I am out of time and you are the last hand I trust."

"I don't want it," Vesper gasped. "Take it back — take it *back* —"

"No one takes it back. That is its mercy and its curse." Maerlin's grip gentled. His eyes were going distant, fixed on something above the ruined ceiling, above the black sky, above everything. "They will come for you. Crown wardens. Serah Dain's hounds. They will call you thief and heretic and worse. Let them. You have spent your whole life being invisible, boy, being *safe*, being small. Invisibility is over. Grieve me fast and run faster."

His hand slid away. The silver in his veins dimmed, thread by thread, like a loom running down.

Outside, far off across the valley, horns began to sound — deep, disciplined, wrong. Not Hollowmere horns. Warden horns.

Vesper knelt by his mentor's body while the silver crawled up past his elbow, and he did not grieve fast, because he could not grieve at all yet. Grief needed somewhere to live, and his whole chest was occupied with terror.

Then the first warden's lantern-light crested the ridge, and terror made room, and resolve moved in like an heir claiming a house.

* * *

He ran north, because north was the border, and the border was death, and death was suddenly the smaller fear.

The dead lands beyond the last farmstead had always been shown to children as a warning: gray soil where nothing grew, boundary stones carved with warnings in a script no one read anymore, air that tasted of cinders. *Beyond this line the world ends,* the elders said. *Beyond this line the war still burns.* Vesper had touched a boundary stone exactly once, at nine years old, and Maerlin had caught his wrist hard enough to bruise and said, with real fear in his voice: *Never again. Never, do you understand me?*

Now Vesper crossed the line at a stumbling run with warden-horns behind him and a god's signature burning in his blood.

The moment he passed the stones, the change came over him like stepping through a waterfall. The air thickened. Smells arrived that Hollowmere had never contained — wet earth, woodsmoke from distant fires, something green and enormous and alive. Wind. Real wind, moving with intent, tugging his hair toward a horizon he had never seen. Above him, through rents in the dying veil, a handful of stars still burned, and they seemed — he could not help it — they seemed to be looking at him.

His left arm ached to the shoulder. When he pushed back his sleeve, the veins beneath his skin glowed faintly silver, a map of light pointing northwest, toward the Spine, toward Skyhold, toward *home*, whatever home meant now that home was a word Maerlin had used with his dying breath.

Behind him, lanterns spilled over the ridge. He heard the wardens' voices, sharp and carrying across the dead ground:

"There! On the border line — the bearer's crossed!"

"He can't have crossed. *No one* crosses."

"Captain Dain wants him alive. Form up. *Move.*"

Vesper ran. Over the gray soil, past the last boundary stone, into a world three hundred years forbidden, with the sigil singing silver in his arm and the stars dying overhead and grief finally catching him mid-stride so that he ran weeping and did not stop, could not stop, because stopping meant the wardens, and the wardens meant the end of the only promise he had ever been trusted to keep.

*Carry it home.*

The lowlands opened before him, vast and starless and utterly unknown, and somewhere out in that darkness, things were waking that had slept since the sky was stolen.

One of them, he would learn soon enough, carried a sword and no banner.

Another prayed to the very stars going out above his head.

But that was ahead of him. For now there was only the running, and the burning, and the black, and the first true thought of his new life, arriving whole and unafraid:

*I am visible now. Let them see me come.*
