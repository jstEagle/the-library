## 2. A Knight Without a Banner

The lowlands smelled of wet earth and rot, and Vesper had never been so grateful for anything in his life.

He had crossed the border at dawn — or what he still called dawn, though the sky above the valley's rim gave no light back now. The wards had failed in the night with a sound like a bell cracking, and Maerlin's last breath was still warm on his skin, and his right hand still ached where the sigil had burned itself into him like a coal pressed into wax. He walked because stopping meant thinking, and thinking meant grief, and grief would fold him into the mud and leave him there.

The valley behind him had never once let him out. He knew this now the way he knew his own heartbeat: Hollowmere had not sheltered its people. It had kept them. Every childhood game played beneath a ceiling of false stars, every harvest festival, every whispered rule about the border stones — all of it a cage painted to look like home.

And now the cage was open, and the thing that had made it a cage was burning in his blood.

*Carry it home,* Maerlin had said. *Home* meaning what? The sky? The stars that no longer shone? Vesper flexed his fingers and watched, for the hundredth time, the faint silver tracery beneath the skin of his palm — veins of light, thin as spider silk, pulsing in time with something that was not quite his heart.

By midday he heard the hounds.

Not true hounds — warden-hounds, things bred in the Crown kennels from shadow and appetite, silent runners that did not bark but *hummed*, a low note that vibrated in the teeth. Vesper felt them before he saw them: the sigil flared hot against his ribs, a warning written in pain.

He ran.

The lowlands were flat and flooded, fields gone to marsh since the sky died — nothing up there to pull the rain along, Ivetta would later tell him, nothing to steer the seasons — and his boots filled with water and his lungs with fire. Behind him the humming swelled. Ahead, a stand of dead alders rose from the water like drowned men.

He nearly made it. He almost reached the trees when the first warden stepped out from between them.

She was tall, armored in black-lacquered scale, and her face under the helm was calm as a ledger. Serah Dain. He didn't know her name yet. He only knew her eyes went straight past his face to his right hand, as if she could see through flesh to the fire underneath.

"Sigil-bearer," she said, and drew her blade. "In the name of the Crown, you will—"

The arrow took her horse, not her. A second arrow split the warden's shield-arm guard; a third pinned her cloak to the alder trunk as she lunged. Vesper stumbled backward into the shallows as figures moved among the dead trees — two of them, one huge and armored but wearing no colors, no crest, a knight's harness scoured blank, and beside him a slighter figure in grey robes whose eyes were fixed not on the wardens but on the empty sky.

"Run," said the blank knight, in a voice like gravel settling. "We'll hold."

Vesper ran.

He did not stop until the humming faded behind him and the marsh gave way to scrub hills, and then his legs simply quit, and he sat down hard in the bracken and shook. The sigil pulsed. Somewhere in the silver tracery of his hand he could feel it *listening*, tasting the air, and he hated it and clutched it closer all at once, because it was the last piece of Maerlin he would ever have.

They found him at dusk. Of course they found him. The blank knight and the grey-robed woman, walking out of the failing light like something out of an old story.

"You're the one who crossed the valley border," the woman said. Not a question. Her eyes were pale, colorless almost, the eyes of someone who had spent decades looking upward. "I'm Ivetta. This is Cassian." She crouched, peering at his hand without touching it. "May I?"

Vesper should have said no. Strangers, arrows, wardens — every instinct Maerlin had drilled into him said trust nothing beyond the border stones. But the border stones were ash now, and so were all his other rules.

Ivetta turned his hand over gently. When she saw the sigil, she made a sound he had never heard a person make — a small, broken note, like a hymn cut off mid-verse.

"It's real," she whispered. "All my life I prayed to a sky I couldn't see, and it's *real*, and it's in your *hand*."

"The sky went dark three nights ago," Cassian said. His armor was scarred, mismatched, a knight's panoply assembled from salvage. No banner, no badge. "Every star-priest in every order felt it. She felt it before anyone." He nodded at Ivetta. "She walked out of her cloister the same hour. Said the dark had a shape. Said it came from the east."

"From Hollowmere," Vester said quietly. "It always came from Hollowmere."

He told them. All of it — the wards, the stolen stars, Maerlin dying on the observatory floor with the sigil blazing in his grip, pressing it into Vesper's palm like a father handing over a debt. He told them about the cage dressed up as safety, the generations born and buried under a false heaven. His voice cracked on Maerlin's name and he let it.

Cassian listened the way soldiers listen: completely, without interruption. When Vesper finished, the knight was silent a long moment.

"I refused an order once," he said at last. "A village that hadn't rebelled. The Crown wanted it made an example. I said no." He turned his hands over — calloused, ordinary. "They took my rank, my banner, my name from the rolls. Kept my sword arm, because good killers are scarce." A grim half-smile. "So I understand a little, about cages."

"We can't stay here," Ivetta said. "Serah Dain commands that company. She doesn't stop. Ever. And if the sigil is what I think it is—"

"What do you think it is?" Vesper asked.

She looked at him, and her pale eyes held something terrible and bright.

"I think it's a star, child. Or the heart of one. I think your valley reached up and *tore it out of heaven*, and everything since — the war, the dark, the wardens hunting you — is the sky trying to take back what was stolen." She closed his fingers over the silver tracery, gentle as a benediction. "And I think it's killing you while it waits."

That night they made camp in a hollow beneath a ruined wayshrine. Vesper slept fitfully, waking each time the sigil flared, and twice he woke to find Cassian sitting awake at the hollow's rim, watching the black horizon, sword across his knees. The second time, the knight spoke without turning.

"You keep waiting for us to leave. Or sell you." It wasn't a question either. "I'd wait too, in your place."

"Why are you helping me?" Vesper asked the dark.

Cassian was quiet long enough that Vesper thought he wouldn't answer. Then: "Because I've spent nine years learning exactly how much it costs to obey the wrong people. This time I'd rather pay for doing right." He settled back against a stone. "Sleep, sigil-bearer. Dawn's coming. Even if we can't see it."

* * *

Serah Dain knelt beside the pinned cloak on the alder trunk and did not move for a long time.

Three arrows. Clean groupings. A bowman of considerable skill, or considerable desperation — often the same thing. She pulled the shafts free, catalogued the fletching: grey goose, temple-cut. Star-priest work. So. The thief had found the exiles already, or the exiles had found him.

"Captain?" The young warden shifted at her shoulder, uneasy. "The trail goes north, toward the old Skyhold road. Orders?"

Skyhold. Serah looked east, toward the valley that had swallowed the sky, toward the sealed archives and the orders she carried in a lacquered case against her heart — orders that grew heavier every mile. Hunt the bearer. Recover the sigil. *Terminate all witnesses.*

Her superiors had used the word *terminate* the way other men used *sweep the floor*. She had stopped asking questions years ago, because asking questions in the Crown's service was a slow way of resigning.

But the sky had gone out three nights ago. And whatever was in that boy's hand had done it — or ended it, or both.

"North," Serah said, standing. "Double pace. And send word ahead — I want the Skyhold road watched." She flexed her bandaged arm, where the stranger's arrow had kissed bone. "No one burns witnesses on my watch until I know why they're burning them."

The young warden blinked. "Captain?"

"You heard me." She turned her horse north, toward the dark. "Move."

* * *

On the fourth night, the sigil began to climb.

Vesper woke screaming, though he didn't remember screaming — only the light, silver fire crawling up his forearm like ivy made of moonlight, rewriting him vein by vein. Ivetta was there before his eyes fully opened, her hands hovering over the tracery, murmuring in a language older than any scripture he knew.

"Hold still. Hold *still*."

"It burns—"

"It's *growing*," she said, and there was awe in it, and horror. "Look."

He looked. From wrist to elbow, his veins glowed faintly silver, and where the glow passed his skin showed the mapwork of constellations he had never seen but somehow recognized — patterns with names he didn't know and couldn't forget.

"It's not just resting in you," Ivetta breathed. "It's *becoming* you. The longer you carry it, the less of you there'll be to carry it away from." Her pale eyes lifted to his. "Vesper. We have maybe weeks. Perhaps less. You cannot return it alone — the altar rites require more than one pair of hands, more than one life. But alone—"

"—alone it eats me whole," he finished.

Cassian appeared at the edge of the firelight, drawn by the glow. He looked at the silvered arm, at Vesper's white face, at Ivetta's expression — and Vesper saw the moment the knight understood everything, and decided anyway.

"Well," Cassian said, sitting down heavily and drawing his whetstone from his belt. "Then we'd best get you to Skyhold before it finishes the job. Both of us will be there." He began working the stone along his blade, steady as a heartbeat. "Whatever it costs, it won't be paid alone. That's not how this ends."

Vesper stared at him — this bannerless knight sharpening his sword by firelight for a stranger's sake — and something in his chest that had been clenched since Maerlin died finally, slightly, loosened.

Trust, he thought. Maerlin had never taught him what to do with it. There had never been anyone outside the cage worth trusting.

Above them, the sky stayed black. But far to the north, on the horizon, Vesper thought he saw — or dreamed he saw — a single point of light flicker, like a star remembering itself, like a promise.

Toward Skyhold. Toward the truth of what the valley had stolen.

Toward whatever price waited at the top of the world.
