## 3. What the Valley Stole

Ivetta had prayed to the stars every night of her life, and for nine days the stars had not answered.

She felt their silence like a missing tooth — the tongue returning again and again to the absence. The sky above the lowlands was a smooth black slate, unbroken by a single light, and she had begun to understand that this was not night as the world had always known it. This was a wound.

Now, on the tenth evening, the wound led them home.

Skyhold rose out of the mountainside like the ribcage of some god that had lain down and died there. Ivetta saw it first from the switchback trail: a crown of broken arches against the dark, the great dome of the observatory split open like an egg, its shattered lens spilled down the slope in glittering shards that caught even starless moonlight and made it strange. Wind came off the peaks carrying the smell of old stone and older snow, and beneath it something else — something burnt and sweet, like incense burned down to nothing.

"It's real," Vesper said behind her. His voice was hoarse. He had barely spoken in three days, and when he did speak it was with one hand pressed flat against his chest, over the place where the sigil lived under his skin. "I thought — Maerlin only ever told me stories about Skyhold. He said it was a fable."

"Most true things are," Cassian said.

The knight climbed ahead of them, moving with the economy of a man who had spent years learning which ground betrayed him. His banner-less cloak snapped in the wind; his sword arm hung loose and ready out of habit rather than fear. Ivetta watched him glance back at Vesper every few steps — quick, unobtrusive, the way a man checks on something he has decided matters more than himself. She had seen that look before, in monasteries, on the faces of those who tended relics they knew would one day consume them.

The climb took the last of the light. By the time they passed beneath the first arch, the world was colorless, and Skyhold's interior opened around them like the inside of a bell: vast, hollow, waiting to be struck.

They crossed a floor of mosaic — thousands upon thousands of tiles forming a map of the heavens, constellations Ivetta knew by heart rendered in lapis and gold. She knelt without meaning to. Her fingers found the Hunter, the Weeping Sisters, the Lantern at the World's Edge. All of it correct. All of it ancient.

"This was no heretic's den," she whispered. "This was the motherhouse. Before the schism. Before—" She stopped, because the sentence ended somewhere terrible.

Before the stars went out.

* * *

The archive lay beneath the dome, behind doors of black iron that Cassian forced with a crowbar and three minutes of profanity. Inside, dust hung in the air like held breath. Shelves of vellum rose into darkness. And at the center of the chamber, on a plinth of white stone, stood an empty setting — a cradle of silver filigree shaped exactly like the mark burning on Vesper's palm.

Vesper stopped walking.

Ivetta watched him stare at it, watched his face do the thing faces do when a person understands something all at once and wishes they didn't. She went past him to the lectern by the wall, where a single book remained, its cover stamped with the sigil of the old star-priests — her order, before her order disgraced itself, or was disgraced, she had never learned which.

She read. The dust of centuries came away under her thumb. And the truth came up out of the pages like water rising in a flooded mine.

"Hollowmere wasn't a refuge," she said. Her voice sounded far away to her own ears. "It was a vault."

Cassian turned. "Say that again."

"The war — the one the histories call the Ember War, the one that 'ended' four hundred years ago — it never ended. It *paused*. The founders of Hollowmere were the last wardens of the old covenant, and they were losing." She turned the pages faster. "So they did the unthinkable. They reached up into heaven itself and took the Sigil of the First Light — the keystone of all celestial magic — and they pulled it down. They built the valley around it. They warded it so thoroughly that no power could cross the border, theirs or anyone else's. They blinded the sky so no enemy could navigate by it, no spell could draw on it. They ended the war by stealing the battlefield."

"And everyone born inside the valley?" Cassian asked quietly.

"Caged. Every generation since. Born, living, dying inside a prison whose walls they couldn't see, told it was *safety*." Ivetta looked up at Vesper. He had gone very still, the way animals go still. "Your whole life, Vesper. Everyone's whole life. Four hundred years of stolen skies."

"No," Vesper said.

"Vesper—"

"*No.*" His voice cracked across the archive. "Maerlin wouldn't — he raised me, he taught me, he—" He stopped. His hand was shaking against his chest. "He said the valley kept us safe. He said the wards were mercy."

"They were," Ivetta said gently. "Mercy and a cage are often the same shape. But listen to me, because this is the part that matters." She drew a breath. "The sigil doesn't belong in flesh. It belongs in the sky. The book is clear: it can be returned, but the return requires a life poured through it. A bearer who carries it back must give everything — every year, every breath, all of it, spent in a single act. That is why Maerlin put it in you dying instead of letting the wardens take it. Not to save you. To spend you."

The silence that followed had weight. Ivetta heard the wind moving through the broken dome overhead, moaning across the shattered lens-glass like a mourner.

Vesper sat down slowly on the plinth's step, in the dust of four hundred years, and put his face in his hands.

* * *

Serah Dain found them an hour later, though "found" was generous — she had been following them since the lowlands, and they had all known it, and none of them had said it aloud, because saying it aloud would have meant deciding what to do about it.

She came through the archive doors with six wardens at her back and her crossbow leveled, and the lamplight caught the silver of her captain's badge. "Sigil-bearer," she said. "Step away from the plinth."

"You're too late for orders, Captain," Cassian said. He had moved — smoothly, without any apparent decision to move — until he stood between Serah and Vesper. "Read the room. Or better, read the book. Your Crown sent you to retrieve a weapon. There is no weapon here. There's only a debt."

"My orders are the sigil and the bearer, delivered alive to the capital."

"And then what? They'll cut it out of him?" Ivetta stepped forward, arms spread. "Captain Dain. I am a priestess of the old rite, however fallen. I am telling you what the texts say. If they open that boy to take the sigil, the sigil dies, and the sky stays dead forever, and whatever paused war your masters think they're managing starts again in the dark. Is that what you carried those orders up a mountain for?"

Serah's jaw tightened. Ivetta had watched her for ten days now, through a spyglass and across riverbanks, and she had catalogued the small wars always running behind the warden-captain's eyes. Duty against doubt. Orders against evidence. Tonight, in this dead holy place, the evidence was winning — Ivetta could see it winning — but Serah Dain had survived this long precisely because she did not let things win.

"Move away from the boy, Sir Cassian," she said. "Last time."

"I was stripped of my rank," Cassian said. "You can't address me as sir. That was the point of the ceremony — remember? You attended." He smiled, and there was no humor in it. "But I'll tell you what I told the general at Redford Ford, when he ordered me to burn a village of civilians to flush out rebels. I told him no. And I'll tell you the same. No."

"Then you die here."

"Yes," Cassian agreed pleasantly. "Probably. But here's the arithmetic, Captain, and I want you to actually do it, because you're smarter than the men who wrote your orders." He spread his arms, unhurried, offering himself. "Kill me, and you gain nothing. I'm an exile; no one will mourn, no one will avenge. But in the time it takes your six crossbows to do the work, the priestess wakes the old wards of this observatory — they'll answer her, the motherhouse recognizes its daughters — and the boy walks out the north face into snow your wardens won't survive. So trade me. My life for his escape. It's the best offer anyone will make you this year."

"Cassian, no—" Vesper was on his feet.

"Sit down, lad." Cassian didn't turn around. "You've got something left to carry. I don't. This is the only thing I'm still good for."

And Ivetta, watching, understood with terrible clarity that he meant it — that the knight had done this arithmetic long ago, on some road, in some rain, and had simply been waiting for the sum to come due. Fury rose in her, hot and useless: fury at the founders who stole the sky, fury at the Crown that hunted children, fury at this brave stupid man who thought his life was currency and nothing more.

"That's not how it ends," she said aloud, to all of them, to the dead house around them. "None of you gets to decide the price alone. That's how we got here. That's how the valley happened — someone decided alone, and everyone else paid for four hundred years."

Serah's crossbow dipped — a fraction, an inch, a tremor she would deny later.

And then the lamplight guttered, and every shard of broken lens-glass on the mountainside outside lit silver at once, and Ivetta felt it through the soles of her boots: the sigil waking, answering the place that had once held it, pulling at Vesper like a tide going out.

Vesper cried out — one short, sharp sound — and staggered toward the plinth, toward the empty silver cradle, his veins showing through his skin like bright wire.

"Vesper!" Cassian lunged for him.

Above them, through the split dome, the dead sky began — very faintly, very terribly — to glow.
