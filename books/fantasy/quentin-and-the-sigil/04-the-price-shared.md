## 4. The Price, Shared

The sky-altar had been beautiful once. Vesper could see its ghost beneath the ruin — the sweep of white stone like a cupped hand, the ring of pillars that had held up a ceiling of glass and starlight, now shattered into a field of glittering shards that crunched underfoot like frost. The mountain wind came through the broken dome and smelled of iron and old snow.

He knelt at the altar's heart, where a shallow basin had been carved in the shape of an eight-pointed star. The sigil burned in his palm, patient as a coal.

*Carry it home,* Maerlin had said.

Home was here. Home was this broken bowl of stone under an open wound of sky.

Behind him, Ivetta was arranging her last candles at the rim of the basin, their small flames bowing in the wind. Cassian stood at the shattered doorway with his sword drawn, watching the stair — because Serah Dain was coming. They all knew she was coming. She had been coming for three chapters of his life, it seemed, since the night the wards fell and the stars went out and everything invisible ended.

"Vesper." Ivetta's voice was gentle, but he heard the fear folded inside it, thin as gold leaf. "Say the words once more before you begin."

"I know them." He did. He had known them since Hollowmere, since they'd surfaced in his blood like drowned things rising. *What was stolen must be returned. What was returned must be paid for.* He flexed his fingers. The silver in his veins had climbed past his elbow now, filigree-bright beneath the skin, and when he moved he could feel it — a cold singing, like wire drawn through his marrow.

"You don't have to do this alone," Ivetta said.

"Yes, I do." He tried to smile. It came out crooked. "That's rather the whole arithmetic of it, isn't it? One sigil, one bearer, one life poured out. The old priests designed it that way on purpose. A price no one would share is a price no one can refuse."

"That's not arithmetic," Cassian said from the doorway, without turning around. "That's cruelty wearing arithmetic's clothes."

And then the wind changed, and Serah Dain came up the stair.

She looked worse than the last time he'd seen her — armor scorched, one arm bound, her warden-grey cloak torn to ribbons by whatever had hunted them both through the lowlands. But her sword was steady, and her eyes were steady, and those eyes went first to Vesper kneeling at the altar, then to the sigil burning openly in his open hand.

"So it's true," she said. "The valley stole the sky."

"You've read the archives too," Ivetta said quietly.

"I've *been* the archives." Serah stepped forward, boots grinding glass. "I grew up under a black sky and called it protection. I spent my life guarding a prison and calling it peace. Do you know what that does to a person, boy, when she finally sees the bars?" Her jaw tightened. "It makes her want to burn something down. Preferably the people who built it."

"Then let him finish," Cassian said. "Stand aside, Captain."

"I didn't come to stop him." Serah's voice dropped, and for the first time since he'd known her — hunted her, been hunted *by* her — Vesper heard something underneath the command: exhaustion, and grief, and a terrible kind of hope. "I came to watch. Someone should witness it. That's what wardens are for, in the end. Witnessing."

Vesper looked down at the sigil. It pulsed against his palm like a second heart, and he understood suddenly that it had always been waiting — not for courage, exactly, but for consent. Maerlin hadn't given him a burden. He'd given him a question.

*I'm ready,* Vesper thought, and pressed his bleeding hand into the star-shaped basin.

The world went white.

Not light — *white*, the color of the page before the story, the color of Hollowmere's false dawn. The sigil left him the way a river leaves its banks: all at once, everywhere, taking the shape of him with it. He felt his veins unspooling into silver rivers, felt his heartbeat slow to the rhythm of something vast turning over in its sleep, felt the price arrive as it had been promised — total, immediate, final. His knees buckled. His vision narrowed to a coin of dark. Somewhere very far away, Ivetta was screaming his name.

*So this is what it costs,* he thought, with the strange calm of the nearly-finished. *One boy. Fair trade for a sky.*

Then two hands closed over his.

One was calloused and sword-hard — Cassian's, still bandaged from the fight on the stair, still trembling, gripping Vesper's wrist as if letting go were the one order he would never again obey. The other was small and warm and smelled of candle smoke — Ivetta, her lips moving through the old return-liturgy, feeding the words into him like breath into a drowning man.

"No," Vesper gasped. "The cost — you'll —"

"We know the cost," Cassian said. His voice was wrecked and certain. "We read the same archives. The rite never said *one*. That was just what everyone assumed. What everyone was *told*." His grip tightened. "A price no one will share, Vesper. So we're sharing it."

"What was stolen was stolen by many," Ivetta whispered, tears freezing on her cheeks in the white wind. "Let it be returned by many. Let it be *carried* by many. Old words, Vesper. Older than the theft. They were there all along, waiting for someone to say them."

The sigil turned in the basin like a sun deciding whether to rise.

And then it split.

Not broke — *split*, the way light splits through a prism, one flame becoming three without becoming less. Vesper felt the terrible river divide into three channels, felt the burning pour away through Cassian's scarred hands and Ivetta's candle-scented ones, felt the price become bearable precisely because it was being borne by more than him. Not escaped. Not refused. *Shared.* It hurt — saints, it hurt, all three of them crying out as the silver blazed up their arms — but it was the hurt of carrying something heavy together, the hurt that means you are not alone under the weight.

"NOW," Ivetta shouted, to the sigil, to the sky, to every founder who had ever thought one life was a fair price for a prison. "GIVE IT BACK!"

The sigil rose.

It went up through the shattered dome like a reversed falling star, trailing silver, and the sky — the long-wounded, long-blind sky — *opened*. Vesper watched it happen on his back in the snow-dusted ruins, Cassian's hand crushing his, Ivetta's voice breaking on the last line of the liturgy: the darkness peeling back like a bandage, and beneath it, one by one, the stars.

They came back the way memory comes back — slowly, then all at once. First a handful, tentative. Then constellations, spilling across the black like coins from a cut purse. Then whole rivers of them, the great wheeling deeps of the old sky, so many and so bright that the ruined observatory filled with light and the snow on the mountains blushed blue and Serah Dain fell to her knees in the doorway with her sword flat on the stones before her, weeping at a sky she had never once seen in her armored life.

The war's embers, wherever they still smoldered in the world, cooled. Vesper felt it happen — felt the sigil's vast attention withdraw from the wounds of the earth, felt the old anger settle into old sorrow, felt sorrow itself begin, at last, to become memory. Somewhere far below, in valleys that had forgotten the word, dawn was going to mean something again.

Cassian laughed. It was a broken, wonderful sound. Ivetta simply lay down among the shards and stared upward, her lips still moving, though the liturgy was finished and what she said now had no words, only names — the dead of Skyhold, the dead of the war, Maerlin, all of them, greeted one by one by the returning light.

Vesper looked at his palm. The sigil was gone. In its place, faint as a scar from a childhood fall, lay a small silver star — and on Cassian's hand, and on Ivetta's, the same mark, three points of one constellation.

Serah Dain rose. She sheathed her sword — deliberately, formally, the way a soldier ends a watch — and looked at the three of them lying in the ruins like children after a storm.

"The Crown will send more wardens," she said. "Wardens who still believe the lie. You'll need to be gone by morning, and you'll need someone who knows which roads the Crown doesn't watch." She paused, and something almost like a smile touched her weathered face. "Fortunately, you'll have me."

Vesper stared up at her, then at the sky — the true sky, endless and alive — and found that he was laughing too, or crying, or both.

Three marks. Three carriers. One home, finally found in each other.

Far to the north, beyond the mountains, a single new star kindled where no star had ever been recorded — bright, deliberate, watching. Ivetta saw it first. She went very still.

"Vesper," she said softly. "Someone just answered."
