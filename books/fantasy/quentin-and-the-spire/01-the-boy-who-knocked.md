## 1. The Boy Who Knocked

The rain-charms were lying again.

Wendell Marchetti could tell from across the shop, the way a man knows his own children are fibbing — by the set of their shoulders, by the guilty little shimmer they gave off when they thought no one was looking. He had made forty-two of them that week, and if pressed he would admit, in the privacy of his own skull, that perhaps nine of them contained anything resembling actual weather-magic. The rest were glass beads and river-stones and dried lavender wrapped in wax paper, sold to Corvane's anxious citizens at three coppers apiece because three coppers was cheaper than dread, and dread was what this city traded in most reliably.

"Charm's not working," grumbled Old Mistress Pell, who came every Tuesday to say exactly that, and every Tuesday to buy another one anyway. She held the little glass bead up to the lamplight as though she might catch it confessing. "My knees say rain's coming Thursday and my knees have never once been wrong."

"Then buy the charm for Wednesday," said Wendell, wrapping it, "and you'll have rain on time for once in your life."

She snorted, paid him, and shuffled out into a street so dry it creaked. Corvane had gone eleven days without weather of any kind. No rain, no wind, not even cloud — just a hard blue nothing stretched over the rooftops like a lid on a pot. The wardens said it was a blessing. The priests said it was providence. Wendell, who knew precisely what still air meant, and whose left shoulder blade had been aching for eleven days like an old debt come due, said nothing at all, which was the only thing he'd said consistently for twelve years.

He turned the sign to CLOSED and began putting away his wares: luck-knots that imparted mild optimism; salt rings against bad dreams, which worked only because salt was pleasant to look at; and the small brass bells he sold as "household harmonizers," which did nothing except ring, beautifully, when the wind touched them. There had been no wind in eleven days. The bells hung silent in the window like a row of small open mouths.

Twelve years ago, Wendell Marchetti had called down lightning with his bare hands. He had spoken storms off the sea the way other men whistled up dogs. He had been, briefly and disastrously, one of the finest storm-callers the Order of the Broken Sky had produced in a generation — and now he was a charm-seller in a city where magic carried a fine of fifty crowns, a branding iron, and in stubborn cases, a short walk to a tall scaffold. He had gotten very good at being small. Smallness, he had learned, was a craft like any other. It required discipline. It required daily practice. It required, above all, never, ever answering a knock after dark.

Which is why, when the knock came — three soft raps, polite as a schoolchild — at well past midnight, with the whole city holding its breath under that lidless blue sky, Wendell sat perfectly still behind his counter and thought: *no.*

The knock came again. Three soft raps.

"No," he said aloud, to the empty shop, because talking to himself was one of the few indulgences smallness allowed him.

The knock came a third time, and this time it was followed by something worse than knocking.

It was followed by words.

They came through the door the way cold comes through a window seam — thin, patient, finding every gap. Wendell didn't hear them so much as feel them arrive in his chest, in the old scar tissue over his heart where the Order had sealed his first binding, twelve years and one lifetime ago. Three syllables in a language with no living speakers. A language that was not supposed to exist anymore outside of forbidden books and older scars.

*Ael-eth-ka.*

The wind that had been missing for eleven days arrived all at once, pressed itself flat against the shutters, and waited.

Wendell stood very slowly, the way one stands in a room where a wasp has gotten in. Every instinct he'd spent twelve years sharpening told him to blow out the lamp, climb out the back window, and be two streets away before the door finished opening. That was the smart play. That was the *small* play.

But his hands were already moving. His hands had apparently held a vote without consulting him, and his hands had decided to open the door.

*Traitors,* he thought. *Both of them.*

* * *

The boy standing on his step couldn't have been more than seven or eight, though there was something about him that made age feel like the wrong instrument for measuring — like trying to weigh smoke on a butcher's scale. He was small and thin, dressed in gray wool that was wet through despite the fact that it had not rained in eleven days, and his hair was dark and stuck to his forehead in curls. His eyes were the color of the sky right before a summer storm breaks, and they were fixed on Wendell with an expression of such grave, unhurried certainty that Wendell felt briefly like the child in the exchange.

"You shouldn't be out here," Wendell said. "Whatever you're selling, I'm not buying. Whatever you're running from, I'm not running with. Those are my two policies. Very firm ones."

The boy looked at him.

Then the boy spoke again — not aloud, not entirely, but in that place behind the breastbone where the First Tongue lived. *Ael-eth-ka. Sorren-vael.* And Wendell's scars lit up like banked coals blown upon, and eleven days of missing weather rattled every shutter on Copperlane Street, and somewhere a church bell rang once, all by itself, in a city where no one had pulled its rope.

"*Stop,*" Wendell hissed, and grabbed the boy by the shoulder and hauled him inside and shut the door, and leaned against it, heart slamming, listening to the street settle back into its unnatural quiet. "Do you know what they do to people who speak that here? Do you have any idea? There are *wardens*, there are *inquisitors*, there's a woman named Voss who keeps a ledger thick enough to stop a crossbow bolt—"

The boy was dripping on his floor. The boy was shivering. The boy was looking around the shop with wide, wondering eyes, taking in the charms and knots and silent bells as though he had walked into a cathedral.

And the boy said — aloud this time, in a voice rusty with disuse, cracked and small and ordinary as any child's voice in any city anywhere:

"Wendell."

Just that. His name. In common speech, badly pronounced, the double-l mangled into something closer to a hiccup. But it was his name, his actual name, the one he hadn't answered to since the night he'd run, the name that belonged to a man who had died neatly on paper and been replaced by a charm-seller named Marlow Finch, who was boring, who was careful, who was nobody.

"I don't—" Wendell's voice came out wrong and he started over. "I'm Finch. Marlow Finch. You've got the wrong shop."

The boy shook his head. Slowly. The way you shake your head at someone insisting the sun rises in the west.

"How do you know that name?" Wendell asked, and heard the crack in his own voice, the longing underneath the fear, and hated himself for it, because longing was the one thing he had sworn off even more thoroughly than magic.

The boy reached up and touched Wendell's hand — the right one, the burned one, the one that ached on nights when storms gathered far out at sea beyond anyone's knowing — and where the small fingers landed, the ache went suddenly, shockingly quiet. Twelve years of dull pain, silenced like a bell cupped in a palm.

Wendell stared at him. The boy stared back. Outside, the sky-less sky pressed down, and the whole coast of the world seemed to lean in and listen.

"Oh no," Wendell said quietly, to himself, to the ceiling, to whatever powers arranged these things. "No, no, no. I know what you are. Or I know what you *aren't*, which is worse. And I know what it means when things like you start showing up on doorsteps."

The boy tilted his head. Waiting. Patient as weather.

"The last time the covenant stirred," Wendell said, mostly to the floorboards, "half the Order died and the other half ran, and the ones who ran spent the rest of their lives learning to be very, very small." He looked at the boy — this silent, soaked, impossible child who had knocked three times on the one door in Corvane that should never have been knocked upon. "I'm asking you, with everything I have, which is not much: tell me you're lost. Tell me you're anybody's grandson. Tell me anything small."

The boy opened his mouth. Closed it. Opened it again.

And in the silence between them, in the space where an answer should have been, the first drop of rain in eleven days struck the windowpane — fat, warm, deliberate — and slid down the glass like a finger drawing a word.

Wendell watched it go, and felt the past, buried twelve years under lavender and wax paper and careful smallness, stir offshore like a storm deciding whether to come home.

He sighed, fetched a towel, and got to work committing treason.
