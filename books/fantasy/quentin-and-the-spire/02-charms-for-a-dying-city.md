## 2. Charms for a Dying City

The boy ate three of Wendell's breakfast buns and then looked at him as though the transaction were somehow incomplete.

"There's more," Wendell said. "There's always more with children. My mother used to say feeding a child is like pouring tea into a hole in the world."

Quentin did not answer. He had not answered anything since the night he'd arrived, and Wendell was beginning to suspect the silence wasn't rudeness so much as architecture — something built into the boy the way load-bearing walls are built into a house. You could hang things on it, but you couldn't take it out.

It was morning now, gray and windless in that wrong way Corvane mornings had become lately. The city should have smelled of salt and gull and fish-rot by this hour. Instead it smelled of nothing at all, like a room someone had just swept.

Wendell dressed the boy in one of his own spare shirts, which fit Quentin the way a sail fits a rowboat, rolled the sleeves up four times, and considered his options.

Option one: take the boy to the Wardens of the Hearth, who handled foundlings. This option died instantly, because the Wardens also handled reports of unlicensed weather-working, and Wendell Marchetti had spent twelve years making sure no report with his name on it reached anyone's desk.

Option two: turn the boy out into the street. Wendell looked at Quentin, who was carefully aligning the crumbs on the table into a pattern that was either meaningless or a map of somewhere Wendell didn't want to know about, and found that option two had quietly died as well, without ceremony, sometime around dawn.

Option three, then: hide him among the wares and pretend nothing had happened, which was, when you got down to it, the only strategy Wendell had ever truly mastered.

"Right," he said. "Here's how this works. I sell charms. Rain-charms, mostly. Calm-sleep charms for the docks. Little knotted things that are supposed to keep your roof from leaking." He held one up — a twist of cord and river-glass that hadn't done anything but look decorative since the day he'd made it. "They don't work. That is the entire point of them. They work exactly as much as any honest trinket works, which is to say not at all, and the people of Corvane buy them anyway because buying them makes them feel less afraid, and feeling less afraid is worth two coppers to nearly anybody."

Quentin watched the charm with an expression of profound offense, as if it had personally disappointed him.

"Don't," Wendell said warningly.

* * *

The trouble began, as most troubles do, with an old woman.

Marta Ostrow came in at mid-morning for her monthly calm-sleep charm — she bought them for her husband, who shouted at ships in his sleep — and Wendell tied the knot, murmured the little nonsense words he used instead of real ones, and handed it over with his practiced seller's smile.

Marta stood there. She turned the charm over. She held it to her ear like a seashell.

"Wendell," she said slowly, "this one feels different."

"It's the same as always."

"It's *warm*."

"Cord gets warm when you knot it. Friction. Very natural phenomenon. The scholars write whole pamphlets about friction."

Marta squinted at him with the terrible x-ray vision of old women everywhere, then paid her two coppers and left, and Wendell told himself firmly that it meant nothing.

By afternoon he could no longer sustain the telling-firmly. He had sold eleven rain-charms, and Mrs. Pellow from the chandlery had come back to report that hers had produced actual water — clean, cold, sweet-tasting water — from a knotted cord that had never once managed so much as dampness. A fisherman named Cobb had bought a storm-warding charm on a dare and then stood outside in the street while the one cloud over Corvane arranged itself politely above his house all afternoon, waiting for him to go in before it dared to drizzle anywhere else.

And the shop itself — Wendell noticed it now, the way you notice a fever in yourself — the shop felt *full*. Not crowded-full. Full the way a bell is full after it rings. Every shelf hummed faintly against his senses, and the river-glass in the window caught light that wasn't there, and when he walked past the rack of sleeping-charms they swayed gently toward him like plants toward sun.

He knew exactly one explanation, and it was sitting cross-legged on the floor behind the counter, sorting buttons by some system invisible to mortal eyes.

"You," Wendell said, crouching down. "What are you doing?"

Quentin held up a button. It was ordinary. It was, Wendell happened to know, a button he'd sewn onto his own coat and lost two years ago, and here it was in the boy's palm, warm as a fresh bun.

"That's mine."

Quentin put it back in the pile and continued sorting.

"Listen to me. Whatever you are — whatever it is you're doing, or leaking, or *breathing* on my merchandise — you have to stop. Do you understand? In this city, what's happening in this shop doesn't make me rich. It makes me *kindling*. There are people whose entire job is noticing things like warm charms, and they have ledgers, and they have long memories, and they burn what goes in the ledgers."

Quentin looked up at him. His eyes were that impossible color again — the gray of sky deciding something — and for a moment Wendell had the distinct sensation of being read. Not examined. *Read*, the way you'd read a letter from someone you'd been waiting years to hear from.

Then the boy went back to his buttons, and Wendell went to the window, and tried to breathe like a man who had never once called lightning down out of a clear sky, and mostly failed.

* * *

Three days. That was how long the illusion lasted.

On the first day, Wendell sold out of everything. He had never sold out of everything. In twelve years of deliberate mediocrity — and he wanted it noted, by whatever powers kept accounts, that achieving reliable mediocrity in a dying market was harder than it sounded — he had never once run out of stock. Now Mrs. Pellow was back with her sister, and the sister's neighbors, and a woman from the tanners' row who wanted a charm against the smell, and Wendell turned them away one by one with his heart hammering and his hands shaking and a smile nailed to his face like a sign over a door.

*"Come back next week,"* he said, seventeen times. *"I've only got the two hands."*

On the second day, the neighbors started talking. He heard them through the window — Marta Ostrow's voice carrying beautifully across the lane, as it carried across every lane within earshot:

"—told you, the Marchetti one, the charm-seller, his luck's turned. Or something's turned. Tam says his grandmother remembered a weather-worker in Vell once, before the ban, and—"

"*Tam's grandmother* would remember the Flood if you gave her enough wine. But still. Warm charms. And did you see the sky behave for Cobb? Behave! Sky doesn't behave. Sky's like my husband—"

Wendell closed the shutters and sat down on a crate and put his head between his knees.

This was the thing no one warned you about exile: it wasn't the running that wore you down. Running was simple. It was the *smallness*. Twelve years of making himself small — small magic, small shop, small life, a man shaped like a footnote. He had gotten good at it. He had gotten so good at it that sometimes he woke up and couldn't remember what the large version of himself had even looked like. And now, in three days, one silent child had inflated him back to full size without lifting a finger, simply by existing near his inventory, and every eye in the lane was turning toward the place where the small man used to be.

He thought of his friend Ilse's voice, from another lifetime: *You can't half-be a storm-caller, Wendell. You're a kettle or you're off the fire.*

"I'm off the fire," he said aloud. "I'm off the fire, I'm off the fire, I'm off the—"

From behind the counter, Quentin made a sound.

It was not a word. It was barely a sound at all — a soft exhalation, almost amused, the noise a person makes watching someone argue with themselves and lose. But it landed on Wendell's ears strangely, the way the boy's first word had landed on his scars, and for a heartbeat the shop seemed to lean in and listen.

"Laugh at me, will you," Wendell muttered. "Everyone laughs at me. The order laughed at me. Aldric laughed at me—" He stopped, because that name had a taste, like copper coins left in the mouth too long. "Never mind who Aldric is."

Quentin tilted his head. *Tell me anyway,* the tilt said.

"No," said Wendell, and went to inventory the stock he no longer had.

* * *

On the third day, the warden came.

Not a Hearth-Warden — those were neighborhood men with cudgels and opinions. This was a city warden, gray coat, brass pin shaped like an open eye, the kind who wrote things down. He appeared in Wendell's doorway at the slowest part of the afternoon, when the light through the shutters lay in bars across the floor and Quentin was hidden, as per the new arrangement, behind the curtain in the back room among the unsold stock, where he had been instructed — firmly, repeatedly, with gestures — to be absolutely silent.

"Marchetti," the warden said. He had a ledger under his arm. Of course he had a ledger. Everything bad in Corvane came with a ledger attached.

"That's me. Charm-seller. Licensed. Here's the license." Wendell produced it from its drawer with the smooth speed of long practice, holding it at exactly the distance that made reading unnecessary and compliance obvious.

"Mm." The warden took it anyway. He read it the way cats read rooms. "Been reports. Charms working. Actual effects. Weather-effects." He let the last word sit in the air between them, fat and dangerous.

"Weather-*adjacent*," Wendell said. "A charm that keeps a roof dry is weather-adjacent. Perfectly legal. Perfectly inert. It's in the charter, third clause. I can show you the charter."

"I've seen the charter."

"Beautiful document."

The warden looked at him. Wendell looked back with the expression he'd spent twelve years perfecting: the face of a slightly dull man with nothing behind his eyes but inventory and mild anxiety. Inside, his heart was doing something undignified and percussive. Because here was the truth he could not say, the truth sitting behind the curtain ten feet away sorting buttons: the charms *were* working. Something in his shop was pulling power through him like water through a reed, filling his dead little trinkets with live current, and if the warden asked to inspect the stock — if the warden touched the stock —

"Mind if I look around?" the warden said.

"Help yourself," said Wendell, in the voice of a man with nothing whatsoever to hide, while every object in the shop quietly glowed like a lantern festival behind his composure.

The warden drifted along the shelves. He picked up a calm-sleep charm and weighed it. He held it to his ear. He frowned. Wendell watched him with the fixed pleasant attention of a man watching a fuse burn toward his feet, calculating distances, exits, the weight of the door bar, whether he could still do it — the old thing, the calling — with these hands, these ruined scarred hands, if it came to that. Whether he could pull rain out of a dry sky in a warded city and get a child out through the chaos.

He didn't know. That was the worst of it. Twelve years of not-knowing, and he might be about to find out in front of a ledger.

The warden put the charm down. He opened his ledger. He licked the pencil.

And behind the curtain, very softly, Quentin sneezed.

Everything stopped. The dust hung. The warden's pencil paused above the page.

"What was that?"

"A cat," said Wendell instantly. "Stray. Gets in through the cellar. I keep meaning to drown it but I'm soft-hearted, my late wife would tell you—" He heard himself say *late wife* and winced; he had no wife, late or otherwise, but grief was the one currency no warden ever checked for counterfeit. "She drowned herself, actually. The cat. No — she didn't — the cat didn't—"

The warden stared at him with the expression of a man deciding how much paperwork a strange man deserved.

"The cat," Wendell finished, "is fine."

Another pause. Then the warden grunted, snapped the ledger shut, and pointed the pencil at Wendell's chest like a small wooden accusation. "Reports keep coming, there'll be an inspection. The proper kind. With the *other* kind of warden." He tapped the eye-shaped pin on his own collar. "Inquisition. You know what they do with weather-workers, Marchetti?"

"They burn them," Wendell said quietly. "Everyone knows what they do."

"Good man." And the warden tipped his hat, and left, and the door swung shut behind him with the gentleness of a lid closing on a box.

Wendell stood in the middle of his humming, glowing, catastrophic shop and listened to his own breathing until it steadied.

Then he pulled the curtain aside.

Quentin looked up at him from amid the unsold charms, entirely unrepentant, a button in each hand.

"You sneezed," Wendell said.

Quentin shrugged, eloquently, the shrug of a child who has never in his life accepted blame.

"You *sneezed*. While hiding. During a *warden inspection*." Wendell dragged both hands down his face and discovered, distantly, that his palms were tingling — a deep, familiar, awful tingling, the feeling of a channel reopening after years of silt. He stared at his hands. The old scars, pale lines across the backs of them, had gone faintly luminous in the dim shop, glowing the color of distant lightning seen through cloud.

Quentin followed his gaze down to the hands. Then he looked up at Wendell's face, and something in the boy's expression shifted — from mischief to something older, sadder, certain. He raised one small hand and touched Wendell's scarred palm, and where his fingers fell the glow brightened like dawn coming up behind hills.

And Wendell slept that night — he had no choice in the matter, exhaustion took him like a tide — and dreamed.

Not his usual dreams. Not the gray ones, not the running ones.

He dreamed of a spire. White stone rising out of cloud, taller than anything built by human hands, wrapped in winds that sang in a language he almost remembered. He dreamed of figures on its summit, robed in gray and silver, raising their arms to a sky full of storms that bowed toward them like courtiers to a king. He dreamed of a covenant, vast and shining, a rope of light binding one world to another — and he dreamed, with a lurch of recognition that woke his heart like a slap, of a face beneath one of the hoods.

Alder-gray eyes. A beard going white at the chin. The particular patient smile of a man who has already decided how the story ends.

*Aldric*, Wendell thought, inside the dream, inside the memory that was not his. *Master Aldric. What are you doing in the storm's own house?*

Beside him in the narrow bed, the boy breathed slow and deep, and the dream rolled on like weather coming in off the sea, and somewhere far above the sleeping city the single cloud over Corvane thickened, and gathered, and waited.
