## 4. The Inquisitor's Ledger

The report arrived at the ninth hour, which Sera Voss considered an insult. Serious crimes — murders, arsons, the occasional necromancer with ambitions — were reported at dawn or in the dead of night, when the city's fears had time to curdle properly. But illegal weather-working? That came in at the ninth hour, folded into a stack of complaints about unlicensed oyster vendors and a dispute over a fence that both parties insisted was on the other's land.

She read it anyway. She read everything. That was the whole of her reputation in the Inquisition of Corvane: Voss reads everything.

*Three witnesses,* the report said, *independently confirm rain of unnatural persistence in the Saltcandle district on the nights of the 14th through the 17th. Rain confined to a single street. One witness claims to have seen a woman walk from one end of Lantern Row to the other without wetting her shawl.*

Sera set down the page and looked out the window of her office, where ordinary, law-abiding rain fell grayly against the glass, soaking everyone equally, as the founders of Corvane had intended.

"Unnatural persistence," she said to the empty room. "In a coastal city. In autumn."

The room declined to answer, which was more sense than most informants showed.

Still. Confined rain was not nothing. Weather did not respect property lines, not naturally. And the Inquisition existed for exactly this: the small magics that leaked through the cracks of Corvane's great and pious prohibition, the hedge-witches and charm-sellers who thought that because they worked in pennies and trinkets rather than thunderbolts, they were beneath notice.

They were beneath notice, mostly. Until someone like Sera noticed them.

She pulled the district file for Saltcandle. It was thin, as it always was — Saltcandle being the kind of neighborhood where people paid their taxes in fish and regarded wardens as a species of large, unpleasant seagull. But there was a name in it. There was always a name.

*Marchetti's Charms & Small Mercies. Proprietor: W. Marchetti. Licensed vendor, category four (trifles). No violations recorded in eleven years.*

Eleven years. Sera raised an eyebrow at the page. In her experience, a charm-seller who had gone eleven years without so much as a caution was either genuinely harmless or very good at being small. Both possibilities required a visit.

She took her coat from its hook, checked that her ledger-book, her seal, and her knife were all in their proper places, and went down into the rain.

* * *

Lantern Row in Saltcandle smelled of brine, frying onions, and the particular damp-wool misery of people who could not afford dry seasons. Sera walked it slowly, as was her habit, letting the neighborhood look at her. They always did. The gray coat of the Inquisition moved through a crowd the way a heron moves through a pond: nobody ran, but everybody stopped what they were doing and became very interested in something else.

She stopped at a fish stall where a woman with forearms like mooring ropes was scaling mackerel with terrifying efficiency.

"You're the fourth gray coat this month," the woman said, without looking up. "We're a lawful street. Mostly. The Hendersons' fence is another matter."

"I'm not here about the fence." Sera held up the complaint sheet. "Rain. Four nights running. Confined to this row. Someone here bought charms."

The scaler finally looked up, and her eyes went, briefly and involuntarily, down the street to the left. Then she caught herself, and her face became a wall.

"Don't know anything about charms," she said. "We're honest folk."

"We are indeed," Sera agreed pleasantly. "Honest folk who all buy from the same shop."

The woman's jaw tightened. Sera watched the arithmetic happen behind her eyes — the cost of loyalty weighed against the cost of an Inquisitor's attention — and understood it completely, because she had run the same arithmetic herself once, long ago, in a different life, on the wrong side of a different ledger.

She reached into her coat and produced, instead of a warrant, a small leather purse. From it she took three coppers and set them on the stall.

"Mackerel," she said. "Two, please. Whatever you'd pick for yourself."

The woman blinked. "You're buying fish."

"Inquisitors eat," Sera said. "It surprises people."

While the woman wrapped the fish with the grudging care of someone revising her entire assessment of a situation, Sera asked her questions the way she preferred to ask them: sideways, gently, wrapped in commerce. Had business been good lately? Oh, better than usual, actually — strange weather brought customers in. Had anyone new been selling along the row? Just Marchetti, same as ever. Was his stock any good?

And there it was — the hesitation, half a heartbeat too long.

"It's fine," the woman said. "Charmy."

"Charmy," Sera repeated gravely. "High praise."

She carried her fish down the row to Marchetti's Charms & Small Mercies.

* * *

The shop was narrower than it appeared from outside, as if it had been built by someone apologetic about taking up space. Charms hung in the window like a shoal of small dull fish: knotted cords for safe travel, pressed-tin suns for colicky infants, little wax figures of gulls that supposedly kept real gulls off laundry lines. Everything about it whispered *harmless*. Everything about it had been arranged, Sera suspected, by someone who understood precisely how harmless should look.

The bell above the door gave a tired jangle.

"One moment!" called a voice from the back — a man's voice, warm, slightly breathless. "If you're here about the wax gulls, I've had complaints, and I stand by the gulls."

He emerged wiping his hands on an apron: a broad-shouldered man somewhere past forty, dark hair going silver at the temples, with the kind of face that had clearly once been handsome and had since decided that handsome was too much effort. His smile was professional. His eyes, when they met hers and found the gray coat, went carefully, completely blank.

That blankness told Sera more than any confession would have.

"Good morning," she said. "Inquisitor Voss. You're the proprietor?"

"Marchetti. Wendell Marchetti." He said it easily, but Sera had spent fifteen years listening for the seam under easy words, and she heard it: the faintest hitch, like a floorboard that knew someone heavy was coming. "Is — is something wrong, Inquisitor?"

"Nothing's wrong. I'm making inquiries about weather-working. Unlicensed."

"Here?" He laughed, and it was a good laugh, rounded and rueful. "Inquisitor, look around. My most powerful item is a tin sun that may or may not soothe babies, depending on the baby. If I could work weather, do you think I'd be living above a shop that smells of beeswax and disappointment?"

"And yet," Sera said, "four nights ago, rain fell on this street and nowhere else. Three of your neighbors will swear to it. One of them says a woman walked the length of Lantern Row dry."

"Miracles happen," said Marchetti. "Perhaps the woman was blessed."

"Corvane doesn't recognize blessings as a legal category."

"No," he agreed pleasantly. "It really doesn't."

They regarded each other. Sera had played this game a thousand times — the courteous circling, the two professionals pretending one of them wasn't lying. Usually it bored her. This time something about it snagged, like a hook in wool. The man was afraid. Not guilty-afraid; hunted-afraid. She knew the difference. Guilty men performed innocence. Hunted men simply stood very still and hoped the hawk would pass.

She opened her ledger-book on the counter, and Marchetti's gaze flicked to it and away, quick as a blink.

"Routine matter," she said. "I keep a register of licensed charm-vendors in the districts affected by anomalous reports. Your license number?"

He recited it without hesitation. Category four, trifles, renewed annually for eleven years. Eleven years of perfect, immaculate smallness.

"Business good?" she asked, writing.

"Modest." He smiled. "I aim for modest."

"Your neighbors say your stock has improved lately."

"My neighbors are generous."

"One neighbor says your rain-charms worked."

Something crossed his face then — fast, gone before she could name it. Not fear. Closer to grief, or guilt, or some private weather of its own.

"Charms work or they don't," he said quietly. "Mine mostly don't. That's the trade."

Sera closed the ledger. She had what she needed, which was not evidence — there was no evidence, and they both knew it — but shape. A shape was enough for now. Shapes led somewhere.

"Thank you for your time, Mr. Marchetti." She paused at the door, hand on the bell-frame, and let herself ask the question she hadn't planned, the one that belonged less to the Inquisitor than to whatever was left underneath. "How long have you been in Corvane?"

"Twelve years," he said.

"Not eleven, then. The record's wrong."

"The record's right. I spent the first year learning not to sell anything." He said it lightly, but he didn't smile, and neither did she.

Outside, the lawful rain received her again. Behind her she heard the shop bell settle, and imagined him standing behind his counter in his modest shop with his modest charms, waiting to find out if the hawk would pass.

* * *

Back at the Inquisition hall, Sera did what she always did with an unsolved inquiry: she built a file, and then she sat with it after everyone else had gone home, when the lamps burned low and the building creaked like an old ship.

The file was thin. A complaint. Three witnesses. A charm-shop with eleven clean years and a proprietor whose fear fit him like a borrowed coat. It should have been enough to request a formal inspection, maybe a seizure of stock, the slow bureaucratic grinding that passed for justice in cases involving trifles.

Instead she found herself pulling the older files. The ones from twelve years ago. The Purge years.

She told herself it was procedure. Cross-referencing. Any storm-caller fleeing the coastal orders in those days would have come through Corvane; it was the last big city before the marshes, the natural funnel. So she pulled the intake registers, the detention lists, the deportation manifests, and she ran names.

She wasn't looking for Marchetti. She told herself that firmly, the way one tells oneself one is only having one glass of wine. She was looking for a pattern.

What she found, three hours in, in a manifest dated two weeks after the worst night of the Purge, was a name she had not permitted herself to say aloud in twelve years.

*Mirela Voss. Age nineteen. Detained, Lantern Row, Saltcandle district. Charged: unlicensed weather-working. Disposition: remanded to the Order's custody for judgment.*

Remanded. Judgment. The words were soft as down and meant exactly what they meant. Mirela had been taken from a street not four doors from Marchetti's shop, and two months later the Inquisition's records ended her with the gentlest euphemism in the language: *transferred beyond jurisdiction.* Sera had been twenty-three, newly sworn, still believing the ledgers were true. By the time she understood what *beyond jurisdiction* meant, understanding it was treason, and she had signed her name to forty documents since that used the same phrase.

Her sister had died for a rain-charm. Or for a candle-charm. Or possibly for nothing at all except being young and frightened and in the wrong city during the wrong season. The file did not say. Files never said. Files were how the Inquisition avoided saying things.

Sera sat in the lamplight for a long time, the manifest open on her desk, her finger resting beside her sister's name without touching it, the way one touches the edge of a burn to test whether it still hurts.

It still hurt.

And now, absurdly, cruelly, the trail led back to that same street. A charm-seller on Lantern Row, hiding weather-magic inside eleven years of harmlessness, while the rain behaved strangely and neighbors defended him and a woman walked dry through the wet. Either Wendell Marchetti was the luckiest innocent alive, or he was exactly what he appeared to be: a storm-caller in hiding, small on purpose, careful for twelve years.

Careful, until something made him careless.

Sera closed the file. Procedure said: request inspection, escalate, notify the Order's liaisons, let the machine do what the machine did. The machine had a name for people like Mirela, and the name was *disposition*.

But procedure also said — her own procedure, the private rule she had kept like a splinter under the skin for twelve years — *Voss reads everything. Voss decides alone.*

She lit a fresh lamp, took out a clean page, and wrote at the top, in her precise, unforgiving hand:

*Saltcandle anomaly — continued inquiry. Subject: W. Marchetti. Method: surveillance, informal. Duration: at discretion of investigating officer. Signature: S. Voss.*

No escalation. No liaisons. For now, the hawk would circle alone.

Below the signature, after a moment, she added a line she would deny under oath:

*Note: subject's shop stands four doors from where M. Voss was detained, 12 years prior. Coincidence noted. Not accepted.*

She blew out the lamp. Outside, over the rooftops of Corvane, the rain went on falling — evenly, lawfully, on everyone alike. But far off toward the coast, she thought she could hear, or imagined she could hear, a silence in the wind where storms ought to be gathering. Something was changing in the weather of the world. And every instinct she owned, dutiful and doubting in equal measure, told her that Wendell Marchetti's narrow little shop was standing at the center of it.
