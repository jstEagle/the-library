## 5. Twelve Years of Small Mercies

The rain-charms were lying again.

Wendell knew it the way a father knows when his children are misbehaving — not by looking at them, but by the quality of the silence they made while doing it. He stood behind the counter of The Gilded Umbrella, which was neither gilded nor much concerned with umbrellas, and watched a woman in a fishmonger's apron turn one of his charms over in her broad red hands.

"It's supposed to keep the damp off my window sills," she said.

"Absolutely."

"Last one I bought from you grew mold."

"That," Wendell said gravely, "was a different charm. That was for *encouraging* damp. You'll notice it worked beautifully."

She stared at him. Somewhere behind her left ear, where the light caught it, he could see her deciding whether to laugh or report him. In Corvane that decision usually went the same way. He slid a fresh charm across the counter before she could finish deliberating — copper wire twisted around a river stone, sealed in wax, utterly inert as far as he could make it, which was the entire point.

"On the house. For your trouble."

She took it, sniffed, and left without laughing or reporting him, which Wendell considered the ideal outcome of most human interactions.

The bell over the door had barely stopped swinging when Quentin appeared from between the shelves of dried lavender and dubious candles, holding up a slate. On it, in careful chalk strokes: *Why do you sell things that don't work?*

"They work," Wendell said. "Just not at what the label says."

*Then what do they do?*

"They make people feel like someone is looking out for them." He turned the slate over, wiped it with his sleeve, and handed it back. "That's not nothing. In a city that hates magic, a little honest fraud is practically a public service."

Quentin considered this with the terrible seriousness only children and cats can manage, then wrote: *You could do real magic.*

"I could," Wendell agreed pleasantly, and began sorting candle stubs by color, which was a job requiring no thought whatsoever, which was why he did it so often. "And I could also be burned in the square, but thank you for the career advice."

The boy didn't write anything else. He just looked at Wendell with those grey eyes that were never quite the right grey — storm-grey, sea-before-a-squall grey, a grey that belonged to no sky over Corvane — and Wendell felt the look land somewhere under his ribs and stay there.

That night, after Quentin fell asleep in the back room on a pallet that still smelled of the previous tenant's pipe smoke, Wendell sat at his workbench with a cup of chicory going cold beside him and found himself talking.

He hadn't meant to. It simply happened, the way water finds a crack in a wall. One moment he was carving a new charm blank; the next he heard his own voice, low, addressed to nobody, saying:

"There was a boy once who could call lightning out of a clear sky."

* * *

His name had been Tomas Vane, and he had been the loudest person Wendell ever loved.

They'd come up through the Order of the Broken Sky together — Wendell at fourteen, all elbows and hunger, Tomas at fifteen, already famous among the novices for whistling thunder off the cliffs at Cape Merren just to watch the older storm-callers come running. The order had been old then, older than Corvane, older than the hatred. Its monastery clung to the mountains above the drowned coast like a barnacle on the hull of the world, and its halls smelled always of ozone and lamp oil and the particular staleness of stone that has been rained on for nine hundred years.

"You feel it?" Tomas used to say, hand pressed flat against the storm-wall glass during the great autumn gales, grinning like an idiot, hair standing straight up. "It's *talking*, Wen. Everyone else hears weather. We hear *conversation*."

Wendell had felt it. God help him, he had felt it — the First Tongue moving through him like blood through a limb, the sense that every storm offshore was a vast animal turning in its sleep, and that he, small warm mortal thing that he was, could put a hand on its flank and it would know him. The covenant bound Ael to the mortal coast, storm to soil, and the storm-callers were its maintenance crew, its translators, its priests without a god worth worshipping. Renewal rites every generation. Small mercies, the masters called them — a little rain here, a storm turned aside there, the covenant fed on willingness and gratitude.

Tomas wanted more than maintenance. Of course he did. Tomas wanted everything, immediately, loudly, preferably with an audience.

"You're wasted on small mercies," he told Wendell once, sprawled across the observatory floor while Wendell tried to study. "You're the strongest caller we've had in two hundred years and you spend your days coaxing drizzle onto bean fields."

"Somebody's beans deserve nice weather."

"You know what I mean." Tomas rolled onto his stomach, chin in hands, and for a moment the grin dropped and something older looked out of his face. "The covenant's thinning. Master Aldric says so himself. Every renewal takes more from us and gives less back. What happens when it goes? What happens to *us*?"

"We renew it properly, then. Like we're supposed to."

"With what? Half our order can't even hold a squall anymore. The burnings—" He stopped. Even Tomas had limits, then. The burnout sickness took callers slowly, first the color draining from their eyes, then their voices, then everything. Three elders had gone silent as stones that winter alone.

"Then we ask Ael for more," Wendell said. "That's the whole arrangement. That's why the Spire stands."

Tomas looked at him a long time, and then the grin came back, and Wendell would spend twelve years wondering if he'd seen the warning in it and chosen not to.

* * *

In the back room, Quentin slept with one hand curled near his mouth, and Wendell talked to the dark over his cold chicory.

"The Purge didn't start with fire," he said quietly. "People think it started with fire. It started with a ledger."

Corvane had always hated magic — hated it the way the poor hate the rich, intimately and with good reason. But hatred is patient, and for generations it had contented itself with taxes and sneers and the occasional mob. Then came the Inquisition, and the Inquisition brought paperwork. Lists of names. Ledgers of suspected workers. And one autumn, a new Grand Inquisitor arrived with a theory: that the storm-callers weren't maintaining the covenant at all, but hoarding its power, bleeding the coast dry to feed their own strength.

It was a lie. Wendell had known it was a lie. Aldric had known it was a lie. But it was a *useful* lie, because fear travels faster than truth, and because the city had watched three storms in five years eat whole districts of the waterfront and wanted someone to blame.

"They came for the monastery on the night of the Long Dark," Wendell said. "First frost. I remember because Tomas had stolen honey cakes from the kitchens and we ate them on the wall like criminals, laughing, and the frost came down the mountain behind us like it was following the smell."

The wardens came at midnight. Not soldiers — worse, neighbors. Men whose roofs Wendell's rain had sheltered, women whose children he'd pulled from the flood tide two springs before. They came with torches and iron chains and the special courage of people acting on instructions.

And Wendell ran.

Not immediately. That was the part he could never forgive, the part he circled like a tongue finding a broken tooth. When the first alarm bell rang, he had run *toward* the dormitories, toward Tomas's cell, and found the corridor already full of smoke and shouting, and Tomas at the far end of it, pinned under a fallen beam, calling his name.

*I'm here,* Wendell had said. Or started to say. What actually came out was a word of the First Tongue — the calling-word, the gathering-word, the one that pulls a storm down out of a clear sky into your open hands — and the corridor had lit blue-white, and two wardens had died where they stood, and everyone still living had turned to look at the boy who had just shown them exactly what the Inquisitor's ledger promised they were.

After that, running wasn't a choice. It was arithmetic. Stay, and confirm every lie they'd been told, and die proving the Purge right. Flee, and live to — to what? He'd told himself he'd live to fight another day. To gather allies. To return.

Twelve years. He had sold rain-charms that didn't work.

"Tomas got out," Wendell said to the sleeping room, and his voice cracked on it like ice on a puddle. "That's the worst of it, boy. He got out. Pulled himself free of that beam — I saw him, later, I saw the burn scars where the beam had held him — and he got out of the fire and out of the mountains and spent four years looking for me. Because I was the strong one. Because if anyone could fix what was breaking, it was me."

He'd found Wendell in a fishing village south of Corvane, three years into exile, thinner, quieter, selling charms under another false name. And Wendell — Wendell had looked at the friend he'd abandoned, at the man who had crawled out of a burning monastery on broken hands to find him, and felt something in himself close like a door.

Because Tomas had a plan. Of course Tomas had a plan. Rebuild the order in secret. Gather the survivors. March on Corvane and tear the Inquisition down stone by stone, and take the covenant's power into their own hands so no mob could ever touch it again. Power to protect. Power, always, to protect.

"I said no," Wendell told the dark. "I told him I'd sworn off it. Told him great magic was how the fires started. Told him small mercies were all the world could bear from the likes of us." He laughed, and it came out wrong. "He called me a coward. Rightly. And I let him walk away thinking I was a coward, because the alternative was admitting I was afraid — afraid of the power, afraid of the mobs, afraid most of all that he might be right and I might be the thing standing between the world and its rescue."

Tomas had died two winters later. Burnout, the quiet kind, alone in a boarding house in some northern town, his eyes gone white as salt. Wendell had learned of it from a letter forwarded through three hands, and had read it standing in his shop doorway in the rain, and had gone inside and finished inventorying candle stubs, because that was what small mercies looked like. That was what survival looked like. A man counting candles while his best friend's ghost asked him, forever, in that grin: *What happens to us, Wen?*

* * *

He must have slept at the bench, because when he surfaced the shop was grey with early light and there was a sound at his elbow.

Quentin stood beside him, slate in hand, having clearly been there some time. His eyes went from Wendell's face to the cold cup to the charm blanks scattered across the bench, cataloguing, the way he catalogued everything.

*Who were you talking to?* the slate said.

"No one."

Quentin erased it. Wrote again. *I heard a name. Tomas.*

The name landed in the quiet room like a stone dropped down a well. Wendell waited a long time for the splash.

"He was my friend," Wendell said finally. "The best one I ever had. I left him in a fire and then I left him again when he walked out of it, and now he's dead, and I tell the story to nobody because nobody is the only audience that doesn't make it worse."

Quentin looked at him. Then, slowly, deliberately, he set the slate down on the bench, face-up and blank, and did something he had never done in all the weeks since he'd appeared at Wendell's door.

He spoke.

Not common words. Not the halting, half-remembered syllables he sometimes produced, tasting them like foreign fruit. This was the First Tongue, full and unbroken, rolling through the little shop like weather — and though Wendell hadn't heard it spoken aloud since the monastery, since the last elder's voice had gone silent, he understood it the way he understood his own heartbeat.

*The storm remembers him,* the boy said. *The storm keeps what we lose. Nothing given to the wind is gone.*

Wendell's hands, scarred and steady-for-twelve-years, began to shake.

"How," he whispered. "How do you speak it. How do you—"

But Quentin had already picked the slate back up, and whatever door had opened in the child had closed again, or perhaps merely narrowed, and what he wrote next was so ordinary it broke Wendell's heart in an entirely different way:

*Someone is watching the shop. The man with the ledger. He's been there since before dawn.*

Wendell crossed to the window in three strides and looked past the hanging charms, past the fogged glass, down the length of Saltcandle Row.

Across the street, beneath the leaning eave of the chandler's shop, a figure in warden grey stood very still in the early light, consulting a book.
