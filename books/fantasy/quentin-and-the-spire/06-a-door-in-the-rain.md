## 6. A Door in the Rain

The trouble began with Mrs. Halberd's roses.

Wendell had sold the woman a rain-charm eleven days ago — a cheap thing, a knuckle of river-glass wrapped in blue thread, the kind of trinket he'd been peddling for twelve years without a single one working as advertised. That was the point. A charm that did nothing kept everyone safe: the buyer got a pretty story, and Wendell got to keep his hands unburned and his name unknown.

But since Quentin had come through his door, nothing he touched stayed small.

Mrs. Halberd's charm had worked. It had worked *enthusiastically*. Her garden had received what witnesses described as "a private monsoon of remarkable generosity," and her roses — leggy, sulking things that hadn't bloomed properly since the wardens fined her for an untidy fence — had burst into flower so violently that three of them had climbed the trellis, over the gutter, and onto the roof of the house next door.

By noon, half of Copperlane Street had come to Wendell's shop.

He watched them queue outside his window with the particular dread of a man watching his own funeral procession form up early. There was the cooper's wife, who wanted a charm for her husband's bad knee. There was the tanner, who wanted rain for his hides and swore he'd pay double. There was a boy of maybe nine holding out two coppers for "a wind one, mister, so my kite can go to the moon."

"Charms are decorative," Wendell told them through the cracked door, smiling the smile he'd practiced until it fit like an old boot. "Pretty things. Conversation pieces. You hang it by the window and you say, *there, isn't that nice.*"

"But Mrs. Halberd said—"

"Mrs. Halberd has excellent soil," Wendell said, "and a devoted nature."

He closed the door and leaned against it, heart hammering, and found Quentin watching him from behind the counter with those grey eyes that saw far too much.

"They know," the boy signed. He'd begun communicating in gestures during their second day together — quick, economical movements, as if words were coins he was saving for something important.

"The neighbors don't *know*," Wendell said. "The neighbors think I've gotten better at my trade, which is flattering and fatal in roughly equal measure." He rubbed his face. "It's the wardens we worry about. And after them—"

He didn't finish. He didn't need to. Somewhere east of Corvane, in a tower full of clean desks and cleaner ledgers, someone would eventually connect a dead street's worth of impossible weather to a charm-seller whose charms had never once worked before this month.

*They will come,* Quentin signed.

"Yes," Wendell admitted. "They'll come."

*Then we should be somewhere else when they do.*

Wendell looked at the boy — at this silent child who had walked out of a storm into his shop and rearranged his entire understanding of the universe with a handful of words in a language older than cities — and felt something he hadn't felt in twelve years.

It took him a moment to identify it, because it had been gone so long.

It was purpose. And it was terrifying.

* * *

He spent the afternoon preparing, and the preparation itself felt like a confession.

From under the floorboards he took the box he had sworn, on the worst night of his life, never to open again: a coil of silver thread spun during a storm; a vial of rain caught on the mountain where the sky came down to meet the earth; a strip of leather from the glove he'd worn the last time he called lightning, scorched at every fingertip. His old life, packed away like a body.

"You understand," he said to Quentin, who sat cross-legged on the counter watching him with grave attention, "that what I'm about to teach you is not a trick. It's not charming. It's the reason there are ledgers with my description in them. If you ever do this where anyone can see—"

Quentin reached out and touched Wendell's wrist, right over the pulse, and held it there a moment. Then he drew back and made a gesture like a door opening.

*I know what doors cost,* the gesture said.

Wendell swallowed. "Right," he managed. "Well. Good."

He spent an hour showing the boy how to listen to water — how to find the rain sleeping in a barrel, the damp in a wall, the sweat on your own skin — and how to ask rather than command. *Storm-calling isn't shouting,* his mentor Aldric used to say, back when Aldric's voice had been the warmest thing in Wendell's world. *A storm is not a dog. It's a horse. You don't break it. You offer it your hand.*

He hadn't thought about Aldric in years. The memory arrived now like weather, sudden and pressure-heavy, and he set it aside because there wasn't time.

The knock came at dusk.

Not the polite knock of a customer. Three strikes, spaced evenly, delivered with the flat authority of someone who knocks expecting doors to open. Wendell blew out the lamp. Through the shutters he could see lantern-light in the street, four or five points of it, and the shapes of men in warden grey arranging themselves around his little shop with the unhurried competence of people who have done this many times and enjoyed most of them.

"Wendell Marsh," a voice announced. "By order of the Corvane Wardenship. Open in the name of civic law."

*Marsh,* Wendell thought. Twelve years, and they still couldn't be bothered to learn his real name. Some small, spiteful part of him found this funny. Most of him was busy being afraid.

"Quentin," he whispered. "Behind the counter. Whatever happens, whatever you see — stay low, stay quiet, and when I say *now*, you take my hand and you do not let go. Understood?"

The boy nodded once. Then he did something unexpected: he reached up and pressed his small palm flat against Wendell's cheek, the way you'd steady something fragile. His eyes were calm. Calmer, Wendell thought wildly, than the eyes of any child had any right to be, as if somewhere inside that slight frame there was a patience much older than childhood.

The door shuddered under the first blow of the ram.

* * *

Later — much later, on a road with no name — Wendell would try to explain to himself what happened next, and fail, and keep trying, because some part of him needed to make sense of how good it had felt.

But in the moment there was only instinct.

The door broke. Lantern-light poured in across his floor, and warden boots followed it, and a sergeant's voice was already reciting charges — illegal weather-working, harboring, false registration — when Wendell stopped thinking like a shopkeeper and remembered what he was.

He put his hands flat on the counter. Both palms, fingers spread, the way he'd done ten thousand times in another life.

And he asked.

The rain answered.

It had been waiting all evening above Copperlane Street — a grey, patient ceiling of it, heavy with everything the drought-years had taught it to withhold — and when Wendell Marchetti opened himself the way he'd sworn never to open again, the whole sky leaned down to listen. He felt it the way you feel a name called from a great distance: first as vibration, then as recognition, then as joy. The storm knew him. Twelve years of selling fake charms and wearing a false name and keeping himself deliberately, painstakingly *small*, and the sky had known him the whole time, had waited for him like an old dog at a gate.

"Oh," Wendell breathed, and it was almost a laugh. "*Oh.*"

The rain came through the broken roof.

Not as flood — he was careful, even now, even exhilarated; he asked it to fall thin and everywhere at once, a curtain instead of a hammer — and it filled the shop in a heartbeat, floor to rafter, a wall of moving water lit gold by the wardens' dropped lanterns. Men shouted. Someone fired a bolt that splashed into the curtain and died. Through the shimmering veil Wendell saw faces he would remember later: the sergeant's furious disbelief, a young warden's open-mouthed wonder, the cooper's wife standing in her doorway with a hand pressed to her mouth, watching the outlawed miracle happen over the heads of the men sent to stop it.

*Now,* Wendell signed, and Quentin's hand found his, small and certain.

Then Wendell did the forbidden thing. The truly forbidden thing, the thing beyond calling weather, the thing that had gotten the order purged and his friends burned out and scattered: he *shaped*. He gathered the falling water between himself and the boy and pulled it taut, and asked it to remember what it had always been — every sea and river and cloud is the same water going home, and water remembers every road it has taken — and he asked it to take one more.

A doorway opened in the rain.

It stood in the middle of his wrecked shop: an arch of falling silver, and within it not his back room but somewhere else — somewhere green and dark and smelling of pine and distance, rain falling there too, gentle rain, on ground that had never heard of Corvane. It hummed. It sounded, faintly, like singing.

"Go," Wendell said.

Quentin went — through the silver, quick and sure as a fish, and turned on the far side to wait for him with that maddening, ancient calm.

Wendell followed. And the moment he crossed the threshold, the price arrived.

He'd known it would. Every storm-caller knew the arithmetic: shaped magic burns its maker, always, in proportion to the asking. A rain-charm costs a warm tingle. A doorway between two rains costs—

Fire ran up both arms from wrist to shoulder, white and total, as if he'd plunged his hands into the heart of a lightning strike and left them there. He heard himself scream. He felt himself fall. He hit wet grass and lay curled around his own hands, which had gone from flesh to agony to something worse than either — a deep, ringing numbness threaded with fire, the skin split along every finger, the old scars across his knuckles torn open and shining.

Above him, the doorway of rain hung for one more heartbeat — long enough for Wendell to see, framed in silver, the young warden with the wondering face, staring straight at him across the impossible arch. Not at the magic. At *him*. Memorizing him.

Then the doorway folded shut, and the night was just a night, and the pain was all his own.

* * *

He woke to rain on his face. Real rain, ordinary rain, falling softly through pine branches onto whatever forgotten corner of the world his doorway had bought them.

His hands were bandaged. Clean linen, tied in neat, practiced knots — knots Wendell recognized, because he'd taught them, years ago, to students in another life. Quentin sat beside him cross-legged, a crushed paste of some green herb on a flat stone, watching Wendell with an expression that was not quite guilt but was adjacent to it.

"You did this?" Wendell rasped. His voice sounded like gravel in a bucket.

The boy nodded. He tapped the stone of paste, then pointed southeast, toward the mountains barely visible as a darker darkness against the dark. Then he held up his small hands, palms out, and mimed — carefully, precisely — the shape of burning.

*You paid,* the gesture said. *I know what paying looks like.*

Wendell laughed, and immediately regretted it, and laughed anyway, because the alternative was screaming and he'd done enough of that. "Look at you," he croaked. "Twelve years old, maybe. Can't say a word. And you patch up a fugitive storm-caller in the middle of nowhere like you've done it all your life." He let his head fall back against the pine trunk. "Who *are* you, Quentin?"

The boy was quiet a long moment. Rain ticked against the leaves overhead. When he finally moved, he didn't sign. He spoke — hoarse, halting, rusty as a gate in winter, but spoken aloud in the common tongue, the first words Wendell had ever heard from him:

"The storms... remember you." He paused, gathering each word like a child crossing stepping stones. "That is why... they came for me. To find you."

And then, because the night demanded one more impossibility, Wendell looked at his ruined hands — at the light leaking faintly, unmistakably, from between his bandaged fingers — and understood what the young warden had seen, and what that warden would write in his report by morning, and what every ledger-keeper in Corvane would read:

*A storm-caller lives. He walks with a mute child. They went south, toward the Spire.*

Somewhere behind them, a bell began to ring in the distant city — not the alarm bell, but the deeper one beneath it, the one Wendell had heard rung only twice in twelve years and hoped never to hear again.

The hunting bell.

Quentin rose, shouldered the small bundle of their belongings, and held out his hand — the same gesture, Wendell realized, that the storm had offered him on the other side of the sky. An invitation. A hand extended to a horse.

South, then. Toward the mountains. Toward the Spire he'd fled from half a lifetime ago, and the mentor waiting there whom he'd betrayed, or who had betrayed him — he still, after all these years, could not decide which.

He took the boy's hand with what remained of his own, and they walked into the rain.
