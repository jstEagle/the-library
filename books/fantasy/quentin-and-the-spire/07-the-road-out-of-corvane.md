## 7. The Road Out of Corvane

The salt marshes began where Corvane's cobbles ended, as if the city had simply given up pretending it could hold back the sea.

Wendell Marchetti stood at the edge of the last dry road with his coat collar up and his burned hands wrapped in linen strips, watching the tide creep across the flats in long silver sheets. Behind him, smoke still rose from the shop district — not his shop, thank every indifferent god, but close enough that the wardens would be combing the streets for a charm-seller who had walked out of a locked building through a wall of rain.

"Right," he said to the boy beside him. "No turning back now."

Quentin said nothing. Quentin mostly said nothing, though Wendell had begun to suspect the silence was less emptiness than fullness — a cup so filled there was no room left for ordinary words. The child stood with his bare feet half-sunk in the wet sand, face tilted toward the gray sky, listening to something Wendell couldn't hear.

Or maybe he could hear it, if he let himself. That was the trouble with the marshes. They were quiet the way an empty house is quiet, and quiet had always been Wendell's oldest enemy.

He tested his weight on the first hummock of tussock grass. It held. He took a step, then another, and found a rhythm: press, test, step, like crossing a floor he didn't trust. His hands throbbed inside their wrappings. Storm-calling after twelve years of abstinence had done what the Inquisition never managed — it had nearly cooked him from the inside out. The old texts called it *the burn-back*. His order had called it *the price of pride*. Wendell called it a lesson he apparently needed to learn twice.

"Stay behind me," he told Quentin. "Step where I step. If the ground starts sucking, throw yourself flat and crawl sideways. And if anyone follows us out here—"

He stopped. The boy was looking at him with those pale eyes, patient as weather.

"—if anyone follows us," Wendell finished, softer, "we run."

They ran nothing for the first hour. They waded, mostly, through channels shin-deep and cold, past stands of dead reeds rattling like old men gossiping. Gulls wheeled overhead, screaming their opinions. Somewhere far off, thunder muttered — real thunder, honest thunder, the ordinary kind that came off the sea without any covenant attached to it. Wendell found himself flinching at it anyway.

Twelve years of being small. Twelve years of selling rain-charms that worked exactly badly enough to be legal — a little luck with the weather, a little protection against damp, nothing that would make a warden look twice. He had gotten good at small. Small was safe. Small was alive.

And then a mute child had knocked on his door speaking the First Tongue, and small had ended forever.

"You could have knocked on anyone's door," Wendell said, hopping a channel. "Any storm-caller in the world. There must be dozens left. Well. Dozens is generous. A handful. A *sprinkling*." He grimaced as his boot sank deeper than expected. "Why me?"

Quentin looked at him. Then, haltingly, like a man remembering a language he'd studied as a child:

"Wendell... loud."

"Loud?" Wendell nearly lost his footing. "I am many things, young man, but loud is not—"

Quentin tapped his own temple. Then pointed at Wendell. Then made a sound low in his throat, a rumble like distant surf.

*Your dreams are loud,* Wendell translated, and felt the bottom drop out of his morning. The shared dreams. The visions of Ael, the storm-realm, the Spire standing at the root of the sky. The boy had been walking through his sleep for who knew how long, and what he'd found there had been worth crossing a continent for.

"Wonderful," Wendell muttered. "Even unconscious, I'm a disappointment to myself."

But Quentin smiled — the first true smile Wendell had seen from him, quick and crooked and startlingly human — and something in Wendell's chest, something he'd kept boarded shut since the Purge, rattled on its hinges.

* *

The marshes dreamed at night.

Quentin did not know the word *dreamed*, but he knew the feeling: the world going soft and silver, the mud breathing, the water holding the sky inside itself so carefully you couldn't tell which was which. In Ael they would have called this place holy. Here, it was only empty, and emptiness was its own kind of sacred.

He sat on a hummock while the tall man slept, wrapped in his coat, hands curled against his chest like wounded birds. Quentin watched him the way he watched everything — completely, patiently, the way rain watches a roof for the one loose shingle.

The man was loud. Even awake, even trying to be small, Wendell Marchetti roared. Not with his mouth. With the thing underneath, the part of him that still remembered how to call storms down like calling a dog home. Quentin had heard a thousand sleepers on his long walk from the coast where he'd fallen out of the sky like a dropped stitch. Most people dreamed in whispers. Some dreamed in colors. Wendell dreamed in *thunder* — great rolling griefs and guilts, twelve years of them stacked like stormclouds, and underneath all of it, faint but unbroken, a thread of the First Tongue that the man didn't even know he still spoke.

That thread was why Quentin had chosen him. The covenant was failing. The bridge between the worlds was fraying strand by strand, and when it broke, everything on both sides would fall. Quentin had been sent — no. Quentin had *volunteered*. There was a difference, and the difference mattered more than anything.

But a bridge needed anchors on both shores, and the anchor on this shore had to be someone who still loved the storm enough to grieve it.

Wendell grieved it beautifully.

A night bird cried. Quentin turned toward the sound and practiced, silently, shaping his mouth around the heavy common words. *Marsh. Bird. Night.* The words were strange — so many sounds for so few meanings, like a language built by someone who hadn't learned to listen yet. But the tall man seemed to need them. When Quentin stayed silent too long, Wendell's eyes went worried, and worry made him clumsy, and clumsiness would get them both killed before the mountains.

So Quentin practiced. *Marsh. Bird. Cold.* He touched the mud. "*Mud*," he whispered, and almost laughed at how ridiculous it sounded, this magnificent ugly word.

Above him, the stars were going out. One by one, so slowly that no one without his eyes would ever notice. Each dark star was a voice in Ael falling silent. Each silence was a door closing somewhere in the storm-realm, and behind each closed door, something was getting hungrier.

Something was very hungry already.

Quentin pulled his knees to his chest and did not let himself think about it. Instead he thought about the tall man's face when Quentin had smiled at him — the surprise of it, like a man finding a coin in an old coat. He decided he would practice smiling too. It seemed to do things to people.

* *

Dawn came up the color of pewter, and with it came breakfast, or the idea of breakfast.

"Behold," Wendell announced, holding up a wriggling mudflat eel he'd speared with a sharpened reed. "The cuisine of my ancestors. Well. Someone's ancestors. Possibly eels'."

Quentin regarded the eel with deep suspicion.

"It's better than it looks." Wendell paused. "Actually, that's a lie. It's exactly as bad as it looks. But it's food, and food is fuel, and fuel is how we get to the mountains before the wardens or worse find us." He set to cleaning the catch with his bandaged hands, hissing once when the salt water found a burn. "You know, most children would have run screaming by now. Marshes, fugitives, questionable eel. You haven't said a word of complaint."

"*Hungry*," Quentin offered.

"There we go! Progress." Wendell grinned despite himself. The boy's common speech was arriving in pieces, single words dropped like stones into a pool, and every one of them landed with disproportionate weight. "You know, when you talk, it's like — hm. Like hearing a bell ring in a room you thought was empty."

Quentin tilted his head, considering this. Then he pointed east, toward the distant smudge of mountains, and said carefully: "*Spire. Breaking.*"

The grin died on Wendell's face.

"What do you mean, breaking?"

Quentin frowned, hunting for the words. His hands moved first — fingers spreading apart, pulling away from each other, a seam coming undone. "*Bridge. Old. Fraying.*" He looked up, and his pale eyes held something far older than a child should carry. "*Storms. Going...*" He pressed his fist to his mouth, then opened it slowly, empty. "*Quiet.*"

Wendell sat down heavily on a drift-log. The marsh wind moved through the reeds, and for once he listened to it properly — really listened — and heard what he'd been refusing to hear since the boy knocked on his door. The wind was thin. The pressure sat wrong on his old scars. The whole coast felt like a held breath.

"The covenant," he said quietly. "It's failing. That's why the storms have been dying. That's why—" He flexed his ruined hands. "Why the magic's been burning us out faster. We're drawing from a well that's running dry, and the rope's starting to fray." He laughed, short and bitter. "Twelve years I thought I was hiding from my past. Turns out my past was collapsing whether I watched it or not."

Quentin sat down next to him on the log, close enough that their shoulders touched. The boy said nothing, but he leaned, slightly, deliberately, the way a small creature leans into warmth. Wendell found he didn't mind.

"*Starving*," Quentin said suddenly.

"What?"

The boy pointed up, past the clouds, to a place no eye could follow. His expression was terrible in its simplicity — not fear, exactly, but a kind of aching pity. "*In Ael. Starving.*"

Wendell stared at him. "There's something *in* Ael? Alive? And it's starving because the covenant's failing?"

Quentin nodded once, slow.

"And if the covenant breaks entirely—"

Quentin made a gesture Wendell understood without translation: fingers opening, opening, opening until the hand simply wasn't a hand anymore. Everything, let go. Everything, gone.

The gulls screamed somewhere overhead. Wendell looked down at his bandaged hands — the hands that had called a doorway out of rain, the hands that had betrayed him the moment he'd asked them for greatness again — and understood, with the clarity that only arrives at the worst possible times, that the choice he'd fled twelve years ago had never actually been escaped. It had just been waiting. Patient as tide.

"All right," he said, to the boy, to the marsh, to whatever was starving beyond the sky. "All right. We go to the Spire."

Quentin leaned harder against his shoulder.

* *

They made better time the second day, though "better" was relative; the marsh measured progress in humility. By midafternoon they struck the old causeway — a raised road of broken stone running straight toward the mountains, built by some empire that had assumed it would last forever. Empires usually did assume that.

Wendell walked with his sleeves rolled up despite the chill, letting the wind move over his scars. It was a stupid risk, a small indulgence, but the wind felt like company, and company had been scarce these twelve years.

"So," he said, mostly to keep his spirits above water. "When this is over — assuming *when*, I've decided it's *when* — I'm reopening the shop. Properly. No more charms that barely work. Real work. Weather-wrights' guild, maybe, if they'll have a disgraced exile. I make a decent living off people's fear of damp; imagine what I could do with honesty."

Quentin walked beside him, matching his stride with the tireless economy of children. He was learning words the way sand learns water — absorbing, shifting, holding shape briefly. Every few minutes he'd point at something and attempt it. "*Stone.*" "*Bird.*" "*Road.*" Once, pointing at Wendell stumbling on a cracked flagstone: "*Old.*"

"I am *not* old. I am seasoned. There's a difference, and it's dignity."

"*Old*," Quentin repeated, with the serene cruelty of the very young, and Wendell laughed — actually laughed, a rusty sound that startled a heron into flight.

Gods, when had he last laughed? Before the Purge. Before Aldric's face at the council door, before the friend whose name he still couldn't say. Twelve years of careful silence, and this child had cracked it open in two days with a vocabulary of forty words and a smile like a stolen coin.

They camped that night in the lee of a collapsed watchtower, and Wendell woke near midnight to find Quentin sitting upright, staring north, rigid as a fence post.

"What is it?" Wendell whispered. "What's wrong?"

For a long moment the boy didn't answer. Then, in the First Tongue — the language that made Wendell's scars ache and his heart lurch like a homesick man hearing his mother's voice — he said three words, soft and clear beneath the stars.

Wendell didn't know their meaning. But he knew their tone. Anyone who had ever lived through a storm knew that tone.

It was the tone of something very large, very far away, beginning to turn its attention toward you.

Quentin looked at Wendell then, and for the first time since the shop, the child looked afraid.

"*Coming*," he said in common, pressing his small hand flat against the cold stone of the earth, as if he could feel footsteps through it. "*Fast.*"

Far to the north, along the dark spine of the mountains, lightning flickered — once, twice — in a sky that held no clouds.

And it was not natural lightning. Wendell had spent his life telling the difference, and every instinct he owned stood up screaming at once.

Someone else was climbing toward the Spire.
