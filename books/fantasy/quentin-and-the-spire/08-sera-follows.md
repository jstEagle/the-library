## 8. Sera Follows

The report landed on Sera Voss's desk on a Tuesday, which she would later consider the last ordinary day of her life.

*Unlicensed weather-working, Saltrow district. Charms exceeding permitted efficacy by a factor of four. Vendor unknown.*

She read it twice, then a third time, because the third reading was the one where her eye caught what the first two had skipped past in their eagerness to be dutiful: *factor of four.* Most confiscated charms in Corvane didn't work at all. The charm trade was a graveyard of knotted string and dried lavender sold to people who wanted to believe the sky could be reasoned with. A factor of four meant someone was actually doing it. Which meant either a fraud of unprecedented competence or a storm-caller.

Sera had spent eleven years hoping never to meet a storm-caller again.

"You're frowning at that paper like it insulted your mother," said Doss from the next desk. Doss was built like a wardrobe and had the investigative instincts of one. "Want me to look into it?"

"No." She filed the report before he could see her hands weren't quite steady. "I'll take this one myself."

It wasn't procedure. Procedure said two investigators minimum for anything touching weather-working, because weather-working touched the Purge, and the Purge touched everything, and the Inquisition had learned — or claimed to have learned — that such matters required witnesses. But Sera had discovered over eleven years that procedure was an excellent place to hide things, and what she wanted to hide was herself, alone, in a room with whatever was making rain happen in Saltrow.

She told herself it was efficiency. She was getting better at telling herself things. Eleven years of practice.

* * *

The shop was called Marchetti's Curiosities, which was already a confession, because nobody honest named a shop after themselves in a city where honesty was a tax bracket most people couldn't afford.

Sera watched it for three days from the window of a pie shop across the street whose proprietor had stopped asking why she ordered one small plum pie every four hours and had started simply leaving it at her table with a look of pity. The shop's owner was a lean man with grey coming in early at his temples and the careful movements of someone who had taught his body to be unremarkable so thoroughly that it had forgotten how to be anything else. He sold charms. He haggled badly. He gave a discount to a widow with a sick goat and pretended it was a business decision.

On the fourth day, a child appeared in the window behind him.

The reports hadn't mentioned a child. The reports mentioned *unlicensed efficacy*, and Sera had spent three days constructing a version of Wendell Marchetti — because of course it was Marchetti; the ledger she'd pulled from the wardens' raid had led here like a thread pulled from a hem — as a dangerous remnant of the old orders, a man who had hidden for twelve years the way embers hide in ash. The child did not fit the construction. The child stood at the glass with both palms pressed against it, watching the street with an expression of such open, unguarded wonder that Sera felt something in her chest shift its weight uncomfortably, like furniture being moved in a room she'd thought was fully furnished.

Then the wardens came, and the shop came apart in rain, and Sera arrived forty minutes too late to find anything but a wet doorway, a scorch mark shaped like a handprint on the counter, and a warden sitting in a puddle saying, "There was a door. In the rain. There was a door made of rain," in the voice of a man who knew exactly how it sounded.

Sera looked at the handprint for a long time.

Storm-callers didn't leave scorch marks. Storm-callers left frost, or ozone, or nothing at all — they were trained to be clean. This burn was ugly, desperate, the signature of magic done by someone out of practice and in pain, someone who had paid for every inch of it.

Someone who hadn't used it in twelve years.

"Where are you going?" Doss asked later, when he found her packing a travel satchel in the records room.

"Salt marshes. They'll head for the mountains."

"Alone? That's not—"

"I know what it's not, Doss."

He studied her with the slow, bovine patience that occasionally, infuriatingly, resembled wisdom. "You've been different since the Saltrow file," he said. "Tighter. Like a boot that doesn't fit anymore but you keep wearing it because breaking it in again sounds worse than the limp."

"Go count your quills, Doss."

He went. But at the door he paused and said, without turning around, "Whatever you're chasing — make sure it's worth what it costs to catch it," and Sera sat alone in the records room for a while after that, among the ledgers, listening to the particular silence of ten thousand names.

* * *

She'd signed some of those names.

That was the thing about the Purge that the histories got wrong, or rather, the thing they got right in the wrong shape. They wrote it as mobs and fire, and there had been mobs and fire, but the machinery of it had been paper. Ledgers. Testimonies cross-referenced against guild rolls. And at the bottom of each page, a signature — neat, upright, *S. Voss, Junior Inquisitor* — because she had been twenty-three and certain, and certainty at twenty-three is just fear wearing a uniform.

Her sister's name was not in any ledger. Lira had never been accused, never been tried, never been anything except a girl of nineteen who liked storms the way other people liked music, who would stand out in the rain with her face turned up and laugh. Lira had died in the third week of the Purge, in the crush outside the tribunal hall, when a crowd that had learned to be afraid of magic decided to be afraid of everything else too. Trampled. Not by witch-hunters. By neighbors. By the climate the ledgers had made.

Sera had signed forty-one more names after that. She told herself it was to keep the process orderly, humane, *procedural* — that if people like her didn't hold the pen, people worse would. It was even partly true. But underneath the truth was the simpler, uglier thing: if the Purge was righteous, then Lira's death was tragedy. If the Purge was rotten, then Lira's death was waste, and Sera had spent eleven years building a life on top of it, and you cannot dig under your own foundations without bringing down the house.

So she rode east alone, toward the salt marshes, telling herself she was pursuing fugitives, and let the marsh wind do what marsh wind does, which is strip a person down to the questions they've been avoiding.

* * *

Tracking them was almost insultingly easy, and that worried her more than difficulty would have.

Marchetti was leaving a trail a first-year investigator could follow — a charm-seller's habits dying hard, bartered goods at farmsteads, a broken fence mended with a knot only guild-trained hands tied. He was moving fast but not evasively, as though part of him still believed he was a legitimate traveler on a legitimate road. Or as though he wanted to be followed.

The marshes began on the second day, and with them the rain, which came in long grey sheets off the coast and turned the world the color of old pewter. Sera walked her horse where the going was too soft to ride and thought about how Lira would have loved this weather, absolutely loved it, would have been out ahead of her with her hood down, catching raindrops on her tongue and daring Sera to admit it felt like freedom.

On the third evening she found their camp — or rather, found where their camp had been, still warm, a fire ringed with stones arranged with a precision that made her snort despite herself. Even fugitive, the man couldn't help being tidy. She was crouched over the ashes when she heard the voice.

Not Marchetti's. Higher. Younger.

She moved toward it through the reeds with her hand on her baton, and stopped.

The boy — and it was the boy from the window, she was sure of it, though he was turned away — was standing at the edge of the water, talking. Not to Marchetti; Marchetti was visible further back, asleep or pretending to be. Talking to the marsh itself, to the rain, to the dark. And the language was one Sera had heard exactly once before, eleven years ago, from a terrified old woman in a tribunal cell who had babbled it while they burned her instruments, a language the Inquisition's scholars called extinct, pre-imperial, impossible.

The First Tongue.

Except the old woman had spoken it like a drowning woman cries out. The boy spoke it like a native. Like it was his mother tongue, which, Sera realized with a coldness that had nothing to do with the rain, it might literally be.

And woven into the syllables — she couldn't say how she knew, only that the rain seemed to know, leaning in around her, softening — was a name.

*Lira.*

She came out of the reeds with her baton drawn before she had decided to, eleven years of training executing the decision her body had made without consulting her. "Hands where I can see them. Both of you."

Marchetti was up instantly, placing himself between her and the boy with a speed that contradicted everything careful and unremarkable about him. His hands were wrapped in stained bandages. His eyes, when they found hers, went through recognition, calculation, and resignation in the space of a breath, arriving finally at something almost like courtesy.

"Inquisitor Voss," he said. "You've been behind us since the marsh road. You walk loudly for someone so quiet."

"And you camp messily for someone so tidy. Turn around. Slowly."

"Sera." He said her name gently, and she hated that he knew it, hated more that hearing it aloud did something to her spine. "Whatever you think I am—"

"I know what you are. Wendell Marchetti, Third Circle, Order of the Ninefold Gale. Escaped the Purge. Twelve years selling rain-charms to farmers." She advanced a step. "Turn around, put your wrists out, and maybe the tribunal remembers you were young when you ran."

"The tribunal," Marchetti said quietly, "burned a woman in Cell Nine in the spring of the Purge for owning a rain-glass. Her name was Odile Fen. She was seventy. She used it to tell her lettuce when to grow." He didn't move. "You signed that one. I've seen the ledger."

The rain fell between them. Somewhere behind the boy, thunder muttered offshore like an old dog dreaming.

"That was lawful," Sera said, and heard, in her own voice, the exact tone of every justification she had ever buried.

"It was *legal*. Those aren't the same word, Inquisitor, and you know it, because you've known it for eleven years, and you're out here alone, following us alone, off the books—" he gestured at the empty marsh, the absent Doss, the missing escort "—because some part of you already knows how this ends. You corner us, you take us back, you sign the page. And then you go home and sit with it. Again."

"Shut up."

"Lira Voss," said the boy.

Everything stopped.

He had turned around. He was looking at her — not at her baton, not at her badge, at *her* — with eyes the grey-green of sea-light, and he said it again, carefully, the foreign syllables shaped with a child's precision: "*Lira Voss.*" And then more words in the First Tongue, flowing around the name like water around a stone, and though Sera understood none of it, she understood all of it, the way you understand a lullaby in a language you don't speak. The rain gentled. The thunder settled. And the boy's expression held no accusation and no pity — only a kind of terrible tenderness, the look of someone delivering a message they've carried a long way.

"How," Sera heard herself say, "do you know that name."

Marchetti had gone very still. "Quentin," he said softly, warningly, or pleadingly, it was hard to tell.

"He dreams," the boy said — common words now, halting, rusted, as though speech were a door he was learning to reopen. "Everyone dreams loud, near the water. Hers were..." He searched for the word, found it, offered it up like something breakable. "Loud. She liked the rain. She laughed in it." He tilted his head. "She would be older than you now. But she isn't. I'm sorry. I don't know why I'm sorry. The words come and I say them."

Sera's baton arm had lowered at some point. She had no memory of lowering it.

"This is a trick," she said. Her voice sounded far away, borrowed. "This is some — charm, some manipulation, he's a construct, you've—"

"He's a child," Marchetti said. "Who has been telling me things for two weeks that I've spent twelve years trying not to know. Things about the Spire. About the covenant. About why the storms are failing." He took a slow step forward, hands still raised, bandaged and shaking slightly. "I'm not asking you to believe me. I'm asking you to do the thing you apparently can't stop doing, Inquisitor, which is investigate. Come with us. Cross the river. Look at what's at the end of this road with your own eyes, and then decide whether to put me in irons. If I'm lying, you'll have lost nothing but a week and your dignity, and I'm told dignity is overrated."

"And if you're not lying?"

Marchetti glanced at the boy. Something passed between them — not words, Sera thought, or not only words. Weather.

"Then," he said, "the Inquisition's ledgers are kindling, the Purge was the greatest crime in Corvane's history, and you've got eleven years of signatures to answer for. So I understand," and his voice went quiet and honest, "why you might prefer the irons."

The rain kept falling. Offshore, the thunder rolled over and went back to sleep. And Sera Voss stood in a drowned marsh at the edge of the map, holding a baton that suddenly weighed as much as a ledger full of names, looking at a child who knew her sister's laughter, and understood that she had already made her choice somewhere back along the trail — back at the pie shop, maybe, or the scorch-marked counter, or eleven years ago in a tribunal hall — and that everything since had merely been the long, slow business of catching up to it.

"The ferry," she said at last, holstering the baton. "The drowned river crossing. It's half a day north."

Marchetti blinked. "You know it?"

"Everyone knows it. Nobody crosses it twice." She shouldered her satchel and started walking north, and did not look back to see whether they followed, because if she looked back she might see triumph on the exile's face, or worse, gratitude, and she wasn't ready for either. "The ferryman takes payment in memories. So whatever you're planning to say to me on that boat, Marchetti, say it cheap. I can't afford much."

Behind her, she heard the boy laugh — a bright, startled sound, like the first drop before a storm breaks — and heard Marchetti say, ruefully, "You're going to like her," and hated how much she hoped it was true.

They walked north together into the rain, three fugitives of one kind or another, toward a river that remembered everything and forgot nothing, and the price of crossing it waiting on the far bank like a bill come due.
