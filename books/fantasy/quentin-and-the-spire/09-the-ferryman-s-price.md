## 9. The Ferryman's Price

The drowned river did not look drowned at first glance. It looked like any other river — wide, brown-green, patient — except that it flowed uphill for a stretch near the far bank, and except that the mist over it had a way of forming shapes if you stared too long. Faces, mostly. Or the suggestion of faces, like someone had pressed their thumb into fog.

"That's it," Sera Voss said. "The Sereth."

"You say that like you've met it," Wendell said.

"I've read about it." She adjusted her pack and did not look at him. "In books the Inquisition burned. Which is to say: I read about it before they were burned."

Quentin stood at the water's edge with his bare toes in the mud, utterly unbothered by the cold, watching the mist-forms drift past as though greeting old acquaintances. He raised one small hand. One of the shapes raised what might have been a hand back.

"Boy," Wendell said carefully, "don't make friends with the river."

Quentin glanced up at him with an expression of mild, silent reproach — *I make friends with whoever I like* — and Wendell sighed, because twelve years of hiding had taught him every shade of fear and yet he still could not muster any against a child who fed gulls from his palm.

The ferry came when the light went the color of pewter. It did not come from downstream or upstream; it simply assembled itself out of the mist, plank by plank, until there was a flat barge where there had been nothing, and standing at its stern a figure in a hooded coat of woven reeds. Where a face should have been there was smooth grey water, rippling faintly, reflecting the sky even though the sky was darkening.

Wendell's scars ached. They always ached now, since Corvane, since the doorway of rain, but here they ached in a particular key — the key of something very old paying attention to him.

"Storm-caller," said the ferryman. Its voice was the sound of water finding its level. "And a warden's shadow. And—" The ripple-face turned toward Quentin, and for a moment the whole river seemed to still. "—and a voice. Well."

"We want to cross," Voss said. Her hand rested on her belt where her badge used to sit; she'd left it behind on the marsh road, a small surrender neither of them had commented on.

"Everything crosses," the ferryman agreed. "Nothing crosses free." It gestured with a reed-sleeved arm toward the barge. "My price is not coin. Coin is only memory made dull. I take the memory itself. One each. Something true. Something yours. You will give it, and you will forget you ever had it, and you will feel the hole where it was for the rest of your life."

Silence, except for the river.

"That's a terrible deal," Wendell said.

"It's the only deal," the ferryman said pleasantly.

Voss stepped closer to Wendell, lowering her voice. Inquisitors, apparently, whispered the same way they interrogated — precise, clipped, as if even conspiracy required proper procedure. "We could go around. The marshes loop north."

"Four days through the drowning flats, with a child, with Aldric already a week ahead of us." Wendell shook his head. "No. We pay."

"And what do we pay?" she asked. "Have you thought about that? Because I have been thinking about it for two miles and everything I own that matters is either dead or sitting right here."

"I know," Wendell said quietly. "That's rather the point of it."

* * *

They camped that night within sight of the water, because the ferryman had said the crossing must happen at dawn and dawn meant facing your ledger before you slept. Wendell built no fire. Fires drew wardens, and although Voss was technically no longer a warden, habits die slower than people do.

He sat with his back against a leaning willow and tried to inventory himself.

This was the exercise, he realized. The ferryman hadn't asked for their most precious memory outright, but it didn't need to. Whatever you offered would be weighed, and anything less than irreplaceable would be refused — and you had only so many irreplaceable things. Twelve years ago, Wendell Marchetti had owned a great many memories worth keeping. Now he owned a life of deliberate smallness: charms that barely worked, rooms rented under false names, conversations kept shallow on purpose. Most of his days had been designed to be forgotten.

What remained was the part of him he'd buried deepest.

"Quentin," he said. The boy was lying on his back in the grass, hands folded on his chest like a tiny effigy, watching stars appear. "Do you understand what it's going to ask of you?"

Quentin rolled his head toward him. He nodded. Then, after a moment, he spoke — haltingly, in the careful Common he'd been building word by word across the marshes, each sentence like stepping stones.

"Not... fair. But river. Old rules. Older than bad men." A pause, tongue working around the words. "Older than good men too."

"Yes," Wendell said. "That's about the size of it."

"What will you pay?" Voss asked from the other side of the cold fire pit. She was cleaning a knife she did not need to clean. Inquisitors fidgeted with procedure the way other people fidgeted with prayer beads.

Wendell looked at the dark river, at the mist-shapes drifting along its surface, and told the truth, because tomorrow he wouldn't remember it anyway and tonight it deserved saying aloud once more.

"I was married," he said.

The knife stopped moving.

"Her name was Liora. Before the Purge, before I ran — she wasn't of the order, she was a cartographer, she mapped the flood-plains east of the mountains. She had ink permanently on the side of her hand, always the left, always the same spot, and she laughed—" He stopped. Started again. "She laughed like weather breaking. That awful, huge laugh that embarrassed everyone except me. We married in spring, on a hill above the order's monastery, without asking anyone's permission, because we were young and arrogant and certain the world would bend for us. It rained during the ceremony. Real rain, warm rain, and I — I was a storm-caller, and I couldn't resist. I parted the clouds just over the hill. Just for an hour. Made a little roof of clear sky, and everyone swore it was luck, and Liora looked up at me and knew exactly what I'd done, and she laughed that laugh, and the sun came down through the gap like a blessing I hadn't asked for."

The river murmured. Somewhere out in the mist, a shape drifted past that might have been listening.

"When I fled the order, I fled her too. Not because she betrayed me. Because staying near her would have gotten her killed, and because—" His jaw tightened. "Because I was ashamed. Ashamed people can't bear to be seen by the ones they love. She wrote to me for three years. Letters to a dead drop that stopped being checked. Then she stopped writing, and I told myself that was mercy, hers or mine, and I never let myself wonder which."

Voss had gone very still. "You'll give them your wedding day."

"The whole day. The hill, the rain, the laugh. All of it." He smiled, and it hurt his face. "It seems fitting. I've spent twelve years pretending it didn't matter. Now the pretending becomes permanent, and the crossing gets paid. Efficient."

"That's not efficiency," Voss said. "That's punishment."

"Punishment implies someone's keeping score." He lay back against the willow bark. "Get some sleep, Inquisitor. Tomorrow we find out what the river thinks of our accounts."

Across the ashes, Quentin watched them both with eyes that caught starlight oddly — and said nothing, which from Quentin was its own kind of eloquence.

* * *

Dawn came grey and slow, and the ferry was waiting.

The mist-shapes crowded nearer the bank than they had the day before, and Wendell understood with a lurch that they were previous passengers — or parts of them. The things the river had taken, given shape. He saw what might have been a dancer mid-turn. What might have been a man holding a child's hand. They drifted and drifted, going nowhere, belonging to no one.

"Don't look long," the ferryman advised, sounding genuinely kind about it. "They notice being noticed."

The barge was wider than it had appeared, planks pale as bone and worn smooth by centuries of grief. The ferryman poled them away from shore, and the current took them — that strange uphill stretch, water climbing toward mountains it could see but never touch, and Wendell thought, absurdly, *that's me, that's been me for twelve years.*

"Now," said the ferryman. "Who pays first?"

Quentin stepped forward immediately, chin up, and Wendell grabbed his shoulder before he could speak.

"No. Absolutely not. You don't get to volunteer first, that's — that's my job, I'm the adult, I'm supposed to go first at all the terrible things, that's the entire arrangement."

Quentin gave him a flat look, then spoke in the First Tongue — a short phrase, soft, addressed past Wendell entirely to the ferryman. The water-face rippled with something like amusement.

"He says," the ferryman translated, "that he has ten thousand years of memory and one childhood, and he knows exactly which one he'd sooner lose. But he also says he'll wait. Out of courtesy."

"Out of—" Wendell pinched the bridge of his nose. "Fine. Fine. Me first. Before I find another reason to be a coward."

He faced the ferryman directly. Up close, the ripple-where-a-face-was showed him his own reflection, warped and wavering, a man made of moving water.

"I offer my wedding day," Wendell said. "Spring. The hill above the monastery. Liora. The rain I called, the hour of open sky, her laugh. All of it. Freely given."

The river went quiet in a way rivers should not be able to do. Even the wind held off.

"Freely given is the only kind I take," the ferryman said. "Hold it in your mind. All of it. Do not flinch from any piece. If you withhold a corner of it, the payment fails, and I keep something of my choosing instead. Do you understand?"

"I understand."

"Then let it go."

Wendell closed his eyes and built the day one last time, deliberately, brick by brick, the way he had rebuilt it in the dark a thousand nights: the smell of cut grass, the ink stain on Liora's left hand, the specific weight of her grip, the sound of the rain thinning as he coaxed the clouds apart, the gasp of their handful of witnesses, the sun falling through the gap —

*Liora laughing like weather breaking.*

—and then he opened his hands, physically, palms up, an old storm-caller's gesture for releasing what you held, and the memory poured out of him like heat leaving a stone.

He did not scream. He wanted to. Instead he felt the day lift out of him whole, seamless, and the absence where it had been closed over like water over a dropped jewel — smooth, unmarked, and hollow. He knew, abstractly, that he had been married. The fact sat in his mind like a word in a language he no longer spoke. There had been someone. Her name was gone. The hill was gone. The laugh—

There had been a laugh. He was almost sure there had been a laugh.

His knees buckled. Quentin caught him — a small boy catching a grown man shouldn't have worked, but Quentin's hands were steady as mooring posts, and Wendell clung to them and breathed.

"The account is paid," the ferryman said gently. "Sit, storm-caller. Breathe. The hole fades to an ache, and the ache becomes a companion. Better company than most."

Wendell sat. He was shaking. He could not remember why, which was somehow worse.

"Inquisitor," said the ferryman, turning its ripple-face toward Voss. "Your turn."

* * *

She stood at the prow with her spine straight, because of course she did. Wendell watched her gather herself the way she gathered evidence — completely, methodically, refusing shortcuts.

"I offer," Voss began, and stopped.

The river waited. Rivers, Wendell was learning, were patient in a way institutions pretended to be.

"My sister's face," Voss said finally. "Mira Voss. She died eleven years ago in the third Purge — she was a hedge-witch, nothing dangerous, she lit candles that never guttered and found lost things, and the Inquisition came for her anyway, and I signed the district order that put her street on the list because I believed the work was clean and the lists were accurate." Her voice did not waver, which was how Wendell knew how much it cost. "I offer her face. Every version of it. Her at four years old with a scraped knee. Her at nineteen, laughing at me in my first uniform. Her the last time I saw her, through a crowd, before they took her. I keep her name. I keep that she existed and that I failed her. But I give the river her face, freely given, so that I can cross this water and tear down the machine that killed her."

"Mira," Wendell said softly, without meaning to. The name surfaced in him like a fish rising — Voss had spoken of her once, on the road, three sentences in the dark. *Mira could find anything. Except a way out.*

"Hold it all," the ferryman said. "Do not flinch."

"I don't flinch," Sera Voss said, and let it go.

She made no sound at all. That was the worst part. She simply stood straighter and straighter as it lifted out of her, a woman being emptied of a face while refusing to bend, and when it was done her eyes were wet and furious and fixed on the far shore.

"She was—" Voss started, and her brow furrowed, and Wendell watched her reach for something and close her hand on empty air. "She was my sister. Mira. I don't—" Her breath came ragged. "I know her name. I know I loved her. Why can't I see her?"

"Because you paid," the ferryman said, with a gentleness that seemed to cost it something. "The living carry their dead in images. I took the images. The love remains. Love is heavier than faces; it doesn't fit through my toll."

Voss sat down hard on the prow bench. Quentin moved to her without being asked and put his hand flat on her knee, and she covered it with both of hers like a drowning woman gripping driftwood, and cried without making a single sound, and Wendell looked away to give her the privacy of it.

* * *

The far shore grew slowly, the way important shores do. Mist thinned. The uphill current gentled. And somewhere in the middle of the crossing, in the long grey silence, Wendell became aware that Quentin was humming.

Not a tune. A language. The First Tongue, sung low, addressed — Wendell realized with a chill that had nothing to do with cold — to the mist-shapes drifting alongside the barge. The lost memories. The dancer, the man with the child. As they passed, the shapes turned toward the boy's voice like flowers toward sun, and for a heartbeat each one seemed *settled*, seemed calm, before drifting on behind them.

"What are you telling them?" Wendell asked.

Quentin considered. "That they were loved," he said. "Once. By someone. That the someone is walking around up there missing them, and missing is a kind of keeping." He shrugged, a small, old gesture. "Someone should tell them. River won't."

The ferryman poled in silence, but its water-face rippled steadily, and Wendell could have sworn the ripples formed, for just a moment, something like gratitude.

When the keel touched the far bank, none of them moved at first.

"There it is," Voss said at last, hoarse. Ahead, beyond the last of the marsh, the foothills rose into mountains, and high among them, half-lost in cloud, stood a needle of stone that pierced the sky itself. The Spire. It had been a story his whole life. Now it was a destination, and Aldric was somewhere between here and there with a stolen child-shaped key and a week's head start.

"One more thing," the ferryman said as they stepped ashore. Its voice had changed — quieter, almost human. "A gift, since you paid well. Storm-caller: you gave me the happiest day of your life. So hear this. The woman whose name you cannot recall — she survived the Purges. She lives. She maps the eastern floods still, ink on her left hand. I keep many things. I do not keep them carelessly."

Wendell stood very still on the muddy bank.

"Why tell me that?" he managed. "Why give me anything back?"

"Because you crossed freely," the ferryman said, "and because the bridge-child asked me to." Its ripple-face inclined, very slightly, toward Quentin. "Now go. The mountain is cruel, and the man ahead of you is crueler, and I think — though I am only a river — that the world has very little time left for cruelty."

They climbed the bank. Behind them, the mist-shapes watched them go, and ahead the Spire stood in its collar of cloud like a needle waiting for thread.

Wendell flexed his scarred, half-burned hands and started walking.

He did not know who Liora was anymore. But his feet turned east, toward the floods, without being told — and he suspected, in the hollow place where a laugh used to live, that some part of him always would.
