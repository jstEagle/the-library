## 10. Ashes of the Order

The monastery announced itself long before they saw it.

Wendell felt it first — a wrongness in the air, like a chord played one note flat — and then Quentin's hand tightened around his sleeve, and then the road bent around a shoulder of granite and there it was: the Eyrie of Saint Vellum, or what remained of it. The great tower had lost its crown to some old violence; the cloisters stood open to the sky like a rib cage picked clean by crows. Ivy had done its patient, indifferent work over the walls where lightning had not. The bell that had once called them to dawn-prayer hung silent in its arch, green with age, tilted at an angle that suggested it had given up on ever ringing again.

"Home sweet home," Wendell said.

Quentin looked up at him with the particular expression the boy reserved for Wendell's jokes — a solemn, slightly worried frown, as though humor were a dialect he was still learning to parse. Twelve days on the road together, and the boy had collected perhaps forty common words, most of them nouns, several of them concerning food. He could say *bread*. He could say *cold*. He could say *Wendell* in a way that made the name sound like something worth keeping.

"You lived here?" Quentin asked. Slowly, carefully, each word placed like a stone across a stream.

"Eleven years." Wendell squinted at the ruin. "They said I'd remember it forever. They were right, though I don't think that's what they meant."

The truth was that remembering hurt less than he'd feared. He had spent twelve years imagining this moment — the return, if it ever came, as a wound reopening. But the monastery looked smaller than memory had kept it. Shabbier. The storm-callers of his youth had been giants in his mind, men and women who spoke thunder into obedience, who stood on the tower-top in gale winds without rope or fear. Looking at the broken arches now, he understood that he had been measuring the building against the boy he'd been when he left it.

He was not that boy anymore. He was a man whose hands still bore the shine of old burn-scars, traveling with a mute child who wasn't mute at all but simply waiting for a language worth speaking.

"We should go around," Wendell said, and meant it. Every instinct honed across twelve years of deliberate smallness told him that ruins on the mountain road were exactly the kind of place hunters watched. "There's a shepherd's track past the scree—"

Quentin did not go around. Quentin walked directly toward the front gate with the untroubled certainty of weather itself, and Wendell, after a heartbeat of useless protest, followed.

That was the thing about the boy no one warned you about. You could spend twelve years learning to be invisible, and then a child would walk through your invisibility like fog through an open window.

* * *

The gate stood open. That was his second warning. The first had been the flat note in the air, but the open gate confirmed it: nobody abandoned a door in these mountains, not even a ruined door, because wood was scarce and wind was not. Doors here were closed out of habit older than the monastery itself.

Beyond the gate, the outer court had been swept clean. That was his third warning, and by far the worst. Ruins did not sweep their own courts.

Someone had repaired the eastern range of the cloister — new timber in the roof, glass in the windows, smoke rising from a chimney in a thin gray thread that rose straight up in the windless air. Straight up. Wendell stared at that thread of smoke for a long moment, because smoke that rose straight meant stillness, and stillness at altitude, in these mountains, in this season, meant only one thing.

A storm-caller was holding the sky still.

"Quentin." His voice came out low and rough. "Behind me. Now."

The boy, to his credit, moved. Wendell felt him settle at his back like a small warm shadow, and absurdly — given everything, given the burned hands and the wardens and the Inquisitor who might be following them down the length of the drowned river valley — that small weight steadied him more than any charm he had ever sold.

They crossed the court. And then the doors of the refectory opened, and Wendell Marchetti stopped pretending he hadn't known, somewhere beneath the dread, exactly who he would find.

Aldric Vane had gone white at the temples and gray through the beard, but otherwise the years had treated him like a favored son. He stood in the doorway wearing storm-caller gray, the old order's colors, cut finer than any robe Wendell remembered — silver worked along the cuffs in patterns that made Wendell's scars itch. Behind him, filling the refectory doorway and the windows above it, stood perhaps two dozen figures in similar gray. Storm-callers. Living ones, whole ones, when Wendell had believed for twelve years that the Purge had taken nearly all of them.

"Well," Aldric said. His voice was exactly as Wendell remembered it — warm, deep, unhurried, the voice of a man who had never once needed to raise it against a student. "You're late. Dinner's cold."

* * *

They sat at the long table where Wendell had eaten eleven years of meals, under rafters he had helped mend as a novice, and Aldric poured wine from a jug with the easy grace of a host who had never doubted his guest would come. Quentin sat pressed against Wendell's side, watching everything with those wide gray eyes that saw too much, and the other storm-callers watched the boy too — openly, hungrily, in a way that raised every hair Wendell owned.

"You look well," Aldric said. "Exile agrees with you. Who would have thought?"

"I sell charms," Wendell said. "Rain-catchers. Luck-knots. Mostly they don't work."

"I know. I've bought six." Aldric smiled. "I keep them on my desk. They're very bad, Wendell. It's almost impressive."

Despite himself — despite the flat sky and the hungry watchers and the twelve years of ash between them — Wendell felt his mouth twitch. That was the trap of Aldric, and always had been. The man was genuinely funny. He had been the one who smuggled wine to the novices, who taught lessons with jokes folded inside them, who once convinced an entire class that the First Tongue word for *cloud* also meant *sheep*, which had led to a visiting dignitary being told, with great solemnity, that the weather forecast depended on the flock. Wendell had loved him. That was the whole horror of sitting here now: some stubborn root of that love had survived the burning, and it stirred in his chest like a scar waking before rain.

"The Purge," Wendell said. "They burned the Eyrie. I watched the light from the coast. Everyone said the order was dead."

"The order was dead," Aldric agreed pleasantly. "The order deserved to die. We had grown fat and proud and stupid, hoarding secrets, calling ourselves shepherds of the storm while we couldn't even keep the covenant renewed." He turned his cup slowly on the table. "But storm-callers aren't the order, Wendell. Storm-callers are just people who hear the sky. You can burn a building. You can't burn an ear."

"How many?"

"Enough. More than enough." Aldric glanced at Quentin, and for just a moment the pleasant warmth in his face went utterly, perfectly still — a lake freezing over. "More than we need. What we've lacked, all these years, was a reason. A direction. And then reports started reaching us. A charm-seller in Corvane whose rain-charms worked too well. Wardens chasing a man out of a shop through a door made of rain." He spread his hands. "And a child. A child who speaks the First Tongue like it's his mother's milk."

"He's a boy," Wendell said. "He's frightened and tired and he hasn't had enough bread today, which is a crime I intend to correct immediately—"

"Wendell." Aldric said his name gently, the way you'd steady a spooked horse. "Don't perform for me. I taught you. I know every face you own, including that one." He leaned forward. "The covenant is failing. You've seen it. You've dreamed it — oh, don't look surprised, we all dream it now, everyone who can hear the sky. The storms going silent, realm by realm. Ael starving. The coast will drown within ten years, maybe five, and every soul who carries the gift will burn out from the inside like a lamp out of oil. Including you. Especially you — you've been running on a covenant that isn't feeding you for twelve years, and I'd wager my tower your hands ache every night."

Wendell said nothing, because his hands ached every night, and there was no dignity in confirming it.

"So here is what I offer," Aldric said. "Not renewal. Renewal means the old bargain — a heart on the altar, a willing sacrifice, some poor fool climbing the Spire to give himself away so the storms stay fed for another three centuries. I read the same histories you did. I knelt at the same altar. I am not interested in feeding the machine anymore." His eyes gleamed. "I mean to take it. Bind the covenant's power to living hands instead of a dead bargain. No more sacrifice. No more exile. Magic free, magic *ours*, and every warden and Inquisitor in the world learning to bow to the people they spent a generation burning."

Around the table, the storm-callers murmured assent. One woman — Wendell half-recognized her, a novice from the year below, Marta, who used to cry during thunder-practice — looked at Wendell with naked hope, as if he were the answer to a prayer she'd been saying for years.

"And the boy?" Wendell asked quietly.

"A key," Aldric said. "Nothing more. The child opens the way; we hold the door. He'll want for nothing. He'll be honored. Protected. Loved, even — I give you my word on that."

It was a good speech. That was the terrible thing. It was a good speech delivered by a good voice to a room full of people who had earned their anger honestly, and if Wendell had heard it twelve years ago, raw from the Purge, he might have wept and signed his name in lightning.

But he had spent twelve years among ordinary frightened people, selling charms that mostly didn't work, and he had learned to hear the difference between mercy and appetite dressed in mercy's clothes.

"You said *key*," Wendell said. "Keys get used. Keys get worn down. Keys get *broken* in locks, Aldric, that's practically their profession."

"He's a bridge, then—"

"Bridges get walked on too." Wendell set down his cup. "I've spent twelve years being small, and I'll tell you what I learned about power, since apparently I traveled a thousand miles to say it to your face. Power doesn't ask. That's the whole of it. It never asks twice, it barely asks once, and everyone who serves it tells themselves they're the exception until the day it spends them." He nodded at Quentin, small and grave beside him. "This child walked out of a dying world and crossed the sea to find me — me, of all the failed, frightened, burnt-up men in creation — because someone in Ael believed I'd listen. Not take. *Listen.* So no, old man. I'm not helping you seize anything."

The silence that followed had weight, like the pause before lightning.

Aldric sighed, and for a moment — one strange, suspended moment — he looked old, and tired, and genuinely sad. "I hoped you'd understand," he said. "I truly did. Absolution was on the table, Wendell. Everything you ran from, forgiven. Everything you lost, restored. Your name cleared. Your hands healed." He rose, and the storm-callers rose with him, twenty-odd faces gray and grim in the lamplight. "You should have taken the wine and stayed for dinner."

Outside, the held-still sky finally moved. Clouds began to turn overhead, slow and deliberate, wheeling around the monastery's broken tower like water around a drain.

"You'll rest tonight regardless," Aldric said, gentler now, which was somehow worse. "You've walked far. Marta will show you to the east cells — the ones with the good shutters." A pause, delicate as a blade laid flat. "The boy will sleep in the tower. Just for safekeeping. You understand."

Wendell's blood went cold and then hot and then very, very quiet.

"He stays with me," Wendell said.

"That," said Aldric, with real regret, "is the one thing I'm afraid I can't allow."

Quentin's hand found Wendell's sleeve. And in the First Tongue — soft, clear, unmistakable, a language Wendell knew only in fragments and dreams but understood in his bones the way you understand fire — the boy spoke a single sentence to the room.

Every storm-caller present flinched. The lamps guttered. Somewhere above, the wheeling clouds stopped turning.

Aldric stared at the child, and Wendell watched the last of the mentor he had loved disappear behind something colder and older and infinitely more careful.

"Yes," Aldric breathed. "*Yes.* There it is. Oh, Wendell — you have no idea what you've been carrying."

The doors of the refectory closed. All of them, at once, on their own.
