## 11. Quentin Speaks

Before I was a boy, I was a word.

That is the first thing you must know, though I did not know it myself for a long time. In Ael — in the storm-country, where the wind has bones and the rain remembers your name — children are not born the way children are born here. We are *spoken*. Our mothers and fathers gather at the high windows during the long storms and they sing into the lightning, and if the lightning loves them, it comes down through the roof-stones and leaves something behind: a small warm weight of thunder, shaped like a child.

I was spoken by two mothers and one father, which is unusual, and by a very old storm called Meren-of-the-Long-Evening, which is more unusual still. Four voices to make one boy. The elders said this meant I would be heavy with purpose. I mostly thought it meant I was loud inside.

I was right about that part, anyway.

* * *

I should tell you what dying sounds like, because that is how my story truly begins, though I was too young then to understand what I was hearing.

It sounds like quiet.

You would think a realm made of storms would die loudly — all crashing and burning, the way things end here below, with smoke and shouting. But Ael died quietly. One storm at a time, like lamps going out in a great dark house. First the little ones, the squalls that lived in the valleys and chased each other for sport. Then the mid-sized ones, the steady rains who kept the rivers of light running. Each time one went out, the whole sky felt smaller. Like a coat that had been warm suddenly having holes in it.

And the ones who remained grew hungry.

I remember asking my mother — the one whose voice is most like low thunder — why the storms were going silent. She held me against her shoulder, and her shoulder rumbled the way shoulders do when they are trying not to weep, and she said:

"The covenant thins, little word. The bridge between us and the world below grows old. And bridges, when no one mends them, become walls."

I did not understand. Bridges were stone. Walls were stone. How could one become the other? But the elders understood, and their faces were grey as rainless clouds, and that frightened me more than any explanation could have.

The covenant — I will say it plainly now, because I am tired of the word being passed around like a coin nobody wants to hold — the covenant was an agreement, older than any kingdom below, between Ael and the world of men. The storms would give the coast its rains and its wild weather and its magic; the world below would give back *listening*. Voices. Every generation, there were those below who spoke the First Tongue, the language that storms dream in, and their speaking was like food, like sunlight, like a hand held in the dark. That was the bargain. They gave us voice; we gave them wonder.

But the world below forgot. Kingdom by kingdom, city by city, people decided that storms were only weather and magic was only trouble, and the listening stopped. Corvane was the worst of them. Corvane hated magic so thoroughly that even its name for wonder was a swear.

So Ael starved. Slowly. Politely. The way old people starve when they are too proud to ask for bread.

Until the night the elders came to my window.

* * *

I was young — young even by the counting of my kind, where childhood lasts as long as the first great storm of your life. Mine was named Sorrowful-Bright, and she had not yet finished her first circuit of the sky. The elders stood in my chamber like a row of broken masts, and the eldest, a woman whose body was half rain already, fading at the edges the way the starving do, took my face in her hands.

Her hands were cold and wet and gentle, and I remember thinking: *this is what it feels like to be chosen*, and also: *I do not want this*, and both thoughts were true, and neither one won.

"You are born of both realms," she said. "Spoken by storm, quickened below — the last such child. When the covenant fails entirely, the way between worlds will close forever, and everything on either side of it will be lesser for the closing. Unless someone crosses. Unless someone carries the First Tongue into the world below and finds the way to mend the bridge."

"Find it how?" I asked. I was a child. Children ask this question because adults so rarely answer it honestly.

"We do not know," she said, and I loved her for that, even as it terrified me. "But you will know it when you find it. You are a word, Quentin. Words know where they belong."

They opened the door in the storm — the old crossing, thin as paper now, tearing a little as I went through, the way old cloth tears — and they sang me down through ten thousand miles of falling sky, and I landed in the world below in the shape of a boy, mute, because the First Tongue does not fit easily in a human mouth, and I lay in a ditch outside the city of Corvane and cried without sound.

That is how I arrived. Not with trumpets. With mud.

* * *

Now I must tell you about the dreams, because the dreams are how I found him, and finding him was the only thing I ever did that felt entirely mine.

The elders had given me instructions like a list: go among them, listen for the speakers, seek the remnant of the order, find one who can climb. But lists are cold things, and I was small, and Corvane was enormous and smelled of fish and fear. For a season I survived in the ways street children survive, which I will not describe except to say that I learned which rubbish-heaps warm fastest after sunset and that dogs can be friends if you share honestly.

All the while, at night, I listened.

Here is a thing about being made of spoken words: you can hear other people's dreaming. It comes to you the way smells come, unasked. Most humans' dreams were quiet little things — bread, boats, a person they missed, a debt they feared. Sweet and small, like moths. But every few nights, from somewhere across the city, there came a dream so loud it woke me.

Storms in it. Real storms, remembered from inside, from the center, from the place where the lightning decides. A man standing in the middle of his own thunder with his hands raised, laughing — actually *laughing* — and then the dream would turn, as dreams do, and the laughter would curdle, and there would be fire, and a friend's face, and a door closing, and twelve years of a man folding himself smaller and smaller until he fit inside a shop barely wider than his shoulders, selling charms that worked just badly enough to keep him safe.

I knew who he was before I ever saw him. Everyone in Ael above knew the shape of that grief; it rang up through the failing bridge like a bell rung underwater. Wendell Marchetti, storm-caller, exile, coward — no. I will not write that word, even knowing he would. Frightened man. There is a difference, and I have had a long time to think about it, and the difference is that cowards stay frightened and frightened men keep walking anyway, slowly, in the wrong direction sometimes, but walking.

His dreams were the loudest in the whole sleeping city. Loudest with power and loudest with shame, which is a particular kind of loudness that the First Tongue hears best of all, because shame speaks our language. Shame is a storm that turned inward.

The elders had said: *seek the remnant.* The remnant of the order was scattered, hidden, hunted. But here was one remnant, alive, close, dreaming loud enough for me to follow the sound.

I chose him. I want that recorded clearly, in case anyone ever says otherwise, in case he ever says otherwise. Nobody sent me to Wendell Marchetti specifically. I picked him out of all the sleepers in Corvane the way you pick a shell out of a hundred shells on a beach — because when you lift it to your ear, it sounds like the sea.

His sounded like the storm.

* * *

The night I knocked on his door, I was more frightened than I had been since leaving Ael.

I had watched him for three days first. (This is not stalking. This is reconnaissance. Though I admit the difference is mostly a matter of whether you get caught.) He was smaller than his dreams. That surprised me. Dream-men stand very tall, but the man himself came only to my chin-and-a-bit, with grey coming into his beard early, and hands that never fully stilled — always fidgeting with a charm, a string, a coin — as if stillness were a country he'd been exiled from twice.

He talked to his wares. I heard him through the window, apologizing to a rain-charm for overcharging a customer. *"You're worth four coppers, don't let him tell you three, you beautiful little fraud."* I decided in that moment, with the absolute certainty available only to children and storms, that this was a good man. Adults take years to decide these things and are wrong half the time. Children decide in an instant and are wrong only slightly less than half the time, but we get to be smug about the instances we get right.

Still, when I knocked, my heart went like a drum in a hard rain.

Because here is what I had not told the elders, what I have not told anyone until this page: I was afraid he wouldn't hear me. Not the knocking — anyone hears knocking. I mean *hear* me. The First Tongue was wearing me out. Every day below, my true voice got heavier in my chest, like a stone I carried under my ribs, and the longer I stayed silent, the more I feared the stone would win, that I would arrive at the Spire — if I ever found the Spire — as nothing but a mute boy who had once been a word, carrying a word that had gone out.

And when he opened the door, and I looked up at him, and I reached down under my ribs and lifted that stone with both hands and spoke —

— and his eyes changed. Something behind them woke up. Twelve years of folded-small man, and in one breath he unfolded, just a little, just enough.

He heard me.

I have been asked — by Voss, later, in her gruff way, pretending not to care — why him. Why not a stronger man, a braver man, a man with two working hands and a better opinion of himself. And I never had a good answer that wasn't embarrassing. So here is the embarrassing answer, written down where everyone can see it:

Because his dreams were loud. Because inside the loudest dream was the saddest man. Because the saddest man was still, deep down under twelve years of ash, *laughing in the middle of his own thunder*, and I thought: that laugh belongs to Ael. That laugh is a piece of home that fell down here and thinks it's garbage. Somebody should pick it up.

Also, he apologized to his charms. I stand by that.

* * *

Living in the shop was strange and wonderful and smelled of dust and copper wire.

I learned that Wendell hums when he works, always the same tune, and that when I finally asked him — in halting, clumsy common speech, my mouth still learning the shapes of human words — what the tune was, he went pink and said it was nothing, and it took me a month to learn it was a lullaby from the order, from the monastery, from the life he buried. He hummed his grave every day and didn't notice.

I learned that he ate standing up, like a man who expects to be interrupted, and that he left a saucer of milk out for a cat that never came, and when I asked why, he said, "Habit," in a voice that meant *grief*, and I drank the milk myself for a week before he caught on, and when he caught on he laughed — a real one, short and rusty like a gate unused — and I counted that laugh as the second greatest achievement of my mission so far.

I learned that his hands hurt. I didn't know yet how much, or why — that knowledge was waiting for me further up the mountain, the way bad news always waits politely at higher altitudes — but I could feel it humming in him at night, an ache with an old shape, and sometimes I would put my small hand over his big damaged one and he would pretend not to notice, and I would pretend not to notice him pretending, and that is most of love, I think. Mutual noticing-without-noticing.

But I have gotten ahead of myself, and a word must learn to walk before it runs, so let me return to the order of things.

The charms began working. Of course they did — I couldn't help it. Being near me was like being near the memory of weather. His rain-charms rained properly, his calm-wind charms calmed winds that had no business calming, and the neighbors noticed, and the neighbors in Corvane notice the way knives notice. I watched him grow afraid again, watched him fold himself smaller by the hour, and I grieved, because I was doing to him exactly what the elders had done to me: handing a person a purpose heavy enough to bend them.

At night I dreamed of home, and my dreams leaked, the way mine do, and some of the leaking got into him. I didn't mean it. Or — no, honesty — I did mean it. I needed him to *remember*. You cannot ask a man to climb to the sky's root for a covenant he doesn't believe in. So I showed him Ael in his sleep: the singing lightning, the valley-squalls playing tag, the Spire itself, white and endless, rooted in the world and crowned in the storm-realm both, the hinge of everything. And I showed him a face I found in the oldest layer of the memories, a face from the covenant's making, and watched him wake gasping with a name on his lips:

*Aldric.*

His mentor. His betrayer-to-be, though he didn't know that part yet. Neither did I, then, and I wish — well. Wishes are for people with time to spare.

* * *

There is one more thing I should set down, and my hand hesitates on the page, because it is the heaviest thing I know.

I know why the elders chose a child.

It is not because children are pure. Children are not pure; I have bitten people. It is not because children are innocent; innocence is just ignorance with good lighting, and I have seen enough now to lose mine several times over.

It is because the covenant demands willingness, and willingness cannot be commanded or bought or shamed into being. Adults are too full of debts and duties to be willing at anything. Their yes is always tangled with ought. But a child's yes — a true one, given with open eyes, after seeing the whole price — a child's yes is the rarest currency in either realm.

I think the elders hoped I would be small enough to say it.

Some nights I hate them a little for that. Some nights I understand them completely, which is worse. And most nights, lately, I lie awake next to the sound of Wendell snoring — he snores like distant summer thunder, which I find unbearably comforting and he finds mortifying — and I practice saying yes in my head, in the First Tongue, where the word is less a word than a door opening, and I try to imagine what walks through it.

Then, three nights ago, everything changed.

We were on the road by then, past the marshes, past the ferry, walking with the Inquisitor who was no longer quite an Inquisitor — Sera, who carries her sister's absence the way Wendell carries his hands — and we had come within sight of the mountains, and the old monastery on their shoulder, where Wendell's whole face went complicated as a storm front.

And I heard it.

Not with my ears. With the place under my ribs where the First Tongue lives. A voice, vast and starving, calling up from beneath the world — from the Spire's root, far beyond the monastery — calling in my own language, calling *home, home, home,* the way a lost child calls, except the child was a god-sized silence and it was calling for anyone at all who could answer.

And underneath that voice, closer, wronger, came another sound. Boots on mountain stone. Many boots. And riding the wind down from the monastery came a scent I knew from my elders' stories and from the oldest layers of the dreams: ozone and incense and ambition.

Aldric was already there.

Wendell saw the banners before I did. I felt his heart change rhythm — I always feel it; it is the loudest drum I know — and he put his burned hand on my shoulder, and his voice came out flat and careful, the voice of a man stepping onto ice:

"Quentin. Whatever happens when we reach that gate — whatever he offers, whatever he shows you — you come to me first. Promise me."

I promised. Children promise easily; we haven't yet learned how much a promise weighs.

I regretted it almost immediately. Because I could feel, humming up through the soles of my feet from the stones of the mountain road, the covenant failing another degree — another storm above going quiet, another lamp out in the great dark house — and I understood, with the terrible clarity that is the one inheritance my elders did give me freely, that whatever waited for us in the ruins of his old life,

Wendell was going to need my promise far more than I was going to need it.

And I was no longer entirely sure I intended to keep it.
