## 12. The Betrayal at the Monastery

The monastery of Saint Vellum-and-Thunder — as the novices had irreverently called it, though never within earshot of Aldric — sat on its shoulder of mountain like an old man who had given up arguing with the weather.

Wendell had not seen it in twelve years, and it was worse than he remembered. Not ruined, exactly. Ruin would have been honest. Instead the place had been *tidied*. The shattered bell tower rebuilt in pale new stone that didn't match the old grey walls. The courtyard swept clean of moss. The great oak doors re-hung and oiled, their iron hinges gleaming like a lie told with a straight face.

"Someone's been keeping house," Voss said beside him. She had her hood up against the wind, her hand resting on the ledger she still carried out of habit, though she'd stopped writing in it somewhere past the drowned river. "Monasteries don't tidy themselves."

"No," Wendell said. His hands ached inside their wrappings — the burns from the rain-door had scabbed over into something tight and shiny, and every flex of his fingers felt like pulling stitches he couldn't see. "Someone's been *claiming*."

Quentin stood between them, small and silent as ever, looking up at the doors. The boy had spoken more on the road — halting common words, fragments of the First Tongue that made the air taste like copper — but now he said nothing at all. He only reached up and took the hem of Wendell's coat in one fist, the way he had the first night, twelve days ago, when he'd knocked on a charm-seller's door and spoken words that made old scars ache.

"It'll be all right," Wendell told him, which was a lie, and Quentin looked up at him with those storm-grey eyes and clearly knew it was a lie, because children always know, and said nothing, which was worse.

* * *

Aldric received them in the refectory.

That was the first wrong thing. The second was the table — the long scarred plank where forty boys had once eaten gruel and dared each other to lick the lightning-rods in winter — replaced with polished darkwood, laid with actual plates. And the third wrong thing was the people: a dozen figures in storm-caller grey, seated and eating bread and cheese like it was any evening, except that storm-callers hadn't gathered openly anywhere in the world since the Purge, and these ones looked *well*. Fed. Rested. Their eyes clear instead of scorched.

"Wendell." Aldric rose from the head of the table, and for one treacherous heartbeat he was exactly what Wendell had spent twelve years trying to forget: broad-shouldered, silver-bearded, his voice like distant thunder heard through a window on a summer afternoon. He opened his arms. "Boy. Look at you."

"Don't," Wendell said.

Aldric let his arms fall. Something flickered across his face — hurt, or a very good imitation of it — and then he smiled, warm as a hearth, and gestured to the table. "Sit. Both of you. Eat. You've walked half the world to get here; the least I can do is feed you before we argue about how to save it."

Voss didn't sit. She stood with her back to the wall, watching the dozen grey-robed figures the way she watched everything: like a woman counting exits and estimating how many bodies each exit could hold.

Wendell sat, because his legs were shaking and pride was a luxury for people with working hands. Quentin climbed onto the bench beside him and did not touch the food.

"You knew we were coming," Wendell said.

"I've known since Corvane burned a hole in its own sky the night you left it." Aldric poured wine himself, no servant, an old habit of humility he wore like a ring. "A doorway of rain, Wendell. In a city full of wardens. Twelve years of hiding and you spend your whole purse in one night." He set the cup before Wendell gently. "I confess I was proud. And furious. Mostly furious."

"The Purge—"

"Was necessary. Was monstrous. Was both, and I have carried both for twelve years while you carried nothing but your own guilt and a stall full of charms that barely worked." Aldric's voice stayed gentle, which somehow made it worse. "I don't blame you for running. I blamed myself enough for both of us. But the covenant is failing, boy. You've seen it — the boy has shown you. Storms going silent. Callers burning out from the inside like bad candles. The coast will drown within a generation unless someone renews the pact at the Spire."

"So renew it," Wendell said. "That's why we came. That's why *he* came." He put his bandaged hand on Quentin's shoulder. The boy leaned into it, slight as a bird.

"And so we shall." Aldric sat down across from him, folding his hands, and for a moment the old warmth was real — Wendell could almost believe it. "But renewal requires sacrifice, Wendell. A willing heart. A storm-caller's heart, freely given. And I have watched too many good people burn to believe the world deserves another one." He leaned forward. "Unless."

"Unless what?"

"Unless the covenant doesn't need a sacrifice at all. It needs a *key*." Aldric's eyes went, very briefly, to Quentin. "The last true voice of Ael. Born of both realms. If we bind the boy to the Spire — not kill him, Wendell, never that, just *bind* him — the covenant holds forever. No more sacrifices. Ever again. Every storm-caller who comes after us lives. The coast is saved. Magic walks free in the world." He spread his hands. "All it costs is one child's freedom."

The silence stretched. Somewhere above them, wind worried at the new bell tower.

"That's not renewal," Wendell said quietly. "That's a cage with better masonry."

"It's *mercy*—"

"It's the same arithmetic the Inquisition used!" Wendell was on his feet without deciding to stand, his chair scraping, and half the grey robes at the table went tense. "One child, weighed against the world. I've heard that sum before, Aldric. I watched them do it in the Purge. My friend Tam did that sum with his own life and the answer was *acceptable losses*, and I ran, and you—" His voice cracked. He hated that it cracked. "You signed the order."

For just a moment, something moved behind Aldric's eyes — old and tired and possibly even ashamed. Then it closed, like a door.

"I did what kept our people alive," he said. "And I will do what keeps everyone alive. You're tired, Wendell. You're burned and grieving and you've been carrying this child's weight up a mountain. Rest tonight. We leave for the Spire at dawn — all of us. You can decide by morning whether you'd rather be a founder or a footnote."

He turned away to speak to one of the grey robes, and the audience was over.

* * *

They gave Wendell a cell. Of course they did. His old cell, third floor, east side, with the window that whistled in high wind — a detail so precisely cruel it had to be deliberate. On the sill, someone had left a candle and a strip of dried meat, the way the kitchen brothers used to leave treats for boys who'd been punished.

Quentin wouldn't stay in the cell they offered him. He appeared at Wendell's door after lights-out, slipped inside like weather through a crack, and sat on the narrow bed with his knees drawn up.

"We're leaving," Wendell whispered. "Tonight. Back down the mountain, around, whatever it takes. I'm not letting him near you."

Quentin shook his head slowly.

"No?"

The boy pressed his hand flat against his own chest, then pointed up — toward the Spire, invisible beyond the walls, but unmistakable anyway. Then he touched Wendell's chest. Then his own again. His meaning arrived without language, the way his meanings always did: *it has to be me, and you have to be there.*

"There are other ways," Wendell said. "There have to be other ways. I didn't walk twelve years and lose everything just to hand you to another man who thinks he knows best."

Quentin reached out and took Wendell's bandaged hand in both of his small ones. Warmth seeped through the wrapping — not healing, exactly, but easing, like a hot cloth on a bruise. And in the warmth, unasked, came an image: the two of them, on the road, the boy laughing — actually laughing, mouth open, soundless — at some joke Wendell couldn't remember making. An image of ordinary days that hadn't happened yet, or might never.

*Don't decide for me,* the image said. *You of all people.*

Wendell laughed, wetly, despite everything. "You're twelve years old and you're already wiser than my entire order."

Boots in the corridor. Many boots, moving fast, not trying to be quiet.

Wendell was on his feet when the door burst open, and the last thing he saw clearly was Quentin turning toward the noise, calm as deep water, as if he had known — as if he had perhaps been buying time for this conversation knowing it would end here — and then the grey robes were everywhere, and someone struck Wendell across the temple, and the floor came up kind and dark.

* * *

He woke to cold and the smell of pine, and for a merciful moment he didn't remember. Then his hands reported in — both of them screaming now, the burns torn open, and when he tried to push himself up off the frozen mud of the mountain road he found his right wrist wouldn't take his weight at all. It folded. Something inside crunched, softly, the way ice does.

He screamed. There was no one to hear it, which was either mercy or insult.

They had dumped him off the road's edge, ten feet down a slope, like refuse. Above him the monastery glowed with lamplight. Below him the road switchbacked down into darkness, toward valleys and rivers and the whole indifferent world. And somewhere ahead, higher, past the tree line, torches were moving — a procession climbing toward the Spire, and at its center, wrapped in Aldric's own grey cloak, a small figure walking with terrible docility.

"No," Wendell said. It came out as a croak. He tried again. "No — Quentin!"

The wind took his voice and gave nothing back.

He crawled up the slope. It took him four attempts and cost him skin from his elbows and a fresh white flare of agony from the wrist each time, and when he finally hauled himself onto the road the procession was a quarter-mile gone, torchlight bobbing up the switchbacks like a string of dying stars.

He tried to call the storm. It was the stupidest thing he had ever done — burned hands, broken wrist, twelve years out of practice — and the sky above the ridge answered with a single sullen flicker and then silence, and the effort dropped him to his knees and left him tasting blood.

Twelve years. Twelve years of being small, of selling charms that barely worked, of choosing survival over everything — and in one night he had lost the boy, lost the argument, and lost even the dignity of a proper fight. He knelt in the middle of the mountain road with his ruined hands in his lap and understood, with total clarity, that this was the bottom. There was no further down. He had arrived.

"Well," he said to the empty road. "This is humiliating."

"You're telling me."

Sera Voss stepped out of the trees.

She looked terrible — mud to the knee, a cut over one eyebrow, her Inquisitor's coat exchanged for a plain oilskin that fit her badly. She had a sword, though, and a coil of rope over one shoulder, and her expression was the one Wendell had learned to read on the ferry and at the gate of every hard mile since: duty and love locked in a fistfight behind her eyes, and love, lately, winning on points.

"They took the boy," she said.

"Yes."

"Aldric means to bind him to the Spire. Use him as a key."

"Yes."

"And your hands are done, and your wrist is broken, and you can't climb, and you can't fight, and there are eleven armed storm-callers between here and the summit." She crouched in front of him, close enough that he could see the exhaustion carved under her eyes. "Say yes to all of it so I don't have to pretend I followed you up this mountain for the scenery."

Wendell stared at her. "Your order will call this treason."

"My order fed my sister into a purge built on lies, and I signed the ledgers." Her voice didn't shake, but it went very flat and very careful, the way voices do when they're holding something heavy. "I've been treasonous since the ferry, Wendell. I'm just doing it properly now." She pulled the rope off her shoulder and held it out. "Can you walk if I carry most of you?"

"I can walk," Wendell said. It wasn't entirely true. He took her forearm with his left hand and let her haul him upright, and the world swam, and settled. "Why?" he asked, because he had to ask once, honestly. "Why throw away everything for a boy you met three weeks ago?"

Voss looked up the mountain, at the little string of torches climbing toward the sky's root, and her jaw tightened.

"Because when he said my sister's name," she said, "in that language — it wasn't a trick, and it wasn't a weapon. It was a *gift*. He gave me back one word of her, Wendell. One word, freely, to a woman sent to arrest him." She started up the road, hauling him with her, steady as a mule and twice as stubborn. "Men like Aldric look at that child and see a key. Your whole order looked at people like me and saw fuel. I am done being on the side that sees things as *useful*."

They climbed. The torches above grew no closer and no farther, and the cold came down off the glacier like a tide, and Wendell's hands burned and his wrist sang its one cracked note with every step. Twice he stumbled and twice she caught him without comment, adjusting his weight across her shoulders as naturally as breathing.

Somewhere past the tree line, under a sky beginning — very faintly, very wrongly — to tear along its seams like old cloth, Wendell found his voice again.

"Sera. When we catch them. I have one working hand and a speech I've been rehearsing for twelve years, and I suspect none of it will matter."

"Good," said Sera Voss. "Speeches are how Aldric wins. Keep up."

Above them, the sky split a little wider, and the light that came through was the color of Quentin's eyes.
