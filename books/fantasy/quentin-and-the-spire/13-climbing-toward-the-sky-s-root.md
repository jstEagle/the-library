## 13. Climbing Toward the Sky's Root

The mountain did not have a name anymore. It had probably had one once — some shepherd's word for "the place where the clouds go to die" or a pilgrim's blessing scratched into a waystone — but the maps of Corvane stopped at the marshes, and the maps of everywhere else had been drawn by people who believed the Spire was a story mothers told to frighten children into coming in from the rain.

So they climbed an unnamed mountain, toward the root of the sky, and the sky came down to meet them.

By midmorning the clouds were close enough to touch. They moved wrong, Wendell noticed — not drifting but circling, slow as millstones, around the peak above them. He had spent twelve years pretending he didn't know what storms felt like from the inside, and now every step upward was like walking back into a room he'd sworn never to enter, where all his old furniture still stood exactly where he'd left it.

"You're limping," Voss said behind him.

"I'm *climbing*. There's a difference. Limping is what you do on flat ground with dignity."

"Your left boot is soaked through and you've been favoring that ankle since the scree field."

"And you've been counting my steps since the scree field," Wendell said. "Which is either concern or surveillance, and given your profession I'm going to assume surveillance."

Voss made a sound that in anyone else would have been a laugh. In her it was more like a door opening half an inch and then thinking better of it.

She walked three paces behind him, which he'd decided was habit rather than tactics. She still wore the grey coat of her order, though she'd torn the insignia off at the ferry crossing with the brisk efficiency of a woman peeling a label off a jar she no longer intended to return. Underneath, she was just a tall, tired woman with a sword, a quarter-loaf of bread, and — though neither of them had said so aloud — nowhere else in the world to go.

Wendell's hands were wrapped in strips of his own shirt. The burns from the doorway of rain had scabbed, cracked, and scabbed again on the climb, and every rock he gripped cost him something. He kept them low at his sides where she wouldn't look at them too long. He wasn't sure why he bothered hiding them. She had seen him weep over a memory the river took. Hiding things from Sera Voss at this point was mostly for his own benefit, a small performance he put on each morning like lacing boots.

They stopped at noon at a shelf of stone where a dead pine leaned out over nothing, silver-grey, struck by lightning so long ago it had become part of the view. Voss divided the bread with a knife and handed him the larger piece without comment. He accepted it without comment. This, too, was a kind of conversation they had learned to have.

"The path forks above us," she said. "The western route follows the ridge."

"The eastern one goes through the hollow." Wendell chewed. The bread was stale and tasted faintly of the drowned river, which he suspected would follow them in their dreams for years. "The hollow is faster."

"Faster and worse."

"Yes."

Voss looked up at the circling clouds, then down at his hands, then away, in the specific order of a woman deciding whether to say something. Wendell had watched Inquisitors do this in interrogations, back when he'd been on the other side of such rooms. With her it looked almost painful, like watching someone swallow a fishbone.

"When we cross the hollow," she said, "you will need to be carried. Part of the way. Possibly most of the way."

"I—"

"Don't argue yet. Hear the whole sentence first." She turned the knife over in her fingers. "Your hands cannot hold rope. You said so yourself last night, when you thought I was sleeping. You tried to test your grip on the anchor line, and you couldn't close your right hand past the third finger. So on the crossing — where there is rope work, where a fall is fatal, where I cannot catch you because you weigh as much as I do — you will be on my back, or you will wait below alone while I go on, and we both know which of those you'll choose, because you're the sort of fool who'd try to climb it with ruined hands rather than be left behind."

The wind moved through the dead pine. Somewhere far above, thunder rolled — or something that sounded like thunder, if thunder could be patient.

"I hate being carried," Wendell said finally.

"I know."

"It's undignified."

"I know."

"I once carried a mule across a flooded ford and complained about it for a week."

"I believe it," said Voss, and there it was again — the door opening half an inch. "You can complain to me for a week as well. I'll bill you by the mile."

* * *

The hollow earned its reputation.

It was a notch in the mountain's shoulder where the rock had fallen away in some ancient age, leaving a gap crossed only by three anchors of iron driven into stone older than any kingdom, and a chain, and then a stretch of bare sloping slab polished by ten thousand winters of ice. Below the slab: four hundred feet of air, and at the bottom of the air, a fan of broken rock that even the birds avoided.

Wendell stood at the edge and felt the storm-caller in him wake up and take notice. That was the worst part. Some traitorous corner of him looked at that gap and thought, *I could call a wind to carry us across,* and he had to answer it, patiently, the way you answer a drunk friend suggesting one more round: *No. Last time you opened a door in the rain it burned your hands to the bone and told every hunter in the world exactly who you are.*

"We tie together," Voss said. She was already uncoiling their single length of line, checking knots with the automatic thoroughness of a woman who trusted procedure the way other people trusted gods. "I lead. You stay three arm-lengths behind me. On the slab we do it the other way — I go first, facing downslope, and you come to me."

"And in the middle section."

"In the middle section," she said, not looking at him, "I carry you."

He opened his mouth. She raised one eyebrow, and whatever he'd been about to say died of natural causes.

The first two anchors went easily enough, if "easily" could describe crossing a chain bolted over a void with hands that felt like boiled meat. Wendell talked himself across, actually talking, muttering charms under his breath — harmless ones, little rhymes his grandmother might have used to coax cream from milk — because the sound of his own voice saying nonsense words kept the real words, the First Tongue words, from rising in his throat. Quentin's words. Words that made the air listen.

Voss waited at the far side, anchored, arms out. "Slab next. I go first."

She moved down the polished slope the way water moves — no wasted motion, weight always over her feet, testing each hold before committing. He tried to learn from it. His body remembered things his pride wished it didn't: twelve years ago he had climbed the towers of the monastery in a storm for the sheer joy of it, laughing into the wind, and the wind had laughed back.

Then came the middle. The place where the slab narrowed to a spine of rock barely wider than a coffin lid, where the chain ran slack between anchors and the drop yawned on both sides, and where Voss crouched, braced, and held out her arms like a woman waiting to catch a child jumping from a wall.

"This is the part I'll deny ever happened," Wendell said, "if we survive."

"Noted. Turn around. Arms over your shoulders. If you fall backward, I lose you; if you fall forward, I don't."

He turned. He stepped back. Her arms closed around his chest with a grip like an anchor chain, and for one vertiginous moment his boots left the stone entirely and there was nothing beneath him but weather.

Then she stood, and he was simply *on her back*, absurd as a sack of turnips, and she began to walk.

She was stronger than she looked. He'd have expected that from a soldier, but this was different — this was the strength of a woman who had carried things a long time. Ledgers. Orders. A sister's name she couldn't say. She carried him across that terrible spine of rock with short, deliberate steps, breathing evenly, and Wendell, pressed against her shoulder, found himself looking down four hundred feet at the fan of broken stone and thinking about how strange it was to be saved by the person sent to destroy you.

Halfway across, she spoke. Not loudly. Just above the wind.

"My sister's name was Lira."

His breath caught. At the ferry, the spirit had taken the memory of her sister's face — Wendell had watched it happen, had watched Voss reach afterward for something that wasn't there anymore and find smooth blank water where a face should be. But a name was not a face. A name had survived.

"She was eleven," Voss went on, stepping carefully, the rock humming faintly under her boots. "When the Purge came to our district. I was sixteen, already in training, already believing everything they taught us — that magic-workers were rot, that burning them out was mercy, that the ledgers were justice." Her breath fogged. "Lira used to make the rain come sideways over our roof when she was happy. Little storms. Nothing. The kind of thing that gets a child called *charming* in one city and *executed* in another. Someone reported her. Someone on our street. And I—" the pause here lasted three steps, four, five, "—I signed the witness ledger confirming the report. Because my instructor said it was my duty. Because I wanted to be the sort of person whose duty didn't shake."

The wind gusted. She planted herself against it, Wendell's whole weight and her own riding the balance beam of that rock, and did not stop.

"They took her to the purge house. I never saw her again. I told myself for twenty years that the ledgers were clean, that everyone burned deserved it, because the alternative was admitting that I fed my own sister to the fire with a signature." Another gust; she rode it out. "And then a mute child said her name in a language I don't understand, on a riverbank, and it sounded like — forgiveness. Or maybe accusation. I honestly can't tell the difference anymore. Maybe there isn't one."

"Sera." He almost never used her first name. It startled both of them. "That wasn't your doing. You were a child yourself."

"So were you," she said, "when you ran. And you've spent twelve years doing penance for it anyway, so I imagine my argument is worthless."

He had no answer for that. She was right, which was the most annoying quality a person could have.

* * *

Past the spine, the hollow relented into a broad ledge, and there she set him down, gently, the way you set down something you've decided matters. They sat with their backs to the mountain, legs dangling over the drop, sharing the last of the water. Above them the circling clouds had lowered again, close enough now that the light through them had gone green-grey and submarine.

"That's why you tore the insignia off," Wendell said quietly. "Not at the ferry. Before. You'd already decided the order was rotten. The ferry was just the first time anyone asked you to prove it."

"The ledgers were clean," she said. "That's what I told myself. Clean paper, proper signatures, due process. It took me twenty years to understand that a lie written neatly is still a lie." She turned the empty waterskin over in her hands. "And you? Your confession. Fair is fair — I gave you mine on a cliff over a four-hundred-foot drop, which I'm told is the traditional setting."

Wendell laughed despite himself, and the laugh echoed strangely against the circling clouds, and the clouds seemed — he would have sworn it — to lean in slightly, listening. He was getting used to that. Or trying to.

"There was a friend," he said. "At the monastery. Tomas. We trained together since we were eight. He was the better storm-caller, honestly — I was flashier, but he was *truer*, if that means anything. When the Purge reached us, Aldric came to us both and said the order would shelter those who renounced great workings. Swear the oath, live small, survive." He turned his bandaged hands over, palms up, useless as a pair of winter gloves. "Tomas refused. Said a storm-caller who swears off storms is just a man standing in the rain. He was going to run north, to the free coast. And I — Aldric needed someone to confirm the roster, to say which students had fled and which remained, and I gave him Tomas's name. Thinking — I told myself I was thinking — that if I stayed obedient, I could protect the others. That one name bought safety for twenty."

"And?"

"And they burned the tower anyway. All of them. My twenty names bought nothing except a seat on the council for the man who collected them." He watched the green-grey light move over the valley below. "Tomas got out. I learned that much later. He made the free coast. And I have spent twelve years wondering whether he knows it was my signature that put the fire behind him. Whether he lies awake hating me, or worse — whether he's already dead and I'll never get the chance to find out."

The wind came up soft from the valley and moved through his hair like a hand.

"You could ask the sky," Voss said.

He looked at her. She wasn't mocking. Her face, usually so disciplined, had gone open in the way it only did at altitude, or at confession — the two being, apparently, the same country.

"Tomas," she said, simply. "Ask."

And God help him, he almost did. The words rose in his throat — the First Tongue, Quentin's language, the language that made the air *attend* — and for one moment the circling clouds overhead stilled, the whole green vault of them holding its breath, waiting for him to speak a name into it.

Then he let the words die unspoken, and exhaled, and the clouds resumed their slow turning.

"After," he said. "If we live. If the covenant holds. Then I'll stand on the Spire and shout every name I owe an apology to, until the whole coast hears it. But not as a bargain. Not as payment for safe passage up a mountain. If I use it now, it's just another transaction, and I've done enough transactions with the truth to know how they end."

Voss studied him for a long moment. Then she reached over and, with great formality, tapped her waterskin against his.

"To transactions," she said, "and their endings."

* * *

They climbed the rest of the day, and when the light failed they made camp in a hollow beneath an overhang, and the cold came down with the dark, and they sat close for warmth without discussing it, because discussing it would have required vocabulary neither of them owned.

Somewhere in the middle watch, Wendell woke to find Voss sitting up, awake, looking east through the gap in the rocks.

"What is it?" he murmured.

"Light," she said. "Far down the southern approach. Moving."

He struggled upright, hands throbbing, and followed her gaze. Far below — impossibly far, a day's climb behind them and yet visible, which meant it was no ordinary flame — a pale gold radiance crawled along the mountainside. Steady. Deliberate. Ascending.

"Aldric?" he whispered.

"No," Voss said slowly. "Aldric would travel dark, if he has any sense left. That's lantern-light. That's *order* light." She was quiet a moment, and when she spoke again her voice had the flat calm he had learned to fear, the calm she wore when duty and dread shook hands. "That's a purge column. Lanterns blessed against weather-working. They're following our trail."

"But the order cast you out—"

"The order doesn't know that yet. And it doesn't matter." She turned to him, and in the starlight her face was very still. "Whatever happens at the Spire, Wendell — whoever reaches that gate first — they'll be right behind us. By tomorrow night."

Above them, at the crown of the world, the circling clouds parted for a heartbeat, and through the gap they both saw it: a single point of light at the summit, ancient and patient, like an eye opening.

Like a door, waiting to be knocked upon.
