## 14. The Gate of Living Stone

The mountain stopped pretending to be a mountain about a mile below the Spire.

Wendell noticed it first as a wrongness in the stone — the granite gave way to something paler, something that seemed almost to breathe. The path beneath their boots had the faint warmth of skin under wool, and when he pressed his palm against the cliff face for balance, it did not feel like rock. It felt patient.

"It's alive," Sera said beside him, not slowing. She'd been climbing for two days on half rations and no sleep, and she climbed the way she did everything else: as if inefficiency were a moral failing.

"Living stone," Wendell said. "The old texts called it *vess-ael*. Bridge-stone." He flexed his burned hands experimentally. The bandages Voss had wrapped them in at the monastery road were stiff with salt and old blood, and his fingers answered him the way a stubborn door answers a shoulder — eventually, and with complaint. "It grows up from Ael's side of things. Or down from ours. Nobody agreed on which."

"Your texts," she said dryly, "sound like they were written by people who enjoyed being confused."

"That was rather the point of most scholarship, yes."

The wind came off the peak above them in long, slow breaths, warm despite the altitude, and it smelled — this was the part Wendell could not explain and so did not try — it smelled like the inside of his mother's kitchen. Rain on hot slate. Bread left to rise. Twelve years since he'd smelled anything kind, and here it was, drifting off a mountain like an apology.

Quentin would have loved this. Quentin, who was somewhere above them now, carried up the mountain in Aldric's iron grip, and whose small voice Wendell had heard only once more since the betrayal — a single word flung backward down the switchbacks like a thrown stone:

*Hurry.*

So they hurried.

* * *

They made camp that night in a hollow where the living stone curved inward like a cupped hand, and Wendell slept badly, which was standard, but dreamed strangely, which was not. He dreamed of doors. Doors of every size, standing open in a field of grey grass, and behind each one a version of his life he hadn't lived. In one, he stayed at the monastery and fought beside Tomas during the Purge. In another, he never learned storm-calling at all and died at forty-one of something ordinary and gentle.

In the last door there was nothing. Just wind, waiting.

He woke with Voss's hand on her knife and dawn bleeding pink over the ridge.

"Something's coming up the path," she whispered.

But nothing came. Nothing ever came. The mountain simply continued, and the warmth in the stone grew warmer, and by midmorning they rounded a shoulder of cliff and saw it.

The Gate of Living Stone was not built. That was Wendell's first thought, and his second, and his third, because the mind kept reaching for *built* the way a tongue reaches for a missing tooth. It was grown — a wall of pale stone rising three hundred feet out of the mountainside, seamless, unjointed, and set into its center was an archway perhaps twenty feet tall, framed in carvings that moved. Not flickered. Moved, slowly, the way clouds move: figures of rain and lightning and small human shapes walking between them, all of it flowing through the stone like fish through water.

And before the archway, seated on the ground as if they'd been sitting there since the world's first morning, were three figures.

They were roughly people-shaped. Roughly. They wore robes the color of wet slate, and their faces were smooth as river stones, featureless except where a suggestion of eyes or mouth surfaced briefly, like shapes seen through fog. One held a lantern with no flame. One held a book with no pages. One held nothing at all, and somehow that was the worst one.

"Oh good," Voss said flatly. "A welcoming committee."

"Guardians," Wendell murmured. His scars had begun to ache, deep and rhythmic, like a second heartbeat. "The texts mention them. Barely. *Those who ask.*"

"Ask what?"

"I suspect we're about to find out."

The three figures rose without appearing to move — one moment seated, the next standing, no transition a eye could catch. The one with the empty hands drifted forward and stopped ten feet away, close enough that Wendell could see that its robe wasn't fabric at all but compressed mist, turning slowly, like cream stirred into tea.

It had no voice. Its words simply arrived inside the skull, courteous and cold as well water.

***Two come to the gate. The gate asks all who enter.***

"We know why we're here," Wendell said aloud. His voice cracked on the second word; two days of smoke and grief and burned hands had sanded it raw. "There's a boy. He was taken from us — taken up the mountain by a man named Aldric Vane. We've come to bring him back."

***All say why they are here. The gate does not ask why.***

The figure tilted its smooth head. Where a face should have been, the mist shifted, and Wendell felt the peculiar horror of being looked at by weather.

***The gate asks what you have done.***

* * *

Later, Wendell would try to describe what happened next and fail utterly, the way you can't describe a dream to someone at breakfast without killing it. But the shape of it was this:

The guardian raised one mist-wrapped hand, and the world folded.

He was still standing before the gate. He was also twelve years younger, standing in the rain outside the monastery's east wall, and Tomas was looking at him, and the look was the same look Tomas had worn in every dream Wendell had refused to have since.

"Wendell." Tomas's voice. Exactly his voice, with that little rasp at the end from the childhood scar on his throat. "Come with me. There's time. There's *time*, Wen, if we go now —"

The night of the Purge. The night the order's own wardens came up the mountain with white hoods and writs signed by men Wendell had eaten breakfast with. Wendell had been supposed to meet Tomas at the east wall and run south together, two storm-callers gone to ground, two brothers of the rain. And Wendell had stood in the dark with his bag already packed and heard the wardens' boots on the gravel, and he had done the arithmetic that cowards do, quick and quiet and damning: *two of us are harder to hide than one.*

"I can't," he'd said. "Tomas, I—"

"You can." No anger in it yet. Just disbelief, the pure kind, the kind that hasn't yet curdled into understanding. "We said — you *promised* —"

And then the boots got louder, and Wendell ran north instead of south, alone, and he heard — he would always hear, in the small hours, in the space between one heartbeat and the next — the sound of Tomas calling his name until the hooded men reached him, and then the other sound, the sound after, which Wendell's mind had mercifully blurred into static for twelve years.

Now the memory unfolded around him whole, and the blur lifted, and there was nothing merciful in it at all.

He was in the rain again. He was running again. And the guardian's presence filled the memory like frost filling a window, patient, absolute.

***You may turn away,*** it said. ***Most turn away. Look elsewhere within your own remembering, and the gate will still open. The price is only that you carry it onward unwitnessed.***

That was the trap, Wendell understood dimly. Not the memory — the escape from it. Everyone had rooms inside themselves they kept locked; the gate offered the lock, offered the courtesy of not forcing him to stand in the worst room of his house and *look*. All he had to do was flinch. Look at the rafters. Look at his boots. Think of anything else, and walk through the archway carrying the same sealed box he'd carried for twelve years, lighter by nothing, changed by nothing.

Tomas was still calling his name. The static was lifting. Underneath it —

Underneath it, Wendell made himself listen.

It was worse than anything he'd imagined, which meant his imagination had spent twelve years protecting him, and he resented it even as he stood there and took the truth in full: that Tomas had fought. That it had taken four hooded men. That his last word, before the silence, was not Wendell's name at all but "*Run*" — shouted northward, toward the trees, toward the coward fleeing through them. Even abandoned, even surrounded, Tomas's last act had been to cover his friend's retreat.

Wendell stood in the rain of his own worst hour and did not look away.

"I see it," he said aloud, and his voice was wrecked and steady. "I see all of it. I left him. I chose myself, and I dressed it up as caution, and I let him die loud while I survived quiet." The rain ran down his face, real rain, remembered rain, no difference. "I won't seal it back up. Whatever's past your gate — I'll carry this where I'm going. Witnessed."

The static fell silent. The rain thinned. And through it, impossibly, faintly, came the sound of birdsong — the monastery courtyard at dawn, some ordinary morning years before the end, when they were young and the worst thing either of them had done was steal honey cakes from the refectory.

The guardian withdrew its hand. The world snapped back to the grey mountainside, the pale wall, the archway with its slow-flowing carvings. Wendell swayed. Voss's hand caught his elbow — steadied him — and let go quickly, as though the steadying had been accidental.

She was staring at him with an expression he couldn't read. Something had happened to her face while he was gone; something had been taken out of it and something else put in.

"Your turn," he rasped.

"I know," she said.

* * *

They took her next, and Wendell watched it happen, which was somehow crueler than enduring it himself.

Voss went rigid. Her chin came up — even inside the trial, whatever the trial showed her, she met it like an audit. Her lips moved once, twice, forming words too quiet to hear. Then her whole body shuddered, and her free hand — the one not gripping her knife — opened slowly, palm out, fingers spread, as if releasing something she'd held clenched for years.

Wendell knew what was coming before the guardians moved her lips again. He'd read her file during those first terrible days of pursuit, before she'd become an ally, back when she was just the thing hunting them. He knew about the sister. Lira Voss, nine years old, taken in the third year of the purges, listed among *the necessary losses* in an internal memorandum that Sera Voss herself had co-signed at twenty-four, young and certain and hungry to be taken seriously by older, crueller men.

He watched her sign it again. Watched her watch herself do it. Watched her not look away.

When it ended, Sera Voss stood very straight for a count of five, and then sat down abruptly on the living stone, hard, like a puppet with cut strings. She didn't cry. Wendell suspected she'd forgotten how, or had decided the skill was inefficient. She just sat there breathing, one hand flat on the warm ground.

"They offered me the way out," she said finally. Her voice was scraped clean. "Inside my own head. A door. I could've looked anywhere else."

"I know."

"Twelve years I've told myself the purge was lawful. Necessary. That I was different from the ones who enjoyed it." She laughed — one short, ugly syllable. "I signed my sister's name into a ledger, Wendell. I filed her under *acceptable*. And I went home and ate dinner."

"I know," Wendell said again, and knelt beside her, awkward, his burned hands useless in his lap. "I ran while my friend died. Between the two of us, we're quite the pair."

That startled a real laugh out of her — brief, disbelieving, gone almost before it arrived, but real. Gentle humor, the kind that grows in ruins.

The guardian with the empty hands drifted near again.

***Both have looked,*** it said, into them both at once. ***Neither has turned away. This is rare.***

"Is there a prize?" Voss asked, hauling herself upright with tattered dignity. "A ribbon? A small ceremonial key?"

***The prize is passage.***

"Thrilling."

***One thing more.*** The guardian's blank face turned toward the archway, and beyond it, for the first time, Wendell could see what lay through the gate: not a corridor but a vast upward hollow, the Spire's interior, lit from within by a light that had no color he could name. And far above, impossibly high, something flickered. Small. Quick. Like a candle guttering.

Like someone signaling.

***The one who was taken passed this gate three days ago,*** the guardian said. ***He is not yet at the core. The false teacher is slower than he believes. But the child dims, storm-caller. Each hour, the bridge between realms thins, and he thins with it.***

The bottom dropped out of Wendell's stomach.

"He's dying?" His voice came out as a croak. "He's a *child* — Aldric needs him alive —"

***Aldric needs a key. Keys may be broken in the turning, if one turns carelessly.*** The guardian began, slowly, to fold itself back toward its place before the wall. ***The core asks its question soon. Climb.***

Wendell was already moving, grabbing his pack one-handed, fumbling the strap with fingers that wouldn't obey. Voss caught the buckle and cinched it tight, and for a moment they stood close enough that he could see his own fear reflected in her face and recognized it as identical to his own.

"Three days' climb ahead of us," she said. "Maybe less, if we kill ourselves doing it."

"Then let's be reckless," Wendell said. "I hear it's redefining strength these days."

They stepped together through the Gate of Living Stone, and the light inside closed over them like water.
