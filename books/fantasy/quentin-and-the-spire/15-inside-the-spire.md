## 15. Inside the Spire

The first thing Quentin learned about the inside of the Spire was that it did not have an *inside*, not really.

Doors were a thing of the mortal realm. Doors had hinges and handles and the satisfying small drama of opening them — Wendell knocked on doors the way other people greeted old friends, two raps and a pause, as if giving whoever was inside a moment to compose themselves. Quentin liked doors. Doors meant someone was expecting you, or at least pretending to.

The Spire had no doors once you passed the Gate of Living Stone. It simply *received* you, the way the sea receives a river, and after that you were somewhere else entirely: a place where walking in one direction could bring you to a room you'd left three days from now.

Quentin stood on a stair made of cloud-stuff and morning light, and he listened.

That was the trouble, see. He couldn't stop listening. He was the last true voice of Ael, which sounded very grand when you said it quickly, but what it actually meant was that his ears were open all the way down, like wells, and everything the storm-realm felt poured into him whether he wanted it or not.

And Ael was dying.

He heard it the way you might hear a great house settling in winter — creaks and silences where there should have been voices. Far off in the storm-realm, a thunderhead named Old Brannigan (Quentin knew all their names; they had sung to him before he was born) went quiet for the first time in nine thousand years. Not dead, exactly. *Faded.* Like a lamp running out of oil. Like a voice growing hoarse from calling into a wind that never answered back.

*One by one,* Quentin thought. *One by one by one.*

He sat down on the cloud-stair and hugged his knees, because there was no one to see him be small, and being small felt honest.

* * *

Time folded here, which took some getting used to.

In Corvane, time was a straight road. You walked it, and behind you was the fish market and ahead of you was supper, and that was that. Inside the Spire, time was more like the ribbon his mother — no. Like the ribbons the charm-sellers wound around their stall-posts. Everything coiled together, touching itself in places it shouldn't, and if you pulled one end the middle came loose and hit you in the eye.

So Quentin would walk down a corridor of pale stone and come out in yesterday. He watched himself arrive through the Gate, small and muddy and holding Wendell's sleeve with both hands because Wendell's hands were burned and couldn't hold back. He watched Voss stand guard at the threshold, jaw set, sword drawn against nothing, guarding a doorway that led to everywhen.

"Stop doing that," Voss told him, catching him watching. She didn't sound angry. She sounded tired in the way of people who have been tired so long it's become a kind of furniture.

"Doing what?"

"Looking at me like I'm already gone." She sheathed the sword. "I'm standing right here."

"You are," Quentin agreed. "But I've seen the corridor where you aren't."

Voss went very still. Then she crouched down to his height, which she almost never did, and looked at him with her sharp Inquisitor's eyes — though she wasn't an Inquisitor anymore, not properly, not since the ferry crossing, not since she'd heard her sister's name spoken in the First Tongue by a mute child who shouldn't have known it.

"What do you see?" she asked quietly.

Quentin considered lying. He was quite good at lying, actually, in the way that children who cannot speak become good at it — whole elaborate falsehoods conducted entirely through the arrangement of objects on a shelf. But lying to Sera Voss felt like lying to a knife.

"I see you carrying someone up a mountain," he said. "I don't know who yet."

Something moved across her face, quick as a fish under ice. "Then let's hope your corridors are wrong."

"They're never wrong," Quentin said. "They're just early."

* * *

He found Wendell in a chamber that remembered being a kitchen.

This was the strange mercy of the Spire: for all its vastness and its folding time, it kept making rooms that were *small*. A kitchen with a hearth that had no fire but remembered ten thousand fires. A window that looked out on a sea that existed nowhere. Wendell had found it within an hour of arriving, because Wendell had a talent — buried under twelve years of exile and fear — for finding the human-shaped hollows in enormous things.

He was sitting by the unlit hearth, hands wrapped in clean linen, trying to teach himself to hold a cup of tea.

Quentin watched from the doorway. Wendell's fingers wouldn't close properly. The storm-calling at the shop door had burned them down to the tendons, and now the man who had once held lightning like a rope held a teacup like it was a live eel. The cup slipped. He caught it against his chest. Tea everywhere.

"*Bollocks,*" Wendell said, with feeling.

"That's the third one," Quentin said.

Wendell jumped, then laughed — a short, cracked laugh, but real. "How long have you been standing there?"

"Long enough to learn some new words."

"Don't repeat those to anyone important." Wendell sighed and looked at his bandaged hands. "You know, I used to call down thunderbolts. Actual thunderbolts. The kind that split oaks. And now—" He wiggled his ruined fingers ruefully. "Now I'm defeated by crockery."

Quentin came and sat beside him. The hearth, sensing company, produced a faint ghost of warmth, purely out of nostalgia.

"Wendell. When we reach the core. What's it going to ask for?"

The warmth in the room went still, the way a room does when everyone stops breathing.

"Why do you ask that?"

"Because I've been listening," Quentin said. "Because the storms are going silent one by one, and the Spire is scared, and scared old things tell you the truth if you listen long enough." He looked at his own hands, small and unburned. "It's going to ask for a heart, isn't it? A storm-caller's heart, given freely."

Wendell didn't answer. Which was, of course, an answer.

"You're going to offer yours," Quentin said. "I can hear you practicing. At night. You talk to the dark like it's Aldric, and you say, *'You should have let me do this twelve years ago,'* and then you say, *'Well. Better late.'*" He shook his head. "You practice being brave the way other people practice scales. It doesn't work, you know. Bravery isn't a scale."

"It isn't?"

"No. It's more like... dancing. You can't practice it alone in a room. You can only do it when the music's playing, with someone else there, and mostly you get it wrong and step on toes." Quentin stood up. "So stop rehearsing. You'll just tire yourself out before the dance."

Wendell stared at him. "How old are you?"

"Nobody knows," Quentin said cheerfully, and left before the conversation could turn into something that would make his chest hurt.

* * *

But the chest-hurt came anyway, later, alone, in the deep places.

Because that was the loneliness of it. Everyone assumed that being the last voice of Ael meant being *important*, and it did, and importance was fine, importance was even flattering sometimes. What nobody understood was that it also meant being the only one who could hear the whole song. And the song was dying.

He climbed — or fell upward, time being what it was — to a high gallery of the Spire, a place where the walls thinned to gossamer and you could feel both realms pressing close, like two faces leaning toward each other across a shrinking gap.

There he opened his ears all the way down.

Ael came pouring in.

He heard the Thunderfields, where the great storms grazed like luminous cattle, and counted them, and came up three fewer than last week. He heard the Rainmother's cascade slowing to a trickle. He heard the Windroads emptying, the gales that carried messages between realms lying down in the sky like tired dogs and not getting up. And underneath it all, fainter than any of it, he heard the covenant itself — the ancient pact, the bridge-treaty, the promise made ten thousand years ago that storm and mortal would sustain each other forever — and it sounded like a bell that had been struck too many times. Still ringing. Ringing thinner.

*Hello,* Quentin said to it, in the First Tongue. *I'm here. I came.*

The covenant stirred, the way an old dog stirs when it hears a familiar footstep. It knew him. Of course it knew him. Half of him had been woven out of its music.

*Little voice,* it said, and its voice was vast and cracked and glad. *Little voice. You are late.*

"I know," Quentin said. "I'm sorry. It's hard to travel when you're a secret."

*Are they with you? The ones who climb?*

"One of them is. The loud dreamer. And the knife-woman who turned out to be soft in the middle."

The covenant made a sound that might have been laughter, if bells could laugh. Then it faded, and the fading hurt worse than anything, because Quentin understood suddenly and completely that the covenant wasn't speaking to him from strength. It was spending its last words on greetings. Every sentence cost it. It was a dying grandmother using her final breaths to ask about his schooling.

*Don't,* he whispered. *Save your breath. I'll fix it. That's why I'm here.*

*Little voice,* the covenant said again, gentler now. *Do you know what renewal costs?*

Quentin's stomach dropped. "Wendell thinks it wants his heart."

*It does want his heart. It will take his heart, if it is offered freely, and it will be enough, and the storms will live.* A pause, thin as paper. *But hearts are not the only currency. And bridges are not only built of sacrifice.*

"What does that mean?"

But the covenant had gone quiet, conserving itself, and the high gallery filled with ordinary silence, and Quentin was left alone with a riddle and a world's weight and the particular misery of a child who has been given half an answer by something too tired to finish the sentence.

* * *

It was in the folding hours — the Spire's midnight-that-was-also-dawn — that he saw Aldric.

He hadn't meant to. He'd been wandering, following the pull of the core, the great slow heartbeat at the Spire's root that he could feel through the soles of his feet like a drum under a floor. And the corridors, being corridors of a place outside time, delivered him to a ledge above a vast chamber he had never seen and somehow already knew: the Bargaining Floor.

Below, lit by light that came from nowhere and everywhere, stood Aldric.

Quentin had seen him only in dreams and memories — Wendell's memories, mostly, and in those Aldric wore the face of a beloved teacher: silver-bearded, patient, hands that could gentle a storm the way a horseman gentles a horse. The man below had the same face. That was the horror of it. Nothing had rotted. Nothing had twisted. He looked exactly like the mentor in Wendell's stories, and he was kneeling before the Spire's core with his arms spread wide, and he was *bargaining*.

Quentin crept to the ledge's edge and listened.

"—not asking to destroy it," Aldric was saying, and his voice was reasonable, warm, the voice of a man proposing a sensible amendment to a foolish old rule. "I'm asking to *administer* it. Think clearly, old one. You've been run by sacrifice since the beginning. Heart after heart after heart, poured into the gap, and for what? A bridge that sags. Storms that starve. Look at yourself. You're *dying* of generosity."

The core pulsed. Quentin felt it in his teeth.

"I offer a better way," Aldric continued. "Bind the covenant to me. One will, one keeper, one hand on the wheel — no more burning out good men, no more children sent through as keys. I have studied this my whole life. I am *qualified*." He smiled, and it was the smile of a teacher praising a student, and Quentin's skin crawled because he realized the smile wasn't for the core at all. It was habit. Aldric smiled like that at everyone he intended to use. "Give me the power, and I will save the storms. No one has to die. Isn't that worth more than another martyr?"

*He believes it,* Quentin realized, and the realization was cold water. *That's the worst part. He actually believes it.*

Because here was the thing Quentin knew, the thing that lived in his bones alongside the First Tongue itself: the covenant could not be *held*. It could only be *kept*. It was not a treasure. It was a promise. And a promise seized by force stopped being a promise — it became just a chain with extra steps, and chains strangled whatever they were wrapped around, even when the wrapping was done lovingly. Even when the wrapper was qualified.

Below, the core flared — refusing, or considering, or grieving; Quentin couldn't tell. And Aldric's smile flickered, just for a heartbeat, into something older and hungrier, and Quentin saw the truth underneath the teacher's face: twelve years ago, when the Purge came and Wendell fled, Aldric hadn't been disappointed that his student ran.

He'd been *relieved*. One less witness.

Quentin pressed himself flat against the stone, heart hammering, and did the hardest thing he had ever done, harder than crossing between realms, harder than going mute for years while the words of two worlds crowded his throat:

He stayed silent, and he memorized everything.

The chamber's shape. The approach to the core. The place where Aldric had chalked — *chalked*, like a schoolmaster, like a lesson plan — the binding-circles that would turn a covenant into a crown.

Then he backed away along the ledge, one careful step at a time, a small boy carrying the largest secret in two worlds, and went to find Wendell.

Because the music had started. The dance was coming.

And somebody had to warn him that the floor was rigged.
