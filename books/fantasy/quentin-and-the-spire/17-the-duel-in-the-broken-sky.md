## 17. The Duel in the Broken Sky

The covenant chamber had no floor anymore.

That was the first thing Wendell understood, standing on a shelf of stone that had been the floor of the world's most important room perhaps an hour ago and was now simply a ledge — one of many, floating in a shaft of open sky like the broken teeth of some enormous comb. Above him, where a ceiling should have been, there was only weather: clouds churning in a slow spiral around the Spire's crown, lit from beneath by a light that had no business being gold and green at once. Below him, a very long way below, the mountain's shoulder fell away into mist.

Aldric stood on another ledge across the gap, arms folded, robes snapping in the wind, looking for all the world like a man waiting for a tardy apprentice rather than a thief caught mid-robbery of heaven itself.

"You always did arrive late," Aldric called over the wind. "Twenty years at the monastery, and I never once saw you early to morning prayers."

"I never once *saw* you at morning prayers," Wendell shouted back. "You were always already there. It was unsettling."

Somewhere below them — or above, or possibly everywhere at once; the chamber's geometry had given up on such distinctions — Quentin was climbing toward the core. Wendell could feel it, the way he could feel rain coming in his knees: a small bright thread of the First Tongue winding upward through the Spire's living stone, singing under its breath. The boy was moving. That was what mattered. Everything else — the wind, the ledges, the old man with lightning gathering in his fists like a debt come due — everything else was just the price of time.

"Quentin doesn't have to do this," Aldric said. His voice carried strangely well across the gap, as if the Spire itself were listening and passing his words along. "You know that, don't you? A child, Wendell. A child holding up the sky because two grown men couldn't hold it themselves. But I can. I've spent thirty years preparing. Let me take the weight, and no one has to be sacrificed at all."

It was a good speech. Wendell had heard versions of it before, back when Aldric still gave them honestly, about duty and burden and how someone had to be strong enough to carry what others couldn't. Back then the speeches had been true. That was the cruelest part — that Aldric hadn't become a liar so much as a man whose truths had curdled.

"You're not taking the weight," Wendell said. "You're taking the *reins*. There's a difference, and the sky knows the difference, and it will tear itself apart trying to shake you off."

"And you'd know? You who ran from every storm you ever summoned?" Aldric's hands opened. Lightning pooled in his palms, patient and white. "Twelve years selling charms to farmers, Wendell. Little knots of power small enough to hide in your pocket. Don't lecture me about the difference between holding and hoarding."

Wendell looked down at his own hands. They were burned — had been burned since Corvane, since the doorway of rain, since the night he'd finally stopped being small — and they ached now with a deep, structural pain, like the ache of a house settling. Every instinct he owned was screaming at him to seize the storm. To gather it, shape it, aim it, the way he'd been trained, the way Aldric had trained him. That was the whole art as the order had taught it: control, control, control. A storm-caller who lost control was a storm-caller who burned out from within. He had watched it happen. He had helped bury the ones it happened to.

But the order had also taught him, in a dusty footnote of a lesson he'd slept through at fifteen, something else. Something Aldric had dismissed as superstition even then.

*The storm is not a horse,* old Master Ellery had written. *It does not want a rider. It wants a partner. The caller who grips tightest falls fastest.*

"Last chance," Aldric said, almost gently. "Step aside. Go back to your shop. Sell your little charms. I'll even let you keep the boy's memory of you, which is more than you deserve."

"You know," Wendell said, "for a man about to steal godhood, you talk an awful lot like someone asking permission."

He stepped off the ledge.

For one long, glorious, idiotic moment he was falling, and then the storm caught him — not because he commanded it, but because he asked, the way you ask an old dog to move over, and the wind slid under him like a palm under a falling cup. He rose. Aldric's eyes widened a fraction, and Wendell felt a small, mean flicker of satisfaction at that, and hated himself for it, and kept it anyway. Some pleasures were too small to refuse.

"Aldric," he said, rising level with his mentor across the broken air, "let's talk about the weather."

* * *

They fought the way storms fight, which is to say badly, beautifully, and with enormous collateral damage.

Aldric struck first, because Aldric always struck first; patience had never been among his virtues, only among his performances. A lance of white fire crossed the gap and Wendell threw himself sideways off the wind, and the bolt hit the ledge behind him and blew a hundred tons of ancient living stone into gravel that rained down into the mist and did not, notably, ever land. The Spire was eating its own ruins. Or perhaps feeding them somewhere. Wendell decided not to think about it.

He answered with a gust — a proper one, a full-bodied shove of pressure that sent Aldric staggering back along his ledge, robes flapping, dignity dented. And then it was on, truly on: bolt answering bolt, wind against wind, two men hurling the sky at each other across a chamber that had once held the peace treaty between the mortal world and the realm of storms.

And losing. That was the thing Wendell realized about forty seconds in, ducking under a scything arc of lightning that sheared the hair off the left side of his head. He was losing. Not because Aldric was stronger — though he was, dreadfully, decades of hoarded power humming in him like a struck bell — but because Wendell was fighting the way he'd been taught, and the teaching was wrong. Every time he seized a current, the current fought him. Every time he shaped a gust, half its strength went into the shaping. He was spending himself on control, and control, it turned out, was expensive.

Aldric, meanwhile, wasn't controlling anything. He was *taking*. Where Wendell gripped, Aldric ripped — tearing power out of the air, out of the Spire, out of the failing covenant itself, drinking the storm instead of speaking with it. It should have been sloppy. It was horrifyingly effective.

"You feel it, don't you?" Aldric called, walking calmly through a crosswind that should have flung him into the void. "The difference. All those years I told you power was discipline. I lied. Power is appetite, Wendell. Discipline is just the table manners we put on top." He raised both hands, and the spiral of cloud overhead tightened, darkened, began to turn like a wheel. "I'm done pretending to have manners."

The wheel came down.

There was no dodging it. It was too wide, too much, a descending column of rotating storm filling the whole chamber, and Wendell felt his training rise in him one final time — *brace, anchor, seize the nearest current and hold* — and he did exactly that, and it nearly killed him. He grabbed the storm the way you grab a runaway horse by the bit, and the storm screamed through him, and his burned hands went white-hot inside their bandages, and he held, and held, and felt himself beginning to burn out from within, exactly as the old stories promised, light leaking through his skin like a lantern with a cracked shade—

—and then, through the roar, he heard it.

Small. Far below. A child's voice, speaking the First Tongue, not in fear but in the tone of someone explaining something simple to someone very slow.

*You don't have to hold it. You never had to hold it. It isn't yours to hold.*

Quentin. Still climbing. Still singing. Taking one second out of carrying the weight of two worlds to tell a grown man the obvious.

Wendell let go.

Not all at once — not a surrender, not a collapse, nothing so dramatic. He simply opened his grip the way you open a fist you didn't know you'd clenched, finger by finger, and stopped holding the storm and started *being in* it. And the storm, which had been fighting him with the fury of a captured thing, went — there was no other word for it — *relieved*. It poured around him instead of through him. The burning in his hands cooled to an ache. The descending wheel of cloud broke apart around him like water around a stone, and Wendell Marchetti stood in the eye of his opponent's greatest blow, unhurt, unburned, held aloft by nothing he controlled and everything he'd finally stopped trying to.

"Oh," he said aloud, to nobody. "Oh, you absolute idiots. All of us. It was never about control."

"What are you doing?" Aldric's composure cracked, just slightly, just enough. "What did you *do*?"

"The opposite of what you're doing," Wendell said. "Come down and try it sometime. Oh — wait."

He moved, and for the first time in the fight, moving was easy. No seizing, no shaping. He simply asked the wind to carry him left, and it carried him left, quick as a thought, and Aldric's counterblast tore through empty air. He asked to be lifted, and was lifted. He asked the rain — because it had begun to rain, warm and sudden, inside the chamber, the Spire weeping or laughing, hard to say — to fall on Aldric's side of the room and nowhere else, and it did, a polite localized downpour that soaked the old man to the skin in three seconds flat.

Aldric roared and pulled more power, tearing it out of the covenant's failing weave, and Wendell watched him do it and finally understood the whole shape of the tragedy. His mentor wasn't stealing the storm. He was *starving*, and eating the only food left, and the more he ate the hungrier he got, and the hungrier he got the harder he gripped, and the harder he gripped the more the storm fought him, and so it would go until either the storm died or Aldric did, and it no longer mattered much which.

"You can't win like this," Aldric snarled, hurling another bolt, missing, hurling another. "Whatever trick this is, it won't last. You're soft. You've always been soft. Twelve years of playing shopkeeper—"

"Yes," Wendell agreed, drifting out of reach again, rain streaming off his chin. "Terribly soft. I cry at market day. Children like me. Once a woman gave me a free scone because I looked 'gently sad,' which I chose to take as a compliment." Another bolt, another miss. "You know what I learned selling charms, Aldric? People don't want power. They want *weather*. They want rain for the crops and sun for the washing. Nobody's ever once asked me for control."

"Sentiment," Aldric spat. "Weakness dressed up as wisdom."

"Comfort," Wendell corrected. "Dressed up as weakness dressed up as wisdom. It's layers all the way down with us storm-callers." And then, because the joke had bought him the half-second he needed, he stopped joking.

He didn't attack. That was the trick of it, the thing he could never have explained to the order, the thing that would have gotten him laughed out of the training yard: he didn't attack at all. He simply stopped resisting. He opened himself to the storm in the chamber — Aldric's stolen, furious, terrified storm — and offered it what Aldric never had: a choice. *You don't have to serve him. You don't have to be eaten. Come home.*

The storm considered this.

Wendell felt it considering, the way you feel a startled animal deciding whether to bolt or trust, and the whole broken chamber held its breath — Aldric mid-incantation, lightning dying unborn in his palms, his face going through surprise and betrayal and, underneath it all, briefly, terribly, grief.

Then the storm left him.

It went out of Aldric like tide out of a harbor, streaming across the gap in ribbons of silver and blue, and it came to Wendell and it did not enter him, did not fill him, did not make him mighty. It simply *stayed near*, swirling around him in slow spirals, close as a flock of birds, and Aldric — drained, soaked, suddenly just an old man standing on a rock in the rain — staggered and dropped to one knee on his ledge.

"No," Aldric whispered. "No. I spent thirty years. I gave up *everything*. My students, my order, my—" His voice broke, and reassembled itself into fury. "I did it *right*, Wendell. I followed every rule. I made every sacrifice. And you — you sold *charms*—"

"I know," Wendell said quietly. The spiraling storm hummed around him, and it sounded, faintly, like laughter. "That's the worst part, isn't it? You did everything right and it didn't work, because the rules were wrong. I'm sorry, old man. I'm sorry about all of it. I'd take it back for you if I could."

"Don't you dare pity me."

"Too late. Been doing it for twelve years. You were my favorite person in the world, and I'm going to keep pitying you right up until they arrest you, and probably after."

Aldric tried to rise. Failed. Tried again, and this time got one knee under himself, teeth bared, reaching — not for the storm now but for the Spire's core, for the raw failing covenant beneath them, for one last theft—

And the sky above the chamber tore.

Not metaphorically. A seam of black split the churning clouds directly overhead, widening with a sound like the world clearing its throat, and through it Wendell glimpsed something that was not sky and not sea and not anything with a name he knew, and every hair on his body stood up, and the storm around him went utterly silent and still.

Below — everywhere below, in the stone, in the roots of the world — Quentin's song changed key.

It rose. It became urgent, and bright, and very young, and it was no longer the sound of a child climbing. It was the sound of a child arriving.

*Aldric later,* the First Tongue seemed to say, from everywhere, in the voice of a boy who had never once raised his voice. *The core is open. And something is waiting for us.*

Wendell looked at his mentor, kneeling in the rain on a crumbling ledge at the top of the world, and found he had nothing left to say to him that wasn't either cruel or true, and he'd promised himself, twelve years ago, to stop being both.

So he turned, and asked the wind — politely, the way you'd ask a friend — to carry him down into the heart of the Spire, toward the small bright voice that was holding up the sky.

Behind him, in the rain, Aldric began to laugh.
