## 22. Rain on the Shop Window

The rain over Corvane had changed.

That was the thing nobody could quite stop talking about — not the amount of it, which was unremarkable enough, but the *feel* of it. The old rain had come down like a grudge, cold and sideways and full of salt scraped off some distant, dying sea. The new rain came down straight, soft, and warm as bathwater, and it fell with a patience that made people stand in doorways just to watch it, the way they might watch a stranger who looked vaguely like someone they'd loved.

Wendell Marchetti noticed it every single morning, because his shop window faced east, and every morning the light came through the water on the glass and scattered itself across his counter of charms in little trembling coins of gold.

He'd reopened the shop in the spring. Not hidden now, not behind false fronts and apologetic signage. The letters painted on the window read MARCHETTI'S CHARMS & WEATHERWORKING in plain, cheerful script, and beneath them, smaller: *Rain guaranteed. Storms by appointment.*

The last line had been Sera Voss's suggestion. She claimed it was good business. Wendell suspected she simply liked saying it aloud.

Business, as it happened, was absurd. Half the city that had once reported their neighbors for so much as a sneeze that sounded magical now queued down the street for luck-knots and fair-weather bells. The other half queued out of sheer curiosity, wanting to see the man from the stories — the storm-caller who climbed the Spire, the one who dueled the great Aldric at the top of the world while the sky tore open above them all.

Wendell told those customers the truth, more or less, which is to say he made it boring.

"There wasn't much dueling," he'd say, wrapping a charm in brown paper. "Mostly climbing. Terrible on the knees."

They always looked disappointed. He'd learned to live with it.

This particular morning was a Tuesday, gray-gold and gentle, and Wendell was doing inventory when the bell above his door gave its small bright cough and Sera Voss let herself in without waiting for an answer, which was her way.

"You're out of the blue ones," she said, setting down a paper bag of honey rolls on the counter like a tribute payment.

"I'm out of the blue ones because you bought the blue ones. All of them. Twice."

"My office gets gloomy." She shrugged out of her coat, which still bore the cut and bearing of the Inquisition even though the Inquisition itself had been formally dissolved four months ago, its ledgers burned in the square in a ceremony Sera had insisted on conducting personally, page by page, so she could read each name before it went into the fire. "Also, the Council sent another letter about the coastal wards. They want you to consult."

"They want me to *do*, is what they want. Consult is the polite word for it."

"Is that a no?"

Wendell looked at the window. The rain wrote slow silver sentences down the glass and erased them, wrote and erased, patient as breathing. Somewhere far off, past the marshes and the drowned river where he'd paid away his wedding day to a ferryman-spirit whose face he could no longer quite recall — the memory gone like a coin dropped into dark water — the Spire stood at the world's edge, wrapped in light and wind and a boy who had chosen to become weather.

"It's a 'not yet,'" he said. "It's a 'let a man sell charms in peace.'"

Sera snorted, took a honey roll, and settled onto the stool behind the counter that she had long ago annexed as her own territory. For a while neither of them spoke. That was the other thing that had changed: silence used to be something Wendell filled frantically, the way you stuff cloth into cracks against a draft. Now silence was just a room they sat in together.

"The dreams are quieter," she said eventually. It wasn't really a question. Everyone in Corvane had them now, faintly — dreams of tall grass moving under a sky the color of hammered tin, of vast slow shapes turning overhead like whales made of cloud. Quentin's dreams, leaking gently across the bridge between realms. Most people found them soothing. Wendell found them like hearing a child humming in the next room.

"Quieter," he agreed. "Not gone."

"He visits?"

"Once or twice a season. Knocking on the glass, usually, like a bird that's forgotten what birds do." Wendell smiled despite himself. "He doesn't come inside anymore. I think it's hard for him — holding a shape that small. But he comes."

Sera turned the honey roll over in her hands. When she spoke again her voice had the careful flatness she used when she was about to say something that cost her.

"I gave up my sister's face," she said. "At the ferry. You know that. Some mornings I try to remember her and there's just... a shape. A dress I remember. A laugh. But the face—" She stopped, pressed her lips together, started again. "Last week I dreamed the tall-grass dream, and there was someone standing in it. Small. And when she turned around—"

She didn't finish. She didn't have to. Wendell reached across the counter and put his scarred hand over hers, and she let him, staring very hard at the jar of fair-weather bells as though one of them might ring and save her from having felt anything.

"He can't bring things back," Wendell said quietly. "That's not what the bridge does."

"I know what the bridge does." A breath. "But he *showed* me her. Just for a moment. Enough to—" Her jaw worked. "Enough to say goodbye properly this time. Instead of signing the order that killed her."

"Sera—"

"Don't. Don't make it a moment. I'll start crying and then you'll start crying and then we're two middle-aged disasters weeping over honey rolls in a charm shop, and the neighbors will talk." She dashed her eyes briskly with her sleeve, exactly as unsentimental as she'd been the day she'd cornered him at the ferry crossing with a warrant in her fist and doubt eating holes in her conviction. "Anyway. That's my news. Your turn. Any storms by appointment?"

"Two weddings and a funeral. The funeral party wants clear skies; the widow wants rain. I'm charging them both and letting the sky decide."

"That," Sera said, pointing at him with the remainder of her honey roll, "is the most corrupt thing I've ever heard, and I worked for the Inquisition."

"I contain multitudes."

* * *

After she left — taking three blue charms and pretending Wendell hadn't slipped a fourth into her coat pocket, because her office got gloomy and she deserved one for free — the afternoon went long and gold. Wendell did the small work of the small life he'd built twice over: winding luck-knots, tuning the rain-bells, mending a weather-vane for old Mistress Callow upstairs who swore it pointed at her sister's house no matter which way the wind blew. (He checked it. It did. He decided some mysteries were none of his business.)

Twelve years he had spent being deliberately small. Two years since the boy knocked. Sometimes the arithmetic of it caught him off guard — how a whole life could be measured in doors. One door opened to a silent child speaking words that made his scars ache. Another door torn open in a wall of falling rain while wardens hammered at his shutters. A gate of living stone that showed him the worst thing he'd ever done and asked only that he look at it without flinching.

And now this door, this ordinary shop door with its cheerful bell, and outside it the ordinary street, wet and warm, full of people who were learning — slowly, clumsily, the way people learn everything — to stop being afraid of the sky.

Wendell was laughing at something Mistress Callow had shouted down the stairwell ("and tell your vane if it does that again I'll melt it for candle-tops!") when the light in the shop changed.

Not dimmed. Not brightened. *Changed* — the way air changes before a storm, except there was no storm coming; he'd have known. Every storm-caller knows. This was something else: a hush with a heartbeat in it.

The rain on the window stopped mid-fall.

Not ceased — *stopped*, each drop hanging against the glass like beads on a thread, catching the late sun, and through them walked a shape made of light and moving air, child-sized, barefoot, unhurried.

Wendell set down the weather-vane very carefully, because his hands were shaking and the vane was old and he had promised Mistress Callow.

"Quentin," he said.

The shape resolved the way fog resolves, and then there was a boy standing in his shop — a boy made mostly of brightness, edges trailing off into wisps like breath in winter, eyes the deep restless gray of a sky deciding what to be. His mouth moved and the First Tongue came out of it, that old language that had once bound the mortal world to the storm-realm of Ael, and the words went through Wendell like warmth through cold hands:

*I wanted to see if your window still fogs up when it rains.*

"It does," Wendell managed. His voice cracked in the middle like a boy of fourteen instead of a man of forty-odd. "Terribly. I have to wipe it twice a day. It's a scandal."

Quentin grinned — and oh, it was the same grin, the mute child's grin from the first night, the one that had said *I know what you are* without a single word. He drifted rather than walked to the counter, trailing motes, and peered at the jars and knots and bells with the fierce concentration of a scholar reviewing a colleague's work.

*Your luck-knots are crooked,* he observed.

"They're *rustic.*"

*Crooked.*

"Crooked luck is still luck. Ask any gambler." Wendell came around the counter, slowly, drinking him in. This was the part he never got used to, never would get used to: the boy was here and not here, solid as sunlight and just as impossible to hold. "How is it? Up there. In the — between."

Quentin considered the question with the gravity it deserved, head tipping, light shifting through him like weather seen from very high up.

*Loud,* he said at last. *Loud and kind. There are storms being born right now that won't touch ground for a hundred years, and they sing while they wait. And the quiet places aren't dead quiet anymore. They're resting quiet. Like after snow.* He looked up. *You should hear the Spire at dawn. It hums.*

"I bet it does." Wendell laughed — a real one, helpless, startled out of him. "I bet it does, you ridiculous brave creature."

And there it was. The sound hung in the shop, strange and rusty, and Wendell realized with a small internal jolt that this was the first time in twelve years — twelve years of hiding, grieving, selling rain-charms that barely worked and calling it survival — that he had laughed with his whole chest, no floor under it, nothing held back.

Quentin's whole luminous face lit like a squall-line catching sunset. *There it is!* he said in the First Tongue, delighted. *Sera owes me. We had a wager.*

"You did *not* place bets on my emotional recovery with the former Inquisitor of Corvane."

*Two coppers and a honey roll.*

"She has a whole bag of them in her office, you know. You've been bribing my friends."

*Bridge privileges,* Quentin said serenely, which apparently covered a multitude of sins.

* * *

They sat together while the light went low. Quentin couldn't stay long — Wendell knew that, had learned it the hard way the first visit, when he'd begged and bargained and finally gone silent with grief until the boy had pressed his small bright hand to Wendell's cheek and shown him, wordlessly, that there was no *leaving* in what Quentin had become. Only *reaching*. You cannot lose someone who is the distance between all things.

Still. Still, the visits ended, and each ending cost something, and Wendell had decided long ago that paying it gladly was the whole point.

"Tell me what you saw today," Quentin said, curled on the counter like a cat made of morning, watching Wendell close up the jars. "Down here. The small things. I like the small things best."

Wendell told him. About Mistress Callow's treasonous weather-vane. About the wedding party who'd wanted sun and gotten a brief, ceremonial shower instead, and wept with happiness anyway, because warm rain was lucky now, everyone knew it. About the children who played a game called *bridge-bridge* in the gutters, arms spread, running through puddles shouting words that were almost, almost the First Tongue, garbled and glorious.

"One of them got it right yesterday," Wendell said. "A girl, maybe six. Shouted *come home safe* in the old tongue at her brother, no idea what she'd said. Just liked the sound of it." His throat tightened. "The language isn't dying anymore, Quentin. It's growing up wild, like weeds in a crack. Nobody's teaching it. It's just... arriving."

*Good,* Quentin said softly. *That was the whole point. That was always the whole point.* He was quiet a moment, and when he spoke again the playfulness had gentled into something older than any child. *The covenant was never meant to be a chain, Wendell. Or a sacrifice. It was meant to be a conversation. My grandfather's generation made it heavy. I just... made it light again.*

"You made yourself light again," Wendell said roughly. "Don't think I don't know the difference. Don't think a day goes by—"

"Wendell." The boy slid off the counter and stood in front of him, and put one hand of light and wind against Wendell's burned and scarred palm. Where they touched, the old ache — the ache that had lived in his hands since the doorway of rain, since the duel, since he'd finally learned to let go of the storm instead of seizing it — went perfectly, blessedly quiet. *Twelve wasted years, you called them once. They weren't wasted. Everything you learned about being small, about surviving, about loving people in secret — that's why you could climb. That's why I knocked on *your* door. Loud dreams, remember? Yours were loud because you never stopped hoping, even when you thought you had.*

Wendell knelt — his knees complained, as they always did, and he ignored them, as he always did — and rested his forehead against the boy's bright hair, which smelled like the minute before rain begins.

"Come back safe," he whispered in the First Tongue, the words clumsy on his tongue after all these years, growing wild in him too.

*Always,* Quentin answered. *That's the whole job description.*

* * *

When the boy faded — slowly, waving, leaving motes drifting in the lamplight like lazy golden snow — Wendell stood a long while in the empty shop with his hand pressed flat over his heart, feeling the echo of the touch.

Then he wiped the fogged window with his sleeve, the way he did twice a day, the scandal of it, and looked out at the street. Warm rain was starting again, fine and silver, falling straight and patient. Under the lamp across the road, a knot of children were playing bridge-bridge in the puddles, arms wide, shrieking their beautiful broken words at the sky.

And the sky, for the first time in recorded history, shrieked something broken and beautiful back.

Wendell Marchetti laughed — second time that day, a personal record — pulled his coat off its hook, stepped out into the warm rain without an umbrella, and went to teach six-year-olds the proper pronunciation of *come home safe.*

Above him, far beyond the clouds, at the root of the sky, something young and vast and kind hummed along.

**THE END**
