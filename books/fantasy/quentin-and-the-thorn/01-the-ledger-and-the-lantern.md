## 1. The Ledger and the Lantern

The candle had burned down to its last finger's width when Edmund Halloran finished the forty-third contract of the evening.

She set down the pen and flexed her right hand, watching the cramp loosen finger by finger in the candlelight. Ink stained her knuckles to the second joint — it always did, no matter how carefully she worked — and she had long ago stopped trying to scrub it away. A scribe with clean hands was a scribe with nothing to do, and Brother Aldous noticed such things. Brother Aldous noticed everything.

Around her, the copying hall of the Thornfell temple lay quiet as a held breath. Forty desks, forty candles, and only six still lit at this hour. The other scribes had gone up to their cells hours ago, but Edmund had learned to love these last solitary stretches: the great vaulted dark above her, the smell of tallow and old vellum, the tick of cooling wax, and beneath it all the mountain's own silence pressing down through the stone like a patient hand.

She was not supposed to be here this late. Scribes were allotted their hours, and the temple was generous with them precisely because generosity cost nothing — a debt-bound worker who loved her work asked fewer questions about why she could not leave it.

Edmund opened the ledger drawer at her hip and drew out the night's true labor.

It was a slim book, bound in grey leather gone soft with handling, small enough to vanish inside her sleeve. To anyone who found it, it would look like practice work: a novice's exercises in cartography, mountain passes rendered in careful ink. And that was even true, in its way. She *was* practicing. She had been practicing for three years.

By candlelight she turned to a fresh page and began, from memory, the pass above Hollowmere — the one the shepherds called the Shepherd's Tooth, where the trail narrowed to the width of a coffin and dropped away on either side into country no map in the temple library admitted existed. She drew the switchbacks first, then the cairn that marked the safe line across the scree, then the hollow beneath the overhanging rock where a traveler could wait out weather or worse. Her pen moved without hesitation. She had walked that pass eleven times before her thirteenth year, back when she had still been allowed a name that fit and a body the world did not need to be lied to about.

Her father had carried wool over those passes. Before the debts. Before the temple's moneylenders had sunk their courteous teeth into the Halloran family and begun, year by patient year, to swallow them whole.

*Forty-three contracts,* she thought, looking at the neat stack beside her inkwell. *Forty-three families bound.*

That was the arithmetic of Thornfell, and she knew it better than any priest alive, because she copied it out daily in her own fair hand. The temple lent coin at charity rates — so the proclamations said — and took contracts in return, contracts written in language so old and knotted that only the temple's own scribes could read all of it. Edmund read all of it. Every clause. Every contingency. She knew what happened when a family missed a third payment, and what happened at the fifth, and what the fine print said about children born into arrears. She had copied her own name into that machinery once, four years ago, in a boy's handwriting she had invented for the purpose: *E. Halloran, scribe, indentured until such time as the debt of the House of Halloran be discharged in full.*

Until such time. Four words, and they weighed more than the mountain.

The candle guttered. Edmund trimmed the wick with a fingernail and made herself look at the little grey book — at the eleven passes she had mapped, the three river fords, the shepherd bothies marked with tiny crosses, the places where the old border wards still stood and the places where they had rotted to nothing. It was an escape drawn in installments. Every night a few more lines. She told herself she did not know yet whether she would ever use it, and every night she believed herself a little less.

A floorboard creaked at the far end of the hall.

Edmund's sleeve was over the grey book before the sound finished, her pen already moving across a fresh contract page, her face arranged into the mild concentration of a tired young man doing honest work. She did not look up. Looking up was for people with nothing to hide.

"Still burning temple wax, Halloran?"

Brother Aldous. His voice carried the particular softness he saved for those he owned — not cruelty, never cruelty, only the gentle certainty of a man who had never once needed to raise his voice because the contracts did his shouting for him. He came down the aisle between the desks with his lamp held high, and the light slid across her work like a tax collector's eye.

"The northern ledgers wanted finishing, brother," Edmund said. "I thought to spare myself the morning."

"Mm." He set the lamp on her desk, unhurried, and stood reading her copy upside down, which he was skilled enough to do. "Your hand improves. That is the trouble with you, Halloran. Everything about you improves."

"I'm grateful for the teaching."

"You're grateful for nothing. Gratitude is a virtue of free men." He smiled as he said it, a small dry smile, and Edmund smiled back the way one smiles at weather. This was the liturgy of their acquaintance, performed nightly: he reminded her what she was, and she thanked him for the reminder, and neither of them pretended it was anything but what it was.

He lingered, though. That was new. His lamp-light moved across the stack of finished contracts, counting them, and something in the set of his shoulders made the dread she carried everywhere anyway — the low constant hum of it, under her ribs, like the temple bells heard through walls — tighten into a single cold note.

"Forty-three," Aldous said. "You are fast becoming the finest copyist in Thornfell. Which presents a difficulty."

"Brother?"

"The northern houses have asked for you. Specifically." He turned the lamp slightly, and the flame threw his shadow huge against the pillar behind him, so that it seemed to lean over her. "The Greywater toll ledgers want reorganizing. Three years' work, they estimate. They've offered a transfer price."

Edmund kept her face still. Somewhere below her fear, her mind was moving fast and cold, the way it did when the dice were falling. *North.* Greywater sat on the far side of the high fells, two weeks' hard travel from the Thornfells, past every pass she had ever mapped — past every person who had ever known her father's daughter. A scribe sent north did not come back to visit. A scribe sent north was a scribe buried alive in someone else's ledger room, where no one would ever think to look for a girl named Edmund.

"They'd pay the temple well for my hand?" she asked, and was proud of how level it came out.

"They'd pay the temple *adequately*." Aldous picked up the top contract, glanced at it, set it down. "Less than your current output earns us, frankly. I told them so. I told them we might prefer instead to sell your contract locally — there's a wool factor in Vell's Crossing who wants a literate man to keep his books, and he pays better than the north does, and Vell's Crossing is close enough that we can keep an eye on the investment."

Vell's Crossing. Two days' walk. Where she had been born. Where her mother was buried, and where at least nine people still living could look at E. Halloran's face and see through the boy's clothes and the boy's name in a single unwary moment.

"And if neither house buys?" she asked.

"Then you stay, and we speak of it again at quarter's end." He lifted the lamp and looked at her directly then, and for a moment the softness fell away entirely, and what looked out of his face was simply arithmetic. "But understand me, Halloran. Your family's debt has been renegotiated twice, and the temple's patience is not a bottomless purse. Debts that cannot be paid in coin get paid in other ways. You are the most valuable thing the House of Halloran owns. Do not make me consider selling you somewhere you'd rather not go."

"I understand, brother."

"Good lad." The softness returned like a mask settling back into place. He collected his lamp, paused at the end of the row of desks. "Go up when you finish. Wax costs more than you do."

His footsteps faded up the aisle. The great door boomed shut behind him, and the silence came flooding back, and Edmund sat very still in the circle of her small flame with her heart going like a hammer in a locked room.

*Do not make me consider selling you somewhere you'd rather not go.*

There was nowhere she'd rather not go more than anywhere at all. That was the truth she had been circling for three years, drawing it pass by pass in grey ink: that every road out of this temple ended in some other temple, some other ledger, some other man holding her contract and calling her an investment. North, south, sold or unsold — the direction changed nothing. The only exit the contract permitted was the one it did not mention, because the men who wrote such contracts never imagined their property might simply walk off the page.

She pulled the grey book from her sleeve and laid it flat beneath the candle.

Eleven passes. Three fords. Nine bothies. Enough, if a body was quick and lucky and willing to sleep in ditches, to cross the whole spine of the Thornfells without touching a single ward-gate or toll-road. She had built it the way her father had built his wool routes: not to be fast, but to be *unfindable*. And she had told herself, every night for a thousand nights, that it was only habit, only comfort, only a game.

She dipped her pen and wrote at the head of a fresh page, in the smallest hand she could manage:

*One month.*

Not a decision, exactly. Decisions were things free people made. But the numbers had finally finished their slow argument, and she had run out of ways not to hear them. If she stayed past the quarter's end, Aldous would sell her — north or to Vell's Crossing, it hardly mattered; either way the grey book became a dead thing, a map of roads she would never walk. One month to finish the remaining passes. One month to save coin, steal bread, learn the guards' rounds. One month, and then the night she chose, she would walk out of the Thornfell temple as E. Halloran the scribe and cease to exist somewhere on the far side of the first ridge.

And her family? The thought arrived on schedule, punctual as guilt. Her father, bent now and half-blind, her sister Maren with two children and a husband who drank. Sold contracts passed down; that was in the fine print too. If she ran, the temple would come for them, would squeeze the balance out of whatever they had left —

— unless the debt was paid. Unless someone paid it. Unless *she* paid it, someday, from somewhere, out of a life that did not legally belong to the temple. Free, she could earn. Bound, she could only be spent. She had done that sum a hundred times and it always came out the same: running was not abandonment. Running was the only way she would ever hold enough in her hands to buy them back.

She blew out the candle.

Darkness took the copying hall whole, and Edmund sat in it a moment longer, letting her eyes adjust, listening to the mountain breathe around her. Through the high slit windows she could see a scatter of stars above the black teeth of the peaks — the constellations the shepherds steered by, the Wanderer, the Crown, the Sisters walking hand in hand. She counted them without meaning to, an old childhood habit, a way of proving the sky was still where she had left it.

They were all there. All of them, bright and indifferent, keeping their ancient appointments over a country full of ledgers.

*One month,* she thought again, and gathered her papers, and went up through the dark toward her narrow cell, carrying her stolen maps against her ribs like a second heartbeat.

She did not know — could not have known, that night, with the ink still wet on her resolution — that the stars she had just counted were already dying. That far above the sleeping fells, something vast and patient had begun to eat the light itself, star by star, and that within a handful of nights the whole sky over Thornfell would go black as an unpaid account.

She slept, and did not dream, and the candle she left unburned was the last ordinary hour she would ever keep.

* * *

Morning came grey and early, as it did in the mountains, and with it the bell that summoned the scribes down to the hall. Edmund washed, bound her chest, pinned her hair flat beneath her cap, and studied the stranger in the cracked mirror until the stranger's eyes went steady — a ritual four years old, performed so often it had worn smooth as a prayer bead. *E. Halloran. Scribe. Nobody's daughter.* Then she went down to spend another day copying other people's chains in her beautiful improving hand.

At midday, Brother Aldous sent for her.

She climbed the stair to his study with her stomach tight, rehearsing innocence. But Aldous was not alone. A second priest stood at the window — a visitor, by the travel dust on his robes, a thin man with the grey complexion of someone who had crossed the high passes recently and often. On Aldous's desk lay an open ledger, and Edmund's stomach dropped through the floor of the world, because she knew that ledger. It was the old one. The one from her first year, the one with the practice pages in the margins.

"Halloran," Aldous said pleasantly. "Brother Corvan of the Greywater house tells me the northern ledgers are in worse disorder than we thought. He'd like to leave within the week. With you."

Corvan turned from the window and looked at her — a long, appraising, *accounting* look — and outside the study window, far above the temple roofs, the morning light seemed suddenly thinner, as if a cloud had crossed a sun that no cloud could reach.

"A week," Edmund said. Her voice held. It was the finest work she had ever done. "I'll need three days to put my current ledgers in order, brother."

"Two," said Aldous, and smiled his small dry smile, and somewhere in the deep of her the grey book seemed to warm against her ribs like a live coal.

Two days. Not a month after all.

The sky, though she could not yet see it, had already begun to go out.
