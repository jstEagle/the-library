## 2. Night Without Stars

The candle had burned down to its last finger's width when Edmund Halloran finished copying the final contract of the day — a widow in Lowmarsh who owed the temple four silver crowns a month until her sons came of age or died trying to pay it. Edmund wrote *paid unto the temple of Thornfell, in perpetuity* in her careful clerk's hand, blotted it, and set it on the stack with the others. Forty-one contracts today. Forty-one lives measured out in ink.

She flexed her fingers, feeling the ache of them, and glanced toward the scriptorium door. Brother Aldous had gone to vespers an hour past. The other scribes had drifted out one by one, eager for supper and the small beer that passed for celebration on the eve of the Feast of Vigilance. The room was hers alone, lit by one guttering candle and the grey last light leaking through the arrow-slit window.

Edmund reached beneath her copy-stack and drew out the slim book she kept hidden there — a ledger, ostensibly, of temple correspondence. In truth its margins had become something else entirely: a map drawn in fragments across forty pages, so that no single leaf would betray her if the book were ever seized. A smuggler's path through the Thornfells, assembled over three years from the confessions of debtors, the grumbles of caravaneers, the drunken boasts of guards who thought the quiet scribe with the ink-stained fingers was listening to nothing at all.

Everyone talked in front of a scribe. That was the first lesson she had learned here, and the one that would save her life.

She dipped her pen and added tonight's intelligence to the margin of page thirty-one: the toll-keeper at Greywater had been replaced; the new man was temple-sworn, which meant the old road through the tollhouse was dead to her. She sketched instead the goat-track above it, the one the shepherds used when the snows were kind, and marked the place where the path crossed the stream twice — twice-crossed water, her father had called it, good luck on a bad road.

Her father. Edmund pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes until sparks bloomed in the dark. Her father, who had signed the family into bondage with a flourish because he could not read the terms. Her mother, who still did not know that the daughter who'd vanished into service as a "nephew" was here, twenty miles north, wearing a boy's name and a boy's coat and copying down the very chains that bound them all.

*Paid unto the temple, in perpetuity.* Unless someone paid it off. And no Halloran would ever earn enough in ten lifetimes to pay it off.

Unless someone stole the means.

She closed the ledger and slid it back beneath the stack. Tomorrow night — tomorrow, the eve of her escape, when the temple would be drunk on feast-day wine and the gates would stand unbarred until matins for the pilgrims — she would walk out of Thornfell carrying nothing but this book, a knife, and three years of memorized roads. By dawn she would be in the high passes. By winter she would be somewhere the temple's writ did not run, and she would send for her family, and they would be free, and the name Edmund Halloran would be buried along with the boy she had pretended to be.

It was a good plan. It was a careful plan. She had checked every seam of it a hundred times.

Which was why, when the light through the arrow-slit changed, she noticed at once.

Not brightened. Not dimmed. *Changed* — as if the world outside had been a painting and someone had leaned close and breathed upon it. Edmund rose slowly from her desk and crossed to the window, her heart beginning a low, confused drumming.

The mountains stood black against the sky. That was normal. But beyond them, where the peaks of the far range should have been etched in starlight — the Thornfells wore their stars like a crown, everyone said so, you could navigate by them blindfolded — there was nothing. Not cloud. Cloud had edges, cloud had movement, cloud had the faint grey breath of moonlight behind it even when the moon itself was hidden.

This was absence. Clean, total, absolute absence, running from horizon to horizon like a stroke of the censor's brush.

"Brother Aldous," she said aloud, to hear a voice, and her voice sounded wrong in the empty scriptorium, too small. "Brother Aldous, come look at—"

The wards shrieked.

Every soul in Thornfell knew the sound of the border wards the way farmers knew the sound of their own dogs. They hummed, always, a note below hearing, a comfort more than a noise. Edmund had lived inside that hum for three years without knowing she heard it until now, now, when it tore — a sound like a harp string snapping inside her skull, then another, then dozens, a whole instrument of them failing at once, and beneath the shrieking came a second sound she felt in her teeth rather than heard: a deep, grinding groan, as if the mountain itself had been struck below the waterline.

The candle went out. Not blown — *drained*, the flame folding inward into a point of darkness, and every other flame in the scriptorium with it, and when Edmund looked wildly out the window she saw the same thing happening across the whole valley: the temple's windows going dark one by one, the watch-fires on the wall guttering to embers, the lantern in the gatehouse dying even as its keeper shook it.

Only the stars' absence remained, vast and total, a darkness with weight to it. Edmund felt it pressing against the glass like deep water.

Then the screaming started.

* * *

She found Brother Aldous in the corridor, white-faced, holding his lantern up as though he could shame it back to life. Around him the temple was coming apart in orderly panic — novices running with candles that wouldn't light, the bell in the south tower ringing not the alarm but the older pattern, the one Edmund had only ever seen described in books: *the Vigil of the Broken Sky*, rung last, according to the chronicle, two hundred years ago, on a night no chronicler would name.

"The wards have failed," Aldous said. His voice was steady but his hands were not. "All of them. North, east, west — Father Anselm says the western line went first, all at once, like a throat cut."

"What does that mean?" Edmund asked, and made her voice the flat, stupid voice of a junior scribe asking a senior brother a question. Inside, her mind was already moving differently, coldly, cataloguing: the crypt passage behind the reliquary, unlocked since the flooding last spring because no one remembered to bar what no one remembered existed. The guard rotation, doubled on feast eves. The kitchen door, chained. The stables — no, not the stables, too watched. On foot, then. On foot through the pass, and pray the dark held.

"It means," Aldous said, and stopped, because he didn't know, and the not-knowing was worse than any answer. "It means get to the chapel, boy. Everyone to the chapel. The Father Superior will lead the Vigil."

Edmund nodded and let herself be swept along in the current of brown robes, counting exits under her breath the way other people counted prayers.

The chapel was full and getting fuller, hundreds of bodies pressing in, torches relit now — fire worked again, whatever had quenched it passing like a wave — though the flames burned strangely, leaning all one direction as if in wind that touched nothing else. At the altar Father Anselm stood in full vestments, and Edmund, wedged near a pillar, saw something in his face she had never seen in three years of studying that face for weakness: fear without calculation. He looked like a man reading a contract whose terms he had never agreed to.

"The sky has been taken," Anselm said, and his voice carried the way it always carried, but thinner. "Taken, or given away, or spent — we do not know. The wards are silent. What I tell you is this: the temple stands. The temple has stood through worse than a dark night. Kneel. Pray. And let no man leave these walls until the Fathers understand what has happened."

*Let no man leave.* Edmund felt the words settle over the crowd like a net over fish. Already the great doors were being barred from within; already brothers with staves were taking positions at the entrances, ostensibly to keep order, actually to keep the flock penned. Panic made property of people faster than any debt-contract. She had known that for years. She had simply never expected to be inside the pen when the bars came down.

She knelt with the others. She bowed her head. And she watched, through her lashes, the one person in the chapel whose movements mattered: the sub-prior, Brother Caewin, who commanded the night guard, and who was even now whispering to two armed men by the door, and who kept glancing — not at the altar, not at the doors, but at the reliquary stair. The stair that led down past the tombs of the minor benefactors, past the flooded crypt, to the old postern passage that came up inside the boundary wall.

The passage only she knew about. Or so she had believed for three years.

Caewin posted one of his men at the head of that stair within the quarter hour.

Edmund closed her eyes and did the arithmetic of terror. Either Caewin knew about the postern — in which case her escape route was dead and possibly her cover with it — or he did not know, and was merely sealing every downward way out of a building full of frightened people, in which case the route lived but was guarded. There was a third possibility, the one that made her palms sweat against the stone floor: that the temple knew something about tonight that she did not, something that made the crypts important, something worth guarding in a crisis when every other able body was needed above.

She thought of her ledger hidden in the scriptorium. If they searched the rooms while everyone was penned in the chapel—

She needed to move. Now, while the Vigil was at its height, while every eye was fixed on Anselm and the dark, while the guards at the doors were watching the crowd and not each other.

Edmund Halloran, who had spent three years being invisible, chose the moment the incense smoke lay thickest and slipped backward along the pillar, into the shadow behind the baptismal font, through the disused sacristy door that stuck unless you lifted it exactly right — she had learned that in her first month, mapping the temple the way she mapped everything, because a map was the only inheritance her father had ever given her that was worth anything.

* * *

The scriptorium had already been searched. She knew it before she touched the door — the hinge oiled, the air disturbed, the smell of tallow that wasn't hers. Inside, by the light of the one torch burning in the corridor (fire behaved normally again here, though the window stayed black as wet slate), she found her desk drawer open and her spare pens rearranged. The ledger was gone.

She stood very still in the middle of the room and let the fear move through her and out, the way she had taught herself to let cramps and cold and hunger move through her, acknowledged and released. So. They had the margins. Page thirty-one with tonight's additions, page seven with the Greywater ford, all forty leaves of a smuggler's education. Someone had read them. Someone would be reading them still, matching routes to names, and among those names would be debts forgiven quietly, favors granted, and one junior scribe named Eddic Halloran whose handwriting appeared nowhere in the temple's official record but everywhere in its secrets.

They might not reach her name tonight. They might not reach it at all — the margins were coded, the routes fragmented, and half the intelligence was stale. But the toll-keeper's replacement, the twice-crossed stream, the Widow's Teeth track — fresh marks, fresh ink, unmistakably a plan.

Caewin hadn't posted a guard at the crypt stair because of the crisis. He'd posted it because of *her*.

And yet — Edmund turned this over, turning it the way she turned a difficult contract, looking for the clause that cut both ways — if they suspected her, why not simply seize her? Why search quietly, why leave her in the chapel with the rest? Because they weren't sure. Because a scribe's marginalia might be theft, might be treason, might be simple doodling. Because seizing the wrong servant on the holiest vigil of the year would raise questions the Fathers could not afford, on a night when the sky itself had failed and the wards lay dead and the temple's authority hung by a thread thinner than any of them would admit.

They were watching her. They did not yet know what she was. That gap between suspicion and certainty was the only door left open, and it was closing.

Edmund took the knife from her boot, checked its edge out of habit rather than hope, and went down into the temple's bones.

* * *

The reliquary stair was guarded, as she knew it would be. She did not go that way. Three years of invisibility had taught her the temple's second geography, the one that existed in the gaps between rooms: the laundry chute that dropped past the undercroft, the gap behind the wine racks wide enough for a thin boy who had learned to make himself thinner, the drainage channel — dry this time of year — that ran beneath the chapel foundations and surfaced, if you crawled long enough in the dark with your cheek in the grit, in the flooded crypt.

The water was knee-deep and cold enough to stop her breath. Tombs stood in rows on either side, the minor benefactors, merchants and minor lords who had bought their slabs with coin instead of virtue, and their effigies loomed above the black water like drowned men standing at attention. Edmund waded between them with her knife in her teeth and her heart going like a smith's hammer, and told herself the shaking was the cold.

The postern passage began behind the third tomb from the flood-line — she counted them in the dark, by touch, the way she had counted them a dozen times before on nights when escape had been a comfort imagined rather than attempted. The stone slab shifted at her shoulder. Cold air moved against her face, air that smelled of pine and snow and open country, and Edmund almost wept at the smell of it.

Behind her, far above, the Vigil bell changed its pattern — a new rhythm, urgent, arrhythmic, the ringers abandoning the office entirely. Something else had happened. Something worse, or nearer, or both. Shouts echoed down through stone, indistinct, and then a sound she felt through the soles of her feet: a deep concussion, like a door slamming somewhere enormous, followed by a silence that had texture to it, a silence like held breath.

The wards. Or what remained of them. Or something coming through where the wards had been.

Edmund did not wait to learn which. She pulled the slab shut behind her, sealing the crypt at her back, and went up the narrow spiral of the postern stair with her hand on the wall and her mind fixed on the one thing that mattered, the only thing, the thing she had carried through three years of servitude like a coal cupped in bare hands: *her family*. Somewhere south, in a house with a leaking roof, her mother was sleeping under a debt that Edmund meant to burn. Whatever howled in the dark tonight, whatever hunted through a starless sky, it would have to be quicker than that purpose.

The postern door opened onto the inner slope of the boundary wall, behind the woodstack, in the dead angle no watchman covered. Beyond it the valley lay under a darkness Edmund had never seen the equal of — no moon, no stars, the mountains mere suggestions, the world reduced to sound and smell and the memory of maps. Somewhere upslope, a wardstone was still glowing, the last one, a sickly amber pulse in the black like a dying coal, and around it — she squinted, refusing to believe it, believing it — shapes were moving. Many shapes. Wrong shapes, moving with a gait that was almost walking and wasn't, pouring down out of the pass where the border had been, where the border was supposed to be, where three years of carefully memorized smuggler roads now ran straight through the mouth of whatever this was.

Every road she knew led through that dark. Every road she knew was the only road there was.

Edmund Halloran pulled the boy's cap low over her eyes, turned her back on the temple that owned her, and ran for the pass — because the pass was north, and north was away, and away was the only direction freedom had ever faced.
