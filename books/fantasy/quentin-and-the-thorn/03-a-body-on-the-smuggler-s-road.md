## 3. A Body on the Smuggler's Road

The dark was not like other darkness.

Edmund had walked the high passes at night before — she had been born to them, in a way, her father's maps inked by lantern-light while she memorized each switchback as a child learns prayers. She knew how night behaved in the Thornfells. It pooled in the hollows and thinned along the ridgelines. It had texture, and weight, and above all it had *stars* — those cold scattered witnesses that a smuggler learned to read the way a priest reads scripture.

Tonight there were none. The sky was a single unbroken blackness, seamless as temple velvet, and Edmund kept catching herself looking up with the animal expectation of a woman who has stepped off a stair that isn't there. Twice she stumbled on ground she could have crossed blindfolded, because without the stars her body refused to believe its own knowledge.

*The wards are down,* she told herself, and made herself say it plainly, the way she'd been taught to copy out contracts: *the border wards have failed, and you are outside them, and no one is coming after you because everyone is too busy screaming.*

That last part was almost true. Behind her, far below, the temple of Thornfell glowed against the mountainside like an ember in ash — every window lit, bells ringing in patterns she'd never heard, the panic audible even at this distance as a thin continuous wail. They would seal the gates. They would count their scribes twice over and find one missing, but they would assume he had died in the crush or fled toward the lowlands like any sensible thief. No one would guess that "Brother Aldous" had gone *up*, into the teeth of the pass, along roads that existed on no ledger the temple owned.

She allowed herself one breath of something that was almost laughter. Then she climbed.

The smuggler's road was barely a road at all here — a shelf of broken stone cut into the mountain's flank, wide enough for goats and men who did not mind praying with their feet. Below it the valley dropped away into nothing; above it the cliffs rose into nothing. Edmund moved with her lantern shuttered to a knife-slit of light, one hand trailing the rock, counting steps out of habit. Forty paces to the cairn. Sixty more to where the path bent around the shoulder of the mountain. After that, the long scree run, and then Greywater by dawn if the weather held.

The weather did not hold. It never held. But dawn was a flexible concept tonight, with no sun promised and no stars to mark its approach.

She smelled the blood before she saw anything else.

It came to her on the wind as iron and wet wool, sudden and wrong, and Edmund stopped so completely that her boot heel clicked against stone. In the mountains you learned what smells meant. Smoke meant shelter or death. Pine meant cover. Blood meant one of two things: something had been killed, or something was dying, and either way the smart move was the same one.

*Walk away,* said the part of her that had survived twenty-two years of being Edmund Halloran. *You have maybe six hours before the temple notices which direction you went. You have a body that hasn't eaten properly in three days. You have a head start you cannot afford to spend.*

She stood there in the starless dark, listening to the wind carry that slow copper reek up from around the bend, and hated herself with great precision.

Because she knew what lay around that bend. There was a wardstone there — one of the old border stones, taller than a man, carved with the sigils of the first kingdom, standing where it had stood since before her grandmother's grandmother. The smugglers used it as a landmark. *Past the singing stone, keep left.* And if the wards had failed everywhere else in the Thornfells—

She went around the bend.

The wardstone was shattered. Not cracked, not chipped — *shattered*, burst outward from some point at its heart, its fragments flung across the path in a spray of granite shrapnel. The largest piece still bore half a sigil, and even in the slit of lantern light Edmund could see that the carving was scorched black, as though the symbol itself had burned from within. The air around it tasted of lightning and old copper. Her back teeth ached when she breathed.

And against the stone's broken base, crumpled like a puppet whose strings had been cut, lay a man.

He was dressed well — that was her first absurd observation. Fine riding leathers, dark, good stitching, the kind of clothes that cost more than her father had earned in five years of honest cartography. One sleeve was soaked through and shining. There was blood on the rocks beneath him, a shocking quantity of it, black in the lantern light, spreading in a slow fan. His breathing came in shallow, ragged pulls, each one whistling faintly through his teeth.

Edmund crouched beside him, keeping her lantern low, and looked at his face.

Young. Handsome in a battered way, the kind of face that belonged on coins or cathedral windows rather than bleeding out on a smuggler's road. Dirt-streaked. Pale as whey. And across his brow—

Her breath caught.

A crown. A thin circlet of gold, fused to his skin — not sitting upon his head but *grown into* it, the metal disappearing into his temples on either side, rising to a single point above his forehead. It gleamed dully in her shuttered light, and where it touched his skin the flesh was raw and weeping, inflamed around the band of gold like flesh around an infected wound.

No. That wasn't right. Crowns were things you took off. Crowns were ceremony, brass and velvet, worn for an hour and locked in a vault. This was not a crown. This was a *scar* wearing the shape of one.

"Hey." She touched his shoulder, carefully, the way you touch a snare you think might still be sprung. "Hey. Can you hear me?"

His eyes opened. They were grey, unfocused, swimming — and then they found her lantern, and fixed on it with desperate, drowning attention, as if it were the only real thing in a world gone liquid.

"Don't," he rasped. "Don't tell anyone."

"Tell anyone what? That I found you?"

"That you found—" He coughed, and the cough wracked him, and fresh blood welled at the corner of his mouth. "Me."

"You're bleeding out on a mountain road," Edmund said. "I think the secret's mostly out."

Something moved through his face that might have been a laugh, if laughs could be performed by a dying man through gritted teeth. His hand crept along the stone, found her wrist, gripped it with fingers that had astonishing strength left in them and no endurance at all.

"There were men," he whispered. "Behind me. On the road. They had— they had lights that weren't lights. If they find me—"

"They won't find you," Edmund heard herself say. "They'll find a dead man, if you don't shut up and let me look at that arm."

It was, she would reflect later, the moment she lost whatever argument she'd been having with herself. Because she had already knelt. Already set down the lantern. Already pulled open the satchel that hung at her hip — the satchel of a temple scribe, stocked with linen strips and a flask of grain spirit and a needle and thread, because Brother Aldous was famously careful about paper cuts and everyone had laughed at him for it.

*Leaving him feels like murder.* The thought arrived whole and unwelcome, and she pushed it down where she pushed all inconvenient truths: into the margins, to be dealt with never.

The wound was a spear-thrust through the meat of his left shoulder, entered clean, exited filthy. She poured grain spirit into it without warning and he made a sound through clenched teeth that was less a scream than a refusal to scream, and she thought, distantly, *he's done this before, he knows how to be hurt.*

"Hold still."

"What are you, a healer?"

"A scribe." She threaded the needle. "Hold still or I'll write my name inside you and charge your estate for the ink."

Another ghost of a laugh. She stitched him in the lantern slit, her hands steady, her mind cataloguing with the cold efficiency that had kept her alive: he'd lost a lot of blood but not all of it; the wound was ugly but survivable; he was feverish but lucid; and whoever had done this to him had wanted him to *run* first, had chased him up a mountain in the dark of a starless night, past a wardstone they had apparently torn apart like bread.

"Why were the wards down?" she asked, mostly to keep him talking. People who talked stayed conscious. "Do you know?"

"I don't know anything." His voice had gone strange. Flat. "I don't remember anything."

"People say that when they don't want to answer."

"I mean it literally." He turned his head — slowly, wincing — and looked at her, and his eyes were clear now and terribly honest. "I woke up on this road. Before that there is nothing. Just—" his hand drifted up, trembling, toward the gold at his brow, and stopped short of touching it, the way you stop short of a hot stove, "—just weight. Burning. Like something pressing down behind my eyes. And a name."

"Whose name?"

"Mine." He swallowed. "Quentin. That's all. Quentin. Everything else is—" His hand closed into a fist against the stone. "Gone. Burned out. Like someone took the house and left the door."

Edmund sat back on her heels and studied him, and everything in her that had survived by reading people read him now. Liars, she knew liars; she copied their signatures daily, watched them swear false oaths with ink-stained hands. Liars performed. They gave you too much detail or too little, watched your face for belief. This man did none of that. He simply lay there bleeding, telling her the shape of his emptiness like a man reporting the weather.

Either he was the finest liar in Thornfell, or he was exactly what he appeared: a crowned man with no memory, hunted through a night when every star in the sky had been devoured.

"Crowned," she said aloud. "You're wearing a crown and you claim no crown exists."

"It's not—" His jaw tightened. "Look at it. Look at it properly. Does that look like something a man *wears*?"

She raised the lantern. Against her better judgment she leaned close, and in the fuller light she saw what she had half-seen before and refused to know: the gold did not sit on his skin. It emerged from it. At his temples the metal sank into flesh without seam or scar, smooth as a river entering the sea, as though the crown had grown there, or been *put* there in some way that involved no fitting at all. Around its band the skin was angry and cracked, and — she leaned closer, hating herself — faintly, in the raw flesh, she could see fine dark lines radiating outward, like veins, like roots, like the branches of something feeding.

Like a contract, written in a living hand, signed in a living body.

Edmund had spent four years copying debt-contracts in the Thornfell temple. She knew what binding looked like. She knew what it looked like when a thing was owed and the owing had grown into the bone.

"You should take it off," she said, and immediately felt foolish.

"You try." His voice cracked on it. "I've tried. There's no edge to grip. There's no *join*. It's just—" He breathed once, hard, controlling it. "It drinks. At night. I don't know how I know that word, but it's the right one. When the dark comes, it drinks something out of me, and I wake up smaller."

The lantern flame guttered between them. Above, where stars should have been wheeling, the sky was a lid.

*The stars went out,* Edmund thought. *The same night this man crawled up my mountain wearing a crown that drinks.*

She was not a superstitious woman. Superstition was a luxury for people who didn't have debts. But she had grown up among smugglers and mapmakers, and both trades taught you the same lesson: when two impossible things arrive together, they are usually the same impossible thing.

"Can you walk?" she asked.

"Probably."

"Lie. Can you actually walk?"

"...With help."

"Then we have a problem, because I'm leaving you here."

He looked at her. In the slit of lantern light his grey eyes were fever-bright and utterly exhausted, and he did not argue, did not plead, did not perform. He just nodded, once, and said, "Thank you. For the stitches. For stopping at all." A pause. "Most people wouldn't have."

"No," Edmund agreed. "Most people wouldn't."

She gathered her satchel. She picked up the lantern. She stood, and turned toward the path, toward Greywater, toward the border and freedom and a life that belonged to no temple ledger —

—and stood there.

The wind came down the pass and moved his blood on the stones, thinning it, carrying the iron smell away into the dark. Somewhere below, very faintly, the temple bells were still ringing. Somewhere behind him, men with lights that weren't lights were climbing. He had no memory, no name beyond one word, no strength to stand unaided, and a thing on his brow that fed on him in the dark. If she walked away, he would die here, alone, in a night with no stars to watch him go.

*Leaving him feels like murder.*

"Damn it," Edmund Halloran said, to the mountain, to the missing stars, to her own traitorous conscience. "Damn it, damn it, *damn it*."

She turned back, knelt, and dragged his good arm across her shoulders.

"Listen to me," she said, hauling him upright while he hissed through his teeth. "I am not saving you. Understand? I'm a smuggler — or I was, before I got stupid and got caught — and I'm getting out of these mountains tomorrow, and you are going to be quiet, and heavy, and grateful, and the moment you can walk on your own, you're on your own. I have enough trouble without collecting strays."

"Understood," he managed.

"And if whoever did this to you comes looking—"

"They won't find us." Something flickered across his ruined face — not quite a smile, but the place where one lived. "You seem very hard to find."

"I'm the hardest thing in these mountains to find," Edmund said, and started walking, bearing most of his weight, his blood soaking warm through her sleeve. "Which is why I'm leaving. Keep your feet under you. Step where I step. And if you fall, fall quietly."

They went up the pass together, two fugitives under a lidless sky, the shattered wardstone falling away behind them. Edmund counted her steps out of habit and lost the count almost at once, and found, to her considerable irritation, that she did not care.

* * *

They made perhaps two miles before the fever took him seriously.

It began as a lean — his weight shifting incrementally onto her, then decisively, then entirely, until Edmund was less guiding him than dragging him, her boots skidding on scree, her shoulders burning. He muttered as she lowered him against a boulder out of the wind. Fragments. A brother's name spoken with love and something else tangled in it. *Molten.* He said *molten* twice, and shuddered. Once, clearly, terribly: *"Not yet — the coronation is not yet —"* and then his eyes rolled and he was gone into whatever burning country the fever built for him.

Edmund crouched over him in the starless dark and did arithmetic.

She had water for two days, food for one. She had a wounded man who weighed as much as a loaded pack and could not be abandoned without becoming a murderer. She had hunters somewhere behind them on the road. She had a forged seal, a stolen route, and a head start measured in hours.

And she had, she realized, looking down at his fever-lit face, a problem larger than all of those: the crown.

Because she had seen it again while supporting him, close against her shoulder, and in the dark — true dark, starless dark — the gold had *glowed*. Faintly. A dull ember-light pulsing in time with his heartbeat, and with each pulse Edmund had felt something, a faint tugging sensation behind her own eyes, like the moment before a sneeze, like a hook testing the water.

Whatever wore that crown, it was awake. And it was hungry. And the stars had not merely gone out.

They had been *eaten*.

Far below, at the edge of hearing, a bell tolled once — not the temple's pattern, not any pattern she knew. And on the road behind them, small and distant and unmistakable in the dead air, a light appeared. Then another. Then a third, moving in formation, climbing.

Not coming for her, she told herself. They couldn't be coming for her.

But her hand, entirely without permission, checked the satchel where her forged seals slept — and found, beneath them, the contract she had stolen back, her own name written in the temple's hand, and the words *property of the Thornfell temple* in ink that suddenly felt very much like a brand.

Edmund looked at the lights, and at the man who remembered only his name and a burning weight, and made the only choice left.

She shouldered him up, turned off the lantern altogether, and walked into the dark she alone knew how to read.
