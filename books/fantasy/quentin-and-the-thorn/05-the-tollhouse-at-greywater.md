## 5. The Tollhouse at Greywater

The river came first — a sound like a held breath released all at once, grey water tumbling over black stones somewhere below the mist. Then the road widened, and the tollhouse rose out of the fog the way a tooth rises from a gum: small, hard, inevitable.

Edmund stopped Quentin with a hand flat against his chest. He was still pale from the night's walking, still moving as though his skull were full of hot coals, which she suspected it was. The bandages beneath his hood had soaked through twice already, and she had no more linen worth the name.

"The Greywater crossing," she murmured. "One bridge, one keeper, one ledger." She could see the building clearly now: squat stone, slate roof furred with moss, a lantern burning in the window with that particular amber steadiness of temple-blessed oil. A chain hung across the road on the near side, dripping. On the far side, the road climbed toward the high fells and vanished into cloud.

"One keeper," Quentin repeated. His voice had gone rough. "And how many soldiers?"

"That's the question, isn't it."

She studied the tollhouse the way she'd once studied contract clauses — looking for the flaw, the loophole, the sentence written in small print that everyone else skimmed past. Smoke from the chimney. Wood stacked under an awning. A dog — no, not a dog; a goose, enormous and white and malevolent, patrolling the yard like a hired blade. No horses in the stable. No boots by the door but one pair, large and mud-caked.

One keeper, then. One goose.

"Temple seals open every tollhouse between here and the coast," she said. "Even now. Even with the wards down. Especially with the wards down — nobody wants to be the fool who turned away a priest during a crisis."

"You're not a priest."

"I'm a scribe with access to a wax supply, a signet ring I was never supposed to touch, and four years of watching how documents are meant to look." She patted the satchel at her hip. "The temple trains its copyists to forge nothing and notice everything. It never occurred to them those are the same skill."

Quentin looked at her for a long moment. In the grey light, with the fog beading in his dark hair and the bruised shadows under his eyes, he looked less like a wounded stranger than like something the mountain had coughed up — beautiful and broken and not entirely sure it wanted to keep living.

"And if he notices everything too?"

"Then we run." She adjusted her hood, dropped her shoulders into the stoop she'd worn for four years — Halloran the scribe, narrow-chested, ink-stained, harmless — and put her hand back on the seal she'd pressed into warm wax an hour before dawn. "Stay behind me. Say nothing unless you must. If you must, complain about your feet. Nobody suspects a man who complains about his feet."

The goose saw them coming from forty yards and began to scream.

It was, Edmund thought grimly, the loudest thing she'd heard since the stars went out.

* * *

The toll-keeper was a broad woman of sixty-odd with forearms like ship's cable and a face that had been weathered into something between leather and slate. She came to the door with a crossbow already half-raised, lowered it when she saw the temple grey of Edmund's cloak, and did not lower it entirely.

"Temple business?" Her eyes went over them both — the stooped scribe, the tall hooded man swaying slightly on his feet. "Crossing's a copper a head. Temple passage is free, but I'll want to see the seal and write the passage in my book. Rules are rules. More so now, with the sky gone wrong."

"Of course." Edmund produced the sealed chit with the ease of long practice. "Brother Aldous sends us north with dispatches. There's been — well. You'll have seen the sky."

"Seen nothing else for two nights." The toll-keeper took the chit, turned it over, and grunted. "Wax is soft. You lads were up early."

"We were up all night, mistress. As I imagine everyone was."

That earned her a thin smile. The woman stepped back, jerked her chin toward the interior. "Come in out of the wet while I mark you down. Mind the goose."

Inside, the tollhouse smelled of woodsmoke, tallow, and river damp. A single room: hearth, table, bed behind a curtain, and on the table the thing Edmund had been dreading since the bridge came into view — a great ledger bound in green hide, its corners worn round by decades of hands. Beside it, a pot of ink, a sand-shaker, a stub of quill.

The toll-keeper sat, opened the book to the current page, and copied out the details of the seal in a hand like cart-tracks. Name of bearer. Purpose. Seal-mark. Date.

Date. Edmund's stomach dipped. She had written yesterday's date. In the panic of the darkened sky, in the terror of the crypt passage and the bleeding man and the endless starless miles, she had written yesterday's date on a forged temple chit, and any clerk worth their salt would—

But the toll-keeper didn't pause. She finished the entry, blotted it, and slid the chit back across the table.

"There. You'll want to make good time to the high fells before the mist burns off. Road's clear past the shepherd country, but—" She stopped.

Edmund felt it happen. Felt the woman's gaze slide from the chit in her hand to Edmund herself, and then to the ledger, and then back — a slow triangulation, the kind of look Edmund had spent four years training herself to deflect. The toll-keeper's brow creased. She licked her thumb, turned back three pages in the green-hide book, and ran a finger down a column of old entries.

"You wrote this one," she said quietly. Not a question.

"Mistress—"

"Three years back. Spring. Two men claiming temple passage, seal signed 'under hand of E. Halloran, scribe.'" She looked up, and there was nothing hostile in her face, which was somehow worse. There was recognition. "I remember because the hand was fine. Finer than most priests manage. A careful hand. A left-slanting hand." Her eyes dropped, deliberate and slow, to Edmund's right hand resting on the table. "You write with your left when you think no one's watching. You're doing it now — the way you hold your wrist."

The fire popped. Outside, the goose screamed at something only a goose could hate.

Edmund's mind moved fast and cold, the way it always did, laying out exits like cards: the door, ten feet; the crossbow leaning against the wall, loaded, closer to the toll-keeper than to her; Quentin behind her, too weak for a fight, too recognizable under his hood; the bridge beyond, thirty yards of open stone in full view of the window.

"I don't know what you mean," she said, and hated how thin it sounded.

"I mean," the toll-keeper said, "that last night a rider came through from the temple, before the sky went black, carrying a general summons. Every toll, every ferry, every ford from Greywater to the sea." She reached beneath the table and drew out a folded paper, cracked along its creases, stamped with the Thornfell sunburst. "They're looking for someone. And the description—" she unfolded it, smoothed it flat with two huge fingers, and read aloud, almost gently, "'A scribe of the temple scriptorium, given name Edmund Halloran, believed to be masquerading in male dress. Small stature. Writes with either hand. Fled the temple on the night of the ward-failure. Considered dangerous. Reward: remission of debt-contract, plus fifty crowns.'"

The words landed one at a time, each with its own small weight, like stones dropped into still water.

*Remission of debt-contract.*

Her family. Her mother's shaking hands. The little house in Lowmark with its leaking roof and its mortgage written in blood-red ink in the temple's books. Fifty crowns and a clean ledger, all for the price of her own face.

"They're not hunting your friend," the toll-keeper said, nodding at Quentin without taking her eyes off Edmund. "Or if they are, it's you they want more. That paper's been on my wall since last night, girl. I've had time to memorize it."

The silence stretched. Somewhere in it, Edmund heard her own heartbeat, quick and shallow as a bird's.

She thought about lying. She thought about the crossbow, about the distance to the door, about whether Quentin could run. She thought about the goose.

What she said was: "How much of the reward is paid on capture, and how much on delivery to the temple?"

The toll-keeper blinked. Then, slowly, she laughed — a dry, creaking sound, like a gate that hadn't swung in years. "Oh, you're a cool one. I'll give you that." She folded the summons closed. "Full payment on delivery. Nothing on capture. Which means if I take you myself, I've got to haul you thirty miles back down that road, past whatever it is that made the stars go out, and hand you to priests who are currently tearing their own temple apart." She shook her head. "I'm a toll-keeper, not a hero. But I'll tell you what I am, girl: I'm a woman who keeps books. And in forty years of keeping books, I've learned that the ones who write the finest hands are usually the ones being written about."

She stood. She took the crossbow off the wall — and set it down on the far side of the room, deliberately, out of her own reach.

"Bridge is free," she said. "Go. And when you get wherever you're going — burn that chit. Your date's wrong anyway."

* * *

Quentin's legs nearly failed him on the bridge.

He made it look like stumbling — he'd gotten good at stumbling, these last days, good at letting the weakness have him in small controlled doses — but the truth was that inside the tollhouse, while the two of them talked in that quiet, lethal way women talk when they've decided something about each other, he had understood exactly what was happening. He'd understood it from the moment the toll-keeper unfolded the paper.

*Edmund Halloran.* A name. A real one, with a family attached, a debt, a price on it. Four days he'd traveled with this person, and he hadn't even known her name until she gave it to a stranger to save them both.

And the summons — he'd seen the sunburst stamp upside-down across the table. He knew that stamp. He'd grown up under it. Which meant the Usurper's people, or the temple's, or both, had moved within hours of the ward-failure. Within hours. Someone had known Edmund would run before she'd decided to run herself.

Someone was hunting her specifically. Not the crowned ghost bleeding in the fells. *Her.*

"Why?" he managed, when they reached the far side and the road bent up into the mist and the tollhouse shrank behind them. "Why you? You're a copyist. You copy things. What could they possibly—"

"Because I know the routes." She didn't slow her pace. The climb was steepening, the road giving way to switchbacks cut into the fell-side, and she climbed them with the sureness of someone ascending stairs in her own house. "Every smuggler's path through the Thornfells. Every ward-gap, every blind valley, every place the temple's writ doesn't reach. I've been drawing them in the margins of their own ledgers for four years, Quentin. They audit those ledgers. Eventually somebody reads the margins."

"So they want the maps."

"I expect they want the maps." Her voice was very level, the way it went when she was holding something down. "And they want the example. A scribe who runs teaches every other scribe that running is possible. Temples can't abide that. Kingdoms neither." She glanced back at him, and in the flat white light her eyes were the color of the river below — grey-green, cold-moving, hiding depth. "You should go your own way. Truly. They're not spending fifty crowns on me because I'm interesting company. Whatever's after *you*, it's after me cheaper, and I'm dragging you slower."

"No."

"It wasn't really a question I expected you to answer sensibly."

"No," Quentin said again, and stopped walking, which cost him — the fell tilted, the mist spun, and for a moment the weight on his brow flared so hot he tasted copper. He breathed through it. "You pulled me off a mountainside when leaving me would have been easier. You lied to a toll-keeper with a crossbow when running would have been easier. I don't—" He searched for the words and found, to his horror, that they were honest ones. "I don't have anything to offer you. I can't pay you. I can't protect you. Half the time I can't stay upright. But I'm not leaving you to be hunted alone. That's not — that's not a thing I'll do."

She looked at him for a long moment. Something moved behind her eyes — he couldn't read it, this person whose whole life had been learning not to be read.

"You're a strange man, Quentin," she said finally. "Whoever you were before you crawled onto that road."

"Nobody," he said, and the lie sat on his tongue like a stone, heavier than the crown. "Nobody at all."

* * *

They climbed through the morning, and the mist burned off, and the high fells opened around them like a grey-green sea — mile upon mile of tussock grass and scree and standing stones, wind-combed, treeless, empty. Empty except, Edmund noted, for the smoke.

She saw it first as a smudge against the northern horizon. By midday there were three smudges. By late afternoon, riding the wind, came a sound she knew from childhood and had hoped never to hear again: hunting horns, thin and far, calling to one another across the valleys in the old relay-pattern. *Found. Found. This way.*

Not behind them. Ahead and to the east, sweeping along the line of the fells, cutting off the low passes.

"They're not chasing," she said quietly. They stood on a ridge with the wind pulling at their cloaks, and below them the land fell away into a bowl of shadow where the horns echoed. "They're herding. Driving us up. Somebody knows these hills too, Quentin. Somebody knows exactly where a runner from Greywater would go, and they're closing every door but one."

"Which door?"

Edmund looked north, where the fells broke apart into jagged teeth of black stone — the peaks the smugglers called the Widow's Teeth, unmapped, unwarded, unloved. The only way through that wasn't a way at all unless you'd grown up learning it from a father who'd hauled contraband over it in the dark.

"Theirs," she said.

Far below, at the mouth of the eastern pass, torches were lighting in the gathering dusk — dozens of them, orderly as a rosary being counted. And at the head of the torchline, a banner she could just make out, snapping in the wind: not the temple's sunburst.

A crown.

She touched Quentin's arm. "We walk tonight," she said. "And we don't stop."
