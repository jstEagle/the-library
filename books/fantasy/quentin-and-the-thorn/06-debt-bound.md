## 6. Debt-Bound

The cave Edmund found was less a cave than the mountain's idea of one — a hollow where the rock had shrugged and left a gap behind, tucked beneath an overhang of grey stone that wept a thin line of water down its face. It smelled of cold iron and old lichen, of wet wool and pine sap, and when she crouched at its mouth she could not see her own hand in front of it.

That was the worst part. Not the cold, though the cold had teeth now that autumn was sharpening itself against the high fells. Not even the wound in the stranger's side, which had soaked through two bandages and was soaking into a third. The worst part was the dark itself, total and starless, pressing against her eyes like a thumb. For nineteen years there had always been stars. Even in the temple's deepest cellars, even on the nights the fog came up the valleys like milk poured over the world, she had known they were there. You could feel them the way you felt the floor under your feet. Now the sky was a lid, and under the lid something breathed.

"Edmund."

She flinched. The name sounded wrong in his mouth — because it *was* wrong, and he didn't know it yet, but he would, and soon, and she had been rehearsing this moment for two days without once getting past the first sentence.

"Hold still," she said, and heard how her voice pitched itself lower than it lived, the scribe's borrowed baritone she'd worn so long it fit like a callus. "I'm changing the dressing."

"You've said that four times." He shifted against the stone, breath hissing through his teeth. In the light of her shuttered lantern — a knife-slit of yellow, no wider than she dared — his face was all angles and pallor, sweat standing on his brow below that ridge of scarred flesh she still refused to look at directly. The thing fused to him. The burning weight he claimed not to remember putting there. "Four times, and each time you talk less. Scribes who stop talking are either dying or deciding something."

"I'm deciding how to say something."

"Then say it badly," Quentin said. "I'm not in a position to grade penmanship."

A laugh escaped her before she could strangle it — short, cracked, more exhale than humor. She busied her hands instead: unwinding the filthy bandage, wetting clean cloth from the waterskin, pressing it to the gash along his ribs where a hunter's blade had opened him to the bone's white doorstep. His blood was too dark in the lantern light. Everything was too dark. The whole world had been reduced to this slit of gold and the sound of water ticking off stone.

He watched her work. She could feel his attention the way you felt sun through a window.

"The toll-keeper knew my handwriting," she said.

Quentin went very still. Stillness, she'd learned, was his native language; he moved like a man who had been taught that movement cost something. "You said that already. On the road."

"I said it. I didn't explain it." She tied off the knot with fingers that wanted to shake and forbade them. "There are things about me that you should know before the hunters find us. Things that might make you wish you'd crawled into that ravine on your own after all."

"Edmund—"

"*Let me finish.*" The words came out harder than she intended, cracking the disguise right down the middle, and she heard it happen — heard the alto underneath break through the baritone like a crack through ice — and once the crack existed she saw no sense pretending otherwise. She sat back on her heels. She looked at the lantern instead of at him.

"My name is not Edmund Halloran."

The water ticked. Somewhere above them, wind worried at the scree with small patient hands.

"It never was. Halloran is my mother's name, or close enough to pass — I changed one letter, the way you change one letter in a ledger entry and hope no auditor ever looks twice. My name is Edmure. Edmure Halloran, daughter of Aldric Halloran of Hollowmere, who owned a mill, and then owed money, and then owned nothing except what the Thornfell temple allowed him to keep." Her voice had gone flat and reciting, the voice she used for contracts. It was safer that way. Contracts didn't bleed. "My father borrowed against the mill when the river shifted course three winters running. Then against our land. Then against himself. That's how temple debt works — you think you're borrowing coin, but you're borrowing yourself. Every year the interest compounded, and every year the priest showed us the ledger, and every year the number got bigger no matter how much we paid. My mother died owing them. My father will die owing them. And the debt passes down. That's doctrine. *The sins of the ledger are visited upon the children.*"

"Edmure," Quentin said quietly. Testing it.

"When I was sixteen the temple took inventory of the household." She made herself go on. Made herself look at him now, because this part deserved to be seen. "They couldn't take the mill — the mill was already theirs. They couldn't take my father; a dead debtor pays nothing. So the head priest looked at me across the table and said the family could discharge a portion of the principal by way of *service*. A contract of indenture. Seven years of labor in the temple scriptorium, wages forfeit, room and board deducted, the deduction calculated at temple rates." Her mouth twisted. "Temple rates, Quentin. Board alone ate half my wage. By their arithmetic I'll be paid free sometime after the sun burns out."

His face did something complicated. Pity, she expected, and pity was there — but underneath it, something sharper, something that looked almost like recognition, and that undid her more than pity would have.

"So I became Edmund," she said. "Cut my hair, bound my chest, dropped my voice. Nobody looks hard at a boy copying ledgers. Girls are counted; boys are furniture. I copied contracts for five years. Thousands of them. I know every clause the temple uses, every penalty schedule, every trick of the compounding tables — I *wrote* them, over and over, until my hand knew them better than my prayers." A breath. "And in the margins, where no auditor looks, I drew maps. Smuggler routes. Every path through the Thornfells that avoids a wardstone and a tollgate. Because I always meant to run. From the day I signed, I meant to run, and I told myself that knowing the ways out was as good as going."

"And now?"

"Now the stars are gone, the wards are dead, the temple wants its property back — because that's what the hunters are for, Quentin. They're not chasing a lost prince. They're chasing a runaway contract. I am *goods*." The word came out of her like a splinter coming out of flesh, relief and pain in the same motion. "Legally, formally, in ink that I copied out myself in a fair hand for the master ledger: I am the property of the Thornfell temple until such time as my family's debt is discharged, and my family's debt is eleven crowns, four marks, and nine pence, and it grows by six percent every year, and it has grown every year since before I could write. If they take me back, they'll sell my contract north to the salt mines, because runaways are docked value and salt mines take damaged goods."

She stopped. The confession stood between them in the lantern light, ugly and complete, and she waited for him to do what everyone did — to look away, or worse, to look at her the way creditors look at collateral.

* * *

What Quentin did was sit up. Slowly, wincing, one hand braced against the stone, until they were level with each other, until the difference in their heights — the difference she had worked so carefully to erase — stopped mattering.

"They can own a person," he said. "By law. With paper."

"With paper and wards and men with crossbows. Yes."

"And your father. He signed you over."

"He cried while he did it, if that helps."

"It doesn't." Something moved behind his eyes — that darkness again, the place where his memories should have been, the burned country he never spoke about. "It doesn't help at all."

"You're not saying anything else," she said, and hated how small her voice came out. "You're not saying it isn't so bad, or that we'll fix it, or any of the things people say. Say one of those things. Lie to me. Everyone lies; I'll know how to hold it."

"No." He shook his head, and the lantern light caught the ridged scar tissue at his hairline, the crown-shaped ruin she still hadn't asked about. "No more lies between us. That's the only payment I have left to offer, so hear it all. You gave me your truth; it's worth mine." He pulled a breath like a man pulling a splinter. "I don't remember who I am."

"You said — Quentin—"

"Quentin is all I have. One name, and a weight on my skull that burns every night like someone holding a poker to my brow, and dreams I can't keep hold of — faces, a hall, gold pouring somewhere it shouldn't, a voice saying words I should know. I woke in that pass with a hole in my side and a shattered wardstone beside me, and I don't know if I broke it or someone broke it over my *head*, and I don't know why anyone would bother, unless—" His jaw tightened. "Unless whatever I am is worth bothering about. Someone hunted me, Edmund. Before they ever hunted you. Someone cut me down on that road and left me for the crows, and I can't tell you who, or why, or whether they're done. I am a man with nothing in his pockets but a name that may not even be his. You've given me your real name. I can't give you mine, because it isn't *there*."

The honesty of it struck her sideways. She had braced for secrets — everyone had secrets, the temple taught you that in your first week among the ledgers — but not for a secret shaped like an absence, a hole where a life should be.

"And the crown," she said softly. "On your head. You claim there's no such thing."

"I claim I remember it being put there." His hand rose, hovered near his brow, didn't touch. "I remember fire, and metal, and laughing. I remember that it *hurts*, every night, like it's drinking something out of me. And I remember that when I touch it, some animal part of me screams to stop, so I don't touch it. That's everything I know about the most important fact of my own body. Does that satisfy the auditor?"

It was a joke, mostly. But underneath it she heard the same shame she'd been carrying since she was sixteen years old and signing her life away in a fair copperplate hand — the shame of a person whose own story had been taken out of their keeping.

"Yes," she said. "It satisfies the auditor."

* * *

The lantern oil was low. She trimmed the wick anyway, buying them another hour of gold, and dug the last of the waybread from her pack and split it unevenly — the larger piece toward him, because he was bleeding, and he pushed it back toward her, because she hadn't eaten, and the argument lasted long enough to become its own small language, and ended with both of them eating half of half.

"You should leave," he said, around the last crust. Quietly, the way you set down something heavy. "When we reach the next valley. Go south, take one of your smuggler roads, get to the coast. Your face isn't known outside these fells. Start again under a third name."

"And let them catch you."

"I'm not yours to protect. I'm a stranger who bled on you."

"You're a stranger who *talks*," she said, "which is worse." But her throat had gone tight, and she looked away, out past the overhang, into the blind black where the stars should have been. "I spent five years learning that the safest thing is to be furniture. Don't be noticed, don't be valued, don't be *owned out loud*. And then one night I find a bleeding man on my escape route and I stop, and I bind him, and I know — I *knew*, kneeling in that pass with your blood on my hands — that I'd just stopped being furniture forever. You can't unknow a choice like that." She turned back to him. "So no. I won't leave you in a valley. If we're caught, we're caught together, and I'll spend every clause I memorized making their paperwork regret it."

Something eased in his shoulders — a tension so old it must have been structural, load-bearing, the tension of a man who had been carrying himself alone in the dark for longer than two days. He looked at her for a long moment, and in the guttering lantern light his expression was stripped of everything performative, down to something raw and young.

"Edmure," he said. Using the true name like a gift handed back. "Thank you for telling me."

"Quentin," she answered. "Thank you for having nothing to tell."

Another ghost of a laugh, shared this time. And then, because the night demanded it, because honesty was a door that once opened stayed open: "The dream I had, before you woke me. There were stars in it. Hundreds. And they were going out *one at a time*, like candles pinched by fingers, and something vast was breathing them in." His voice dropped. "And I understood — the way you understand things in dreams — that the sky wasn't empty. It was *swallowed*."

Edmund went cold in a way that had nothing to do with the mountain air. Because she had copied ten thousand documents out of the Thornfell archives, and one of them — a fragment, misfiled, older than the temple's oldest liturgy — had spoken of *the hoard of heaven*, of light kept in jars beneath the earth, and she had filed it in memory as myth, as margin-matter, as nothing.

Now nothing felt like a map she hadn't read closely enough.

"Quentin," she said slowly, "the old king's summer palace is three days north of here."

His eyes found hers in the dying light, and above them, through the mouth of the cave, the starless dark pressed close — and somewhere far off down the valley, faint but unmistakable, a hunting horn sounded once, and was answered by another, closer.
