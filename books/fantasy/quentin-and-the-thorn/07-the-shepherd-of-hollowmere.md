## 7. The Shepherd of Hollowmere

The rain found them before the shepherd did.

It came down the fells in grey sheets, turning the peat to porridge and the wind to something with teeth, and Quentin learned that night what his body had become: a thing that could be cold. He had not been cold in twenty years. The crown saw to that — it kept him warm the way a hearth keeps a house warm, by burning what was inside it. But since the stars had gone out, its heat came slower, meaner, like a landlord who had stopped pretending to care whether his tenants lived.

"Here." Edmund's hand closed on his arm — her fingers were rough as rope, always surprising him — and hauled him toward a smear of light in the murk. "Hollowmere. Old Tam's stead. He doesn't ask questions if you bring him something to mend or something to eat, and we have neither, so we lie."

"You've thought this through."

"I think everything through. It's the only inheritance my family managed to keep."

The stead materialized out of the rain the way things did in the high fells: all at once, as if it had been standing behind a curtain. A low stone house with a sod roof, a byre breathing warmth and dung-smell, sheep pressed together in the dark like grey thoughts. And in the doorway, a shape that resolved into an old man holding a lantern high, squinting against the weather at two figures stumbling out of the starless dark.

"Well," the old man said. "The mountain's finally sent me something interesting."

* * *

His name was Tam, though everyone called him the Shepherd of Hollowmere because there had never been anyone else in Hollowmere to be confused with. He fed them barley broth with a heel of hard cheese drowned in it, and he asked no questions while they ate, which Quentin understood was its own kind of courtesy. The fire was peat-fire, slow and sweet, and the smoke hung in the rafters like something tame.

Quentin sat close to the flames and felt the crown drink, and hated it. He could feel the pull now more clearly than ever — a fine cold thread running up out of his skull into the ruined sky, as if somewhere above the clouds something was sipping at him through a straw. Each night it took more. His dreams were going first; then, he suspected, would come other things. Names. The color of his mother's dress. Whether he had ever been happy, or only told stories about it afterward.

Edmund sat across from him, mending the seam of her coat with needle and waxed thread she produced from some bottomless pocket, and watched the old shepherd over the fire with the particular stillness of someone cataloguing exits.

"So," Tam said at last. He was a broad ruin of a man, hands like tree roots, face seamed deep enough to plant crops in. "Temple folk, are you? I smell the ink on you, lad. Scribes always smell of ink. It gets into the blood."

"I'm — yes," Quentin said, before Edmund could arrange a better lie. "A scribe."

"Hm." Tam turned his lantern wick down with a thick fingernail. "And your friend here is what? Another scribe?"

"My cousin," Edmund said smoothly. "Also a scribe. We're a very literate family."

The old man laughed, a sound like stones rolling downhill, and Quentin felt the lie settle around them both, comfortable and thin as the roof over their heads. He should have let Edmund do the talking. She was better at it. She lied the way other people breathed, without ceremony, whereas every falsehood he spoke felt like carrying water in his hands — he could feel it leaking out of him even as he held on.

But then Tam looked at him across the fire, and the laughter went out of his face the way a candle goes out — suddenly, completely — and Quentin knew, with the sick certainty of a man watching an arrow already in flight, that the lie had failed before he'd ever spoken it.

* * *

"Turn your head," Tam said.

"What?"

"Towards the light. Just so." The shepherd rose, joints cracking like green wood, and lifted his lantern. Its glow crossed Quentin's face — left cheek, brow, jaw — and the old man's breath caught audibly. "By the Mother's mercy," he whispered. "I thought I was seeing a ghost. I *knew* I was seeing a ghost, but ghosts don't eat broth."

"I don't know what you—"

"Don't." Tam set the lantern down with exaggerated care, the way a man sets down something that might go off. "Don't insult us both, boy. I've carried your face in my pocket for twenty years." He dug into the layers of his clothing — wool, oilskin, more wool — and brought out a small leather purse, worn soft as skin, and shook into his palm a single coin.

He held it out. Quentin took it because refusing would have taken longer than taking.

Silver, old-struck, the edges rubbed to a soft blur by years of hands. On one side, the crowned head of a young man — sharp-boned, serious, seventeen perhaps, with the particular unhappiness of someone who has just learned what he is going to be for the rest of his life. On the other, the words QUENTIN, FIRST OF HIS NAME, BY THE GRACE OF THE STARS.

The coin trembled in Quentin's fingers. Not because his hand shook. Because his whole body did.

"That's not me," he said quietly.

"No," Tam agreed. "That's you twenty years ago, before the world went wrong. I was at your father's last muster, did you know that? Down in the vale, before the Usurper burned it. Every man who fought got paid in new-minted silver with the prince's face on it. Said it was good luck. Said the boy would bring back the bright years." He lowered himself onto his stool with a groan. "Most of those coins bought coffins in the end. I kept mine. Sentimental old fool, keeping a dead man's face in his pocket."

"I'm not—"

"The dead prince," Tam said, to the fire, to the sheep, to the rain hammering the sod roof. "Lost at the coronation. Some say murdered. Some say fled. Some say the Usurper turned him into a dog and hunts him still on moonless nights, for sport." He glanced up, and his eyes in the lantern-light were pale and steady and terribly kind. "None of them say he turns up on my doorstep in a storm, smelling of ink and eating my cheese. Life's funnier than any of the songs."

Quentin became aware of Edmund beside him. She had gone very still — the needle stopped mid-stitch, the thread hanging — and when he forced himself to look at her, he found her watching him with an expression he couldn't read. Not horror. Not quite. Something adjacent to horror. Something standing on horror's doorstep deciding whether to knock.

This was the moment. He had known it would come eventually; he had simply hoped, the way a drowning man hopes the shore is closer than it looks. She would stand, or she would stay seated, and either way she would leave — tonight, tomorrow, at the crossroads past Hollowmere — because who stayed? Who bound themselves to a hunted name, a walking treason, a boy whose face was on coins that bought coffins?

"Say nothing," Edmund said to Tam. Her voice was flat and cold as slate.

"I've kept secrets in these hills for sixty years, girl. Kings' riders came through twice looking for rebels, and twice I showed them empty valleys and my own stupidity. Your secret's safe as any."

"Not mine to keep quiet." She folded the needle away. "His."

* * *

Later — much later, when the broth pot had been scraped and the sheep had settled into their low complaining sleep — Quentin sat on the edge of Tam's bed with the coin in his fist, digging its rubbed-silver edges into his palm, and told the truth for the first time in twenty years.

He told it badly. That was the thing no one warned you about truth: lies came rehearsed, polished through repetition, but the truth came out raw and stumbling, with splinters in it.

"My brother's name was Aldous." The fire had burned low; Tam had left them alone in the main room, claiming his knees required his bed, though Quentin suspected the old man's ears were better than his knees. Edmund sat across from him, arms wrapped around her own elbows, listening. "Is Aldous. He's not dead. I have to start there, because everyone assumes usurpers kill their predecessors' kin, and Aldous made sure everyone assumed it. It was better for him if I was a ghost. Ghosts can't come back, but they also can't be *found*, and finding me was the one thing he could never risk."

"Why?" Edmund asked. Not gently. She didn't do gentle; she did precise, and he had learned that her precision was its own kind of mercy.

"Because of this." He touched his brow, where beneath the dirt and the healing cut the gold lay fused to bone. He had shown her the wound once, briefly, in the pass — enough to stop her questions, not enough to tell her anything true. Now he peeled back the ragged bandage and let the firelight find it.

She leaned forward. Even expecting it, she drew a sharp breath.

The crown was not a crown. That was the horror of it, the thing he had never been able to say aloud: it had been a circlet once, thin gold, the coronation band his father had worn and his grandfather before him. But when Aldous's sorcerers had seized him at the altar — mid-oath, the words of kingship half-spoken in his mouth — they hadn't removed it. They had *finished* it. Molten gold poured on living bone, shaped by hands that knew exactly what they were doing, until crown and skull were one piece, one wound, one instrument.

"It drinks," he said. "Every night. I don't know where it sends what it takes. I used to dream normally — badly, like anyone — and then the dreams started getting... eaten. Now most nights there's just the pulling, and something on the far end of the pull that feels—" He stopped. Swallowed. Started again, quieter. "*Hungry*. And satisfied. Like something at a trough."

"And the stars," Edmund said slowly. "The night they went out."

"I don't know. I swear to you I don't know. But I know it was the same night the wards fell, and I know whatever wears this crown — whatever is on the other end of the straw — has been drinking from me harder since. As if it's..." He laughed, and the laugh came out wrong. "As if it's gorging itself before a journey. Or hiding what it ate."

Silence. Rain on the roof. A sheep coughing in the byre.

"Tam said something," Edmund said at last, "when you were sleeping yesterday. About the Usurper's court."

"He talks too much for a man who lives alone with sheep."

"He said the court never sleeps. That the King's shadow moves wrong — that men who've seen him at court swear his shadow lags, or leads, or moves when he stands still. That servants are rotated every few weeks because the ones who stay too long come out with white streaks in their hair and no appetite and a look like—" She paused, choosing words carefully, and he loved her a little for how carefully she chose them, even now, even knowing what he was. "Like people who have seen the inside of a thing they shouldn't have."

"Aldous was always pale," Quentin said. "Even as a boy. Our mother joked that he was born in winter and winter never quite let go of him." He looked into the fire. "I used to think he resented me for the crown. The heir's crown, the true one. But it wasn't resentment. It was — inventory. He looked at me the way a man looks at a horse he intends to buy. And now I understand why. Whatever he serves — whatever he *became* — it wanted the crown on a living head. It wanted a vessel that would last. And mine lasted twenty years, and now it's wearing thin, and Aldous is coming to collect."

"Coming to collect," Edmund repeated.

"He'll know I'm alive. The moment the wards fell, the moment I crossed into Thornfell — he'll have felt it through the gold, the way you feel a hook in your flesh. He knows I'm coming home." Quentin closed his fist around the old coin until the edges bit. "I'm not leading you anywhere, Edmund. I want you to understand that. I'm dragging you. There's a difference, and the difference is everything, and I am so sorry—"

"Stop."

He stopped.

"You apologize like a king," she said, "and I've met enough lords' sons in temple records to know what that's worth. So here's what I actually need to know, and none of your apologies answer it." Her eyes were very steady in the dying firelight. "Where are we going? You didn't drag me onto the smuggler's road by accident. You've been steering us east since Greywater, toward the old summer country, and I let you because I was curious and because the roads east are the roads away from the temple's hunters. But now I want it said plainly. What's east?"

Quentin opened his mouth. Closed it. The coin bit into his palm, and he realized he had known this moment was coming too — the moment he would have to say the other true thing, the larger one, the one that made his being a fugitive seem simple by comparison.

"There's a vault," he said. "Under the ruins of the summer palace. My father told me about it when I was twelve, swore me to secrecy, told me a riddle instead of a map — said maps could be stolen, but a riddle spoken into a son's memory could not. I thought it was a game. I thought—" His voice cracked, and he hated it. "I thought a lot of things. The vault holds the old treasury. And more than the treasury. Things my father's fathers sealed away. Things the first kings thought were too dangerous, or too precious, or both."

"Starlight," Edmund said softly.

He stared at her.

"I've copied a thousand debt contracts, Quentin. Temple contracts. And the oldest of them — the founding charters, the ones they keep chained shut — they don't say *by the grace of the gods*. They say *by the grace of the stars*, and there are clauses in the margins, payment clauses, about light owed and light returned." Her jaw tightened. "I didn't understand what I was reading. I thought it was poetry. Scribes always think the strange parts are poetry." She looked at him across the ashes of the fire. "You're going to that vault. And whatever's in it, it matters enough that the thing wearing your brother is already hunting you for it."

Outside, the rain was slackening. Through the one small window, the sky remained black and starless as a closed eye, and Quentin felt the crown give its slow nocturnal tug, patient as tide.

"Yes," he said.

* * *

He expected her to leave at dawn. He had prepared for it, rehearsed the conversation, composed and recomposed the words — *go south, take the toll road, I'll draw them off* — the way he had once rehearsed speeches he'd never give.

Instead she woke before him, repacked their gear with ruthless economy, and stood over him in the grey pre-dawn with her coat already patched, her boots already laced, her face already pointed east.

"Get up," she said. "Tam's giving us bread and pointing us at the drover's track. It adds two days but skips the ford at Hollowmere, and fords are where hunters wait." She tossed him his pack. "Also he wants you to have this."

She put the coin in his hand — his own face, twenty years younger, minted in hope and spent in coffins.

"He said you'd need reminding," Edmund said. "Of who you were before. He said—" and here her voice did something complicated, softened and sharpened at once, "he said the boy on the coin looked like someone who hadn't yet learned to flinch. He thinks you can get that back. I think he's a sentimental old fool." She turned toward the door, then stopped, her back to him, the dawn-grey light catching the edge of her jaw. "I think he's right."

Quentin stood there with the coin biting his palm and something enormous lodged in his chest, unnameable, terrifying, warmer than anything the crown had ever given him.

"Edmund," he said. "Why? You know what I am now. What following me costs. Every road east is a road deeper into his reach, and you owe me nothing — less than nothing, I've lied to you since the pass—"

She looked back at him then, fully, and her eyes were the color of wet slate and utterly without pity, which was, he was learning, the same thing as trust.

"Because I've spent my whole life being owned," she said. "By debts. By contracts. By other people's signatures on other people's paper. And you're the first thing I've ever chosen." She pushed open the door onto the cold and the rain-washed dark. "Now get up. We've a long way to go, and I intend to arrive before the thing that's hunting you does."

Far off to the east, beyond the fells, beyond the ruined capital, thunder rolled across a sky that held no stars — and did not sound entirely like thunder.

Tam stood by the byre with the bread, and as they passed he pressed it into Quentin's hands along with something else, quick and hidden, pressed palm to palm: a second coin, identical to the first.

"For luck," the old shepherd murmured. "And lad — if you're going where I think you're going — mind the Widow's Teeth. And mind, too, that the King's riders passed through the valley three days ago. Heading east. Like they knew the way before you did."
