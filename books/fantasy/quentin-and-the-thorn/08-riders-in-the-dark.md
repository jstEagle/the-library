## 8. Riders in the Dark

Edmund woke to a sound she had known all her life and never once heard from this side of it.

Hooves. Many of them, moving at a walk, spread out across the lower fell like fingers feeling their way along a table's edge. She had copied the temple's hunting orders often enough — *pursuit by night, lanterns hooded, riders spaced to sweep* — and she had always imagined, in the abstract way one imagines other people's misfortunes, that being hunted would feel like panic. It did not. It felt like arithmetic. She lay in the cold bracken above the shepherd's hut and counted hoofbeats, and the answer was six, maybe seven, and the answer was not enough distance between them and dawn.

Beside her, Quentin was already awake. She could tell by his breathing — the shallow, held quality of it, the way he had gone still as stone. In the weeks since she'd pulled him bleeding off the smuggler's road he had learned her language of silence: when she froze, he froze; when she breathed slow, he did too. Tonight there was nothing to teach. His hand found hers in the dark and gripped it, and she felt the tremor in him that was not fear for himself but something worse, something that had no clean name.

*The shepherd,* she thought. Old Bram of Hollowmere, who had taken them in with a shrug as if harboring fugitives were a chore like mending fence, who had looked at Quentin's face on a coin minted before Edmund was born and said only, *Well. You'll want soup, then.* Bram, whose snoring she could hear even now through the turf roof below them, slow and untroubled, a man sleeping the sleep of someone who believed the dark held nothing new.

The hoofbeats stopped.

Not all at once. They stopped the way rain stops, here and then there, until the whole fellslope was quiet and the quiet itself became the loudest thing Edmund had ever heard. Then a voice carried up through the starless air, flat and official, the voice of a man reading from a document rather than speaking to human beings:

"In the name of the Temple of Thornfell, sovereign creditor of all debts outstanding. Surrender the scribe Halloran. This is a matter of contract, not of conscience."

*Scribe Halloran.* Not the prince. Not the pretender. Her.

Quentin's grip tightened. In the blackness she could not see his face but she knew what it would be doing — the jaw setting, the brow furrowing under the bandage where the crown sat fused to his skull like a second bone, drinking from him even now. He would be about to do something noble and stupid. She put her mouth close to his ear.

"Not yet," she breathed. "They want me alive. Contracts don't pay out on corpses. That gives us until they realize I'm not worth taking quietly."

"And Bram?"

She had no answer for that. The arithmetic failed her for the first time, and the failure sat in her chest like a swallowed coal.

Below, torchlight bloomed — hooded lanterns unshuttered all at once, seven points of flame arranged in a crescent around the little hut, and against that light Edmund saw the shepherd's door open and old Bram step out into the night with his staff in his hand and no surprise on him anywhere, the way a mountain has no surprise when the weather turns.

"Well," he said, loud enough to reach her on the slope. "You'll be wanting the boy and girl, then. They're gone over the ridge since midnight. But you're welcome to search, and I'll charge you for the trouble, same as anyone."

It was a lie so generous it hurt. They had been asleep in his loft an hour ago. He was spending whatever came next buying them minutes, and he was doing it in the tone of a man discussing the price of wool.

The leader — a broad shape on a grey horse, torch-gilded at the edges — said something Edmund couldn't catch. One of the riders dismounted. There was a sound of a door being kicked, a sound of things being thrown about inside the hut, and then a sound that made Edmund's stomach drop through the mountainside: a cry, brief, and the crack of a body hitting frozen ground.

"Come away." Quentin's whisper was ragged. "Edmund. Come *away*. If we go down there they take all three of us and Bram's sacrifice is nothing—"

He was right. She knew he was right with the whole cold clarity of her mind, and her body simply refused the knowledge, every muscle pulling downslope toward the torchlight like water finding its level. She had spent her whole life learning the difference between what was owed and what was chosen, between the ledger's claims and the heart's, and here in the dark the two columns finally balanced against each other at exactly zero, and she could not move either direction.

Then the rider dragged Bram upright by his collar, and the leader's voice came again, and this time she caught every word:

"The woman is temple property. Unreturned property accrues interest. You are harboring temple property. Under the terms of the Bonded Debt Act—" a pause, the rustle of paper, the obscene domesticity of it, "—harboring constitutes interference with collateral. Interference is punishable by forfeiture."

"Forfeiture of what?" Bram asked. And then, because he was old and proud and had never learned to leave a question alone: "I own four sheep and a roof."

"Of yourself."

The word went through the night like a nail through cloth. Edmund heard Quentin inhale sharply beside her; he understood contracts now, had spent enough nights listening to her explain the machinery of debt that owned her, and he understood what *forfeiture of yourself* meant better perhaps than she did — that somewhere in the temple's ledgers, Bram of Hollowmere had just ceased to be a man and become an entry, transferable, saleable, collectible.

They took him. That was the worst of it, later — how orderly it was. No violence beyond the necessary. They bound his hands, hauled him across a horse like a rolled carpet, and turned back down the valley at a walk, unhurried, because they were not fleeing anything. They had gotten what the paperwork required. Six riders and their burden dwindled into the dark, and the last torch shrank to an ember, and the ember to nothing, and the fells were black again, blacker than before, as if the night itself had closed over the wound.

Bram had told them, three days ago, laughing over his soup: *"If the temple comes, run east along the watershed. Don't be heroes. Heroes die and fools get captured, and neither makes a good story for the grandchildren."*

Edmund ran east along the watershed. She did it with tears freezing on her face and Quentin's hand locked around her wrist, hauling her onward when her legs wanted to turn back, and she hated him a little for it, and loved him entirely, and knew both those things were the same thing wearing different clothes.

* * *

They walked through what remained of the night because stopping meant thinking and thinking meant going back.

The land under the vanished stars had grown strange in the weeks since the sky emptied. Without starlight to season it, darkness was no longer merely the absence of seeing; it had texture, weight, a pressure against the skin like wading upstream. Edmund had grown up in these mountains and had never once feared the dark — a scribe's daughter learns early that night is just day with the ink dried — but this dark was *occupied*. It felt watched. Sometimes, on the high shoulders of the fells, she caught herself hearing breathing that was not theirs, vast and slow, and told herself it was wind in the caves. Quentin never told himself anything. He would go rigid, hand pressed to the bandage on his brow, and say, "It knows where I am. It always knows where I am," in a voice like a man describing his own funeral.

Dawn came gray and grudging, a light without warmth, the sun a pale coin behind wool. They were well into the high ground by then, the country of scree and standing stones where the mountain had shrugged its skin off in slides centuries ago and never bothered gathering it up again. A million loose rocks, grey on grey, clinking faintly underfoot like a field of broken teeth. Good ground for hiding. Bad ground for running.

"We should keep moving," Quentin said. His voice had thinned overnight. She glanced at him and saw what she had been pretending not to see for days: the hollowing. His cheekbones stood out like architecture. The skin around his eyes had gone the color of tallow, and when the pale light touched the bandage at his hairline she could see, seeping through the linen, not blood but a faint golden shimmer, as if the crown beneath were sweating light it had stolen from somewhere.

"You need to rest."

"I need—" He stopped. Swayed. Put a hand against a boulder taller than himself and hung there, breathing. "Edmund, when we stop, it feeds harder. I don't know why. Distance from the capital, maybe. Or proximity. I can't make the pattern lie flat. But when we move, when my mind is on the walking, it's — quieter. Like it has to work through me to get at anything, and I'm giving it nothing to work with." He laughed, and the laugh was awful, bright and cracked. "Twenty years I've been carrying it and I still talk about it like a bad tenant."

"So we don't stop."

"So we don't stop."

But the mountain decided otherwise. The watershed path they'd followed narrowed where a slide had cut it away, and beyond that point the route she knew — the goat-track her father's maps traced in faded brown ink, *the widow's stair, passable, single file, do not attempt in wind* — climbed through a jumble of fallen stone where speed was impossible. Single file. Careful footing. An hour, perhaps more, exposed on the open shoulder of the ridge with nothing to screen them from the valley below.

Edmund paused at the mouth of it and looked back the way they'd come, a habit drilled into her by every smuggler whose routes she'd memorized: *always know your back trail, girl; the road behind you is the road they'll use.*

The riders were already there.

Seven of them now — the six from Hollowmere plus one more, and Bram's horse was empty, which meant the shepherd had been handed off somewhere in the night to some other arm of the temple's long bureaucracy, filed away in some cellar of souls. The riders had abandoned the valley floor and were coming up the watershed directly, walking their horses through the scree with the patience of men being paid by the result. At their head rode the broad grey shape from the hut, and even at half a mile Edmund could see the white rectangle of the warrant in his hand, and the absurd, obscene care with which he consulted it, as though the document might tell him which rocks his prey were hiding behind.

"There," Quentin said.

"I see them."

"Down. Between the stones — if we can get below their line of sight—"

"They're not looking for our line of sight, Quentin. They're following the contract." She was thinking fast now, the old cold part of her mind opening like a drawer. "They know where we are. They've known since Hollowmere. They let us walk all night because tired people make mistakes, and now they're going to herd us onto the widow's stair where we can't run sideways, and they'll take us at the top or the bottom, whichever's easier." She swallowed. "That's how the temple hunts. I used to copy the manuals."

Something moved through Quentin's face then that she had never seen on him before. Not fear — he had worn fear like a garment for weeks, and she knew its every fold. This was underneath the fear, older and quieter. When he spoke, his voice had dropped into a register she hadn't heard, low and level, the voice of someone remembering a duty.

"Then we don't give them a herd. We give them a fight on ground where numbers mean nothing." He was already moving, pulling her off the track, up and leftward through the boulder-field toward a place where three great slabs of fallen rock leaned together and made a room — a stone tent, floored with gravel, open on two sides but walled enough. "Scree funnels them. Horses can't follow past the slide. They come at us two abreast at most, in the dark among loose stone, and we—" He caught himself. Looked at her. Whatever he saw in her face made him gentler. "We survive. That's all. We survive, and we grieve Bram after, and we keep the promise we made him by living."

"He told us not to be heroes."

"He told us heroes die." Quentin drew the short knife she'd stolen from the tollhouse weeks ago from his belt and set it down between them on the gravel. "Fools get captured. So we'll be neither. We'll be something the manual doesn't have a page for."

She picked up the knife. It was lighter than she expected. Everything about violence was lighter than she expected, she would think later; it was the aftermath that weighed.

They waited.

Waiting, she discovered, was its own country. The gray light thickened by degrees. Wind moved through the scree and made it sing — a thin, glassy note, a thousand stones worrying each other, the mountain muttering in its sleep. Quentin crouched beside her at the gap where the slabs met, and his trembling had stopped, and that frightened her more than the riders did. She pressed her hand flat against his back, between the shoulder blades, and felt his heartbeat: slow. Too slow. Steady as a drum keeping time for soldiers.

"Quentin. Your heart."

"It does that," he murmured. "When it's hungry. Mine or the crown's — I honestly can't tell anymore whose appetite it is." He turned his head, and in the gray half-light his eyes found hers, and for a moment the prince and the fugitive and the vessel all fell away and it was just the man she had crossed half a kingdom with, looking at her as if she were the last lit window in a burned city. "Whatever happens in the next hour — whatever I do — it isn't me. Do you understand? Remember the man who laughed on the goat-track. Remember the soup at Bram's table. If you see something else wearing my hands, don't believe it."

"Quentin—"

"Horses," he said.

He was right. She heard them a moment later: the deliberate crunch of shod hooves on loose stone, closer now, and voices — clipped, procedural, the temple's grammar of command. *Two on foot, likely. Check the formations. Warrant says alive, both of them, the scribe especially — damages reduce recovery value.*

*Damages reduce recovery value.* Her own worth, itemized. Edmund found that somewhere beneath her terror, fury was kindling — a small, clean flame, the first pure thing she'd felt in days. They had made her a figure in a ledger. They had taken an old man who fed strangers and turned him into a line entry. And now they were coming up the mountain to collect, patient as interest.

The first rider appeared at the edge of the slide.

He had dismounted, as Quentin predicted — the horses could go no farther — and he came on foot with a hooked pole, the instrument they used to take bond-debtors without marking them, and he was younger than she expected, and afraid, and trying hard not to show it. Behind him, wedged in the funnel of stone, came the second, and behind the second the gray-cloaked leader's voice carried down the rocks: *"Alive. Both alive. The contract requires—"*

Quentin moved.

She had seen him fight once before, briefly, badly, on the night the hunters found them at the river bend — a desperate scrabble, all flailing retreat. This was not that. This was a man stepping forward into the narrowest point of the gap with the economy of someone who had been trained in royal yards before he was old enough to grow a beard, and underneath the training something else, something that made her breath catch: he fought the way she imagined a man fights when he stands between a wolf and the thing the wolf wants. Every motion was protective. He gave no ground backward, only sideways, turning the attackers' force into the scree, letting the treacherous stone do half the work. The hooked pole caught on a boulder and the young rider stumbled, and Quentin struck the pole from his grip and drove him back with the flat of the knife, not the edge — *alive*, even now, honoring the constraint they'd placed on themselves — and shouted over his shoulder:

"Edmund! The gap — watch the gap!"

She almost missed it. Almost lost the moment in watching him, in the terrible fascination of seeing gentle Quentin become this other thing. But the third hunter had circled wide, climbing the leaning slab above their shelter, and now he came over the top of the wall of stone with a weighted net and a short blade, dropping into the space behind Quentin where there was no one to guard it except her.

Everything after that happened in pieces, and the pieces never quite fit together again into a whole she could carry comfortably.

She remembered the knife in her hand. She remembered the hunter's boots hitting the gravel, the net unfolding with a soft whump like a sail catching wind. She remembered that he was aiming it at Quentin's back, and that Quentin was still facing the gap, holding two men at bay, and that if the net took him the whole story ended here in this stone room on a nameless ridge, and Bram's sacrifice would have bought nothing, and the stars would stay eaten, and the thing wearing the crown would wear it forever.

She remembered stepping forward. She remembered the hunter turning — surprised, genuinely surprised, as if he'd forgotten the scribe was a person rather than inventory — and she remembered that he raised his short blade, not in rage but in procedure, because the warrant permitted reasonable force against resisting property.

And she remembered the knife going in.

Under the collarbone. Angled down, the way a butcher angles, the way her father had once — drunk, expansive, gesturing at the temple guards outside their window — said they'd angle it if ever they came for his children. She had not known she knew that. Her body had known. Some inheritance deeper than ledgers had lived in her arm all along, waiting.

The hunter looked at her with an expression that was not hatred. That was the unforgivable part. He looked *confused* — a workingman confused, a man doing a job who had stepped wrong, the way you'd look at a staircase that wasn't where you remembered. The net slid from his fingers. He sat down among the gravel, slowly, the way tired men sit, and pressed his hand to his collarbone, and looked at the blood there with what might have been mild reproach, and then he lay back against the stone and stopped being a hunter and became a dead man whom Edmund Halloran had killed.

She froze.

Later, Quentin would tell her that the fight at the gap lasted another forty heartbeats — that the remaining hunters, finding their man down and their quarry turned, broke off and withdrew to the horses rather than press a fight that had stopped being a collection and become a battle. She would have to take his word for all of it. From inside, there was no fight. There was only the gravel under her knees and the spreading dark on the dead man's shirt and the sound of her own breathing, huge and mechanical, a bellows working without her permission.

*I killed him.*

*I killed a man for the temple's arithmetic.*

*He was somebody's son. Somebody taught him to angle a blade or he taught himself, and somewhere a woman will wait for him and he will not come home, and the ledger will write: damages incurred during recovery of collateral, one (1) hunter, deceased, cost borne by the estate—*

"Edmund."

Hands on her shoulders. Quentin, blood-spattered, breathing hard, kneeling in front of her. His eyes were wet. Whether from grief or relief or the crown's golden hunger she couldn't tell, and perhaps neither could he.

"Edmund, look at me. We have to go. More will come. Bram—" his voice broke on the name and he mastered it, "—Bram bought us this hour. We don't waste it sitting in it."

"I killed him." Her voice came out flat, foreign. "Quentin, I — I didn't decide. My hand just — the temple made me into this. They wrote me into a corner until the only exit was through a man's chest, and I went through it, and I don't know how to be the person who did that."

He took her face in both hands. His palms smelled of blood and pine and the strange warm gold scent that seeped from beneath his bandage, and his forehead came to rest against hers, crown to brow, and when he spoke his voice shook.

"Then don't be. Be the person who did it *for* something. There's a difference. It's the whole difference. Men kill for the ledger all day long and sleep soundly. You killed to keep a promise to a shepherd and to keep me alive, and it will cost you, and the costing is how I know you're still — " his throat worked, "— still you. Still mine. Still the woman who wouldn't leave a stranger bleeding on the smuggler's road."

Above them, far off, a horn sounded. Once, twice — the temple's recall, or its summons. The surviving riders were falling back down the watershed, dragging their horses, and whether they fled or regrouped she could not tell and could not bear to learn.

They went up. Away from the dead man, away from the funnel of stone, onto the spine of the ridge where the wind tore at them and the widow's stair climbed into cloud. Edmund climbed with the knife still in her hand because she could not make her fingers open, and Quentin climbed behind her with one hand at her back, steady as a wall at her back, and neither of them spoke, because everything that needed saying had gone too large for mouths.

At the top of the first pitch she stopped. Below, tiny in the gray distance, she could see the hunters gathered at the foot of the slide — and among them, on the lead rider's saddlebow, a bundle that had not been there before. Cloth-wrapped. Man-sized. Something glinted at the temple camp that should not have been there, and her heart, already broken, cracked further.

Because she recognized the bundle. Recognized the shape of it, and the glint — the brass buckle of a shepherd's belt, the one Bram had worn every day of the twenty years he'd tended Hollowmere.

They hadn't handed him off in the night after all. They had brought him back up the mountain with them — a bargaining piece, a lever, a hostage folded into the rear of the column like a clause in fine print. And now, as the wind shifted and carried fragments of shouted negotiation up the rocks, she heard the leader's voice ring out across the scree, pitched deliberately to carry, cruelly calm:

"Scribe Halloran! The temple offers revised terms! The old man forfeits at sundown unless claimed — *claimed in person!*"

Quentin went rigid beside her. On the horizon behind the riders, far to the south, the clouds over the ruined capital had begun — very faintly, very wrongly — to glow gold.

And Edmund understood, with the sick certainty of a woman who has memorized every clause of every contract ever written against her, that the temple had never been hunting Quentin at all.

They had been herding her. All along. Every road closed, every trap sprung, every kindness punished — steering her, step by step, toward whatever waited in that golden glow, using everyone she loved as the pen to write her there.

Sundown was five hours away.
