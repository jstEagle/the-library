## 9. The Map Only She Knows

The Widow's Teeth did not appear on any map Edmund had ever copied, and she had copied maps the way other people said prayers — nightly, compulsively, with her whole heart in it.

They appeared on hers.

She unrolled the scrap of oiled leather at dawn, weighted at the corners with stones, while Quentin stood behind her breathing carefully through his ribs. The wound along his side had closed to a dark seam; the crown was another matter. Every morning she checked the gold fused into his brow the way a farrier checks a hoof, and every morning it seemed to sit a little deeper, a little more like something growing rather than something worn. This morning there was a fine crackle of light beneath the metal when he turned toward the sun, faint as frost on a windowpane.

"Don't," he said, when her fingers rose toward it.

"I wasn't going to touch."

"You were going to look at me the way you look at ledgers." He almost smiled. "Like you're trying to find the error in the sum."

Edmund folded the leather shut. "There is no error. There's only what I know." She pointed east, where the peaks of the Widow's Teeth stood up white against the grey like broken teeth indeed — which was how the range had gotten its name, according to her father, who had gotten it from a smuggler, who had probably invented it over bad wine. "Every road into the old capital goes through the valley roads. The valley roads have tolls, garrisons, hunters, and priests. All of them are looking for us. But above the valleys—" she traced a line that climbed and climbed and never came down until it reached the high saddle beyond the last peak— "there is a goat-track my mother used when she ran wool past the temple tithe-collectors. It isn't on any chart because no surveyor has ever been drunk enough or desperate enough to walk it. It's narrow, it's cold, and one stretch of it crosses a face of scree that will kill us if we sneeze. But it goes over, not through. No gates. No eyes."

"And your mother? Where is she now?"

"Gone." Edmund said it evenly, because she had practiced saying it evenly for eleven years. "The temple holds her contract too, if you want the truth of it. That's why I know these paths. I learned them before I learned my letters."

Quentin looked at the line she'd drawn for a long moment. Above the cloud deck that lay over the valleys like a lid, the sky was hard blue and clean — the first clean sky they'd seen since the stars went out, though the blue held nothing in it. No birds. No sun-warmth worth the name. Just emptiness where light should be.

"Then we climb," he said.

* * *

By midmorning they were above everything.

It happened so gradually that neither of them marked the moment — the cloud deck dropping away below their boots like a floor falling out of the world, the air thinning to something bright and glassy, the peaks around them standing shoulder to shoulder in the silence of things that have been waiting since before names. Edmund walked in front because the track was hers, and the track demanded attention: a hand's width of ledge here, a scramble over frost-shattered stone there, a stretch where the mountain simply fell away on both sides and the wind came up the face with a sound like a long-held note.

"Your mother ran wool this way?" Quentin called from behind her. His voice sounded strange up here — small, and then enormous, flung off the rock walls.

"She ran anything the temple taxed and anyone the temple wanted. Debtors mostly. Families who'd signed contracts and changed their minds about being property." Edmund stepped across a gap where the ledge had crumbled into nothing. "She charged them half what the smugglers asked and twice what she should have, and fed them at every stop, and swore at them in three languages. She said the mountains were the only honest thing in Thornfell. They'll kill you, but they won't pretend otherwise."

"And you?"

"What about me?"

"Did you love it? Up here?"

Edmund stopped.

She hadn't expected the question to land anywhere, and it landed everywhere. Below them the clouds rolled like a white sea, and above them the empty sky went on forever, and between those two vast absences there was a ledge the width of a coffin and a woman who had spent her whole life learning to move along edges without ever belonging to either side of one.

"Yes," she said. It came out rougher than she meant. "I loved it. It was the only place where nobody could read me. Down there I'm a boy scribe, or a girl debtor, or a fugitive, depending on who's squinting. Up here I'm just the person who knows the way." She started walking again, faster, because her throat was doing something inconvenient. "Come on. The col ahead is sheltered. We can rest while the light lasts."

The col, when they reached it, was a shallow bowl of stone cupped between two fangs of the Teeth, floored with old snow gone to granular ice and walled from the wind. The sun — dim, wrong-colored, but still the sun — fell straight into it like water into a cup. And something about the altitude, or the exhaustion, or the sheer absurdity of being alive this high above a world that wanted them both dead, cracked them open.

It began with the snow. Quentin picked up a handful of the granular ice, weighed it in his palm like a man judging coin, and threw it at her head.

Edmund ducked — twenty years of brothers in the temple yard had made her quick — and it burst against the rock behind her, and she turned around with what she knew was a dangerous expression, and he grinned at her. Actually grinned. She had seen him grimace, flinch, go white with the crown's feeding, press his forehead against her shoulder in the worst hours of the night. She had not once seen him grin, and it changed his whole face, took ten years off it and put twenty years of someone else's life back into it, some boy who had existed before coronations and molten gold.

"That," she said, "was a mistake."

The fight lasted perhaps four minutes and left them both breathless and laughing, real laughing, the kind that hurts the ribs and comes out in snorts and cannot be stopped by dignity. Edmund got snow down his collar. Quentin got an entire armful dumped over his head while he was mid-sentence about honor, which served him right. At some point they ended up sitting against the same boulder, shoulders touching, watching their ragged breath hang in the still air, and the laughing faded into something quieter that was not sad at all.

"There," Quentin said softly.

"Where?"

"Look. Down there — the valley, through the break in the cloud." He pointed. Far below, a seam had opened in the cloud deck, and through it glinted a thread of river and, impossibly small, a village with smoke rising from its chimneys. Ordinary smoke. Someone down there was baking bread, or burning weeds, or keeping a child warm, and had no idea the sky above them was a wound.

"It's beautiful," Edmund said.

"It is." He was quiet a moment. Then: "I used to think I'd give anything to see it from up here. When I was — before. In the palace, there were windows, and I'd look at the mountains and think: somewhere up there a person can stand and no one owns him. Not the crown, not the court, not the god the temple invented to keep us all kneeling. Just him and the rock." He turned his head and looked at her, and the light under the gold in his brow flickered, and for once she didn't look away from it. "I didn't know it would take losing everything to get here. But I'm glad you're the one showing me."

Edmund's heart did something complicated and painful and warm.

"You'd have hated the scree crossing," she managed. "You scream like a temple choirboy."

"I do not scream."

"You screamed at the marmot yesterday."

"That marmot came out of nowhere. It looked like a small angry god."

And they were laughing again, helplessly, leaning into each other on the frozen col at the top of the world, two liars with nothing left to hide from each other and nowhere left to run except forward. Edmund thought: *this*. Whatever this was, whatever it was becoming between them — the way his shoulder fit against hers, the way he'd begun finishing her sentences and she his, the way she caught herself memorizing not routes anymore but the sound of his voice — she thought: *I could belong here.* Not to a place. To a person.

Neither of them said it. Some words are load-bearing; say them too early and the whole structure comes down. So they sat in the borrowed sunlight and let the unsaid thing settle over them like snowfall, soft and patient, certain of its ground.

They stayed too long. That was the price of joy in the high places — you forgot the sun was rationed.

When the light began to slant, Edmund stood and stretched and felt, for one full moment, entirely unafraid. "Half a day more to the far saddle. There's a shepherd's hut on the north side — dry, abandoned, maybe a blanket. We sleep warm tonight for the first time in a week."

Quentin got up beside her.

He got up, and then he didn't stay up.

He swayed, and his hand shot out for the boulder and missed it, and Edmund lunged and caught him around the chest as his knees buckled, and they went down together onto the ice with his full weight suddenly, terribly, against her. He was shaking. His skin, where her cheek pressed it, was cold as the snow they'd fought with.

"Quentin. Quentin, look at me."

His eyes opened. For a heartbeat they were wrong — filmed with a pearly light, like windows lit from a room she couldn't see — and then they were his again, wide and frightened.

"The Teeth," he whispered. "We're in the Teeth. I can feel it. Something down there in the old city knows I'm close, and it's — Edmund, it's pulling. Like a hook behind my eyes. It's drinking harder, it knows the way home, and I don't know how much longer I can—"

He convulsed. The light beneath the crown flared — not the gentle frost-flicker of morning but a hard white blaze, and for an instant Edmund saw the gold for what it truly was: not jewelry, not symbol, but a root. A taproot sunk into a living skull, drawing up whatever grew there.

Then he went limp in her arms.

Above the col the empty sky pressed down, starless even at noon, and far to the south — so far it might have been imagination, but Edmund had spent her life trusting the small details others missed — she saw a smudge rising over the ruins of the old capital.

Smoke. Too much smoke. Or dust.

Or something, coming up the mountain road, moving fast.

* * *

Edmund worked the way her mother had taught her to work a frozen rope: fast, precise, no wasted motion, panic saved for later.

She dragged him into the lee of the boulder, out of the wind's knife. She got his pack off, got her cloak under his head, chafed his hands, pressed her ear to his mouth and found breath — shallow, uneven, but there. His pulse fluttered like a bird trapped under cloth. She talked to him the whole time, low and steady, nonsense and route-talk mixed together — *the ledge past the col, mind the ice, your bootlace is loose, you stubborn fool, breathe* — because her mother had sworn that the unconscious could hear, and because if she stopped talking she would hear herself instead, and herself was screaming.

The crown burned under her fingertips when she finally dared touch it. Not warm. *Hungry.* There was no other word. The gold had a texture now that her hand flinched from, a sense of inward motion, of appetite, and when she pulled her fingers back they ached as though she'd gripped a live coal.

"You're not taking him," she said aloud, to the sky, to the thing in the south, to whatever wore a king's shape and ate the light out of heaven. "Do you hear me? He's not yours. He was never yours. You can't have him."

The sky, of course, said nothing. The empty sky had been saying nothing for weeks, and Edmund had learned to hate its silence more than any threat.

But Quentin's eyelids moved. His lips parted.

"...Edmund," he breathed, and it was not the first time he'd said her name, but something in how he said it now — stripped of irony, stripped of everything but the raw fact of reaching for her — undid her completely.

"I'm here." She took his hand and pressed it flat against the ice, against the solid ancient bones of the mountain her mother had loved. "Feel that? Real rock. We're on my map now, do you understand? My track, my Teeth, my rules. Nothing finds us up here unless I let it. So you're going to breathe, and you're going to wake up properly, and tomorrow you're going to hate that scree crossing, and in three days we'll be past all of them. Past the crown-thing, past the usurper, past the temple and its contracts. Do you hear me?"

A ghost of movement at the corner of his mouth. Almost a grin. "...Choirboy," he murmured.

Edmund laughed, and the laugh broke halfway through into something else, and she bowed her head over his hand and let it shake out of her — fear and fury and the terrible new tenderness she had no ledger column for — while the light failed in the west and the smudge over the old capital grew, slowly, patiently, like a shadow climbing stairs.

Below them, unseen through the closing clouds, the first riders were already entering the mouth of the valley road.

They were not looking for a goat-track.

But the temple had taught Edmund one lesson above all others: debts always find a way to be collected.
