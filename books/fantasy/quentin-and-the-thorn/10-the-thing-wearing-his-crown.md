## 10. The Thing Wearing His Crown

The dream did not come like a dream.

It came like a door opening in a house he had lived in all his life without knowing the room existed behind it — a slow swing of weight, a breath of cold air that smelled of tallow and old stone and something sweeter beneath, something like honey left too long in the sun until it turned to rot. Quentin stood on a threshold he had never seen, and yet his feet knew the flagstones, knew the way they dipped toward the center where ten thousand years of feet had worn them hollow.

Beyond the threshold burned a hall lit by no fire.

The light came from above — from a ceiling of black glass through which stars should have shown but did not, because the stars were *here*, in the hall, hanging in glass vessels along the walls like lanterns in a festival street. Hundreds of them. Thousands. Each one a small captive sun, each one dimmer than the last, arranged in rows that receded into darkness the way a ledger's columns recede into the past. And before each vessel, at intervals down the whole impossible length of the hall, sat a chair.

In the nearest chair sat a king.

He was not old. That was the first horror, and it took Quentin a long moment to understand why it was a horror at all. The man had dark hair going grey at the temples, a broad weathered face, hands folded in his lap like a farmer resting between tasks. He might have been fifty. He might have been any age at all, because his eyes were wrong — not clouded, not dead, but *full*, the way a well is full, deep beyond any bottom a rope could reach. When he turned his head, the movement had the quality of something very large pretending to be small, a bear folding itself into a doghouse.

"You're awake," the king said. His voice was gentle. It was the gentlest thing Quentin had ever heard, and it made his skin crawl like a hand drawn slowly up his spine. "Good. They so rarely wake. The others just... arrive already hollowed out, you know how it is. But you've been fighting me. I respect that."

"I don't know you," Quentin said. His voice came out thin. He looked down at himself and found he was wearing white — coronation whites, the high-collared coat with the silver thorn embroidered at the throat, the one he had never actually been crowned in because the coronation had gone *wrong* —

"Ah." The king smiled, and the smile was warm, and it was the second horror, because Quentin recognized it. He had seen that smile across a dinner table for twenty years. He had seen it tilted toward their father, toward ambassadors, toward servants who had displeased him. "You do know me. You simply don't know that you know me. Sit. Please. Standing tires the body, and we have so much to discuss about the body."

"I'd rather stand."

"Of course you would." The king gestured, and a second chair slid from the darkness as if the hall itself were obliging. Quentin did not sit. The king did not seem to mind. He settled back, laced his fingers over his stomach, and looked up at the black-glass ceiling with an expression of profound contentment. "Do you know what a star is? Truly? Not what the priests tell you — little fires, the lamps of heaven, the eyes of the dead watching. No. A star is a debt being paid. Light is payment. Every photon that crosses the void is a coin changing hands, and the universe keeps immaculate books." He tapped his temple, right where — right where — "I learned that from the men who built this place. Before Thornfell was Thornfell. Before there were borders to ward or smugglers' roads to walk them. There was only hunger, and the sky, and the long problem of how to reach the sky."

Quentin's hand rose without his permission and touched his brow. In the waking world there was a scar there now, ridged and hot, where the gold had fused. Here, in the dream, when his fingers found the spot, he felt the crown entire — felt its weight, its circumference, its roots sunk into his skull like the roots of a tree into a hillside. And through those roots, faintly, he felt the hall. Felt the rows of dimming lights. Felt that he was standing in a room inside his own head that was also a room inside someone else's, and the walls between were thinner than paper.

"What are you?" he whispered.

The king spread his hands, modest, almost shy. "I am Thornfell."

* * *

They walked, after that. Or rather the king walked and Quentin was drawn along, the way iron is drawn, the way water is drawn downhill. Down the length of the hall, past the vessels of light. As they went, the king talked, and his talk was worse than any threat, because it was patient, and reasonable, and full of care.

"The first vessel was my own," he said, trailing his fingers along a shelf of glass. Inside one jar, a star no larger than a plum turned slowly, shedding a light the color of a dying ember. "I built this body myself, you understand. Grew it from nothing, the way you grow a crop. It lasted ninety years. A good body. I was fond of it. But bodies fail — that's their nature, that's their *charm* — and I had not yet solved the problem of transfer. So I took a younger one. My son's, as it happened. He objected." A pause, mild as milk. "They always object, at first. Then they stop objecting, because they stop being anything at all, and then it's just the two of us in here, you and I, getting acquainted."

"How many?" Quentin's mouth was dry. In the dream his dryness was real; he could feel the dream-body failing in the world above, feel Edmund's hands on his shoulders shaking him, her voice coming down to him through fathoms of dark water — *Quentin, Quentin, wake up* — and he could not, because the crown would not release its hook from the underside of sleep.

"Count them," the king said pleasantly.

And Quentin counted, because some part of him — the scribe's part, the part that understood ledgers — could not help itself. Vessels hung in alcoves along the walls, each one a figure seated in stillness, each one wearing a face. Kings, mostly. Some queens. One who could not have been more than twelve years old, a boy in miniature armor, sitting with the terrible patience of the emptied. Quentin counted forty-one before his counting broke, and the king said, softly, "Forty-six. You missed the ones who fought. They take longer, and I keep them apart, out of respect."

They passed an alcove set slightly deeper than the rest, and Quentin saw a man with his own jawline, his own crooked nose, his own way of holding his shoulders — his father, or the shape his father had worn last, and beside him another, younger, smiling even in emptiness, and Quentin knew that smile, knew it the way you know a knife by the scar it left —

"Aldric," he said.

"He was a fine vessel," the king said. "Strong. Willful, though. Willfulness burns fuel. I got eleven years out of him, which was generous, considering." The king stopped walking and turned, and for the first time his face did something other than smile: it considered. The fullness of his eyes swung onto Quentin like a lantern being raised. "You want to hate me. I can feel it in here—" he tapped Quentin's brow, lightly, the way a father might tap a son's forehead in blessing, "—and I want you to know that hatred is permitted. Encouraged, even. Hatred is fuel too. Do you know what I eat, Quentin? What I have eaten since before your people had a name?"

Quentin said nothing. The hall waited. Somewhere far off, in the waking world, Edmund was shouting his name, and the sound came to him warped, like bells heard underwater.

"Light," the king said. "I eat light. Every night, when your body sleeps, I open the crown and I drink. A little from you — vitality, years, the bright particular stuff that makes a man *himself* — and more from the reserves. From the jars." He gestured at the walls, at the thousands of dimming stars. "But jars run dry. Even the sky runs dry, if you drink from it long enough. Did you wonder, boy? Did you lie awake in your exile and wonder why the wardens' maps showed fewer stars than your grandfather's maps, and his fewer than his grandfather's? Stars do not die, Quentin. Stars are eaten. And three weeks ago, the harvest ran so low that I had to take the sky clean. All of it. Every light above the Thornfells, swallowed in a single night, to keep this hall lit while I searched for you."

The floor seemed to tilt. Quentin caught himself against a shelf of glass, and the star within flared weakly at his touch, recognizing, perhaps, a fellow prisoner.

"You," the king continued, resuming his stroll, "are the solution to a very old accounting problem. Your line was bred for capacity. Forty-six generations of careful husbandry — I chose your great-grandsire's great-grandsire myself, from among the mountain clans, for the width of his spirit. A wide spirit holds more light. Your brother held a remarkable amount. You hold more. I have been drinking from you for twenty years through the crown, slowly, the way one draws off cream, saving the body. And now the body is nearly ready, and the reserves are nearly spent, and the timing, I confess, is elegant. When I finish the transfer, I will be able to go back to sipping. A star here, a star there. The sky will come back, in time. I'll let it. A shepherd lets the grass grow back; that is the whole art of shepherding." He paused before the empty chair at the end of the hall — a chair sized for Quentin, carved with thorns, waiting. "You will not even be gone, properly speaking. I keep the shapes. Look—"

He snapped his fingers, and the alcoves lit, and every vessel turned its head.

Forty-six faces, in ranks, all looking at him with the king's full, bottomless eyes. His father. Aldric. The boy in miniature armor. Faces out of ballads Quentin had sung as a child, kings whose names were carved over tavern doors. All of them wearing that same gentle, patient smile, and none of them home.

"This is what you are," the king said, and his voice wrapped around Quentin like a shroud, like a blanket tucked in by loving hands. "Not a prince. Not a pretender. Not a runaway with a smuggler girl who draws lovely maps. You are a *vessel*. A cup. And a cup does not grieve when it is filled. Come and sit, Quentin. It doesn't hurt. That is the one mercy I have kept perfect across all these centuries: it has never once hurt."

And Quentin — God help him, God damn him — felt his knees begin to bend. Because the chair was warm. Because the hall smelled like his childhood, like beeswax and cedar and the oil they'd used on his first sword. Because forty-six faces smiled at him with his family's smiles, and the alternative was the cold, and the running, and the woman upstairs shouting herself hoarse over a man who was only ever going to be a cup.

His knee touched the seat.

* * *

He woke screaming her name.

Not his own name — hers. *Edmund, Edmund, Edmund,* torn out of him raw, because somewhere in the drowning dark of the dream, in the last moment before his knee touched that warm and waiting throne, it had not been his own face he'd thought of, or his father's, or Aldric's smiling ghost. It had been hers. Sharp-faced, ink-stained, lying to toll-keepers with a straight spine and a liar's beautiful calm. Edmund, who had killed for him and frozen after. Edmund, who owned nothing in the world except a forged seal, a stolen contract, and a map drawn in the margins of other people's debts — and who had handed him half of it anyway.

A cup did not get to love anyone. That was the thought that came up with him out of the dark like a diver breaking water, gasping. A cup did not get to choose. And he had *chosen* — he had chosen in the dream, at the very lip of surrender, and the choosing had been the one thing in that ancient hall the king could not drink.

Hands seized his shoulders. Lamplight. Her face, white, close, her hair loose and her eyes wide with a fear she would deny later. Around them the ruined bothy of their sheltering rocks, the wind, the vast starless pressure of the sky.

"You were screaming," she said. "You were screaming and your eyes were open. Quentin, your eyes were *open*."

He tried to answer and found his throat wrecked. He reached up instead, with a hand that shook like a leaf, and touched his brow — and froze.

Because under his fingertips, faint as a heartbeat, faint as a coin dropped in an empty room, the crown was *warm*. Warmer than his skin. Warm the way a chair is warm when someone has just risen from it.

And far away, southward, in the direction of the ruined summer palace, something that wore his brother's smile had felt him refuse — and had begun, patiently, hungrily, to move.

"It knows where we are," Quentin rasped. "Edmund. It knows where we are, and it's coming."
