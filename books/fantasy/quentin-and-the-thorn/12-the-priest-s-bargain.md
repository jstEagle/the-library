## 12. The Priest's Bargain

They took her at the ford below Cindermere, where the water ran thin as hammered tin and the reeds had gone the color of old bone. Edmund should have seen them — she had spent her whole life seeing things, reading the small betrayals of a landscape: the bird that flushed too early, the dust that hung wrong above a ridgeline, the silence where a sentry's breathing ought to be. But Quentin had been feverish for two days, leaning on her shoulder with his weight growing heavier by the hour, and her attention had narrowed to him alone, to the heat of his brow and the rasp of his breath, and so the world behind her had gone unwatched.

The first she knew of it was a crossbow bolt burying itself in the ford's gravel an arm's length ahead, throwing up a little fan of white stone.

"Down," she said, and pulled Quentin with her into the reeds, but it was already too late; men were wading out from both banks, six of them in temple grey, their cowls pushed back, their faces ordinary and tired and utterly without malice. That was the worst of it. They did not look like hunters. They looked like clerks come to collect an overdue account.

"Edmund Halloran." The speaker was older than the rest, grey-bearded, with the ink-stained fingers she knew too well because she saw them every time she looked at her own hands. A senior archivist, then. Someone who had sat three desks away from her for years without ever once looking at her. "You'll come quietly. The brother is not our charge."

"He's hurt."

"The brother is not our charge," the man repeated, with the mild patience of someone reciting a rule that had outlived everyone who wrote it. "Leave him, and walk."

She looked at Quentin, half-collapsed in the shallows, his face bloodless under the brown stain of road dust, his lips moving around words that wouldn't come. The crown beneath his skin was drinking him even now; she could almost see it happening, the way a candle flame leans when something unseen passes over it.

"If I leave him he dies."

"Then that is between him and the gods," said the archivist, and something in Edmund went cold and quiet and very clear, the way it always did just before she did the unforgivable thing.

* * *

They bound her wrists with ledger-tape — actual ledger-tape, the waxed ribbon used to mark pages, and she would think about that later, about how the temple reached for you with its own stationery — and walked her up out of the valley to a waystation she knew from the maps: the old tithe-barn at Harrow Fell, built in her grandfather's grandfather's time, its stones furred with lichen the color of verdigris. Inside, the smell was of tallow and cold incense and mouse droppings, and the light came down through a smoke-hole in a single dusty column that turned slowly all afternoon, like the hand of a clock counting toward something.

They put her in the tithe-room, which had once held grain owed to the temple and now held debts instead — shelves of contract-boxes rising to the rafters, each one labeled in a neat clerk's hand. She found her own family's box within thirty seconds of being left alone. She didn't need to read the label. She could have found it blindfolded, by smell alone, if contracts had a smell — and to her they did, always: lamp-oil and fear.

Quentin they took elsewhere. She heard his voice once, raised, and then not again, and the not-again was worse than any scream.

The archivist came at dusk, carrying a lantern and a writing case, and set them both on the table before her with the ceremony of a man laying out instruments for surgery.

"You'll want to sit," he said.

"I'd rather stand."

He shrugged and opened the writing case. From it he took a document, and Edmund's stomach dropped before her mind caught up, because she knew the paper — heavy cream stock, temple-watermarked, the kind used for one document only. He laid it flat and turned it to face her, and there it was: *Contract of Redemption, Halloran Family Debt,* in her father's desperate hand countersigned by her own forged one, and beside it, a second sheet, blank except for a heading she had copied a hundred times for others and never once expected to see above her own name.

*Act of Surrender and Delivery.*

"Read it," the archivist said. "I'll wait. I know you'll actually read it. You're the only debtor we've ever had who reads everything."

"That's because everything is written to be misunderstood."

"And yet you understand it better than the priests do." He settled onto the bench across from her, unhurried, and folded his ink-stained hands. "I'm going to tell you a thing that is told to perhaps four people in a generation, Edmund Halloran, because you've earned it by being inconvenient. The temple does not serve the Usurper. The Usurper serves the temple. Or rather—" he tilted his head, correcting himself with a scholar's precision, "—they both serve the same master, and have since before either had a name. You've seen what wears the crown. You've traveled with it. You know."

Her mouth was dry. "I know a man."

"You know a vessel. A failing one. The last vessel lasted sixty years; this one has been eaten through in twenty. The master requires a new one, and the old king's line produces the only bodies that fit." He slid the Act of Surrender across the table until it touched her bound hands. "Deliver him to the summer palace. Sign nothing here — your signature will be taken at delivery, witnessed, sealed. In exchange: the Halloran debt is struck. Not paid. *Struck.* Struck clean, as though it never existed. Your mother's freedom, your brothers' freedom, yours. Every name lifted from every ledger in every house of Thornfell. Tonight, if you like. I have the authority. I have the wax."

From his sleeve he produced a seal — black, not the temple's red — and set it down beside the papers, and Edmund stared at it and felt the whole architecture of her life tilt.

Struck clean. She turned the phrase over and over, tasting it. Not bought, not bargained for, not ground out over decades of copying other people's sins in bad light. Gone. Her mother released from the weaving-house. Tam and Rill allowed to keep their names when they spoke them. Herself able to walk into any town in Thornfell and say *my name is Edmund Halloran* — no, not even that, she thought, with a laugh that nearly escaped her — say *my name is Edith*, her true name, the one she hadn't spoken aloud in four years, and owe nothing to anyone for the saying of it.

"How long have you known my name?" she asked quietly. "My real one."

"Since the day you were contracted. We know everything in the ledgers, Edith. That's rather the point of us."

* * *

They left her alone with the documents and the lantern, which she understood was not carelessness but method. Temptation needs privacy the way a seed needs soil. They wanted her alone in the dark with her mother's face.

And she was. For a long while she simply sat, and let herself want it — truly want it, without shame, the way a starving woman lets herself look at bread. She imagined the scene at home: her mother at the door, the debt-notice burned in the yard, Tam laughing the way he used to before the creditors came. She imagined signing, walking out of Harrow Fell, pointing the grey men toward the pass where Quentin slept or didn't sleep, and then going home. Home. The word had become so abstract to her that picturing it felt like picturing a country she'd read about in someone else's travel book.

Then she looked at the lantern, and thought about starlight.

Because that was the thing the archivist hadn't understood — couldn't understand, a man who had spent his life inside the temple's walls believing its story. Edmund had seen the vault's glass cases in Quentin's fever-talk, had pieced together from his broken memories what the crown-thing was and what fed it. And she had done sums her whole life. Sums were her gift, her curse, her inheritance: every contract she had ever copied had taught her exactly how debts worked, and the oldest truth of them was this — *a debt forgiven by the creditor is not forgiveness. It is refinancing.* Strike the Halloran debt, and the Hallorans would owe nothing to the temple. But the temple served the crown-thing, and the crown-thing ate stars, and every year of its continued existence was a year the sky stayed empty and every soul under it lived in the dark of a debt none of them had signed.

Her family's freedom, purchased with a sky.

She got up, wrists still bound, and walked to the shelf where her family's contract-box sat. The tape bit her skin. She didn't care. She drew the box down, set it on the table beside the Act of Surrender, and looked at the two documents side by side: the debt that owned her, and the price of ending it.

"You know what's funny," she said aloud to the empty room, because talking kept the wanting honest. "I've copied ten thousand contracts, and not one of them ever asked what was being sold. Just names and sums and seals. Never the thing itself." She touched the Act of Surrender with one finger. "This one sells the sky. And nobody even has to write that part down."

Somewhere below, a floorboard creaked. Then another. Footsteps — but wrong ones, uneven, dragging slightly, and she knew that gait, had supported that gait on her own shoulder for two days.

Quentin. Being moved. Being *delivered* already?

The lantern guttered. Through the smoke-hole, the column of dusty light had died with the sun, and the room went dark except for the small yellow circle around the table, and in that circle sat her whole life reduced to paper: everything she was, everything she loved, priced and ready for signature.

"No," she said. Quietly, at first, and then louder, to the room, to the temple, to the black thing wearing a crown at the top of the world. "*No.*"

* * *

Escaping Harrow Fell was, absurdly, the easiest part, and she had the temple to thank for it. Four years of copying their documents had taught her their habits, and their habits were the habits of men who believed paperwork was reality. The guard on the tithe-room door checked her bonds twice but her escort's chit only once; the chit was, of course, in her own handwriting, because she had written it herself during the archivist's absence, having picked the lock of the writing case with a quill-nib and eleven minutes of patience. Temple guards trusted temple paper the way farmers trust rain.

She walked out past two sleeping men and a boy who was supposed to be awake and wasn't, down the back stair into the undercroft, following a smell she had been cataloguing since childhood: the particular sourness of old lime and older shame that marked every temple's necessary-house. Every tithe-barn had one. Every necessary-house had a waste-chute down to the midden. And every midden, in Edmund Halloran's hard-won experience, had a drainage culvert wide enough for a thin person willing to be thoroughly disgusted.

She went down the chute feet-first, in the dark, biting her sleeve to keep from making sounds that would bring the guards running, and came out the culvert into night air that smelled, by comparison, like a garden in June. She lay in the nettles for a moment and laughed until she cried, silently, shaking, wrists still taped, covered in the temple's least dignified leavings — and free. Temporarily. Provisionally. Free the way a coin is free while it's still in the air.

Then she stopped laughing, because she remembered the dragging footsteps, and made herself get up.

Getting Quentin out was harder, and she would remember it afterward only in flashes: the storeroom where they'd put him, unlocked because locked doors were for prisoners and he was technically cargo; his weight against her, hotter than fever has any right to be; the crown glowing faintly through the skin of his brow now, a dull ember-light that made her want to scream and run and hold him all at once. Two grey men in the corridor, dealt with not by force but by forgery — she stepped out ahead of him waving the archivist's own transfer-order, shouting about the master's impatience and the change of schedule, and men trained to obey paper obeyed it, right up until the moment she and Quentin were through the gate and running.

They ran badly. They ran like two people who had nothing left. But the night was moonless and starless and utterly blind to them, and the fell paths were hers, and by the time the temple's horns began to sound behind them — late, confused, furious — Edmund and Quentin were already dropping into the next valley, into the bracken, into the dark.

* * *

They stopped when they couldn't hear the horns anymore, in a hollow where a stream cut through black stone, and there Edmund finally tore the ledger-tape off her wrists and sat down in the freezing water up to her ankles and let herself shake.

Quentin crouched beside her. The ember-glow under his skin had dimmed to almost nothing, but his eyes, when he found her face in the dark, were terribly clear.

"They offered you my body for your family," he said. It wasn't a question. He'd heard enough, in that storeroom, half-conscious, to piece it together.

"Yes."

"And you said no."

"I said no."

"Why?" The word cracked in the middle. "Edmund — Edith — your mother. Your brothers. I *heard* what they offered. Struck clean. All of it. No one on earth would blame—"

"Someone has to pay the sky's debt," she said, "and it isn't going to be you."

He stared at her. Above them the starless dark pressed down like a lid, and somewhere far to the north the summer palace waited with its treasure and its hunger, and behind them the temple's horns had gone quiet, which meant the hunt had changed shape, gone patient, gone clever.

"We'll find another way," Quentin said at last. "For your family. I swear it. Whatever's in the vault — whatever it takes—"

"Don't swear," Edmund said. She stood, wrung out the hem of her coat, and looked north, where the mountains made their ragged signature against a sky with nothing written on it. "Just walk. We owe each other nothing yet. Let's keep it that way until we can pay in full."

She took his hand — the first time she had ever taken it without the excuse of steadying him — and they went up out of the hollow together, two fugitives with no claim on anything, toward the ruined palace where the first king's stolen light still burned behind glass.

Behind them, unseen in the dark, a rider in temple grey watched them go, and did not follow — and turned instead, slowly, toward the north, to carry word to the court that never sleeps.
