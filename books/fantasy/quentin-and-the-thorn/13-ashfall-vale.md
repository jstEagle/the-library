## 13. Ashfall Vale

Edmund smelled the valley before she saw it — a dry, mineral stench like a forge left cold, like ash that had never known fire. She crested the ridge with Quentin's arm draped across her shoulders, his weight a slow betrayal of her knees, and looked down into Ashfall Vale and understood, for the first time, what they were walking toward.

Nothing. Nothing green. Nothing living.

The vale lay below them like a cupped hand of grey. Grass gone to powder. Trees standing as pale skeletons, bark sloughing off in sheets to expose wood bleached white as bone. A stream still ran through the middle of it — she could hear it, a thin silver thread of sound — but where it touched stone, the rock was stained black, and no moss grew along its banks. Even the stones had given up.

"It's the crown," she said.

Quentin didn't answer. He hadn't answered much in three days. The fever had come on after the Widow's Teeth, after that one impossible day above the cloud line when he'd laughed at her terrible singing and she'd let herself believe — foolishly, dangerously — that they might simply walk out of this story alive and together. Then the laughing had stopped. Then the eating had stopped. Now he walked only because she held him up, and each night the crown drank from him with a thirst that seemed to grow as they moved east, toward the old capital, toward whatever waited beneath the ruined summer palace.

She got him down the slope by increments, sliding through ash that rose around their boots in soft grey ghosts. It clung to everything. By the time they reached the stream, Edmund's hands were grey to the elbow, her hair streaked like an old woman's, and Quentin was shaking so hard she had to lower him against a dead tree trunk before he fell.

"Water," he managed.

"Not from that." She'd watched what the water did here. Instead she dug through their pack — lighter than it should have been, the food nearly gone — and found the last skin of clean water from the high fells, and pressed it to his lips. He drank like a man remembering how. His eyes, when they found hers, were sunken and too bright.

"You shouldn't have carried me down," he said.

"You couldn't have stayed up there."

"I could have." His voice cracked on it. "You should have let me."

* * *

That night they sheltered in the hollow of the largest dead tree Edmund had ever seen — an oak, once, its heart rotted out but its shell standing firm, wide enough for two people to sit inside like children hiding from a storm. The ash kept falling. Not from any sky; there was no fire to rain down. The ash simply drifted on the wind off the higher peaks, sifting down through dead branches with a whisper like turning pages.

Edmund sat with her back against the tree's inner wall and Quentin's head in her lap, and she worked the knots out of his hair because it was the only useful thing her hands could do. Above them, through a gap in the branches, the sky was a flat, starless black. It had been nine nights now. She had stopped expecting the stars to return. But some animal part of her still looked up every few minutes, still flinched at the wrongness of it, the way a tongue returns to a missing tooth.

"The first king ate them," Quentin murmured. He said it the way other men said prayers. "All of them. Centuries of nights, and he ate them one by one."

"Hush. Sleep."

"If I sleep, I dream about him."

"Then don't sleep."

A ghost of a smile crossed his cracked lips. "You've solved everything, scribe."

She almost smiled back. Then his body seized — a single violent shudder, his spine arching, his jaw clenching — and she felt it happen, felt the moment the crown took its tithe. His skin went cold under her fingers. His breathing thinned to nothing for three seconds, five, seven, long enough that her heart stopped with his, and then he dragged air back in like a drowning man surfacing.

When it passed, he lay very still, and tears ran sideways from his closed eyes into the ash.

"Edmund." Her name came out broken. "I need you to listen to me. Properly listen."

"I'm listening."

"Leave tomorrow. At first light. Go south, over the Saddle, down to the coast road. You know paths nobody else in Thornfell knows — you told me yourself, you've memorized every smuggler's route since you were a child drawing in ledger margins. You can be in Vellamere by month's end. You can be *free*."

"And you?"

He opened his eyes. In the dark they were just wet gleams. "I'm not going to make it over the mountains. We both know it. Every mile we go east, it feeds harder. It's eating me faster because we're getting closer to it — closer to home, whatever home means to a thing that old. If you stay, you die of starvation in a dead valley next to a dying man. That's not love, Edmund. That's just two people losing."

"Don't." Her voice came out sharp enough to cut. "Don't you dare use that word and then tell me to leave."

"That's exactly why I'm using it. Because if I don't say it now, I'll never get the chance—"

"Then say it and mean something different by it." She was shaking. She realized her fists were knotted in his shirt and made herself let go, made herself breathe. "Say it like a man who wants to live."

He was quiet a long time. The ash whispered down outside the tree.

"I'm frightened," he said finally, so quietly she had to lean close to hear. "I've been frightened since the coronation. Twenty years of running from my own face. And you're the first thing — the first *anything* — that's made me want to stop being afraid and start being angry instead. So yes. Say it however you like. I love you. And I am telling you to leave me, because loving you is the only unselfish thing I have ever done."

"No," Edmund said. "It isn't. Leaving would be. And I refuse."

* * *

In the morning he was worse. He tried to stand and his legs folded, and Edmund caught him, and they ended up sitting in the ash together, her arms around him, both of them filthy and starving and laughing — actually laughing — at the absurdity of two people who had survived ward-hunters and temple soldiers and the Widow's Teeth, undone by a dead oak tree.

"Stop it," he wheezed. "It hurts to laugh."

"Good. Then feel this instead." She pulled the maps from inside her coat.

They weren't maps, exactly. Not anymore. They were the margins of twenty years of debt ledgers, torn free and folded small, covered edge to edge in her own cramped hand: passes and goat-tracks, shepherd trails and smuggler stairs, springs marked with a crosshatch, tollhouses marked with a circle, the houses of people who owed her family favors or grudges, annotated in a private shorthand no temple clerk could read. She spread them out between them in the ash like a fortune-teller laying cards.

"What's this?" he asked.

"Our route. Except you've been following me like a pack mule for a hundred miles, and I'm done carrying the map alone. So." She smoothed the oldest page flat against her knee. "Lesson one. This ridge line here — see how I've drawn it with the little hooks? Those hooks are scree fields. Loose stone. You never camp below scree in Thornfell, because the mountain shrugs in its sleep and buries you. Where do we sleep tonight if we leave this vale heading east?"

Quentin stared at the page. His brow furrowed — and she saw him wince as the crown pulsed, saw him fight through the pain to think, which was its own kind of courage. He traced the hooks with a finger gone thin as a bird's leg.

"Below them," he said. "No. Wait." He looked up at the dead trees, at the silent slopes. "There's no scree here. Everything's soft. Ash holds nothing up."

"Better. Keep going."

"So we camp anywhere. Which means..." He turned the page over, studying her marginalia. "Which means we don't need to reach the next shelter. We can stop when *you're* tired." His eyes came up to hers. "I always assumed you pushed us because the route demanded it. Because you knew things I didn't."

"I do know things you don't."

"Yes. But I thought—" He stopped. Started again. "I thought you were driving us. I didn't realize you were pacing us. To me. The whole way."

"Lesson two," Edmund said, and her voice wasn't quite steady. "This mark. Circle with a slash. That's a tollhouse with a keeper who takes bribes. Circle with a cross — keeper who doesn't. Crossed-out circle — keeper who sold me out once already." She tapped the last one. "Greywater. Never again."

"You remember everyone," he said softly. "Every person who's ever wronged you or helped you."

"I remember everyone who's ever *cost* me. It's the same skill, used differently." She flipped to a newer page. "Now. Lesson three, and pay attention, because this is the one that keeps us alive. The summer palace ruins. Everyone thinks you approach from the west road, because that's where the road goes. Roads are for people who want to be seen. We go from the north, through this—" her finger traced a line so faint it was barely a pencil ghost, "—the drainage tunnel from the old cisterns. My father helped dig it when he was young and stupid. It comes up inside the outer wall."

Quentin was silent. When she glanced up, he was looking at her with an expression she couldn't name — something between wonder and grief.

"Why are you teaching me this?" he asked. "If you won't leave, why does it matter whether I can read your maps? I'm the one slowing us down. If anything, you should be memorizing how fast I fail."

"Because," Edmund said, "a route belongs to whoever carries it. That's the first law of smuggling. My father taught me that. A map in one person's head is a hostage. A map in two heads is a *road*." She folded his fingers closed over the oldest page. "So carry it. Learn it. Argue with it. Add to it. Because I am not leaving this vale alone, Quentin — either we walk out of here together, or we don't walk out at all. And if we're going to die in these mountains, I will not die as someone's guide. I'll die as someone's partner."

The wind moved through the dead trees, and the ash fell like snow that had forgotten how to land gently, and Quentin of Thornfell — heir to nothing, crowned with a curse, twenty years an exile in his own kingdom — bowed his head over her father's handwriting and began, in a voice roughened by fever, to recite the route back to her. East through the vale. North fork past the black-stained stream. The cistern tunnel. He got one detail wrong, and she corrected him, and he corrected himself, and when he finished she took the pencil stub from her coat and handed it to him.

"Mark it," she said. "Your way. Your hand. It's yours now too."

His hand shook. The marks he made were clumsy, a soldier's marks, nothing like her clerk's precision — but he drew a small star above the dead oak where they'd slept, and beside it he wrote, in letters that wobbled: *worst inn in Thornfell.*

Edmund laughed until she cried.

* * *

They rested a full day in the hollow tree because Quentin could not walk, and a second day because Edmund could not wake him. On the second night the dream took him completely, and Edmund sat holding his burning hands and listened to him speak in a voice that was not entirely his own — an older voice, dry as the ash, patient as stone.

*"Scribe," it said. "Little thief of margins. You carry him toward me and call it love."*

"You leave him alone," Edmund whispered.

*"I have worn thirty-one heads. His is nearly done. Bring him to the vault and I will show you wonders. Or turn back and watch him gutter like a candle. Either way, scribe, you bring him to me. All roads do."*

"Then all roads are wrong," Edmund said, "and I'll make a new one."

The voice laughed — a sound like a landslide far away — and released him. Quentin woke gasping her name, clutching her wrists hard enough to bruise, and for a moment neither of them spoke. The ash sifted down. Somewhere in the dead branches above, a branch settled with a crack like a distant door closing.

"He talks to you now," Quentin rasped. It wasn't a question.

"He talks *through* you. There's a difference, and I intend to exploit it." She wiped his face with the last clean cloth. "He thinks all roads lead to him. Fine. Let him think it. We'll use the road he's not watching."

"There isn't one."

"There's always one. That's lesson four." She pressed the water skin to his lips and made him drink. "Drink. Tomorrow we move. And Quentin — when we reach the vault, when we find whatever's down there in the dark with him — I need you to promise me something."

"Anything."

"Don't promise anything. Just listen. Whatever he offers you — your body back, your brother, your crown, the whole rotten throne — remember that he has worn thirty-one heads and every one of them believed they'd be the exception." She tucked a strand of damp hair behind his ear, and her hand lingered there longer than it needed to. "You're not the exception. You're the end. There's a difference, and I intend to prove it."

Quentin caught her hand and held it against his cheek. Under the fever-heat of his skin, the crown pulsed once — hungry, patient — and he turned his face into her palm like a man drinking from the last well in the world.

"East, then," he said. "Past the black stream. North fork."

"And the cistern tunnel."

"*Worst inn in Thornfell,*" he agreed, and for one moment, in the hollow of a dead tree in a valley the crown had murdered, they smiled at each other across the ash like conspirators — which, Edmund supposed, was exactly what they were.

Far to the east, beyond the ridgeline, something vast shifted in its sleep beneath the ruins of the summer palace. And in the vault below, sealed glass jars of stolen starlight glimmered in ranks upon shelves upon shelves — enough light to buy a hundred families' freedom, or to fuel one great working — waiting, as they had waited for centuries, for someone to choose.
