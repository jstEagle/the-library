## 15. Two Debts

The vault did not want to be counted.

Edmund had spent six years counting — candle-stubs, quills, coins owed against coins held, breaths between her mother's letters and the temple's replies — and so she knew the difference between a hoard that could be tallied and a treasure that simply *was*. This was the second kind. The shelves ran back into the dark of the mountain like the ribs of some buried god, and on every shelf stood glass vessels: flasks, orbs, long sealed tubes the length of her forearm, each one holding a measure of light that moved when she moved, leaning toward her hand the way a cat leans toward a warm window.

Starlight. Bottled before the stars went out. Bottled, perhaps, over centuries, one theft at a time.

She had been in the vault for what she judged to be most of a day. There was no way to be certain; the ruined summer palace above them had no windows left whole, and the mountain kept its own hours. Quentin slept behind her on a pallet of cloaks they'd stripped from a guard's long-abandoned post, his breathing shallow and too quick, the crown's black filigree standing out against the grey of his skin like veins of ore in poor stone. Every few minutes he made a small sound — not words, never quite words — and her hand found the nearest shelf and gripped it until the sound stopped.

Count, she told herself. Counting was the thing she knew how to do. Counting was the only prayer she had ever been taught that worked.

So she counted.

The gold alone would stagger a kingdom. Stacks of struck coin from reigns whose names had worn off their own faces. Torques and diadems tangled like fish caught in nets. A chest of uncut garnets the color of arterial blood. She did not bother estimating past three kingdoms' ransoms; the number lost meaning somewhere around the second shelf, the way distances lose meaning in fog.

But the gold was not the point. The gold had never been the point.

She lifted a flask down from eye level and turned it in the lantern light. The glass was old — older than glass had any right to look, its surface pitted as though by wind that had never blown underground — and through it the light inside shifted colors slowly, patiently, like weather seen from very far away. When she held it near her cheek she felt warmth, faint but unmistakable, the kind of warmth she remembered from childhood mornings before the debt came north and took everything with names on it.

One flask, she thought. What would one flask buy?

She tried to work it out the scribe's way, laying sums in her head the way she'd once laid them in ledgers. Her family's debt stood at four hundred and eleven crowns, nine marks, and whatever interest the temple had since invented — and the temple invented generously; she had written the entries herself, had watched the numbers swell under her own pen because refusing meant her father went to the mines and her sister went to whoever paid. Four hundred eleven crowns. A single starlit flask, glowing like this, would fetch more than that from any collector in the lowlands. Half a flask, perhaps. A third, if the buyer were cruel, and buyers of miracle were always cruel.

There were hundreds of flasks. Hundreds of orbs. Tubes beyond counting, receding into the dark where her lantern gave out.

Enough to free her family forever. Enough to burn every contract Halloran had ever signed and salt the ashes. Enough to buy land in the south where the sun still came up, enough to endow her nieces and nephews and their children's children, enough that no Halloran would ever again kneel in a candlelit scriptorium writing the arithmetic of their own captivity in a fair and careful hand.

Her hand was shaking. She set the flask back on the shelf with exaggerated care, the way one sets down a sleeping child.

And beside it — she had known it was there, had known for hours, had been circling it like a moth circles a flame it cannot decide to die in — lay the working.

It was not labeled. It did not need to be. The deepest shelf, closest to the wall where the mountain's heart beat slowest, held a case of black iron banded in silver, and inside the case, nested in cloth gone stiff with age, sat seven flasks arranged in a pattern she recognized from the old contracts she'd copied in the temple's archive — the binding-shape, the seven-point seal, the geometry that held things. Seven flasks, each larger than the others, each burning brighter, and beneath the case a tablet of slate covered in the first king's own hand. She had read it twice. She had understood it on the first reading, which was the worst part. It was a contract. Everything in this cursed country was a contract.

*Unbind what is bound,* the slate said, in the elegant, terrible shorthand of sorcery-as-accounting. *Render unto the bound that which was taken. Break the vessel of the debt.*

Seven flasks. One working. The crown cracked off Quentin's skull, the stolen centuries poured back into the sky, and the stars came home.

Or: sell the seven. Sell all of it. Buy a life.

Behind her, Quentin made the small sound again, and this time it broke open into words.

"Edmund."

She was across the vault before she'd decided to move. He was half-risen on one elbow, eyes unfocused, sweat standing on his forehead in beads that caught the light of the crown — the crown *glowed*, now, faintly, hungrily, drinking faster as though it knew what this room held, as though it smelled its own ransom stacked on shelves within reach of its dying host.

"I'm here." She knelt, pressed him back down. His skin burned under her palm. "I'm here, you're in the vault, we made it. Sleep."

"Can't." His mouth twisted. "It won't let me. It knows we're close. It's — Edmund, it's *happy*."

The word came out shredded. She smoothed the hair back from his brow, careful of the black metal, and felt him lean into her hand the way the starlight leaned toward hers, and thought, with a clarity that felt like falling: *I love him.* Not for the first time. But for the first time without any argument ready against it.

"The fever's talking," she said.

"No." He found her wrist and held it, grip feeble and absolute. "Listen to me while I can still say it plainly. I've watched you count since we walked in. You think I don't see? You've got your ledger-face on. The one you wear when you're adding up something you're afraid of."

"You have a ledger-face?"

"Everyone has a ledger-face." A ghost of his smile, there and gone. "Yours is when you go very quiet and your thumb moves like it's turning pages. Go on, then. Say it aloud. Say what the sums tell you."

She should have lied. She was good at lying; lying had kept her alive through six years of being Edmund-the-scribe, a boy so unremarkable that guards forgot his name between shifts. But he was burning up and looking at her like she was the only fixed point left in a world that had eaten his name, his brother, his face, his sky — and lies, just then, felt like a cruelty she couldn't afford.

"There's enough here," she said, "to pay my family's debt a hundred times over. To pay every debt my family will ever incur. There's enough to make us untouchable." Her voice stayed level. Scribe's training. "And there are seven flasks in an iron case that could break the crown off you and give the stars back."

The silence after was very large. Somewhere deep in the mountain, water dripped, keeping time like a patient clerk.

"And?" he said softly.

"And the temple doesn't forgive. That's rather the entire architecture of a temple." She heard her own voice go dry, academic — retreating into the ledger because the ledger was safe. "If I spend the starlight on the working, there's nothing left. No payment, no proof of intent, nothing to bargain with. My contract stands. I'm temple property until the debt is discharged or until I die, whichever comes first, and the temple has a vested interest in which comes first. They'll hunt me the way they hunted the shepherd. They'll find my family and hold them tighter, not looser, because a runaway asset's kin are collateral worth collecting." She swallowed. "I'd be trading my freedom for yours. Permanently. And my family's safety with it, or at least gambling it."

"Yes," Quentin said.

"That's all? Yes?"

"What do you want me to say? That I don't want you to choose me? I'm not a saint, Edmund. Saints don't lie awake hating the weight on their skull and wishing — " He stopped. Breathed. Started again, quieter. "Take the gold. Take all of it. Leave me the seven if you like, leave me one, I'll drink it like wine and go out into the fells and let the crown finish what it started. You didn't sign on for this. You signed a smuggler's road and a wounded stranger, and both of those debts are paid. More than paid. You owe me nothing, and I—" the fever made his voice crack, "—I have nothing to offer you except a name that gets people killed and a skull that eats the sky. Take the gold. Marry a man who owns his own coat. Forget the pass where you found me."

He said it well. He'd clearly rehearsed it through the fever-hours, turning the speech over the way he turned the map she'd given him, learning its folds.

She almost did it.

That was the truth she would carry afterward, the truth she made herself sit with in the lantern light while he watched her with dying-fire eyes: she almost took the gold. Not because she loved him less — but because duty is older than love, and heavier, and it speaks in your mother's voice. She saw it all, laid out clean as columns: the southern farm, her mother's hands finally empty of mending-for-hire, her sister's daughters fed on bread with no temple tax baked into it, the Halloran name scoured clean of red ink down every generation. All of it purchasable. All of it *here*, in reach, tonight, and all she had to do was walk away from one burning man in a dark room and let the world keep its arrangement — the temple keeps its ledgers, the crown keeps its king, the sky keeps its absence. Everyone in their proper place. Every account balanced.

Every account but one.

"Quentin." She sat down on the edge of the pallet, deliberately, and took both his hands in hers. They were hot as bricks. "Do you remember the tollhouse? Greywater?"

His brow creased. "You forged the seals. The toll-keeper read your handwriting and went white as tallow."

"He recognized it because I copied his region's ledgers for three years. He knew my *penmanship*, Quentin. Do you understand what I'm telling you? The temple owns my handwriting. They own the shape of my letters. For six years I wrote myself into their books so carefully that even strangers knew my hand on sight — and I told myself it was survival, and it was, but it was also—" She stopped, surprised by what came next, and said it anyway. "It was obedience wearing competence as a mask. I am so tired of being *owned*, Quentin. Owned by the temple, owned by the debt, owned by the fear. If I take the gold and run south, I will spend the rest of my life listening for riders. I know how debts work. I've written thousands of them. The temple doesn't lose accounts — it waits. It waited twenty years for your father's line. It will wait fifty for me."

"So you'll free yourself and damn me instead," he said, and there was no accusation in it, only exhaustion, and somehow that was worse. "That's the honest sum, isn't it? Someone pays. That's the whole theology of this country. Someone always pays."

"Yes," Edmund said. "Someone always pays. That's what the first king built. That's what the usurper maintains. That's what the temple administers — a machine where suffering is always transferred, never ended. Debt passes down. Pain passes sideways. The crown eats a prince; the prince's hunger eats the sky; the dark eats the crops; the famine fills the temple's poor-boxes; the poor-boxes buy the contracts that bind girls like me into scriptoriums." She laughed, short and bitter, and heard how close to tears it was. "I have read the whole machine, Quentin. From the inside. Line by line. And I am telling you as a professional: the only way out of a machine like that is not to pay your way out. It's to break the *ledger*."

He stared at her. Above his brow the crown pulsed, slow and greedy, and she realized with cold clarity that it had gone quiet — listening. Let it listen. Let it hear what was coming.

"Seven flasks," she said. "One working. And when it's done, I'll have nothing — no payment, no protection, no proof. The temple will own my contract until I die, and I will likely die running from them, and my family will live under the shadow of that debt my whole life, and I may never see them again, and none of it will matter to anyone's history books because princesses get chronicles and scribes get footnotes at best." Her thumb was moving against his knuckles, turning invisible pages, and this time she let it. "But I will have done one thing in my life that no one made me do. One entry in my own hand, in my own book. Paid in full — not transferred. *Ended.*"

"Edmund—"

"And if you," she said, leaning close, so the fever had nowhere to hide from her voice, "tell me one more time to take the gold and leave you, I will forge your signature on a marriage contract so fast it'll make your head spin. I'm very good. The toll-keeper wept."

Something happened to his face — the wrecked, feverish, crowned face of a dead dynasty — something that started as a laugh and ended as something wetter, and he pulled her hands up against the awful heat of his brow, against the crown itself, and said, "You are the most terrifying creature Thornfell ever produced, and I include the thing that's eating my skull."

"Good," Edmund said. "Fear me. Everyone else does."

Then, because the moment was true but not infinite, she stood, and went to the iron case, and opened it, and looked at the seven flasks burning in their nest of ancient cloth — seven small captive suns, waiting two thousand years for someone foolish enough to spend them on anything but herself.

She picked up the first one. It was warm as a living hand.

That was when the mountain spoke.

Not shook — *spoke*. A voice came up through the rock itself, vast and unhurried, vibrating in the floor and the shelves and her teeth, a voice she recognized without ever having heard it, because she had transcribed its decrees for six years, had copied the Usurper's proclamations in her own careful hand:

*"Scribe."*

The lantern guttered. On the pallet, Quentin convulsed upright, the crown blazing black fire, and far above them, through a hundred fathoms of stone, came a sound like many boots finding the same rhythm at once.

*"You have something of mine. Come up. Bring the heir. Your ledger will balance — I promise you that much, little accountant. I always honor my contracts."*

Edmund closed her hand tight around the flask, felt the starlight press back against her palm like a heartbeat, and reached for the slate tablet with her free hand.

"We're about to be late," she said, "for our own appointment."
