## 16. The Usurper's Court

The wind changed before the horses came.

Quentin felt it first as a wrongness in his teeth — a cold that had nothing to do with altitude. He had learned to read the crown's moods the way sailors read weather: a pressure behind his eyes when danger gathered, a low singing in the fused gold when the thing inside it grew interested in something. Now it sang like a struck bell, and Edmund looked up from her map with her hand already at her knife.

"Riders," she said. "West of the vale. A lot of them."

He counted what he could hear over the wind: hooves on scree, many-shod, unhurried. Armies hurried. Kings did not.

They had made camp in the ribs of the ruined summer palace — the old Halloran vault was somewhere beneath them, and Edmund's maps said so, but the way down was through halls where the roof had fallen in and the mountain light fell in pale columns across floors furred with ash. Ash everywhere now. Since Greywater the green world had been dying around them by inches, and here at the heart of it even the lichen on the stones was grey as old skin.

Quentin stood, and the standing cost him. The crown drank deeper every day now; he could feel it the way a man feels a leech working under his collarbone. His hands shook. Three days ago he could have carried Edmund over this ground. Today he leaned against a broken pillar and watched the western pass and waited for his brother to come home.

Because he knew. He knew the way you know your own name.

* * *

They came down out of the pass at dusk, which was no longer distinguishable from any other hour, the sky being a seamless lid of black. Torches burned along the column's length — dozens of them, and the flame of each one bent *inward*, toward the center of the procession, as though the air itself were being drawn into someone's lungs.

At the head rode a man in silvered mail without heraldry, on a white horse too fine for the ash-choked road. And behind him —

Behind him walked his shadow.

Quentin had heard the shepherd tell it in the firelit dark at Hollowmere, had heard it as a fireside fright: *the Usurper King whose shadow moves independently, whose court never sleeps.* He had half-believed it, the way one believes ghost stories, politely, with the rational mind already filing the tale away.

The shadow moved wrong. It lagged a half-step behind its owner and then caught up too fast, like a puppet remembering its strings late. When the king raised his hand to signal halt, the shadow raised its hand a heartbeat afterward — and kept it raised a heartbeat longer. It was taller than he was, too. Taller than any man, and thin, and its head was crowned whether or not the king above it wore one.

Edmund pressed close against Quentin's shoulder. "That's him? That's Aldric?"

"Yes." Quentin's mouth was dry enough to crack. Twenty years since he'd seen his brother, and twenty years had worn Aldric Halloran-into-something-else the way water wears stone. The face was still his brother's face. The face smiled the same smile — the one from the coronation morning, warm and easy and full of love, right up until the moment the molten gold touched Quentin's brow and Aldric had held his head steady so the priests could pour true.

"He'll want to talk," Edmund murmured. "Men like that always want to talk first. It's cheaper than fighting."

"And if I listen?"

Her hand found his wrist, grip fierce. "Then listen. But Quentin — whatever he offers — remember there is nothing in this world he gives freely. Nothing."

The column halted two hundred paces below them among the broken colonnades. One rider came forward alone.

* * *

Aldric dismounted at the foot of the steps where they stood, and for a long moment neither brother moved.

Up close, the wrongness was worse. Aldric's face was unlined. That was the detail that undid Quentin most thoroughly — their father had gone grey at thirty, and Aldric had been prone to laughter-lines even as a boy, but the face beneath the crown of office (a lesser crown, a working crown, gold wire and iron) was smooth as poured wax. Only the eyes were tired. Only the eyes were still human.

"Quentin," Aldric said. His voice hadn't changed either. "You look terrible."

"You should see the other fellow," Quentin managed.

Aldric laughed — actually laughed, a real sound, startled out of him, and for one vertiginous second they were boys again in the palace gardens, trading insults over stolen apples. Then the laugh died and the tiredness came back and the shadow behind the king shifted its weight with a sound like sand sliding.

"I'm not going to pretend," Aldric said. "You've earned better than pretending, running the way you did. Living, the way you have. So: plainly. You know what's on your head. You've known since the pass, I think — the dreams would have started by now."

"The first king," Quentin said. "It has a name?"

"It had names. It's older than names we'd recognize. We called it the Patron when I was young. The temple calls it the Benefactor. It doesn't care what it's called. It cares about vessels." Aldric spread his hands, and the gesture was so familiar, so *Aldric* — the open-handed reasonableness of a boy explaining why the punishment was really a kindness — that Quentin's stomach turned. "Yours is failing. You've maybe a season left. Less, the closer you get to the old capital; it feeds harder near its seat. I've watched three others go the way you're going. It isn't gentle, Quentin. At the end it takes everything — your memories first, then your name, then whatever's left. You wake up one morning and you're a coat it's wearing."

"I know," Quentin said quietly. "I dreamed it. It showed me."

Something flickered across Aldric's face — guilt, perhaps, or the memory of guilt, some fossil feeling preserved in the wax. "Then you know I'm not lying to you now."

"Say it anyway. Say what you came to say."

The Usurper King of Thornfell looked at his brother for a long moment. Behind him, torches guttered inward. Above, the starless sky pressed down like a lid on a pot.

"Give yourself up," Aldric said. "Walk down to it willingly. Kneel at the high altar in the capital and let it take you cleanly instead of gnawing at you for a season. In exchange—" and here his voice did something Quentin had never heard it do, something small and raw, "—in exchange I give you back your body. Your own body. Unmarked. Whole. The crown comes off, the scars close, the hunger stops. You walk out of the temple a free man with forty years ahead of him and nothing on his brow but skin."

"That's not possible," Edmund said sharply, from beside him. "Nothing unbinds that—"

"*I* can," Aldric said, and did not look at her. "It will allow it. For me. It allows me certain things, in payment." He touched his own smooth face, briefly, as if checking it was still attached. "Do you understand what I'm offering you, Quentin? Not mercy — I don't expect you to believe in my mercy. Arithmetic. It wants a vessel that lasts another century instead of another season. You are worth more to it healthy. And I am worth more to it with you delivered. Everyone profits. Everyone walks away. Even her—" now he glanced at Edmund, and his eyes were shrewd and knowing and terrible, "—even the scribe with the forged seals and the debt-contract and the family in Thornfell city. There's paper in my train that burns debts. Any debt. Hers. Her family's freedom, bought and sealed tonight, if she helps me persuade you."

Quentin felt Edmund go very still against his side. Felt her breath catch — one small, involuntary sound, quickly killed.

And that was the cruelest stroke of all, he thought dimly. Not the offer to him. The offer to *her*, laid out in front of him, so that refusing meant watching her refuse her own salvation for his sake.

"Aldric." He made his voice steady. "What did it take from you to make you its voice?"

His brother's composure slipped — just a hairline crack, just for an instant. "That's not—"

"Your shadow moves wrong. Your face never changes. Your court never sleeps because none of them can, can they? What did you pay, Aldric? What do you keep paying?" He took one step down, and the crown on his skull sang its low hungry note, and the shadow far below lifted its head as if scenting him. "Was it worth it? Being the hand instead of the glove?"

"I kept us alive!" The crack widened; Aldric's voice broke into something twenty years younger. "Father was dead, the lords were circling, the treasury was empty, the temple owned half the kingdom's paper — you were going to be crowned into a meat-grinder, Quentin, and *it* offered a different road. Yes, I paid. I've been paying for twenty years. But the kingdom stands. The roads are safe. Nobody starves. You'd have burned it all down being righteous, and I chose the kingdom over my brother, and I have never once—" his breath hitched, "—never once slept a whole night through since. Do you think I don't know what I am?"

For a moment — a single, swaying moment — Quentin almost pitied him enough to weep.

Almost knelt.

That was the horror of it, he realized later. Not the fear, not the pain in his skull, not even the promise of his own body back. It was the *ease*. All winter he had bled and run and frozen and starved, and here stood a door out of all of it, held open by the only person whose forgiveness he had ever wanted. Kneel, and be healed. Kneel, and stop being hunted. Kneel, and the burning weight would lift and his hands would stop shaking and he would sleep, truly sleep, for the first time since the coronation morning when his brother had held his head steady while the gold was poured.

Kneel, and become a coat.

He saw it suddenly, whole and clear, the way Edmund must have seen ledgers all her life: the arithmetic underneath. The crown-thing did not bargain. It harvested. A willing vessel fed it better — surrender was seasoning. And his body returned to him, unmarked and whole? A gift with a hook in it. It would take him back whenever it pleased, from a healthy body, in a healthy kingdom, with nowhere left to run and no one left who remembered why he'd run. Aldric wasn't offering him freedom. Aldric was offering him a well-fed lamb's last hour in a green field.

And beside him, Edmund had not spoken. Had not moved. She stood with her jaw set and her hand on her knife and her family's freedom dangled in front of her like a lantern over deep water, and she waited — not for his answer. For *his* choice. Because she would follow whatever he chose, and they both knew it, and that knowledge was the only thing in the world heavier than the crown.

"Brother," Quentin said gently. "Take your paper about my friend's debts and burn it yourself."

Aldric closed his eyes. "Quentin—"

"No. Listen, because I'll only say this once." Every word cost him; the crown had begun to pull, sensing prey, and the shadow at the bottom of the steps had begun to drift forward without its king. "I spent all winter hating you. It was the warmest thing I owned. But I know now what's wearing your face, and I know what it does to the men who serve it, and I am not going to kneel to save my skin and spend my eternity feeding it. You want my surrender? Come and take it. But hear me, Aldric — the thing you serve ate the stars. *Our* stars. The ones Father used to name for us on the summer terrace. Whatever's left of my brother is welcome to come home. The rest of you can burn with it."

Silence, except for the wind and the inward-bending flames.

When Aldric opened his eyes again, they were dry and smooth as the rest of him, and Quentin understood that whatever lived behind them had finished listening.

"Pity," the Usurper King said softly. "It wanted you willing. It will make do."

The shadow moved.

Not stepped — *moved*, pouring off the ash like spilled ink running uphill, and it was fast, faster than anything that shapeless had any right to be, and its crowned head turned toward Edmund first—

Quentin threw himself between them, arms wide, crown screaming, and felt the shadow hit him like a wave of absolute cold — felt it *recognize* him, hesitate, hunger — and then Edmund's knife was flashing firelight and the vault-door beneath their feet was booming open (how — later, later) and the mountain itself groaned as ancient wards woke along the ruined halls, blue-white light flooding the colonnade.

Through the cold and the noise, the last thing Quentin saw clearly was his brother's face, turning away, and his brother's voice — small, almost human, almost lost under the rising roar:

"Seal the ruins. No one leaves. I'm sorry, Quentin. I was always sorry."

Then the floor tilted, the vault swallowed them, and the stars that weren't there pressed down like a closing fist.
