## 19. Stars Returning

The first star came back while Edmund was still holding him.

Quentin did not see it at first — he could not have seen anything, not through the white roar that had swallowed the world when the crown cracked. But he felt it. That was the strange thing, the thing he would carry for the rest of his ordinary, unremarkable, unbearably precious life: he *felt* it arrive, like a single warm drop falling onto a desert that had forgotten rain.

He was lying on his back on the shattered slope of the summer palace's mountain, and Edmund's hands were pressed to his face, and her voice was coming from very far away and very close at once — *Quentin, Quentin, look at me, look at me* — and then the drop of warmth landed somewhere behind his eyes, in a place the crown had hollowed out so long ago he had never known it existed.

Then another drop. Then another.

"Edmund," he said, or tried to say. His mouth was full of blood and gold dust. "The sky."

"I know." Her voice broke on it. "I know. Look."

He looked.

The peak above them had been torn open by the unbinding — he would learn later how she had done it, with ink and declaration and a scribe's cold fury turned into something holy — and through the wound in the mountain poured a light that was not firelight, not sorcery, not any of the borrowed gleams the world had made do with for twenty years. It came down silver and patient. And above it, in the black that had been absolute since the night they met, one star burned.

Just one. Small, blue-white, trembling as though it too had just woken from a long suffocation.

Quentin stared at it and wept without meaning to, without deciding to, the way a man weeps when a tourniquet comes off a wound he'd stopped believing could heal.

* * *

The second star came an hour later, low over the western fells. The third was red, and Quentin remembered it — remembered it the way you remember a word from a language spoken in childhood, without knowing what it means anymore. *Vashen's Eye*, his mother had called it, pointing from the nursery window of the old capital, telling him that kings were born under it and fools died under it, and little Quentin had asked which he intended to be, and she had laughed until the candle guttered.

He had no mother now. He had no nursery window. He had no crown — the thought arrived and kept arriving, each time fresh, each time with its own small earthquake attached. He raised his hand to his brow and found skin. Scarred skin, ridged and tender where the gold had lived, a seam of shining scar tissue circling his head like the ghost of a circlet, but skin. His own. Warm under his fingertips. Bleeding a little where the shattering had cut him.

No weight.

Twenty years of weight, and now nothing, and the nothing was so vast he did not know how a skull could hold itself up without it.

"It's gone," he said. His voice sounded ruined, scraped raw by screaming — he had screamed, he realized dimly; the last thing before the white roar had been the first king's voice pouring out through his throat, centuries of stolen starlight burning off in one long shriek, and beneath it his own voice, and beneath *that* — Edmund's, steady as a ledger line, declaring the debt paid, paid, *paid*.

"Gone." Edmund knelt beside him in the rubble. She was filthy, scorched, one sleeve burned away to the elbow, glass dust glittering in her cropped hair. She looked, he thought hazily, like someone who had argued with a mountain and won on points. She was also crying, quietly, the way she did everything — efficiently, as if tears were a task to be completed and filed. "All of it gone. I checked twice."

"You checked my skull twice."

"I check everything twice. You knew this about me." She wiped her eyes with the back of her wrist, smearing soot across her cheekbone, and looked at him with an expression he had no name for yet. He suspected he would spend years finding one. "How do you feel?"

How did he feel. He took inventory the way she had taught him, back on the high passes, when she'd made him co-author of their route: start with the body, work outward.

His body: wrecked. Every joint ached; the places where the crown had fed nightly were raw, as if teeth marks ringed his soul; his brow throbbed where the scar tissue pulled. But the ache was *his*. That was the miracle buried inside the misery. For twenty years every pain had been shared, siphoned, reported upward to the thing wearing his crown, every ounce of his strength measured and taxed by something ancient and hungry. Now the pain belonged to Quentin alone, entire, undivided, and he loved it the way a freed man loves even the cold.

Outward: Edmund, alive, kneeling in the ruin she had made. The mountain, broken open, breathing silver. And below —

Below, in the vale where the summer palace had rotted for two decades, torchlight. Many torches. And banners, and the sound of horses, and a voice amplified by sorcery rolling up the slopes like a tide of oil.

The Usurper had come after all.

* * *

They heard the decree from the mouth of the vault, where they had dragged themselves to shelter among the fallen stones. Edmund wanted to run deeper — there were chambers below, she said, passages, the first king's hoard went down farther than any map showed — but Quentin caught her wrist.

"Wait." He tilted his head, listening. "Listen to what he's saying."

The voice rolled up the mountainside, grand and resonant, the voice of a man who had practiced majesty in mirrors for twenty years:

"—and let it be recorded that the pretender Quentin, traitor to the realm, is hereby stripped of name and line, that his lands revert to the crown, that his—"

The decree faltered.

It was small, at first. A hitch, as if the speaker had mislaid a word. Then another hitch. Then a sound none of them had ever heard a king make: a wet, rattling confusion, the sound of a man whose own mouth had stopped obeying him.

"What—" the Usurper said, to no one. "What is — where is it. Where *is* it—"

And then, faintly but unmistakably across the night air, came the sound of a shadow screaming.

Quentin had seen the Usurper's shadow only once, from a distance — moving wrong, moving late, lagging behind its owner like a dog on a leash held by someone else. He understood now, with the clarity of the newly unburdened, what that shadow had been: not a corruption, not a curiosity. A conduit. A root. The crown-thing had grown its power down through the usurper's line the way ivy grows through a wall, holding the whole rotten edifice upright, feeding it borrowed light, and Edmund had just burned out the root of it with a declaration and a case full of stars.

On the valley floor, the torches scattered. Horses screamed. Men who had come expecting to witness a coronation of ashes instead watched their king stand rigid in the light of returning stars and begin, gently, horribly, to come apart.

They crept to the edge of the overlook — Edmund insisting on cover, Quentin too weak to argue and too compelled to refuse — and looked down.

The Usurper King of Thornfell stood in the center of his shattered court with his arms half-raised, mid-gesture, frozen in the shape of a decree. And he was crumbling. Not falling, not dying — *crumbling*, the way a statue crumbles, the way dry bread crumbles, fine grey flakes lifting from his hands and face and robes and drifting upward on a wind that touched nothing else. His guards had fled to a respectful distance and stood watching in a silence more terrible than any battle-cry. His banner-bearers had dropped the banners.

His face, in the last moments, was not monstrous. That was the worst of it. It was the face of Quentin's older brother, aged twenty hard years — bewildered, reaching, mouth shaping words that flaked away before they could become sound. For one instant his eyes seemed to find the dark shape of the two watchers on the ridge, and Quentin saw in them not hatred, not triumph, but the naked animal terror of a boy who had wanted the throne so badly he had never asked what sat underneath it.

Then the wind took the rest of him, and Aldric — for he had been Aldric once, before he was the Usurper, before he had smiled at his little brother across a coronation dais and signed the working that fused molten gold to a child's skull — blew away between the returning stars, and there was nothing on the valley floor but banners, and torches, and men slowly learning how to kneel to no one.

* * *

Afterward, Quentin sat with his back against a fallen pillar and shook.

Not from cold, though the altitude cold was real enough, biting through his ruined coat. He shook the way ground shakes after an earthquake has already done its worst — aftershocks of a grief he couldn't locate, because it had no proper object. His brother was dead. Had his brother been dead already? Had anything of Aldric remained inside that courtly shell, or had the crown-thing worn him out decades ago, the way it had meant to wear out Quentin, vessel after vessel, heir after heir, a dynasty of lanterns burned down to nothing?

He had hated his brother for twenty years. He had built himself out of that hate the way a prisoner builds a wall. And now the hate had nothing left to press against, and all that remained was a memory of a boy teaching him to skip stones on Greywater, laughing, golden, doomed — doomed even then, chosen even then, by the thing beneath the throne.

"He didn't deserve your tears," Edmund said quietly, settling beside him. She offered him a waterskin. "But I think you're allowed to cry anyway."

"He was eight when I was born." Quentin drank. The water tasted impossibly clean. "He used to carry me on his shoulders through the palace gardens. He told me the hedges were a maze built to keep out giants." A breath that was almost a laugh. "I keep waiting to feel that he got what he deserved. I don't feel it. I just feel — empty where the hate used to live."

"Good," said Edmund.

He turned to look at her. Soot-streaked, exhausted, contractless at last and not yet knowing what to be instead, she gazed out at the valley with her jaw set in the particular way that meant she was about to say something true and had decided not to soften it.

"Empty is where things grow," she said. "I had an empty where my family used to be, for six years. Everything I am grew in it. Hate fills you up and holds your shape, but it doesn't feed you. It's just — scaffolding." She glanced at him, and the corner of her mouth moved. "You're going to have to figure out who you are with no scaffold at all. No crown. No claim. No brother to define yourself against. No temple owning you, no rebels wanting to hang you from a wall like a flag." Her voice gentled, which for Edmund meant it dropped half a register and slowed. "Who is Quentin, when nobody wants anything from him?"

He considered the question seriously, because she deserved seriousness, and because he had genuinely no idea.

"A man," he said at last, "who is very cold, extremely tired, and in love with a scribe."

She blinked. In the new starlight — three stars now, five, a scatter of them climbing the eastern dark like lamps being lit room by room in some enormous house — he watched color rise in her face, watched her decide whether to deflect, watched her decide not to.

"Well," she said. "That's a start. Write it down. We'll revise in spring."

* * *

The stars kept coming.

By midnight there were dozens. By the hour before dawn, hundreds — and the people in the valley below had stopped being soldiers and courtiers and had become simply people, standing in the open with their faces tipped back, weeping, singing, silent. Someone started a fire. Someone else began, tentatively, an old song that every Thornfell child knew and no adult had dared sing in twenty years, a song about the Shepherd's Lamp and the Nine Sisters and the road home lit by heaven's own candles. By the time the sky reached its full, unbearable abundance — thousands upon thousands, the Milky Way pouring across the zenith like a river remembering its course — half the camp below was singing it, badly, through tears.

Quentin sang it too. His voice cracked on every third note and he did not care.

He had forgotten, truly forgotten, what a full sky did to a person. Under the starless years the night had been a lid, a ceiling, a blindfold. Now it was a depth again — he could feel its distance in his chest, could feel how large the world was and how little of it was a throne. Somewhere up there, he thought, were all the lights the first king had eaten. Centuries of them. They had not been destroyed, only hoarded, sealed in glass and flesh by a creature so old it had forgotten it was once a man who must also, once, have looked up at this same river of light and felt small in the good way.

Edmund had given it all back. Every coin of it. She had stood in front of enough starlight to buy her family's freedom a hundred times over — freedom, for the Hallorans, forever, the one thing she had crossed a kingdom and killed a man and burned her own name to win — and she had spent it on him. On unmaking a crown. On lighting a sky for strangers she would never meet.

"You're staring at me," she said, not looking away from the heavens.

"I'm told it's customary."

"It's wasteful. The sky's right there."

"The sky will keep." He shifted closer, shoulder against hers, and felt her lean into the contact by a degree so slight anyone else would have missed it. He had learned to read Edmund the way she read ledgers: line by line, margin to margin. "Edmund. What happens now?"

She was quiet for a long moment. Below, the song rose and fell. Above, the Nine Sisters climbed toward the meridian, exactly where his mother's finger had pointed, twenty-three years and one lifetime ago.

"Now," she said finally, "the lords of Thornfell wake up tomorrow in a kingdom with no king, no heir, and no idea what to do about either. And some of them are going to remember that a dead man's brother walked out of these mountains alive. Some of them are going to want a symbol. A restoration. A story with a crown in it, because crowns are easier than governing." She turned her head and looked at him, and the starlight lay in her eyes like frost. "They're going to come looking for you, Quentin. Within the week. Possibly within the day."

"Let them come," he said, and found, testing the words, that he meant them. "Let them bring their banners. I have nothing left to give a throne and nothing left to take from one. I'm not an heir. I'm not a symbol. I'm—" he laughed, soft and wondering, at the strangeness of it— "I'm a free man with a scar around his head and no prospects whatsoever."

"Mm. Terrible marriage material," Edmund said.

He went very still.

She did not look at him. She studied the sky with tremendous scholarly attention, ears pink, jaw set, and said, in the tone she used for correcting a misfiled entry: "I only mention it because I've recently come into a surplus of freedom myself, and I find I don't know what to do with it, and I've been thinking — purely as a matter of logistics — that two people with nothing might manage better than one. If the other person happened to be you. Which it does."

Above them, unhurried, magnificent, the stars went on returning, one by one by one, and Quentin Yarrow — no title, no throne, no crown but the pale seam of scar where one had been ripped from his skull — began, helplessly, to laugh, and then discovered that the laughter had turned into something else, and that Edmund Halloran was kissing him under the first full sky either of them had seen in twenty years.

Far below, in the valley, a rider was already saddling a horse.
