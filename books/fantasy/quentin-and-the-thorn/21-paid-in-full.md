## 21. Paid in Full

The temple of Thornfell had always looked to Edmund like a fist closed around the mountain's throat. She saw it now from the ridge above the pilgrim road — grey walls, grey spire, the bell tower where she'd spent three years copying other people's ruin into ledgers by candlelight — and it did not look like a fist anymore. It looked like a hand that had forgotten what it was holding.

The stars were back. That was the thing no one below had yet understood, or perhaps they understood and could not speak of it, because a sky full of returned stars was a confession written in fire across the whole vault of heaven. Every light up there was a debt the crown-thing had stolen and never meant to repay. Now they hung in their old constellations — the Ploughman, the Weeping Sisters, the Wolf that the shepherds said chased winter down into the valleys — and Edmund stood on the ridge with her hood down for the first time in weeks, letting them look at her.

She was not the same woman who had crawled out of the crypt passage on the night the sky went dark. She knew that the way you know your own handwriting has changed: same letters, different pressure. Her hands were scarred now. One fingernail would never grow back properly. There was a line across her cheekbone from a hunter's blade in the scree, thin as a scribe's rule drawn carelessly. And in the pocket of her coat, wrapped in three layers of wool against her ribs, sat a handful of starlight.

Not enough to buy a loaf of bread in any honest market, if honest markets traded in such things. Enough, she judged — she had spent the whole descent judging, weighing it in her palm like a coin whose value only she could read — to burn one page thoroughly. Contracts were her trade. She knew exactly how much fire it took to unmake ink.

She started down toward the gates.

* * *

The temple guards did not stop her. That was the first strangeness. She had rehearsed a dozen ways past them — the forged seal still tucked in her boot, though its edges were soft now with handling; the story about returning debts; even, in the darkest rehearsal, the memory of how to break a man's grip the way Quentin had taught her, turning with the force instead of against it. But the gate stood open. The guards inside were not guarding anything. Two of them were carrying pews out into the courtyard and stacking them like cordwood, and a third sat on the mounting block eating an apple, watching the mountains with the vacant patience of a man waiting for orders that were never coming again.

"Gate's open," the apple-eater said, as if announcing the weather. "Has been since the mountain shook."

Edmund walked through the courtyard she had crossed ten thousand times with her eyes down and her shoulders curved inward, making herself small the way property learns to make itself small. She kept her eyes down out of habit for the first dozen steps. Then she lifted them, because she had earned the right to see this place clearly, and seeing it clearly was half of what she had come to do.

The temple was coming apart.

Not collapsing — dismantling. She saw it everywhere once she knew how to look. Scaffolding clung to the eastern tower, and workmen were prying off the lead flashing with the unhurried competence of men salvaging a ship. The great doors of the nave stood wide, and through them she glimpsed acolytes rolling up the carpet that ran the length of the aisle, the carpet on which she had knelt at eleven years old while her father signed the first contract and the priest's voice had said, gently, *the debt is assumed; the child is security.* Someone had already taken down the silver lamps. The hooks they'd hung from made little dark constellations of their own against the whitewash, empty circles where light used to live.

And the altar — the altar was bare. The obsidian slab remained, but the reliquary that had crowned it for as long as anyone could remember, the black iron box that held the fragment of the First Crown, the relic every high priest had sworn was blessed by the thing in the mountain itself — gone. In its place someone had left a candle stub, burned down to nothing, wax frozen mid-drip like a small white scream.

Her patron was dead. The whole temple knew it, even if none of them could say it aloud. You could dismantle a god's house only after the god stopped answering.

"Brother Halloran." The voice came from behind her, dry as ledger dust, and Edmund turned slowly, letting him see her face without flinching. High Priest Aldous Vane stood at the top of the nave steps in his grey vestments, and he had aged a decade in six weeks. His hands, which had never trembled when they pressed seals into wax over the terms of a family's bondage, trembled now. He looked at her — at the stubbled jaw she no longer bothered to deepen, at the line on her cheek, at her eyes, which she let be exactly as tired and exactly as unafraid as they were — and something moved through his face that might have been recognition or might have been dread. "So it's true," he said. "They say a woman walked out of the mountain with the prince."

"They say a lot of things," Edmund said. "Mostly I wrote them myself, so I'd know."

"The lords are coming. All of them, within the fortnight, to argue over—" He stopped himself, and his jaw worked. Over the throne. Over the vacancy. Over the shape of power when the power underneath it had been pulled away like a tablecloth. "You should not have come back, Halloran. Whatever you think is owed to you—"

"That," Edmund said, "is precisely what we're going to settle."

She walked past him up the nave, and he did not order the guards to stop her, because there were no guards left who answered to him, and because some animal part of him already understood what she carried. She smelled it before she saw it: the archive room, third door on the left, the room where she had spent three years of her life breathing dust and lamp oil, and under those familiar smells something else — smoke, old and cold. The shelves nearest the door were scorched. Someone had tried to burn the records.

Someone had failed, because contracts are stubborn things, and hers most of all.

* * *

The ledger room looked smaller than it had in her memory, the way all prisons do once the door stands open. Rows of shelves. Rows of ledgers, their spines cracked, their pages fat with the names of families who had come to Thornfell temple hungry or frightened or grieving and left bound. The Halloran account lived in the third case from the window, fourth shelf, red leather, and Edmund found it without looking for it, the way her hand still reached for a lantern that wasn't there.

She set the ledger on the central table. Opened it to the page. There it all was, in her own neat copyist's hand — oh, the irony of it sang in her like a struck bell. She had recopied her own contract two years ago, when the temple reorganized its archives, and the clerk assigned to verify the transcription had been her, disguised as a boy named Edmund Halloran who did not exist, checking the accuracy of her own enslavement. Principal sum: forty marks, borrowed against her father's cooperage when the blight took the barley crop. Interest compounded at the temple rate. Security tendered: one daughter, Edmund, aged eleven, service until the debt is discharged.

Forty marks. A family's freedom had cost less than a good horse. That was the arithmetic of temples, and she had copied it out in fair hand for six years without ever once being permitted to write the word *unjust* in a margin.

Aldous Vane stood in the doorway. Behind him, hesitantly, gathered a few of the older clerks — Brother Merek, who had taught her her letters and never asked why a boy's wrists were so fine; Sister Anwen, who ran the infirmary and had stitched Edmund's split lip once and said nothing, only *mind the quill angle next time*, which Edmund had understood even then for the kindness it was. They came into the room like people entering a tomb, or leaving one.

"You can't," Vane said. "Whatever you're thinking. The contract is a sacred instrument. It binds in law and in—"

"In law," Edmund agreed. "That's the word I wanted. Law." She unwrapped the wool from her ribs, layer by layer, and the starlight came into view, and the room changed.

It was hard to describe what a shard of returned starlight does to a dim room. It didn't brighten it, exactly. It *remembered* brightness at it. The lamp brackets on the walls seemed briefly to recall the flames they'd held. The faces of the clerks went young and then old again, flickering between the people they'd been and the people they'd become. And the red ledger lay under that remembered light like something awaiting judgment, which was, Edmund thought, exactly what it was.

"Where did you get that?" Vane whispered.

"From the First King's own vault." She let him hear it plainly. "From beneath the summer palace, where your patron kept centuries of stolen sky bottled up like a miser's hoard. The stars didn't die, Aldous. They were eaten. By the thing you served. By the thing whose blessing you swore over these contracts." She touched the page. "Every family bound in this room was bound with stolen light as collateral. Did you know that? The old binding-rites call the stars to witness. I've read the rite. I performed it often enough, copying it out. *Let the lights above bear record.* Well. The lights above are back now, and I've brought a piece of one down to collect."

Vane's face had gone the color of the ash in the grate. "The temple will restore everything," he said quickly, and there it was, the true voice under the vestments, the merchant's voice, the voice that had negotiated her family's bondage in the first place. "Your family — discharged, fully, formally, I'll write it now, today, with witnesses—"

"No."

The word came out quieter than she expected, and steadier. The clerks had gone very still. Somewhere outside, the workmen were levering another pew into the courtyard, wood groaning, and the sound came through the window like the temple's own joints creaking as it let go of itself.

"No," Edmund said again. "Because that's the trap, don't you see? If you discharge my debt, then the temple freed me. Then I owe the temple. Then there's a new entry in a new ledger, and in twenty years someone's daughter sits in this room copying it out. I'm done being an entry." She picked up the largest shard of starlight. It weighed almost nothing and more than a mountain, both at once, the way all true things weigh. "I'm here to close the account myself."

"It won't burn," Vane said desperately. "Contract vellum is treated — it resists flame, it's warded, it's—"

"I know. I helped treat it." She smiled, and felt the scar on her cheekbone pull, and did not care. "Wards need a patron, Aldous. Yours stopped answering the night the mountain shook. This isn't ordinary fire." She laid the shard on the center of the page, over the words *security tendered*, and spoke the closing formula — not the temple's formula, which invoked obedience, but the smugglers' one, which invoked passage, the little litany her mother had murmured over maps before every run through the passes: *the road is paid, the road is free, walk on.*

The starlight caught.

There was no smoke. That was the uncanny thing, the thing she would remember longest. Fire without smoke, burning downward into the vellum like water into sand, and where it burned, the ink didn't char — it *lifted*, rising off the page letter by letter, each character glowing gold for one instant before it came apart into motes that drifted upward toward the ceiling and, she chose to believe, upward beyond it, home to the sky they'd been stolen from. She watched her own name unwrite itself. *Edmund.* Gone. *Aged eleven.* Gone. *Security.* Gone, and the room seemed to breathe out, all of them at once, clerks and priest alike, a long collective exhalation like wind through the passes.

When it was finished, the page was blank. Not scorched. Blank — clean as the day the vellum was stretched, holding nothing, owing nothing. Forty marks and six years and a childhood, erased by a handful of light.

Edmund's legs decided, without consulting her, to sit down. She lowered herself onto the bench beside the table and discovered her hands were shaking, and let them, and laughed once, a strange cracked sound that echoed oddly in the little room.

"Well," said Brother Merek, into the silence. He had gone to the shelf, and he was pulling down ledger after ledger, the whole row of them, the whole terrible library, and stacking them on the table with the deliberation of a man building a pyre. "I don't suppose anyone kept any more of that light."

Sister Anwen was already reaching for the second shelf.

* * *

They burned accounts until dawn.

It took some organizing — the shards had to be rationed, one page at a time, and Anwen appointed herself treasurer of the remaining starlight with the fierce competence of a woman who had spent thirty years rationing medicine. Word spread through the temple the way fire spreads through dry grass. Families began arriving before midnight — not summoned, just *knowing*, the way people know things now that the wards were down and the truth was loose in the world like a scent on the wind. A cooper's widow with three children, clutching a copy of her contract wrapped in oilcloth. Two brothers who had been bound since before Edmund's birth, grown grey in temple service, standing in the doorway of the archive room unable to cross the threshold until she waved them in like a hostess.

By the time the sky went grey and then rose-gold over the eastern peaks, the archive room's shelves stood empty, and the courtyard outside was drifting paper — no, not paper. Ash that glittered. The wind off the fells was lifting the burned ink of six hundred debts and carrying it up toward the mountains, and Edmund stood in the doorway watching it go, and every mote that caught the sunrise flashed gold, so that for one impossible moment it looked as though it were snowing stars.

Vane came to her there. He looked hollowed out, wrung, a man whose house had fallen and who was only beginning to count what remained. But he bowed to her. Actually bowed — a small motion, stiff, but unmistakable.

"What happens to you now?" he asked. "To any of us? The treasury is gutted, the patron is dust, the lords are coming with their armies and their claims. The temple cannot pay its own servants, let alone—" He gestured at everything. "We built all of this on the assumption that the light above was infinite and ours to spend. What do we build on now?"

Edmund considered him for a long moment. Six years of fear sat between them, and she found, examining it, that she didn't want to carry it any further than this doorstep.

"You build on what people owe each other," she said, "when nobody owns anybody. It's a harder ledger to keep. No interest compounds. But it balances." She dug in her coat and pressed the last shard of starlight into his trembling hand — the smallest one, the crumb she'd saved against some need she couldn't name. "For the infirmary debts. Anwen knows the formula. And Aldous — teach the new clerks to write the word *unjust* in margins. It's a start."

He stared at the light in his palm like a man handed his own heart back, and Edmund turned and walked out through the gates, past the stacked pews, past the apple-eating guard who touched his forehead to her — to *her*, to the woman in the boy's coat with the scarred face — and down the pilgrim road into the morning.

Quentin was waiting at the milestone where the road bent toward the low country, sitting on the stone like any traveler resting his feet, crownless, his brow bearing only the pale star-shaped scar where the binding had shattered. He didn't ask how it went. He could read it in her walk; she saw him read it, saw the answer arrive in him like sun through a window.

"Well?" he said anyway, because some questions you ask just to hear the answer spoken aloud.

"Paid in full," Edmund said. "Everything. Everyone. There's nothing left in that building but ash and honesty." She sat down beside him on the milestone, shoulder against his shoulder, and looked back once at the temple — smaller than ever from here, a grey toy against the vast green shoulder of the mountain, its scaffolds swarming with men taking it apart nail by nail. "They'll tear it down by autumn. Maybe they'll plant barley on the ground. Real barley, this time."

"And you?" Quentin said. "What does a free woman with no debts and no disguise do with a morning?"

Edmund looked east, where the road ran down out of the mountains into the wide world — into valleys she'd only mapped from rumor, towns she'd only seen as entries in other people's accounts. Somewhere down there her parents were waking in a house that belonged to them, truly theirs, for the first time in fifteen years. Somewhere down there the lords were riding with their quarrels and their armies toward a throne nobody sane should want. And somewhere along those roads were people fleeing the collapse, refugees and debtors and runaways, all of them needing what she alone in all Thornfell knew how to draw: a way through.

"I was thinking," she said slowly, "that the maps are all wrong. Every pass, every ford, every smuggler's cut. Half of them died with the shepherds who knew them, and the rest the ward-collapse has rearranged entirely. Somebody ought to remap the whole range. Properly. For everyone." She glanced sideways at him, and found him already smiling. "It's slow work. Years of it. Terrible roads, worse inns."

"I've got time," Quentin said. "I'm not heir to anything."

"No," Edmund said, and laced her fingers through his, and stood, and turned her face to the open road with the whole returned sky blazing overhead like a witness finally telling the truth. "Neither am I. Come on — we're burning daylight, and daylight's free."
