## 22. The Road They Make Together

*Edmund*

Spring came to the Thornfells the way forgiveness comes — slowly, and then all at once.

Edmund noticed it first in the water. All winter the smuggler's streams had run silent under ice, and she had learned to read their buried courses by the shape of the snow above them, the way a scar tells the story of a wound. Then one morning in the third week of thaw, she woke in the hollow beneath the split boulder they called home-for-now and heard water moving everywhere at once — a hundred small voices under the snow, all of them saying the same thing: *open, open, open.*

By noon the first crocuses had broken through the scree by the stream, purple and absurd against all that grey, and Edmund had knelt down in the mud to look at them properly, because she had not seen anything grow in a year that had not been grown in fear.

Quentin found her there. He said nothing — he had learned that about her, that she needed silence the way other people needed reassurance — but he crouched beside her and turned one of the blooms toward the light with a forefinger gone brown at the nail from road-dust, and together they watched it tremble in the wind like something deciding whether to stay.

They stayed too. That was the difference this spring made. For eleven months they had been a pair of arrows in flight, and now, for the first time since the night the stars went out, they were somewhere rather than *toward* somewhere.

The valley below them filled slowly with people. Refugees came down out of the high passes where the war's last cruelties had driven them — families from the burned holdings along the Greywater, weavers from Ashfall Vale whose fields would not green for a generation, temple orphans with the Thornfell sun-wheel scraped off their wooden charms. And every one of them needed roads. Not the king's roads, which were rubble, nor the temple's processional ways, which were being pulled up stone by stone now that the temple had no god left to serve. They needed the old roads. The hidden ones. The ones that existed only in the memory of a woman who had spent six years copying debt contracts by candlelight and drawing smuggler paths in the margins where no overseer would think to look.

So Edmund Halloran did what she knew how to do. She made maps.

She worked at a plank table Quentin had dragged from the ruins of the summer palace, sanded smooth and propped level on stones, and she drew with charcoal and berry-ink on anything that would hold a line: birch bark, cured hide, the backs of old temple ledgers she bought by the sackful from the dismantling priests, who were glad to be rid of them. She drew the Widow's Teeth goat-track with its three false summits. She drew the ford below Hollowmere that ran knee-deep in April and waist-deep in May. She drew the crypt passage out of Thornfell temple itself, because there were still people inside those walls who needed to leave, and she remembered what it was to need a door no one else could see.

And in the margins, where once she had hidden her escape routes from her own masters, she drew small things now. Crocuses. A crown with a line struck through it. Two figures walking, rendered so simply they might have been anyone, which was the point, which was the whole point.

"You've given away half the mountain," Quentin said one evening, coming up behind her with his hands full of the season's first wild garlic. He set it down by her elbow and read over her shoulder the way he always did, his breath warm against her ear, his weight settling against her back as though her spine were a place he lived.

"I've given away none of it," she said. "You can't give away a road. It was never mine. I only borrowed the knowing of it."

"Modest scribe."

"Former scribe." She tapped the ledger page. "There's no contract anymore. No debt. I'm nobody's property, which means I'm finally allowed to be generous."

He laughed — that laugh she had coaxed out of him over a winter of bad jokes and worse trail food, the one that started careful and ended unguarded — and pressed his mouth to the top of her head, and outside the fire crackled and the snowmelt sang and somewhere down-valley a child was shrieking with laughter at nothing, at everything, at being alive in spring.

* * *

*Quentin*

Quentin had been many things in his life — prince, prisoner, vessel, ghost — but he had never until this year been useful, and he found he liked it better than all the rest.

His body still carried the marks of the crown. In certain lights the scar across his brow shone faintly gold, a seam of metal in the skin where the molten circlet had fused and then shattered, and when the weather changed it ached like an old break. Some nights he dreamed of the first king — not screaming now, the way he had in the vault, but simply *present*, a vast cold attention at the edge of sleep, diminished to almost nothing, dwindling further each season as the stolen starlight burned out of him at last. Quentin had stopped fearing those dreams. There was nothing left in the old thing to envy. It had eaten the sky and kept itself alive for nine hundred years, and it had never once, as far as Quentin could tell, been happy.

Quentin was learning to be happy. It was harder work than kingship would have been, and far more rewarding.

In the mornings he walked the lower passes with the refugees' men and women, teaching them what Edmund's maps meant in the flesh — how to read a cornice before trusting it, how to find the cairns the smugglers had built generations ago, low and triple-stacked, invisible unless you knew to look. His hands remembered things his mind had lost; the mountains had held him even when he hadn't known his own name. Now he gave that knowledge back, freely, to people who would never kneel to him and never need to.

In the afternoons he hauled stone for the new waystation at the valley mouth, side by side with a mason from Greywater who had lost two sons in the ward-collapse and who talked, while they worked, about ordinary things — the price of nails, the stubbornness of goats, whether the spring lambs would come early. Nobody asked Quentin about the throne. The lords who had come seeking him in the autumn had gone away empty-handed and loud about it, and the story of the prince who refused his own crown had spread through the fells like woodsmoke, and here, among people who had owned nothing their whole lives, it was not madness. Here it sounded like sense.

But it was the evenings he lived for. The evenings belonged to Edmund.

She would finish her drawing when the light failed, blow the charcoal dust from the page, and come sit with him at the fire, and they would eat whatever the valley had given them that day, and she would tell him what she'd seen — not routes and elevations, but the world itself, catalogued in that dry precise voice that broke, always, at exactly the moments she most wanted it not to. The marmots were out of their dens. The ice on the Greywater had gone rotten overnight, dangerous and beautiful, all its winter secrets showing through. A hawk had ridden the thermals above the tollhouse ruin, and she had watched it for an hour and thought of nothing at all, and it had been the best hour of her week.

"And yours?" she would ask, because she always asked, because she had decided somewhere in the dark of that terrible winter that a man who had been consumed for twenty years deserved to be asked after daily, forever.

"Mine was the garlic," he said tonight. "And this. And you."

"Sentimental ex-prince."

"Former prince." He tapped her ledger, mirroring her gesture from the night before. "There's no throne anymore. I'm nobody's heir, which means I'm finally allowed to mean it."

She smiled at him across the fire, and the light moved on her face, and Quentin thought: *this is what the first king never had.* Not the gold, not the years, not the devoured sky. This. A person across a fire who chose you again every evening, with no contract binding her and no crown compelling her, purely because she looked at you and saw someone worth choosing.

He had worn a kingdom on his skull. He would take this instead.

* * *

*Edmund*

On the last night of April, Edmund climbed.

She told Quentin she wanted to check the high route past the Shepherd's Crag before she committed it to ink — true enough; the melt had been fast and she trusted no path she hadn't walked that season. But the truth underneath the truth was simpler. There was a ridge above the valley, the highest ground for thirty miles, where the scree gave way to bare wind-scoured stone, and she had been waiting all spring for a clear night to stand on it.

Because it was from that ridge, a year ago, that she had first seen the sky go wrong.

She had been running then — out through the crypt passage with a stolen lantern and a head full of memorized roads, and she had paused on the shoulder of the mountain to get her bearings by the stars, the way her father had taught her before the debts took everything, and there had been nothing to steer by. Nothing. A sky like a closed eye. She had stood in the black and felt the world tilt under her, felt every certainty she owned dissolve, and then she had heard, far below in the pass, a man crying out in pain beside a broken wardstone, and going to him had been the second-worst decision of her life and the beginning of everything good in it.

Now she climbed the same shoulder in the blue end of the day, boots finding the old holds, breath steaming in the thin cold, and came out onto the ridge as the sun went down behind the western peaks.

The sky was enormous.

It was the thing she could never make Quentin understand fully, though he had begun to — he who had grown up a court prisoner, who had seen stars mostly through barred windows. The Thornfell sky was not a ceiling. It was an ocean standing on its head, and it went down and down into itself, deep past bearing, and the stars were not points but *depths*, and a person could fall upward into them forever.

And they were all there. Every one. The Wainwright tilted on his wheels above the northern crags. The Sisters burned close together in the west, silver-white, unmistakable. And low on the southern horizon, just clearing the shoulder of the world, the Star of Thornfell itself — the old king's star, the one the first king had eaten from, the one whose absence had started everything — rising for the first time in a year, red-gold, steady, alive.

Edmund sat down on the cold stone and let herself cry, quietly, the way she did everything, and did not try to stop.

Footsteps behind her. She didn't turn. Only one person walked like that up a mountain — sure-footed, unhurried, arriving without announcement, as though the ground had agreed in advance to hold him.

"The high route's clear," she said, when she could speak evenly. "One washout below the Crag, easily bypassed. I'll mark it tomorrow."

"Mm." Quentin settled beside her, close enough that their shoulders touched, and followed her gaze upward. For a long time neither of them spoke. The stars wheeled. Somewhere far below, a fox barked once and fell silent.

"I stood here," Edmund said at last, "the night they went out. Right here. I'd just escaped the temple, and I thought — I'll navigate by the Sisters, I know the pass blindfolded, I'm free. And then the sky was empty and I understood that I wasn't free at all. I was just loose. There's a difference." She turned her hand palm-up on the stone between them, an offering and an invitation both. "I spent my whole life in ledgers, Quentin. Rows and columns. What's owed, what's paid, what's forfeit. I thought freedom would feel like a balanced book. Everything squared, nothing outstanding, clean margins."

"And it doesn't?"

"It feels like this." She gestured at the whole wheeling immensity overhead. "Unbalanced. Unaccounted for. More than I can ever repay and no one asking me to." Her hand turned over and found his. "I keep waiting for someone to present me with a bill."

"No one will." His thumb moved across her knuckles, over the ink stains that never quite washed out, the callus on her middle finger shaped by six years of pens. "I checked. I'm very thorough about debts these days."

She laughed, wet and rough, and leaned into him, and they sat like that while the Star of Thornfell cleared the horizon entirely and began, very slowly, to climb.

* * *

*Quentin*

Later — much later, when the cold had driven reason out of the question and neither of them had moved anyway — Quentin said, "Do you remember what you asked me? In Ashfall Vale. When I told you to leave me."

"I remember telling you to shut up and learn the map instead."

"That's what I remember too." He smiled at the sky. "But before that. You asked me what I wanted. Not what Thornfell wanted, not what the crown wanted, not what my brother wanted. What *I* wanted. And I couldn't answer. Twenty-six years old and heir to a kingdom, and I had never once been asked the question, so I didn't have anywhere in me to put the answer."

"And now?"

"Now I have a whole shelf for answers." He turned to look at her — her profile against the stars, sharp-nosed, wind-burned, ink-smudged, the most familiar landscape he knew. "I want mornings. I want your terrible tea. I want to grow old watching you argue with mountains. I want to be asked that question every day for the rest of my life, and I want you to be the one asking it."

Edmund was quiet for a moment. Below them the valley lay dark and small, the waystation's new walls catching the starlight, the smoke of banked fires rising straight up in the still air. All those people down there, sleeping safe on roads she had drawn. All that life, flowing through passes that had been secrets when she was a girl scribbling in margins.

"Then ask me mine," she said.

"What do you want, Edmund Halloran?"

"To stop running." She said it simply, the way she said everything that mattered. "We've been running since the night the stars went out. Even after the crown broke, even after the usurper turned to dust — we kept moving, kept the packs ready by the door, kept one eye on the road south. And I'm done. I don't want a house, particularly. I don't want walls. I want—" she searched for it, and found it, and gave it to him plainly— "I want wherever you're standing to be the place I'm not leaving."

Quentin looked at her for a long moment. Then he reached into his coat and brought out something he had been carrying for weeks, wrapped in soft leather, waiting for a night clear enough and brave enough.

A ring. Not gold — he had sworn off gold, and she would have laughed at gold anyway. Iron, forged at the waystation forge from a broken wardstone pin, plain and dark and warm from riding against his chest. On the inside, cut small and deep in a scribe's careful lettering, a single word: *paid.*

Edmund stared at it. Then at him. Then she did something he had never seen her do in fourteen months of knowing her: she laughed and cried at the same time, completely, gracelessly, with her whole face.

"You absolute sentimental ex-prince," she managed.

"Former prince," he said. "There's no throne. I'm nobody's heir. Which means I'm finally allowed to give you everything."

She held out her hand — the ink-stained, pen-callused, map-making hand that had rewritten a nine-hundred-year-old binding with four words and a declaration, the hand that had chosen him over treasure, over safety, over sense — and he slid the iron ring onto her finger, where it fit as though the mountain itself had measured her.

Above them the sky turned, vast and unbalanced and free, every star accounted for and none of them owed.

Below them the road went on — north over the Shepherd's Crag, east to the Greywater ford, west into the Widow's Teeth where the goat-track threaded the false summits — every mile of it drawn now in her hand, walked by his feet, open to anyone who needed it. They would walk it again tomorrow, and the day after, mapping the thaw, meeting the travelers, belonging to no crown, no ledger, no kingdom, no one.

No one but each other.

Edmund laced her fingers through his and looked out at the returning stars, and Quentin felt the last of the running go out of her, out of both of them, like tension leaving a bowstring at rest.

"We're stopping," she said. It wasn't a question. It was a cartographer declaring a border, a scribe closing a ledger, a woman writing the final line in her own margin: *here, and hereafter.*

"We're stopping," Quentin agreed.

And on the highest ridge in all the Thornfells, under a sky that remembered them, they stood still together, and the world, for once, did not require them to move.
