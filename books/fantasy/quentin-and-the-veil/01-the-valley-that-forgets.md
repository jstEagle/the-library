## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The valley forgot things on purpose. That was the first lesson Ines Okafor had ever learned, though no one had ever said it aloud to him. It forgot the names of travelers three days after they left. It forgot the color of the sky over the ridge by midwinter, so that every spring the children swore the blue was new. And it forgot him, mostly, which was how he liked it.

He climbed to the Hollow Gate at dusk, as he had every night for eleven years, carrying a lantern he did not need and a basket of bread he would not eat. The path wound up through the terraced orchards, past the shrine where old Marta left offerings to a saint whose name had worn off her own statue, past the well where the water tasted of nothing at all — not mineral, not rain, not memory. Nothing-water. The valley's signature. Magic could not follow you here. Neither, if you were careful, could anything else.

The Gate itself was not much to look at, which was the point. A hollow in the hillside, ringed with stones so old their inscriptions had been sanded down to suggestion. Moss grew over them in patterns that were not quite natural but never quite moved when you watched. Ines knelt, swept the hollow clean with a broom of birch twigs, laid out the bread, poured a cup of the nothing-water into the stone basin, and spoke the words his mother's mother's mother had spoken before the valley had a name anyone remembered.

*What sleeps behind, sleep on. What waits behind, wait long. What hungers behind, hunger elsewhere.*

Nine words. He said them slowly, letting each one settle into the stone like water into chalk. Then he pressed his palm flat against the coldest rock and held it there until his skin ached, feeling for what he had felt only twice in eleven years: a tremor. A pressure. Something on the other side of the Veil leaning its weight against the door of the world, the way a cat leans against a closed door it has decided is rude.

Tonight the stone was silent. Cold, patient, dumb as any other rock. He exhaled and sat back on his heels.

Above him the moon was four nights from full, hanging fat and yellow over the eastern ridge, and Ines thought — not for the first time, but with more feeling than usual — that he would give almost anything for someone to climb this hill with him. Not to know. Never to know. Just to be there, breathing in the dark beside him, so that the ritual was not always a conversation between himself and a silence.

He was twenty-nine years old and he had lived in this valley his whole life, and he was lonely in the specific, structural way of a load-bearing wall. Everything leaned on him. Nothing held him.

He snuffed the lantern, took the bread back down (the offering was symbolic; the valley's poor got his baking, and thought him merely generous), and went home to his small house at the edge of the square, where he would boil tea, mend a shirt, and be asleep by ten, because a keeper who overslept was a keeper whose hands shook at the Gate, and shaking hands had consequences no one in the valley would ever understand.

His life was a locked room with excellent lighting. He had furnished it carefully.

* * *

Quentin had arrived nine days ago, on the mail cart, with one bag and a laugh that carried further than it should have. He was passing through, he said. Everyone passed through. But Quentin asked questions — real ones, the kind with teeth. Why the well water tasted of nothing. Whether the orchards really fruited twice a year or whether everyone had agreed to say so. What Ines did, exactly, since he seemed to have no trade and yet bread enough to give away.

Ines had answers to all of these, and every answer was a small locked box painted to look like furniture. *Family land. Old habits. I keep bees, sort of.* Quentin accepted all of it with a smile that made Ines feel, uncomfortably, like the smile was doing the accepting on purpose.

They had met at the market, over a dispute about plums. Quentin had picked up the last good basket and Ines had reached for it at the same moment, and instead of the usual silent standoff they'd ended up splitting it on the churchyard wall, talking until the light went strange and gold and the baker started giving them looks. Quentin talked the way rivers run — easily, around obstacles, occasionally flooding. He was from somewhere far east, he said, a city whose name he pronounced with an accent Ines couldn't place and didn't want to; he worked, vaguely, with maps; he had a way of looking at people as though they were terrain worth surveying, and Ines had caught himself being surveyed and found, to his alarm, that he didn't mind.

Now it was the evening of their third proper meeting — Ines refused to call the first two dates, and Quentin had teased him mercilessly for it — and they were walking the long loop around the valley floor, past the millpond, up the sheep track to the overlook where you could see the whole bowl of the world at once: rooftops, orchards, the silver thread of the river, and the hills beyond, folded and folded again into haze.

The date was going strangely well. Strangely because Ines had planned it within an inch of its life — route timed to catch the sunset, conversation topics pre-selected like orchard fruit — and none of the planning mattered, because Quentin kept knocking the itinerary sideways with questions Ines hadn't vetted, and Ines kept answering honestly by accident, and it turned out that honesty, unpracticed, tasted extraordinary.

*I'm boring,* Ines had told him on the second night, preemptively, the way you warn someone about a dog. *My whole life fits in this valley and I like it that way.*

*Boring people don't know the names of every tree on the sheep track,* Quentin had said. *Boring people don't flinch when I mention the word "ward." Boring people don't look at the empty hollow in the hillside like it owes them money.*

Ines had laughed, and then gone cold all over, because Quentin was right, and he had no idea how right.

But tonight the sunset did its work, laying copper along the ridgeline, and Quentin sat close enough that their shoulders touched, and Ines let them touch, and something in his chest that had been braced for eleven years shifted half an inch. Hope, he supposed. He'd read about it. It felt like a stitch coming loose.

"You know what's funny," Quentin said, watching the last light go off the hills. "I've been having this dream. Same one, over and over, since before I came here. Since — hmm. Since about a year ago, maybe."

"Mm," said Ines, who was listening with the polite surface of his mind while the rest of him quietly began to sweat.

"There's a door in it. Not a house door — older than that. Set into a hillside, kind of? Or a hillside shaped like a door, I can't explain it. And there's something on the other side, and it isn't knocking. It's just *leaning*. Like it's been leaning for so long it's forgotten there was ever a time it wasn't leaning." Quentin turned to him, eyes catching the last of the light, warm and utterly unguarded. "And in the dream, I open it. Every time. I don't want to. My hand just goes out and does it, like it's not mine. And then I wake up."

The wind came up the valley and moved through the orchards below, and every tree shivered at once, and Ines felt the sentence land in him like a stone dropped down a well — falling, falling, waiting for the sound of water.

"A door," he said. His voice came out level. Eleven years of practice will do that. "In a dream."

"Set into a hillside," Quentin agreed cheerfully. "Isn't that odd? I've never seen anything like it. Well —" he paused, and something crossed his face, quick as a fish under ice, "— actually, now that I say it out loud. This valley. The way the hills fold. It's a little like the dream. Isn't that funny?"

It was not funny. It was the opposite of funny. It was the thing Ines had spent his entire adult life making sure would never happen, arriving on a mail cart with one bag and a beautiful laugh, sitting next to him on a sheep track with his shoulder warm against Ines's shoulder, saying *I open it, every time.*

Ines looked at him — at the easy mouth, the curious eyes, the hand resting on the grass between them, ordinary, five-fingered, harmless — and felt the future split into two futures, the way a river splits around a stone. In one, he walked Quentin back to the inn, said goodnight, and never saw him again, and the valley went on forgetting, and the stone stayed silent under his palm, and he grew old alone in his locked room with its excellent lighting. In the other —

He didn't let himself think about the other. Not yet. Not on the hilltop, with hope still loose in his chest like a bird that had gotten indoors.

"It's late," he heard himself say. "Cold coming up off the pond. I'll walk you back."

"You okay?" Quentin asked, and there it was again, that surveying look, gentle and thorough. "You went somewhere just now. I saw you go."

"I'm fine," Ines said, and it was even true, in the way that a dam is fine.

They walked down through the darkening orchards, and Quentin talked about maps, about a coastline he'd once drawn that turned out not to exist, about his sister who wrote letters he answered too slowly. Ines listened, and catalogued, and smiled at the right intervals, and all the while some cold clerk in the back of his skull was writing everything down. *Dream of a door. Recurring. One year. Compulsion to open.* The clerk filed it in a drawer Ines kept locked even from himself, and the drawer was already full, and the lock was already straining.

At the inn door, Quentin hesitated. "Tomorrow?" he said. Not a question, quite. An assumption wearing a question's clothes. "There's a spot past the mill I want to show you. Water runs backward there, I swear it does. I want to know if you see it too."

Water ran backward there because the wards bent around it. Ines knew this. Quentin could not know this. Quentin wanted to show him, share it, stand beside him and point.

"Yes," Ines said. "Tomorrow."

Quentin smiled — the smile that did the accepting — and went inside, and Ines stood in the lane a long time after the door closed, watching his own breath fog in the cooling air.

Then he turned, not toward home, but up the hill, toward the Hollow Gate, moving fast through the dark without a lantern, the way you only move when you are going to check on something you're afraid you already know.

The stone was still silent when he got there. He pressed his palm against it anyway, harder than usual, hard enough to bruise, and waited in the dark for a tremor that did not come.

Not tonight.

But the moon was four nights from full, and Ines Okafor had grown up on stories about what the thirteenth turn of the moon meant, and somewhere beneath his fear, colder and quieter than the fear, a different thought had begun to form:

He needed to know why a stranger dreamed of his door. And there was only one place in the world that answer could live — the keepers' archive, under his own floorboards, in ink older than the valley's name.

He had never dared open it looking for a name.

He was going to open it tomorrow.
