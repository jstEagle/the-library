## 2. A Name in Old Ink

The archive opened the way it always opened: reluctantly.

Ines knelt on the kitchen floor of the keeper's house with the iron ring in his fist, and the floorboards came up like a held breath released — a square of pale wood lifting on hinges that had not creaked in his grandfather's time and did not creak now, because the valley forgot sounds as easily as it forgot magic. Beneath them, stairs descended into air that smelled of dust and tallow and something older underneath, the smell of paper that had outlived everyone who wrote on it.

He had gone down those stairs every night for eleven years. He had never once gone down looking for a *name*.

The ritual was done. He'd walked the circuit at moonrise with the salt and the black candle, spoken the words that were less words than weights, felt the seal settle against the thing behind the Veil the way a hand settles against a door someone is leaning on from the other side. The pressure had been normal tonight. Ordinary. The Hollow Gate held, as it had held for three hundred years.

But Quentin had dreamed of a door, and described it — the arch without a doorframe, the hill that swallowed itself, the grass that grew wrong around the edges — with the accuracy of a man reading from a map he'd owned all his life. And Ines had smiled and changed the subject and kissed him goodnight under the crooked pear tree, and then lain awake until dawn listening to the valley's silence and hearing, for the first time in years, the faintest rhythm inside it.

Like breathing. Like something waiting to be asked a question.

So now, in the gray light before the bakery opened and Mrs. Adeyemi's cart rattled past and the world resumed its performance of being ordinary, Ines lit the archive lamps and went looking for prophecy.

* * *

The keepers' archive was not large. That was the first lie anyone who imagined it would believe — they pictured vaulted shelves, chained folios, a library. It was a single stone room, low-ceilinged, its walls lined with cedar cabinets whose drawers were labeled in a hand so old the ink had gone the color of dried blood. Catalogues. Testaments. Ledger after ledger of nightly observations, three hundred years of men and women writing *the seal holds* in increasingly tired handwriting.

Ines knew where everything was. That was the problem with being born to a duty: you inherited not just the work but the map of the work, and there was no corner of it you could claim not to know.

Prophecies lived in the third cabinet, second row, drawers marked with a symbol he'd been taught to recognize before he'd been taught to read — a circle broken by a vertical line. The Veil, drawn by someone who had seen it whole.

He pulled the first drawer and the smell rose up out of it, sharp as a struck match.

There were nine documents with the broken-circle mark. He knew this because as a boy he had counted them, lying on his stomach on these very flags, tracing their edges through their oilcloth wrappers without ever daring to unwrap one. His grandmother had caught him once. She hadn't scolded him. She had sat down beside him on the cold stone and said, *When you're ready, you'll know which one matters. Until then, knowing is heavier than not knowing.*

He was thirty-four years old, and he unwrapped all nine.

Most were fragments. Marginalia from dead keepers, dreams recorded and dismissed, a half-burnt page describing "the turn of moons" in language so gnarled it might have meant anything. He worked through them with hands that stayed steady through discipline alone, and found nothing about doors, nothing about strangers, nothing about names.

The ninth document was different. It was wrapped not in oilcloth but in silk gone brittle as leaf-skin, and beneath the wrapper the page itself was vellum, thick and cream-colored, and when Ines lifted it into the lamplight the ink seemed to sit *above* the surface rather than in it — raised, almost, like a scar.

The header read, in a formal antique script:

**THE WORD OF THE VEIL, AS SPOKEN TO THE FIRST KEEPERS ON THE NIGHT OF ITS MAKING. SET DOWN IN THE YEAR THE VALLEY TOOK ITS NAME.**

Three hundred years old, then, though the copy was younger — a hundred years, maybe, by the hand. A transcription. Someone, a century ago, had copied out a text the original keepers claimed was older than the valley itself.

Ines read it standing up, because his knees had informed him they could no longer be trusted.

Most of it was what he expected. The Veil's purpose. The sealing. The thirteen turns of the moon as a cycle of testing — *and on the thirteenth turn shall the seal be weighed, and found holding or found wanting.* His whole life fit inside those lines like a foot in a boot.

Then, near the bottom, the handwriting of the transcriber changed. Tighter. Hurried, or excited. And the words stopped being scripture and started being instructions:

*It is written that in an hour of thinning there shall come to the Gate one who has seen it in sleep, unbaptized by our order, unmarked by any craft — a vessel clean of intention, whom the power may enter without resistance. This one shall be named for the renewal. Seek the name. Keep the name. When the dreamer comes to the door of the keeper, know him, and do not let him leave unchanged.*

Ines read it twice. Three times. The lamps guttered and he didn't relight them.

*A vessel clean of intention.* A stranger. Someone with no craft, no order, no mark — someone the power could slide into like water into a cup, because there was nothing in the cup already.

Someone like a warm, curious outsider who moved to the valley in spring and took a job at the mill and asked too many questions and dreamed of doors.

"No," Ines said aloud, and the archive ate the word.

Names. The passage said *seek the name*. Which meant somewhere — in this room, in some other record, in whatever the first keepers had left behind besides stone and silence — there was a name written down. Written a hundred years before Quentin Vale ever drew breath.

His hands were shaking now. He set the vellum down flat on the reading table with exaggerated care, the way you handle something when you're afraid your body will betray what your mind refuses to hold, and he began going through the drawer again, page by page, faster and less carefully, looking for a list, a register, anything—

He found it folded inside the back panel of the drawer itself, hidden where only someone dismantling the furniture would look. A single sheet, same vellum, same raised ink. Not a document. A rendering.

A portrait.

Drawn in fine line and wash, the way the valley's few surviving artists still worked: a young man, three-quarter view, dark curling hair cut close at the sides, a face built out of easy angles and one eyebrow slightly higher than the other, as if permanently mid-question. There was a small mole below the left eye. The mouth was drawn just beginning to smile, the smile of someone who finds the world interesting and isn't embarrassed about it.

Ines had last seen that exact expression four hours ago, across a table at the Gilded Perch, while Quentin explained, delightedly, why he thought bread was humanity's finest achievement.

Below the portrait, one word, in the raised ink that looked like a scar:

***Vale.***

Not Quentin. Just the surname. But surnames in the valley were few and rooted deep, and there was exactly one family of Vales, arrived six generations back, and Quentin wore the name like it fit him perfectly because it did.

Ines sat down on the cold floor. He didn't decide to; his legs simply ended the argument.

He looked at the face for a long time. Then he did the arithmetic he'd been refusing to do since the drawer opened: a transcription a century old, describing a man who appeared in the valley last spring, dreaming a door he should never have seen. Either the prophecy was true in the way scriptures are true — vague enough to catch anyone, sharp enough to terrify —

Or it was precise. Or someone had made it precise.

*Seek the name. Keep the name. When the dreamer comes to the door of the keeper—*

Quentin hadn't come to the door of the keeper. Quentin had come to the bakery, to the river path, to the crooked pear tree. To Ines. Had he? Or had he come to the Gate wearing a charming man's face, and Ines had simply been the door it happened to open?

He thought of Quentin's laugh. He thought of the way Quentin listened — really listened, head tilted, like Ines's opinions on rain barrels were worth gold. He thought of the dream, told shyly, like a gift: *I keep seeing this door in the hills. Isn't that strange?*

"Isn't that strange," Ines repeated to the empty archive, and heard how it sounded either way. Like innocence. Like bait.

Above him, faintly, the house ticked and settled. Somewhere down the lane, Mrs. Adeyemi's cart. The valley performing ordinariness, and Ines sitting under its floorboards holding a hundred-year-old picture of the man he'd kissed last night, unable to tell whether he'd fallen in love or been harvested.

That was the worst of it. Not the fear. The not-knowing which parts of last night had been real — whether the warmth in Quentin's voice had been a person, or a lure wearing a person, cast out a century ago by keepers clever enough to forge scripture and patient enough to wait for the fish.

Because that was the thought he couldn't put down, the one that kept circling: *the founding keepers wrote it.* If the passage was genuine — if the first keepers had truly received this word on the night of the making — then the seal Ines tended every night wasn't just a lock. It was part of a mechanism. A trap laid across centuries, with a description of the bait written into its instructions.

And he had walked straight into it smiling.

* * *

He climbed out of the archive at noon, closed the floor, washed his face, and did not eat.

At dusk he walked to the mill road because his feet decided before he did, and found Quentin waiting by the fence with two bottles of cider and a grin that started before Ines was even in speaking distance. "I hoped you'd come. You vanished on me this morning — I knocked, no answer. I thought maybe you'd gotten eaten by your own garden."

"I had work," Ines said.

"You always have work." Quentin handed him a bottle. Their fingers touched. Quentin's hands were warm; Ines catalogued that automatically now, horribly, the way a physician catalogs symptoms. Warm. Human. Or very well made.

"Quentin." His own voice sounded like someone else's, level and apologetic, a voice rehearsed on the walk over. "About last night. I — I shouldn't have. It moved fast, and I'm not — I'm not in a place where I can be seeing anyone. It's me, it's my circumstances, and it wouldn't be fair to you."

The grin died by degrees, and watching it go was like watching a lamp be carried out of a room.

"Oh," Quentin said. "Okay." A pause. "Did I do something?"

"No." *Yes,* said the vellum, folded against Ines's ribs where he'd hidden it like a tumor. *You were written. You were drawn a hundred years before you were born, and I have your face in my pocket.* "No. It's me."

Quentin looked at him for a long moment with that tilted-head listening look, and Ines understood that Quentin did not believe him, and that Quentin was too proud to say so, and that the cider bottle in his hand was suddenly the loneliest object in the valley.

"Well," Quentin said lightly, badly. "If your circumstances change."

"They won't."

They stood there. A pheasant complained in the hedgerow. Quentin nodded once, turned, and walked toward the mill road, and Ines watched him go and memorized the mole below the left eye and hated himself with a thoroughness that felt almost professional.

* * *

He did not sleep. He went back down into the archive and spread the portrait and the prophecy side by side under the lamps, and read, and reread, hunting for the seam in the forgery — because there had to be one, because the alternative was that the kind man with the cider was a fishhook — and found none. The ink was consistent. The hand was consistent. The *Vale* was consistent.

Sometime past midnight he caught himself whispering the passage aloud, fitting it over Quentin like clothing: *unbaptized by our order* — yes, Quentin had never set foot in the old chapel. *Unmarked by any craft* — Quentin couldn't light a candle without burning his thumb. *One who has seen it in sleep* — the door, the hills, the grass growing wrong.

It fit. Every line fit.

And that was when the colder thought arrived, the one that finally got him off the floor: if the prophecy was a lure, it had a maker, and makers kept records. If it was genuine, it had a source, and sources could be questioned. Either way, the answers Ines needed weren't in a hole under his kitchen. They were out in the world he'd spent his life pretending didn't exist — among exiles and scholars and people who read broken circles without flinching.

But first, before roads and cities and heresy: he had to know one more thing. Whether the dreamer came to his door by accident.

He would watch. Tonight, tomorrow night, every night — the ritual gave him the perfect excuse to stand at the Hollow Gate in the dark, unseen. If Quentin came near the Gate again, if the dream pulled him up the hill the way prophecy said it must—

The lamps burned down. Ines reread the page until the words wore grooves in him.

And on the third night, from the shadow of the Gate, he saw a figure climbing the hill through the moonlight — walking in his sleep, eyes open and fixed on the door — and it was Quentin.
