## 3. Thirteen Moons

The note Ines left on my doorstep said, *I'm sorry. It's better if we don't see each other again.*

It was written in careful handwriting, the kind people use when they're lying. I'd spent three days staring at it—on my kitchen table first, then pinned to the corkboard above my desk like a specimen, then back to the table where I could hate it properly. Three days of replaying our date: the way he'd laughed at my terrible joke about the baker's sourdough, how his hand had hovered near mine across the table without ever landing, and then—the moment everything curdled—how his face had gone white as bleached linen when I mentioned the dream.

*There's a door,* I'd told him, because it felt safe to tell him things, because he listened like listening was a skill he'd practiced for years. *A door standing in nothing. And something behind it knows my name.*

He hadn't touched his wine after that. He'd walked me home at a pace just short of rude, said goodnight without lingering, and vanished into his house across the lane like a man escaping a fire.

Now, on the third night, I watched from behind my curtain as he slipped out of that same house with a lantern and a bundle under his arm, moving with the practiced silence of someone who has done this every evening of his life. He turned toward the hills.

Well. I've never been good at leaving mysteries alone. My mother used to say I could find trouble in an empty room. I pulled on my coat and followed him.

* * *

The path climbed through pines that smelled like resin and cold stone, and Ines moved through them like water finding its channel—not hurrying, not hesitating, choosing each step before he took it. Twice he stopped and stood motionless, head tilted, and twice I pressed myself against a trunk and held my breath until my lungs burned.

After the second stop, I realized he wasn't checking for followers. He was listening. Listening the way you'd listen outside a sickroom.

The trees thinned. The hill opened into a bowl of bare granite, and there it was.

The Hollow Gate.

I'd heard about it the way you hear about weather—as background, as furniture of the valley's identity. A stone archway older than the town records, set into the mountainside, that the keepers tended. Nobody talked about what it kept out. That was the point of it, maybe. The valley forgot so we wouldn't have to remember.

But standing there in the moonlight, I understood that nobody had forgotten. They'd simply agreed, all together and all at once, to look away.

Ines knelt before the arch. The lantern light caught chalk lines already drawn on the granite—circles within circles, script I didn't recognize running along their rims like a fence made of language. From his bundle he took a clay bowl, a knife, and a stoppered flask that glimmered even in shadow. His movements were liturgy. Every gesture had the weight of ten thousand repetitions.

He poured the flask's contents along the outer circle, and the liquid flared—a low silver burn without heat—and the whole arch woke up.

I don't know how else to describe it. The Gate *woke*. The stone darkened to the color of deep water, and between its pillars the air thickened into something like a membrane, like the skin stretched over a drum, and beneath it—

Beneath it, something moved.

My body knew before my mind did. Every hair on my arms lifted. The air went taut as wire. Behind the Veil—or the Veil itself, I couldn't tell—something vast shifted its weight, slow as a whale turning in dark water, and pressed outward.

The membrane bowed. Just slightly. Just enough.

And in the bowing of it, in the way the pressure distributed and gathered, I saw something that stopped my heart: the shape of a face. Not pressing against the surface like a child against a window—more like the way wind shapes sand. Suggestion. Implication. A face the way a dream has a face.

It was looking at Ines.

No. It was looking past Ines.

It was looking at me.

I must have made a sound. Later, I couldn't say whether I screamed or simply breathed too loudly; both seemed possible. What I know is that Ines spun around with a speed no quiet archivist should possess, knife in hand, and froze when he saw me—and in the frozen half-second before recognition, I watched him decide to kill me.

That's not an exaggeration. I've thought about it since, and I'm sure. For one instant, Quentin the charming outsider ceased to exist for him, replaced by a threat approaching the seal, and his whole body reorganized around ending that threat. Then his eyes found my face in the moonlight, and the knife dropped point-down in the dirt, and he looked like a man watching his own house collapse.

"Quentin." My name came out cracked. "You shouldn't be here."

"No," I agreed. My voice worked, barely. "Seems like a lot of places I shouldn't be tonight." I pointed at the Gate, at the thing behind it still pressing, patient as tide. "What is that? And why does it—" I swallowed. "Why does it know me?"

"You dreamed about it," he said flatly. Not a question.

"There's a door. Standing in nothing. Something behind it knows my name." I hugged my arms against the cold that had nothing to do with temperature. "You looked at me like I'd handed you a death sentence when I said that. So start talking, Ines. Because I walked up this mountain to get answers from the man who kissed my cheek two weeks ago, and instead I found him feeding some kind of god through a hole in the world."

"It's not a god." He picked up the knife, wiped it, sheathed it—all without looking away from me. "It's worse. Gods can be bargained with."

* * *

We sat on the granite with the lantern between us and the Gate at our backs, and he wouldn't let us face it while he talked. He made me sit with my shoulder to the arch, facing down the valley, facing the lights of home scattered below like embers.

"The valley is hollow," he began. "Not metaphorically. There's a pocket cut out of the world here, warded so magic can't follow anyone inside. No spells, no curses, no scrying, none of it. Everything the old war spilled—the wars you've never heard of because history doesn't survive crossing those wards—we locked it behind the Veil. The Hollow Gate is the lock. Keepers have maintained it since the sealing. My family was bound to it nine generations ago."

"And you?"

"I'm the last keeper. The ritual you saw keeps the seal tight. It fails if I miss a night." He laughed, and it was the worst sound I'd ever heard from him—dry and small. "I missed a night once when I had fever. I was delirious for three days, and when I finally crawled up here, the Veil had worn thin enough that I could hear it breathing."

"What's on the other side?"

"The sealed power. The thing the war was actually about, underneath all the territory and the vengeance. The mages of the old order tried to claim it, one faction after another, and it ate them. So the first keepers did the only thing left—they built a door and locked it, and they built a valley where the door's key couldn't reach." He was quiet for a moment. Below us, a dog barked in the town, absurdly ordinary. "Magic can't cross the wards, Quentin. But dreams aren't magic. Dreams are just... attention. And things behind doors notice when someone pays attention."

"So it's been dreaming at me."

"I don't know. Maybe. Maybe it's been dreaming at everyone in the valley and you're the only one who remembers." He rubbed his face. "Or maybe it's the prophecy."

"Don't." The word came out sharp. "Don't do the thing where you know something and ration it out to protect me. I followed you up a mountain at midnight. I earned the whole truth."

He looked at me for a long time, and I watched him weigh it—the kindness of concealment against whatever the truth would cost us both. I hated that he was doing the arithmetic. I hated more that I understood it.

"In the archive," he said finally, "there's a text called the Lattice of Turns. Written a hundred and twelve years ago by a keeper named Aldous Merrin. It describes the renewal of the Veil—because seals fail, eventually; they need renewing—and it names the conditions. Thirteen turns of the moon from the first true dream of the door. At the thirteenth turn, the Veil reaches its thinnest point, and either it's renewed or it breaks." He paused. "And it gives a description of the one who dreams the door. Brown skin. Left-handed. A scar over the right eyebrow. And a name."

The cold went through me completely then. My scar—I'd had it since I fell off a wall at eleven. My left hand, ink-stained from years of drawing. I thought of the date, three weeks ago now. I counted backward, moon by moon.

"One," I said.

"Tonight makes two," Ines said quietly. "Eleven turns left."

We sat with it. The lantern hissed. Somewhere downslope an owl asked its question and got no answer.

"Why did you lie to me?" I asked. It wasn't the question I'd planned to lead with, but it was the one that mattered. "Three days of silence. A note. You were going to just—disappear from your own life? From mine?"

"Because I've read what happens to people in prophecies." His voice frayed at the edges. "They don't get to be people anymore. They become instruments. Everyone who learns your name will want to aim you at something—the Wardens, the exiles, the thing behind the Veil itself. I thought if I cut you loose early, cleanly, you might slip free of it. Be nobody again."

"And instead?"

"Instead you climbed the mountain." He almost smiled. "Of course you climbed the mountain."

Behind us, the Gate hummed—a sound I felt in my teeth more than heard—and I refused to turn around. Whatever was pressing on the other side could wait. I had a man to negotiate with.

"Here's what happens now," I said. "You don't get to decide alone anymore. You don't get to lie to me, ration truths, or sacrifice our—for God's sake, Ines, we had *one date*, and you decided my fate without consulting me. If there are eleven moons left, then we have eleven moons, and we spend them together, or I'll follow you up this mountain every single night until you give in. I'm very stubborn. Ask literally anyone."

"That's a terrible plan."

"It's the only plan on offer."

He stared at me, and something in his face shifted—the guilt loosening, or perhaps being joined by something else. Relief, maybe. The relief of finally putting down a weight he'd carried long enough to grow into his spine.

"If we do this," he said slowly, "we do it properly. You learn what the signs mean. You stay inside the wards where nothing can touch you. And when the time comes—" He stopped.

"When the time comes, what?"

"When the thirteenth turn arrives, someone has to stand at the Gate. Either to renew the seal or—" He didn't finish. He didn't have to.

"Then we'd better figure out which," I said. "Together."

Above the eastern ridge, the moon hung fat and full, and I found myself counting without meaning to: two gone, eleven remaining. Eleven chances to understand what wanted my name. Eleven nights of this man beside me, guarding a door in the world, pretending he was only a quiet neighbor with a boring life.

As we packed the lantern and started down, Ines said, without turning, "For what it's worth—I'm sorry about the note."

"Forget the note. But next time something terrifies you about me, you tell me first."

"Next time," he repeated, and there was something almost warm in it.

Neither of us noticed, then, the faint shimmer that passed over the valley lights below—the wards flexing, testing, like a fence discovering a gap. Neither of us knew yet that the valley itself had begun to reject the very person it was meant to protect.

But the moon was already waning.
