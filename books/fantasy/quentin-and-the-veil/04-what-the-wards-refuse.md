## 4. What the Wards Refuse

The first sign came on a Tuesday, which felt insulting. Catastrophe, in Ines's experience, should have the courtesy to arrive on a night with some drama to it—a storm, an eclipse, at least a wind. Instead it was a mild gray morning, and he was carrying two cups of chicory coffee up the stairs of his own house when he noticed that the third step had stopped humming.

Every keeper learned the valley's song before they learned their letters. The wards were not walls; they were a chord, sustained for nine hundred years by the gate's first architects, woven into stone and root and rain. Ines had grown up inside it the way a fish grows up inside water—unaware of it until someone taught him to listen. The third stair of his house sat atop a ward-anchor, one of thirteen buried stones ringing the valley like the points of a star, and it had hummed against his heels every morning of his life.

It was silent now.

He set down the coffee with exaggerated care, as if slowness could unmake what he already knew, and pressed his palm flat to the banister. Nothing. Not silence exactly—the way a dead thing isn't sleeping—but a thinning, a note held so long it had gone past hearing. He knelt and put his ear to the wood like a child listening for a train through the rails, and there, deep underneath, he caught the chord still playing. Faint. Frayed at the edges. And fraying worst directly above him, directly around the small upstairs room where Quentin slept.

Ines climbed the rest of the stairs without touching them.

Quentin was awake, sitting cross-legged on the guest bed in yesterday's shirt, turning Ines's grandmother's compass over in his hands. He'd taken to it the way he took to everything—with a kind of greedy tenderness, wanting to know how things worked, why they were kept, who had loved them first. The needle swung lazily, uselessly. It always had; iron didn't hold true in a valley where the world's deeper currents were dammed behind a seal. Quentin looked up when Ines came in, and his whole face did what it always did—arrived, entirely, all at once—and Ines felt the old grief open under his ribs like a trapdoor.

"You're doing the face," Quentin said. "The one where you're deciding how much to tell me."

"The wards are failing," Ines said, because he had promised himself, after the gate, after the confession, no more arithmetic of truth. "Around you specifically. I checked the eastern anchor before you woke up. The whole ring is dimming where your—" He stopped. "Where you are."

Quentin set the compass down gently. "Meaning what? Am I hurting it?"

"No." Ines sat on the edge of the bed because his legs had opinions of their own. "You're *dissolving* it. The wards don't keep things out, exactly. They make this valley forgetful. They press a kind of sleep into everything—the magic in the ground, the power behind the Veil, anything that might notice this place exists. You're the opposite of forgettable, Quentin. Whatever's in you, the wards can't decide what to do with it, and they're wearing themselves out trying."

"Then I'll leave." Quentin said it fast, easy, the way people say things they've already decided and are only now announcing. "I'm not staying somewhere I'm a leak in the hull. Where would I even—"

"Vael Corrin."

The name came out of Ines before he'd voted on it. Quentin blinked. "The city? That's four days' walk."

"Three, if we take the shepherd road." Ines heard himself planning aloud and let it happen; planning was easier than feeling. "There's a man there. Sefu Adeyemi. He was the keepers' archivist before they exiled him for asking the wrong questions about the prophecy. He's the only person alive who's read more of it than I have. If anyone knows why the wards refuse you—if anyone knows what the second half of that page says—we find him."

"And the Wardens?"

"We avoid the roads. We travel like pilgrims, not fugitives." Ines almost smiled. "Pilgrims get fed. Fugitives get reported."

Quentin watched him for a long moment, and Ines recognized the look—he'd seen it across dinner tables and market stalls and once, memorably, over a shared umbrella during a summer storm: Quentin cataloguing him, reading the fine print under his face. "You've never left the valley," Quentin said quietly. It wasn't a question. "Have you."

"I was born three houses from where you slept last night. My mother was born in that same house. Keepers don't leave. Leaving is—" He gestured vaguely at the window, at the gray light, at the hills folded around them like cupped hands. "The whole point of us is staying."

* * *

He spent the afternoon saying goodbye to a place that didn't know he was leaving.

That was the cruelty of it, he thought, walking the perimeter road with a canvas pack he hadn't packed yet, touching the anchors one by one the way his father had taught him, pressing two fingers to each buried stone and listening. The valley wouldn't miss him. It couldn't. The wards made sure of it—not just forgetting magic, but forgetting *edges*, the sharp particularities of the people who lived here. Ines Okafor had spent thirty years being deliberately boring so that no one would ever look too closely, and now, preparing to go, he found there was nothing sharp enough in him to leave an outline. Mrs. Adeyemi—no relation, everyone assumed, though he'd never confirmed it—would water his tomatoes out of neighborly habit for a week and then stop. The baker would wonder idly, someday, whether the quiet man who liked the seeded rolls had moved away or died, and then sell out of seeded rolls to someone else.

He crouched at the western anchor and rested his forehead against the cold grass above it.

Underneath, the chord was thinner here than it had been a month ago. He could hear the difference now that he knew to listen—could hear the wards leaning toward Quentin's room across the valley like tired men leaning into wind, spending themselves against something they couldn't contain. A hundred years from now there would be no valley at all unless the Veil was renewed, and renewal meant—

No. He stood, brushed off his knees. One catastrophe at a time.

At the gate itself, he lingered longer than was wise. The Hollow Gate sat in its fold of hills like a scar in a palm, and Ines laid his hand on the seal-stone and told it, silently, the way his mother had told it every night of her life: *Still here. Still holding.* Then, because everything was already breaking anyway: *I'm going to try to fix what we built this on. Forgive me.*

The stone gave back nothing but cold. It always did.

* * *

They left before dawn, when the valley was blue and breath-froze cold and the wards' forgetting worked hardest, wrapping early risers in a fog of not-quite-noticing. Ines led them up the shepherd road with a lantern shuttered to a coin of light, and Quentin followed in boots Ines had oiled for him, carrying a pack that weighed less than his questions.

At the ridge—the last point from which the whole bowl of the valley was visible—they stopped. Ines hadn't planned to. His feet simply quit, and he stood looking down at the rooftops silvered with frost, at the smoke beginning to rise from chimneys in threads so straight they looked drawn on, at the green dark line of the trees that hid the Hollow Gate, and something in his chest went tight and small and grieving.

"I used to think this view was a promise," he said. He hadn't meant to say it aloud. "Like the valley was holding me. Turns out it was holding everything *away* from me. There's a difference, and I'm only learning it now, at the exit."

Quentin didn't answer right away. When Ines glanced over, he wasn't looking at the valley at all. He was looking east, down the long descending road toward the lowlands, toward Vael Corrin, toward a world Ines had only read about—and there was something in his face that Ines had never seen there before. Not fear. Hunger, maybe. Or recognition.

"Ines," Quentin said slowly, "the fog down there—it's moving wrong."

Ines followed his gaze and his stomach dropped through the mountainside.

Fog didn't behave that way. Fog didn't pool in a straight line across the road like a wall waiting to be signed. But this did—a white bank lying across the switchbacks below them with geometric patience, and as he watched, shapes moved within it. Tall shapes. Orderly. The gray traveling cloaks of the Wardens of the Old Order, warded themselves, invisible to any eye that belonged to this valley—which meant, he realized with a cold clarity that felt almost like laughter, invisible to every eye except the two standing on this ridge.

Except theirs. Because Quentin didn't belong to the valley anymore. The wards had already let him go.

"They can't see us," Ines breathed. "We're outside the forgetting. We're the only ones who can see them." He pulled Quentin back from the ridgeline by the sleeve, heart hammering a rhythm that had nothing to do with exertion. "Which means they're not here for us. They're watching the valley. Waiting for something to come *out*."

"Then we go another way."

"There is no other way. Every road down funnels through the pass." Ines closed his eyes and did the math he'd been refusing to do since the archive: the moon's age, the pages Sefu had hidden, the distance, the odds. When he opened them, the decision had already finished making itself somewhere below his fear. "We wait for full dark, we cut along the river gorge instead of the road, and we walk all night. And Quentin—" He gripped his shoulder, felt the warmth of him, the impossible ordinary weight of him. "Whatever happens on that road, whatever you start to feel when the valley lets go of you—you tell me. All of it. No more arithmetic."

Quentin nodded. Then, softer: "What happens when the valley lets go?"

Below them, patient as a held breath, the fog waited on the road, and Ines understood that the question wasn't rhetorical—that neither of them knew, and that the answer was already climbing toward them through the dark like weather.
