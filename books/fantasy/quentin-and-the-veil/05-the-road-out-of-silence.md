## 5. The Road Out of Silence

The valley let go of me like a hand opening.

I didn't notice it at first. I noticed the road instead—the way it unspooled north through the pass, gravel and old cart-ruts, hemmed in by pines that smelled like rain and resin. Ines walked half a step ahead with a canvas pack on his shoulders and his jaw set in that way I was learning meant he'd already rehearsed every bad thing that could happen and hadn't liked any of the answers. He'd said almost nothing since we left the last farmhouse at dawn. Neither had I. There wasn't much to say that wasn't *you lied to me* or *I'm still here anyway*, and both of those were too heavy to carry out loud on an empty stomach.

The morning was ordinary. That was the strange part. Birds, mist burning off the fields, a dog barking somewhere behind us like even it disapproved of our leaving.

Then we crossed the wardline.

I felt it as a sound first—a single low note, like someone plucking a wire strung somewhere behind my sternum—and then the world came *in*.

Not gradually. All at once, the way a held breath releases. Color went from color to something louder than color. The pines weren't just green; they were green the way a choir is loud, each tree humming its own narrow frequency, and beneath them the soil sang a long brown chord of roots and worms and slow stone. The stream we forded didn't babble. It *announced itself*, every drop a struck bell, and when the wind moved through the grass it moved through me too, a pressure against skin that wasn't quite touch.

I stopped walking. My knees did something unreliable.

"Quentin?" Ines turned. Whatever he saw in my face made him drop his pack and cross back to me in three strides.

"It's—" I put a hand over my eyes. Too much light. Not light exactly—more like everything had grown a second skin of meaning and it was all pressing against mine. "Everything's talking. Everything is *loud*."

His face did a complicated thing—fear and guilt wrestling for control of his mouth—and he took my elbow, steadying me like I might blow away. "You're past the wards. The valley keeps magic out; outside, it's everywhere. Most people feel it as weather. A hum. You're feeling it as—"

"A symphony with no off switch," I managed.

"That," he said quietly, "is not most people."

We stood there while I breathed. Ines talked me through it in a low, patient voice, the same voice he'd used at the gate when he confessed thirteen years of secrets in the dark: *find one thread and hold it*. So I found the stream and held its bell-note like a railing, and slowly the flood resolved into layers I could sort—near, far, alive, inert. And underneath all of it, underneath even the stones, I felt something else. A second heartbeat. Mine, but not mine. Something coiled behind my ribs with its own slow rhythm, waking up, stretching, tasting the air of a world it had been locked away from.

"Ines." My voice came out thin. "There's something inside me."

He didn't pretend not to understand. That was the one kindness available to him, and he took it. "I know," he said. "Or—I suspected. The wards failing around you. The way the gate ritual bent toward you that night. Quentin, I think you've been carrying it your whole life without knowing. Like a coal wrapped in ash."

"Is that supposed to make me feel better? Because mostly it makes me feel like luggage."

Something that was almost a laugh escaped him, brief and exhausted. "It's supposed to make you feel less alone. I'm working with limited materials."

We walked on. By afternoon I'd learned to walk inside the noise—to narrow my attention until the world's chorus dulled to something bearable—but the thing behind my ribs never dulled. It rose and fell with the moon's position, which was absurd, because the moon was a pale ghost in the daylight sky, and yet I could feel it the way you feel someone watching you from across a room. Counting down. Thirteen turns, Ines had said. We'd used up two getting ready to leave.

The road climbed out of the valley's shadow and into country I'd only ever seen in the books I'd brought on that first, doomed date: hills stitched with heather, a ruined watchtower wearing ivy like a scarf, distance upon distance going blue at the edges. Any other year of my life, this would have been the best day of it. Instead every beautiful thing arrived pre-loaded with dread, because I now knew what the beauty was *made of*, and I knew there were people in that beauty who wanted to cut me open and read me like a letter.

Ines had told me about the Wardens on the second night, in the flat tone of a man reciting an inventory: the old order sworn to guard the Veil, keepers of the law Ines had inherited, scattered now but not gone, and absolutely certain that prophecy was instruction rather than warning.

"They'll come for you," he'd said. "Sefu says the mark shows, once you're past the wards."

"What mark?"

"The kind you can't wash off."

I'd checked my own arms about forty times since. Nothing visible. But that afternoon, resting in the lee of the ruined watchtower while Ines built a small fire, I caught my reflection in a shard of old mirror-glass wedged in the stonework—and there it was. Faint, silver-white, curled along the inside of my left forearm like a vein of frost: a spiral of tiny interlocking lines, dense at the center, unraveling toward the wrist. A door drawn in miniature. A keyhole drawn in miniature.

I didn't tell Ines right away. I sat with it, watching the firelight move across the frost-spiral, and felt the shape of my life quietly rearrange itself around the fact that I had apparently always been marked for something, by someone, before I was ever born into choices of my own.

That was when the dogs started.

Not dogs. I realized that a heartbeat too late—they had no warmth in my new senses, no wet animal chorus, just four cold silences moving fast through the heather where sounds should have been. Ines was on his feet before I finished standing, snuffing the fire with a twist of his hand, and then the world's noise *tore*.

Magic hit the clearing like weather. The temperature dropped. Shadows pooled wrong, flowing uphill, gathering around five figures who stepped out of the heather as if they'd been standing there all along and had merely permitted themselves to become visible. They wore grey traveling cloaks and iron-grey expressions, and the tallest of them carried a staff tipped with a lens of cloudy crystal that drank the evening light.

"Warden," Ines said. Not a question. He stepped in front of me, which was ridiculous—we both knew it—and also made something hot and complicated bloom in my chest.

"Hollow keeper." The tall one's voice was dry as old paper. Her eyes—pale, lidless-seeming—went straight past Ines to me, to my arm, which was glowing now. I hadn't done anything. The frost-spiral had simply decided to introduce itself, silver light bleeding up through my sleeve like ink through paper. "Well," she said softly. "The thirteenth moon finds its vessel walking free."

"I'm not a vessel," I said. My voice cracked on it. Behind my ribs, the second heartbeat surged, answering her attention like a struck tuning fork, and I hated it, hated how it proved her right. "I'm a person. My name is Quentin."

"Names are for things that get to remain themselves." She said it without cruelty, which was worse—with the gentle certainty of someone stating arithmetic. "Child, do you know what happens if the Veil fails? Every sealed horror of the old war pours into the world at once. Your life is one coin set against millions. Spend it."

"She doesn't spend anyone," Ines said, and there was something in his voice I'd never heard before—not the careful keeper, not the gentle archivist, but something older and harder, a voice from whatever version of him existed before the valley taught him to be quiet. "The law names me warden of the gate. Stand down, Maren Solde. I know the roll of the order, and I know your name is on it."

"And I know yours, Ines Okafor." Her mouth thinned. "Last of a line that let the seal rot for a century while it played at being a farmer. The circle will hear of this. The circle decides what the vessel is for—not you, not him."

"Then the circle can write letters," Ines said, "because we won't be here to read them."

Maren raised her staff, and the crystal lens flared, and I felt the world's noise bend toward it like iron filings toward a magnet—bend toward *me*, actually, a hook of cold light reaching for the spiral on my arm. Panic did something clever and stupid in me at once. I didn't decide what happened next; the thing behind my ribs did. It reached back.

Light met light. The hook shattered. Every Warden in the clearing staggered, and the shadows they wore peeled off the ground like scorched paper, and for one impossible second I stood at the center of a silence I had carved myself—a perfect sphere of quiet in a screaming world—and it felt *magnificent*, and that frightened me more than the staff did.

Then the moment broke, and I was on my knees in the heather with blood in my mouth and Ines's hands under my arms hauling me upright.

Maren had regained her footing. She looked at me differently now—still certain, but newly hungry, the way a scholar looks at a page she thought she'd memorized that has suddenly produced a line she's never seen. "It answers him," she murmured. "Untrained. Unbound." To her companions: "Fall back. We don't take him by force tonight."

"You're just letting us go?" I rasped.

"No, child." She was already retreating into the heather, her pale eyes fixed on me the whole way, the staff's lens dimming like a closing eye. "I'm letting you run. There's a difference, and you'll understand it soon. The faction in Vael Corrin wants what you carry far more than we do—and they don't knock."

The shadows swallowed them. The heather closed like water over a stone. The night noises crept back in, ordinary and enormous, and I stood shaking in the middle of them with frost-light still fading off my arm and the taste of my own power like copper coins on my tongue.

Ines didn't ask if I was all right. He looked at me for a long moment—really looked, at the arm, at my face, at whatever was behind my ribs—and then he picked up both packs and said, "Vael Corrin. Tonight, while they regroup. Can you walk?"

I could. I did. But somewhere on the darkening road, with the ruined tower shrinking behind us and the city's distant glow smudging the horizon, the wonder finally curdled all the way through. Everyone out here had a use for me. The Wardens wanted to spend me like a coin. The mage faction wanted to burn me like fuel. Even the thing inside me wanted—something. I could feel it wanting, patient as tide.

And the worst part, the part I kept turning over like a stone with something wriggling under it, was that for one shining second in that clearing, when my power answered and the world went silent around me—

*I* had wanted to do it again.

* * *

We walked until the stars wheeled and the road began, at last, to descend toward the plain, and Ines finally called a halt in the hollow of a dry streambed where the banks hid our fire. He handed me tea that tasted like pine needles and apology, and sat close enough that our shoulders touched, which was its own kind of sentence neither of us had worked up the courage to finish.

"Maren said the faction doesn't knock," I said eventually. "What does that mean?"

Ines stared into the fire. When he spoke, his voice was very quiet, very level, the voice of a man reading a report about his own execution. "It means that Sefu Adeyemi sent word ahead of us, and the word came back wrong. Vael Corrin isn't waiting for two travelers asking questions." He passed me a scrap of paper, creased and travel-worn, bearing a single line in cramped handwriting:

*They know you're coming. Leave the road. Trust no one who offers shelter.*

Above the message, pressed into the paper as if by a signet ring, was a mark I recognized with a sick lurch: a spiral. Tiny, interlocking, unraveling toward the edge.

The same mark that glowed on my arm.

The same mark, I realized, looking at Ines's stricken face in the firelight, that he had seen before—a hundred years ago, in the margins of a prophecy written by the gate's first keepers.

"Ines," I said. "Who else has this mark?"

He didn't answer. But he didn't have to, because the wind shifted, carrying up from the direction of the city a sound that didn't belong to any bird or beast—a long, low hum, like a door being unlocked somewhere far below the world, and behind my ribs, my unwelcome passenger *turned toward it like a compass needle finding home.*
