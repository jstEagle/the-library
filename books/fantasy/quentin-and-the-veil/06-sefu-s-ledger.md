## 6. Sefu's Ledger

Vael Corrin smelled of rain on hot stone and old smoke, a city built in layers like the rings of a felled tree. Ines had never seen anything like it. He had never seen *anything* like it, if he was honest—thirty-one years inside a valley that forgot, where the tallest structure was the gatehouse and the boldest color was moss on slate. Now he walked streets where lanterns burned without flame, where a woman argued with her own shadow in a market stall, where the air itself hummed with a pressure he could feel in his teeth.

Magic. Everywhere. Pressing against his skin like weather.

Quentin walked close beside him, hood up, one hand hooked through Ines's belt loop as though they were lovers instead of fugitives. In some ways they were closer than lovers now. You did not watch a man see the thing behind the door of the world and remain strangers.

"It's loud," Quentin murmured for the tenth time since the valley's edge. "All of it. Like standing inside a bell."

"You get used to it."

"You've been here four hours."

"I'm very good at pretending." Ines steered them down an alley where the wards of the old order had been scratched away generations ago—Sefu's instructions had been specific about which alleys, which doors, which signs to ignore. *Look for the ledger shop with no name. Knock twice, then once, then not at all.* It sounded like nonsense until you understood it was a lock: the third knock would have summoned the Wardens, who watched Sefu's street the way cats watch a mousehole.

The shop sat between a dyer's and a shuttered chapel, its windows fogged with grime, its door wood gone silver with age. Ines knocked twice. Once. Then stood with his hand hovering, heart hammering, until the door opened inward on its own.

Inside: paper. Floor to ceiling, wall to wall, stacked in towers that leaned at angles geometry should not permit. Ledgers, folios, scrolls in oilcloth, loose pages weighted under river stones. A single lamp burned green. And behind a desk buried under a century of correspondence sat a man so old his skin had gone the color of the parchment around him, spectacles thick as bottle bottoms, a quill still moving across a page as though he'd been writing since before the valley existed.

"You're late," Sefu Adeyemi said without looking up. "The moon is eleven days from full. I told your grandmother's grandmother I'd help her line when it mattered. Sit down before my knees change their minds about hospitality."

Ines sat. Quentin remained standing, and Ines felt him trembling—not with fear, exactly. With recognition. The way a tuning fork recognizes a struck note.

"You knew," Ines said. "You knew we'd come."

"I knew *someone* would come, eventually, wearing that expression." Sefu set down his quill and removed his spectacles, and his eyes, magnified, were startlingly young. "The expression of a man who has read half a prophecy and liked neither the ink nor the silence around it. Show me what you found."

Ines drew the page from inside his coat—the page from the keepers' archive, the one he'd stolen three nights ago with hands that shook so badly he'd torn the corner. The name rendered in old ink. *Quentin.* The face sketched in charcoal beneath it, unmistakable even across a hundred years: the same crooked smile, the same eyes that looked at doors as if doors might look back.

Sefu read it the way Ines imagined surgeons read wounds. Then he turned it over, and Ines saw his hands stop.

"There's more," Quentin said quietly. "Isn't there."

* * *

What Sefu brought out next came from beneath the floor—a slab he shifted with a word that made the lamp gutter, revealing a cavity lined in lead. From it he lifted a book bound in grey leather, cracked along the spine, its clasps shaped like interlocking gates.

"The founding keepers' own hand," Sefu said. "Not the archive copies. Not the sanitized liturgy your order recites at each turning of the moon. The original. Written by Halvard Okafor and his six companions in the year the war ended." His gaze flicked to Ines, deliberate. "Okafor. Your line kept the gate from the beginning. Did they never tell you what else they wrote?"

"They told us the gate was mercy," Ines said. "That sealing the power ended the war. That our family's duty was vigilance."

"Vigilance." Sefu turned the word over like a coin he suspected was counterfeit. He opened the book.

The pages were dense, cramped, annotated in at least three different hands across decades. Sefu moved through them with terrible patience until he reached a leaf marked with a pressed sprig of something silvered and dead. Then he turned the book toward Ines and said nothing at all.

Ines read.

*And the Veil shall thin upon the thirteenth turn, and the vessel shall come to it, named thus:* —and there was the name, Quentin's name, in Halvard Okafor's own careful script— *and the power shall know its door, and pass through, and be bound anew.*

"Bound anew," Ines repeated. "Renewed. That's—that's what the archive says. The vessel renews the Veil. That's the whole of it. A renewal, not a—" He stopped, because Sefu was shaking his head slowly, and because the old man's face held the particular grief of someone who has carried bad news a long way.

"Read the marginalia," Sefu said. "The part in the second hand. The part your order cut when they copied it into the archive."

Ines read the margin. The handwriting was different—sharper, later—but the words were worse.

*The binding requires a living hinge. The vessel does not renew the Veil; the vessel IS the renewal. What passes through the gate does not return. This is not a flaw in the design. It is the design.*

The room went very quiet. Somewhere outside, the city went on humming, indifferent.

"No," Ines said. It came out small. He cleared his throat and tried again. "No. The keepers weren't—they sealed the power to *end* the killing. Everything my family did, every night at that gate, every ritual, every year of my life spent being boring on purpose so no one would ever look too closely—we were keeping people safe. We were the reason the war stayed over."

"Were you?" Sefu asked gently, and it was the gentleness that made it unbearable.

Ines read the page again. Then the next. Then the letters folded between the pages, correspondence between the founding keepers themselves, and the picture assembled itself with the slow, sick certainty of a nightmare becoming morning. Halvard Okafor had not merely sealed the power. He had written a *prophecy*—had dressed a mechanism in the language of fate, had scattered it where seekers would find it, had let a hundred years of mystics and scholars and desperate fools believe destiny spoke those words. Because a prophecy could travel where a trap could not. Because a prophecy would walk itself, willingly, eagerly, to the gate.

They hadn't built a lock. They'd built bait.

"The thirteenth turn," Ines heard himself say. "The Veil doesn't thin on its own schedule. It thins because the prophecy pulls the vessel there. The whole deadline—it's not astronomy. It's a lure with a fuse."

"Now you understand why I stopped answering letters from your valley forty years ago," Sefu said. "I found this book as a young man. I wrote to your grandfather's grandfather to ask how the order could possibly not know. His reply thanked me for my concern and informed me that the keepers' duty was to the letter of the rite, not its interpretation." His mouth twisted. "Either they never read their own founding text—which I doubt—or they read it and chose the lie, because the lie was load-bearing. Because without it, what are they? Not guardians. Butchers with good posture."

Quentin had not spoken. Ines turned and found him standing very still, one hand flat against a tower of ledgers as though steadying himself against the weight of the room. When he finally looked up, his expression wasn't fear. It was something that frightened Ines far more: clarity.

"So everyone who ever told me I was special," Quentin said, "every dream, every pull, every time I felt like a door was waiting for me somewhere—I wasn't chosen. I was *addressed*. Like a letter. Someone wrote my name on an envelope a hundred years before I was born, and everything since has just been me walking myself to the mailbox."

"Quentin—"

"Don't." But his voice cracked on it, and he laughed, wet and awful. "You know the worst part? It worked. I *feel* it. Whatever they put in the ink, whatever hook they baited—I feel the thirteenth turn coming like it's a homecoming. They designed me to want this."

Ines crossed the room and took Quentin's face in both hands, the way he had at the gate, the night everything broke open. "Listen to me. They wrote a prophecy. They didn't write you. There is a difference, and I will spend whatever remains of my life making sure you feel that difference in your bones."

"That's very romantic," Quentin whispered, "for a man who lied to me for a week to keep me away from all this."

"Yes, well. I'm told guilt makes excellent fertilizer."

Something loosened, barely—a huff of breath that was almost a laugh. Sefu watched them both over his spectacles with the expression of a man adding a final entry to a very long ledger.

* * *

Later, by the green lamp, Sefu laid out the rest of it like cards on a table. The mage faction—those who called themselves the Renewalists—had gotten hold of fragments of the true text decades ago. They didn't want to save Quentin from the prophecy. They wanted to *fulfill* it early, on their terms, harvesting the power as it passed through him. And the Wardens, the old order's enforcers, wanted the vessel delivered intact to the gate for the orthodox ritual—which was to say, they wanted the same thing as the faction, with better manners and cleaner vestments.

"Two fists," Sefu said, "closing on the same throat. And the moon will be full in eleven days whether anyone closes or not."

"Then we don't go to the gate," Ines said. "We don't fulfill it. We break it."

Sefu raised his eyebrows. "Break a prophecy?"

"It's not a prophecy. It's a document. Documents can be forged—" his voice caught on the word, on the enormity of it, on a hundred years of his family kneeling at a lie— "and forged documents can be *burned*. Tell me what it would take."

The old man was quiet for a long moment. Outside, the city bells rang the ninth hour, and the sound came through the walls like water through stone.

"What it would take," Sefu said finally, "is more than two people and a dead scholar. It would take a Warden who knows the sanctum rites from the inside. It would take someone who can stand at the Veil's edge without being devoured by it. It would take," and here he smiled, thin and dry, "a circle. Which is fortunate, because I have been collecting strays for forty years, and I believe two of them are already looking for you."

He tapped the ledger before him—an entry fresh in ink, hours old. Ines leaned over and read it, and felt the floor tilt gently beneath his chair.

*A Warden, defected, requesting sanctuary. Claims knowledge of the Renewalists' sanctum. Name: Bryn Vashti.*

And beneath it, in different ink, stranger and older:

*A spirit of the half-Veil, self-delivered. Says it has been watching the gate for a hundred years. Says it knows what the vessel looks like when it walks. Asks, in exchange for its help, only to be remembered.*

Ines looked up. "When do they arrive?"

Sefu glanced at the lamp, at the window, at the moonless dark beyond the glass. "The spirit is already here," he said. "It's been in the shop since sundown. It's the reason the lamp burns green."

And the lamp, very softly, flickered—as if something in the room had just exhaled.
