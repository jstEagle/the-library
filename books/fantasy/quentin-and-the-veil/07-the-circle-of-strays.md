## 7. The Circle of Strays

The safehouse Sefu had promised turned out to be a cistern.

That was the first thing I understood about Vael Corrin: it kept its kindnesses underground. We came down through a hatch behind a dyer's shop, past shelves of pickled eels that Ines refused to look at, and into a vaulted chamber where water had once been stored and now only echoed. Someone had made a home in the dark anyway—cots, a stove, strings of dried peppers, a wall of books stacked like sandbags against despair.

"You'll forgive the accommodations," Sefu said, lighting lamps with a snap of his fingers that made me flinch. Magic still did that to me—arrived without warning, like a stranger's hand on my shoulder. "The Wardens know my face above ground. The faction knows my handwriting. Between them, I have become a man who lives with eels."

Ines set down our packs and looked around the room the way he looked at everything now—like he was memorizing it in case he had to mourn it later. "How long can we stay?"

"Here? As long as you like." Sefu's smile was thin and warm at once, the smile of a man who had learned to give comfort in small denominations. "Out there—" he tipped his head toward the ceiling, toward the city, toward everything hunting us— "I'd say three days. Perhaps four. They lost your trail at the river, but the faction has scent-hounds bound with Veil-glass, and you, Quentin, are beginning to smell like an open door."

"Thank you," I said. "That's exactly what I wanted to hear on my first week of knowing magic exists."

He laughed—a real laugh, startled out of him—and for a moment the cistern felt less like a hiding place and more like a kitchen where someone had told a good joke. I was learning to treasure those moments. I was learning they had to be treasured quickly, because they didn't keep.

* * *

Bryn arrived on the second night, and she arrived armed, which is how I learned that trust, in this city, was something you extended at knifepoint and hoped grew roots.

She came down the ladder backward, one hand on her blade, and stopped when she saw me. For a long moment neither of us moved. She had Warden grey still in her coat, worn soft at the elbows, and eyes that catalogued exits the way other people catalogued faces.

"So," she said. "The prophecy-mark."

"Bryn." Sefu's voice carried a warning in it, gentle but real.

"I defected six weeks ago," she said, not taking her eyes off me. "I want that on the record before anyone gets sentimental. I spent nine years hunting people like him. I'm not going to pretend one fire and a bad conscience makes me family."

"No one's asking you to pretend," Ines said quietly. He stepped slightly forward—not between us, exactly, but into the space where a blow would land if it came. I'd seen him do that all week. It wasn't protectiveness, or not only. It was habit. A keeper's habit. Standing where the door was thinnest.

Bryn studied him, then me, then sheathed her knife with a motion sharp enough to be an insult. "Fine. Point me at a task and stop looking at me like I'm a rescue."

"You could start by telling us how close they are," Sefu said.

"Close." She dropped onto a cot like her bones had been borrowed from someone older. "The Wardens think you're heading north to the old shrines. The faction knows better—they've stopped chasing and started herding. There's a woman called Vess Corvane leading their ritual team. She doesn't want to catch you, Quentin. She wants you cornered somewhere with a moon overhead and nowhere left to run but through."

The words settled over the room like cold water. I sat down on the floor because standing suddenly seemed ambitious.

"And the thirteenth turn?" Ines asked.

"Nine days," Bryn said. "Maybe ten, if the weather turns."

* * *

Moss came on the third night, and Moss changed everything, though it took me until the fire to understand why.

Sefu heard the knock himself—three slow raps, then two fast, some code from another decade—and opened the hatch with an expression I hadn't seen on him before. Hope, maybe, or its anxious cousin.

The thing that climbed down was not quite a person. It wore a person loosely, the way light wears a window: a figure of grey-green, hair like lichen, eyes the color of deep water under ice. Where it stepped, the damp stone bloomed faintly with moss that faded behind it like a wake.

"This is Moss," Sefu said. "Half-born on the far side of the Veil, half-raised on this one. A child of the seam."

"A child of a mistake," Moss corrected. Its voice was soft and layered, like wind through reeds. It looked at me, and I felt the look the way I felt magic—directly, without permission. "You're the vessel. You smell like home."

"I smell like *what*?"

"The other side." Moss tilted its head. "The sealed place. I was born near it, in the years after the war, when the Veil still wept a little. Everything there is quiet, Quentin. Everything there is patient. And lately—" it pressed a hand flat against its own chest, as if listening— "lately the patience has been ending. It knows you're coming. It isn't angry. That's the worst part. It's *grateful*."

I thought about the dream—the door, the warmth behind it, the sense of being expected. I had told myself it was longing. Everyone longs for a door that opens onto welcome.

But gratitude. Gratitude was different. Gratitude meant it thought I was bringing something.

"Then we don't go near it," Ines said, in the voice he used for facts. "We find another way. Sefu, the second half of the ledger—you said the founding keepers wrote the prophecy themselves. If they wrote it, it can be unwritten. Amended. Prophecies are just ink with good publicity."

Moss made a sound that might have been laughter. "You want to argue with a hundred-year-old spell using footnotes."

"I've been a bureaucrat of the sacred for twenty years," Ines said. "Footnotes are my native tongue."

And there it was again—that moment, small and stupid and enormous, where I loved him. Not the dramatic way, not the way songs mean. The way you love someone who stands between you and the thin part of the door.

* * *

We built the fire in the center of the cistern, in an old iron basin, because Bryn insisted open flame lifted morale and Sefu insisted the stone would hide the smoke-scent, and because, I think, none of us wanted to admit we were doing it for the light.

We roasted the last of the peppers. Bryn produced a flask of plum brandy from somewhere in her Warden coat and dared anyone to comment. Sefu read aloud from his ledger, translating the dead keepers' cramped script into something almost like a story, and Moss lay with its head in my lap—which should have been strange, but its hair smelled like rain on stone, and it hummed a note I recognized from the dream, and somehow that made it restful instead of terrifying.

"Do you know what I did," Bryn said, apropos of nothing, staring into the fire, "the day I defected?"

No one answered. She answered herself.

"I went back to my posting. Signed out. Filed my reports. Made sure the junior wardens under me had their winter pay sorted." A short, bitter laugh. "Nine years hunting exiles, and my last act as a Warden was paperwork. I couldn't just *leave*. Leaving felt like admitting I'd been wrong the whole time, and I wasn't ready. So I finished my shift first."

"That's not cowardice," I said. "That's just being a person."

She looked at me like I'd spoken a language she'd half-forgotten. "The Wardens teach that people like you are weather. Something that happens to the world. Not someone things happen *to*."

"Well," I said, "the weather has opinions now."

That got a laugh out of everyone—even Moss, whose laughter was less a sound than a feeling, like sun through fog. Even Ines, who had been carrying the weight of every archive page like stones in his pockets, laughed, really laughed, and put his arm around my shoulders, and for a moment the cistern was just a room full of people who were tired in compatible ways.

"My order cast me out for asking questions," Sefu said, turning his cup slowly. "Bryn's order will kill her for answering them. Moss was never allowed to belong to anything. And you two—" he gestured at Ines and me with the weary affection of a man cataloguing a very specific disaster— "you are a keeper who broke his vows to tell the truth, and a prophecy who refuses to be read."

"When you say it like that," I said, "we sound like the world's worst book club."

"We sound," Sefu said, "like a circle."

The word landed strangely. I felt it more than heard it—in my chest, in the mark beneath my skin, which had been a cold weight since the ambush and which, for one heartbeat, warmed.

Around the fire, five people who fit nowhere fit together, the way broken edges do: jaggedly, improbably, completely. I thought about the valley I'd never see again, about the life I'd had before a dream of a door, about how I'd spent twenty-six years believing belonging was something you found, a place you were born into or lucked into. Nobody had told me it could be built. Nobody had told me it could be built out of defectors and exiles and half-ghosts and a man who hid miracles behind a boring routine.

"Nine days," Bryn said eventually, into the quiet.

"Then we have eight to make a plan and one to panic," Sefu said.

"Make it seven," Moss murmured, drowsy against my knee. "The Veil is thinning faster than your calendars. It leans toward him now. Time bends around wanting."

The fire cracked. Somewhere above us, the city went on with its ordinary night—carts, arguments, someone singing badly three streets away—and I held onto those sounds like proof that the world was still worth the fight.

Ines squeezed my shoulder. "Whatever comes," he said quietly, "we do it together. No prophecies. No orders. Just us deciding."

"Just us deciding," I repeated, and the mark under my skin pulsed once, softly, like something on the other side of a door had heard—and agreed.

Then Bryn sat up so fast her cup spilled. "Someone's at the hatch," she whispered. "Three slow raps. Two fast."

"But that's our code," Sefu said. "No one else knows it."

The knock came again. Three slow. Two fast.

And then, in a voice none of us knew, muffled through the iron: *"They have Vess Corvane's seal. Open the hatch. I'm the reason you lost them at the river—and I need to tell you what the faction is planning for the twelfth moon."*

Bryn's hand went to her knife. Ines rose without a sound, keeper-quick, positioning himself between the hatch and me.

Above us, patiently, the knock came a third time.
