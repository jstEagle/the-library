## 8. Turn of the Twelfth Moon

The twelfth moon rose thin as a paring of bone, and Bryn was gone.

Ines knew it before he opened his eyes—knew it the way he had once known the Hollow Gate's moods, through a silence that had a shape to it. The fire in their camp beneath the aqueduct ruins had burned down to red eyes in the ash. Sefu's bedroll was empty but warm. Moss hung where Moss always hung at night, a pale suggestion of a figure against the broken archway, half here and half somewhere the eye couldn't follow.

"Bryn," Ines said to the dark.

Moss's voice came like wind through reeds. "Gone since the moon turned. Walked out soft. I felt him pass through me and said nothing." A pause, long enough for shame to pool in it. "I thought—I hoped he was only walking."

Bryn had been walking every night for a week. Ines had told himself it was a soldier's habit, the old Warden restlessness, a body that didn't trust sleep. He had told himself a great many comfortable things because Quentin needed them to be true, because they all needed this strange little family to hold.

He stood. His knees cracked. Forty years of kneeling at a gate lived in his joints now.

"Sefu," he called softly, and the old scholar emerged from behind the fallen column with her ledger already clutched to her chest, spectacles pushed up into gray-streaked hair. She had not slept either. None of them had slept properly since the Wardens' horns had sounded twice from the eastern ridges three nights past.

"He went back to them," Sefu said. It wasn't a question. She had read Bryn the way she read prophecies—looking for what was suppressed between the lines. "They have his sister, Ines. They showed him the letters. What would you have done?"

"I'd have told us," Ines said, and heard how hollow that was, how easy, spoken by a man who had spent thirty years telling no one anything.

* * *

They found the trail an hour past dawn, or rather Moss found it, drifting along the ground like fog with a purpose. Bryn had left signs only a Warden would read—and, apparently, only a half-Veil spirit could see: a bent sprig of rowan, a cairn of three stones, marks that meant *followed against my will* in the old order's silent code, and then, heartbreakingly near the end, marks that meant nothing at all. Marks made by a hand too tired to lie anymore.

The trail ended at the mouth of the Karst Vale, where the mage faction's sanctum had been carved into the living rock two centuries ago, when the exiles first came down from the mountains with their grudges and their beautiful, terrible theories. Ines had seen it once, years ago, on the one diplomatic errand the keepers had ever asked of him. He remembered white stone and cold courtesy and the sense of standing inside a throat.

Now he stood at its teeth.

"They'll have wards keyed to intent," Sefu murmured beside him. She had insisted on coming, ledger strapped across her back like armor. "You cannot want your way in. You cannot plan your way in. The door reads desire."

"Then we go in wanting nothing," Ines said.

Moss laughed—a sound like water finding a crack. "Oh, keeper. No one wants nothing. That's why doors were invented."

* * *

Quentin was inside. Ines knew that the way he knew the gate's silences, except this silence screamed. Since the circle had formed—since that firelit night when five people who fit nowhere had somehow fit together—he had carried a thread of awareness of Quentin, faint as warmth from a hearth in another room. The mages had cut it two days ago, cleanly, the way you'd sever a rope. He had felt the moment of cutting and had told himself Bryn was simply leading Quentin somewhere safe.

Lying to himself was, Ines reflected, the one magic he had never needed wards to perform.

Sefu spread her ledger on a flat stone and turned to the page she had shown him in Vael Corrin—the second half of the prophecy, the half the founding keepers had written in cipher and buried under pious language. *And the vessel shall pour itself out, and the Veil shall drink, and be renewed.* Quentin, burned out as fuel. Used up and discarded by people who called it destiny.

"The front entrance is a formality," Sefu said. "There is another way. The founders built these sancta on Veil-adjacent stone—places where the boundary is thin. Moss can walk the thin places."

Moss drifted closer, and for a moment their outline sharpened, almost solid, almost sad. "I can carry one of you through the between. Not two. Not three. And it will hurt you in ways I can't describe and you can't imagine."

"One," Ines said. "Me."

"Ines—" Sefu began.

"No." He surprised himself with how steady his voice came out. All his life he had asked permission—to open the gate, to close it, to live, to love, to hope. He had knelt before rules so long he'd mistaken the floor for holy ground. Three days ago he had learned that the law he served was written by liars, and something in him had finished cooling, the way metal finishes cooling, going from red to black to hard.

"Sefu, you're the scholar. If there's a ritual running in there, you're the one who can unwrite it—but you need time and quiet, and you get neither if you're crawling through the between. Moss takes me. I find Quentin. I buy you your time."

"And what exactly will you do," Sefu said quietly, "when you find him?"

Ines looked at the sanctum's white face, at the light leaking gold from its high windows, gold like the color the Veil turned when it thinned.

"Whatever I have to," he said. "I'm done asking."

* * *

The between was worse than Moss had promised and different than Ines feared. It was not darkness; it was *unwritten*—a place where his body felt like a rumor, where he passed through the memory of stone, where somewhere very far away a door he had tended for thirty years seemed to call his name like a mother calling a child home late. Moss held him the whole way, a grip like cold silk, and whispered—not comfort, exactly, but company:

"You love him loudly, keeper. Even in here, where loudness should be impossible. It's the loudest thing about you."

Then the world slammed back, and Ines was on his knees on cold mosaic tile, in a corridor lit by lamps that burned without flame, and somewhere ahead, chanting had already begun.

He moved. He did not creep, though everything in his training said creep; he walked fast and low with a stolen kitchen knife in one hand and a length of iron pipe in the other, because he was not a swordsman and had never pretended to be. Twice he pressed himself into alcoves as acolytes passed, robed in white shot through with gold. Once, a young woman looked directly at him—and Moss poured out of the wall behind her, a bloom of pale wrongness, and she fled screaming without raising any alarm that mattered.

The chanting grew. It came up through the floors now, resonant, rhythmic, and beneath it Ines felt the other thing: the pull. The same pull he had felt at the Hollow Gate all those years, the pressure of something vast leaning against a thin place—but inverted, eager, *invited*. They weren't holding the Veil shut. They were peeling it open.

He found the ritual hall by following the light—gold pouring from beneath double doors banded in bronze, gold flickering in the seams like the whole room behind them was on fire in slow motion.

He also found Bryn.

The big man sat slumped against the wall opposite the doors, guarded by no one, which told Ines everything: they hadn't needed to guard him. His hands were chained—not to restrain his body but wrapped in warded iron that ate at the skin, and his eyes were red-rimmed and empty, fixed on the doors.

Bryn saw him and flinched like a man expecting a blow.

"Ines. Gods. Ines, I—" His voice broke apart. "They had Mara's letters. Her handwriting. They said if I didn't— they said they'd only need her until the renewal, and after the renewal—" He stopped, because the sentence had nowhere honest to go. After the renewal, Quentin would be ash, and promises to collaborators would be worth exactly that.

"How long has the ritual been running?" Ines asked. Flat. Cold. He did not recognize his own voice and decided he could live with that.

"Since moonrise. They're building toward the thirteenth turn—they want it *at* the turn, full force, no margin. Quentin's on the dais. He's awake, Ines. They've got him awake for it, they say the vessel must consent, some legal fiction, they make him say yes over and over—" Bryn's chains rattled as his shoulders shook. "I told myself I was saving my sister. I don't know what I told myself anymore."

Ines crouched, studied the warded iron, and did the only thing he could think of: he took Bryn's chained hands in his own and pulled, hard, the way he had once pulled a jammed gate-bar in a flood year, with his whole history in his arms. The ward flared, bit into his palms, and shattered—cheap work, mass-produced coercion, and rage, it turned out, was a key that fit most locks.

"Go find Sefu," Ines said. "She's outside the vale with a ledger and a grudge. Tell her the ritual's live and I'm going through those doors. Tell her to start unwriting."

Bryn stared at him, wrists bleeding, free. "They'll kill you."

"Probably," Ines agreed, and stood, and put his hand on the bronze-banded door.

It was warm as skin. Through it, faintly, he could hear Quentin's voice—hoarse, repeating something, *yes, yes, yes*, each word dragged out of him like a nail.

Ines thought of a valley that forgot itself so it could stay safe. Of a boy who dreamed of a door. Of a prophecy written by liars, and a seal kept by fools, and a family assembled out of everyone the world had thrown away. He thought: *I kept a gate for thirty years. I know exactly how doors break.*

He stopped asking permission—from the keepers dead three centuries, from laws written in bad faith, from the universe itself.

He shoved the doors open onto a blaze of gold, onto a ring of chanting mages, onto a dais where Quentin stood bound in light and lifted his head—and even now, even burning, even used—his eyes found Ines first.

"*Yes,*" Quentin gasped—but this time, Ines realized, he wasn't answering them.

He was answering the thing behind the Veil. And he was smiling.
