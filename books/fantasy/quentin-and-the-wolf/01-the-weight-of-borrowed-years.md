## 1. The Weight of Borrowed Years

The merchant on the Salt Stair road was humming when Paloma found him.

That was the first thing she noticed about him — the humming. It drifted up through the pines in a tuneless, cheerful warble, the kind of sound a man makes only when he believes himself entirely alone and entirely safe. It was a good tune, if you could call it a tune. It wandered off-key, doubled back on itself, gave up twice and started over. Paloma crouched behind a granite outcrop with her pack settled against her shoulder blades and thought: *there is a man who has never once been robbed.*

She intended to correct that.

Below her, where the Salt Stair switchbacked down toward the river crossing, the merchant had stopped to rest his mule. He was a broad man gone soft at the middle, with a beard the color of wet sand and a coat too fine for this road — dyed wool, brass buttons, the kind of coat a man wears when he wants the world to know he's done well. A spice trader, by the smell of him. Cinnamon and clove rolled off his cart in waves so thick Paloma could taste them from thirty yards up the slope.

He'd spread a cloth on a flat stone and laid out his lunch: bread, hard cheese, a flask of something amber. And beside the flask, glinting in the slant of afternoon light, sat the reason Paloma had followed him for two days.

A memory-vial. Silver-capped, no bigger than her thumb, hanging from a chain around his neck like it was a saint's relic instead of what it actually was — which was, as far as she could tell, somebody's wedding day.

She'd seen him buy it three nights ago in the market town of Lowmark, from a dealer with ink-stained fingers and eyes like a man who counted everything twice. The merchant had held it up to the lantern light, turned it, laughed at something the dealer said, and paid for it with the easy flourish of a man who had more coin than sense. A keepsake, probably. A curiosity. Rich men bought memories the way they bought exotic birds — to have them, to show them, never to understand them.

Paloma had been following him ever since, waiting for exactly this moment: alone, unguarded, humming.

*Never keep what you steal.* Her grandmother's rule, drilled into her before she could write her own name. *You lift it, you sell it, you forget it. The moment you keep a memory that isn't yours, it starts keeping you.*

Paloma had broken that rule exactly once, and she did not think about the once. She thought about the coin instead, because coin was simple and honest and didn't look back at you.

She moved.

Down through the pines, quiet as falling snow, each footfall placed with the care of a woman who had learned young that the mountains remember careless people. The smuggler roads were hers — all of them, every goat track and washed-out stair and forgotten pilgrim path stitched through the Thornfell highlands like veins through a wrist. Other thieves needed maps. Paloma needed only her feet and the particular silence of knowing exactly where she was, which was a kind of wealth most kings never touched.

The merchant didn't hear her. Of course he didn't. She came around the outcrop onto the switchback below him, then climbed back up on the blind side, arriving at his little picnic as though she'd grown out of the hillside.

"Mind if I share?" she said.

The merchant startled so badly he nearly upset the flask. His hand went to his belt knife, then stopped, because Paloma Thackeray standing in the open with her palms visible and a friendly crook to her mouth did not look like a threat. She looked like weather. She looked like something that had always been there.

"Saints," he breathed. "Where did you—"

"Path above." She nodded at nothing in particular; there was no path above, but he'd believe there was, because people believed whatever filled the gap where their own attention should have been. "You've got cheese. I've got nothing but appetite. Seems an unfair arrangement."

He laughed. They always laughed, at first. That was the craft of it — not the lifting, which was almost incidental, but the arriving. You couldn't take a memory from a clenched mind any more than you could take bread from a fist. You had to be let in.

"You're a long way from anywhere, girl," the merchant said, but he shifted over on his stone, making room. "These roads aren't safe."

"No," Paloma agreed solemnly, sitting. "Terrible. Full of thieves."

His name was Aldous Vey, though she wouldn't learn that until later, and he traded spice out of Marrowgate, and he talked — saints, how he talked. About the roads, about the price of saffron, about his brother who'd married badly and his daughter who hadn't married at all and his mule, whose name was Duchess and who, according to Aldous, held grudges with the patience of a saint and the memory of an elephant. Paloma ate his cheese and asked questions in the right places and laughed once, genuinely, at the story of Duchess refusing to cross a bridge for an entire afternoon out of what Aldous insisted was principle.

And underneath the laughing, her fingers were working.

It wasn't sleight of hand, not really. Sleight of hand was for card sharps and cutpurses, people who moved objects. What Paloma moved was attention. She'd learned it from her grandmother, who'd learned it from nobody anyone would name: the trick of touching a person's awareness the way you'd touch a soap bubble — lightly, from beneath, where it couldn't feel you. You found the bright thread of what they were attending to — the story, the laugh, the warmth of the sun — and you pulled it gently sideways, and while their whole self leaned after it, you slipped your other hand into the space they'd left open.

Every mind kept its memories in vials, whether it knew it or not. Little sealed moments, stoppered with feeling. Most people carried hundreds. Some carried thousands, hoarded like Aldous hoarded spice, and never once opened the good ones.

Aldous's wedding-day vial hung at his throat, and the thread of his attention was wrapped around his daughter — *unmarried, twenty-six, stubborn as her mother* — and the space he'd left open was wide as a barn door.

Paloma reached across for the cheese knife. It was a small motion, natural, forgettable. Her knuckles brushed the vial where it lay against his chest, silver cap to skin, and she whispered the word her grandmother had taught her, the one that had no letters and lived under language:

*Unstopper.*

The memory rose into her like a swallow of hot wine.

She saw it all in the half-second it took Aldous to notice his knife had moved: a chapel with blue smoke and too many candles, a bride laughing into her veil because Aldous had stepped on her hem, his own younger hands trembling as he fumbled the ring, his mother weeping in the third pew, sunlight coming through glass the color of honey. Joy, whole and uncomplicated, the kind that doesn't know yet what it will cost.

Paloma lifted it out clean, the way you'd lift an egg from a nest, and sealed it into the empty vial she kept in her sleeve, and set the filled vial down beside the cheese, and all of it happened inside the time it took Aldous Vey to say:

"—and that's why I don't trust bridges, come to think of it. Girl? You've gone pale."

"Just hungry," Paloma said, and smiled, and hated herself a little, and smiled anyway.

* * *

Here is the thing about stealing memories that no ballad gets right: it doesn't hurt the thief at all. Not during. During, it feels like being handed the sun.

Afterward is different. Afterward, you're walking down a mountain road with somebody's wedding day sealed in your pocket, and you can still feel the shape of it against your ribs — the candle-smoke, the bride's laugh, the trembling hands — and you know, with a certainty that sits in the stomach like a swallowed stone, that none of it is yours. That you are a courier. A pipe through which other people's happiness flows on its way to market. That somewhere in Marrowgate a collector will pay you forty crowns for a stranger's joy, and the joy will sit in a glass case in a rich man's study, gathering dust, belonging to no one.

Paloma had been a pipe for eleven years. Pipes don't get to drink.

She sold Aldous's wedding day in the border town of Cinderhatch two days later, to a broker named Fenwick who kept his shop behind a taxidermist's and paid in unmarked silver because marked silver asked questions. Forty crowns. Fair rate for a first-rate memory — joy ran higher than grief, oddly enough; grief was common, joy was scarce, and scarcity set the price the way it always does.

"He won't miss it?" Fenwick asked, as he asked every time, weighing the vial in his palm.

"He'll miss it the way you miss a coat you lent someone years ago," Paloma said. "He'll know it's gone somewhere. He won't know where, and mostly he won't think about it." She took the silver. "That's the mercy of minds. They paper over the holes."

"And you? You never tempted?"

"Fenwick." She said it patiently, the way you'd explain rain to a fish. "I'm a thief, not a magpie."

But she walked out of Cinderhatch with forty crowns and an ache she couldn't spend, and the ache had a shape she recognized, and the shape was a chapel full of blue smoke and a bride laughing, and the truth underneath it, which was this: Paloma Thackeray knew other people's happiest moments better than she knew her own. She had carried hundreds of them. Weddings, births, first snows, last embraces, the moment a shy boy finally kissed the girl at the well — she had held them all, briefly, warmly, and given every one away, and what remained in her own keeping was a professional emptiness, tidy as an empty shelf.

Her grandmother had left her two things: the rule, and the roads. Sometimes Paloma suspected those were the same thing.

* * *

The trouble started, as trouble usually did, with money that looked wrong.

She was three towns past Cinderhatch, resting her boots in a smuggler's den called the Drowned Bell — a cellar tavern where the ceiling sweated and nobody used real names — when a stranger came down the stairs.

She noticed his hands first. Everyone else in the Drowned Bell had hands like tools: scarred, greasy, honest. This man's hands were pale and still, folded on the table like something waiting to be unwrapped. He ordered nothing. He simply sat in the corner farthest from the fire, in a coat of undyed wool that cost more than everything the taxidermist next door owned, and he watched her with the patience of a man who had already decided how the evening would go.

Paloma finished her cider. Told herself she'd finish her cider and leave by the back way.

He crossed the room before she could stand.

"Mistress Thackeray." His voice was soft and cold, a cellar voice. "They said on the Salt Stair road I might find the finest thief in the highlands. They said you could lift a memory from a sleeping bear without waking it."

"They flatter me," Paloma said. "Bears wake up furious."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth. It might have been the ancestor of a smile. He sat down across from her without asking, which told her he was either very rude or very dangerous, and the stillness of those pale hands suggested both.

"I have work," he said. "Retrieval work. There is a vault in the old Thornfell country — deep country, the kind no map admits to. Inside it, a single memory-vial, sealed a century ago. I want it brought to me. Unopened."

"A century-old vial." Paloma turned her cup slowly on the table. "Sealed memories go flat. Whatever's in it will be stale as last winter's bread. Who pays for stale bread?"

"I do." He set something on the table between them, and the cellar went quiet around it.

Coins. Old ones. Not the current crown mint with its smug profile of the usurper king — these were older, heavier, struck with a sigil Paloma knew from the boundary stones and the oldest songs: a wolf's head, jaws open, crowned.

Royal coin. Pre-Wolf coin. Worth ten times its weight to collectors, and worth considerably more than that to whoever minted it, and Paloma felt the greed arrive in her chest like an old friend who doesn't knock.

"That's a great deal of money," she said carefully.

"It is a deposit." He slid half the coins across. "The rest when the vial reaches my hand. One condition, Mistress Thackeray, and it is absolute: you do not open it. You do not unstopper it, you do not taste it, you do not let it touch your skin longer than handling requires. Do you understand what I am telling you?"

"I understand you think I'm an amateur."

"I understand," the pale man said, and now the coldness in his voice had a floor under it, something like fear, "that some memories are not stale. Some memories wait. And some doors are locked for the protection of the thief."

The fire popped. Somewhere upstairs, a smuggler laughed at a joke Paloma would never hear. She looked at the coins — the crowned wolf gleaming up at her, patient, hungry — and she thought about her empty shelf, and her rule, and the fact that rent was due on her loft in Harrow's Bend and the roof leaked and the landlord had teeth.

She thought about all of that, and she heard herself say:

"When do I leave?"

The pale man's still hands unfolded at last, and he pushed a scrap of paper across the table — directions, written in a spidery antique script, to a vault in the deep Thornfell country — and as Paloma took it, their fingers brushed, just barely, and for half a heartbeat she caught the edge of what he was.

Not a man. Something wearing the idea of a man, the way a coat wears shoulders.

Then it was gone, smooth as a card vanishing up a sleeve, and the stranger stood, and bowed slightly, and said, "Three weeks. The Drowned Bell. Come alone," and climbed the stairs and left the smell of cold stone behind him.

Paloma sat a long while with the coins in her palm and the directions in her lap, and the fire burned low, and the sweating walls dripped their slow salt water, and she told herself the shiver at the base of her spine was just the cellar draft.

Old royal coin. A century-sealed vial. A client with no heartbeat she could find.

Her grandmother's voice, from so long ago it lived in her bones more than her memory, said: *Some jobs are bait, little wren. The question is never whether the hook is gilded. The question is what's holding the line.*

Paloma closed her fist around the crowned wolf and grinned at the dark, because she was lonely, and broke, and very good at her work, and those three things together had always been more than enough to make her stupid.

"Deep Thornfell, then," she said to nobody. "What's the worst that could be in a hundred-year-old bottle?"

The Drowned Bell, being a cellar, did not answer. But somewhere far north, under stone older than kingdoms, something in a sealed vial turned over in its sleep — and waited for her.
