## 2. A Client with Cold Hands

The rain came down on Millbrook in the particular sideways way that meant the season was turning, and Paloma Thackeray sat in the back booth of the Drowned Hen with her boots propped on a stool nobody else wanted and her fingers wrapped around a cup of cider she had no intention of drinking.

Cider at the Hen was a gamble. The last three cups had been water with ambitions. This one, she'd determined by smell alone, was mostly vinegar with a rumor of apple, and she was keeping it purely as an excuse to occupy the booth. Booths mattered. In her trade, you learned quickly that people told you more when they thought they'd chosen where to sit.

She was pretending to read a broadsheet — *WOLF SIGHTINGS ON THE THORNFELL ROAD? PROBABLY JUST DOGS, SAY OFFICIALS* — which was doing its job beautifully, because it made her look bored, and bored people were furniture. Furniture got left alone.

The stranger arrived at the ninth bell, exactly as the note had promised, and Paloma knew him before he cleared the door, because he was the only person in Millbrook who seemed to have brought his own weather with him.

He was tall in the way of things that have been stretched rather than grown. Pale — not sickly pale, but pale like paper, like something that had spent a very long time away from suns of any kind. His coat was good wool, cut well but worn wrong; the shoulders sat as though the coat had been fitted to a slightly different man and this one was borrowing it. And when he crossed the room, the lamplight bent around him in a manner she might have called her imagination if she hadn't spent eleven years training that imagination out of superstition and into craft.

Cold, then. She'd had cold clients before. Grief ran cold. Guilt ran colder. She braced for either.

"Thackeray." He said it quietly, standing over her table without asking to sit, which was rude, and then he did ask, in a voice like a drawer sliding open, "May I?" — which retroactively forgave it. People who remembered their manners after forgetting them were usually safe. Usually.

"You found my note," she said.

"I found your note." He sat across from her, folding himself down joint by joint, and placed his hands flat on the scarred wood between them.

His hands were white as candle tallow, and — here was the thing she would turn over later, much later, when it was far too late to be useful — they did not fog. The room was warm enough that her own cup breathed steam. His breath, when he spoke, made nothing at all.

"I'm told," he said, "that you retrieve things."

"I retrieve memories." She kept her voice pleasant and flat, the voice of a tradeswoman quoting prices. "From heads, usually. Sometimes from houses. I don't do objects unless the object remembers something."

"And if I asked for an object that remembers?"

"Then I'd ask why you couldn't just buy it."

"Because it's locked in a vault," he said, "in Thornfell."

The word dropped into the conversation and sat there like a stone in a soup. Around them, the Drowned Hen went on being the Drowned Hen — the tapman arguing with a fishwife about the dignity of eels, two carters losing gracefully at dice, someone's dog dreaming audibly under a bench — but Paloma felt the temperature of her own attention change. Thornfell. Everyone had heard of Thornfell the way everyone had heard of the bottom of the sea. It was the old royal seat, up past the pass, sealed since the Usurper took the crown forty years ago. Sealed, officially, "for the kingdom's protection," which in Paloma's experience was what powerful people wrote on doors they were afraid of.

"Thornfell's vaults are crown property," she said. "Stealing from them isn't theft. It's treason with extra steps."

"Yes," the stranger agreed mildly. "I'm prepared to pay accordingly."

He set something on the table.

Paloma had seen a great many coins in her life, most of them briefly, none of them long. She knew money the way a gull knows fish: instantly, hungrily, and without sentiment. So it took her only half a heartbeat to understand what she was looking at, and the other half to wish she didn't.

Old royal coin. Pre-Usurper minting. Gold so deep in color it was nearly red, worn smooth by a century of hands, stamped with the face of a king in profile — and beneath the king's chin, small enough that a careless eye would take it for a maker's mark, a sigil.

A wolf's head, jaws open.

She had never seen the Wolf sigil on coin before. Nobody had. It wasn't a thing that appeared in the world; it appeared in stories, told low, in the kind of voices people used for the names of diseases. The Wolf-King of Thornfell. The story every child in the borderlands got instead of a lullaby: the king who bargained with something hungry, and lost, and whose castle still stood full of everything he'd been.

Her hand hovered over the coin. Her whole body leaned toward it the way plants lean toward windows.

"How many?" she asked, proud of how steady it came out.

"Twenty now. Thirty on delivery." He turned the coin so the wolf's face caught the light, and she noticed his finger didn't bend quite right at the knuckle, the way a glove doesn't. "There is a vial. Glass, sealed with black wax, kept in the deep records vault beneath the east tower. You will know it by the seal — it bears a thorn and a crescent moon. Bring me the vial unopened, and thirty more coins follow. Open it, spill what it holds, or substitute anything for it—" his mouth did something that was almost a smile, on a face that seemed to be practicing the concept, "—and the arrangement concludes unhappily."

"Unhappily how?"

"I'd rather not specify. It discourages optimism."

"Well," said Paloma, "we wouldn't want that."

Here is what you should know about Paloma Thackeray, and what she would have told you herself if you'd bought her enough drink first, which nobody ever had, because she bought her own drinks precisely so that nobody could: she had rules. Three of them, carved into her the way initials get carved into trees — slowly, painfully, and by somebody young.

Rule one: never keep what you steal. A memory taken was a memory held in trust, delivered to the client, gone from her head inside a day. What stayed in her head rotted there, grew roots, started telling her it had always been hers. She'd seen what happened to thieves who hoarded memories. They became crowded houses. They forgot which griefs were rented.

Rule two: never steal from anyone poorer than the client. Not morality, strictly. Just arithmetic. The poor had less to lose and longer to hold grudges.

Rule three: never take a job you can't walk away from.

She looked at the coin with the wolf on it, and rule three cleared its throat politely in the back of her mind, and she looked at the coin some more.

Thirty coins of pre-Usurper gold was a year of work. Thirty was a roof that didn't leak. Thirty was the good boots, the ones with actual soles, and a winter where the innkeeper's bill was paid before it was presented, and — she caught herself at the edge of the thought, the way you catch yourself at the top of stairs in the dark — thirty was enough to stop. To put the knife-kit and the vial-cases in a drawer and be a woman who drank bad cider on purpose, in a town, among people, for years, until she was someone those people knew.

That was the hunger talking. She knew its voice better than her own. It always dressed the wanting up in sensible clothes.

"The vial," she said. "What's in it?"

"That," said the stranger, "is not information I possess."

"Everyone possesses some information about their own business."

"Not about this." For just a moment, something moved behind his face — behind it, she'd have sworn later, the way weather moves behind a hill — and his voice dropped into a register that made the dog under the bench lift its head and whine. "It belonged to a man who is no longer available to describe it. I am instructed to recover it. That is all I am, in this matter: instructions."

"Instructed by whom?"

The pause lasted exactly long enough to be an answer, and then he said, "By the party concerned," which was not an answer, and both of them let it stand there like a piece of furniture they were pretending not to see.

Paloma turned the coin over with one fingertip. The king's profile stared off the edge of the gold, serene, unaware of the wolf at his throat. Forty years dead, if the histories were right. Maybe not even that — maybe worse, maybe *erased*, because the Usurper hadn't killed the old royal line so much as *unwrote* them, and there were still old women in the border villages who'd start a sentence about the true king and then stop, frowning, holding the end of the story like a key to a door they couldn't find.

And the sigil. Why put the Wolf on the payment? Unless whoever sent him wanted it seen. Unless the seeing was part of it.

Unless it was a test, and she was failing it right now, salivating over cursed gold like a mule over a carrot on a stick, and somewhere a fat merchant was laughing at how easy the famous Thackeray was to steer.

Or — quieter thought, meaner thought — unless it didn't matter what was on the coin. Money was money. Cursed or clean, it bought the same bread. And she'd eaten a lot of bread that other people would have had moral opinions about.

"When?" she asked.

"The vault's wards renew at each new moon. Between now and then, the deep records stand... drowsy." He produced a folded paper — real paper, thick, cream-colored, absurdly fine for a smuggler's tavern — and slid it across. A sketch of the vault's inner door, drawn in a hand so precise it looked printed, annotated in a spidery script. "The eastern stair is unwatched. The lower gallery floods knee-deep with something that is not water. Do not touch it. Do not taste it. Walk through it briskly and think about nothing."

"Think about nothing," Paloma repeated. "In a haunted cellar."

"If it helps, hum."

"It does not help."

"It helped me," he said, and rose, unfolding upward again, and for a moment his shadow on the wall did not rise with him but stayed seated, patient, in the booth. Then the lamplight steadied and she decided she'd imagined it, firmly, the way you decide not to itch.

"One more thing." He paused at the table's edge. "If you meet anyone on the road — anyone — who asks what you carry, tell them the truth. Tell them you carry nothing yet. Liars are loud. Truth is quiet. Quiet gets past the eaters."

"The eaters," said Paloma, "being the crown's memory-men."

"Being the crown's memory-men," he agreed, and something in the flat way he said it made her glance up sharply, but his paper face gave her nothing.

He counted twenty coins onto the wood — ten, fifteen, twenty, each one landing with a sound too heavy for its size, like pebbles dropped down a well — and then he bowed, actually bowed, an antique gesture from a century that had better manners and worse dentistry, and said:

"A pleasure, Mistress Thackeray. I've read a great deal about you."

"Nobody reads about me. I'm a rumor with boots."

"All rumors are written somewhere," he said pleasantly, and left.

She watched him go. He didn't push the door; the door opened for him, a half-second early, the way doors open for people the house likes, or fears. Rain followed him in for one step and stopped, as if reconsidering. And when he reached the street and turned north — toward Thornfell, she noted, walking *toward* the place he claimed to need her help entering, which raised questions she filed under Later, a folder that had been getting distressingly thick lately — the puddles he stepped in didn't ripple.

Paloma sat for a while. The dog went back to sleep. The eel dispute was resolved, badly, in favor of the eels.

She picked up a coin and bit it. Gold, soft and honest against her teeth. She held it up to the lamp and looked at the little wolf, jaws open, frozen mid-hunger for a hundred years.

"Rule three," she said aloud, to herself, to the coin.

The coin said nothing. Coins never do. But as she swept it into her pocket, she felt the weight of all twenty settle against her hip, and the weight was wrong somehow — heavier than gold should be, the way a secret is heavier than the words it's made of — and she found herself thinking, in a voice suspiciously like her grandmother's, the one memory she permitted herself to keep:

*Girl, when the price is too fair, you're not the buyer. You're the goods.*

Paloma finished the vinegar cider anyway, because waste was also a sin, and because she needed both hands free to count her excuses, and discovered, around the third swallow, that she'd already decided to go.

Of course she had. That was the trouble with hunger. It signed the contracts before the rest of you had read them.

* * *

Three days later, on the smuggler road above the Greywash, with Thornfell's towers finally visible through the rain — black teeth against a grey gum of sky — Paloma stopped at the crossroads shrine to check her bearings and found that someone had left an offering.

Fresh bread. Still warm. At a shrine no one had passed in weeks.

And tucked beneath it, weighted with a stone, a single page torn from a ledger, written in a fine old-fashioned hand:

*For the thief, when she passes. The vial is the least of what waits below. Look for your name.*

She read it twice. The rain dripped off her hood. Somewhere up the valley, thunder rolled along the mountains like furniture being dragged across the floor of the sky.

"My name," Paloma said to the empty road, "had better not be on that vial."

But her hands, counting her kit for the fourth time that morning, had already begun to shake — and not, she noted grimly, from the cold.
