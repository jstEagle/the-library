## 4. Quentin at the Crossroads

The road told me she was coming before I saw her.

I had learned to read roads the way other men read faces — the smuggler ways especially, which is to say the ways that pretended not to exist. This one was called the Hollow Stair by people who used it and nothing at all by people who didn't, because it wound down through a fold of hills where the mapmakers had long since given up pretending anything lived there. It smelled of pine resin and old rain and, faintly, of someone's supper two valleys away. Roads do that. They carry what travels on them.

And this road was carrying her.

Not footsteps — she moved like a debt collector, quiet as regret. But a road remembers weight, even borrowed weight, even the weight of a woman who walked like she was apologizing to the ground for touching it. I felt her coming through my boots. Then I felt her coming through something worse: through the thing in my skull that stirred whenever anything *valuable* came near, the way a dog lifts its head at the sound of its name.

*There,* said the Wolf, in the cellar of my mind where it kept itself. *There is something worth eating.*

"Shut up," I murmured.

*I haven't spoken.*

"You were about to."

I crouched behind a fallen birch at the crossroads where the Hollow Stair met the King's Old Road — a king's road now only in the way that a dead man's coat still hangs on a hook. Three paths met here, and every one of them lied about where it went. That was why smugglers loved it, and why I loved it, and why I had waited in the cold damp of it for four hours with a cramp in my left calf and a growing certainty that I had made an idiot of myself.

Because here is what you must understand about me, whoever you are, reading this: I am not a brave man. I was never trained to be one. Princes are trained to be *certain*, which is a different animal entirely, and much easier to fake until the day certainty kills you.

Four hours. A cramp. And then, around the bend of the stair, she came.

I had expected — I don't know what. Someone larger. Someone with more knives visible. The ledger-thief, the Thackeray thief, the name scrawled in a dead woman's handwriting across a page that should have crumbled a century ago: I had built her out of shadow and menace in my head, seven feet tall, eyes like coin slots.

What came around the bend was a small woman in a grey coat, walking with her hood up and her hands in her pockets, humming. Badly. Something that might have been a lullaby if a lullaby had been dropped down a well first.

She stopped three paces from the crossroads stone. She did not look at the birch. She did not look at me. She looked at the middle distance with the expression of someone doing arithmetic, and then she said:

"You've been breathing behind that tree since before dawn. Either come out or stop breathing, because the rhythm is putting me off my song."

I stood up too fast, which is the traditional response, and my cramped leg buckled, which is the traditional punishment, and so the exiled heir of Thornfell introduced himself to his kingdom's last hope by falling into the road at her feet.

She looked down at me. Her hood fell back. She had a face like a locked drawer — sharp at the corners, nothing wasted, everything shut — except her eyes, which were doing something complicated, something that looked almost like recognition and almost like grief and mostly like a woman deciding how much trouble she was in.

"Well," she said. "You're not crown."

"No."

"You're not memory-eater. They smell like cold candlewax and they don't fall over."

"No."

"You're not banditry, because bandits announce themselves. You're not clergy, because you swore when your knee went." She crouched, elbows on her knees, studying me the way one studies a strange insect. "Which leaves either lost pilgrim or something interesting. You don't have the face of a pilgrim. Pilgrims have hope. You have the face of a man who has been waiting four hours in the wet for someone he's afraid of."

"I wasn't afraid," I said, from the ground.

"Liar," she said, without heat, almost fondly. "Good liar though. Almost convincing. Who are you?"

I got up. Slowly this time, with dignity, or the rubble of dignity. "My name is Quentin."

Something happened in her face then. Small, but I caught it — I have spent years learning to catch small things in faces, because small things were all anyone ever let me see. The name meant something. Not the way a name means something to a stranger. The way it means something to someone who has read it somewhere she shouldn't have.

"And I know," I said, "that you have a ledger in your pack that was written before either of us was born, and that it knows tonight's date, and that it knows yours."

Now the drawer opened. Not all the way. One inch.

"Everyone knows about the ledger," she said. "It's been the talk of every market between here and the Salt Stair. *Prophetic ledger found in Thornfell vault, names a thief, doom and wonder.* I'm surprised the ballads aren't written yet."

"They will be. Ballads take eleven days. It's been nine." I paused. "That's how I know you'd come this way. The vault is north. Every road south of it funnels here eventually, if you're good enough to use them. And you're good enough. Your footprints on the stair — you walk on the stones' edges. Only smugglers do that. Or cats."

"Cats are better company."

"*Quentin,*" she repeated, tasting it. "That's a court name. That's a *palace* name."

Here it was, then. The part I had rehearsed against the birch tree for four hours, all of which abandoned me at once.

"It's also," I said, "a stolen one."

* * *

We sat on the crossroads stone because she insisted on sitting — "conversations go worse standing, everyone postures" — and because she produced from her pack a flask of cider and the universal law of the road states that he who brings the cider sets the terms.

She drank. She passed it. I drank. Somewhere in the trees a wood pigeon made its idiot two-note complaint, and I remember thinking, absurdly, that I would remember this moment, the pigeon and the cider and the cold, and I was right, though not for any reason I could then have guessed.

"The Wolf," she said. "Start there. Everyone says the Wolf-King took Thornfell ninety years ago. Everyone says it differently. My grandmother—" she stopped, jaw tightening, as if the word had edges— "the stories say a beast wore the crown. The crown's own story says a king tamed a beast. Neither version holds water. Water needs two sides. So. Truth."

I laughed. I couldn't help it; it came out cracked and rusty, a gate that hadn't swung in years. "You want truth. From me."

"You're the first person in nine days who hasn't tried to sell me something. Even the cider was free."

"It's my cider."

"All the more trustworthy, then."

So I told her. Not all of it — some of it I couldn't have told her if I'd wanted to, because it wasn't mine anymore, and we will come to that, gods help me, we will come to all of that — but I told her the shape of it.

"There was a true king," I said. "A hundred years ago and a little more. Good man, by every account that survived. Not a great man — great men make better stories and worse kingdoms — but decent. Careful. He kept his memories like a miser keeps coins, counted every night. Because he knew something most kings never learn."

"Which is?"

"That a king is not a body wearing a crown. A king is a *memory* wearing a body. The crown doesn't care whose head it sits on. It cares what the head contains. Take the memory of ruling away, and you can put the crown on a wolf and it will hold the shape, because the shape isn't cloth and gold. It's habit. It's a hundred thousand mornings of ceremony worn into the world like a path worn into grass."

Her eyes had gone very still. "You talk like someone who's read the crown's private papers."

"I talk like someone who grew up in the palace," I said, "under a father who was not my father, wearing a face that was not his face, while everyone I loved smiled at a lie and called it loyalty."

The pigeon complained again. The wind moved in the birches. She said nothing, which was, I was beginning to understand, her way of being kind. Most people fill silences. She let them sit there like guests.

"The Wolf ate him," I said. "Slowly. Memory by memory. First the small ones — his mother's voice, the smell of his schoolroom — because those are the roots, and if you cut the roots the tree falls easy. Then the big ones. His coronation. His wedding. His son's face." My voice did something on that word. I let it. "Until one morning the king woke up and there was nothing in him that objected to the Wolf, because there was no *him*. Just a warm room the Wolf moved into. The palace calls it the Long Coronation. Officially, the king 'withdrew into contemplation.' Unofficially—"

"Unofficially he was eaten," she said quietly. "Like a meal. Over years."

"Like a banquet. With toasts."

*And you,* said the thing in my skull, soft as frost forming, *you were dessert.*

I flinched. She saw it.

"It talks to you," she said. Not a question.

"It hums," I said. "Mostly. It hums old coronation anthems, badly, which is somehow worse than threats. And sometimes, when I sleep near places where a lot of memory has been spilled — battlefields, graveyards, the Salt Stair on market day — it gets louder. It wants me to bring it things. Shiny things. Remembered things. It's very patient. Patience is the whole of its nature. It spent a century eating one man. What's a few more decades?"

"How much of you has it had?"

I looked at the cider in my hands. "Enough that I don't know my mother's face. I know I had one. I know I loved her. There's a hole shaped like her and everyone tells me I resemble her, and I have to take their word for it, like taking directions from strangers to my own house." I turned the flask. "Enough that when I try to remember my childhood, I get the *furniture*. Rooms. No people. A nursery full of chairs and no child in it. It started on me the day I was born, they think. Kings feed their heirs to it early now. It keeps the bloodline... pliable."

"That's—" She stopped. Recalibrated. "That's monstrous."

"Yes."

"No, I mean—" she gestured vaguely at my entire person— "you say it like the weather. Like it's just the arrangement of things. Doesn't it make you furious?"

"Fury takes room," I said. "It's using the space."

She was quiet a moment. Then she held out her hand for the flask, drank, and handed it back, and something in the handing of it felt like a signature on a document neither of us had read aloud.

"So the ledger," she said. "Your turn to ask."

* * *

I asked carefully, because I had watched her all through the telling and I knew by then that she startled like a cat — not visibly, never visibly, but internally, some sentry inside her going from relaxed to armed at the first hint of a cage.

"The ledger was written in the eighteen-hundreds," I said. "In a script that died with the scriptoriums. It names a thief — *the Thackeray thief* — and it names deeds not yet done, and it bears a seal that no living house uses. A seal of a mountain with a door in it. I know the seal because I've seen it twice. Once in the palace archives, on documents the Wolf keeps chained shut. Once—"

I stopped.

"Once where?" she said.

"On my father's letters," I said. "My true father's. The ones I'm fairly sure existed. I have one memory that isn't furniture — one. I'm holding onto it like a man holding a rope over a drop. In it, a woman with ink-stained fingers seals a letter with wax, and presses a ring into it, and the ring has a mountain with a door in it, and she says—" the words came back to me the way they always did, in her voice, warm and dry as autumn— "*what is given to the mountain, the mountain keeps.*"

Paloma — she had given me the name by then, tossed out sideways like it cost nothing, which is how she gave everything — Paloma had gone very pale under the road-dust.

"Ink-stained fingers," she repeated.

"Is that—"

"My grandmother taught me letters," she said. "My grandmother wrote in a hand nobody else could read. My grandmother died before I could walk, and I know exactly four things about her, and one of them is that she kept a ring she never let anyone touch." She stared at the crossroads stone between her boots. "This is a joke. Someone is playing a joke with excellent production values."

"Do you have the ledger on you?"

"I have the ledger on me."

"May I—"

She hesitated. I watched her weigh it — watched the whole internal argument, the sentry and the merchant and whatever else lived in that locked drawer of hers, all of them shouting. Then she pulled it from her pack, and the sight of it hit me like cold water, because I had seen it described in the archive and description does nothing. It was small, leather-bound, scarred, ordinary as a hymnal, and it radiated wrongness the way a frozen lake radiates danger: perfectly still, perfectly lovely, and absolutely not to be trusted.

She didn't hand it to me. She opened it herself, to a marked page, and turned it so I could read.

I read. And the cellar-thing in my skull, which had been humming its ruined anthems all morning, went silent — utterly, totally silent, the silence of a predator that has just realized it is inside a trap and is deciding whether to panic.

*On the night the thief comes down the Hollow Stair,* said the page, in that dead script, in handwriting that made my chest ache for reasons I couldn't name, *she will meet the half-eaten prince at the crossing of three lies. He will offer her the truth. She should take it. It is the only free thing he has left.*

"She wrote this," Paloma whispered. "Before. Before everything. Before me. She wrote *this conversation.*"

"Not this conversation," I said, because pedantry is a comfort in horror. "An earlier draft of it. Look at the margin."

In the margin, smaller, in the same hand: *He will be rude about the details. Forgive him. Grief makes pedants of us all.*

Paloma looked at me. I looked at Paloma. Above us the pigeon finally gave up and flew off, and I swear on every throne I'll never inherit that it laughed.

"Your grandmother," I said slowly, "knew me. A hundred years before I was born, she knew *me*. Which means this isn't prophecy. Prophecy is weather. This is architecture. Somebody built this. Built *us*."

"Why would she write you into it? Why would she write—" Paloma's voice climbed, cracked, came down hard— "why would she write *me* into it? Naming a thief. Naming a *Thackeray*. Do you know what the crown does to named things? I was a rumor yesterday. Rumors are safe. Now I'm a *line in a book* and every memory-eater between here and the capital can read."

"Then we should probably stop sitting on an open road," I said, "discussing it."

* * *

We didn't leave immediately. Later I would understand why — she was deciding, the whole time, running me through whatever scales thieves keep behind their eyes — but at the time it simply felt like two exhausted people failing at strategy.

"Where would you even go?" she asked. "If not the road."

"Away from the passes. The memory-eaters hunt by resonance — remembered places sing to them. The deep lowlands are forgettable on purpose. Nothing there worth remembering, so nothing there worth hunting."

"Charming sales pitch. *Come to the lowlands, we have nothing.*"

"I'm not selling anything."

"You keep saying that like it's a virtue. It's suspicious, is what it is." She stood, brushed off her coat, and looked south along the Hollow Stair, then west, then at me, and I watched her arrive at whatever she arrived at, because her whole face changed — not softened, nothing about her softened, but it *settled*, like a lock finding its tumblers.

"Here's my situation, Quentin-of-the-palace-name," she said. "Two days ago I was a thief with a rule: steal anything, keep nothing. Clean life. Simple. Then I opened a vault I was paid to open, found a book that knows my name, and discovered that my grandmother — who I have mourned my entire life as a stranger — may have spent her death arranging my life like furniture. I am being hunted by professionals, paid in cursed coin by a client with cold hands, and I have a ledger in my pack that comments on my conversations before I have them." She pointed at me. "And you. You are a prince-shaped catastrophe with a monster in your skull, holes where your family should be, and exactly one rope of a memory, and you waited four hours in the wet to intercept me because some dead woman's book told you I'd come down this stair. We are both, objectively, doomed."

"That's... accurate."

"But." She held up a finger. "But. The book says I should trust you, and I hate that, and the fact that I hate it means some part of me believes it, and I hate *that* more. Also you fell over, which no assassin has ever done convincingly. Also—" and here something crossed her face, quick and unguarded, gone as soon as it came— "you talked about your mother's face being gone, and you didn't perform it. People perform grief. You just stated it, like inventory. *One mother, missing.* I know that voice. That's my voice."

The wind came down the stair and moved through the birches, and for a moment the three lying roads all pointed the same direction, and the crossroads stone beneath us — older than kingdoms, older than wolves — seemed to lean in and listen.

"So here are my terms," Paloma said. "You walk with me. You tell me everything you know about the seal, the ledger, the Wolf, and the vault, in that order, and you don't edit the parts that flatter you. In exchange, I don't rob you."

"That's generous. You'd get nothing."

"I'd get your boots, your ring, and that memory you're so proud of, and you'd wake up tomorrow a pleasant man with no past who sells turnips. Don't test me. I'm having a very bad month." She slung her pack. "Also, second term: when this is done — whatever *this* is, whatever your grandmother and mine were building a hundred years ago — we find out who wrote the book. All of it. Every page. Nobody writes me into a story without my consent and lives peacefully in my memory. Dead or not."

For the first time in longer than I could measure — literally, in my case, longer than I could measure — I laughed and meant it.

"Agreed," I said. "All terms. And my counter-term: when the Wolf in my skull starts humming, you tell me a story. Any story. About anything. Because it hates stories it hasn't eaten yet, and your lullaby was so bad it actually flinched."

She gave me a look that on another face would have been a smile, and on hers was merely the *possibility* of one, which I would learn was worth more.

"That," she said, turning south onto the Hollow Stair, "can be arranged. Keep up, your highness. And watch the third stone past the bend — it rolls."

I followed the thief down the road my grandmother-in-law — gods, I'd need a stronger word for her, *architect*, maybe, or *executioner* — had written for us a century before either of us drew breath. Behind us the three lying roads folded shut like a book. Ahead of us, though I couldn't know it yet, the first of the memory-eaters had already picked up our scent on the wind: two lives entangled now, singing together, loud as a choir.

And in the cellar of my skull, the Wolf stopped humming, and began, very softly, to laugh.
