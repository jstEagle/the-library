## 5. What the Wolf Wears

The fire had burned down to a sullen orange, and the thief was watching me the way people watch a dog they suspect might bite.

I couldn't blame her. I'd dragged her off the Salt Stair road at knifepoint—or rather, I'd stepped out of the treeline looking like exactly what I was, which is a man who hasn't slept in a proper bed in four years and has been having arguments with something inside his own skull for most of them—and then I'd put my hand on her ledger and said, very calmly, "Where did you get this," in the tone of voice that makes even brave people take a step back.

She hadn't taken a step back. That was the first thing I liked about Paloma Thackeray.

"From a vault," she said now, for the third time, drawing her knees up toward the fire like she could hide behind them. Her boots were muddy to the ankle. Her fingers were long and quick and currently wrapped around a tin cup of my terrible tea, which she kept drinking anyway, which was either politeness or proof she'd stolen worse things than tea. "A vault under Thornfell, if you must know. Sealed. The kind of seal you don't pick so much as apologize to."

"The vault knows you're coming before you arrive," I said. "Yes. It's old magic. Older than the kingdom."

"And you are?"

I looked at her across the fire. She was small, sharp-faced, all elbows and economy of motion—a person who had learned early that taking up less space meant fewer questions. There was a smudge of ash on her cheekbone she didn't know about. For some reason this undid me more than anything else about the evening.

"My name is Quentin," I said. "Quentin Aldous Vane, second of his name, heir to the throne of Vaelmark." I turned my cup in my hands. "Which will mean nothing to you, because by every official record, Quentin Aldous Vane died four years ago in a hunting accident."

Her eyes went to the ledger between us. To the wax seal on its back cover—the crowned wolf, pressed deep into black wax gone gray with age. Then back to me.

"You don't look dead," she said.

"I'm told I look worse."

* * *

Here is what I have learned about telling the truth: it comes out sideways or not at all. You spend four years holding a thing too heavy to lift, and when you finally set it down, you find you can only hand it over in pieces, one at a time, like a man passing bricks up a ladder.

So I started with the easy brick. The one about my father.

"King Aldous was—" I stopped. Started again. "My father was a large man. Enormous appetite for everything. Food, laughter, argument, my mother's terrible lute playing. He once ate an entire roast goose at a diplomatic dinner out of spite because the ambassador from Carrow had called Vaelmark cuisine 'austere.' He stood up afterward, bowed to the table, and said, 'We are austere no longer.'" I smiled. It hurt. "You would have liked him. Everyone liked him. It was infuriating."

Paloma watched me over the rim of her cup. "And now?"

"Now there is a king in the palace who has my father's face," I said, "and my father's crown, and my father's voice, and sits in my father's chair, and laughs my father's laugh—slightly wrong, slightly late, the way a portrait of a laugh would laugh—and he is not my father."

The fire popped. Somewhere up the mountain, a nightbird made its one sad sound.

"He's the Wolf," I said. "The Wolf-King of Thornfell. The thing the old stories warn children about, except the old stories got it wrong, because they say the Wolf devours travelers on the pass, and it doesn't. It devours *memory*. And it wears what it eats."

* * *

I want to explain what that means, because I've had four years to turn it over and I still can't make it sit still.

Memory isn't just remembering. Not the way we usually think of it—not a dusty archive where facts wait in rows. Memory is the mortar between stones. It's why your hand knows the weight of your own sword. It's why your face settles into the shape it has. Take enough of it away and the stones come apart, and what's left standing is a shape with nothing inside it.

The Wolf cannot hold its own shape. Whatever it truly is—old god, hungry spirit, the first lie ever told—I don't know, and I've stopped guessing. What I know is this: alone, it is formless. A pressure. A cold spot in a warm room. But feed it a life, a whole rich life, decades of mornings and grudges and songs learned badly, and it can wear that life like a coat. Walk around in it. Be *seen* in it.

It ate my father slowly. That's the part I can't stop turning over at three in the morning. Slowly. Over years. A memory here, a memory there—the taste of his childhood dog, the name of his first tutor, the grief of his own father's funeral. Each theft small enough that no one noticed. Each theft leaving my father a little smoother, a little more generic, a king-shaped hole where a king used to be.

By the end, my father could stand in front of a mirror and not recognize the man looking back. I know because I found him there once, staring, and asked him what was wrong, and he said, in a pleasant, reasonable voice, "I was just wondering who that fellow is," and laughed, and the laugh was already wrong, and I was fourteen and I thought he was joking.

He wasn't joking. He was disappearing.

* * *

"The court noticed eventually," I said to Paloma. "Some of them. My mother noticed. Mothers do." I fed a stick to the fire, watched it catch. "There were whispers. My mother started keeping records—private ones, hidden ones—comparing what my father used to be against what sat on the throne wearing his face. She gathered allies. Servants, guardsmen, a few lords with long memories and short tempers. They called themselves the Remembrancers."

"What happened to them?"

"The Wolf happened to them." I said it flatly because the alternative was saying it any other way. "You can't fight a thing that eats what you're fighting with. Every plan they made, it learned by eating the memory of making it. Every secret they kept, it took. My mother hid her records in six places and the Wolf found five, and the people who knew about the sixth forgot the sixth existed, because the Wolf reached into their heads and folded that knowledge away like a letter no one reads twice."

Paloma had gone very still. Thieves, I was learning, went still when the stakes got high; it was their version of running.

"And you?" she asked. "How does a crown prince end up on a smuggler road looking like a scarecrow that lost an argument with winter?"

"Humor," I said. "Good. Keep doing that."

"It wasn't humor. You look genuinely terrible."

"I'm aware. Thank you." I pulled my collar tighter, mostly for something to do with my hands. "When I was nineteen, the Wolf came for me directly. Not to eat—to *keep*. It had eaten nearly all of my father. It needed... scaffolding. Something young and royal to shore itself up while it finished digesting the rest." I touched the side of my head, just above the ear, where the cold lived. "It's still in here. Part of it. It got its teeth into me before my mother's people got me out. They smuggled me over the border the same week they buried an empty coffin with my name on it."

"And the Wolf let you go?"

"The Wolf doesn't let things go." I met her eyes. "It keeps a piece of me the way a fisherman keeps a hook in a fish. Everything I remember, it tastes. Everything I love, it learns. It whispers to me at night in my father's voice, and some mornings I wake up and I have to check my hands to make sure they're mine."

Silence. The fire settled. Paloma set down her cup.

"That's why you grabbed me," she said quietly. "The ledger."

"The ledger." I nodded toward it, black-bound and patient between us. "That seal is my mother's work. The Remembrancer cipher. Which means somewhere in that vault you cracked open tonight, someone wrote down a prophecy naming a Thackeray thief, and a date." I leaned forward. "The date is tonight, Paloma. Yesterday it was a riddle. Today it's history. So either your grandmother was the finest fortune-teller in a hundred years, or—"

"Or someone arranged it," she finished. Her voice had gone thin.

"Or someone arranged it," I agreed. "And either way, the Wolf felt that vault open. Felt it the way you feel a tooth break. It knows a prophecy exists about its unbinding. It knows a thief walked out carrying the first page of it. And it knows"—I tapped my temple, where the cold coiled and listened even now—"because it hears everything I hear."

Paloma stared at me. "It's listening right now."

"It's listening right now."

"So everything you've just told me—"

"Is already in its mouth. Yes."

* * *

You'd think that would end the conversation. Most conversations about the Wolf end quickly; terror is efficient that way. But Paloma Thackeray only frowned, the frown of a woman doing arithmetic, and after a moment she said:

"Then why tell me anything? Why not knock me out and take the ledger?"

"Because I need you."

"You need a thief."

"I need *you*." I heard how it sounded and pushed on anyway. "The prophecy names a Thackeray. The vault sealed itself to everyone but you—you said so yourself, the locks apologized. My mother spent eleven years building a trap for this thing and whatever the trap is, it has your family's name on the trigger. I've spent four years running and hiding and going slowly hollow, and I am done hiding." I spread my empty hands. "I'm half-eaten, Paloma. Half a prince, half a man, and the other half leaks away a little more every winter. If I'm going to be consumed either way, I'd rather it happen walking toward the thing than running from it."

She looked at me for a long moment. Firelight moved on her face.

"You grieve him," she said. "Your father. Even though he's technically alive."

That was the brick I hadn't planned to hand her yet. It slipped out anyway.

"I can't mourn him," I said. My voice did something embarrassing. "Do you understand? There's no body. No funeral I'm allowed to believe in. The whole kingdom looks at that—that *thing*—in my father's chair and sees a beloved king in his middle years, hale and hearty and austere no longer, and if I tell them the truth they'll call me mad, or worse, they'll remember me wrong, because the Wolf edits memories, it *tidies*, and anyone who believes me too hard becomes a loose thread it pulls out." I stared into the fire. "Grief needs a grave. It needs an ending. Mine just... continues. Every day he gets up and wears my father's smile and asks about the harvest and pats children on the head, and every day I lose him again, freshly, on schedule."

Paloma reached over and, without ceremony, refilled my cup with the terrible tea.

"Drink," she said. "You look genuinely terrible."

I drank. It was awful. I was absurdly grateful.

* * *

Later—much later, when she'd rolled into her bedroll facing the fire and I'd taken first watch—I heard her voice out of the dark, drowsy and dry:

"Quentin. The Wolf. When it eats someone... does it leave anything?"

I thought about my father's last true morning, years ago now, when for ten minutes the fog in him had lifted and he'd gripped my shoulder with both huge hands and said, *Quentin. Listen. It's me. It's still me in here, somewhere, find the ledger, find your mother's ledger—* and then the fog closed and he'd smiled pleasantly and asked whether I'd eaten.

"It leaves the hunger," I said.

A pause.

"No," Paloma said, half-asleep. "That's what it leaves in *itself*."

I didn't answer. Up the valley, far off toward Thornfell, something howled—not a wolf's howl, nothing with a throat could make that sound—and the nightbirds went silent all at once, and the cold above my ear stirred and stretched and whispered, in my father's warm ruined voice:

*Come home, son.*

I sat watch until dawn, and I did not go home, and in the morning the thief woke, rubbed the ash-smudge she still didn't know about from her cheek, looked at the road ahead, and said:

"All right, Your Highness. Show me where these memory-eaters hunt."
