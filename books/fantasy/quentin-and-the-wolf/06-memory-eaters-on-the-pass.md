## 6. Memory-Eaters on the Pass

The trouble with running from something that eats memories, Paloma reflected as she scrambled up the scree above the Greywater Pass, was that it knew exactly what you were afraid of forgetting.

She had spotted them an hour ago — three riders in the crown's grey livery, moving with the wrong kind of patience. Smugglers moved fast or they moved invisible; these men moved like men who already knew where you'd stop for breath. She'd counted their gear from the ridgeline: no tents, no cookfire kit, just long coats and the pale glass canisters memory-eaters carried strapped to their forearms like leeches waiting to be fed.

And they weren't following her trail. They were following *her* — cutting straight through terrain no honest traveler would attempt, which meant someone had told them where she was, and that someone had either been very good at tracking a Thackeray or had simply read her name off a ledger written before her grandmother learned to write.

Quentin had vanished into the rocks an hour back, muttering about drawing them off, and she hadn't argued. There was a particular look on his face when he talked about the memory-eaters that she'd decided not to examine too closely yet. It was the look of a man describing his own mugging.

The pass narrowed ahead of her, walls of granite squeezing together until she could touch both sides with outstretched arms. Beyond it, the smuggler road dropped toward the valley and the border stones and, if she was lucky, an inn with indifferent ale and a landlord who didn't ask questions.

Behind her, gravel clicked.

Paloma did what any sensible thief would do: she stopped thinking about herself entirely.

This was the trick of her craft that nobody understood — not the merchants whose sunsets she lifted, not the widows who paid her to take their griefs, not even Quentin, though he was starting to suspect. Everyone assumed a memory-thief's power lived in the taking. It didn't. It lived in the *keeping*. In carrying things that weren't yours so carefully, so completely, that for a few minutes you could walk around inside another person's life as though it fit you.

And she happened to be carrying a memory of this exact pass.

It belonged to a goat-herd named Sera Voss, sixty years old, crook-backed and sharp-eyed, whom Paloma had met two summers ago on the far side of the mountains. Sera had sold her the memory for three silver and a jar of plum brandy, which was a fair price for forty years of knowing every handhold, every loose stone, every place where the mountain pretended to offer footing and then laughed at you. Paloma had kept it because rule one of the trade was never keep what you steal — but Sera's pass wasn't stolen; it was bought and paid for, and anyway, rules were like rope: made to hold things, not to hang yourself by.

She closed her eyes, found the memory where it sat folded in the corner of her mind like a coat on a hook, and put it on.

* * *

The world changed the way weather changes — all at once and then gradually.

Suddenly she was seventy years old in her knees. Suddenly she knew that the third boulder past the narrows looked solid but sat on a shelf of ice-melt and would tip if you trusted it. Suddenly she could smell rain coming from the west before there was a single cloud, because sixty years of goats had taught Sera Voss that mountains lied about weather the way merchants lied about wool.

Paloma opened her eyes and the pass was a different country. Every stone had a name. The scree slope she'd been climbing blind now unfolded in her mind like a letter from an old friend: *lean left here, the rock holds; don't touch the grey slab, it sings before it slides.*

She moved.

Not climbed — *moved*, the way water moves downhill, finding the path that sixty years of a stranger's life had worn smooth inside her. Her hands found cracks she hadn't seen. Her boots landed on ledges that existed only because Sera Voss had once fallen onto them and sworn never to forget. Twice she took routes that made no sense at all — down instead of up, left when right looked obvious — and both times she heard behind her the frustrated clatter of pursuers choosing the obvious way and finding nothing under it.

For ten glorious minutes, Paloma Thackeray was a sixty-year-old goatherd with bad knees and perfect knowledge, and it was the closest thing to flying she had ever experienced.

Then the memory started fighting back.

* * *

Here is what the memory-brokers never put in their sales pitch: a mind is a house with only so many rooms, and when you move somebody else's life into it, the furniture doesn't always stay where you put it.

It started small. A smell — juniper smoke, woodsmoke with a sweetness to it, and she thought, *I know that hearth,* except she didn't, she'd never sat beside any hearth like that, that was Sera's hearth, that was Sera's dog snoring against her ankle—

Her ankle. There was weight against her ankle, warm and breathing, and Paloma nearly stumbled on a ledge forty feet above the valley floor because some traitorous corner of her brain had imported a dead dog into the present moment.

*Let it go,* she told herself. *You're wearing it, not living it.*

But memories are greedy. That was the whole reason for her rule, the whole reason she emptied her pockets after every job, because a kept memory doesn't sit quietly like a coin. It grows roots. It starts rearranging your rooms. And Sera Voss's forty years were old and dense and heavy as river stones, and they were settling into Paloma's mind the way silt settles in still water.

The pass ended. She came out onto the high shoulder of the mountain where the smuggler road bent south, and the wind hit her face, and for one clear second she was entirely herself —

— and then she wasn't.

She was standing on this same shoulder at dusk, sixty years old, counting her goats, and one was missing, the little brown one with the torn ear, and she was calling its name — "*Brindle! Brindle!*" — except Brindle had died decades ago and the voice calling was hers and the grief rising in her chest belonged to a woman she'd spoken to exactly twice in her life.

Paloma gripped the cold rock and hung on to herself like a woman hanging onto a door in a gale.

*My name is Paloma Thackeray.* The thought came slow, like pushing through deep snow. *I steal memories. I never keep them. I am standing on the Greywater Pass and I am twenty-six years old and I have never owned a goat in my—*

A brown goat with a torn ear butted her hip, affectionate and impossible.

She screamed. Or maybe she didn't. Maybe the scream was Sera's, sixty years ago, when Brindle went over the edge. The wind took it either way.

Somewhere below and behind, the riders had reached the mouth of the pass. She heard voices, thin with distance — and then she heard something worse than voices. She heard the *hum*. Memory-eaters carried their canisters charged, and a charged canister sang a note you felt in your teeth, a note like the moment before lightning finds the tallest tree.

They were going to eat her. All of her. Every stolen sunset and borrowed grief and — the thought arrived with cold clarity — every piece of Sera Voss she was currently drowning in, and every scrap of Quentin's story she'd promised to help him finish, and whatever small stubborn thing inside her still insisted she mattered to anyone at all.

*I refuse,* she thought, and the word came out in her own voice for the first time in minutes, and that was how she knew which side of herself was winning. Barely.

She ran south along the shoulder, half a goatherd's surety and half her own desperate instinct, and the two of them argued about foot placement the entire way, which should have been fatal but was somehow exactly enough. Where Paloma would have hesitated, Sera's certainty carried her forward. Where Sera's habits expected a goat-track that had washed away years ago, Paloma's fresh eyes found the new line. For a quarter mile they ran as one person with two lifetimes, and it worked, and it was terrible, and she never wanted to do it again as long as she lived.

The road dropped toward the treeline. Below, tucked in a fold of the valley like a secret, she saw woodsmoke — the Drowned Lantern inn, if Sera's geography could be trusted, and at this point Paloma's own geography couldn't be trusted further than she could throw it.

The hum grew louder behind her. Closer.

And then, from somewhere upslope, a rock the size of a dinner table came crashing down the scree with a clatter that scattered the riders' horses and turned three grey-coated hunters into a swearing, scrambling mess.

From the rocks above, faint but unmistakable, came Quentin's voice — breathless, ragged, and wearing the smuggest tone she'd ever heard from a man who claimed royal blood:

"THE PASS IS CLOSED FOR ROYAL BUSINESS!"

* * *

He caught up with her at the treeline, sliding down through the pines with pine needles in his hair and a wild look in his eyes that she was beginning to understand was simply his face.

"That," he gasped, doubling over beside her, "was incredibly stupid."

"You pushed a rock at armed men."

"I pushed a rock at armed men who were chasing *you*, which makes it heroic, whereas walking into a memory-eater's reach while wearing a dead woman's whole life is stupid." He straightened, squinting at her. "Are you all right? You look — hm."

"Hm?"

"You keep looking at my boots like you're counting goats."

Paloma looked down at his boots. They were good boots, road-worn, exiled-prince quality. There were no goats anywhere near them. But her chest still ached with a grief for an animal that had been dust for fifty years, and when she blinked she saw juniper smoke against a sky that wasn't there.

"I'm fine," she said, in the tone of voice people use when they are visibly not fine, and Quentin had the grace not to argue, which she noted for later credit.

They walked south through the pines while the light went amber and low. After a mile, she risked telling him the truth, because lying to a man who could hear a Wolf whispering in his skull seemed pointless.

"It wouldn't let go." She kept her eyes on the path. "Sera's memory. I've worn bought memories before — quick ones, an hour, a day. This one... it settled. Like it wanted the room." Her hands had gone cold. "There was a moment up there when I couldn't remember my mother's face. I remember *Sera's* mother. I remember a kitchen in a valley I've never seen, and a woman singing with a gap in her teeth, and it took everything I had to push it out and find—" She stopped. Swallowed. "I almost didn't find mine. My own. Do you understand? Twenty-six years of being me, and one old goatherd's forty years nearly evicted me from my own head."

Quentin was quiet for several steps.

"My father used to say a man is just the stories he agrees to keep," he said finally. "I thought it was one of those court sayings that means nothing. Now I think he meant it as a warning." He glanced sideways at her. "How many do you carry right now? Truly?"

Paloma considered lying. Then she did the thief's arithmetic she'd been avoiding since the ridge.

"Bought ones, four. Sera's pass, a fisherman's tide-knowledge, a locksmith's hands, a midwife's calm. Those I can set down when I choose — mostly. And..." She hesitated. "One I didn't buy."

"One you stole."

"One I stole and couldn't give back, because giving it back would mean returning to a man the worst hour of his life, and I have rules, and rule two is you don't hand people their nightmares like change from a purchase." She said it lightly. It didn't come out lightly.

"And rule one?" Quentin asked. "Never keep what you steal."

"Yes."

"And you broke it."

"I *bent* it," she said. "There's a difference. Bent rules spring back."

"Do they?" He wasn't mocking. He sounded genuinely curious, the way a man sounds when he has spent months watching pieces of himself get eaten and has developed strong opinions about what a person can afford to lose.

Paloma didn't answer. Because the honest answer was sitting in her chest right now, wearing a goatherd's coat and grieving a dead goat: no, they didn't. Bent rules stayed bent. Kept memories stayed kept. Every theft she'd ever committed had left a seam in her, and today she'd seen what happened when the seams started to split.

Ahead, through the trees, the Drowned Lantern's windows glowed yellow, and she smelled bread, and for one merciful moment the only memory pressing on her was her own — hunger, plain and human, hers alone.

"There," Quentin said. "Food. Walls. A landlord who owes me a favor from a life I barely remember."

"A landlord who owes you a favor," Paloma repeated. "That's the most exiled-prince sentence I've ever heard."

"I contain multitudes. Most of them eaten."

They came out of the pines into the inn's muddy yard, and Paloma was reaching for the door — actually touching the handle, actually smelling the bread — when it swung open from the inside and a woman filled the frame. Broad, grey-streaked, sleeves rolled, holding a ladle like a scepter. She looked at Quentin for a long moment, and Paloma watched something move across her face that was too complicated to be surprise.

"Well," the woman said slowly. "Marta said you'd come down the pass someday, and Marta's been dead eleven years, so I told her she was talking nonsense." She stepped aside, jerking her head toward the warm interior. "Get in before the greycoats find the yard. Both of you. And you—" the ladle swung toward Paloma, and her eyes narrowed with something that might have been recognition, though Paloma had never seen her before in her life — "you'd better come in too, girl. You look like a woman who's been wearing someone else's shoes."

Paloma froze on the doorstep.

Because the woman was right, of course. But that wasn't why she froze. She froze because, underneath the goatherd's fading grief and the smell of bread and the exhaustion of nearly losing herself, a small voice — her own voice, unmistakably her own — whispered the question she'd been carrying since the vault, since the ledger, since the coin with the Wolf stamped on it:

*Who told them I was coming?*

And the woman's face, kind as it was, looked very much like someone who already knew the answer.
