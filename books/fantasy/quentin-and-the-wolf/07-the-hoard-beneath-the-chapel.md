## 7. The Hoard Beneath the Chapel

*Quentin*

The chapel had been falling down for longer than it had been standing, if you asked the locals. Quentin asked no one. He simply turned off the smuggler road at the split birch — the one that looked like a man mid-scream, which Paloma had remarked on with the particular delight of someone who enjoyed other people's nightmares — and led her through bracken gone gold at the edges, toward a roofline slumped like a tired old dog.

"You're bringing me to a ruin," Paloma said behind him. Not a question. An assessment. She had the voice of a woman who priced things quickly and haggled out of habit.

"I'm bringing you to the most important place in Thornfell," Quentin said. "Which happens to be a ruin. The kingdom has a sense of humor."

"The kingdom has a Wolf."

"That too."

He heard her stop walking. He'd learned in three days of shared road that Paloma Thackeray stopped walking only when something mattered, because stopping made you a target and she had survived twenty-odd years by never being one. So when she planted her boots in the dead leaves and said, "Before we go another step," he turned.

She stood with her arms crossed, the ledger's satchel strap cutting a diagonal across her coat, wind pushing her dark hair into her eyes so she had to squint like a suspicious cat. "I've been thinking," she said.

"Dangerous."

"Don't." A finger came up. "I've been thinking about what you are. What you *say* you are. Because here's my problem, Your Highness: I've met liars before. I've been one professionally. And the good ones don't look like you."

"Like what?"

"Like a man apologizing for existing." She said it without cruelty, which was somehow worse. "You walk like you're trying not to disturb the ground. You say 'please' to horses. Nobody who's lying about being royalty bothers with the manners. They skip straight to the crown part."

Quentin looked at the ruined chapel rather than at her. "And your conclusion?"

"My conclusion is I believe you, which is the stupidest professional decision of my life, and I want it noted somewhere that I knew better." She exhaled. "So if this is a cache of royal memories, and the Wolf wants them, and every memory-eater between here and the capital would trade a finger to find them — why bring me? You've known me four days. You know what I do. I *take things*. That's the whole of my character."

"It isn't," Quentin said.

Her arms tightened. "Excuse me?"

"In the pass, when the eaters nearly had us, you could have run. You had the route memorized — stolen or bought, doesn't matter — you were three turns ahead of me the whole climb. You stayed." He shrugged, feeling awkward under the weight of his own honesty. "Thieves don't stay. Keepers do. I think you've been mislabeled for years and have decided not to notice."

Paloma stared at him with an expression he couldn't read, then said, "That was the most insulting compliment anyone has ever paid me," and walked past him toward the chapel door, chin high, ears faintly pink.

He followed, smiling despite himself, and did not tell her that the smile hurt, because everything hurt these days; the Wolf had left so little of him that joy came through thin as light through a shutter crack, and he took it where he found it.

* * *

*Paloma*

Inside, the chapel smelled of stone and pigeon and time. Light fell through a hole where a window had once argued with weather and lost, landing in a dusty column across the nave. Pews lay rotted to lace. At the far end, where an altar should be, there was instead a slab of mountain granite, and on the granite, nothing at all.

"There's nothing here," she said.

"There's everything here. It's just shy." Quentin knelt beside the altar slab and ran his fingers along its edge until he found a seam she couldn't see. "My mother showed me when I was six. She said, 'A king keeps his treasures where no one would think to look, and a wise queen keeps hers where everyone looks and sees nothing.' Then she laughed at her own joke for about a year."

His face did something complicated when he mentioned his mother. Paloma watched it happen — the flicker of grief, then the strange blankness sliding over it like frost over water, the Wolf's leavings smoothing him flat — and something in her chest pulled tight and unwelcome. She didn't do tightness. Tightness was how you got caught.

"Your mother sounds like a smuggler," Paloma said, to fill the space.

"She would have taken that as the highest praise available." He pressed, and the granite sighed apart along a hidden hinge, exhaling cold air that smelled of cedar and iron. Stairs went down into the dark. "After you? No — after *me*, actually. There's a step that lies."

"A step that *lies*?"

"The third one is a foot shorter than it looks. My father broke his wrist on it. It's the most honest thing in this chapel, really. It warns everyone equally."

They descended. Paloma counted steps out of habit — eleven, twelve — and felt the temperature drop and the light change, and then they were in a chamber that made her stop walking again, which was becoming an embarrassing habit around this man.

It wasn't treasure. She'd expected treasure, honestly; she'd expected gold at least, some fallen kingdom's coin to justify the descent. Instead the room was lined with shelves cut from living rock, and on the shelves sat hundreds upon hundreds of small glass vials, each sealed with wax, each glowing faintly from within — amber, rose, green-gold, the deep blue of a memory made in winter. They lit the chamber like a cave of slow fireflies. Dust motes drifted through their glow. It was, Paloma thought, the single most beautiful thing she had ever seen that she hadn't stolen.

"What am I looking at?" Her voice came out hushed without permission.

"Him. My father. Or — the pieces of him." Quentin moved down the shelf line, trailing his fingers an inch above the vials without touching. "When the Wolf took the throne, it ate the king whole. Memories first — that's how it holds a shape. But my mother got word ahead. She had servants, loyal ones, smugglers mostly—" he glanced at Paloma, almost apologetic, "—carry pieces of him out of the palace. One memory per courier. A sunset he loved. His coronation oath. The way he laughed at bad poetry. Scattered across the country in hiding places like this one. Eleven caches, we think. This is the largest."

"Why vials?"

"Because memory kept loose goes sour. Kept in glass, in cold stone, it stays... true. Like fruit preserved." He picked up a vial at last, held it to the light. Inside, something golden swirled. "This one is the day I was born, I think. Mother labeled them in code, but I cracked some of it. This glow — amber with a red thread — means family."

Paloma stepped closer. Close enough to see the seals. Each vial wore a bead of black wax, pressed with a signet, and the wax had been stamped by hand, slightly off-center every time, by someone who pressed hard and wrote fast and —

Her stomach dropped through the floor.

"Quentin." She took a vial from the shelf — carefully, carefully, the way you handled anything alive — and turned its seal to the light. Curved letters. A thorn worked into the T like it was growing there. A tiny flourish beneath, like a signature deciding against itself at the last moment. "Who sealed these?"

"Mother's archivist. Why?"

"Because I know this hand." Her own voice sounded far away. "I've known it since I was a child. Wax letters from my grandmother. Recipe cards. Notes pinned to my coat. She pressed her seal exactly like this — always off-center, always the little tail under the name, like she started signing and changed her mind." She looked up at him. "This is my grandmother's handwriting, Quentin. On the seals of the true king's memories. In a vault I've never seen, in a country I've barely worked."

The chamber hummed quietly with its hundred small lights, and neither of them spoke for a long moment.

"That's not possible," Quentin said finally, gently, the way you talk to someone standing near a cliff edge.

"I agree completely." Paloma turned the vial over again. The little tail under the name seemed to smirk at her. "And yet."

* * *

*Quentin*

He watched her stand among his father's scattered soul, holding one piece of it up like a jeweler, and thought: *this is either the best luck of my exile or the worst, and with my family's history it will be both.*

"Tell me about her," he said. "Your grandmother."

"Why?"

"Because if she sealed my father's memories, she wasn't just a woman who wrote you letters. She was in the resistance. Deep in it. And I need to know everything she touched, because everything she touched may be a key." He hesitated. "Or a trap."

Paloma set the vial back with more tenderness than he'd ever seen her show an object. "There's not much to tell. She raised me. My parents died when I was small — fever, they said. She was strict. Clever. Terrible at affection and wonderful at pancakes. She taught me roads the way other grandmothers teach prayers. She died two years ago and left me her house, her maps, and a strong opinion about people who keep what they steal." Paloma's jaw shifted. "That was it. That was the whole inheritance."

"And her name?"

"Maren. Maren Thackeray." Paloma paused, and Quentin saw the thought arrive in her face like weather. "*Maren.* The ledger in the vault — the prophecy ledger — it didn't have an author named. But the script. The script was—"

"The same," Quentin finished quietly.

"Yes." She wrapped her arms around herself, and for a moment she looked very young, and very alone, in a way that made his chest ache in the hollow place the Wolf had chewed. "Four days ago I stole a ledger that knows my name, and now I find my dead grandmother's seal on a king's memories, and you're telling me these things aren't connected? Nothing is ever *that* connected. Except apparently my entire life."

"We'll follow the thread," Quentin said. "Carefully. Threads lead somewhere; that's their whole nature."

"Threads also hang people."

"That too." He moved along the shelf, checking seals out of long habit — intact, intact, intact — until he reached the third row and stopped. "Paloma."

"What?"

"Come look at this. And bring the light."

* * *

*Paloma*

She brought the nearest vial over like a lantern, and by its rosy glow she saw what he'd found: a gap on the shelf. Not empty — occupied. One vial sat apart from the others, in a niche of its own, and its seal was different. Newer wax. Redder. And pressed into it, unmistakable even in the dimness, was the thorned-T signet with the hesitant little tail.

But beneath the seal, scratched into the wax itself in fine, urgent lettering, were words:

*For the Thackeray thief, when she comes. Do not open before the mountain. — M.*

Paloma read it twice. Three times. The chamber's small lights blurred and steadied.

"When she comes," she said aloud. "Not *if*. When. She wrote instructions addressed to me on a vial locked in a cellar I've never heard of, guarded by a step that lies." A laugh escaped her, high and slightly unhinged, and she clapped a hand over her mouth. "Quentin, I want you to understand something. My grandmother never told me anything. Not once. I asked her about her past exactly one time, when I was nine, and she gave me a pancake and changed the subject to road safety. The woman communicated exclusively in baked goods. And now — *now* — she's leaving me mail."

"She knew you'd come here," Quentin said slowly. "Years ago. She knew the thief in the prophecy would be you, and she made sure that when you arrived, you'd find—" He stopped. His eyes went to the vial in her hands, then to the niche, then widened. "Paloma. How many caches are there?"

"Eleven, you said."

"How many has your grandmother's seal been on?"

She stared at him. He stared back. Somewhere overhead, a pigeon expressed opinions.

"All of them," Paloma whispered. "Wouldn't she have to be? If the ledger names me, if the seals are hers — she didn't just hide these. She built the whole network. Every courier, every cellar, every lying step. My grandmother was the keeper of the king's soul, and she spent my childhood teaching me smuggler roads because—" The laugh came again, smaller this time. "Because she was training me. All those pancakes. All those *road safety lectures*. I was curriculum."

Quentin reached out — carefully, the way he did everything now, as though afraid his hands might take something without meaning to — and rested them on her shoulders. "We don't know yet," he said. "We don't know what any of it means. But whatever she built, she built it with you in mind. Whatever the trap is—" he winced at his own words, "—whatever the *plan* is, you weren't the instrument, Paloma. Instruments don't get instructions. Instruments don't get letters."

"No?" Her voice wobbled. "What do they get?"

"Chosen," he said simply. "There's a difference. I'm only alive because people chose me — my mother chose to save pieces of my father, couriers chose to carry them at cost of their necks. Being chosen is heavier than being used. It's supposed to be. That's how you know."

Paloma looked down at the vial with its urgent scratched message, at her grandmother's handwriting pressing through a century of silence, and felt something she had no professional vocabulary for.

"Do not open before the mountain," she read aloud. "Well. Grandmother always did hate it when I peeked at presents early."

"One Christmas story and you're scarred for life?"

"Three Christmases' worth. She wrapped my gifts in *locks*." Paloma tucked the vial inside her coat, against her heart, where thieves kept the things that mattered, and turned for the stairs. "Come on, Your Highness. We have ten more cellars to find, a prophecy to outrun, and I intend to have a very long conversation with a dead woman about communication styles."

Quentin followed her up past the lying step, and as the granite sighed shut behind them, he thought he heard — faint, impossible, from the shelves below — the sound of a hundred small lights burning a little brighter, as if pleased to be remembered.

Above ground, the wind had changed. It came down off the northern passes smelling of snow and something else, something wrong: a sweetness like rotting honey.

Paloma stopped at the chapel door, nostrils flaring, all business again in an instant. "Quentin. What does a memory-eater smell like?"

He went cold. "...Like rotting honey. Why?"

"Because," she said, drawing him back from the doorway and into the shadows, "one of them has been following us since the ford. And it just stopped pretending otherwise."
