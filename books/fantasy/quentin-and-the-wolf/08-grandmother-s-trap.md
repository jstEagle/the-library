## 8. Grandmother's Trap

Paloma had read a great many things in her life that were not meant for her eyes. Love letters tucked inside ledgers. Confessions written in the margins of shipping manifests. A bishop's grocery lists, which had been surprisingly scandalous — three dozen candles and a lock of hair purchased from a woman named Marguerite, every month, like clockwork.

But she had never read her own name written before she was born.

She sat cross-legged on the floor of Quentin's hidden cache beneath the ruined chapel, the ledger open across her knees, a candle stub burning low beside her because Quentin had insisted they conserve the good tallow. The stone around them held warmth the way old dogs hold warmth — patiently, without explanation. Shelves of vials glimmered in the shadows, each sealed with a wax stamp she now recognized down to her bones.

Her grandmother's seal. The thistle-and-thread.

"It's not prophecy," Paloma said, mostly to herself, for the fourth time in an hour.

Quentin looked up from the far shelf, where he'd been cataloguing vials by some private system only half a king could understand. "You said that twenty minutes ago."

"I'm saying it louder this time." She turned the ledger toward him, jabbing a finger at the page. "Look at it properly. Prophecies are *vague*. That's what makes them prophecies. 'A thief shall come.' 'In days of shadow.' 'When the river runs backward' — rivers never run backward, that's how you know nobody actually saw it. This—" she tapped the parchment hard enough to make the candle gutter "—this has my name. My *name*, Quentin. Not 'the Thackeray thief,' not 'a daughter of the road.' *Paloma*. And the date of a vault job I did six weeks ago. You don't predict a specific Tuesday. You *schedule* one."

Quentin came over and sat beside her, folding his long frame down with the careful economy of someone who had learned to take up less space than he used to. Up close, in the candlelight, she could see the hollows in him more clearly — the places where the Wolf had eaten down to the wick. His eyes were still kind, though. That was the thing she kept forgetting and kept relearning: whatever had been taken from him, the kindness hadn't gone with it.

"Show me again," he said. "The part you figured out while I was being useful."

"You were alphabetizing."

"I was *organizing*."

"By letters that aren't in order."

"Memory vials resist alphabetical order," Quentin said with dignity. "They rearrange themselves according to emotional weight. Everyone knows that."

"Nobody knows that."

"My mother knew that."

The small silence that followed was the usual one — the grief-shaped gap that opened whenever his mother came up, which was often, because his mother seemed to be woven through everything he'd ever known. Paloma let it sit, then turned back to the ledger, because work was easier than watching a man mourn someone whose face he could no longer picture.

"Here." She smoothed the page flat. "The script changes. See? The first section — the parts that sound like prophecy, all that thunder about the Wolf unbound and the kingdom devoured — that's formal hand. Ceremonial. But underneath it, in between the lines, there's a second layer. Smaller. Quicker. Written by someone who knew exactly what the first layer was going to be used for."

"And the second layer says?"

"The second layer is instructions."

Quentin went very still. It was remarkable, she thought, how still he could go — as though stillness were a door he could close behind himself. "Instructions for whom?"

"For a thief." Paloma heard her own voice go flat and careful, the way it did when she was lifting something delicate out of someone's head. "For a Thackeray. For—" She stopped. Started again. "For me."

* * *

She had known, somewhere below knowing, since the moment in the vault when the ledger had whispered her name. But knowing a thing in your chest and proving it on paper are two different hungers, and only one of them can be fed.

The cipher had taken her most of the night. It wasn't even a clever one — that was the insult of it. Her grandmother had disguised the second layer using the old road-cant that every smuggler child learned with their milk teeth, the substitution code mothers taught daughters so they could pass notes past toll-keepers. Paloma had cracked harder codes before breakfast. This one she could have read at seven years old.

Which meant, of course, that it had been written for a seven-year-old to read eventually. Or for the grown woman that seven-year-old became.

*"The binding requires three things,"* the hidden text ran. *"A crown emptied into vessels. A mountain that holds memory like wax. And a thief of the true line, who alone can carry the hoard up the mountain without spilling it. The Wolf cannot be killed. It can only be carried — and carrying must be chosen. No chain will hold it. Only a willing hand."*

Below that, smaller yet, cramped against the margin as if the writer had been running out of room or courage:

*"I am sorry for the shape of your life, little bird. I tried to leave you a road instead of a cage. Forgive your old gran. The Wolf watches for prophecy; so we gave it one, and hid the truth inside like a coin inside a loaf."*

Paloma read it four times. On the fifth reading, she discovered she had memorized it already and didn't need the page at all.

"Well," she said.

"Paloma—"

"She *planned* it." The words came out of her with a strange, terrible gentleness, the gentleness of a blade being drawn slowly. "My whole life. Every piece of it. She taught me the roads — not because roads are useful, though they are, but because the rite needed someone who could reach the mountain by ways no one else knew. She taught me to lift memories — not because it pays, though it pays, but because the binding needs a hoard, and hoards don't gather themselves. She taught me the rule. *Never keep what you steal.* Do you know why that rule exists, Quentin? Because a keeper's hands stay clean enough to carry the Wolf. Because if I'd ever kept anything, really kept it, I'd be too full to hold a kingdom's worth of emptiness." She laughed, and it came out wrong, jagged at the edges. "I thought it was *discipline*. I was proud of it. I used to think, look at me, look how strong my rules are, look how well I keep myself."

Quentin said nothing. He was good at that too — at letting silence be a place to stand rather than a wall.

"I was raised like a tool," Paloma said. "A good knife doesn't get asked whether it wants to cut."

"That isn't what she wrote."

"It's what she *meant*."

"Is it?" Quentin leaned forward, and took the ledger from her hands with a care she felt all the way down her arms. He turned back several pages, to the earliest entries, and held it toward the candle. "Then why does she write like this? Look at the ink here — it's blotched. Someone cried on this page, or sweated on it, or both. Tools don't make their makers cry, Paloma. And look—" he traced a passage with one finger, not touching the words so much as pointing at their weight "—she left you the choice. Right here. Read it aloud. I want to hear it in your voice, not hers."

Paloma didn't want to. She read it anyway, because she had never once in her life backed down from a page.

*"The rite fails unless the thief chooses freely. If she reads this and turns away, then the trap is only a story, and the Wolf will win as Wolves do, by patience. I have done what I can. The rest belongs to her. May she be braver than her gran, and luckier, and may she forgive me — or better, may she live long enough that forgiving seems like a small thing to spend."*

The chapel cellar was very quiet after that. Somewhere overhead, wind moved through the broken rafters, and dust sifted down through the candlelight like slow snow.

"She could have told me," Paloma said at last, and her voice cracked on the word *told*, splitting it like a dropped plate. "One conversation. One evening by a fire. 'Paloma, dear, the family trade goes a bit deeper than lifting merchant memories; also, there's a wolf.' Instead she died — she *disappeared*, we don't even know — and left me a riddle in nursery cipher, and let me walk into a vault blind, and let the crown's memory-eaters start hunting me, and let—" Her hands had become fists somewhere along the way. She made herself open them. "And she was right to."

"What?"

"She was *right*." Paloma pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes. "That's the worst of it. Don't you see? If she'd told me, I'd have run. I'd have run so fast and so far that the Wolf would've eaten everything by midwinter. I know myself, Quentin. I'm a coward with excellent footwork. The only version of me who climbs that mountain is a version who thinks she's doing an ordinary job until it's too late to do anything but finish it. She knew that. She *built* that. She built me to be exactly stubborn enough and exactly stupid enough to—" 

"To save everyone."

"—to save *everyone*," Paloma agreed bitterly, "while feeling like an idiot about it the entire time."

To her surprise, Quentin laughed. It was a rusty sound, a hinge that hadn't swung in years, and it startled them both.

"I'm sorry," he said, not sounding sorry at all. "It's only — you've just discovered your entire life was designed by someone else, and your response is to critique the design."

"Somebody has to. She's not here to defend herself."

"No," Quentin said softly. "She's not. But she did leave you a defense, if you'd stop glaring at the page long enough to accept it." He set the ledger gently back in Paloma's lap. "She couldn't tell you. Not because you'd have run — although you would have, and you know it. Because the Wolf *listens*. It eats memory, Paloma. Every plan spoken aloud is a plan it can taste. Your grandmother hid the truth inside a children's rhyme and a smuggler's cipher because those were the only hiding places the Wolf never learned to search. She didn't raise a tool. She raised a locked box, and put you inside it, and trusted you to pick your own lock."

Paloma stared at him.

"That," she said finally, "was almost wise. Did you hear it somewhere, or did you make it up?"

"My father used to say something similar about crowns." Something flickered across Quentin's face — the ghost of a face he could no longer summon. "I can't remember if he said it well."

* * *

They worked through what remained of the night, because grief is lighter to carry when both hands are busy.

Paloma translated the hidden layer line by line, reading aloud, and Quentin copied the key passages onto a strip of linen in case the ledger needed to be burned in haste — a precaution he insisted on with the grim fluency of a man who had watched palaces burn from the inside. The rite, laid bare, was maddeningly simple, the way the best traps always are.

The hoard of the true king — these vials, decades of scattered memory gathered and sealed by her grandmother's hand — had to be carried to the shrine on the mountain that remembered. There, in the stone that held memory like wax, the thief would pour them out in the order written, oldest last. The mountain would make the king whole for one breath. And in that breath, the Wolf — drawn by prophecy, hungry for exactly the feast the ledger promised it — would come to feed, and find itself caught in a binding woven from its own hunger.

"A mousetrap," Paloma said, "the size of a kingdom."

"With a cheese made of a dead king's soul," Quentin added.

"You're very cheerful about it."

"I'm half a man sitting in a hole under a chapel with the only person in the world who might be able to fix me. Cheerfulness is all I've got left, and frankly I'm rationing it."

Paloma snorted despite herself, and then, because the candle was dying and honesty keeps poor hours, said quietly: "I'm furious with her. I need you to know that. I'm going to be furious with her for a very long time. Maybe forever."

"I know."

"But she sealed every one of these vials herself." Paloma lifted the nearest one, turning it so the candlelight caught the wax. The thistle-and-thread stamp gleamed, worn but unmistakable — the same stamp that had sealed the letters her grandmother used to send her, care of waystation inns, back when Paloma had still believed she had a childhood worth writing home about. "Hundreds of them. She spent years gathering a dead king's memories, vial by vial, hiding in cellars like this one. She gave her whole life to it. And then she wrote *sorry* in the margins, in road-cant, in the voice she used for telling me bedtime stories." Paloma swallowed. "Furious people don't apologize in bedtime-story voices. So either she loved me and did a monstrous thing out of love, or—" 

"Or she loved you," Quentin said simply, "and did a monstrous thing out of love. There isn't another option. There rarely is."

Paloma looked at him — this gaunt, hollowed stranger-prince who heard a Wolf in his skull and still found time to be gentle with a thief's grief — and felt something shift in her chest that she refused, on principle, to examine.

"We sleep," she said briskly, closing the ledger. "We leave for the mountain at first light. And Quentin — if I turn into a knife on this road, if I start acting like something that was *made* instead of someone who was *born* —"

"I'll remind you that you alphabetize badly and laugh at your own jokes."

"They're good jokes."

"They're terrible jokes. That's why I remember them."

* * *

She banked the candle and lay down on the bedroll he'd offered, the ledger tucked under her head like a pillow stuffed with thunder. Sleep did not come quickly. It never did, in strange rooms — and this was the strangest room of all, full of her grandmother's handwriting and a dead king's yesterdays.

Just before the dark took her, one last line from the margins rose up unbidden, the smallest entry of all, the one she'd almost missed, squeezed beneath the apology in letters so faint they might have been written in breath rather than ink:

*"If you're reading this, little bird, then the trap has teeth on both sides. Be sure you know which side of it you're standing on before you spring it. Ask yourself what the Wolf wants you to want — and then ask what your gran wanted you to want — and mind the difference. One of them is lying to you. I pray to every thread and thistle it isn't me."*

Paloma lay awake a long while after that, listening to the stone hold its silence, wondering which of her wants had been planted like seeds — and whether the ache she'd carried all her lonely life had ever truly been hers at all.

Above, faint through the chapel's broken roof, the stars wheeled on, indifferent and patient, like eyes that had seen this trap sprung before.
