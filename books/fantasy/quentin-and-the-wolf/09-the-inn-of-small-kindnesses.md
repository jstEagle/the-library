## 9. The Inn of Small Kindnesses

The inn had no name on its sign, only a painting of a fox wearing what appeared to be a bishop's mitre, which Quentin found either charming or blasphemous depending on how long he looked at it.

"It's called the Mitred Fox," said the woman who opened the door, before he could ask. "And before you say anything clever, boy, we've heard every joke about it since before your father wore a crown."

Quentin's mouth, which had been open and primed with something clever, closed slowly.

The woman was short, round, and built like a door that had been reinforced against exactly one kind of visitor. Her hair was iron-grey and cropped close, her forearms bore the faded ink of smuggler tattoos — knots and river-marks that Quentin recognized from his mother's court, though he'd been too young then to know what they meant. He knew now. They were oath-marks. Sworn service, in old script.

"You knew my mother," he said.

"I *carried* your mother." She stepped aside to let them in out of the rain. "Over a flooded ford, when she was eight months pregnant with you and too proud to admit the water was up to her ribs. Come in before you drip on everything I love, which is mostly the floor."

Inside, the common room of the Mitred Fox was small and warm and smelled of woodsmoke, wet wool, and onions doing something ambitious in a pot. Six or seven people looked up from their suppers. Quentin braced for it — the look he'd gotten his whole life in the palace, the flicker of *there's the spare, there's the disappointment, mind your manners around the heir who wasn't* —

But nobody here looked at him like that. Nobody here looked at him like anything at all, except possibly as a man tracking mud.

"Marta," said a lanky fellow by the fire, without looking up. "Your onion thing is burning again."

"My onion thing is *caramelizing*, Doss, which is a word you wouldn't understand because you think salt is a spice."

"It's not?"

Marta sighed the sigh of the deeply put-upon and went to rescue dinner. Quentin stood dripping in the doorway, and beside him Paloma stood dripping slightly less, having apparently mastered the trick of shaking off weather like a cat.

"You didn't tell me they were going to be like this," she murmured.

"Like what?"

"Like *people*."

* * *

They ate at a long table with everyone else, which was the first strangeness. At court, Quentin had eaten in a room designed so that no one could approach him with a knife, or indeed a question. Here, Doss passed him the bread with hands still greasy from mending a saddle, and a girl called Nettie — fourteen, all elbows and opinions — interrogated him about the capital with the fearless bluntness of someone who had never once been told to be quiet in the presence of royalty.

"Is it true the palace has a room where all the walls are mirrors?"

"Yes."

"What's it *for*?"

"Looking at yourself, I suppose."

"That's the saddest thing I've ever heard." She turned to Paloma. "Is he always like this?"

"Always," Paloma said, with relish.

"And you're a memory-thief? A real one?" Nettie leaned in. "Have you ever stolen anything *good*? Like a wedding? Or a whole summer?"

"Nettie," Marta said from the stove, "we do not ask our guests to appraise their crimes at the table."

"But *a whole summer*, Marta—"

"Perhaps after supper."

Quentin watched this happen the way a man watches sunlight through a window he'd only known as a wall. The banter moved around the table like weather, touching everyone, excluding no one. When Doss made a joke at Marta's expense, Marta threw a crust at him and Doss caught it and ate it, and nobody died of embarrassment, and nobody's standing diminished, and nobody recorded the exchange in a formal log for later political use.

He realized he was staring when Paloma's boot found his shin under the table.

"You're doing the face," she whispered.

"What face?"

"The one where you look like you've been handed something breakable and don't trust your hands."

"I don't have a face like that."

"You absolutely have a face like that. It's your second-worst face."

"What's my worst?"

"The one you make when you hear the Wolf. Don't make that one here. It'll spoil the onions."

* * *

After supper, the room did something Quentin had read about in books and never once believed: it made music. Doss produced a fiddle with a cracked body and a sound like a cat arguing with a door hinge, and played anyway, badly and with tremendous conviction, until Nettie took it from him and played properly, and an old man called Bram sang a song about a river that had drowned a bridge and felt bad about it afterward, and somehow the song was funny and mournful at once, which seemed to Quentin to be the entire secret of these people distilled into four verses.

He sat with his back to the warm chimney-stone and listened until the fire burned low, and something in his chest — some clenched, hoarded, palace-shaped knot — loosened by a single degree.

It was enough to frighten him.

He slipped out to the stable yard to be frightened in private, but the night had other plans, because Paloma followed him, because of course she did.

"You're doing the face again," she said.

"It's dark. You can't see my face."

"I can *always* see your face. It's one of my skills. Right after theft and right before lying."

He leaned on the fence. Rain had stopped; the sky over Thornfell's foothills was doing that enormous, indifferent starry thing that sky did when there was no palace roof in the way. "I used to stand in the gallery above the throne room," he said, before he could decide not to. "For hours. Watching my father hold court. Counting how many times he looked up at me."

"How many?"

"Once, I think. Maybe twice a year. On feast days, when protocol required it." He laughed, and it came out wrong. "Here, Marta asked if I wanted more onions. She *asked*. As if my answer mattered. As if the evening would be different — worse — if I said yes."

"Would it?"

"I said yes. There were more onions. It was better." His voice did something embarrassing on the last word, and he stopped talking altogether.

Paloma was quiet for a while. In the dark he could just see her profile, sharp-nosed, watchful, the thief's face she wore even at rest.

"When I was small," she said, "I lived in a house where supper was silent. Not angry-silent. Just — nothing to say, no one to say it to. My grandmother worked nights. I'd eat standing at the counter so the chair wouldn't creak and wake the neighbors' dog, because the dog barking meant the neighbors complaining, and complaints meant questions, and questions—" She shrugged. "You had a gallery above a throne room. I had a counter. Everyone has their version of watching from above the room they can't enter."

"You entered rooms," Quentin said. "That's what you do. You slip into anyone's life and take what you want."

"Take what I'm *paid* for." But her voice lacked teeth. "It's not the same as being asked to stay for more onions."

"No," he agreed softly. "I don't suppose it is."

She looked at him then, and there was something in her expression he couldn't name — a hunger looking at a full plate. Then it was gone, shuttered away behind thief's composure, and she pushed off the fence.

"Come inside before you freeze. Marta will make you tea and pretend it's medicinal. It's just tea. But she'll pretend, and you'll pretend to believe her, and that's the whole religion of this house."

* * *

He dreamed of the Wolf that night, but distantly, muffled, as if the thing in his skull had also settled by a fire somewhere and couldn't quite be bothered. For the first time since the crossroads, Quentin woke without clawing at his own temples.

Downstairs, morning light came grey through small windows, and the smell of frying bread filled the Mitred Fox like a benediction. Marta pressed a plate into his hands and said, "Eat, boy, you've got a face like a folded letter," and Quentin ate, and his face was perhaps less folded than usual.

It was Nettie who ruined everything, in the way of fourteen-year-olds everywhere.

"So you're the true king," she said, swinging her legs from atop a barrel. "Or the true king's son. Which is it?"

"Neither, hopefully, for a long time yet."

"But the ledger says the Thackeray thief unbinds the Wolf, and then—" she counted on her fingers with theatrical care, "—the true memory rises whole, and whoever the memory names is heir, and everyone knows the memory will name you, because you're the last of the line, which means—"

"Nettie," Marta warned.

"—which means you're going to be *king*, and you're sitting in our kitchen eating fried bread like a normal person, and in a month you'll be in the palace eating off gold plates and forgetting we exist—"

"*Nettie.*"

But the words had landed, the way careless words do, straight into the softest available place. Quentin set down his bread. The kitchen had gone quiet around them, the particular quiet of people pretending very hard not to listen.

"I won't forget," he said. "Whatever happens. Whatever the memory names. This house taught me more about crowns in two days than the palace managed in twenty years."

Doss snorted from the corner. "Crowns are easy. It's the heads under them that go strange."

"Then keep my head for me," Quentin said. "All of you. If I start eating off gold plates and forgetting onions — come remind me. However far away I am. That's not a royal decree. It's a request between people who've shared a table."

Silence. Then Marta, briskly wiping her hands on her apron as if she hadn't just gone bright-eyed: "Well. Someone fetch the boy more bread before he says things we'll all be obliged to remember."

They laughed, and the moment broke kindly, and Quentin thought: *this is what belonging feels like. It feels like being handed bread.*

He was so busy holding the feeling, examining it, terrified of dropping it, that he almost missed what happened next.

Almost missed Paloma, by the window, going very still.

Almost missed her eyes fix on the road below the inn — where three riders walked their horses slow along the treeline, wearing the plain grey cloaks that hid the silver throat-pins of the crown's memory-eaters.

Almost missed her hand finding his sleeve, her voice dropped to a whisper that carried the whole cold weight of the pass behind it:

"Quentin. They're not hunting us."

Her eyes tracked the riders, past the inn, past the ford, up toward the ridge road that climbed toward Thornfell's vaults.

"They're riding for the chapel."
