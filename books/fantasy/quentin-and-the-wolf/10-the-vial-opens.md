## 10. The Vial Opens

The vial had been sitting in the bottom of Paloma's satchel for six days, and it was beginning to feel like a stone she'd swallowed.

She'd told herself the rules a hundred times since Thornfell. *Never open what you're paid to carry. Never keep what you steal. Never look.* Three rules, carved into her by hard lessons and harder winters, and all three of them were currently being argued with by a small glass tube full of somebody's memory that hummed against her hip like a sleeping wasp.

"You're going to do it," Quentin said. He sat across the fire from her in the hollow beneath the chapel ruins, turning a strip of dried venison over the flames with the weary patience of a man who had watched this exact internal debate play out on her face for six days. "I've seen you talk yourself into things before. You get a little line between your eyebrows. Like now."

"I don't have a little line."

"You have a whole constellation." He gestured at his own forehead with the venison stick. "Right there. It says *I am about to make a terrible decision and I will be magnificent doing it.*"

Paloma pulled her blanket higher around her shoulders and refused to dignify that with an answer, which, judging by his expression, was answer enough.

The truth — the truth she'd only admitted to herself once, in the dark of the third night, when the vial had warmed against her ribs like something alive — was that she wasn't curious about what was *in* the memory. She knew what was in it. The pale client had been clear enough: the final living memory of King Aldric, the true king, the moment of his ending. A century old, sealed in glass, carried through smuggler roads to be delivered to hands she didn't want to imagine.

No, what kept her awake wasn't curiosity.

It was that the vial *hurt* her.

Memories did that, sometimes, when they'd been sealed too long. A memory left in glass for a hundred years went strange — it started to press outward, hungry for a mind to live in again. And this one pressed harder than any she'd ever carried. Some nights it had felt less like carrying a package and more like carrying a cat in a sack: restless, scratching, wanting *out*.

"Here's what I don't understand," Quentin said, in the tone he used when he'd already understood it perfectly and wanted her to say it out loud. "You opened my ledger seals without blinking. You picked the vault lock at Thornfell while humming. But this—" he nodded at the satchel "—this has you chewing your thumbnail down to the quick for nearly a week."

"That's because the vault didn't care if I opened it," Paloma said. "Things in vaults are patient. Things in vials aren't." She hesitated. Then, because the fire was warm and the dark was long and she was so tired of holding it alone: "It's been talking to me."

Quentin went still. The Wolf lived in his skull; he knew what voices in small places sounded like. "Talking how?"

"Not words. More like... weather." She drew the vial out at last and held it up between two fingers, letting the firelight swim through the silver-gray fog inside. It swirled toward her hand, the way a plant turns toward sun. "A hundred years is a long time to be alone in there. Memories want to be remembered, Quentin. That's the whole nature of them. This one has been *waiting*."

"For whom?"

"For anyone." She turned it slowly. "But I think — and this is the part I haven't said, so don't laugh — I think it would prefer me."

"Why would it prefer you?"

"Because I'm a thief," she said simply. "Because I know how to hold a memory gently. Most people who'd receive this thing would crack it open like an egg and let it pour wherever it wanted. I can carry it without spilling. I think it knows that."

Quentin set down the venison stick. For a long moment he just looked at the vial, and she watched the shadow move behind his eyes — the Wolf-shadow, the half-eaten places where his father's face used to be. When he spoke, his voice was careful, the voice of a man walking on ice over deep water.

"That's my father in there. Or the last of him. One breath of him, anyway — the moment everything ended." His jaw worked. "I have spent eleven years unable to remember his face. The Wolf took it. I remember his hands, his voice saying my name, the smell of the stables — but his face is a smudge, like wet ink. And you're telling me that the last time anyone saw that face truly *himself* is sitting in a bottle six inches from your knee."

"I'm telling you it's heavy," Paloma said. "That's not the same as telling you I should open it."

"Isn't it?"

She looked at him. Firelight caught the gaunt planes of his face, the too-hollow cheeks, the way he held himself like a man expecting a blow. Half a person, walking around wearing the rest of himself like borrowed clothes. And here was the terrible arithmetic of it, the sum she'd been refusing to finish for six days: somewhere in that silver fog might be the face he couldn't find. Might be the last true piece of Aldric that the Wolf hadn't chewed and swallowed and worn as a mask.

"You could see him again," she said quietly. "One minute. Maybe less. Memory doesn't keep time well in a vial — it'll be more like living it than watching it. You'd be *there*. In the room. At the end."

"And you?" Quentin asked, and there it was — the question under the question, because he'd learned her faster than she liked. "If you open it, you take it into yourself first. That's how your craft works, isn't it? The thief holds the door. What happens to the thief who opens a door onto *that*?"

Paloma looked into the fire and told the truth, or most of it.

"I don't know," she said. "I've lifted griefs before. Small ones. A merchant's regret over a dead dog. A widow's last afternoon with her husband — she paid me to take it, actually, said she was tired of carrying it. Griefs are heavy but they're *finished*, you understand? They've settled, like silt in a river. This one isn't finished. This one ended mid-scream." She turned the vial once more. "This one is still happening, a hundred years later. That's why it presses. That's why it talks."

They sat with that. Somewhere above the ruined chapel, wind moved through dead branches, and the fire popped, and the vial hummed its small hungry hum against her palm.

"Rule number two," Quentin said at last. "*Never keep what you steal.*"

"I haven't stolen anything yet."

"You will. Opening it means keeping it, even briefly. Even for a minute. Your rule exists for a reason, and I'd wager the reason has a name and a scar." He leaned forward, elbows on knees, and the firelight made his eyes very green and very tired. "So here's what I think we should do. I think you should open it. Not tonight — tomorrow, at dawn, when you're rested and I'm awake and sober enough to pull you back if it takes you under. And I think you should do it *for* me, because I cannot ask it myself, because if I ask it then I owe it to my father's ghost to watch every second and I don't know if I can survive watching every second." His voice cracked, just slightly, on the last word. "And I think you should hate me a little for asking, because I am asking."

Paloma stared at him.

"That," she said finally, "is the worst request I have ever received, and I once had a man pay me to steal his own wedding day because he couldn't stand to remember his wife's cooking."

"He sounds reasonable."

"He was a monster. You're worse. You're polite about it." She lay back on her bedroll and held the vial up against the black ceiling of the ruined roof, watching the gray fog swirl. "Dawn, then. Sleep, Quentin."

He slept. She didn't. She lay listening to the vial breathe — because it breathed, she was sure of it now, a slow tide-like swell against the glass — and somewhere past midnight she caught herself whispering to it, the way she'd whispered to locked doors her whole career: *soon, soon, I'm coming.*

Which was, she reflected, exactly how every disaster of her life had begun.

* * *

Dawn came gray and cold through the broken chapel windows, laying pale bars across the floor like the ribs of some enormous sleeping animal.

Paloma ate nothing. Eating before a lifting was like stirring a pot before reading the recipe — it muddied everything. She cleared a space on the flagstones, laid out her tools in their proper order: the thin copper needle, the wax seal-knife, the stopper-clamp, the little dish of salt water for her hands. Ritual mattered. Her grandmother had taught her that much, before — before whatever had come before, that hole in her history shaped exactly like a childhood.

Quentin sat three paces away, wrapped in his cloak, hands folded, watching. He'd tried twice to speak and twice stopped himself. Now he simply said, "I'm here."

"I know," she said. "Try to stay *there*."

She took up the vial.

Up close, in honest light, it was worse than it had been in firelight. The fog inside had organized itself overnight — she could see shapes now, suggestion of a room, suggestion of figures, the way clouds suggest faces. And pressing against the glass from within, faint as breath on a window, was something almost like a handprint.

"Oh," Paloma said softly. "Oh, you poor thing."

"What?" Quentin's voice came sharp. "What do you see?"

"It's been trying to get out." She swallowed. "Not attacking. Just — knocking. A hundred years of knocking." She set the vial in the salt dish to steady it, took up the seal-knife, and paused with the blade against the wax. The wax was royal purple, stamped with the sigil she now knew too well — no, not the Wolf sigil, she corrected herself, the *true* sigil, the stag crowned in oak leaves, the Wolf having merely squatted in its house. Under her thumb the wax felt ancient, dry, ready.

"One rule," she said to Quentin, and to herself. "However bad it gets, nobody touches me while I'm under. Touching a mind mid-lift tears things. You can talk to me. Say my name. I'll hear it like someone calling from another room. But hands off."

"Understood."

"If I start speaking in a voice that isn't mine—"

"Hands still off?"

"Hands still off. Say my name louder."

"And if you don't come back?"

She smiled at him, and was surprised to find the smile mostly real. "Then you'll have to come get me. You know the way into minds better than anyone alive, Quentin. Unfortunately."

Something flickered across his face — grief and grim humor sharing a bench — and then she stopped watching him, because it was time.

The wax cracked clean. The stopper gave with a sigh, a sound like a long-held breath released after a century, and the smell that rose out of the vial was impossible: woodsmoke and lilies and hot metal and rain, all of it together, the smell of a whole world compressed into one moment.

The memory rose like smoke finding an open chimney.

Paloma had taken hundreds of memories. She knew the geography of a lift: the touch, the opening, the flood. She knew how to stand in the doorway of another person's past and let it wash past her without pulling her feet from the floor.

She had never taken a memory that *grabbed the doorframe on its way in*.

It didn't flow. It *fell* — a century of pressure released at once, and she was underwater, she was underground, she was—

*—standing in a room she did not know, high in a tower, and the windows are wrong. The light coming through them is wrong, gold the color of infection, and King Aldric is standing at his writing desk with a letter half-written and he looks up and his face—*

His face.

Even inside the memory, even as an intruder, Paloma felt it strike her like a physical blow: a broad, kind, tired face, a face built for laughing at feasts and holding firm in storms, and she understood in one instant why Quentin's heart had never healed, because this was a face worth eleven years of grief.

*Aldric looks up, and the door opens, and what comes through the door wears his brother's body—*

The memory screamed.

There was no other word for it. The moment itself screamed — the king's terror, yes, but underneath it something worse: recognition. Because the thing wearing his brother moved wrong, smiled wrong, and Aldric knew, had known for weeks perhaps, had written half a letter about it, had hoped against hope, and the hope dying in him as the Wolf crossed the room was a sound Paloma felt in her teeth, in her spine, in the roots of her hair—

*—the Wolf speaks with a mouth full of borrowed voices, all of them at once, and it says: "Brother. You kept my seat warm." And its hands—*

Its hands closed on the king's temples, and the taking began, and Paloma was no longer watching it happen, she was *having* it happen, she was the king, she was ninety years old in a young man's memory of an old man's last hour, and the Wolf drank and drank and drank—

Somewhere far away, a voice was calling her name.

*Paloma. Paloma. PALOMA.*

She clawed toward it. The memory fought her — of course it did; it had waited a hundred years for a mind, it did not intend to let go — and for one endless moment she was stretched between two worlds like a rope in a tug-of-war: Quentin's voice on one side, thin and desperate and *real*, and on the other side the king's final thought, which was not fear, which was not pain, but a name, whispered into the dark of the taking, a name he sent out like a message in a bottle:

*Quentin. My Quentin. Whatever comes through that door wearing my love — it isn't me. Remember the garden. Remember the garden, and you'll know.*

Then the vial was empty, and Paloma was on her knees on the cold chapel flags with salt water running down her face, and she could not tell for several seconds whether she was weeping or whether the memory had simply overflowed.

Quentin was beside her — three paces away, hands fisted in his own cloak to keep them at his sides, his face wrecked, because he had watched her live his father's death and he had heard every scream and obeyed the one rule she'd given him.

"The garden," she gasped. "Quentin — the garden. Do you—"

"I don't—" His voice broke apart. "There's a garden. There's — I have a *smell*, I have roses and cut grass and I never knew why, I thought it was nothing, I thought it was—"

"It's a message. Your father hid it inside the taking, inside the Wolf's own feast — he tucked it away where the beast wouldn't taste it. *Remember the garden.*" She wiped her face with both hands, and her hands were shaking, and she found she didn't care. "Quentin, your father knew he was going to lose. And instead of spending his last breath on screaming, he spent it leaving you a map."

Quentin made a sound she had never heard a grown man make — half laugh, half wound — and put his head in his hands.

Paloma knelt there in the dust and the dawn light, empty vial beside her, a dead king's last hour lodged behind her eyes like a splinter, and understood that she had just done the thing her rules existed to prevent. She had kept something. It was in her now, permanent, a tenant who paid no rent and asked no leave.

And the strangest part — the part she'd examine later, when her hands stopped shaking — was that it didn't feel like theft.

It felt like being trusted.

"We have to go," she said, when she could speak steadily. "Now. Today. If the Wolf learns that memory survived — if it ever suspects there's a piece of Aldric it didn't digest—"

She didn't finish, because Quentin's head snapped up, his gaze fixed on the chapel doorway, where the morning mist stood between the broken pillars like a curtain — and moving beyond the curtain, silent as fish under ice, were shapes.

Many shapes. Wearing the crown's gray.

"Memory-eaters," Quentin breathed. "They followed the vial. They let us carry it." His eyes found hers, wide with a horror that had nothing to do with the hunters. "Paloma — they weren't hunting us. We were the delivery."

The nearest shape stepped through the mist, and it was smiling the way the courtiers in the capital smiled, and Paloma's hand closed around her needle.

"*Run,*" she said.
