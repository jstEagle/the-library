## 11. Hunters at the Ford

The river came on them all at once, the way rivers do when you've been arguing about whose fault the last wrong turn was.

"That was a left," Paloma said.

"It was *north*," Quentin said. "North and left are not the same thing, but they are cousins."

"They're not even acquaintances." She stood at the lip of the bank with her hands on her hips, surveying the water like a general surveying a battlefield she intended to win by complaining at it. The Thornfell road had delivered them through two days of pine shadow and one memorable incident involving a beekeeper who had tried to charge them a toll in honey, and now here was the ford — wide, shallow, glittering in the late light, and, to Quentin's eye, entirely too peaceful.

"You're doing the thing," he said.

"What thing?"

"The thing where you look at something harmless and decide it's a trap."

"Everything is a trap," Paloma said cheerfully. "Some traps just haven't been dug yet. That's why you bring a thief. I dig them first."

She was smiling as she said it, though. Two days of walking had done something to the sharpness between them — not softened it, exactly, but worn it smooth, the way a river wears a stone into something you could hold without cutting yourself. She'd begun, somewhere past the second night's fire, to talk in her sleep. Small things. A child counting fence posts. He hadn't mentioned it. Some knowledge felt too much like theft to admit to.

The ford itself was honest work: gray water over gray stones, willows leaning on both banks like tired sentries, a ruined tollhouse sagging on the far side with its roof half-caved and its door hanging open on darkness. On the near bank, a shrine post — one of the old ones, pre-Wolf, carved with knots that were supposed to mean *remember* or possibly *beware of geese*, the stonemasons of that era having been ambitious but imprecise.

Quentin touched the knot as they passed. Habit. His mother used to touch them too.

He couldn't, this morning, quite see her face when he thought of that.

That had been happening more. Little absences, small and soft-edged, like moths eating holes in a coat from the inside. The name of the servant who'd taught him to whistle. The taste of the honeycakes in the east kitchen. His mother's face, which should have been the last thing the Wolf could reach, the deepest-buried thing — except the Wolf had been digging in him for years now, patient as water, and the deepest things were the ones it had been drinking longest.

*You'll forget me entirely,* the Wolf murmured in the back of his skull, in his father's voice, which was the cruelest trick of it. *And won't that be a mercy.*

"Shut up," Quentin said aloud.

Paloma glanced over. "I didn't say anything."

"I know."

"Talking to the architecture again?"

"The architecture started it."

She laughed — a short, surprised sound, like she'd been ambushed by her own mouth — and waded in ahead of him, boots slung around her neck, trousers rolled to the knee, testing each stone with her toe before trusting it. She crossed water like she did everything else: competently, quickly, and with an air of being owed money for it.

Quentin followed. The water was cold enough to make him gasp, which he decided not to be embarrassed about, since Paloma had also gasped, and had then pretended she hadn't by making it into a word: "*Hup.*"

Halfway across, the birds stopped.

Not flew away. *Stopped* — mid-phrase, three different songs cut off at once, the way a room goes quiet when someone important walks in. Quentin froze on the slick stone, water tugging at his calves, and every hair on his arms stood up in a language older than words.

"Paloma."

"I feel it." She'd gone still too. Her eyes moved — not to the trees, not to the ruin, but to the shadows *under* the willows on the far bank, where the afternoon light pooled thick and wrong, thick like syrup, thick like something pretending to be shade. "Don't run. Running is a confession."

"To whom?"

"To whatever's wearing that shadow."

It came out of the willow-shade the way ink comes out of a broken bottle — no shape at first, just a spreading, and then a man-shaped absence in the middle of it, tall and narrow, wearing a crown courier's grey cloak with the royal seal at the shoulder. Where its face should have been there was a smoothness, a suggestion, the way a portrait looks if you stare just past it. Memory-eaters didn't bother with faces. Faces were for people who planned to be remembered.

"Two of them," Paloma breathed. "There's another — behind us — don't turn—"

Quentin turned, because he had never in his life successfully obeyed an instruction like *don't turn*, and there it was: a second absence standing in the shallows downstream, close enough now that he could hear it. Not breathing. *Sifting*. A dry whispering, like sand poured through cupped hands, like pages turning in a book written in no alphabet he knew.

"Hallo," Quentin said, because his mother had raised him to greet even monsters, *especially* monsters, because a monster you've greeted is a monster that has paused, and a paused monster is a monster you might survive.

The nearest eater tilted its blank head. And then it spoke, in a voice assembled out of other voices, the way a crow assembles a song out of stolen sounds — and one of the voices it used was his father's.

"*The heir returns to the ford.*"

"Heir?" Paloma said loudly. "No heir here. We're honey merchants. There's been a terrible bee shortage, we're going to fix it—"

"*Thief.*" The eater's attention slid to her and Quentin felt the air change pressure, felt the thing *taste* toward her the way a snake tastes the wind. "*We know what rides in your blood. Thackeray. The ledger names you.*"

"Well," Paloma said, and Quentin heard the exact moment her fear curdled into fury, "the ledger can mind its own business."

The eater moved.

There was no other word for it. One moment it stood ten feet away in the shallows and the next it was simply *closer*, the distance folded away like a letter, and Quentin threw himself sideways off the stone into water barely knee-deep because dignity was for people who weren't about to die. He hit the riverbed, rolled, came up spluttering with a handful of rock gravel and no weapon, because of course he had no weapon, because he was an exiled prince whose entire armory consisted of sarcasm and a corkscrew.

Paloma fought like weather. He'd seen hints of it on the road — quick hands, quicker eyes — but watching her now was like watching a squall decide where to land. She had no weapon either; she had *stones*, and she threw them with a fishwife's accuracy, and when one cracked against the lead eater's smooth nothing-of-a-face it made a sound like a bell struck underwater, a low mournful *donnnng*, and the whole river shivered.

"Quentin!" she shouted. "The shrine post! Get behind the shrine post!"

"Why? What does it do?"

"I don't know! It's old! Old things confuse them!"

It was, he would reflect later, the worst tactical advice ever given by anyone, anywhere, and it worked. He scrambled up the near bank, dripping, and put the carved post between himself and the eaters, and both of them flinched — flinched, actual recoils, their edges rippling like disturbed pondwater. The knots on the stone warmed under his hand. Or he imagined they did.

"*Clever thief,*" the lead eater rasped, circling, its voice cycling through borrowed tones. A woman now. A child. His father again, and Quentin's chest clenched so hard he nearly missed the lunge.

It wasn't lunging for him.

It went for Paloma.

She saw it coming — of course she did — but she was ankle-deep in current and it folded distance, and its hand closed around her wrist before she could twist free, and Quentin heard her scream, a short furious sound, and then he heard something worse:

Silence. From *her*. As if someone had reached into the middle of her voice and taken a page out.

"No!" He was moving before thought, off the bank, back into the water, the corkscrew somehow in his fist like that would matter. "Take me! You want the heir — the ledger wants *him* — I'm the one full of king!"

The eater paused. Turned its blankness toward him. And Quentin understood, in the cold arithmetic way you understand things when your blood has gone to ice, that he had just made a very large mistake, and that some part of him had known he was making it, and had made it anyway, because the scream had been hers.

"*Yes,*" the eater said, gently, in his father's voice. "*Yes, you are.*"

Its other hand shot out — faster than folding, faster than anything — and caught him by the jaw, and the world went white and grainy, like looking into snowfall, like looking into static, and he felt it *dig*.

Not pain. Pain would have been kind. This was *searching* — a vast dry patience riffling through him, shelf after shelf after shelf, and he felt it pause here and there, tasting: the smell of the library at Thornfell, the weight of a wooden sword, the sound of rain on a palace roof — taking sips, horrible little sips, and then going deeper.

Deeper.

Down past the Wolf's damage, down to the sealed cellar where he kept the earliest things, the things he'd hidden even from himself, and he felt the eater find them and go still with something like *delight*.

"*Here,*" it whispered. "*Here is the name.*"

"Don't—" His own voice sounded miles away. "Please—"

Every childhood has a name that isn't the name anyone calls you. Not the formal one, the one on documents. The one your mother says when you've fallen and skinned your knee — not scolding, not the full formal rebuke, but the *soft* one, the private one, the one that means *I know exactly who you are and I am holding all of it*. Quentin had carried his like a coin in a boot, all these years of exile, all these years of the Wolf's slow drinking. It was the smallest thing he owned.

It was the last thing.

The eater took it the way you take a thread from a hem — one clean pull, almost tender.

Quentin came back to himself kneeling in the shallows, water pushing at him, Paloma shouting his name — his *other* name, the public one — and he looked up at her and opened his mouth to say *I'm fine* and discovered, with a calm and total horror, that he could remember everything else. The library. The rain. The wooden sword. Every room of his childhood stood intact around him, furnished, lit —

Except the person who had named him.

He reached for his mother and found a shape where she'd been. Warmth where a face should be. He knew she had brown hair — did she? He knew her hands had been strong, because she'd pulled him up onto horses, onto walls, out of ponds — but the face above the hands was a lit window in an empty house. He knew she had loved him. The love was still there, enormous, structural, load-bearing. But the face the love belonged to was gone, and the soft name it lived in was gone, and when he tried to say it — tried to shape it, *m-* something, *ma-* something, a word that should have been worn smooth as river glass from a thousand uses — his mouth produced only a hollow, and the hollow echoed.

"Oh," he said. Very quietly. In the middle of a battle, in the middle of a river, with monsters closing in. "Oh, no."

"QUENTIN!" Paloma had a fistful of shrine-post gravel and was hurling it two-handed at the eaters like a woman fighting off hail. "Whatever it took, we can get it back, that's the whole point of me, now MOVE—"

The lead eater swayed. It held something up before it — nothing visible, nothing any eye could catch, but Quentin *felt* it, felt his own name displayed like a jewel, felt the thing savor it. Memory-eaters didn't eat what they took. They *delivered* it. Somewhere in the capital, in the vaults beneath the festival halls, his childhood name would be filed in a drawer, catalogued, stored — retrievable. Recoverable. If you got to the drawer.

If the drawer still existed by spring.

"*The Wolf thanks you,*" the eater said, in his father's voice, and then, horribly, kindly: "*It is lighter now, isn't it?*"

And Quentin, kneeling in the ford with his mother's face gone out of the world, felt the truth of it like a knife between ribs — because it *was* lighter. That was the obscenity of it. The grief had lost its address and the loss weighed nothing at all.

Then Paloma hit the eater with the shrine post.

Not thrown — *carried*, sprinted up the bank and swung two-handed like a girl playing village cricket against the apocalypse, and the ancient carved wood connected with the creature's blankness and the sound it made was the bell-sound again, deeper, a whole drowned cathedral ringing, and the eater came apart. Not died — unraveled, unwove, streaming apart into threads of gray that the wind took and the willows took and the water took, and its stolen cargo scattered with it, and Quentin felt — faint, distant, like hearing a shout from another valley — something small and bright fall back toward him and miss, sliding away downstream into the dark under the far bank.

His name. Sinking. *Right there.*

The second eater shrieked — a sound like a book burning — and fled upstream into its own shadow, gone.

"Get it!" Quentin was on his feet, stumbling, scrabbling toward the deep pool beneath the far willows. "Paloma, it fell, my name fell in the water—"

She was already diving.

He watched her go under — a clean thief's dive, barely a splash — and the pool swallowed her, dark water over dark stone, and the seconds stretched, and stretched, and he stood in the shallows dripping and shaking with his heart hammering *her name, her name, her name* against his ribs like a bird against glass, because his own was gone but hers he still had, hers he would keep, hers—

She surfaced, gasping, hair plastered black across her face, and he read the answer before she spoke.

"Gone," she panted. "Silt. It went into the silt. Things that sink in this river—" she hauled breath, "—they're in the river's memory now. And rivers, Quentin, do not give things back."

The evening came down soft around them. They dragged themselves onto the far bank and built a fire in the ruined tollhouse because the alternative was not building a fire, and neither of them suggested it. Paloma wrung out her sleeves with grim economy. Quentin sat with his hands around the flames and did inventory.

Library. Rain. Wooden sword. Honeycakes — no. Gone too, he realized; that had gone earlier, or maybe tonight, the shelves shifting. The love remained. The love was a cathedral with no windows. He knew the shape of everything his mother had been to him and could summon none of her features, and when he tried to grieve properly the grief kept arriving at a door that opened onto nothing.

"I had a name," he said, to the fire. "A real one. Not Quentin — that's the name for strangers. There was one only she used."

Paloma didn't say *I'm sorry*. He watched her decide not to, and loved — in the fierce, unexamined way of the badly wounded — that she didn't. Instead she fed the fire a stick and said, "Describe it."

"What?"

"The feeling. When she said it. Describe the feeling. I'm a professional. Feelings leave residue. Residue can be tracked." She glanced at him sideways, firelight catching the stubborn set of her jaw. "I've followed less."

He closed his eyes. Reached past the hollow. And found — yes. Residue. The feeling had soaked into everything around it the way honey soaks into bread.

"Warm," he said. "Like — being picked up. Being *known*. Like the whole world narrowing down to one person saying one syllable and meaning all of you." His voice cracked on the last of it. "Like coming home before you knew you'd left."

Paloma was quiet a long moment. Then she pulled the client's vial from her shirt — the sealed one, the cold-handed stranger's payment, the thing she'd sworn she wouldn't open — and looked at it in the firelight with an expression Quentin couldn't read, an expression that had longing and dread in it in equal parts.

"There's a place," she said slowly, "three days north, where memories don't sink. Where the ground holds them. My grandmother's seals were cut from its stone." She turned the vial over once, twice. "If a name can fall into a river's memory... then maybe it can be *poured* out of one. But not by me alone, and not for free, and not—" she stopped. Started again, quieter. "I need to tell you what I figured out in the chapel vault. About the ledger. About my grandmother. Because I think—" her thumb traced the vial's seal, "—I think the reason I know how to track your name is that I was *built* to. And I think you deserve to know what you're traveling with before we walk into whatever she set up."

Above the ruined roof, the first stars came out. Somewhere downstream, deep under the willow roots, a name lay in the dark among the pebbles, waiting — and Quentin, who had lost the word for himself, held tight to the two he had left.

"Tell me," he said.
