## 12. I'll Remember For You

The river had taken his name.

That was how I thought of it in the days after the ford — not that the memory-eater had torn it out of him, but that the water had swallowed it, the way rivers swallow coins and keys and small careless children. Somewhere downstream, caught in a bend of silt, there was a boy's name that belonged to Quentin, and neither of us could dive deep enough to fetch it back.

He didn't talk about it. That was the first thing I noticed, and I am professionally good at noticing what people don't say. He talked about everything else with a kind of desperate brightness — the route east, the weather turning, the way his boots were finally breaking in — but when I asked him, once, gently, over the fire, "What did she look like? Your mother?" he went still as a held breath.

"I don't know," he said. "I know I knew. I can feel the shape of knowing, like a coat hook on a wall where a coat used to hang." His hand drifted up to his sternum without his permission. "There's just... the hook."

I should have told him then. I had been carrying it since the ford, tucked behind my ribs where I keep the things that matter, and every hour I didn't tell him felt less like caution and more like theft. But here is the trouble with being what I am: I have spent my whole life learning that the moment you hand something precious to another person, you find out exactly how much you trust them, and I have never once liked the answer.

So instead I said, "Eat your rabbit," and he did, and the moment walked past us both and out into the dark.

* * *

We made for the high country after the ford, because memory-eaters hunt low ground the way owls hunt hedgerows, and smuggler roads climb. The path Quentin chose was barely a path at all — a goat's opinion of a road, switchbacking up through pine and scree — and by the second day he was moving wrong. Not limping. Nothing so honest as limping. Just... drifting. Twice he took the wrong fork at places where there was only one sensible fork, and once he stopped dead in the middle of the trail and stared at a view of the valley below as if he were trying to remember why anyone would bother looking at it.

"You're bleeding," I said, the third time, pointing at his sleeve.

He looked down with genuine surprise. A gash ran from elbow to wrist, shallow but long, already stiffening brown. "When did I—"

"The brambles. This morning. You swore. It was very creative. I was almost proud."

"I don't remember swearing."

"No," I said softly. "I expect you don't."

Here's what nobody tells you about memory-work: the wound isn't always where the knife goes in. When a memory-eater tears something out of you, they don't cut clean. They rip, and the rip runs through everything touching what they took. Quentin hadn't lost just his childhood name. He'd lost the morning he learned it, the person who taught it to him, the little ceremonies around it — and threaded all through the rest of him, loose threads now, fraying ends of himself that kept catching on the world and pulling free.

He was unraveling. Slowly, quietly, in a way he clearly didn't fully know yet, and I watched him not-knowing with my heart going hard and small in my chest.

That night we sheltered in an old drover's bothy, walls of dry stone, roof mostly cooperating. I got a fire going while he sat on the floor with his back against the wall and his knees drawn up, and I watched him try to remember which boot came off first, and fail, and laugh about it — laugh, in this thin cracked way that frightened me more than any scream would have.

"Quentin."

"Hm?"

"Give me your hands."

"Why?"

"Because I'm about to do something either very kind or very rude, and I'll need both of them still."

He put his hands in mine. They were cold. They were always cold; I'd stopped noticing, the way you stop noticing your own heartbeat, except now I noticed, and holding them felt like holding something rescued from a flood.

"What are you doing?" he asked.

"Looking," I said. "Don't fight it. Fighting it feels like drowning, and I'd rather you didn't drown in front of the fire, it's bad luck."

I let myself sink into the shallows of him — just the surface, just the top layer, the way you test bathwater. And there it was, exactly where I'd left it: his mother's face. Not *his* — I mean the memory of her face, the one the memory-eater had ripped out of him and dropped, because even memory-eaters can't carry everything, and a torn-out thing falls somewhere. It had fallen into the space between us, into the residue of the ambush, and I had caught it without meaning to, the way you catch a stranger's child falling off a wall — pure reflex, no plan, and suddenly you're holding someone else's whole life and everyone is staring at you.

A woman with grey coming in at her temples and ink on three fingers. Laughing at something out of frame. There was a smell attached to the memory — beeswax and cedar — and a sound, half a sentence: *"—and if you tell your father, I'll deny everything."* She had a conspirator's eyes. She had, I realized with a jolt, a smile like Quentin's, or rather Quentin had a smile like hers, though his had been worn down by exile until it was more scaffolding than structure.

I opened my eyes. He was watching me with an expression I couldn't read, somewhere between fear and hope, and both of them aimed at me like I was a door.

"Well?" he said.

"She had ink on her fingers," I said. "Three fingers. Left hand. She laughed like she was getting away with something. And she said—" I stopped, because saying it felt enormous, felt like handing him a live coal. "She said, 'If you tell your father, I'll deny everything.'"

Quentin made a sound I have no word for. Not a sob. Something older than sobbing, something the body does before language gets involved. He bent forward over our joined hands until his forehead nearly touched them, and his shoulders shook, and I sat there holding a grown man's grief in my two palms like water, terrified of spilling it.

"She used to forge the chancellery seals," he said into his hands. "For fun. For *fun*, she'd — I remember, I remember the ink—" He looked up, wet-eyed and blazing. "You have it. You have her."

"For now," I said. "It's not mine, Quentin. None of it ever is. That's the whole point of me."

"Then keep it anyway."

"That's against my rules."

"To hell with your rules."

And there it was — the argument I'd been having with myself since the ford, handed to me by the one person with no right to win it. My rule was the oldest thing about me, older than my craft, older than my name: *never keep what you steal.* Because keeping is how thieves become dragons, sitting on hoards until the hoard sits on them. Because every memory I'd ever carried and given back had come home lighter than it left, and I wanted to stay light. Because —

Because none of those reasons survived contact with his face.

"It's not stealing if it fell," I said at last, which was sophistry, and we both knew it, and he grinned at me through the tears anyway, and I decided I hated him a little, in the specific way you hate people who make you better than you planned to be.

* * *

Carrying a memory that isn't yours is a particular discipline. Most people think it's like carrying a parcel. It isn't. It's like carrying a bird — alive, warm, wanting things. His mother's memory wanted her son. All night it pressed against the inside of my ribs, orienting itself toward him across the dark like a compass needle, and I lay awake feeling it flutter every time he shifted in his sleep, thinking: *this is what it must be like to be needed on purpose.*

I've stolen ten thousand memories. Sunsets, first kisses, the smell of a bakery that burned down, a soldier's last sight of home. I've held griefs so large I had to walk carefully so they wouldn't slosh. But I had never once carried something *for* someone — carried it the way you carry a promise, with intent to return. Theft is a straight line: take, run, deliver, done. This was a circle. This had no end until I gave it back, and giving it back meant finding a way to put a memory into a man that the eaters had scooped out, and I didn't know if such a way existed.

Which meant, if I was honest, that I might be carrying his mother forever. Which meant that some part of her would be mine to keep safe for the rest of my life. The thought should have felt like a burden.

It felt like being trusted. By a woman I'd never met, who'd been dead or worse for years, who had ink on her fingers and a conspirator's eyes. *Look after him,* the memory seemed to say, in her voice, in that half-teasing tone. *He's terrible at it himself.*

"He is, rather," I whispered to the dark, and slept.

* * *

In the morning I started teaching him to hold on.

Not to the memory — he couldn't touch that, it lived in me now — but to himself. Because I'd seen what the eaters' rips do, and I'd realized something in the night that I didn't like: the Wolf hadn't chosen Quentin randomly. Half-consumed minds don't heal on their own. They fray. And a frayed heir is a safe heir.

"So," I said, walking, breath steaming, the trail climbing through pines so tall they seemed to be holding the sky up out of politeness. "New game."

"I despise games."

"You're going to love this one. Every hour, you tell me one true thing about yourself. Small things. The smaller the better. What you had for breakfast the morning your father — no wait, not that one yet, too heavy. Start stupid. Favorite color. Worst chore. Whether you snore."

"I don't snore."

"You snore like a landslide arguing with a sawmill."

"That's slander."

"It's data. See? You're already playing." I hopped a root and turned to walk backward ahead of him, which is a habit that has nearly killed me four times and I refuse to abandon. "Here's the theory. The eaters ripped a hole in you, and holes grow when nothing holds the edges. So we hold the edges. Every true thing you say is a stitch. In a week you'll have enough stitches that the hole can't spread past them."

"And if it spreads anyway?"

"Then I'll steal the hole right out of you," I said lightly, "and carry it around for a bit, and give it back smaller. That's rather my specialty."

He stopped walking. The pines creaked overhead. "Can you do that? Actually?"

"I don't know," I admitted. "Nobody's ever asked me to mend anything. People hire me to take. Taking is easy. Any crow can take." I shrugged, and found my throat had gone tight, and pushed through it. "But my grandmother used to say a thief who can't give back is just a magpie with ambitions. So. We're going to find out if I'm a magpie."

Something moved through his face then — that flicker I'd started cataloguing, the one where he looked at me like I was a page in a book he'd read a long time ago and couldn't place. "Your grandmother," he said slowly. "The one whose handwriting was on the seals."

"Don't," I said.

"I didn't say anything."

"You said it with your eyebrows. Your eyebrows said 'the ledger, the prophecy, the Thackeray thief, the trap.' I know what my eyebrows sound like; I assume yours work the same."

"My eyebrows are very sorry."

"They should be. They're getting ahead of the story." I turned and marched on before my face could betray me, because the truth was the ledger lived under my shirt against my skin these days, and some nights I took it out and read grandmother's looping script by moonlight and felt like a key that had just learned it was shaped like a lock. *The Thackeray thief shall unbind the Wolf-King.* Raised like a tool in a box, and the box labeled *destiny*, and I hadn't even gotten a say. But that was a fire for another day. Today's fire was smaller and closer: keep this man stitched together long enough to reach Thornfell.

"One true thing," I called over my shoulder. "Hour's up. Go."

He was quiet for twenty paces. Then: "My favorite color was blue. I say *was* because I'm no longer certain, and it distresses me more than losing the name did."

"Noted. Stitch one."

"Stitch one," he repeated, and I heard him testing the weight of it, and something in his voice settled half a degree.

* * *

We played the game for three days. It worked better than I'd dared hope, and worse than I pretended.

The stitches held — favorite color (blue, probably), worst chore (scrubbing the palace kitchens after the honey festival, a story involving forty barrels and a prince's bright idea), first crush (a kitchen girl named Sera who could juggle knives, "which should have been a warning about my taste in dangerous women"), the exact sound of the palace bells at midwinter ("they ring flat, everyone pretends they don't notice, it's treason to mention"). Little pegs of self hammered into the wall of him, and around each one the fraying slowed, and by the third evening he laughed at something I said — really laughed, the scaffolding gone for a second, and underneath it his mother's smile, and I had to look away fast and pretend great interest in the fire.

But the hole was still there. Stitches don't fill a hole; they just fence it. Some nights I'd catch him standing at the edge of it, staring inward, and I'd see him reach for his mother's face the way a tongue finds a missing tooth, and find the hook, and the empty air where the coat hung.

On the third night he said, abruptly, into the quiet: "What does she look like? Now. Today. Does she change?"

Memories do that, in a keeper's care. They settle, or they fade, or — if you tend them — they deepen. I closed my eyes and checked. "Same," I said. "Grey at the temples. Ink on three fingers. She's laughing at something I can't see. She looks..." I searched for it. "She looks like someone who hid a lot of jokes inside serious sentences."

"That's her," he said, wrecked and grateful. "That's exactly her." He wrapped his arms around his knees. "Paloma. Why are you doing this? Truly. You could walk away. You could sell her to the eaters for more coin than you'd see in ten years. You could sell *me*. Don't think I haven't done the arithmetic — I'm worth more hollowed than whole, to certain buyers."

I fed a stick to the fire and bought myself a moment.

"Because I'm a thief," I said finally, "and I've never once stolen anything worth keeping. Do you understand? Ten thousand memories, and every single one went back to its owner, and I have nothing. No sunset that's mine. No first kiss. No mother with ink on her fingers. I built my whole life on not-keeping, because keeping is how you get hurt, and I told myself it was discipline." The fire popped. "And then some memory-eater drops your mother into my hands, and she fits, and I don't want to give her back — I want to, I *will*, don't look at me like that, I will — but wanting is new. Wanting is the most expensive thing I've ever shoplifted." I jabbed the stick at him. "So I'm keeping her safe until I can return her, because returning her is the first honest job I've ever taken, and if I botch it I'll have proof that I'm exactly what I've always suspected, which is a magpie with ambitions."

The silence went on long enough that I started composing escape routes.

Then Quentin said, very quietly: "I'll remember for you."

"What?"

"When you give her back — if it works, if you can pour her home — and someday you stand there with empty hands again, having kept nothing, having *kept* nothing—" He leaned forward, firelight in his eyes, deadly earnest. "Someone should keep the keeping. So I'll remember it. The woman who carried my mother up a mountain and gave her back. I'll remember for you. That's the trade."

I opened my mouth to say something flippant and absolutely nothing flippant arrived. My chest did a thing I refused to examine. "That's a terrible trade," I managed. "I'm carrying priceless royal memory and you're offering... sentiment."

"Best currency there is. Never devalues. Can't be eaten." He smiled — her smile, worn smooth. "Take it or leave it."

"...I'll take it," I said gruffly, "but I'm rounding down."

* * *

Two mornings later we topped the pass, and the land fell away eastward, and there it was: the capital, far off in its bowl of haze, banners too small to read, smoke rising soft and domestic. From this distance it looked almost innocent.

Quentin stood beside me and went rigid.

"They're preparing for it," he said. "Look at the squares — see the white drapery? That's forgetting-festival bunting. The Feast of Lethe. Once a year, the whole city celebrates. Music, wine, paper lanterns with regrets written on them, burned at midnight. Everyone smiles." His voice had gone flat and careful. "Everyone smiles with nothing behind it. The Wolf loves the festival. It feeds best when feeding looks like joy."

"How long?"

"Nine days. Maybe eight." He glanced at me sideways, and I saw him doing his own arithmetic now — the fraying heir, the ticking city, the ledger against my ribs with my name already written in it. "We'll need papers. Faces. A way through gates that taste thoughts."

"I know a man," I said, "who owes me a favor, who knows a woman, who owes *him* a favor, who owes *me* a threat."

"That's not how favors work."

"That's exactly how smuggler favors work. Keep up." I touched my shirt, where the ledger lay, and beneath it, faint as a second heartbeat, his mother's memory turned toward him in her sleep and settled again. Soon, I promised her. Soon I'll learn how to pour you home.

Eight days. One city full of smiling hollow faces. One Wolf wearing a father's crown.

"Come on, then," I said, and started down.
