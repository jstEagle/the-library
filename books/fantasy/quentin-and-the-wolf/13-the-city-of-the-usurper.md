## 13. The City of the Usurper

*Paloma*

The city announced itself three miles out, the way a feast announces itself to someone who hasn't eaten in days.

Paloma smelled it first — woodsmoke and roasting meat and something sweeter underneath, sugar burned in great vats, the smell of a place spending money it didn't have. Then came the sound: bells, dozens of them, ringing without rhythm or reason, as if the bell-ringers had forgotten what they were ringing *for*. Which, she thought grimly, was probably exactly the case.

"Feast of Forgetting," Quentin said beside her. He'd pulled his hood low, and even so he kept his face angled away from the road, toward the drainage ditch, like a man ashamed of the sky. "It used to be called the Feast of Remembrance. My mother started it. You'd bring an offering to the shrine — a ribbon with a name written on it, someone you loved. The whole capital smelled like cedar and candle wax for a week."

"And now?"

"Now they burn the ribbons." His voice was flat as slate. "At midnight. All of them. It's supposed to be liberating."

They stood at the crest of the last hill, and there it was: Vhalderan, the capital, wearing its festival like a woman wearing her dead husband's coat. Lanterns strung from every eave. Banners in royal blue — but Paloma noticed the sigil stitched on them was wrong somehow, the wolf's head rendered slightly too large for the shield, the way a borrowed garment hangs badly on the wrong shoulders. Crowds poured through the gates in their festival best, laughing, singing, carrying armfuls of ribbon.

"It's beautiful," she said, because it was, and because saying so felt like stepping on a nail.

"That's how you know," Quentin said.

---

Getting through the gate was almost insultingly easy. The guards weren't checking faces; they were checking hands. Every traveler pressed their palm to a slab of grey stone set into the gatehouse wall, and the stone drank something from them — Paloma saw it happen, saw the faint silver shimmer that slid up out of a farmer's calloused hand and vanished into the rock — and then the guard waved them through with a smile that never quite reached anywhere near his eyes.

"What is that?" she murmured as they joined the queue.

"A tithe." Quentin kept his voice under the crowd-noise. "One small memory per entry. A taste. The Wolf keeps the city half-fed so no one ever gets hungry enough to notice they're starving."

"What kind of memory does it take?"

"Whatever you'll miss least. That's the cruelty of it. It knows."

When her turn came, Paloma pressed her palm to the stone and braced herself the way she'd brace against a pickpocket — feeling for the tug, ready to feed it something worthless, a memory of a rainy Tuesday, a bad meal, a stranger's cough. But the stone hesitated. She felt it probe her, delicate as a tongue testing a wound, and then — she would have sworn this on her grandmother's grave, if she'd known where her grandmother was buried — the stone *flinched*.

It took nothing. The guard smiled his empty smile and waved her through, and Paloma walked into Vhalderan with all her stolen years intact and a cold finger tracing down her spine.

*It recognized me,* she thought. *Or it recognized what I carry.*

Beside her, Quentin pressed his hand down and she watched him fight it — watched the muscles in his jaw lock, watched sweat bead at his temple as the stone tried to sip from him and found, apparently, nothing left worth taking. Half a man, half a mind; the Wolf had already drunk deep from this well. The guard waved him through too, and only when they were fifty paces inside did Quentin let out a breath.

"It knew me," he said quietly. "It tasted me and let go. Like finding a cup already drained."

"Mine wouldn't take anything either."

"I noticed." He glanced at her sideways, and there was something in his face she couldn't read — worry, maybe, or the beginning of fear. "Paloma. What exactly are you carrying?"

She thought of the hoard beneath the chapel, the sealed vials, the king's scattered memories. She thought of the client's vial, still hidden against her ribs, unopened since that terrible night in the vault. She thought of everything she'd ever lifted from anyone, wrapped and labeled and kept, breaking her one rule over and over until her pack weighed more than she did.

"Everything I've ever taken," she said. "And I've taken a great deal."

Quentin looked at her for a long moment. Then, unexpectedly, he laughed — a short, rusty sound, like a hinge remembering oil.

"Of course you have. Of course. The prophecy names a thief, and the thief arrives carrying a library." He shook his head. "My mother would have liked you. She collected people the same way. Never let a good soul walk past without pocketing it."

"Your mother sounds like she'd have been arrested for it."

"She was, eventually. Different charge." And his face closed again, the way it always did when his family came up, and Paloma learned not to push, the way you learn not to touch a burn.

---

Inside the walls, the festival swallowed them whole.

It should have been wonderful. That was the horror of it. Musicians on every corner playing tunes that were just slightly too cheerful, the notes a half-step too bright, like smiles held too long. Children ran through the streets with paper lanterns. Roasting chestnuts, mulled wine, a juggler whose clubs caught the lantern-light like falling stars. Everything generous. Everything warm.

And every face, empty.

Not vacant — that would have been easier. Vacant faces you could pity and move past. These faces were *smiling*. They were laughing and toasting and dancing, and behind every smile was a smoothness, a pleasant blankness, like a room that had been tidied by someone who removed more than dust. Paloma caught a baker's eye across a stall and the man beamed at her with tremendous warmth and she knew — she *knew*, the way she knew the weight of a memory-vial by feel — that he could not have said his own daughter's name if you asked him. He'd pay the tithe again tomorrow. He'd keep paying. And he'd smile while the stone drank, because whatever it took, it took the grief along with it, and grief was heavy, and everyone here was so very light.

"Look at them," she whispered. They stood beneath an awning, watching the square fill for the evening's burning. "They're happy."

"They're *managed*." Quentin's hands had curled into fists inside his sleeves. "That's the trick my father never understood. You don't rule people by taking everything. You take just enough that they're grateful for what's left. He—" His voice snagged. He meant the Wolf, wearing his father. "He told me once, before I understood what he was, that the happiest kingdom is the one that can't remember being unhappy. I thought it was wisdom. I wrote it down. I *kept it*." A bitter breath. "I kept it like a treasure."

Paloma did the only thing she knew how to do with another person's pain: she reached over and took his hand. Not a theft. Just a loan.

His fingers were cold. They stayed clasped anyway.

"We need a way into the palace," she said. "During the festival, when every eye is on the burning."

"There's a servants' gate on the river side. Old postern, from my grandfather's day. The guards rotate at the third bell." He paused. "You want to hear the worst part? I know that schedule because I helped write it. I was nine. I wanted to impress the captain of the guard." His mouth twisted. "I impressed him. He told my father I had a gift for logistics. My father said—" He stopped. Swallowed. "My father said he was proud of me. And I've spent six years wondering whether that memory is real or whether the Wolf left it in me like a bone in a fish, something sharp to choke on later."

"Quentin."

"Hm?"

"When we get your father's memories back — the true ones, from the hoard — you'll be able to tell. Real memories have weight. Fakes are light as paper." She squeezed his hand once and let go. "Yours will hold."

He looked at her then with an expression she decided, firmly, not to catalog. Some things you didn't steal, even by looking.

"The third bell," he said. "Come on. And Paloma?"

"Mm?"

"Don't touch anything here. Don't lift anything. In this city, if you take a memory, the stone feels it. The whole city is one big—" he gestured vaguely at the air, "—nerve. We go in quiet, we go in hollow, we come out the same."

"Quentin Ashvale Thorne," she said solemnly, "I have never gone anywhere hollow in my life."

"I know," he said. "That's what frightens me."

* * *

*Quentin*

The postern gate was exactly where nine-year-old Quentin had left it in his head, which was either a comfort or an indictment of how little the palace changed when the thing ruling it never slept and never aged.

Third bell rang across the water. The guards — two of them, young, bored, their smiles already wearing thin at the edges the way new arrivals' smiles did — made their rotation toward the wine stall some enterprising vendor had set up within view of their own post. Quentin counted to forty, slipped from the reeds, and had the gate's inner bolt drawn before Paloma finished exhaling.

"You're enjoying this," she accused softly.

"I'm *competent* at this. There's a difference." Though privately, yes — there was a spark of something in his chest that might, generously, be called enjoyment. Six years of running, of hiding, of hearing the Wolf's voice curl through his skull like smoke under a door, and tonight he was sneaking back into his own home through a door he'd memorized as a child, with a memory-thief at his shoulder and a plan that was mostly audacity. If this was what losing felt like, it was oddly warm.

The servant corridors smelled of tallow and bread — real bread, palace bread, because the Wolf fed its household well. Happy staff, fewer questions. They moved through passages Quentin's feet remembered better than his mind did, turning left where his body turned left before his thoughts caught up, and twice he stopped, disoriented, because his legs knew a shortcut his stolen-away childhood no longer contained.

"This way," his feet said, and went.

The corridor opened onto the gallery above the great hall, and below them, the court of Vhalderan gathered for the festival feast.

Quentin gripped the balustrade.

He had prepared himself for the Wolf. He had prepared himself for the throne, for the crown, for the thing wearing his father's face. What he had not prepared himself for was Lord Aldery — old Aldery, who had taught him chess and lost to him on purpose and pretended Quentin never noticed — standing below among the courtiers, laughing at some jest, his face smooth and pleasant and utterly, catastrophically *empty*.

"He was my friend," Quentin breathed. "He was seventy years old and he cheated at cards and he once hid a kitten in his hat for a whole council session because I was sad, and now he—" His voice failed.

Below, Aldery turned his head, and for one moment — one flicker, like a candle guttering before it dies — the old man's eyes drifted up toward the gallery. Toward Quentin. Something moved behind them, far away, a figure walking on a distant shore.

Then it passed, and Aldery smiled his festival smile, and raised his glass to the throne.

On the dais sat the king.

Even from above, even hooded, even knowing, Quentin's heart lurched with love before it lurched with horror. That was the unforgivable engineering of it — the Wolf wore his father *well*. The broad shoulders. The laugh lines. The way he leaned forward on his elbows when he listened, exactly the way Father used to. Only the eyes were wrong, and only if you knew to look: they were patient the way winter is patient.

"My father is in there somewhere," Quentin whispered. "The memories we're going to gather — they're *him*. All of him. Scattered, but him."

"Then we'd better start collecting," Paloma murmured. "Where does the Wolf keep the tithe-stone's harvest? There has to be a storehouse. No thief leaves coin lying about."

"You would know."

"I would, actually. Professionally."

He almost smiled. Then the doors of the hall boomed open, and the procession began — the ribbons, thousands of them, borne in on silver trays, each one bearing a name in someone's loving hand. Names of mothers. Names of children. Names of friends. The whole city's love, harvested and carried in glittering armfuls toward the great hearth, where at midnight they would burn, and the smoke would rise, and the Wolf would breathe it in.

And beside the throne, holding the first tray, dressed in white, smiling with empty eyes —

Quentin's blood went to ice.

The chamberlain. The man who had carried *him*, as a baby, through these halls. Who now turned his face up toward the gallery — deliberately, precisely, unmistakably — and smiled.

And mouthed, silently, in the hushed language of the half-hollowed:

*"He sees you."*

The bells began to ring. Midnight was coming, and somewhere below, the fire was already lit.
