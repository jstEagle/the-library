## 14. Court of Hollow Faces

The palace of Thornfell had always smelled of beeswax and lilies, and it still did, which was somehow the worst part.

Quentin stood in the shadow of the east colonnade — his colonnade; he had hidden in its alcoves from tutors at seven, kissed a kitchen girl behind the third pillar at sixteen, been told of his father's death beneath its arches at nineteen — and breathed in the smell of home like a man testing whether a wound had closed. It hadn't. The lilies went straight through him like a blade through wet paper.

Beside him, Paloma was fidgeting with the borrowed festival cloak, pulling its hood this way and that as though she could rearrange it into something less conspicuous.

"This is absurd," she muttered. "Everyone's wearing masks anyway. Why do I need a hood?"

"Because you keep making faces."

"I do not make *faces*."

"You are making one right now. That one. The one that says *I have never attended a festival in my life and I intend to judge everyone here.*"

"That is not a face, that is my face."

He almost laughed. In the last week he had learned that Paloma Thackeray under pressure was like a kettle: she built up steam, and if you didn't let her vent it in small sasses, she'd whistle at the worst possible moment. He lifted the hood for her himself, tucking a strand of dark hair out of her eyes, and felt her go briefly, uncharacteristically still under his hands.

"Rules," he said quietly. "We walk slow. We smile at nothing. And whatever happens — however badly your chest hurts when you see what they've done to this place — we do not stop moving. Agreed?"

"Agreed." She paused. "Why would my chest hurt? It's your house."

"Exactly," said Quentin, and stepped out into the light.

---

The Festival of Forgetting filled the great courtyard like wine filling a cracked cup.

Lanterns hung from every balcony, white ones, unmarked — Quentin remembered when they'd been gold and scarlet, stamped with the sun-crown of his father's line. Now they burned blank. Musicians played on the terrace, but the music was wrong: long, droning notes with no melody, the kind of sound a lute makes when the player has forgotten what songs are *for*. And everywhere, everywhere, the people.

Hundreds of them. Dancing without touching. Laughing at nothing. Smiling with eyes like windows of an empty house.

"Saints," Paloma whispered. "They look — "

"I know."

"They look like me," she said, before she could stop herself, and then pressed her lips together as though she could push the words back in.

Quentin glanced at her. She stared straight ahead, jaw tight, and he understood — not everything, because there were whole rooms in Paloma Thackeray that stayed locked even now, but enough. Enough to know that watching a crowd of hollowed people celebrate their own emptiness must feel, to her, like looking into a mirror maze.

"They chose it," he said softly, guiding her toward the servants' door he knew better than the front gate. "That's the horror of it. The festival started as a tax on grief, centuries ago — one night a year where you set down your sorrows. But the Wolf extended it. Every year a little longer. Every year the memories come back a little dimmer. Until people began *paying* to forget, because forgetting feels like rest, and the Wolf made sure their lives gave them very little else."

"And now?"

"Now they dance on the night their children's names slip away from them. And they call it peace."

---

Inside, the palace was worse, because inside, Quentin knew where everything was.

His feet knew it. That was the treachery of coming home: the body remembers what the world has erased. His boots turned left at the second corridor without his permission. His hand reached for the torch bracket at exactly the height he'd have reached at ten years old, running from a scolding. He passed the tapestry of the Hunt of Aldous — moth-eaten now, untended, no one left who cared about Aldous or his stupid boar — and his throat closed around the memory of hiding behind it with his sister while the whole household staged a mock search for them, calling their names with theatrical despair until they burst out giggling.

*Maren.*

He hadn't thought of Maren in three corridors. He stopped walking.

Paloma bumped into his back. "What? What is it?"

"My sister." The name came out of him like water from a cracked jug. "There used to be a portrait of her at the end of this hall. She'd be — saints, she'd be twenty-six now. Do you think she's — "

"Hey." Paloma caught his sleeve. "Hey. Breathe. If she's alive, we find her. If she forgot you—" Her voice gentled in a way he was learning meant she was being careful with him, and hated doing it. "Then we remind her. That's the trade, isn't it? I steal, you remember. Between us we're nearly a whole person."

It should have been funny. It was a little funny. But mostly it landed somewhere deep and warm, and Quentin had to look away from her for a moment and pretend to study the moth-eaten boar.

"Come on," he managed. "The throne room's through the gallery. If we can reach the royal wing before midnight, we can get to the vaults beneath — that's where the crown keeps what it takes."

"What does it take, exactly? Besides everything."

"Memory has weight," Quentin said. "Real weight. The Wolf isn't just wearing my father's face — it's *maintaining* it. A shape that big needs feeding every day. The eaters bring it vials from the provinces, and the vaults store the overflow. A kingdom's worth of stolen lives, stacked in jars under my nursery."

Paloma made a low whistle. "And you want to break into that."

"I grew up there. I know which floorboard squeaks."

---

The gallery was where it happened.

They had nearly crossed it — forty yards of marble and portraits, Quentin counting doors, Paloma trailing her fingers along the wainscoting with the unconscious greed of a thief cataloguing anything portable — when a voice rang out across the marble, bright and delighted:

"Milo!"

Quentin froze.

An old man was hurrying toward them, silver-haired, robed in chamberlain's grey, arms open wide. Quentin knew the stride before he knew the face — that rolling sailor's gait, learned aboard his father's ships and never lost. *Chamberlain Odo,* who had taught him chess and cheated cheerfully at it. Who had carried him, feverish, through three nights of the red sickness. Who had wept at his father's funeral so hard the king's own mourners had to steady him.

Odo reached them and seized Quentin's shoulders with both hands, beaming up into his face.

"Oh, thank the saints. Thank the *saints*, boy. You've no idea how long I've been looking — the whole west wing and nobody sent — " He turned, still gripping Quentin, beckoning urgently back down the gallery. "Over here! He's here! Come and see!"

Three more figures detached themselves from the festival crowd and hurried over: a broad woman in a guard's half-armor, a young man in clerk's blue, an elderly lady with ink-stained fingers. Quentin knew every single one of them. Captain Brava, who had let him hold her sword once when he was six and sworn him to secrecy. Tam, the archivist's son, whom he'd taught to skip stones off the millpond. Lady Vesha, who had read him histories until he fell asleep against her shoulder.

His heart was hammering so hard he could barely hear them. They know me, he thought, wild and giddy. The Wolf took the court, took the city, took the *faces*, but these four — his people — some ember survived —

"He came!" Odo was saying, misty-eyed, shaking him gently by the shoulders. "I told you he'd come. Didn't I say?"

"You said," agreed Brava gravely.

"Every year I say it," Odo went on, turning back to Quentin with shining eyes, "and every year, on the festival night, you come home. Oh, you've grown, lad. You've grown so tall. Are you hungry? You look thin — Brava, fetch him something from the feast, he looks half-starved —"

Quentin's joy curdled, slowly, in real time, the way milk turns.

"...Milo?" he said carefully.

"Milo," Odo confirmed warmly, patting his cheek. "Our Milo. Every year, the same. You wander off, and every year on festival night you find your way back to us, and we start all over again." His eyes were wet and happy and utterly, catastrophically empty of recognition. "You mustn't wander so, boy. You'll worry your mother."

"I — " Quentin's voice cracked. He steadied it. "I'm sorry. I'll try not to."

"That's a good lad." Odo beamed and turned to the others. "You see? I told you he remembers the way home, even if he forgets everything else. Some things stick." He tapped his own temple sagely. "Deep things. Heart-things."

*Heart-things.* Quentin stood very still while the word went through him.

They thought he was Milo. Whoever Milo was — a lost servant's child, a ward, some stray the household had adopted and half-forgotten — they greeted his annual return with the fond, worn tenderness of a ritual. They loved him. That was the unbearable thing. The love was real; he could feel it pouring off old Odo in waves, could hear it in Brava's gruff "good lad," could see it in Lady Vesha's crinkled eyes. The Wolf had eaten their memories of *him* — Prince Quentin, heir of Thornfell, the boy whose bedtime stories Vesha read, whose treason Odo would have died to prevent —

—and left the love itself behind, orphaned, attached to a stranger.

Exile, Quentin thought, is not leaving home. Exile is standing in your own gallery, held by your own nurse's hands, and being called by someone else's name.

Beside him, he heard Paloma make a small sound. He didn't look at her. If he looked at her he was going to come apart right there between the moth-eaten boar and the empty portrait niche where Maren used to hang.

"Hungry?" Odo repeated anxiously. "You do look thin. Vesha, hasn't he grown?"

"He's grown," Vesha agreed, smiling her ink-stained smile. "Though he gets that look again. See? There it is. That faraway look, like he's listening to something none of us can hear."

"He's always had that look," said Tam the clerk, laughing. "Even as a babe."

*I have,* Quentin thought. *You're right. I've always had it. You used to say it meant I'd be a dreamer, a scholar-king. You said it with pride.*

Aloud, he said: "I'm sorry. I — can't stay tonight. Errands. But I'll come back."

"You always say that too," Odo chuckled, releasing his shoulders at last. "Off you go, then. Mind the crowds. And Milo—" He caught Quentin's hand, squeezed it once, hard, with those old ship-hardened fingers. "Whoever you're listening to. Tell them we're waiting."

Quentin walked away before his face could fail him entirely.

---

Paloma found him two corridors later, sitting on the floor of an alcove with his knees drawn up like a child, which was fitting, because that was roughly what he felt like.

She didn't say anything at first. She just slid down the wall next to him, cloak pooling, and sat shoulder-to-shoulder in the dark, and let the distant drone of the festival wash over them both.

"Milo," she said eventually.

"Don't."

"I'm only asking—"

"There was no Milo." He heard his own voice come out flat and strange. "Or — there probably was, once. Some child they lost. The Wolf leaves the shape of a feeling when it eats the memory inside it. So their love for me had nowhere to go, and it — pooled. Found the nearest hole and poured itself in." He laughed, and it was an awful sound. "I'm the nearest hole, Paloma. My father's chamberlain pats my cheek and calls me by a dead child's name and tells me I've grown so tall, and it's the kindest anyone's spoken to me in five years, and none of it is for me."

Paloma was quiet a moment. Then she said, "Vesha."

"What?"

"The old lady. With the ink. She said you had a faraway look, 'even as a babe.'" Paloma turned her head; in the lantern-light through the alcove grille her eyes were very sharp and very soft at once. "A babe, Quentin. She remembers you *as a babe*. Whatever else got eaten — the titles, the treason, the prince-shaped parts — she kept something. Maybe it's small. Maybe it's just a look, and a feeling, and a stone-skipping afternoon. But it stuck, the way Odo said. Heart-things stick."

"You heard that?"

"I hear everything. Occupational hazard." She nudged his knee with hers. "So here's what I think, and I think it as a professional. Memories aren't gone just because they're misplaced. I've spent nine years lifting them out of people's heads, and I'm telling you: they leave tracks. Grief leaves tracks. Love especially. It's the deepest-cut thing there is — harder to eat than anything. So if we win this — if we get the vaults open and pour it all back — you won't have to reintroduce yourself to any of them. Not properly. The track's already there. We just follow it home."

Quentin looked at her for a long moment.

"That's the most hopeful thing anyone's said to me since my father died," he said finally, "and you delivered it like a market haggler."

"I contain multitudes."

"You contain a criminal's optimism, is what you contain."

"Same thing." She scrambled up, brushing off her cloak, and offered him a hand. "Come on, Your Highness. Vault's not going to rob itself, and I believe you promised me a squeaky floorboard."

He took her hand and let her pull him up — and that was when the bells changed.

All across the palace, the festival bells, which had been ringing their slow blank chimes since sundown, shifted key. Dropped lower. Went minor. Quentin felt the note in his back teeth, in the hollow place behind his eyes where the Wolf had sunk its hooks into him years ago, and beside him Paloma went rigid.

"What is it?" she whispered. "What's happening?"

"It knows we're here." His mouth had gone dry. "Midnight bell. It counts its guests. And it's just realized—" He pressed a palm hard against his temple, against the cold whisper rising there like water in a cellar. "—that one of them is *mine.*"

Far down the corridor, in the direction of the throne room, every lantern guttered at once.

And out of the darkness, unhurried, came the sound of claws on marble.
