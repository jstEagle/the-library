## 15. The Wolf's Offer

The palace gave them rooms.

That was the first wrong thing, Paloma decided. Palaces did not give smugglers rooms. Palaces threw smugglers into cells, or out of cities, or — in the case of one memorable colleague named Ferro Dunnet — off of bridges. They did not assign her a suite with a view of the festival lanterns, a fire already lit, and a tray of candied plums arranged in a pattern that suggested someone had thought carefully about what a hungry thief might like.

"They know things about us," she said, when Quentin had shut the door behind them. She said it the way she'd say *the floor is lava* or *that dog bites* — a plain statement of weather.

"Everyone knows things about us now." Quentin stood at the window with his back to the room, watching the festival below. The city had been celebrating for three days. The Festival of Forgetting, they called it here — though officially it was the Feast of Renewal, because no kingdom likes to admit it has built a holiday around amnesia. Lanterns floated up from every quarter like slow orange stars. Somewhere a band played something in seven-eight time that made the teeth ache faintly, the way all music made by hollow men does. "It comes with being hunted."

"This isn't hunting." Paloma lifted a candied plum, sniffed it, set it back down. "Hunting is when they chase you. This is when they set your favorite meal on a table and leave the door open." She turned the plum over once more before abandoning it entirely. "They even got the plums right. I haven't eaten these since — "

She stopped.

She had been about to say *since I was small*, which was impossible, because she had no memory of being small, only a shape where the memory should have been, the way an amputee feels a hand. But her mouth had known. Her mouth remembered the taste of them the way her mouth remembered everything, and for a moment she stood very still in the warm lamplight, listening to that phantom sweetness.

Quentin turned from the window. He looked at her the way he'd been looking at her more and more lately — as if she were a page he was trying to read by failing light. "Since when?"

"I don't know," she said honestly. "That's rather the point."

* * *

The invitation came at moonset.

Not a summons — invitations were for people whose attendance was uncertain, and whatever the Wolf-King was, he was not uncertain about her. It arrived instead as a change in the air of the room, a pressure behind the ears like diving into deep water, and then a servant who had no smell. Paloma noticed this last thing immediately, because she noticed everything, and because a person without smell is a person without memories worth taking, and she had spent enough time among memory-eaters to know what that meant. The crown didn't just employ its eaters. It emptied them first. Made them into cups that could carry anything without spilling their own flavor into it.

"The King will receive the lady Thackeray at the hour of the wolf," the cup said. "Alone."

"She doesn't go alone," Quentin said, from his chair by the fire, where he had not slept and would not.

The servant did not look at him. That too was deliberate; Paloma had watched courtiers do it to Quentin in the corridors for two days, this careful looking-through, as if the eye sliding off him were oil on wet stone. "The King will receive the lady Thackeray," the cup repeated, "at the hour of the wolf."

"And if she declines?"

"The King will be disappointed." A pause, precisely measured, the length of a knife laid flat on a table. "The King dislikes disappointment."

When the door had closed, Quentin said, "Don't go."

Paloma looked at him. Firelight did strange kindnesses to his face, filling in some of the hollows the Wolf had left there, and for a moment he looked almost like what he must have been before — a boy in a bright palace learning to fence badly and love poetry. Then the light shifted and the hollowness came back, and beneath it she could see the Wolf's shape wearing him from the inside, patient as rot.

"It knows my name," she said. "It knew my name before I did. It sent me coin with its own sigil on it. It put a ledger in a vault a hundred years ago with tonight's date written in it." She shrugged, and hated how light the shrug came out, how practiced, the shrug of a woman pretending nothing touches her. "I'm going. Not because it commanded. Because I want to see its face when it lies to me."

"And if it doesn't lie?"

"Then I want to see its face anyway." She pulled on her coat, checked her vials out of habit — empty, all of them, since the chapel hoard; she kept carrying them anyway, the way a sailor carries rope. "Stay here. Lock the door. If I'm not back by dawn—"

"What?"

"Burn the ledger," she said. "And don't let them dress you for anything."

* * *

The throne room at the hour of the wolf was lit by a thousand candles and empty of everyone but the king.

No guards. No courtiers. No ministers dozing upright against pillars. Just the long hall, the candles, the banners of Thornfell hanging motionless in air that did not stir them, and at the far end, on a throne that had held four generations of true kings, a man rising to meet her.

He wore the face she had seen on coins and wanted posters and festival effigies: broad, weathered, kind at the corners, a father's face, a shepherd's face. He wore the crown loosely, the way a man wears a hat he has owned so long it has become part of his skull. And he smiled at her across forty feet of marble with an expression of such perfect, warm recognition that her whole body flinched before her mind caught up.

Because she knew that smile.

Not from him. From mirrors. From her own face, on the rare occasions she let herself smile at it. It was the smile of someone performing warmth for an audience, and performing it well, and knowing exactly how well, and finding that knowledge comfortable.

"Paloma Thackeray," the Wolf said. Its voice was the king's voice, deep and easy, a voice built for telling stories to children. "At last. Do you know, I've read about you for a very long time."

"You've read about me for a hundred years," she said. "You wrote the reading list."

"I wrote a prophecy." It descended the dais steps unhurried, robes whispering, and stopped a respectful distance away — closer than any king should stand to a thief, farther than a predator needs to stand to prey. It understood distances. It understood that she would notice if it closed them too fast. Everything it did, it did knowing she would notice, and this was somehow worse than if it had simply lunged. "Your grandmother wrote the prophecy. I merely... failed to stop it. In my defense, I was somewhat occupied at the time, being bound under a mountain by a woman who loved books more than her own skin." It tilted the king's head. "A family trait, I'm told."

"You're told nothing. You eat tellers. That's the whole of your diet."

"Sharp." It sounded delighted. The king's eyes crinkled. Somewhere in the candles, the flames leaned toward it, the way flowers lean at sun. "Yes. This is why I've wanted to meet you. Do you know how tedious the court is? A hundred years of men who flinch and women who simper and everyone forgetting, on schedule, in the proper amounts. And then in the ledger I find a girl who steals memories for coin and keeps none of them, who navigates roads by griefs she won't admit to owning, who—" it paused, savoring, "—who is standing in front of me right now counting the exits. There are nine, by the way. You've missed the one under the altar. Your grandmother found that one too."

Paloma said nothing. Her heart was going hard and fast and she kept her breathing even through craft, through the same discipline that let her lift a memory from a merchant's head while complimenting his horse. But she was aware, suddenly and completely, of how alone she was in the largest room she had ever stood in, and of the fact that the largest room she had ever stood in belonged to the thing that had eaten a kingdom's memory one noble at a time.

"You're wondering what I want," the Wolf said.

"I assume you want Quentin. Everyone wants Quentin. You've wanted Quentin since he was born, I imagine. Or since you ate his father. Which was it? Do you remember? Oh—" she smiled her own smile, thin as wire, "—of course you don't. You ate that part too."

Something moved behind the king's face. Not anger. Hunger acknowledging a good meal exists. "There she is," it murmured. "Yes. I want the boy. Half of him is already mine; you've felt it, haven't you, when you touch his mind? The rooms where the lights are out? I could finish him from here, some nights. I choose not to. Do you know why?" It began to walk, slowly, along the row of candles, and she turned with it, keeping her side to it the way you keep your side to a strange dog. "Because unfinished things are useful. An heir who remembers just enough to suffer is a lure. Every loyalist, every romantic, every fool who loves the old dynasty comes creeping out of the woodwork to follow him — and I feed on them as they arrive. He is my fishing line, Paloma Thackeray. But you..." It stopped. Turned. Looked at her with the king's kind eyes. "You are my net."

"No."

"I haven't offered yet."

"You were going to offer me my childhood."

The silence that followed was the first honest silence of the evening. She watched it land. She watched the Wolf recalibrate — quickly, smoothly, but she saw it, saw the moment her guess struck home, and she held onto that sight like a coin gripped in a fist, because it meant she could still surprise the thing that had surprised everyone.

"Yes," it said finally, softly. "I was going to offer you your childhood."

"It's gone." She kept her voice level. Kept it thief-level, merchant-level, the voice she used over bargains where the other party thought they held the better cards. "Erased. I took it out myself, years ago. Whatever vault you keep it in—"

"There is no vault." The Wolf spread the king's hands, gentle, reasonable. "Memory isn't stored, child. It's eaten, or it's carried. Yours was carried — out of your head and down the mountain and into the keeping of the shrine-stone, where your grandmother bound all the things she needed kept. I have sat on that mountain for a hundred years with my teeth in the stone, and I have tasted everything in it. Every grief she locked away. Every name. Including a little girl's whole childhood, folded small and labeled in a bookkeeper's hand." It stepped closer. One step. She let it. "I can open the stone. I can pour those years back into you the way water goes back into a dry bed. Every birthday. Every scraped knee. Every night your mother sang until your grandmother decided singing was dangerous and took that from you too." Another step. "I can make you *whole*, Paloma. Isn't that what you've wanted? Isn't that the hunger under all that competence? You steal connection because you can't remember having any. I can sell it back to you."

The terrible thing — the thing she would think about later, in the dark, turning it over like a stone with something alive under it — was that her body believed him. Her chest ached with wanting so sudden and specific that she nearly staggered. She could almost feel it, the missing years arriving, a mother's arms assembling themselves out of nothing, a kitchen, a song, a small safe self who had never yet learned to steal—

"That's not my mother," she said quietly. "That's your bait."

"It's your memory."

"It's my memory *shaped like a hook*." She met the king's eyes, and let it see her steady, and spent everything she had to be steady. "You've done your homework. You know I'm empty. You know I'd trade anything to be full. So here's what your ledger-writers missed, monster: I've been full exactly once in my life, and it wasn't when I had memories. It was in a chapel cellar with a hollowed-out prince, sharing bread with smugglers who laugh at bad jokes, and I didn't own a single second of it. It was given. Freely. By people who'll remember me whether or not I remember myself." Her hands were shaking now, and she let them. Let it see that too. "So no. Keep my childhood. It fits you better anyway. You've always preferred eating things to earning them."

For a long moment the Wolf-king simply looked at her, wearing a dead man's face, and the candle flames leaned, and leaned.

"You misunderstand the transaction," it said at last, and the warmth drained out of the king's voice like water out of a cracked bowl, leaving something old underneath, something with winter in it. "This was courtesy. Courtesy between professionals. I offered because I prefer willing nets." The candles guttered, all together, all down the hall. "But the trap does not require your willingness, Paloma Thackeray. Only your presence. Ask your grandmother — oh. You can't. She's in the stone, with the rest of what I keep."

It turned and walked back toward the throne, and the doors opened behind her, unbidden, and cold corridor air came in smelling of smoke.

"Oh, and thief." It settled onto the throne, arranging the king's robes, becoming again the beloved father-face of the coins. "Tell the boy his father says hello. He'll want to know if it's really him. He'll ask you whether the voice is right. Be kind — lie to him or don't, but decide quickly. You have until the festival ends. After that, my hunters stop following you." The king smiled the mirror-smile. "They'll simply already be wherever you're going."

* * *

She walked out through corridors full of sleeping servants standing up, and past guards who did not see her because they had been instructed not to have eyes tonight, and the strangeness of it barely touched her, because her mind was running the road ahead, the mountain, the shrine-stone, the ledger, the arithmetic of traps.

Quentin was awake when she came in. Of course he was. He took one look at her face and reached for his boots.

"It offered me my childhood," she said. "In exchange for you."

He went still, kneeling there, one boot half on. Then he finished putting it on, calmly, and stood. "What did you say?"

"No."

"Just like that?"

"Just like that."

He looked at her for a long moment, and something in his hollow face eased — not healed, nothing would heal that for a while, but eased, the way a house eases when a window is finally opened after years. "Why?" he asked. Not doubting. Wanting to hear it.

"Because I've decided I'm not a hook," Paloma said. "Whatever else I am. And because—" she paused, and found she meant it, and said it plainly, which for her was the rarest form of courage, "—because you still have rooms with lights on, Quentin, and I've gotten fond of the furniture."

He laughed — a short, rusty sound, like a gate opening after a long winter — and started packing.

Outside, the festival lanterns rose over the city, hundreds upon hundreds, drifting toward the mountains. And Paloma, buckling her coat, understood at last what she was looking at: not celebration. Release papers. The Wolf was letting the whole city forget the week, forget the strangers, forget the girl with the thief's eyes — clearing its plate, tidying its den.

The hunt was over because the hunt was unnecessary now.

They were already expected at the mountain.
