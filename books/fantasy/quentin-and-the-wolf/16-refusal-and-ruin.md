## 16. Refusal and Ruin

The throne room smelled of lilies and cold iron, which Quentin thought was a very accurate perfume for the Wolf: something beautiful draped over something that had been sharpened.

He stood at the base of the dais steps with Paloma's wrist caught in his hand. He hadn't planned to grab her. His body had simply decided, the way a body decides to catch itself falling down stairs — some old instinct older than exile, older than the hollowed-out places in his head where his father's face used to live.

Above them, on the throne that had been his grandfather's and before that his grandmother's and before that carved from a single piece of white mountain oak by men who had believed crowns should be heavy enough to feel, the king smiled.

It was his father's smile. That was the cruelest part. The Wolf had worn the man so long that it had learned all the small mechanics of him — the way his left eyebrow lifted a half-degree higher than the right when he was amused, the crease at the corner of his mouth from years of laughing at his wife's terrible puns. Every detail correct. Every detail a theft.

"You're doing the thing," Paloma murmured out of the side of her mouth.

"What thing?"

"The thing where you stare at it like staring might make it apologize."

"It's wearing my father's face, Paloma. Staring is *restrained*."

"Fair." Her fingers flexed inside his grip. "Are you going to let go of me, or are we doing this?"

"I haven't decided."

The king rose. It moved the way the real King Aldous had never moved — too smooth, like water remembering what a body looked like. The courtiers along the walls bowed as one, their faces smooth as eggs, and Quentin felt the familiar sick lurch of walking through a room full of people who had once known his name.

*Prince Quentin,* they'd called him. *Little Q,* his mother had said, because royal titles were no good for tickling. *That Ishikawa boy* — no, that was wrong, that was another life bleeding through the holes in him, he needed to stop doing that—

"Thief," the king said warmly, and Quentin realized with a jolt that it was speaking not to him but to Paloma. "You've brought me exactly what I asked for. I confess I expected more of a struggle. The prophecy promised me a woman of considerable cunning."

"The ledger oversold me," Paloma said. Quentin could hear the flat merchant-calm she put over fear; he'd learned its texture on the road. "Happens with all my references."

"And you," the king said, turning those borrowed eyes — his mother's hazel, they had been, God, *his mother's* — to Quentin. "Quentin. My boy."

"Don't." The word came out cracked. "Don't call me that. You're not—"

"I am everything he was." The king descended one step. Two. The courtiers didn't breathe. "I have his memories, every one I didn't eat. His coronation. His wedding night. The way he wept when you were born — do you want that one? I keep it close. It's a good one. A strong man crying over seven pounds of furious infant. I can give it back to you, boy. All of them. Everything your mind has lost since you ran — I hold it here, whole, unspoiled."

One step closer. Close enough now that Quentin could smell him: cedar and snow and underneath, faintly, like meat gone off behind a spice wall, the old dark smell of Thornfell.

"All you have to do," the king said gently, "is hand me the thief."

* * *

Here is the arithmetic the Wolf did not know it was doing:

It had counted Quentin's losses. It knew about the childhood name torn away at the ford. It knew about the mother's face, gone soft-focus, then gone entirely. It knew he was maybe a third of a person walking around in an exiled prince's coat, and it assumed — reasonably, the way predators assume — that a starving man will trade anything for a meal.

What it had failed to count was Paloma Thackeray's thumb, hooked into his belt loop, pressing once. Their signal. *Steady.* She'd invented it in chapter — in week three of their travels, crossing the Sallow Downs, when he'd frozen at a scarecrow that looked too much like a gibbeted cousin of his nurse's husband. She'd pressed his hip once and said, "Still here. Keep walking." And he had.

So when the Wolf offered him back his dead father's love at the price of one thief, Quentin felt the press of the thumb, imaginary or not, and did the other arithmetic instead.

"No," he said.

Paloma went very still beside him.

"I don't want them back from *you*." His voice steadied as it went, which surprised him. Grief did that sometimes; it came out level or it came out shattered, and there was no choosing. "Those memories aren't yours to give. They're stolen goods. And I know a little something about stolen goods now — I travel with an expert." He turned his head. Paloma was watching him with an expression he'd never seen on her before, something open and unarmed. He made himself look back at the king. "My father cried when I was born. I believe it without your help. My mother sang off-key and pretended it was on purpose. I believe that too. You don't get to be the vault of my family, you great shagging *maggot in a crown*. You get to be what you actually are."

The courtiers gasped — a rehearsed sound, thirty people inhaling in unison, and under different circumstances Quentin would have laughed.

"And what am I?" the king asked softly.

"A wolf," Quentin said, "that learned to wear suits."

For a heartbeat — one true heartbeat — the face slipped. Not into fur. Into nothing. The smile stayed but the man behind it vanished, the way a house goes strange when you realize no one has lived in it for years, and Quentin saw the throne room for what it was: a stage set, a taxidermy kingdom, a wolf den upholstered.

Then the mask returned, smoother than ever, and the king sighed like a disappointed tutor.

"Pity," it said. "I did offer mercy."

"Guards," said the Lord Chamberlain, who had taught Quentin chess and had never once let him win, and whose face now showed absolutely nothing at all.

* * *

They ran.

Of course they ran. Paloma had already mapped the exits — she always mapped the exits, she'd done it while curtsying, which was possibly the most Paloma thing Quentin had ever witnessed — and she took his sleeve and hauled him left when every instinct screamed right, because right led to the grand corridor and grand corridors lead to grand gates and grand gates have grand guards.

"Left, trust me, *left*—" and they plunged through a servant door behind a tapestry of the Founding Hunt, down a stair that smelled of onions and tallow, past a scullery boy who looked up from a sink full of dishes and — this would stay with Quentin, this small human detail amid the horror — *winked*. Someone in this palace still winked. Someone in this palace still had a face that did things.

"Roof garden," Paloma panted. "Your mother built it. There's a wall low enough on the north side—"

"How do you know about the roof garden?"

"I read everything in that vault, Quentin. Including the household accounts. Your mother spent a scandalous amount on plum trees." She took the last stair at a leap. "Also there's a door key hidden under the third pot because the queen couldn't be trusted with keys, according to a note in the margin that I feel says a lot about your family."

"That note was written by my father and it was *affectionate*—"

The door opened onto sky.

The roof garden hung above the capital like a green secret — plum trees in rows, roses run riot over trellises, a fountain shaped like a sleeping bear that Quentin had fallen into twice as a child. Or so he'd been told. The memory of it was gone; only the knowledge remained, like reading a letter about a place you'd never been. Below and beyond, the city glittered with festival lanterns, ten thousand points of light celebrating the Festival of Forgetting, the crowd below smiling their empty smiles and burning effigies of Memory herself.

And the gardens were burning.

Not all of them. One bed of ornamental grasses near the east wall, flames climbing orange against the dusk — a festival tradition, he supposed, fire eating the year's stored memories so the new year could begin blank. But fire doesn't check traditions, and the wind had carried sparks into the dry hedge along the south walk, and the dry hedge had opinions, and the roses were next.

"The wall's on the north side," Paloma said. "Opposite direction. Of course."

"Story of my life."

They ran through the burning garden anyway, because it was between them and the wall, and because stopping wasn't a thing either of their bodies understood anymore. Heat licked at Quentin's neck. A plum tree went up with a soft whump, fruit bursting in the flames like small red hearts, and he heard himself make a sound he hadn't meant to — grief for a tree he couldn't remember climbing, planted by a mother he couldn't remember seeing.

Paloma caught his arm. Didn't slow. Just held on.

"She grew these," he choked out.

"Then she'd rather they burn free than stand in that room one more minute," Paloma said fiercely. "Trust me. I'm a professional. Nothing hates captivity like something that used to be wild."

They reached the wall. Low, as advertised, moss-slick, overlooking the drop to the servants' lane. Below: darkness, laundry lines, freedom-shaped shadow. Behind: the roof door banging open, the Lord Chamberlain's voice, thin and wrong, calling *Quentin, Quentin, come home, boy* in a tone that had never once belonged to any chamberlain.

Paloma swung over first, dropped, landed badly, swore with a smuggler's fluency that would have gotten her excommunicated from any court including his. Quentin followed. The wall scraped his palms. The drop was longer than it looked. He landed in a heap beside her among somebody's drying sheets, which wrapped around him with maternal enthusiasm.

"Very dignified," Paloma wheezed.

"I'm an exiled prince. Dignity is for people with memories of having it."

She laughed — really laughed, breathless and ugly and bright — and grabbed his hand, and they ran again, down the lane, through the smoke-smell and the far-off music, past the festival crowds who looked at two running figures and smiled their empty smiles and forgot them before they'd passed.

* * *

They stopped running at the river, where the city's noise fell away and the water talked its endless grey talk. Quentin leaned on a bridge rail and shook, quietly, the way a horse shakes after a snake.

"You refused him," Paloma said at last.

"Yes."

"For me."

"For the *memories*, technically. They're evidence. Chain of custody matters in my family."

"Quentin."

He looked at the water. "He had my father's tears. He said he'd kept them safe. Do you understand what that offer was? It wasn't a bribe. It was — he found the exact hole in me and poured himself into it, warm, saying *here, this is yours, take it back.*" His hands tightened on the rail. "And it was still a lie. Because even if it was true — even if those were really his memories, really kept — taking them from that thing's mouth would have poisoned them. You can't eat off a stolen plate. Isn't that your rule?"

"My rule is I never *keep* what I steal."

"Well. Mine's going to be: never take back what was eaten. Some things you have to grieve instead." He said it and heard it land in his own chest like a key turning, and was astonished to find it didn't hurt worse than before. It hurt differently. Cleaner, somehow. "I think I just mourned my father for the first time. On a bridge. Covered in laundry."

"There are worse venues. I once watched a man grieve into a fondue."

He laughed despite everything, and she leaned her shoulder against his arm, and for a while they watched the water carry lantern-light downstream like it was smuggling it somewhere.

"We need to move tonight," she said eventually, quiet again. "It knows our faces now. Worse — it knows what I am. What I'm for. The rite needs me, and it knows it, and it'll stop being polite."

"The smuggler roads," Quentin said. "South, toward the mountains. Toward the shrine."

"Mm. Which means old contacts." Something crossed her face, brief as a fish under ice. "People I've... worked with."

"You don't sound thrilled."

"They're good people. Mostly. They owe me. Mostly." She pushed off the rail. "Come on, Your Highness. We need horses, and I know a man who trades them for memories of the sea, which sounds like a scam until you meet him."

They walked. And neither of them said the thing that both of them were thinking, which was that the Wolf had made Quentin an offer and Quentin had refused it — but the Wolf had made Paloma an offer too, back on the dais, in the language of ledgers and prophecy: *deliver him and have yourself restored.*

And she had never even glanced at the door.

Quentin noticed. He filed it away in whatever remained of himself, in the small warm room that was getting crowded lately with things worth keeping.

Behind them, up on the dark hill, the palace burned its festival fires, patient as a banked hearth. Somewhere in it, a wolf in a crown watched the city through windows and did not smile anymore.

And on the south road, at a crossroads inn with a crooked sign, a lamp burned late in a window that had once — by accident, by debt, by the small treacheries of frightened men — already been sold.
