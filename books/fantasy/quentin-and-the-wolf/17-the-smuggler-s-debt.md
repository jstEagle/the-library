## 17. The Smuggler's Debt

The rain found them an hour before the inn did.

It came down the mountain in sheets, turning the smuggler road into a brown river and Quentin into a shivering, miserable creature who kept insisting he was fine in the tone of voice people use when they are not fine at all.

"There," I said, pointing through the murk.

The Drowned Lantern sat where it had always sat, wedged into the crook of the pass like a barnacle on a ship's hull — a squat stone building with a lantern over the door that had been lit, as far as anyone knew, since before the war, before the usurper, possibly before the invention of fire. It was the kind of place that existed because the crown's maps said it couldn't, and the crown's soldiers could never quite find it, and the crown's tax collectors always got lost in the fog two miles out and went home.

I'd slept under its roof a dozen times. I'd paid for half of those with memories I'd lifted from people who wouldn't miss them, traded to the innkeep, Marta Voss, who collected other people's best days the way some women collected spoons.

"Is it safe?" Quentin asked. He asked it the way he asked everything now, like a man checking each stair before putting his weight down.

"It's safe," I said. "Marta owes me."

"What does she owe you for?"

"The memory of her wedding day." I ducked my head against the rain. "She sold the original to pay a debt, years back. Regretted it within a season. I found a copy in a merchant's hoard outside Vell. She's never charged me for a bed since."

Quentin stared at me through the wet. "You gave a woman her own wedding day back."

"I sold it to her. At a fair price."

"You gave—" he started again, and then seemed to decide this was an argument he couldn't win while hypothermic, and followed me inside.

* * *

The Drowned Lantern's common room smelled of woodsmoke, wet wool, and the particular stew Marta made from whatever the mountain offered, which tonight appeared to be turnips, more turnips, and one defiant piece of salt pork that got passed around like a relic. Half a dozen smugglers sat around the fire playing cards with the focused intensity of people who took cheating very seriously.

Marta herself stood behind the bar, a broad gray-haired woman built like the doorframe she leaned against. When she saw me, her face did something complicated — relief and worry wrestling to a draw.

"Paloma Thackeray," she said. "You look like something the river spat out."

"The river said the same about you."

That got a laugh from the card players. Marta came around the bar and gripped my shoulders, looked me over, and frowned at what she found. "You're thinner. And you've got a boy with you who looks like he's been crying."

"He has been crying," I said. "Repeatedly. It's the rain."

"It is *not* the rain," Quentin said with dignity, dripping onto her clean floor.

Marta looked at him a long moment. Something moved behind her eyes — recognition, maybe, or the ghost of it. "You've got his jaw," she said quietly. "The old king's jaw. My mother served in the palace kitchens, gods rest her. She used to point him out through the service door when he came down to steal honey cakes." A pause. "He'd be about your age now, if—"

"If the Wolf hadn't eaten him," Quentin finished. His voice was steady. His hands weren't. "Yes."

To her enormous credit, Marta didn't flinch, didn't cross herself, didn't do any of the things people did when you mentioned the thing wearing the crown. She just nodded once, the way you nod at bad weather, and said, "Upstairs room's free. Stew's on the fire. And if either of you tells anyone I was kind, I'll deny it to my grave."

"We would never," I said.

"You absolutely would," she said. "You told half the Salt Stair road about the time I cried over a turnip."

"That story improves with every telling."

"So does my denial. Go dry off."

* * *

We ate upstairs, by the little window, because Quentin had developed a horror of sitting with his back to a door and I had developed a horror of letting him sit where memory-eaters might see his face. The stew was better than it had any right to be. The rain drummed the roof like polite applause.

"Do you trust her?" Quentin asked.

"With my life, several times over." I tore my bread in half and gave him the bigger piece, which he pretended not to notice and ate anyway. "Marta's the closest thing this road has to honest. She's hidden deserters, debtors, a man who stole the bishop's horse as a joke and then couldn't figure out how to return it."

"What happened to the horse man?"

"He became a legend. There are three songs. None of them agree on the horse's name."

Quentin smiled — really smiled, rare as summer snow these days — and for a moment the hollowed-out quality in his face filled in a little, like footprints filling with rain. This was what I'd noticed about him on the road, what I hadn't let myself name: he bloomed around ordinary kindness. Court had starved him of it. Every warm word Marta threw at him, he caught like a man catching coins.

I knew the feeling. That was the trouble. I knew exactly how it felt to be handed warmth you hadn't earned and didn't know how to keep.

"Marta will get us to Thornfell," I said. "There's a drover's track behind the chapel ridge that the crown's maps don't have. Her cousin runs sheep on it. We can be at the shrine in four days."

"And then?"

"And then we see what my grandmother left us." I turned my cup in my hands. "A trap, probably. For the Wolf. Built out of me."

"You say that like it's a small thing."

"It is a small thing. It's a very small thing made of one very tired thief." I drank. Outside, the rain kept up its applause. "Sleep, Quentin. Tomorrow we walk."

* * *

I woke to silence, which on a smuggler road is the loudest sound there is.

No clatter from the kitchen. No card-players arguing downstairs. Just the rain, thinner now, and beneath it a sound I knew too well: boots. Many boots. Organized boots. Smugglers scatter; soldiers march.

I was out of bed with my knife before my mind caught up. The window overlooked the yard, and the yard was full of grey cloaks — six of them, maybe eight, moving with the unhurried confidence of men who had already won. At their center stood a tall figure in the crown's livery, and even at this distance I knew the shape of him: a memory-eater, one of the senior ones, his hood pushed back to show the pale brand on his temple where they burned away their own pasts to make room for other people's.

And beside him, wringing her apron in her big capable hands, stood Marta Voss.

My stomach dropped through the floorboards, through the cellar, through the mountain itself.

They were talking. I couldn't hear the words, but I could read the shape of them — I'd spent my whole life reading the shape of conversations through windows. The memory-eater spoke. Marta nodded. She pointed — up the valley, toward the chapel ridge, toward the drover's track, toward the route I'd told her at supper, casually, because I trusted her, because she *owed* me—

Because I had given a woman her wedding day back and called it a fair price, and she had sold my road for whatever they'd paid her, and I stood there watching it happen and felt nothing so much as *stupid*. Not heartbroken. Not yet. Heartbreak could wait its turn. First came the old familiar cold: *of course. Of course. Everyone sells. Everyone always sells. You knew this. You've known this since you were nine years old and learned that the only person who doesn't sell you is the person who hasn't met your buyer.*

Behind me, Quentin stirred. "Paloma? What—"

"Grey cloaks in the yard," I whispered. "Eight. One eater. Marta's showing them the track."

I heard him go still. Then, horribly calm: "How long do we have?"

"Minutes. Maybe less." I was already stuffing our things into my pack — the ledger, the vial, the seals with grandmother's handwriting. "Back window. There's a woodshed roof, then a drop to the goat path. It's ugly but it goes down fast."

"Paloma." His hand closed on my arm. "Are you—"

"I'm fine."

"You're doing the thing where you go flat."

"I am *always* flat. It's a feature. Window. Now."

But he didn't move, damn him. He looked at me with those half-eaten eyes and said, softly, "This isn't your fault."

"Everything on this road is my fault. I chose the shelter. I chose the contact. I chose to believe—" I stopped. Swallowed the rest of it, because the rest of it was a confession I wasn't ready to make: *I chose to believe that being owed makes you loved.*

Down in the yard, the memory-eater raised his hand, and the grey cloaks began to move toward the inn's front door, and there was no more time for confessions anyway.

"Go," I said.

We went.

* * *

The drop to the goat path was, as promised, ugly. Quentin landed badly and swore in what I suspected was courtly High Thorneish, which was somehow funnier for being obscene. I hauled him up and we ran — down the switchbacks, through the wet gorse, the inn shrinking above us until I heard, faint and terrible, the front door bang open and voices shout.

"They'll split," I panted. "Half down the main road, half—"

The horn sounded behind us. Two notes. The signal for *split.*

"—that," I finished.

We ran. The goat path became a deer path became a suggestion of a path became wet rock and prayer. Quentin, to his credit, kept pace, though his breath came ragged and I could feel the Wolf stirring in him the way you feel a storm coming — a pressure behind the eyes, a wrongness in the air. Fear fed it. I'd learned that much. So I talked while we ran, because talking was the only tool I had.

"Marta keeps goats," I gasped. "Did you see the goats? Terrible goats. One of them bit me in 1811 and I still think about it. If we survive this I'm going to find that goat and steal its happiest memory, see how it likes—"

"Paloma."

"—being a *character* in someone's—"

"*Paloma.*" He caught my sleeve, pulled us both into the shadow of an overhang, and held up a hand. Below us, on the switchback, torchlight bobbed. Three grey cloaks, moving fast, cutting the corner we'd just abandoned.

We pressed against cold stone and waited. Quentin's hand found mine in the dark. Neither of us remarked on it. Some things don't need remarking; they need holding.

The torches passed. The shouting faded up-valley, hunting the path we weren't on.

When we finally breathed again, Quentin said, quietly, "Why would she do it? You said she owed you."

"She did owe me." I laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "That's the joke, isn't it? Debts aren't love, Quentin. They're just debts. They expire. Someone offers enough coin and suddenly your wedding day is worth less than my location." I slid down the rock until I was sitting, because my legs had decided the matter without consulting me. "I've spent ten years telling myself that was fine. That keeping score was safer than keeping people. And the first time I actually—" I stopped. Started again, flatter. "The first time I trusted one of them, she sold me before the stew was cold."

Quentin sat down beside me in the mud. He didn't argue. He didn't offer comfort, which I think I'd have thrown back in his face. He just sat there, shoulder to shoulder, letting the dark hold what neither of us could.

"My father trusted his chancellor," he said at last. "Trusted him right up until the night the Wolf wore his face at dinner and my father laughed at his jokes." A long pause. "Trust gets eaten. Both of ours. But—" he turned, and in the thin moonlight his ruined face was fierce, "—you trusted her knowing the price of trust. That's not stupidity, Paloma. That's the bravest thing I've seen anyone do since I left the capital. Everyone down there has stopped trusting entirely. They smile with empty eyes and remember nothing and it's *safe*, and it's the safest way to die I know."

I wanted to tell him he was wrong. I wanted to tell him trust was a currency and I'd simply mispriced it, a merchant's error, correctable next quarter. What I said instead was nothing, because somewhere between his hand in mine and his words in the dark, something in my chest had cracked open, and everything leaking out of it hurt far too much for speech.

Above us, faint on the wind, I heard the inn's bell begin to ring — Marta's alarm bell, the one that warned the road of riders. Except no riders were coming. We were already gone.

Or.

I went very still.

"Quentin. Listen."

The bell rang on. Not the alarm pattern — three-and-one, three-and-one, the pattern every smuggler child learns means *danger, scatter.* This was different. Slow. Deliberate. Five strokes, pause, five strokes.

"That's not warning," I breathed. "Five-and-five. That's... that's the old signal. The one from before the usurper. It means 'the road is watched — take the water way.'" I stared up through the trees toward the distant inn, my mind tearing itself in half. "Nobody's used it in years. Nobody alive remembers it except—"

"Except someone who wanted to warn us," Quentin said slowly, "without the eaters knowing she was doing it."

The rain began again, soft on the leaves. Somewhere below, torches hunted a dead trail. And somewhere above, a woman who had sold our road rang a bell in a code the crown had forgotten, and I sat in the mud with both truths in my lap and could not make them fit together.

Betrayal and warning. Coin and kindness. Marta Voss, holding both in her broad hands, the way everyone holds everything eventually.

"We should hate her," I said. "It would be simpler."

"Simpler," Quentin agreed. He stood, and pulled me up, and nodded down the slope toward the black thread of the river glinting through the trees. "The water way. Which means?"

"Which means the gorge," I said, "which means rapids, which means the eaters won't follow because last winter it ate a crown patrol whole." I shouldered my pack. My legs worked again. Mostly. "And it means someone downstream owes me a favor even worse than Marta did."

"Who?"

I grinned at him, and it surprised me how easily it came, cracked chest and all. "The horse thief. Quentin. We're going to meet the man who stole the bishop's horse."

Below us, the river waited, loud and white and utterly indifferent to prophecy — and as we started down toward it, I heard, carried thin on the rain, the sound of hoofbeats on the main road behind. Too many. Too fast. Coming not along our trail, but parallel to it, as if whoever rode already knew where the water way ended.

Someone had sold the ending too.
