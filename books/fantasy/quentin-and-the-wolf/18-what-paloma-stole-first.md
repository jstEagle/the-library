## 18. What Paloma Stole First

Quentin had learned three things about Paloma Thackeray in the weeks since the ford, and he had learned them the way one learns a language — badly at first, then all at once, then with an ache of fluency that made him wonder how he'd ever spoken anything else.

The first thing: she counted everything. Coins, steps, lies told to customs men, stars visible through smoke-holes. He'd caught her murmuring tallies in her sleep once, a soft inventory of the day, as though an uncounted thing might be lost.

The second thing: she slept facing doors.

The third thing — and this was the one that kept him awake on the third night in Dree's cellar, listening to the smugglers' muffled laughter above and the drip of the cistern below — was that she never spoke of before. Not the way other people did, not even grudgingly. Everyone had a *before*. The smugglers upstairs traded their befores like currency: Dree's burned village, Wren's dead brother, the ferryman's first wife who'd run off with a puppeteer. But ask Paloma what she remembered of her childhood and she'd smile — that quick, bright, deflecting smile — and say something like *"I remember roads."*

Roads. As if she'd been poured into her boots at age nineteen and sent walking.

He hadn't thought much about it at first. There had been no time to think — hunters behind them, the ledger burning a hole in his satchel, the Wolf's whisper threading through his skull like water through a cracked wall. A person's missing childhood seemed a small mystery next to a kingdom eaten by a monster wearing his father's face.

But now they were hiding. Hiding meant waiting, and waiting meant thinking, and Quentin had always been too good at thinking for his own comfort. It was, after all, how he'd ended up half-devoured: he'd *noticed* things at court. Noticed that the king laughed a half-second too late. Noticed that the royal archivist had stopped aging. Noticed that when he brought these observations to his father, his father's eyes went briefly, terribly blank — like windows with nothing behind the glass.

So he thought about Paloma's missing before. And on the fourth night, while she slept facing the cellar door with her fingers twitching through some counted dream, he made the mistake of opening her satchel.

Not to steal. He wanted to be very clear with himself about that, lying there in the dark with the leather flap warm under his hands. He told himself it was practical — he needed to inventory their supplies, plan tomorrow's route, count the vials from the chapel hoard. Practical. Sensible. Absolutely nothing to do with the way she'd said *"I remember roads"* in that voice like a locked door.

Inside the satchel: flint, dried plums, a coil of good rope, the client's vial still sealed and humming faintly against his palm like a trapped moth. And beneath everything, wrapped in oilcloth, a slim book he had never seen her read but which she touched, sometimes, in her sleep.

It wasn't a diary. Diaries were written forward; this was written backward. He could tell by the ink — memory-ink, the gray-silver stuff that thieves used, the color of rain on slate. Each page held a single entry, and each entry was a theft, logged with the meticulousness of a merchant's ledger:

*Borrowed: a widow's wedding day. Returned: within the hour, none the worse.*

*Borrowed: a soldier's last morning at home. Held: two days. Felt guilty. Returned with interest — left him a memory of his daughter learning to walk, found fair trade in a market stall.*

Page after page. Years of them. Her rule, written out in her own hand at the front like a catechism: **Never keep what you steal.** And beneath it, smaller, almost hidden by a thumb-smudge of ink, as though she'd hoped even her future self wouldn't see it:

**Except the first.**

Quentin turned pages with hands that had begun, without his permission, to shake.

*The first.* Every thief's ledger had a first entry. Usually something small — a sweet, a coin, a neighbor's afternoon of happiness lifted on a dare. His own first theft, back when he'd been someone whose name he could barely hold onto anymore, had been a honey cake from the palace kitchens. He remembered the guilt. He remembered the taste. Both had felt enormous at the time.

Paloma's first entry was dated thirty years ago. She would have been seven.

The entry was short. It was also, unlike every other entry in the book, not a borrowing.

*Taken: mine. All of it. The house on Thackeray Lane. Mother's coughing. Father's belt. The winter we ate candle wax. The dog I loved that father — no. No, I won't write it. Taken because keeping it was killing me and forgetting it was the only door out. Taken whole. Not returned. Will not return. If you are reading this, me, remember: you took it so that you could live. Don't go looking for it. Some doors are locked from the inside for a reason.*

Quentin sat in the dark of Dree's cellar with a seven-year-old's agony in his hands, and understood, all at once and forever, why Paloma Thackeray counted everything.

She wasn't counting because she was careful. She was counting because she was *empty*, and empty vessels rattle, and she had spent thirty years filling herself with other people's borrowed sunsets and wedding days and daughters-learning-to-walk so that the hollowness where her childhood should have been wouldn't echo so loudly. She stole memories for coin, yes. But she carried them — carried them carefully, returned them gently, treated every stolen thing like it was holy — because she knew exactly, precisely, intimately what it was to have your own self taken from you.

She was the only honest thief in the world. She knew what theft cost, down to the bone.

"Oh," Quentin said softly, to no one. "Oh, Paloma."

"You're reading my book."

He nearly jumped out of his skin. She was sitting up against the far wall, knees drawn up, watching him with an expression he couldn't parse in the dark — not anger, not quite. Something older and wearier than either.

"I wasn't—" he began, and then, because lying to her had become impossible somewhere around the ford, stopped. "Yes. I was. I'm sorry."

"Which page?"

"The first one."

Something moved across her face, fast, like a bird crossing a window. Then she got up, crossed the cellar, and sat down beside him with the unhurried calm of someone deciding to look directly at a wound for the first time in thirty years.

"Well," she said. "There it is."

"Paloma—"

"Don't do the face."

"What face?"

"The face you're doing. The sad, gentle, oh-you-poor-thing face. Dree does it too, when she thinks I'm not looking. I can't abide the face." She pulled the ledger gently from his hands and looked down at the first entry, her thumb tracing the words without reading them. "I forgot I wrote *no* twice. Look at that. Little me, crossing herself out even in secret."

"How old were you?"

"Seven when I took it. Eight when I wrote this." She tapped the page. "I took the memory first and wrote the note after, once I could think straight again. Which took most of a year. Forgetting hurts, you know. Nobody tells you that part. They make it sound like mercy — *you won't feel it anymore* — but cutting yourself out of your own life is just another kind of wound. It just doesn't bleed."

"Why?" The question came out rougher than he intended. "Why take *all* of it? Surely some of it—" He faltered. He was thinking of his own childhood, or what remained of it: his mother singing off-key in the orchard, his nurse's ghost stories, the smell of the stables. Half of it torn away by a memory-eater at the ford, and the loss still woke him some nights like a hand around his throat. "Surely there was something worth keeping."

For a long moment she didn't answer. Above them, floorboards creaked; Wren was pacing again, the way he did when the crown's patrols passed too near. The cistern dripped its slow count.

"There was the dog," she said finally. "Her name was Biscuit. She was ugly and she loved me." Her voice stayed perfectly level, which somehow made it worse. "And I sat with the whole of it — all of it, the good and the terrible, mixed together the way childhood is, one gray soup — and I tried, Quentin, I swear I tried, to sort it. To keep Biscuit and lose the rest. Seven years old, sitting on my bed with my own life in my lap like a basket of laundry, trying to pick out the clean from the ruined." She laughed, short and dry. "But that's not how memory works. You can't lift one thread. Pull on Biscuit and you get the kitchen where father kicked her. Pull on mother's lullaby and you get the nights she coughed. It was one thread, all of it. One thread or none."

"So you chose none."

"So I chose none." She closed the ledger. "And I'll tell you the funny part, since you're doing the face anyway. For about ten years afterward, I thought I'd made myself *free*. Light as a bird. Nothing dragging behind me. That's what I told the other thieves, when they asked why I was so cheerful. Best decision I ever made, I'd say. Cut loose my ballast." Her thumb moved over the ledger's cover, back and forth. "Then one evening in a tavern in Marrowgate, I watched a woman across the room telling her grown daughter about the day she was born. Just — telling it. The bad bits and all. Laughing at the bad bits. And I realized I couldn't laugh at anything, because I had nothing to measure it against. I didn't know if my jokes were funny. I didn't know if my sadness was earned. I was a person built entirely out of other people's furniture." She glanced sideways at him. "You know what that's like, a little. The Wolf eats you from the edges in. I did it from the center out."

Quentin looked at her — this sharp, competent, counting, door-facing woman — and felt something rearrange itself in his chest with an almost audible click.

All this time, he had envied her. That was the shameful truth he'd never let himself examine closely: watching her work the smuggler roads, slipping past checkpoints with that easy grin, he'd thought *she is what I should have been* — unburdened, unclaimed, belonging to no throne and no prophecy. While he dragged his hollowed skull and his hunted name and his father's stolen face behind him like a sled of stones.

And she had been carrying the same weight the entire time. Just folded smaller. Just worn on the inside.

"We match," he said, before he could stop himself.

She blinked. "We what?"

"Like broken crockery." He was talking faster now, the way he did when an idea arrived too large for his mouth. "You cut out the middle of yourself. The Wolf ate the edges of me. Between us we might assemble one complete person, if we don't mind the seams showing."

To his vast relief, she laughed — a real one, startled out of her, the kind that made Wren pause mid-pace overhead. "That's the bleakest romantic sentiment I've ever heard."

"It wasn't—" Heat climbed his neck. "I didn't mean romantically. I meant structurally."

"Mm." She was smiling at the ledger now, that particular small private smile he'd started collecting without meaning to, the way she collected other people's memories — carefully, returning nothing. "Structurally. Of course."

They sat in silence awhile, shoulder to shoulder in the cold cellar, and it was the first silence he'd shared with anyone since exile that didn't itch.

"Can I ask you something else?" he said eventually.

"You can ask. Whether you get an answer depends on the question and my mood."

"When you return the memories you borrow. When you leave them better than you found them — the soldier, the daughter-learning-to-walk. Why? Your rule says return them. It doesn't say *improve* them."

She was quiet for long enough that he thought she wouldn't answer. Then she opened the ledger again, to the last written page, and turned it toward him so he could see the fresh ink — today's date, an entry only half finished.

*Borrowed: nothing. Given: a man's childhood name, held safe until he wants it back. Kept: nothing of my own. Note to self—*

The sentence stopped there. She'd clearly been stuck on it.

"That's why," she said simply. "Because I know what it is to have a piece of yourself gone. So when I'm holding someone's memory — even for an hour — I try to leave it kinder than I found it. Patched. Mended. It's the closest thing I have to..." She trailed off, frowned at the half-finished line, and handed him the pencil. "Here. You're the one who grew up with tutors. Finish the sentence."

He took the pencil. His hand hovered over the page.

*Note to self:* What? He thought of her sleeping facing the door. Counting stars. Returning widows' wedding days with interest. Building herself out of borrowed furniture, thirty years of it, patient as coral.

*Note to self,* he wrote, *you gave away your childhood to survive. That was brave. But you are allowed, someday, to stop merely surviving.*

He felt rather than saw her go still beside him.

"That's cheating," she said quietly. "That's not finishing my sentence. That's writing your own."

"Yes," Quentin agreed. "Sorry. I've been told I do that."

Above them, the pacing stopped. Footsteps crossed the floorboards — quick ones, uneven, Wren's bad knee — and then the cellar hatch banged open and Dree's voice came down the ladder, stripped of its usual rumble, tight as wire:

"Trouble. Get up here, both of you. Ferro's back from the crossroads inn — and he came back riding double with a crown courier." A pause, and Quentin heard Dree swallow. "He sold your route, boy. Sold it to the eaters. Patrol's on the mountain road by dawn."

Beside him, Paloma was already moving — ledger into satchel, satchel to shoulder, flint checked, coins counted, all of it in the space of four heartbeats, the machine of her competence engaging as smoothly as if nothing in this cellar had ever been soft.

But as she reached the ladder she paused, just once, and looked back at him. And whatever was in her face — gratitude, fear, the beginning of something neither of them had a word for yet — she didn't lock the door on it this time.

"Mountain road," she said. "Then the shrine. Grandmother's trap closes whether we're ready or not."

Quentin took up the ledger's weight in his mind — *except the first, except the first* — and followed her up into the dark.
