## 19. The Mountain That Remembers

### Quentin

The mountain had been waiting for them. Quentin was certain of it by the third day of the climb, when the road — which was not a road so much as a rumor of a road — began to behave like something with opinions.

It forked where no fork should be, then rejoined itself as if embarrassed. It grew steeper whenever Paloma complained and gentler whenever she laughed, which Quentin privately suspected was coincidence but which he chose, for his own peace of mind, to interpret as the mountain having a sense of humor.

"It's doing that on purpose," Paloma said, kicking a stone off the path. The stone rolled uphill. She watched it go with the expression of a woman recalculating her entire relationship with gravity.

"Mountains don't do things on purpose."

"This one is. Look at it. It's *smug*."

Quentin looked at it. The mountain rose ahead of them in terraces of grey granite veined with white quartz, and the quartz did look, now that she mentioned it, rather like a raised eyebrow.

They had been climbing for three days since leaving the smugglers' shelter at Hollowmere, and in that time Quentin had learned several important things about traveling with Paloma Thackeray. One: she talked more when she was frightened. Two: she counted her steps under her breath when she was very frightened. Three: she had not slept properly since the ford, since the memory-eater tore his childhood name out of him, since she had caught it — caught *him*, the missing piece of him — and carried it away inside herself like a coal cupped in bare hands.

He still didn't know what his childhood name was. He knew it existed because Paloma kept almost saying it and stopping herself, pressing her lips together like a woman holding back a sneeze. He'd stopped asking after the second day. Some doors you leave closed until you have the strength to open them without falling through the floor.

"The ledger says the shrine sits above the tree line," he said instead, consulting the battered book for perhaps the hundredth time. His grandmother-in-law's handwriting — no, not his, he corrected himself, *the old thief's*, though the correction fit worse every day — marched across the page in that slanted, impatient script: *Above the last pine, where the stone drinks light.*

"Above the last pine," Paloma repeated. "There are no pines. There hasn't been a pine for an hour. Either your grandmother was lying or we've walked past destiny and it didn't wave."

"Maybe the pines gave up." Quentin nodded at the scrubby wind-bent things clinging to the slope below them. "I can't say I blame them."

That earned him half a smile, which he hoarded the way she once hoarded memories — greedily, guiltily, knowing it wasn't really his to keep.

The truth was that Quentin was afraid. Not of the mountain, exactly, and not even of the Wolf, though the Wolf was always there at the edges of him like weather coming. He was afraid of what the ledger said came next. He'd read the pages again the night before while Paloma slept, by the blue light of a memory-vial cracked open an inch. The old woman's instructions were precise, loving, and terrible. *The rite requires a willing anchor. Let the king give what remains.* And beneath it, smaller, almost apologetic: *There is no other way. Forgive me.*

Quentin had decided, somewhere on the switchbacks below the snow line, that he forgave her. What he could not decide was how to tell Paloma, who had developed a violent allergy to the phrase *no other way* ever since the inn, when he'd offered himself as the anchor and she had called him, with great precision and considerable volume, "an arithmetic-minded idiot with a martyr complex."

She hadn't spoken to him for four hours afterward. He'd treasured those four hours too, in their way. Silence shared with Paloma was worth more than most conversations he'd ever had at court.

The path crested a shoulder of rock, and the trees — all of them, the whole ragged treeline at once — fell away below like an audience dismissed.

And there was the shrine.

### Paloma

Paloma had stolen the memory of a cathedral once. A duke's wedding; she'd lifted the groom's awe along with a purse, purely by accident, and spent a week afterward standing in doorways looking up at ceilings she couldn't afford to enter. So she knew what sacred architecture did to a person from the inside.

This was nothing like that.

The shrine wasn't built. It was *revealed* — a hollow in the mountain's crown where the granite curved inward into a dome maybe forty feet across, its surface smooth as the inside of a bell. No carvings. No altar. Just stone, pale as bone where the quartz ran through it, and the light — that was the thing, the light. The afternoon sun came down into the hollow and the stone *held* it. Held it the way wax holds a seal. Wherever the sun touched, the rock glowed faintly gold, and the glow did not fade when clouds crossed the sun, and when Paloma stepped forward the glow rippled around her feet like the skin of a pond disturbed by a pebble.

"Oh," she said. Then, because one syllable seemed insufficient: "*Oh no.*"

"What?"

"I know this place." Her mouth had gone dry. "I've never been here. But I know it. There's—" She crouched, laid her palm flat against the stone, and the ripple spread out from her hand in rings. "Quentin. It's full. The whole mountain is *full*."

He knelt beside her. She watched him understand it in stages, the way she had: first the warmth under her palm, then the faint pressure against her gift, like pressing her ear to a wall and hearing not voices but the hum of a thousand rooms behind it. Memories. Not stored in vials, not sealed in wax and paper. Soaked into the rock itself, layered over centuries, the way candle grease builds on a sill.

"People prayed here," Quentin said quietly. "For hundreds of years. And the mountain—"

"Kept them." Paloma sat back on her heels. Her hands wanted to shake, so she folded them in her lap like a well-behaved child. "Every prayer. Every grief somebody dragged up here because they couldn't carry it home again. It's all still here." She swallowed. "Do you know what I'd get for even an hour inside a hoard like this? I could buy a duchy. I could buy the *crown*. I could—"

"You're not going to steal anything," Quentin said. Not sharp. Just certain.

"No," she agreed. "That's the horrible part. I don't want to. Do you understand? Me. I'm standing in the greatest unguarded vault on the continent and my fingers are *quiet*. It feels like being introduced to someone's baby and discovering you don't want to pick its pockets."

"Genuinely horrifying," Quentin said solemnly, and she threw a pinecone at him, and for a moment the dread loosened its grip enough for them both to breathe.

But only a moment. Because the ledger was in her pack, and the ledger had brought them here, and the ledger had known tonight's date a hundred years before tonight existed.

### Quentin

They made camp just inside the shrine's mouth, where the stone curved overhead like a cupped hand and the wind lost interest in them. Quentin built a small fire from scrub juniper, and Paloma cooked the last of the Hollowmere bread toasted on a flat stone, and for a while the evening arranged itself into something almost ordinary.

Almost. Because the firelight moved on the walls of the shrine, and the walls remembered firelight too, and sometimes the glow underfoot flickered in patterns that weren't quite random — a swirl here, a burst there — like embers rising, like laughter, like the shapes people see when they close their eyes and remember.

"My mother brought me to a shrine once," Quentin said. The words surprised him on their way out. "Not this one. A little roadside thing in the eastern provinces. Before—" Before the Wolf. Before everything. "I don't remember it. That's the joke, isn't it. I remember that I went, and I don't remember going."

Paloma passed him the bread without commenting, which was its own kind of mercy. She'd gotten good at that — hearing the gaps in him and stepping around them instead of poking at them. He watched her turn her own piece of toast over and over, not eating it.

"Say it," she said finally. "Whatever you've been chewing on since the pass besides this terrible bread. Say it, because if you don't say it tonight, tomorrow will be the wrong day forever, and I refuse to do rites on top of unsaid things."

Quentin looked at the fire. "You'll be angry."

"Probably."

"You called me an arithmetic-minded idiot."

"With love. Mostly. Say it."

He said it. All of it — the ledger's instruction, the willing anchor, the king giving what remains, the *there is no other way* written in a dead woman's patient hand. He said it plainly, the way you pull a splinter, fast and straight, and then he braced for the argument.

Paloma was quiet for a long time. The shrine hummed around them, soft as a sleeping house.

"Here's what I know about traps," she said at last. "Professionally speaking. The good ones never feel like traps. They feel like doors opening. My grandmother—" she said the word carefully, testing whether it still cut, and found it mostly blunted now, worn smooth by fury into something almost like respect — "built this rite a century ago. She wrote me into it before I was born. And every step of the road since the Salt Stair has felt like walking through a house where all the doors are already open and all I'm doing is arriving." She looked up at him, firelight catching the quartz-glow in her eyes. "So when we get to the part where the ledger asks for a king, we are going to be *extremely careful* about what we agree to. Both of us. You don't get to volunteer yourself behind my back, and I don't get to spend myself behind yours. We argue everything. Agreed?"

"That's not how sacrifices work."

"That's exactly how they work when you do them right. Anything else is just waste with good lighting." She held out her hand. "Agreed?"

He took it. Her grip was warm and calloused and entirely unbudging, and he understood suddenly why smuggler roads obeyed her. "Agreed."

"Good." She released him and pointed the rest of her toast at the glowing walls. "Now sleep. Tomorrow we find whatever it is my grandmother left us, and I want us both insufferably well-rested when it turns out to be another trap."

### Paloma

She woke before dawn to the sound of the mountain clearing its throat.

There was no better word for it. A deep, grinding groan rolled through the stone beneath her bedroll, and Paloma came upright with a knife in her hand before her eyes finished opening — old habits — and found Quentin already awake, already on his feet, staring at the far wall of the shrine.

The dawn light was coming down into the hollow, and where it struck, the stone was *showing* them something.

Not glowing. Showing. Images moved in the rock the way images move in smoke: figures, small and indistinct, climbing the same path they had climbed. Pilgrims, centuries of them, ascending with bundles and candles and children on their shoulders. And at the top of each climb, the tiny stone-figures pressed their hands to the wall and — Paloma felt it rather than saw it — *gave*. Set something down. Left it in the rock like a coin left in a fountain.

"It remembers them," Quentin whispered. "All of them."

"It doesn't just remember." Paloma stood slowly. Her heart was doing something complicated. "Look at the base. There."

Where the shrine floor met the far wall, the quartz veins converged — dozens of them, running together like streams to a pool — and at their meeting point the stone bulged slightly outward, the shape of a sealed door that had never been a door. And set into it, plain as a signature, was a mark she knew better than her own.

A small, precise, looping *T*, struck through with a single line.

Her grandmother's mark. On the seals of every vial in Quentin's hidden cache. On the ledger. On the prophecy that had named her before her mother had named her.

"She was here," Paloma said. Her voice came out strange, thin, reverent against her will. "A hundred years ago she stood right here, and she—" The grinding sound came again, deeper, and the bulge in the stone shivered, and dust sifted down from the crown of the dome like the mountain exhaling. "She left something in the wall. Something the mountain is keeping for us. For *tonight*."

Quentin came to stand beside her. Close enough that she could feel him deciding not to touch her shoulder, and then touching it anyway, which had lately become a habit she had decided not to examine too closely.

"Paloma," he said. "The ledger said the rite begins at moonrise."

"I know."

"And the Wolf knows where we are. It's known since the ford. It let us walk here."

"I know that too." She watched the sun crawl higher up the wall, illuminating pilgrim after pilgrim after pilgrim, all of them climbing toward something they trusted. Her grandmother's mark gleamed at the center of it like a pupil in a stone eye. "So here's my professional assessment of our situation, heir of Thornfell, free of charge."

"Which is?"

"We didn't find the shrine." The stone ground again, louder, and the bulge in the wall split along a seam no one had ever noticed — a hairline crack, widening, breathing out air that smelled of cold and cedar and a hundred years of waiting. Paloma took a slow breath of it.

"The shrine found us. And it's opening."

Behind the crack, deep in the heart of the mountain, something that had been patient for a century began — gently, terribly — to move.
