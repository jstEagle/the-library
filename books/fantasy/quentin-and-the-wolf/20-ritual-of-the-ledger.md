## 20. Ritual of the Ledger

The shrine at the top of Thornfell Mountain was not what I expected.

I had imagined something grand — a cathedral of stone, maybe, or at the very least a proper altar with candles and a donation box, because every holy place I'd ever robbed had a donation box and robbing one had paid for three winters of my life. What we found instead was a hollow in the mountainside, walled in by standing stones taller than the inn at Millbrook, each one worn smooth as river glass and warm to the touch despite the snow on everything else.

"It's breathing," Quentin said.

"It is *not* breathing." I put my palm flat against the nearest stone anyway, because I am a hypocrite as well as a thief, and felt what he meant. A slow swell beneath the surface, like the flank of a sleeping animal. "All right. It's breathing. That's fine. Mountains breathe. Everything breathes. I once stole a memory from a woman who swore her house breathed, and she was right, it was the chimney draft, but still."

"Paloma."

"I'm fine. I'm being very calm. Look how calm I am."

He looked at me the way he'd been looking at me since the ford, since I took his mother's face into my keeping and promised him I'd carry it home. It was a look that made me want to either kiss him or pick his pocket, and the fact that I couldn't decide which was becoming a genuine problem.

We built our camp inside the ring of stones, because the wind outside could strip paint off a wagon, and then I sat down with Grandmother's ledger on my knees and did the thing I had been avoiding for two hundred miles: I read the instructions all the way through.

Not skimming. Not hunting for the parts that flattered me. Reading. Like a person who intended to follow them.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about reading your dead grandmother's handwriting when you know she was building a trap for a god: every letter becomes an accusation or an apology, and you can't tell which until you've finished the sentence.

Her script was small and precise, the hand of someone who resented wasting ink. The ledger pages for the ritual were different from the prophecy pages — less formal, more hurried, as if she'd written them late at night by candlelight with cold fingers, which, knowing where I got my habits from, she probably had.

*The unbinding requires four things,* she wrote.

*A thief who keeps nothing.* I qualified. Barely. Though lately I'd been keeping Quentin's mother's face in my hoard like a jewel I refused to sell, so even that qualification was developing cracks.

*A king's blood spilled upon the stone.* I read that line twice before I found the footnote, tucked sideways in the margin in her cramped hand: *not his body, fool girl — his remembering. Blood of the mind. A willing memory, given freely, anchors the rite. An unwilling one will simply feed the Wolf.*

*A night with no moon.* Tonight. Of course tonight. Grandmother never missed a chance to be dramatic.

*And last:* I turned the page, and there it was, the line I'd been dreading since the chapel, since the seals, since the first moment I understood that this whole road had been paved with her intentions:

*The binding must be paid for. Memory calls to memory. To close the cage, the thief must open her own — all of it, every stolen thing, poured into the stone until the mountain is full and the thief is empty. Hold nothing back. The Wolf will smell anything kept.*

I closed the ledger.

Quentin was watching me from across the little fire, turning a strip of dried venison over the flames with the concentration of a man who needed something to do with his hands. He'd gotten better at camp-craft in the weeks since the crossroads. He'd gotten worse at pretending he wasn't afraid.

"Well?" he said.

"Well," I said, "good news and bad news."

"That's usually how it goes with your grandmother."

"The good news is that the ritual works. She was certain. She ran the arithmetic twice." I hugged my knees. "The bad news is that 'a willing king's memory' means exactly what it sounds like. Someone has to give the mountain a piece of themselves freely — a true royal memory, anchored in love of the crown, whatever that means — and hold it steady while the rite pulls the Wolf loose. And the footnote says—"

"I heard you reading the footnote," Quentin said quietly. "You read aloud when you're frightened. You've done it since the vault."

"I do not—"

"*An unwilling one will simply feed the Wolf,*" he recited. "You mouthed every word."

I threw a pinecone at him. He caught it without looking, which was insufferable, and smiled, which was worse.

* * *

Here is the thing about Quentin Ashvale, heir to a throne that ate his father: he had already decided. I could see it settling over him through the evening like snowfall, quiet and total, while he fed the fire and checked the stones and arranged his bedroll with the neatness of a man putting his affairs in order.

I had seen that look before. I wore it myself, most days, under everything else. It was the look of a person doing sums.

So while he pretended to sleep, I sat up with the ledger and did my own sums, and here is what I found, buried in the margins where Grandmother hid the things she didn't want the reader to see first:

*The anchor need not be the whole man,* she wrote. *A single memory will serve, if it is deep enough and loved enough. But it must be given, not taken — and it cannot be returned. What the mountain takes as payment, the mountain keeps.*

One memory. Deep enough and loved enough.

I thought about what Quentin had left. The Wolf had taken his childhood name at the ford — I carried it now, wrapped in silk paper in the innermost pocket of my coat, next to my heart, where I kept the things that weren't mine but were mine to protect. Before that, the Wolf had spent twenty years gnawing at him from inside, eating his father's face out of his memory one bite at a time, eating his mother's voice, eating the color of the nursery curtains, eating and eating until what remained of the boy who'd been exiled was a gaunt, stubborn, dryly funny scrap of a man who caught pinecones without looking.

What did he have left that was deep enough? What did he have left that was loved enough?

And then I thought: *he'll choose the best one. Whatever's left that's brightest, he'll walk up to that stone and hand it over with both hands and make a joke about it, and I will have to stand there and watch, because the rite needs what it needs.*

I looked across the dying fire at his sleeping face — sharp bones, shadowed eyes, mouth finally slack with something like peace — and I hated my grandmother with a purity I hadn't managed since the vault.

"You knew," I whispered to her, to the ledger, to the cold air. "You knew whoever came up this mountain would have to pay. You wrote it so the thief would have to watch. Was that the lesson? Was that the trap inside the trap?"

The stones breathed around me. If Grandmother answered, she did it in creaks of cooling rock, which is the answer most dead people give.

* * *

Morning came gray and moonless-adjacent, which is to say the new moon was coming and the sky knew it. We had until dark. Maybe two nights, if the clouds were generous, but Grandmother's almanac notes were smugly specific: *the third night of the dark moon, not the second, not the fourth; the Wolf sleeps shallowest then.*

"So we wait one more day," Quentin said, "and then we do it."

"We should talk about the anchor first."

"Should we?"

"Quentin."

"Paloma." He said my name the way he always did when he was about to be stubborn — gently, like setting down something fragile. "There's nothing to talk about. I'm the only royal memory on this mountain. The rite needs a king's remembering given freely. I'm giving it. The discussion is concluded."

"The rite needs ONE memory. Deep enough and loved enough. Which means you get to choose, and I know you, and I know you'll choose—" My voice cracked, which was mortifying, so I pushed through it. "You'll choose the best thing you have left. The brightest one. And then it'll be gone forever, and you'll be standing there smiling about it because you think that's what kings do, they pay, they sacrifice, they go up on the mountain and get carved into the stonework—"

"That's generally the job description, yes."

"IT'S A TERRIBLE JOB DESCRIPTION." I was on my feet. I didn't remember standing. "Whoever wrote it should be thrown down the stairs."

"My father would have agreed with you," Quentin said, and there it was, the grief he couldn't mourn, surfacing for a second before he pressed it back down. "That's rather the point. Someone has to be the one who pays, or everyone pays instead. I've done the arithmetic, Paloma. One memory against a kingdom. Against the Wolf wearing my father's face for another century, eating children's names out of their heads in villages that will never know why their mothers forget them. It's not even a hard sum."

"It's a hard sum." I sat back down. My legs had opinions. "It's the hardest sum there is, and the fact that you can do it without crying is the saddest thing I've ever seen."

He was quiet for a while. The stones breathed. Somewhere below us, wind moved through pines like a long exhale.

"What would you give?" he asked at last. "If it were yours to give."

And that was the question, wasn't it. Because I'd been doing my own arithmetic all night, and my column was longer than his. Every stolen sunset in my hoard. Every borrowed grief, every rented joy, forty years of other people's best moments hoarded in the dark of me because I couldn't bear to keep nothing and couldn't bear to keep them honestly. And underneath all of it, the footnote's real demand: *all of it, every stolen thing, poured into the stone until the thief is empty.*

Empty. On purpose. The way I'd been empty before, once, when I was six years old and stole my own childhood out of my own head because living inside it hurt too much.

I'd survived emptiness before. That was the horrible joke of it. I was the one person alive who knew exactly what the price felt like, because I'd already paid a version of it, alone, at six, with no one to hold the ledger.

"A sunset," I said. "The one from the Salt Stair road. The merchant's. It's the most beautiful thing I've ever held." I shrugged, aiming for casual and missing by a mile. "But the rite doesn't want mine. So it doesn't matter."

"No," Quentin said softly. "It doesn't matter." And I watched him file my answer away somewhere, the way he filed everything, and I understood with sick clarity that we were both going to spend today trying to out-sacrifice each other, and that neither of us was going to win, and that Grandmother had designed it that way too.

* * *

We passed the day preparing, because preparation is what you do instead of screaming.

Grandmother's instructions were maddeningly specific about the geometry of the rite. The ledger went on the central stone, open to the ritual page, weighted with four river pebbles — *one for each season she never lived to see*, I translated bitterly. The anchor stood at the north stone. The thief walked the circle sunwise, reading the words aloud, and the words were... well.

"They're a song," Quentin said, when I read the first stanza to him for checking. "Your grandmother wrote a song."

"She wrote a BINDING."

"She wrote a binding in verse. In common meter. It rhymes, Paloma." He took the ledger from me and read a line aloud, pitching his voice into a fair imitation of a hearth-side balladeer: "*'Wolf of hunger, wolf of wear, / what was borrowed, render bare—'* This is a lullaby. She set a god-trap to a lullaby."

"Of course she did." Something loosened in my chest, unexpectedly. "She used to sing to me. Before. I don't remember the songs — I stole those memories out too — but I remember that she sang. Everyone said she sang badly."

"She rhymes 'render' with 'surrender' in the third stanza," Quentin said. "She sang terribly."

And we laughed — both of us, really laughed, the first time since the burning gardens — because grief and laughter live in the same room and share a bed, and sometimes one of them snores and wakes the other. For a minute we were just two tired people on a cold mountain, laughing at a dead woman's bad poetry, and the stones breathed around us, and if the mountain was amused, it kept it to itself.

Then the light began to fail, and the laughing stopped, and there was nothing left between us and the work.

* * *

The dark moon rose — which is to say, nothing rose, and the sky went black as a closed eye, and the stars came out in numbers that made the world feel briefly honest.

I walked the circle sunwise, as instructed, and spoke Grandmother's words, and the stones woke.

I don't know how else to describe it. They had been breathing before; now they *listened*. Warmth spread under my palms from stone to stone as I touched each one, and the warmth had texture, had attention, had the unmistakable feeling of a very old animal turning one ear toward a sound it had waited a long time to hear.

At the north stone, Quentin stood with his eyes closed and his hands open at his sides, and he was already choosing. I could see it happening behind his face — the inventory, the terrible inventory, a man walking through the burned house of his own mind deciding which single room to give away forever.

"Don't take the best one," I said. I hadn't meant to say it. The words came out of me like a splinter working free. "Quentin. Please. Take a good one, take a strong one, but don't take the *best* one, whatever it is, keep one bright thing, just one, hide it from the mountain, hide it from her, I'll help you, I know how to hide things, hiding things is the only thing I've ever been *good* at—"

"Paloma." He opened his eyes. In the starlight they were nearly colorless, and very calm, and that calm was the worst thing I'd seen since the Wolf smiled at us with his father's mouth. "The footnote. Say it."

"*An unwilling one will simply feed the Wolf.*"

"And a half-willing one?"

I couldn't say it. He said it for me, gently, like setting down something fragile.

"A half-willing one feeds it too. There's no clever version of this. No theft. No trick. Your grandmother checked every door, and I've spent two hundred miles learning how thorough she was." He almost smiled. "She raised you to be a thief, and then she built the one lock in the world a thief can't pick. I think that was her idea of a joke."

"It was NOT funny."

"It's a little funny."

The stones pulsed around us, patient as tide. The ledger lay open on its four pebbles, and the ink of the final instruction seemed darker than it had that morning, though I knew ink didn't work that way. Knew it the way you know a house is watching you.

*I stepped to the center stone and read the last lines of the stanza, and the mountain drew its breath —*

—and Quentin stepped forward, away from the north stone, toward me, and took both my hands in his cold ones, and said, quickly, before I could stop him, before the mountain could stop him:

"Before we begin. I've decided which memory. And I need you to hear it first, because after tonight, no one will remember it but you — and you're about to become very busy forgetting everything else." His grip tightened. "So listen. Listen properly, Thackeray. Once."

And he told me.
