## 21. Quentin's Bargain

The ledger lay open between us on the shrine floor like a wound that had learned to read.

Paloma had been working through Grandmother Thackeray's instructions for the better part of an hour — her fingers tracing the faded script, her lips moving around words in a dialect I half-recognized from my mother's private chapel, back when the palace still had a private chapel and my mother still had a self to pray with. The candlelight caught the ink and made it swim. Outside, the mountain hummed. Not wind. Hummed, low and patient, like a throat clearing before a very long speech.

"Here," Paloma said at last, and her voice had gone strange. Flat. Careful. The voice of someone who has read something twice to make sure it says what she thinks it says. "The anchor. It needs an anchor."

I looked up from the map of the pass I'd been pretending to study. "What kind of anchor?"

"A memory." She turned the page toward me. "A willing one. A whole one. The rite doesn't just cut the Wolf loose from the crown — it has to have somewhere *else* to put its teeth while it's being pulled out. Something it can gnaw on instead of the kingdom." Her finger stopped under a line of script. "'Let the anchor be freely given, or let the binding fail.'"

I read the line. Then I read it again, because the first time through, my hands had started shaking so badly the words blurred into weather.

*Freely given.*

I knew exactly how much of me was left. I'd been counting for years, the way a man counts coins when he knows the purse is nearly empty. Half my childhood — gone, eaten, though Paloma carried what the memory-eater tore from me at the ford, and she'd promised, she'd *promised*, to give it back. My mother's face — gone, except for what lived now in Paloma's keeping. My name as a boy — gone. What remained was a king's worth of grief, a smuggler's worth of stubbornness, and whatever strange new thing had grown in me since a thief with cold hands and warm eyes had sat down across from me at a crossroads and said, *You look like a man who's lost something.*

Which had been, I reflected, the understatement of the century.

"I'll do it," I said.

Paloma didn't even look up. "No."

"You haven't heard—"

"I heard you say 'I'll do it.' That's all I needed to hear. No."

I sat down on the stone beside her, because standing felt too much like making a speech, and speeches were my father's weapon, not mine. "Paloma. Listen to the arithmetic."

She loved arithmetic. This was a thing I'd learned about her somewhere on the road between the ford and the mountain, one of those long dark stretches where there was nothing to do but talk, and she'd told me about her hoard the way an accountant tells you about assets: precise, unsentimental, quietly devastating. Every stolen sunset catalogued. Every borrowed grief filed by year. She thought in sums. So I would argue in sums. It was the only language she couldn't wave away.

"Count me," I said. "Go on. Count what's left of me."

"Quentin—"

"Half a childhood. Some grief. A name nobody uses. A face the court already forgot." I held up my hand and folded fingers down as I listed them, one by one, until only two stood upright, which felt about right. "That's the inventory. That's everything. Now count what happens if we don't finish this rite. The Wolf keeps eating. It eats the court, then the city, then the roads, then the inn where Marta makes that terrible soup everyone pretends to love, and eventually it eats every memory in the kingdom until there's nothing left anywhere except a wolf wearing a crown in an empty country, talking to itself forever." I folded the last finger. "Versus me. Which is worth more?"

Paloma finally looked up. Her eyes did the thing they always did when she was about to refuse me — went bright and hard and slightly furious, like chips of river ice in sunlight.

"That's not arithmetic," she said. "That's a guilt-trip with numbers attached."

"It's absolutely arithmetic."

"It's arithmetic done by someone who wants to lose the argument." She tapped the ledger page. "And anyway, your math is wrong. You forgot a line item."

"What line?"

"*You.*" She said it plainly, no flourish, and it landed harder than any flourish could have. "The inventory you just gave me — half a childhood, some grief, a name — that's what the Wolf left. But it's not what exists. There's also the man who walked into a vault full of royal secrets and came out protecting a stranger. The man who heard his father's voice in a monster's mouth and still chose to keep walking. The man who—" she faltered, just slightly, the first crack in the ice, "—who promised to remember things for people. Who kept the promise. You don't get to subtract yourself from yourself, Quentin. That's the Wolf's trick. That's literally its entire method. It convinces you that what's been eaten was the whole of you."

I hadn't expected to be outmaneuvered with my own tragedy. I sat there, mouth open, feeling rather like a chess player who has just realized his opponent has been playing a different game entirely.

"That's cheating," I said weakly.

"That's accounting," said Paloma Thackeray, memory-thief, and turned back to the ledger.

* * *

But I wasn't finished. I want that on the record, wherever records go when kingdoms fall. I was not finished.

I let her work for a while. The mountain hummed. The candle burned down in patient increments. And I watched her — the way her shoulders hunched over the old script, the way she bit the inside of her cheek when a passage resisted her, the way her hands, so steady when she lifted memories from strangers' minds without their ever knowing, trembled now over paper. She'd found her grandmother's handwriting on the seals beneath the chapel and hadn't recovered from it yet. I understood that particular wound. It was mine too, in a way — the discovery that someone who claimed to love you had been building a trap with you inside it, and calling the building love.

"Paloma," I said, after the third candle had given up. "If you won't take me whole, take what's left after."

She went still.

"The rite needs a willing memory. Fine. Give me back what you're carrying — my childhood, my mother's face, my name — and let *that* be the anchor. Let the Wolf chew on the years you've been keeping safe for me. They're willing. I'm willing them. Freely given, per the fine print." I spread my hands. "Then whatever remains afterward, remains. I stay hollow but alive, you stay stocked, and the binding holds. Everyone loses a little. Nobody loses everything. That's actual arithmetic."

For a long moment she said nothing. The silence had weight, like wet snow on a roof.

Then she laughed — a small, sharp, broken sort of laugh, the kind that isn't really laughter at all but grief wearing a disguise it borrowed from joy. "You absolute *idiot*."

"I beg your pardon?"

"Do you know what you just offered?" She turned to face me fully, cross-legged on the stone, and the candlelight made shadows move across her face like weather. "You offered me your childhood. Your *mother*. The last true pieces of Quentin Ashvale, heir of Thornfell, son of the real king — you offered them to me so I wouldn't have to feel the cost of the rite." Her voice cracked on *cost*, and she pushed through it. "Do you understand that I can't tell anymore whether you're brave or whether you've simply been eaten down to the part of you that doesn't mind? Because those look identical from the outside, and one of them is a virtue, and the other is a symptom."

That landed somewhere deep and rang like a bell. I flinched. Honestly, truly flinched, the way you do when someone says a true thing about you that you'd been carefully not saying to yourself.

"And here's the other thing," she continued, relentless as a river in spring. "If I give your memories to the rite — if I pour the years I've been carrying for you into that stone — then they're gone. Not stored. Not kept. *Gone.* The mountain digests them. That's what anchors do; that's what Grandmother designed. And then you'll be standing in the ashes of this with nothing, and I'll be standing next to you having *done that to you*, and I will spend the rest of my life remembering — because remembering is apparently the one thing I'm cursed to be good at — that I fed the last of you to a rock." She shook her head. "No. I won't run that sum. Refused. Denied. Rejected. Pick whichever word hurts least."

"All three hurt quite a lot, actually."

"Good."

* * *

We argued until the candles were ash and I had to light new ones, and the argument went in circles the way arguments do when both people are right.

I tried logic. She countered with logic. I tried the kingdom's survival, and she pointed out that a kingdom saved by spending its last honest heir was a kingdom that had learned nothing from the Wolf, which was honestly such a good point I had to stand up and walk to the other side of the shrine to recover.

She tried sentiment — actual, naked sentiment, which from Paloma was rarer than royal coin. "You told me at the inn," she said, "that you never belonged at court. That the palace was a house full of mirrors and none of them showed anything real. Well, this is the opposite of that. This mountain, these smugglers, that terrible soup, this stupid beautiful doomed plan — this is the first place you've ever *fit*. And you want to trade it for a clean death in a ritual written by a woman you never met."

"I don't want a clean death. I want a useful one. There's a difference."

"Not as much difference as you think."

I tried anger, briefly. It didn't suit either of us and we abandoned it quickly, almost with relief, like a coat that turned out to be on fire.

And near the end — near the very end, when the sky outside the shrine's mouth had gone from black to that bruised grey that means dawn is considering its options — she tried the thing I should have known was coming, because she'd been circling it all night the way a moth circles a flame it intends to scold.

"Fine," she said quietly. "Arithmetic. Since you insist. Here are my numbers." She held up one hand, counting. "One: I am a thief. Taking is what I do. Giving things away is the one skill I never learned, and you're asking me to learn it on the largest possible scale, overnight, under pressure, with a wolf coming. Two: everything I have ever taken, I took because keeping the world's memories safe felt like the closest thing to family I would ever have. If I spend yours, I spend the idea of myself along with it. Three—" and here her voice dropped to almost nothing, and the mountain's hum seemed to lean in to listen, "—if you die in that stone, Quentin, then I will be the person who brought him here. The ledger named me. The prophecy chose me. And I will carry that the way I carry everything, perfectly, forever, with no one to give it back to."

I stared at her.

"You realize," I said slowly, "that you just argued yourself into a corner. Because by your own accounting, if *I* anchor the rite, you're destroyed; and if *you* anchor it, you're destroyed; and if neither of us does, the binding fails and the Wolf destroys everything anyway. There is no version of tomorrow morning where somebody doesn't pay."

"Yes," said Paloma. "I know. I did the math hours ago. That's why I've been arguing with you all night — not to win. To stall." She looked down at her hands. "Because once we stop arguing, one of us has to actually decide, and I was hoping that if I stalled long enough, a third option would wander in out of the snow like a lost mule."

I laughed. I couldn't help it. Somewhere in the middle of the worst night of my life, the memory-thief and the half-a-king had ended up stalling for time like two children avoiding bedtime, hoping the universe would do our homework for us.

"It's a bad strategy," I said.

"It's the only strategy I have left. Stealing got me here. Lying got me here. Running got me here. None of those work against a ledger written a hundred years ago by a woman who clearly saw this exact argument coming, because—" she flipped the ledger pages back, fast, hunting, and stopped, and her breath caught audibly, "—oh. Oh, you *sly old woman*."

"What? What is it?"

She turned the book toward me. Near the bottom of the page, in smaller script, tucked between the ritual instructions like a footnote nobody was meant to find until they were desperate enough to read every word:

*"To the thief who reads this: the anchor need not be one memory. It may be many small ones, gathered from many willing hearts. A hundred candles burn longer than one bonfire. Ask your friends. Ask the road. Ask the ones who remember you anyway. — M.T."*

We both sat very still.

"She knew," Paloma whispered. "Grandmother knew we'd sit here all night doing arithmetic with ourselves. She wrote us an escape clause and hid it in the footnotes, because she knew we'd only find it when we were too tired to be proud." Her eyes were shining and wet and furious all at once. "It was never supposed to be one person. It was never supposed to be you, or me, or anyone alone. It was supposed to be *everyone*. A hundred small memories. A thousand. The smugglers. Marta and her terrible soup. The inn. The road itself. Every soul we've met who remembered us without being asked."

The relief hit me like warm water, and then, immediately behind it, something else — something that felt suspiciously like hope, an emotion I had assumed had been eaten years ago along with my mother's face.

"We'd have to ask," I said. "People would have to *give*. Willingly. That's the terms."

"Then we ask." Paloma was already on her feet, scrubbing tears off her face with the heel of her hand, looking less like a woman undone than a woman armed. "I've spent ten years taking memories that weren't offered. I can certainly spend one night asking for the ones that are."

* * *

We climbed out of the shrine into the grey beginning of morning, and the mountain was still humming, but differently now — or maybe I was hearing it differently. Hard to say. Grief and hope sound alike if you tilt your head.

Below us, far down the switchback trail, smoke rose from the inn of small kindnesses, thin and ordinary and impossibly dear. And beyond it, at the edge of the tree line, at the very limit of what the dawn light would show —

Something moved.

Something large. Something that cast a shadow no animal that size should cast, walking with a gait that was almost a man's gait, almost a king's processional stride, coming up the mountain road with the unhurried confidence of a creature that had read the same ledger we had and knew exactly what we'd just figured out.

Paloma saw it first. Of course she did. Thieves notice things.

"It's early," she said, in a voice that managed to be calm and terrified in the same sentence. "He's early. Wolves are never early."

"Unless," I said, watching the shadow lengthen up the trail toward us, "they've decided the argument is over. And they intend to settle it themselves."
