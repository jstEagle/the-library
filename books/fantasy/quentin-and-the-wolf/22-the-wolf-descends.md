## 22. The Wolf Descends

### Paloma

The mountain had been humming all day.

Not the ordinary hum of stone settling, or wind through the shrine's hollow throat, but something lower, older, the kind of sound you felt in your back teeth. Paloma had felt it once before, years ago, when a bridge she was crossing decided it was done being a bridge. She had run then. There was nowhere to run now.

"Tell me that's normal," she said.

Quentin stood at the shrine's edge, hands pressed flat against the memory-wax stone, and he did not answer right away. That was answer enough. He looked worse than he had that morning — grayer, thinner somehow, as though the mountain were drawing on him the way a candle draws on its wick. The rite had begun to take its toll before the rite had even truly begun.

"It's the Wolf," he said at last. "It knows where we are. It has always known. It was only deciding whether we were worth the walk."

"And?"

"And it's decided." He turned to look at her, and she saw that his eyes had gone the color of a winter sky — that same pale, patient gray. "Paloma. When it comes, whatever it says, whatever it shows you — it will be *true*. That's the worst part. It doesn't lie. It doesn't need to. It just shows you what you want until you forget you wanted anything else."

"Charming," Paloma said. "You've described my entire profession."

Something flickered across his face — not quite a smile, but the ghost of one, the way a candle gutters before the wind takes it. "Yes. Which is why I'm telling you. You of all people know how a beautiful thing looks right before it's taken."

The humming deepened. The stone beneath her boots shivered, and somewhere far below, in the roots of Thornfell, something that had been patient for a hundred years stood up.

The birds left first.

All of them, at once, in one vast silent exhalation off the mountain's shoulders — no cries, no wheeling, just gone, as if the sky had blinked and swallowed them. Paloma watched the empty air where they had been and felt her mouth go dry.

"Quentin."

"I see it."

"Quentin, the *birds*."

"I know." He stepped back from the shrine, and his hand found hers without either of them deciding it should. His fingers were cold. Hers were worse. "The rite needs until moonrise. The ledger said moonrise."

"The ledger also said a Thackeray would unbind the Wolf, and I've been trying very hard not to think about how that's *me*."

"Then don't think about it now either."

The snowline began to move.

That was the only way Paloma could describe it. The white on the upper slopes did not slide or avalanche — it *rose*, peeling off the rock in long ribbons, and beneath it the stone was not stone. It was dark. It was moving. It came down the mountain the way water comes down a windowpane, unhurried, inevitable, filling every crack and channel, and as it came, the humming resolved into something with shape. With breath.

With a voice.

"*Little thief.*"

The voice did not arrive through her ears. It arrived already inside her skull, warm as a hand on the back of her neck, and Paloma's knees nearly went. She had carried a hundred memories that were not hers; she had lived other people's weddings, other people's funerals, other people's first snows. She had thought she knew what it was to be full of someone else.

She had known nothing.

"*I have watched you work,*" the Wolf said, and it sounded — gods help her — it sounded *fond*. "*You have such clever hands. Such careful hands. You never keep what you steal. Do you know how rare that is? How delicious? A thief with no hunger of her own. Only a hunger for everyone else's.*"

"Paloma." Quentin's grip tightened. "Don't answer it. It feeds on answers."

"Does it feed on sarcasm?"

"Paloma—"

"Because if so, we're both about to be *very* well fed."

The darkness crested the last ridge below the shrine and stopped, pooling in the bowl of the valley like spilled ink, and out of it the Wolf built itself a body.

It built the king's body.

Paloma had seen the true king only in memory — Quentin's scattered fragments, the vial's terrible final hour — but she knew the face now the way you know a face from a portrait that hangs over a fireplace you grew up under. Tall. Silver at the temples. A beard trimmed with the particular vanity of a man who has been handsome so long he's forgotten it's an effort. Kind eyes.

These were the kind eyes. That was the horror of it. The Wolf wore the king's face the way a well-worn glove fits a hand, every line and crease correct, and it smiled with the king's mouth, and the smile was warm, and the smile was an open door, and behind the door there was nothing at all.

"Quentin," it said, in a voice like a hearth-fire, like a father calling a child in from the cold. "You've grown."

Quentin made a sound Paloma had never heard from him. Not a word. Something older than words, dragged up from whatever cellar of him still held a boy who had loved his father.

"Don't," Paloma whispered. "Quentin. *Don't.*"

"I know," he breathed. "I know. I know." But he was shaking, and his hand in hers had gone rigid, and the Wolf-King stood in the valley below with snow melting off its shoulders and its arms slightly open, the way a father stands when he has decided to forgive.

The mountain shook.

Not trembled — *shook*, a long rolling shudder that knocked Paloma to one knee and sent a crack of white light skittering up the shrine's face. The memory-wax stone, which had held ten thousand years of pilgrims' griefs and joys without so much as a smudge, *cracked*, and through the crack Paloma saw darkness moving. The Wolf was not merely arriving. It was pressing. It had pressed against this mountain for a century, patient as water, and now it leaned its whole weight against the door.

"*Moonrise,*" it said, and it said it gently, the way you remind a child of bedtime. "*One hour. Finish your little rite, if you like. I am not cruel. I only want what was always mine.*" The king's face tilted, considering her. "*And you, little thief. I have a gift for you. But gifts keep. Come and take it when your work is done.*"

It sat down on a boulder that should not have held its weight, folded the king's hands, and waited.

Paloma had faced cutthroats, memory-eaters, one genuinely memorable tax collector. She had never before been *waited for*.

"Paloma," Quentin said. His voice had come back to him, thin but his own. "The ledger. Now. While it's patient. Patience is just hunger wearing its good coat."

She scrambled up the shrine steps with the ledger clutched to her chest, and behind her, unhurried, the Wolf began — softly, in the king's warm voice — to hum a lullaby.

She recognized the tune.

She had no idea how she recognized the tune.

That frightened her more than everything else combined.

* * *

### Quentin

The lullaby was the worst thing yet.

Quentin knew it. Of course he knew it. His mother had sung it, in the years before the Wolf wore her down to a smiling shell, in the years when the palace had still been a home and not a mouth. *Sleep, my heart, the mountain's old, the mountain's older than the cold —* He could not remember his mother's face. The memory-eater had taken it at the ford. But he remembered the lullaby, whole and perfect, and now the thing wearing his father's body was singing it in the valley below like a man whittling away the last hour of a siege.

It was doing it on purpose. Everything the Wolf did was on purpose. That was the thing no one understood about it, Quentin thought — they expected a beast, all appetite and no thought, and so they kept trying to out-fight it, out-run it, out-cunning it, when the Wolf's true weapon had always been *attention*. It paid attention. It remembered. It was, in its awful way, the best rememberer in the kingdom, and it used that gift the way Paloma used hers — to take.

He checked the shrine anchors for the fourth time. The true king's memories sat in their wax seals in a ring around the summit stone, each one glowing faintly, each one a piece of his father that the Wolf had torn loose over the years and that Quentin had spent six years gathering, one humiliating, dangerous scrap at a time. The seals were steady. The seals were fine.

The seals were not the problem.

The problem was that the Wolf was not attacking, and every instinct Quentin had — the court instincts, the smuggler instincts, the half-eaten instincts of a boy who had grown up watching that thing dismantle a family from the inside — all of them screamed that patience from the Wolf was worse than any assault. An assault you could survive. Patience meant the Wolf had already found the flaw in your plan and was simply enjoying the view.

"Quentin." Paloma's voice from the summit stone, tight. "The ledger's changed."

He climbed to her. She was crouched over the old book, and her face in the stone's pale light was the color of paper.

"Changed how?"

"The last page. The rite instructions. They were — there were four steps. Unbinding, anchoring, the hoard, the sealing. Now there are five." She turned the book toward him, and he read the fresh ink — fresh, still glistening, as though written moments ago, though the ledger was a hundred years old — and the words went through him like cold water through cloth.

*When the Wolf comes wearing love, it will offer the thief her own. The rite holds only if the thief refuses with the Wolf's face before her. Refusal must be witnessed by the stone.*

"It knew," Paloma said. Her voice was very flat, the way it went when she was frightened and managing it. "My grandmother knew it would come tonight. Knew it would offer — she wrote this before I was born, Quentin, she knew it would offer me *something*, and she built the refusal into the rite itself. The trap isn't just the binding. The trap is that the Wolf *has* to make the offer. It's compelled. She wrote it so."

"Then we let it offer," Quentin said slowly, "and you refuse, and the stone witnesses, and—"

"And the rite holds." She looked up at him. "Yes. Simple. Except my grandmother apparently also knew me well enough to know that whatever it offers me, I'll *want* it. She wrote a warning in the margin. Do you want to hear the warning?"

"No," Quentin said.

"'*It will offer her the one thing she stole first,*'" Paloma read, "'*and she will have to look at it and say no. May whatever gods watch over Thackerays give her the strength, because I never found mine.*'"

The lullaby below stopped.

In the silence that followed, the silence was worse, and then the Wolf's voice came up the mountain again, gentle as snowfall, and this time it was not speaking to both of them.

"Paloma Thackeray," it said. "Come down. I will not touch you. I will not touch the boy. I only want to show you something I have kept safe for twenty years."

Quentin reached for her hand and found it already trembling.

"Whatever it is," he said, "it's bait."

"Obviously it's bait."

"Paloma—"

"But Quentin," she said, and her voice cracked clean down the middle, and for the first time since he'd known her — the thief with the careful hands, the woman who never kept what she stole, who was hollow on purpose and called it discipline — for the first time she sounded like someone standing at the edge of something with no floor beneath it.

"It said *kept safe*. Not *kept*. *Safe*."

The mountain groaned. Below them, the Wolf-King rose from its boulder and opened the king's kind hands, and in the valley the darkness began to glow, softly, the way a memory glows when a thief lifts it out of a sleeping mind — and Paloma stepped toward the shrine stairs.

Quentin caught her arm.

The Wolf smiled up at them with his father's face and said, with terrible tenderness:

"Come and see what you looked like, little thief. Before you took her away."

The stone beneath their feet cracked from summit to valley, and the moon began to rise.
