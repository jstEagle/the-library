## 23. Half a King

The Wolf had always been polite about it.

That was the worst part, Quentin thought, standing in the front hall of a house that existed nowhere except inside his own skull. The Wolf knocked first. It wiped its paws. It asked, *May I?* in his father's voice, and then it came in anyway, and the door stayed open behind it like a wound that had learned good manners.

The house was Thornfell — or rather, the memory of Thornfell, which was better and worse than the real thing. Better because nothing here was falling down. Worse because every room was a room he had loved, and the Wolf was burning them one by one, and it did it slowly, so he would watch.

He could hear Paloma outside. That was the strange mercy of it: her voice came through the walls of his mind like light under a door. She was reading from the ledger, her grandmother's spidery hand guiding hers, and every few lines she would mutter something cross about the handwriting — *"honestly, Gran, did you have to write 'the third turning of the stone' like that, is it a three or an eight"* — and Quentin clung to those muttered complaints the way drowning men cling to driftwood. Because they were proof she was still there. Proof the rite was working. Proof that somewhere outside this burning house, a thief with cold fingers and a warm heart was fighting for him without ever once raising her voice.

The Wolf knew it too. That was why it had stopped being polite tonight.

"Quentin." His father's voice, coming from the top of the stairs. "Come up. We should talk."

"We've talked," Quentin said. His own voice sounded thin in here, stretched over too many empty rooms. "We've talked for eleven years. You ate my mother's face out of my head. You took my childhood name last week at the ford — well, no, that was your pet, one of the crown's little eaters, but you trained it, didn't you? You taught it what to look for."

"I taught it to be tidy," the Wolf said, and there it was, the thing wearing his father's face, descending the stairs on all fours now, because politeness was expensive and it was saving its strength. "You mistake me for a vandal, boy. I am a collector. I keep things beautifully."

"You burn houses."

"I *warm* them," said the Wolf, and breathed.

The west wing caught.

Quentin felt it before he saw it — a heat blooming behind his left eye, a smell of cedar smoke where there should have been rain on stone. The west wing was the nursery. The west wing was where his mother had sung to him in a language the court called rustic and he called home. He ran for it, and running through one's own mind, he discovered, is very much like running through a dream: the corridors lengthen when you're afraid of what's at the end of them.

He reached the nursery door as the fire reached the crib.

There was no crib anymore. There hadn't been a crib in thirty years; he'd been nearly grown when the Wolf first came to court, wrapped in diplomacy and winter furs. But minds are honest places, and his kept the crib anyway, kept the singing, kept the woman in the rocking chair whose face was now a smooth grey blank, like an egg, like a stone worn by water. He had grieved her already. He had grieved her at the ford, when the eater tore the name from him — *Maren*, his mother's name, gone like a coin dropped down a well — and grief, he'd learned, does not care how much of the person remains. It takes the whole shape of them and leaves you holding the outline.

"Save it," the Wolf said lazily, from the hallway. "I'm not burning the nursery yet. I'm showing you I can."

"Why?" Quentin shouted back, and hated how young his voice sounded. "Why show me anything? Why not just take it?"

"Because taking is easy," the Wolf said, "and I want more than easy. I want you to *hand* it to me. There's a difference between a theft and a gift, boy, and the difference is everything. A theft can be stolen back. A gift—" it smiled with his father's teeth "—a gift stays given."

Outside, faintly, he heard Paloma swear at the ledger again. Something about ink blots. It made him laugh, actually laugh, here in the smoke, and the laughter did something strange to the fire — it flinched. The Wolf's flames were made of despair, he realized. Not his despair. *Manufactured* despair, imported, the way the Wolf imported everything: his father's face, his father's crown, his father's voice, all of it worn like a coat over a thing that had no shape of its own. And manufactured despair could not survive genuine laughter. It hissed where he laughed, like water on grease.

So he laughed again. Deliberately. It felt unhinged and it felt wonderful.

"What," said the Wolf, "is funny."

"You are," Quentin said. "You're a king-shaped hole pretending to be a king. You're the most elaborate nothing ever built. You've spent a century collecting other people's memories because you have none of your own, and do you know what that makes you? It makes you a magpie. A great big royal magpie with a taste for crowns." He grinned, and the grin hurt, and he did it anyway. "My father used to say the throne room needed fewer crows in it. He meant the courtiers. He'd have adored saying it about you."

The Wolf went very still.

Stillness, in the Wolf, was dangerous. Stillness was the pause before the whole east wing went up. Quentin braced himself — and instead of fire, the thing did something worse. It spoke gently.

"He never said that."

"No," Quentin admitted. "Probably not. I might be inventing it. My memory of my father has been chewed down to the rind — I know his hands, his laugh, the way he smelled of horses and ink, and almost nothing he said. So yes. Maybe I made that up. Maybe I need it." He looked the Wolf dead in its borrowed eyes. "But here's the thing about a man who's lost most of himself: he gets very good at telling the difference between what he remembers and what he wishes. And I wish *hard*, monster, but I don't wish badly. Everything I've kept of him, I've kept honestly. Can you say the same? Is there one single thing in that enormous hoard of yours that you didn't take?"

The Wolf considered this the way a siege engine considers a wall.

Then it burned the library.

*

The library went up like a heart giving out.

Quentin felt it as a sound first — a soft catastrophic rush, pages becoming embers becoming ash becoming a kind of snow that fell upward, because in here even the physics of grief were inverted. Every book in that library was a day of his life. His first hunt, aged nine, when he'd fallen off the pony and been more embarrassed than hurt and his father had laughed until he wheezed. His first lie, aged twelve, told to a kitchen girl named Bess about a ring he absolutely had not found, and the way she'd known instantly and forgiven him anyway, which had taught him more about ruling than any tutor. The night his sister was born. The night his sister died. The long grey years after, when the house had stood empty of laughter and full of his father's silences, and Quentin had learned that silence, too, is a language, and some men speak it fluently.

All of it, burning. Room by room. Floor by floor.

And through the smoke, through the roar, through the terrible quiet spaces where a room had just finished burning and the next one hadn't started — through all of it, Paloma's voice. Reading. Muttering. Once, gloriously, she sneezed — mountain dust, shrine dust, whatever they'd stirred up up there on the stone — and the sneeze echoed through his skull like a bell, and the fire *stumbled*. Actually stumbled. Flames guttered in the corridor outside the library, confused, as though the universe had briefly forgotten what it was doing.

The Wolf noticed. Of course the Wolf noticed. It noticed everything; noticing was most of what it was.

"She's strong," it said, conversational, horrible. "Your thief. Quick hands. I offered her your life, you know. In exchange for walking away. She refused." A pause, and then, with the practiced warmth of a thing that had studied warmth the way scholars study dead languages: "She refused *for* you. Isn't that remarkable? A woman who steals memories for coin, refusing the one payment she truly wants."

"She didn't refuse for me," Quentin said. His throat was full of ash. "She refused because she's decided who she is, and it isn't that. There's a difference. You wouldn't understand it. You've never decided anything in your existence — you've only ever taken."

"And yet," the Wolf said, "she stands on my mountain, reading my enemy's book, performing my enemy's rite, and I am reduced to negotiating with her through the medium of your burning brain. Do you understand what an indignity that is? I am a king. I have eaten kings. And I am being unmade by a smuggler and a ledger written by a dead woman with bad handwriting."

"Gran's handwriting," Quentin said, "is going to be the death of you. There's poetry in that. The great devourer, undone by a blot."

It was bravado and both of them knew it. The Wolf let him have it, the way a cat lets a mouse have the last inch of runway, and then it turned, deliberately, toward the door Quentin least wanted it to turn toward.

The study.

His father's study. The last intact room in the house. Everything else was smoke and memory-of-smoke, but the study still held: the desk with its ink stains shaped like continents, the chair with the broken armrest his father had refused for eleven years to have mended because his mother had broken it laughing, the window seat where Quentin had sat as a boy watching the road for travelers and inventing lives for every one of them. The merchant was a spy. The pilgrim was running from a marriage. The tinker owed money to a dragon. He'd been a strange child. He missed being a strange child. Some of that strangeness was still in the study, folded into the cushions like pressed flowers.

"This one," the Wolf said, "I will not burn."

Quentin's heart, wherever hearts live inside a mind, lurched. "What?"

"I will keep it," the Wolf said. "Intact. Perfect. Untouched. Forever. All you have to do is walk out the front door and stop fighting. Stop helping her. Let the rite fail. Let me finish eating what's left of you at my leisure — I'm in no hurry, I've waited a century — and in exchange, this room survives. Your father's chair. His ink. The window seat. The cushions. The strange child folded into them." It sat down in the doorway, enormous, filling it, patient as winter. "That is my offer. Not mercy. *Curation.*"

And here was the horror, and Quentin would carry the knowledge of it to his grave if he got to have a grave: for three full seconds, he wanted to say yes.

Three seconds. He counted them. They lasted approximately a geological era each. In those seconds he saw the room so clearly it hurt — the afternoon light, the smell of leather and woodsmoke, the ghost of his father humming off-key — and he understood, with a clarity that felt like a blade, exactly why the Wolf had made this offer and no other. Not the nursery. Not the library. The study. Because the study was where he had been happiest alone, and a man can survive losing his mother's face and his sister's name and half his childhood, but losing the room where he learned to be *himself* — that loss doesn't kill you. It just leaves something else walking around wearing your coat.

"No," Quentin said.

Out loud. To the Wolf. To himself. To the three seconds.

"No," he said again, steadier. "Because I know what you are now. I finally see it. You don't want the room. You want me to believe there's a version of this where I get to keep something — anything — by cooperating with you. And there isn't. There never was. You ate my father *while he trusted you*. You wore his face to his own funeral. Every deal you've ever struck ended the same way: with the other party hollowed and you heavier. If I walk out that door, the study burns anyway — maybe slower, maybe prettier, but it burns. And worse, it burns while I watch from a cage, agreeing that it's fair."

The Wolf's borrowed eyes narrowed. "You sound very sure."

"I'm not sure," Quentin said honestly. "I'm terrified. But Paloma taught me something — she probably doesn't know she taught me, she'd be insufferable about it if she did — she taught me that fear and certainty aren't opposites. You can be certain with your knees knocking. She walks into vaults and across borders and into the teeth of the crown's eaters, scared the entire time, certain the entire time. So." He planted his feet in the smoking corridor of his own mind. "No. And also no. And also, incidentally, go to hell — although I suppose you'd feel right at home, it's probably tidy."

Something moved behind the Wolf's face. Not anger. Something older and sadder and far more frightening: recognition. The flicker of a thing that had just heard the truth about itself and did not enjoy it.

"You are your father's son," it said quietly.

"Yes," Quentin said. "Thank you. I've been trying to remember that all year."

And then the Wolf stopped playing.

*

It came for the study at a run.

Not fire this time — fire was slow, fire was theatrical, fire was for teaching lessons. This was the real appetite, the thing underneath all the manners, and it came through the corridor of Quentin's mind like a flood through a valley, and he ran ahead of it, and he was losing, and he knew he was losing, and somewhere above — outside — beyond — Paloma's voice cracked upward into a new line of the rite, a line with force in it, a line that made the whole burning house shudder on its foundations.

*"By the thief's hand and the keeper's word—"*

"That's her," Quentin gasped, running. "Do you hear her? That's her reading your death warrant, and she's *complaining about the penmanship while she does it—*"

The flood took the corridor. Took the gallery of portraits — his ancestors, their faces already blanked, the Wolf having eaten them years ago, course by course, like a man working through a tasting menu. Took the great hall. Took the chapel where he'd prayed uselessly and sincerely. Room after room after room, and Quentin ran through his own collapsing self the way a man runs downstairs in a house that is already lost, grabbing what he could, and what he could grab was pitifully small: his father's laugh. The smell of horses and ink. Bess's face, forgiving him. The strange child in the window seat, inventing dragons.

He gathered them like a man gathering children from a burning school. He held them against his chest. They were warm. That was the unbearable thing — they were *warm*, these fragments, these crumbs, these last few coals of a life, and he cupped them like a man carrying a candle through wind.

The flood reached the study door.

And stopped.

Not because of Quentin. Quentin had nothing left to stop it with; he was down to his last handful of coals and his last stubborn breath. It stopped because of the mountain. Because outside his skull, on the cold shoulder of Thornfell, a thief with ink-stained fingers read the final words of a page written a hundred years ago by a grandmother who had loved her granddaughter enough to build her a trap with teeth, and the words *landed*, and the whole house of Quentin's mind shook like a bell being struck.

The Wolf staggered. Actually staggered, in the doorway of the study, its borrowed face flickering — father, stranger, father, something vast and grey and hungry underneath, father again — unable to hold the shape, because the shape was fed by royal memory and the rite was cutting the supply.

"You can't hold it," Quentin said. He was crying. He noted this distantly, the way you note weather. "Look at you. Look at your face. You're slipping. A hundred years of stealing, and a dead woman with bad handwriting is pulling it apart, and a smuggler is reading the instructions aloud, and *you're slipping.*"

"The thief," the Wolf snarled, and its voice was no longer anyone's voice, it was the voice under all voices, the voice of hunger itself, "will fail. They always fail. The rite requires a price no one pays willingly. It requires a hoard. It requires everything. And your thief—" it smiled, wide and grey and knowing "—your thief keeps things. That's her sickness. That's her secret. Ask her sometime what she stole first. Ask her what she's carrying in that pack of hers, all these years, every sunset, every grief, every borrowed year. She won't give it up. No thief gives up the hoard. When the moment comes, she'll hesitate, and I only need one hesitation—"

"Paloma Thackeray," Quentin said, "has never hesitated in her life when it mattered. She'll hesitate about *breakfast*. She'll stand in an inn agonizing over eggs for ten minutes. But when it matters—" he lifted his handful of coals, his last warm fragments, his father's laugh and his mother's song and the strange child, and he held them up like a lantern "—when it matters, she decides faster than anyone I've ever met. You offered her my life two days ago. She answered before you finished the sentence."

The Wolf looked at him for a long moment. Then it did the last thing Quentin expected.

It stepped backward. Out of the study doorway. Down the ruined corridor, through the smoke, retreating — but not fleeing. Regrouping. Its face reassembled itself, father-shaped, calm, and it said, softly:

"Then we shall see, heir of Thornfell. We shall see what your thief does when the ledger asks her for everything she has. I have eaten a hundred kings, and every one of them believed someone would save them at the last. And every one of them was wrong."

It turned and walked into the smoke, and the smoke closed behind it like a curtain, and Quentin stood alone in the last intact room of his own mind, holding his coals, shaking, alive.

Through the walls, faint and clear and complaining about ink blots, Paloma kept reading.

And deep beneath the rite, beneath the mountain, beneath everything — Quentin heard the ledger turn its page, and heard Paloma go suddenly, completely silent.

* * *

Outside, on the cold stone of the shrine, Paloma Thackeray read the new page twice. Then a third time. Her hands had gone white around the edges of the book.

"Quentin," she said quietly, to the air, to the mountain, to the man inside his own burning head. "Oh, Quentin. It wants the hoard. It wants *everything I've ever taken.*"

The wind picked up. Somewhere below them, far off, the Wolf began to move.

"It wants everything," Paloma whispered, "and I think — I think I already know what I'm going to do."
