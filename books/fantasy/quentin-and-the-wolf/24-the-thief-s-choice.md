## 24. The Thief's Choice

The ledger's new page was written in her grandmother's hand — of course it was; every page was — and it was written in the particular slanting script that meant *I am sorry for what follows.*

Paloma had learned to read that slant at nine years old, when Grandmother Sable had used it on a recipe for plum wine that turned out to be a recipe for exploding plum wine and had taken the roof off the smokehouse. *Sorry for what follows* always meant something was about to blow up.

This time the something was Paloma.

*The binding requires an anchor,* the page read. *Not one memory. A treasury. The Wolf has eaten a kingdom's worth of remembering, and only a kingdom's worth can pull it free. There is no such hoard in all the world.*

*Except one.*

She read it again anyway, hoping the words would rearrange themselves into something kinder. They did not. Words never did. That was the trouble with words.

*A thief who keeps nothing keeps everything. You know this, little bird. You have known it since you were small enough to fit inside my coat pocket. Every memory you ever lifted and could not bear to give back — they are not gone. They are waiting. And they will weigh exactly as much as a kingdom, because I built you to carry exactly that much, and I am sorry, and I loved you, and both of those things are true, and neither of them will make this hurt less.*

Below that, in smaller script, almost squeezed into the margin like an afterthought:

*The rite must be begun before the Wolf reaches the mountain. Do not wait for him. He will offer you a bargain. He is very good at bargains. So are we.*

Paloma closed the ledger with great care, the way you close a door on a room where someone has just died.

Then she sat down on the cold stone of the shrine, pulled her knees up to her chest, and allowed herself precisely one minute of being a twenty-six-year-old woman who wanted very badly to throw the book off the mountain and run away down the smuggler roads and never think about kingdoms or wolves or grandmothers again.

She counted the minute out honestly. She'd stolen a clockmaker's sense of time once, decades of it compressed into a single silver hour, and she had never lost track of a minute since.

At fifty-nine seconds, she heard footsteps on the shrine steps.

"Quentin," she said, without turning around. "You're supposed to be fighting a wolf inside your own head."

"I won," Quentin said. His voice was rough as a gravel road and twice as tired. "Mostly. There's a corridor left. He's barricaded in the pantry." He came and sat beside her, which took some doing, because Quentin sat the way furniture falls. "The mountain told me to come out. It said you'd stopped reading aloud."

"The mountain talks too much."

"It gets lonely. Three hundred years of holding memories like wax and nobody visits." He looked at the ledger in her lap. He didn't reach for it. He'd learned that much about her by now — that she handed things over when she was ready and never a moment before, and that snatching made her bite. "Bad news?"

"Worse than bad. It's *specific*."

"Specific is worse than bad," he agreed solemnly. "Bad is weather. Specific is tax collectors."

So she told him. All of it. The anchor. The treasury. The kingdom's worth. And then the part underneath, the part that had made her hands go white: *a thief who keeps nothing keeps everything.*

When she finished, Quentin was quiet for a long moment. Wind moved through the shrine columns, carrying the smell of pine and far-off snow and, beneath it, faintly, the wet-dog reek of the Wolf coming up the pass.

"You kept things," he said finally. Not accusing. Just checking, the way you check whether a bridge will hold before you step onto it.

"I kept *one* thing." Paloma stared out at the darkening valley. "One. My whole career, my whole life, and I broke my own rule exactly once. Never keep what you steal — that's the rule, that's been the rule since I was eleven years old and Grandmother Sable sat me down and made me say it back to her until I could say it asleep. You take a memory, you sell it, you walk away clean. That's how you stay a person instead of becoming a warehouse."

"And the one?"

"A sunset." Her voice came out smaller than she intended. "A fisherman's sunset. Off the coast near Salt Stair, the year I was nineteen. He was dying — old man, lungs full of salt — and he sold me his last evening because his daughter needed medicine and he'd rather she remember him buying it than remember him coughing. Fair trade. Clean trade. I should have passed it along to a buyer within the week."

"But you didn't."

"But I didn't." She laughed, and it came out crooked. "Do you know what it was? Forty minutes. An old man on a boat deck watching the sun go down orange over water he'd fished for sixty years, thinking about his wife, thinking about his daughter, thinking *this was enough, actually, this was enough.* That's all. Forty minutes of an ordinary man deciding his ordinary life had been good. And I couldn't sell it. Because nobody would have paid what it was worth, and because—" She stopped. Started again. "Because I'd never had anything like it. Something that was *enough.*"

Quentin said nothing. But he shifted, slightly, so his shoulder touched hers, and stayed there.

"So the ledger says my hoard weighs a kingdom's worth," Paloma went on, faster now, because if she slowed down she'd stop. "And I thought — that's mad. I've kept one memory. One. But then I understood what she meant, the old horror, the brilliant awful woman. It's not the ones I kept. It's the ones I *carried.* Every memory I've ever lifted, I held it first. Days, sometimes weeks, between the taking and the selling. And a Thackeray doesn't hold a memory the way other people do. We hold it the way wax holds a seal. It presses in. It leaves its shape." She spread her hands, palms up, empty and calloused and suddenly the most interesting objects in the world. "Grandmother built me to be a vault, Quentin. Not a courier. A vault. Everything I ever carried is still in here somewhere, pressed flat, stacked up, forty years of other people's sunsets and griefs and first kisses and last words. I'm not carrying one memory's worth of a kingdom."

She looked at him.

"I *am* the kingdom's worth."

* * *

They argued about it for two hours.

Well — Quentin argued. Paloma mostly dismantled his arguments with the cheerful efficiency of a woman who had spent her life taking apart locks, and found that arguing with Quentin was, annoyingly, one of the few pleasures she'd be sorry to lose.

"You don't even know what's in there," he said, pacing the length of the shrine. Pacing was Quentin's second-favorite activity after brooding; combined, they accounted for most of his personality. "You'd be pouring out — everything. Every job. Every client. Twenty years of work. You wouldn't just lose the memories, you'd lose—"

"The shape of them. Yes. I read the page, Quentin, I'm illiterate but not *that* illiterate."

"—and what happens to *you*?" He rounded on her, and there it was, the thing under the argument, the thing that had been under every argument since the ford, since the inn, since the night he'd said *I'll remember for you* in a voice he clearly thought was casual and wasn't. "If you pour yourself out, what's left walking around afterward? I've seen hollowed people, Paloma. I *am* a hollowed person. It's not living, it's—"

"It's not the same and you know it." She stood up too. They were nearly nose to nose now, which was how their arguments tended to end up, and neither of them ever mentioned it afterward. "You were eaten against your will. This would be mine. Chosen. There's a difference, and the difference is the entire point, and your grandmother-in-law wrote three hundred pages about it so I suggest you trust her."

"She's not my grandmother-in-law."

"Not yet she isn't."

"*Paloma.*"

"What? I'm going to be empty in an hour. Let me flirt while I still know why."

That landed wrong. She saw it land wrong — saw the flinch move through him like weather, because in an hour she might look at him the way she'd looked at him at the inn, warm-eyed and blank, a stranger in her own story. Quentin's jaw tightened. For a moment he looked so much like the portraits of his father in the burned gallery that her chest hurt.

"I could do it," he said quietly. "The anchor. I offered. It's my kingdom, my wolf, my—"

"No."

"Paloma—"

"*No.*" And this time she wasn't flirting, and she wasn't smiling, and Quentin — who had faced down the Wolf in his own skull, who had walked unrecognized through his own palace, who had watched a memory-eater peel his childhood off him like bark — stepped back. "Listen to me. Your remaining self is half a candle stub. I've seen inside your head, love. There's a boy in there hiding in a cellar with his mother's face memorized on a scrap of paper, and that's nearly all that's left. If I use you as the anchor, the rite burns the stub. The wolf gets the last of you, or the mountain does, and either way the boy in the cellar doesn't make it out." She jabbed a finger against his chest. "Whereas I am a *warehouse*, Quentin. Full to the rafters. I can afford this. That's the horrible joke of it. Grandmother built me with exactly enough room inside to pay for exactly this, and I have spent twenty years wondering why I always felt too big for my own skin."

"That's not an answer to what happens to you."

"No," Paloma admitted. "It isn't."

Wind gusted through the columns. Far below, down in the black folds of the pass, something howled — long, low, patient — and the stone of the shrine hummed in answer, and every memory pressed into the mountain's walls shivered like a struck bell.

"He's close," Quentin said.

"I know."

"An hour, maybe less."

"Then stop trying to talk me out of it and help me pack."

* * *

Packing, it turned out, meant sitting cross-legged in the center of the shrine with the ledger open on her knees and the rite-circle chalked around her in lines of ash and salt that Quentin had mixed from instructions he remembered his mother singing. Paloma closed her eyes and went looking through herself.

It was strange, the inventory. She had expected a warehouse. What she found was more like a house she'd lived in so long she'd stopped seeing it — rooms she'd forgotten, corridors stuffed to the ceilings. Here, a cooper's pride in a barrel that rang true. Here, a bride's terror and joy braided together on a morning in Thornfell before the Wolf came. Here, a child's memory of a dog, of snow, of a mother's laugh — dozens of children, dozens of dogs, dozens of mothers, pressed flat and stacked like letters never sent. Here, the smuggler Marta's memory of the ford at moonrise. Here, the taste of a peach from a man who'd grown them, the ache of a violinist's last concert, the specific loneliness of a lighthouse keeper that Paloma had carried for six months once because no buyer wanted it and she couldn't bring herself to discard it.

Forty minutes of a fisherman's sunset, glowing at the center of it all like a hearth.

"Oh," she said softly. "Oh, you sly old woman. It's all here. It was always all here."

"What do you need?" Quentin asked. He crouched at the circle's edge, careful not to smudge the ash-lines, and his face was doing something complicated that he probably thought was stoic.

"Just your hand. At the end. Someone has to hold the ledger open — the rite needs a witness who remembers me." She managed a grin. "Congratulations. You're promoted from exiled heir to bookmark."

"Paloma." His voice cracked on her name, just slightly, and he covered it by clearing his throat. "When this is done. Whatever's left. I'll tell you everything. Every day if I have to. The inn, the ford, the burning gardens, the terrible jokes—"

"My jokes aren't terrible."

"They're crimes, Paloma. Several of them should be tried at court."

"There'll be a court full of smugglers soon, so honestly, my odds are good."

He laughed — wet, unwilling, real — and she decided that if this was the last sound she got to keep, it was a decent one. Not a sunset's worth. But decent.

She laid her palms flat on the ledger's open page, on her grandmother's slanting, apologetic hand, and felt the mountain lean in around her like a crowd around a card table.

"One condition," she said, loud enough for the stone to hear. "You hear me, mountain? Grandmother? Whoever's listening in the rock? When it's done — when the wolf is bound — whatever fragments survive, whatever scraps the stone can spare, you send them back. Not all at once. I'm not greedy. A piece at a time. A voice. A face. A single afternoon." She swallowed. "Because I've spent my whole life giving other people back what they lost. It would be nice, just once, to be on the receiving end."

The stone hummed. Whether it was agreement or merely indigestion, she couldn't tell.

Down in the pass, the howling stopped.

That was worse than the howling. The Wolf had gone quiet the way avalanches are quiet.

"Paloma," Quentin breathed, rising, staring down into the dark where the treeline ended and the bare flank of the mountain began. "He's coming. Now. He's coming *now*."

"Good," said Paloma Thackeray, thief, keeper, vault, granddaughter, and she opened the first door inside herself. "Let him watch. Let him see exactly what a kingdom's worth looks like when it stops running."

She reached for the fisherman's sunset first.

* * *

The first memory left her like a bird leaving a cage whose door had rusted shut for years — a shriek of rust, then sudden astonishing flight. Forty minutes of orange light poured out of her and into the ash-lines, and the lines drank it and began to glow, and somewhere far below, the Wolf made a sound that was not a howl at all but something startled.

Paloma laughed.

"Oh," she said, to the mountain, to her grandmother, to the dark thing climbing toward her with a dead king's face. "*Is that all you've got?*"
