## 25. Everything I Ever Took

The hoard did not look like much, laid out on the shrine floor.

That was the first cruelty of it. A hundred years of theft — of *keeping* — and when you spread a life out under candlelight it came to forty-one vials, six leather memory-books with wax seals gone brittle as autumn leaves, three locket-stones that still pulsed faintly with other people's heartbeats, and a tin of salt-cured recollections from the coast that smelled, even now, faintly of someone else's childhood summer.

Paloma knelt among them and felt like a miser counting coins in a currency about to be abolished.

"Is this all of it?" Quentin asked gently, from the doorway of the shrine.

"It's everything." She turned a vial in her fingers. The memory inside swam like a minnow — a merchant's wedding day, lifted on the Salt Stair road four years ago. She had given him back a copy within the hour, because that was the rule, but she'd kept the original anyway, tucked against her ribs through blizzards and border crossings and one memorable night in a smuggler's cellar with the memory-eaters two floors up. "Forty-one vials. Two hundred and six loose memories in the books. The lockets are older — grandmother's era, some of them. And the tin is—" She stopped. Swallowed. "The tin doesn't matter."

Quentin came and sat beside her without asking permission, which was still new enough to startle her every time. His shoulder was warm through his shirt. Half of him was somewhere else — she could always tell, now, the way one side of him leaned slightly toward a door only he could hear knocking at — but the half that remained here stayed whole for her.

"The tin matters if it's yours," he said.

"It's not mine. Nothing in here is mine." She laughed, and it came out wrong, thin as thread. "That's rather the point, isn't it? That's the whole wretched design of it."

The ledger lay open between them, her grandmother's hand marching across the page in script so precise it looked typeset:

*The binding asks a price no king can pay and no beggar can refuse: a hoard equal to a kingdom's remembering. The thief must give what she has taken, all of it, freely, and hold nothing back — not even the smallest sunset — or the Wolf will find the seam and slip the knot.*

"Not even the smallest sunset," Paloma read aloud, and hated how steady her voice sounded. "She wrote that knowing. Knowing I'd have a hoard by then. Knowing I'd be standing exactly here, doing arithmetic with my own soul."

"Your grandmother spent her whole life building a trap with teeth aimed at the right throat," Quentin said. "I've thought about that a great deal these past weeks. It takes a particular kind of love to aim a weapon that precisely at something you'll never live to see."

"It takes a particular kind of nerve to make your granddaughter the trigger."

"Yes," he said simply. "It does."

*

The rite began at moonrise, because of course it did. Grandmother's instructions were very clear on moonrise. Paloma suspected the woman had also specified which knee to kneel on and the exact temperature of the ceremonial tea, because she seemed the sort of person who believed the universe ran on proper formatting.

The shrine's stone drank light. That was the thing about Thornfell's mountain that no ballad mentioned — the way memory sat in it like water in a sponge. Paloma pressed her palm flat against the altar and felt centuries looking back: a shepherd's song, a queen's last winter, ten thousand small Tuesdays nobody had bothered to write down. The mountain remembered the way she stole — completely, and without asking.

"Say it again," Quentin said. He stood at the circle's edge where the ledger placed him, the anchor's witness, holding the vial that held the true king's final living moment. His knuckles were white around it. "The words. I want to hear them once more before—"

"'What I took, I return,'" Paloma said. "'What I kept, I release. Let the mountain weigh my hoard against a crown, and let the Wolf find the scales tipped.'"

"And if it isn't tipped?"

"Then we're both going to have a very bad night." She smiled at him, and he smiled back, and neither of them said the third thing, the true thing, the thing they had been carefully not saying since the ford and the inn and the burning gardens and the long grey road here: *if this works, I won't remember you.*

Some things you save for after.

*

She started with the sunsets.

Not because they were the least valuable — quite the opposite. Grandmother's instructions had been explicit: begin with the sweetest, so the mountain learns your generosity before it learns your grief. So Paloma uncorked the first vial — a fisherman's sunset over the Greywater, all apricot and bruised gold, stolen off a dock in a town whose name she'd never learned — and poured it onto the altar stone.

The stone drank it. Light moved beneath the surface like a swallow under ice, and then the color bloomed up through the rock itself, veins of apricot spreading outward across the shrine floor, across Quentin's astonished face.

"Oh," he breathed.

"That's the idea," Paloma said, and her voice cracked straight down the middle.

Because here was the thing she had never told anyone, the thing she had barely admitted to herself: she had watched every memory a thousand times. That was the thief's dirty secret, the addiction under the craft. You weren't supposed to keep anything, and she broke her own rule nightly, alone, in whatever room she could afford, replaying other people's best days like a woman warming her hands at a stranger's fire. The fisherman's sunset. The miller's daughter's first snow. An old soldier's memory of his mother humming while she sewed — Paloma had played that one so many times she sometimes caught herself humming the tune without knowing its name.

All of it went into the stone.

She worked methodically, because grief respects procedure. Vial by vial, book by book. A baker's pride in his first perfect loaf. A widow's courage on the morning she buried her husband and planted his garden anyway. A child's absolute conviction that the moon followed her home. A smuggler captain's memory of his crew singing badly in a storm, which Paloma had taken during a job and returned late because she couldn't stop watching it.

Each one poured out and sank in, and each one took a little of her warmth with it, like embers leaving a grate. By the twentieth vial her hands were cold. By the thirtieth she had stopped feeling her feet.

"You can pause," Quentin said quietly. "The ledger allows—"

"The ledger does not allow pauses, Quentin. Read your part again. 'Freely, and hold nothing back.'" She looked up at him, kneeling there in apricot-and-gold light with the king's vial clutched to his chest, and made herself say it lightly: "Besides, if I stop now I'll just have to steal them all back someday, and my rates are extortionate."

He laughed. It was a broken sound, but it was real.

*

The borrowed griefs were harder.

They lived in the leather books, pressed flat like flowers, and they came out reluctantly, clinging to their pages. The griefs were the ones she'd been proudest of taking, once upon a time — proof of her skill, lifting sorrow cleanly without tearing the surrounding self. A father's guilt over a son lost at sea. A sister's rage that outlived her brother by fifty years. The specific, unspeakable weight of a mother who had forgotten her own child's face and smiled at her kindly across a market stall.

Paloma poured them out one by one and felt each one pass through her on its way down into the stone — not hers, never hers, but carried so long it had worn her shape.

And underneath them, deeper than any vial, waiting the way it had waited for twenty years, was the tin.

She set it on the altar last of all and didn't open it for a long time.

Inside was the oldest theft of her career. Her own. A childhood of cold houses and colder silences, a girl who learned to lift memories before she learned cursive, who erased the worst years of her own life at eleven years old because carrying them was heavier than being empty. She had kept it all this time in case she ever wanted it back. She had kept it the way you keep a snake you're not sure has finished biting you.

The ledger's final instruction sat above it in her grandmother's flawless hand:

*Last of all, the thief gives what was never stolen — the shape left behind. This the mountain cannot take; it must be offered.*

"I don't understand," Quentin said, reading over her shoulder. Then he did understand, and his face did something complicated and terrible. "Paloma. The hollow. All this time, the hollowness in you — it isn't absence. It's a container. She wants you to pour *yourself* in too."

"She wants me to offer the shape the childhood left," Paloma corrected. "Which amounts to the same thing." She picked up the tin. It weighed nothing. It weighed everything. "You know what's funny? Twenty years I thought the emptiness was damage. Something done *to* me." She shook her head slowly. "It was a vessel. She built me a cup and waited to see if I'd fill it with enough worth pouring."

"That's monstrous."

"It's also," Paloma said, turning the tin in her fingers, "rather beautiful, if you squint. Don't tell her I said so. She's dead, but she seems the type to haunt."

*

The mountain had begun to hum.

Not metaphorically — the actual stone under her knees vibrated like a struck bell, low and patient, drinking century after century of other people's living. The apricot light had deepened through copper into something like dawn. Outside the shrine, snow fell upward. Quentin stood rigid at the circle's edge, and the Wolf-side of his face had gone sharp and interested, and the human side had gone soft as candlewax, and he was whispering the anchor-words over and over like a man keeping a lamp lit in wind.

*Let the mountain weigh my hoard against a crown.*

Paloma looked down at what remained of her hoard. Three vials. She'd saved three deliberately, though the ledger hadn't asked her to and the mountain certainly hadn't. One was the fisherman's sunset — no. No, she'd already poured that. These were different. These were the ones she'd taken since the road began: a scrap of the inn at the crossroads, lamplight and bad fiddle music and belonging. A moment from the burning gardens — Quentin's hand pulling her through the smoke, both of them laughing like idiots while a palace burned behind them. And one more, small and warm as a coal: the ford, afterward, when she'd promised to carry his childhood for him, and he'd looked at her the way nobody had ever looked at a thief.

Those three she held a moment longer than the others.

"Quentin," she said.

"Don't," he said.

"If it works, I won't know your name. I won't know why there's a hole in me shaped exactly like a man who laughs wrong and hears wolves." Her voice was perfectly steady, which was its own kind of horror. "So I'm telling you now, while I still can: whatever you decide to do about that — the telling-back, the nightly stories, all of it — don't be gentle about it. Be thorough. I'd rather have the truth at full volume than kindness at a whisper."

"Paloma—"

"And one more thing." She stepped to the circle's edge, inside arm's reach, and put her cold hands on either side of his face, careful of the Wolf-side, careful of everything. "You are the best thing I never stole. Do you understand me? Everyone else's joy, I had to take. Yours you gave me free. That makes it worth more than the entire rest of the hoard, and the mountain can weigh that however it likes."

His eyes were wet. "You absolute infuriating woman."

"I know. Pour me out anyway."

She kissed his forehead, once, like sealing an envelope, and walked back to the altar, and uncorked the first of the last three vials.

Lamplight and fiddle music went into the stone. The burning gardens went into the stone. The ford —

The ford she poured slowly, watching it sink, watching the light of it travel down through the rock toward whatever deep place the mountain kept its treasures, and she thought: *keep that one close to the surface, please, keep that one where a hollowed-out woman might someday stumble over it.*

Then the tin. Then the shape of herself, offered freely, tipping out of her like water from a cup — and oh, it was strange, it was like exhaling a breath she'd been holding since she was eleven years old, except the breath was *her*, and the room it left behind was vast and clean and utterly silent.

The last thing she felt was Quentin shouting her name.

The last thing she thought was: *so that's what my name sounds like in his mouth. I hope somebody remembers it.*

The mountain drank the last of her, and the scales — wherever they were, in whatever dark the ledger meant — finally, finally began to tip.

Deep below the shrine, something ancient opened one eye, found itself bound, and screamed.

Paloma Thackeray, keeper of nothing, thief of everything, fell forward into the arms of a man she no longer knew — and the mountain, having weighed a kingdom against a lifetime and found the lifetime heavier, began to unmake a king.
