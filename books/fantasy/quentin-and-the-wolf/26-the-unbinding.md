## 26. The Unbinding

*Quentin*

The mountain was singing.

That was the only word Quentin had for it. Stone did not sing — he had lived in a palace built of the finest quarried granite in the kingdom and it had never once hummed a note — but Thornfell's shrine was singing now, a low thrum that came up through his boots and settled somewhere behind his ribs like a second heartbeat.

Paloma stood at the center of the ring of standing stones, her arms outstretched, her hoard pouring out of her.

He could see it happening. That was the terrible part — you would think a memory leaving a person would be invisible, a whisper, a breath. But Paloma's memories left her *visibly*, rising off her skin like heat-shimmer on a summer road, threads of gold and green and rust-red, thousands of them, spiraling upward into the ledger that lay open on the altar stone. The pages drank them. He could hear the faint wet sound of ink taking light.

"Paloma." His voice cracked on the second syllable. "How much longer?"

"How much do you think a kingdom weighs?" she called back, not turning around. Her voice was strained but steady, and beneath the strain there was something he hadn't heard from her before — not fear. *Relief.* "Because I've been hoarding since I was nine years old, Your Highness, and I was very good at my job."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one I have!" A laugh escaped her, wild and bright and wrong-edged. "The rite takes what the rite takes. Grandmother's handwriting is very clear on that point. 'The binding drinks until it is full.' Very clear. Extremely unhelpful."

The Wolf was coming. They could all feel it — the smugglers who had carried them up the mountain, old Marta with her crossbow and young Fen with his useless but beloved fiddle, even the stone itself seemed to flinch at intervals, as if something enormous were pacing just beyond the tree line, testing the shrine's edges the way a wolf tests a fence.

"Then hurry," Quentin said.

"I am going as fast as a century of theft allows!"

*Paloma*

It didn't hurt. That was the strangest thing, the thing she would have told anyone who asked, if anyone ever asked. She had expected pain. What she got instead was lightness — an unbearable, expanding lightness, like being a bottle someone had finally uncorked after decades of shaking.

A stolen sunset over the Salt Stair road went. The taste of a merchant's wedding cake, lifted for coin when she was nineteen. A fisherman's grief for his drowned brother — she'd carried that one eleven years because she couldn't bear to give it back and couldn't bear to keep it either, so it had just sat inside her like a stone in a shoe. It rose out of her now and dissolved into gold, and she felt lighter and lighter and lighter, and somewhere underneath the lightness was the hollow opening up, room by room, the house of herself being emptied on purpose.

*Good,* she thought. *Let it be empty. Let it be full of nothing. Let this be what I'm for.*

The ledger turned its own pages now. Her grandmother's handwriting marched across them in that sharp, slanted script she'd spent three sleepless nights learning to read: *and the thief shall pour out what she has taken, and the mountain shall weigh it, and if it be heavy enough — if it be heavy as a kingdom's remembering — then the Wolf shall be known, and named, and bound.*

"Heavy enough," Paloma whispered. "Come on. Be heavy enough."

She fed it everything. The lullaby she'd stolen from a innkeeper's wife in Vell Hollow and kept secretly, shamefully, for six years because it was beautiful. The memory of a first kiss belonging to some farm boy whose name she'd never learned — she'd taken it by accident, her hands still clumsy at sixteen, and she'd never figured out how to return it to him so she'd just... carried it. Carried everyone's best moments around inside her like a magpie carrying other birds' eggs.

They all went up into the light.

And under it all — she knew, she'd known since the chapel, since she'd understood the arithmetic of the rite — under it all waited the last of the hoard. Her own childhood. Stolen first, kept longest, the wound she'd cut out of herself at nine years old because a child can't carry what was done to her and keep walking. It sat at the bottom of her like sediment at the bottom of a well.

The rite reached for it.

"Not yet," she said through her teeth. "Not yet. Him first."

*Quentin*

The trees at the mountain's foot began to move wrong.

Not parting — *bending*, all at once, away from something, the way grass bends from wind, except there was no wind. And out of that bending wrongness came the shape wearing his father's face.

Quentin had fought this thing inside his own mind for what felt like a hundred burning rooms. He had watched it eat his childhood name. He had heard its voice every night since the crossroads, patient and amused, coiling through whatever it had left of him. But seeing it walk upright in the moonlight, wearing King Aldous Ishikawa's broad shoulders and silver-streaked hair and the gentle, tired smile that had once meant *safety* — seeing it was worse than all of it. Grief and horror arrived together and neither would let the other speak first.

"There," Marta said grimly, and loosed her crossbow. The bolt flew true and simply stopped, three feet from the Wolf's chest, and dropped into the snow like a tired bird.

"You should run," Quentin said. "All of you. This is mine to face."

"Mm," said Marta, reloading. "No."

"We're smugglers," Fen said cheerfully, tuning his fiddle with absolutely no plan whatsoever. "Running is our whole profession and we're choosing not to."

The Wolf-King stopped at the edge of the standing stones. It looked at Quentin with his father's eyes, and smiled with teeth that were not quite his father's teeth.

"Little heir," it said. "You brought me a gift."

"It brought itself," Quentin said.

Behind him, the singing swelled.

*Paloma*

She felt the Wolf arrive the way you feel a storm front — pressure, and a taste like copper, and every hair on her arms standing up. But she did not stop. The rite would not let her stop; the ledger pulled at her hoard faster now, greedy, nearly sated.

And then the last of the ordinary thefts rose out of her — the sunset, the song, the stranger's kiss — and the well bottom showed, and there it was.

Her childhood.

Nine years old. A door. The thing behind the door that had made a small girl decide she would rather not be herself anymore, thank you, and had taught her hands their first theft. She had never looked at it directly since. You don't look at a scar while you're using the knife.

"Take it," she said aloud, to her grandmother, to the mountain, to whoever was listening. "Take that too. I want it gone more than I've ever wanted anything."

The gold thread rose out of her, thicker than all the others, dark at its heart.

And the mountain *sang*.

*Both*

The sound was not music anymore; it was a bell the size of the sky, struck once. Every stone in the ring lit from within, veins of white fire crawling up their surfaces, and the ledger on the altar snapped shut with a sound like a judge's gavel, and the light —

The light found the Wolf.

It did not burn. That was the horrifying elegance of grandmother's design, and Quentin understood it in one flash even as it happened: the light didn't attack. It *remembered*. The mountain held memory like wax, and the rite had filled it with a kingdom's worth of remembering, and all of that remembering turned at once to look at the creature wearing a dead king's face and said, in the wordless way of stone and ritual:

*We know what you are.*

The false face cracked.

It started at the edges — the silver-streaked hair unraveling into smoke, the shoulders losing their shape, the gentle tired smile splitting wider and wider until it was all teeth, until it was a muzzle, until the whole borrowed majesty of Aldous Ishikawa peeled away like bark from a rotten log and the thing underneath stood revealed in the moonlight: gray and vast and hungry, eyes like coins left in a dead man's pocket.

It howled. The sound knocked Fen flat and made the snow jump off the pines and Quentin felt it in the hollow where his childhood used to live, and then —

— and then the true king's memory rose.

It came up out of the shrine like a man surfacing from deep water. For one impossible breath, Aldous Ishikawa stood whole in the ring of stones: translucent, luminous, assembled out of a hundred scattered vials and a thief's lifetime of stolen light. He looked exactly as Quentin remembered and nothing like the Wolf had worn him. Tired. Kind. *Real.*

His eyes found Quentin across the burning ring.

"My boy," the memory said, in his father's actual voice, the voice Quentin had spent half a lifetime failing to mourn. "So far from home. So *thin*." A smile, rueful and familiar. "I'm sorry about the crown. I'm sorry about all of it. Listen — the court will fear you. Fear them back gently. And the girl—"

The Wolf lunged at the light, jaws wide, and the mountain closed around it.

There was no better word. The stone of the shrine moved like water, like a mouth, and took the Wolf in to the shoulders, to the chin, to the coin-bright eyes, and the howl went down and down and became a vibration and became a hum and became, at the last, part of the singing. Bound. Not destroyed — grandmother's last page had been clear about that — but held, remembered, *kept*, pressed into the wax of the mountain like a flower that could never rot and never bloom again.

The image of Aldous Ishikawa smiled once more, and came apart into motes of gold that drifted down over the ring like warm snow, and touched everything they landed on. Marta's graying hair. Fen's fiddle. Quentin's hollowed-out heart.

And Paloma Thackeray, who had given everything.

*Quentin*

He was moving before the gold finished falling.

She was on her knees at the altar, and she was *empty*. There was no other way to say it. He had seen her tired, frightened, furious, stubborn as a mule in a doorway — he had never seen her like this, gray-faced and slack, her quick dark eyes open and reflecting nothing, a lamp with no flame behind the glass.

"Paloma." He caught her as she listed sideways. "Hey. Thief. Look at me."

She looked at him. It was worse than if she hadn't. She looked at him the way you look at weather — present, unimportant, unconnected to anything.

"Who—" she began, and stopped, and tried again, and her brow creased with genuine polite puzzlement. "Was I... doing something?"

The words went into Quentin like a blade finding a gap in armor. He had lost his mother's face to a memory-eater's hand and he knew — he *knew* — exactly which country she had just fallen into, and he knew there was no crossing back the way she'd come. Some doors only swing outward.

"She gave it all," Marta said quietly behind him. The old smuggler had lowered her crossbow at last. "Every scrap. I felt it go through me — the whole hoard, like a river. Fool girl. Brave fool girl."

"The king," Fen whispered. He had his hat off. Everyone did, though there was nothing left to honor but gold dust settling on snow. "Did you see? One breath. Whole. We saw the true king."

"We saw," Marta said. "And we'll say so. Loudly. In every tavern from here to the coast."

Quentin gathered Paloma up against his chest. She weighed almost nothing, or perhaps he had simply gotten stronger from carrying so much less of himself. Her head lolled against his shoulder. Snow was beginning to fall — real snow, ordinary snow, the mountain done with its miracles.

"Paloma Thackeray," he said, and his voice broke clean in half. "You don't know your own name right now. So I'll hold it for you. Along with everything else. That's the arrangement — remember? *I'll remember for you.* I keep my promises."

She blinked slowly up at him. And then — absurdly, impossibly, in the ruins of her own mind — she smiled. A small, confused, courteous smile, the smile of a stranger being kind to a weeping man on a mountainside.

"That's very nice of you," she said. "Do I know you?"

"No," Quentin said, and laughed, and the laugh hurt worse than the crying. "Not yet. But you will. I'm going to tell you the whole story. Every night, if I have to. It's a good story — there's a wolf in it, and a thief, and a king made of borrowed light. You'll like the thief best. Everyone does."

He stood, holding her, and turned toward the long dark road down off the mountain, where the inn of small kindnesses waited with its crooked chimney and its too-many chairs, and beyond it a capital full of hollow faces that were about to start filling in, and a throne nobody wanted, and a future with her name nowhere written in any ledger at all.

Unwritten. Free.

"Home," he said to her, though she didn't know the word belonged to her yet. "Let's get you home."

Far below them, deep in the singing stone, something vast shifted in its sleep — and settled.
