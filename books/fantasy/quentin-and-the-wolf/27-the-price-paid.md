## 27. The Price Paid

The mountain stopped shaking, and the silence that came after was worse than any of the noise.

Quentin knelt on the shrine floor with Paloma's head cradled in his lap, and for a long moment he could not tell if she was breathing. The stone around them was warm now — the whole shrine, every wall and pillar, humming like a hearthstone at midwinter — and the light that poured through the broken roof was not the grey light of dawn but something older, something that had been waiting a hundred years to be let out.

"Paloma." His voice came out wrecked. He tried again. "Paloma. Thief. Wake up. You're not allowed to die with my mother's name in your pocket. That's theft. I'll have you arrested."

Nothing.

Then her chest rose — shallow, stuttering, but real — and Quentin made a sound he would deny to his grave, something between a laugh and a sob, and bent over her until his forehead nearly touched hers.

Her eyes opened. And he knew, before she said a single word, before her gaze finished its slow, puzzled drift across his face, that the thing he had dreaded most in all the long terrible weeks since the crossroads had come to pass.

She looked at him the way one looks at a stranger.

"Oh," she said softly. "Oh, you're — hello."

"Hello," Quentin said, and his voice held, mostly. "Yes. Hello."

She frowned up at him, and it was the same frown — the exact frown she'd given the ledger in the vault, the one she'd given the Wolf's offer, the one that meant her mind was working three moves ahead of everyone else's — and Quentin clung to that frown like a drowning man clings to driftwood. The frown was hers. Whatever the rite had taken, the frown had survived.

"Where am I?" she asked. "Why is the floor warm? Why are you crying?"

"I'm not crying. There's ash in the air. It gets in the eyes."

"There's no ash." She lifted a hand — it trembled, and he saw the effort it cost her to lift it at all — and touched his cheek, and came away with her fingertips wet. She looked at them with genuine scientific curiosity. "Huh. So there is. In you, I mean."

"Paloma—"

"I don't know your name." She said it plainly, without drama, the way she'd once told him she didn't remember her own childhood — no, wait, she'd never told him that, had she, that was the thing she'd hidden, the thing he'd found out in the burned garden of her past only days ago — and Quentin felt the full terrible symmetry of it settle over him like snow. She had spent her whole life empty at the beginning. Now she was empty everywhere.

"I'm Quentin," he said.

"Quentin." She tried it out, tasting it. "That's a name for someone who owns a lot of books."

"I do, actually. Owned. It's complicated."

"Everything's complicated," she said, and closed her eyes. "I'm so tired. Why am I so tired? It feels like I've been carrying something very heavy for a very long time and someone has finally taken it away, and I don't know whether to thank them or burn their house down."

Quentin laughed — a broken, wet, astonished laugh — because that was exactly it, exactly her, and grief and gratitude crashed together in his chest and made something that had no name in any language he knew.

"Both," he said. "Definitely both."

* * *

She slept for a day and a night in the shrine, and Quentin did not leave her side except once, to stand at the mountain's edge and look at the valley below and confirm what the silence had already told him: the Wolf was gone. The usurper's palace, far to the south, would be waking this morning to find its king a hollow costume with nothing inside it. The true king's memory — Quentin's father's memory, whole and blazing for one impossible breath before it had risen like smoke through the broken roof — had named him heir with its final light. He had felt it go through him like a bell being struck.

He did not feel like an heir. He felt like a man kneeling on a warm floor, holding a thief who no longer knew his name.

When he came back, she was awake, propped against the shrine wall, frowning at her own hands.

"There's something wrong with me," she said, without preamble. "I can feel it. It's like — you know when you take off a coat you've worn so long you forgot you were wearing it, and the air feels wrong on your arms? I'm missing a coat. Several coats. Possibly an entire wardrobe."

Quentin sat down beside her. Slowly, because his whole body ached, and because he wanted very much not to spook her. "Do you trust me?"

"I don't know you," she said. Then she looked at him — really looked, with those grey eyes that had catalogued a thousand souls and kept none of them — and something in her face shifted. "But apparently I did. I can tell. You look at me like I'm a book you've read a hundred times and I keep getting the ending wrong."

"That's fairly accurate."

"So what did I do? What did I give?" Her jaw set, and even hollowed out, even stripped to the stud, she was the most stubborn person he had ever met. "The mountain's warm. The Wolf's gone — don't look surprised, I can hear it in the stone, the whole mountain is smug. Something was paid. I want to know what I paid."

Quentin took a breath. Then he reached into the inner pocket of his coat, where for three weeks — through the pass, the ford, the burning gardens, the betrayal, the long climb — he had carried the small leather roll he'd taken from her hoard before the rite. She'd have stopped him if she'd known. She'd have called it theft.

He preferred to think of it as an inheritance.

"What are those?" she whispered.

"Memories," Quentin said. "Yours. Kept ones. You were — you were a collector, of a kind. And before you gave everything to the mountain, you gave these to me. To hold. To give back."

"I don't remember giving them."

"No," he said gently. "You wouldn't."

* * *

He gave them back one at a time, and it was the hardest and most beautiful thing he had ever done.

The first was small: a memory of rain on a smuggler's tarp, the particular drumming of it, and the smell of wet canvas and pipe smoke. She took it from his palm — she had to press it to her forehead, the old way, the way her grandmother's ledger had described — and her whole body relaxed, like a note resolving.

"Oh," she murmured. "Oh, that's *lovely*. Whose is it?"

"Yours. You kept it because it was the first night on the road that didn't feel lonely."

She looked at him sharply. "I said that?"

"You wrote it. On the seal. You labeled all of them." He turned the little vial so she could see the faded ink in her own hand: *Rain, first good night. Keep.*

"You labeled your own stolen goods," she said, and there was the ghost of her crooked grin. "That's either very organized or very sentimental."

"Both. Definitely both."

The second was the taste of honey-bread at the Inn of Small Kindnesses, and when it settled into her she laughed out loud — a startled, delighted sound — and said, "I know that inn. I know that inn! There's a woman there, Marta, with flour on her elbows, and she — she *likes* me. She likes me even though I once picked her lock to steal her soup recipe."

"You did what?"

"It's a very good soup, Quentin. Priorities."

The third was the pass at dawn. The fourth was the ford, the cold water, the moment she had caught him when the memory-eater tore his childhood name away — and when that one went in, she grabbed his wrist without knowing why, and held on, and said, "I promised you something. In this one. I promised I'd remember for you." Her eyes went wide. "Did I? Did I remember?"

"You did," Quentin said, and could not speak for a moment. "Every day."

She gave back to him, in that order, the pass and the ford and the inn and the rain and a dozen more — the ledger's cipher, the vault's cold, the moment she had first seen him at the crossroads and decided, in the privacy of her own skull, that the gaunt half-hollowed stranger was *interesting*, damn it all, and interesting was dangerous and she'd never been any good at staying away from dangerous.

And with each one, she came back. Piece by piece, like a house being refurnished room by room — and Quentin thought, with a pang, of the battle inside his own mind, of the Wolf burning through the rooms of him, and how this was the opposite of that, this was a house being *built*, and he was carrying the furniture.

The last vial in the roll was different. Smaller. The seal was written in a hand that wasn't hers — older, spidery, certain.

"What's this one?" Paloma asked.

Quentin turned it in his fingers. "It's not mine to explain," he said. "But I think — I think your grandmother left it. For after."

"For after what?"

"For after you paid the price." He held it out. "She knew. Paloma, she knew everything. She wrote the prophecy, she built the trap, she raised you for it — and then she left you this, because she couldn't stop being your grandmother even while she was making you a weapon."

Paloma took it with both hands, the way one holds something that might be a gift or a bomb.

She pressed it to her forehead.

Her breath caught. Her eyes filled — not with grief, Quentin realized, but with recognition. When she lowered the vial, she was smiling, and crying, and laughing, all at once, and it was the most complete he had ever seen her.

"She sang to me," Paloma whispered. "When I was very small. Before — before I took it all away. She sang a song about a road that goes on walking after you stop. And she — oh, Quentin, she *apologized*. Not for the prophecy. For the loneliness. She said, *I'm sorry I couldn't give you a family that stayed. So I'm giving you ones that choose.*" She pressed the vial to her chest. "She knew I'd be empty. And she knew you'd all be here anyway."

Quentin's throat worked. "The inn. Marta. The smugglers. Me."

"The family that chooses," Paloma said softly. "She was right. She was insufferably, maddeningly right."

* * *

It was nearly dusk when she asked the question he had been dreading.

She asked it gently, which made it worse. She had catalogued his silences for weeks; now, with her memories restored, she read him like her own ledger.

"The hoard I gave the mountain," she said. "It needed everything. A kingdom's worth. You told me that — it's in the ford memory, I can feel you telling me, you were terrified." She paused. "Everything includes the first thing. Doesn't it. The one I told you about. The one I stole before I ever stole from anyone else."

Quentin said nothing. Which was an answer.

"My childhood," she said. "It's gone."

"The mountain keeps what it's given," Quentin said. "Your grandmother's last page says so. *What you give, the mountain keeps.* It's not destroyed, Paloma. It's — held. Like wax in stone. Maybe someday—"

"Don't." Her voice cracked, and she steadied it with visible effort. "Don't give me maybe. I've lived on maybe my whole life. I know how to survive on it and I know what it costs." She looked out through the broken roof at the first stars. "I gave it away. I'd give it again. That little girl and her terrible years — she's the reason I could do this. She's the reason the Wolf is dead. Let her be the price. Let her have *meant* something."

"She meant everything," Quentin said.

"Then that's enough." She was quiet a moment. Then, with the crooked grin surfacing through the tears: "Although I confess I'll miss knowing what I was like at seven. I was probably insufferable."

"You labeled your stolen goods," Quentin said. "I'm betting on insufferable."

She laughed, and leaned into him, and he put his arm around her, and they sat like that in the warm humming shrine while the stars came out over Thornfell — two hollowed people, half-full, leaning on each other like misprinted books holding each other's pages in.

"Quentin," she said, after a long time.

"Mm."

"The memories you gave back. The rain, the inn, the ford." She turned to look up at him. "They're mine again. But they're also yours now — you carried them, you know them, you were there for most of them. Which makes you the only person alive who remembers me *twice*."

"I hadn't thought of it that way."

"I had. It's what I do." Her eyes were bright and tired and utterly, devastatingly fond. "So here's my question, heir-to-nothing, keeper-of-my-rain. What happens tomorrow?"

Quentin looked south, toward the capital, where a hollow throne waited in a hollow palace, and a court of faces beginning, slowly, to remember. He looked at the road below the shrine — the smuggler's road, her road, the one she had walked alone for twenty years.

"Tomorrow," he said, "we go down the mountain. And I think — I think we find out who remembers us anyway."

Paloma smiled, closed her eyes, and slept — truly slept, unburdened, for the first time in two lifetimes.

And far below them, at the foot of the mountain where the stone held memory like wax, something in the shrine's oldest wall gave the faintest, faintest shiver — as if, deep in the stone, something given had just begun, very slowly, to be kept.
