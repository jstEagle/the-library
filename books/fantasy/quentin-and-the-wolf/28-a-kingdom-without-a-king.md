## 28. A Kingdom Without a King

The crown weighed less than Quentin had imagined.

That was the first thing he noticed, standing in the antechamber behind the throne room while a chamberlain with trembling hands held it out to him on a cushion of moth-eaten velvet. He had spent his whole childhood believing the crown must be heavy — that was why kings walked slowly, he'd decided at seven years old. Why his father's shoulders curved forward like a man carrying water. But the thing itself was light as a bird's skull, all gold leaf and hollow places, and it was only when you put it on that you understood where the weight actually lived.

He did not put it on. Not yet.

"Your Majesty," said the chamberlain, whose name was Odo and who had once, Quentin remembered in fragments, been caught stealing candied plums from the royal kitchens by Quentin's mother, who had laughed instead of scolding and given him two more. "The Council awaits your word."

"The Council can wait a little longer," Quentin said. "It's waited eleven years for me. It can survive another hour."

Odo's mouth twitched — the ghost of the plum-thief's humor still buried somewhere under all that courtly dread — and then smoothed itself flat again, because this was the palace, and nothing here was allowed to be funny before noon.

Quentin turned to the window. Below him, the capital sprawled in its festival colors, though the festival of forgetting had ended three days ago and no one had thought to take down the banners. They hung there, faded and cheerful, celebrating an absence nobody could name. Somewhere in that crowd were people who had gotten their memories back — Paloma had seen to that, or would soon; she was downstairs even now in the memory-vaults, elbow-deep in the crown's stolen hoard, unsealing vials with the particular violence of a woman who had spent her whole life being careful and was enjoying one week of not having to be.

He missed her. That was the strange part. She was forty feet below him and he missed her.

* * *

The throne room, when he finally entered it, smelled of lilies.

Someone had arranged them — hundreds of them, white and heavy-headed, spilling from every vase and bracket along the walls — because lilies were the flower of mourning, and the court had decided, in its infinite wisdom, that what the kingdom needed was a funeral. Not for the Wolf-King, who had worn his father's face for a century and been dragged from it screaming into the binding stone. For the *idea* of him. For the shape of a king that everyone had loved and no one could remember.

They mourned a man they could not describe. Quentin stood at the doors and watched them do it and felt something crack quietly in his chest, because he was the only person in that room who knew what his father's laugh sounded like, and he only knew it secondhand, through Paloma, through a vial, through a memory that had never been his.

"Highness." Lord Casserly approached, silver-haired, immaculate, the sort of man whose collar had probably never known a wrinkle. He bowed precisely the correct amount. "The Council has prepared the proclamation."

"I'm sure it has."

"You will understand," Casserly went on, with the gentle condescension of a man explaining weather to a child, "that certain... irregularities attend your claim."

"My claim." Quentin let the words sit in the air between them like something dead. "You mean the fact that none of you remember me."

"We remember *of* you," Casserly corrected smoothly. "There are records. A birth announcement. Portraits." He gestured, and indeed, on the far wall, hung a portrait of a boy of nine in miniature armor, holding a falcon, painted by someone who had clearly never met the boy and made him look like a small disapproving duke. "The records are quite thorough."

"The records are thorough," Quentin agreed. "The records say I died of marsh fever at twelve. I read them last night. There's a very moving epitaph. Someone composed an elegy. It scans beautifully."

A flicker of discomfort crossed Casserly's face — the first honest thing he'd done all morning. "The archives contain errors. The Wolf's reign was—"

"A hundred years long," Quentin finished. "Yes. I know. I was there for the end of it. So were you, my lord. You knelt to it. You smiled at it. You told it your secrets, and it ate them, and then you came back the next day and told it more, because the alternative was noticing that your king had cold hands and no reflection and a smile like a door opening onto nothing." He let that land. He watched Casserly's jaw tighten. "I'm not saying it to wound you. I'm saying it because you're all standing here wondering whether I'm going to make you pay for it, and I need you to know that I noticed, and that I have decided — *provisionally* — not to care."

The chamber had gone very quiet. Somewhere near the back, a young courtier dropped a fan and did not pick it up.

"That is," Quentin added, "as long as no one tells me how to grieve my father. You've filled my throne room with lilies for a man whose face you borrowed and whose name you can't pronounce. I'll thank you to leave the actual mourning to the people who knew what they lost."

He hadn't meant to say any of that. He had planned, on the walk over, to be gracious. Gracious was easier. Gracious was what heirs did. But the room had smelled of lilies and something in him had simply come loose, the way a knot comes loose when you pull the wrong thread, and out had come eleven years of exile in a single ungracious breath.

Casserly bowed again, deeper this time, and there was fear in it now, real fear, and Quentin understood with a sinking feeling that he had just made everything harder.

* * *

Here is what no one tells you about coming home: the home comes back wrong.

Quentin walked the corridors that afternoon with a guard of six soldiers who had volunteered — actually volunteered, which unsettled him more than conscription would have — and tried to find himself in the architecture. The gallery where he'd raced his tutor's dog. The stairwell with the chipped step his mother always tripped on, cursing in her borderlands accent, delighting the servants. The window seat where he'd hidden with a book about smugglers, of all things, dreaming of roads.

All of it was there. All of it was *correct*. And all of it belonged to a boy in a portrait, a stranger with his face, and Quentin moved through his own childhood like a guest in a museum of himself, reading the placards.

Except —

Except the chipped step had been repaired. Except the window seat had been reupholstered in a color his mother would have hated. Except the dog's water bowl was gone from the kitchen, and when he asked old Marta, who had been cook since before the Wolf, whether she remembered the dog, she looked at him with wide wet eyes and said, "What dog, highness?" and then wept without knowing why, and he had to hold her hands and tell her it didn't matter, it didn't matter, some things the mountain keeps and some things it keeps *from* us, and neither of them believed it didn't matter, so they drank tea instead and said nothing.

Paloma found him there, eventually, sitting in the kitchen among the flour sacks like a displaced cat.

"You look terrible," she said warmly, dropping onto the bench beside him. She smelled of vault dust and lamp oil and, faintly, of lilies, because someone had stuffed a sprig in her pocket as thanks and she hadn't noticed yet. "Have you eaten?"

"I don't know. Possibly. There was a tray."

"There was a tray," she repeated, with the tone of a woman cataloguing evidence. "And did the tray survive?"

"Some of it."

"Then you haven't eaten." She produced, from somewhere in her coat — the coat had pockets like a conjurer's hat — a paper bundle of fried dough from a stall by the south gate, still warm, still greasy, still better than anything the palace kitchens had made in a century, if Marta's tea was anything to go by. "Eat. Then tell me what the vultures said."

"They want me crowned within the fortnight. Before the people start asking questions."

"What questions?"

"Why the heir appeared out of nowhere with a thief on his arm and no memories anyone can verify." He bit into the fried dough. It was, genuinely, excellent. "Casserly called you 'the irregularity.'"

"Did he." Paloma's eyes took on a light he had learned to find either charming or alarming depending on context. Currently: both. "I've been called worse by better-dressed men. What did you say?"

"I said I'd consider it."

"And did you mean it?"

He set down the food. Outside the kitchen window, evening was starting to gather over the city, and the first lamps were being lit, and somewhere a child was shrieking with laughter in a way that no child had shrieked in this palace for a hundred years — the festival crowds still lingering, still half-drunk on returned memories, telling each other stories they'd only just gotten back.

"No," he said honestly. "I don't know what I meant. Paloma — they're afraid of me. Did you see their faces? Half of them flinched when I entered. Not because I threatened them. Because I *remembered*. I'm the only person in that entire room who knows what the last century actually was, and they can feel it, the way animals feel a storm. They want a king who doesn't know anything. A king they can arrange around, the way you arrange furniture. And I—" he laughed, without much humor in it, "—I sat on the steps of the dais for an hour after they left, just looking at the throne. It's smaller than I remembered. Everything is. And I kept thinking: my father sat there. My father sat there and the Wolf wore him like a coat, and the whole court watched and applauded, and now they want to put *me* in the same chair and call it healing."

Paloma was quiet for a moment. She was good at quiet in a way that most people weren't; her silences had floors in them, places to stand.

"When I was fourteen," she said finally, "I stole my first memory off a caravan master on the Salt Stair road. And afterward I felt awful about it, properly awful, for about a day. And then I realized something that changed how I did everything after: the worst part wasn't taking it. The worst part was that he didn't miss it. He walked on whistling. Whatever I'd taken, it hadn't been load-bearing." She turned to look at him. "These people aren't afraid of you because you remember. They're afraid of you because you're load-bearing. Because if you stand up there and say *this is what happened*, then it happened, and they have to be people who lived through it. A king who remembers makes everyone else's forgetting into a choice. Do you see? You're not the storm, Quentin. You're the lightning that shows them the shape of their own house."

He stared at her. "Has anyone ever told you that you should write this down?"

"Constantly. I ignore them. Writing things down is how prophecies happen." She grinned. "Grandmother's rules."

* * *

Three days later, the Council presented its terms.

They arrived, as such things do, on expensive paper with too many seals, drafted by four different committees and softened by a fifth until the original meaning had been polished away entirely. Quentin read it twice in the council chamber while twenty great lords and ladies watched him with the fixed attention of people watching a lit fuse.

The terms were generous. That was the trap of them. They offered him the crown, the title, the palaces, the armies — everything his father had held. In exchange, they asked for almost nothing. A council of regency for the first year, "to ensure stability." A general amnesty, "to bind the nation's wounds." And one clause, small, tucked near the bottom in letters only slightly smaller than the rest, that read:

*His Majesty shall refrain from public inquiry into matters pertaining to the previous reign.*

Refrain from public inquiry. Do not ask. Do not look. Do not remember out loud.

There it was. The price of the chair. Not gold, not blood — just silence, dressed up in the language of mercy. Bind the wounds by agreeing never to learn where the knife had gone in.

Quentin looked up from the page. Twenty faces looked back at him, and he searched them, one by one, for a single ally, and found — this was the part that hurt, this was the part that would keep hurting for weeks — that he recognized several of them. From the hoard. From the vials Paloma had unsealed, from the memories the crown had stored like wine in a cellar. He knew these people. He knew their kindnesses and their cowardices, the letter one lord had written begging the Wolf to spare his village, the bribe another lady had taken, the night a young Casserly had gotten drunk and wept and sworn to his dead friend that he would do better. Quentin knew them better than they knew themselves, and they knew him not at all, and the asymmetry sat in the room like a third party at the table.

"May I have until morning?" he asked.

The relief in the chamber was audible. Of course, they said. Of course. Take the night. Rest, highness. You've had a long journey.

They thought they were giving him time to accept gracefully. What they were actually doing was handing him a night alone with the biggest decision of his life and no one to make it with — except that he did have someone, except that at midnight there was a scratch at the service door of his chambers, and Paloma came in smelling of vault dust again, carrying not fried dough this time but a lantern and a stack of ledger pages, and the look on her face was the look of a woman who had found something.

"You're hesitating," she said, sitting down on the floor beside his fire like it was her fire, which by now it arguably was.

"I'm considering."

"You're hesitating. Good. Hesitation means you've noticed the trap." She patted the pages. "I've been reading the Council's private correspondence. Old habit. Don't look at me like that — half of it was in the archive vaults you now technically own, and the other half a clerk sold me for two coins and a compliment."

"That's theft."

"That's *archival retrieval* with flexible consent. Listen." She spread the pages across the hearthstones. "Your lords are frightened, yes. But they're also split. Look — here's Casserly writing to his son: *if the boy is truly the heir, God help us all, and if he is not, God help us more.* Here's Lady Vess, who's been lobbying the others to delay the coronation indefinitely. And here—" she held up a page, "—here's the interesting one. A petition, unsigned, circulating among the younger courtiers and the guildmasters. Forty names so far. Want to guess what it says?"

Quentin guessed. He was wrong. It was better than he'd guessed.

*We the undersigned, having recovered what was taken from us, do not ask for a king who remembers nothing. We ask for one who remembers everything, and stays anyway.*

"They're not afraid of you," Paloma said softly. "The ones at the top are. The ones with the most to lose. But underneath them — the clerks, the guildsmen, the soldiers, the people who got their memories back this week and cried in the streets holding strangers — they're waiting for you. They don't want a blank king they can arrange. They want a witness on the throne. You've been thinking the court's fear is the country's fear. It isn't. The court is twenty people. The country is everyone who whistled past the Wolf for a hundred years and hated themselves for it and would follow anyone brave enough to say *I saw it too.*"

Quentin sat back. The fire cracked. Somewhere below, the city murmured through the open window, alive in a way he was only beginning to trust.

"So what do I do?" he asked. "Refuse the terms and they declare me unfit. Accept them and I'm a king under gag. Either way I inherit a kingdom built on pretending."

"You do what thieves do," Paloma said, gathering up her pages with a thief's quick economy. "When the front door's watched, you use the smuggler roads. Sign nothing tonight. Crown yourself on your own terms — not theirs. And when they ask what gives you the right..." She smiled, and it was the same smile she'd worn on the Salt Stair road a lifetime ago, back when he'd been a gaunt stranger hearing wolves in his own skull, and he felt it again, that vertiginous gratitude, that sense of standing at a crossroads that kept turning out to be a doorway. "...you tell them the truth. That a thief gave this kingdom back its memory, and the memory named you. Let them argue with that."

She stood, brushed off her coat, and paused at the door.

"Oh — and Quentin. One more thing. There's a delegation arriving tomorrow. From Thornfell. From the inn." Her grin went soft at the edges. "The smugglers heard you might be crowned, and they're coming to see it done proper. Maren says if you let the Council keep them out of the hall she will personally steal the ceremony."

He laughed — really laughed, the first time in days — and the laugh did something to the weightless crown sitting on its cushion across the room, because suddenly it seemed less like a burden and more like a thing that might, with the right people standing close enough, be carryable after all.

Morning would bring the Council, the seals, the refusal dressed as negotiation. Morning would bring Maren's boots in the great hall and forty signatures and whatever came next, which was guaranteed to be chaos of the best kind.

But that was morning. For now there was the fire, and the pages, and the quiet, enormous knowledge that a kingdom without a king was not an empty thing after all.

It was a full thing, waiting.
