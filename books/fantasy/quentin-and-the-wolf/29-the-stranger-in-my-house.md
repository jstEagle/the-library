## 29. The Stranger in My House

The first thing Paloma learned about the inn called the Wayward Compass was that it had no compass.

"It's a joke," explained a broad-shouldered woman named Sefa, who was teaching her to knead bread on her third morning there. "Old Doss bought the sign from a shipwreck salvage lot and never noticed it was missing the needle until he'd already nailed it up over the door. He decided it was fate."

"Fate meaning what?"

"Meaning nobody who stays here knows where they're going." Sefa punched the dough with the easy violence of long practice. "Which is most people, if they're honest."

Paloma looked down at her own hands, white to the wrist with flour, and felt the truth of that settle somewhere beneath her ribs — in the hollow place she had stopped asking about, because every time she asked, someone's face did a complicated thing.

They told her she had been sick. That was the story everyone had agreed on, and they told it so smoothly, so many times, in so many overlapping voices, that she suspected it had been rehearsed. She had been sick. She had nearly died. Her memory was full of holes like a moth-eaten coat, and the kindest thing anyone could do was not tug at the threads.

She appreciated the kindness. She also noticed that when she walked into a room, conversations went quiet for half a heartbeat before resuming at double volume, as though everyone were determined to prove nothing had been interrupted.

And she noticed the man.

He arrived on her ninth morning, riding through the drizzle with his hood back and rain in his dark hair, and the whole inn erupted around him like a dropped beehive. Someone shouted his name — Quentin — and a boy of maybe twelve came sprinting out into the mud to hold his stirrup, which the man pretended not to notice while clearly being deeply moved by it.

Paloma watched from the kitchen doorway, a dish towel forgotten over her shoulder.

There was nothing remarkable about him, she told herself. He was thin, tired-looking, with shadows under his eyes and the particular careful posture of someone whose body had been broken recently and repaired imperfectly. He laughed at something the stable girl said and the laugh seemed to surprise him, like a door he'd forgotten was in the wall.

Her chest hurt.

Not dramatically. Not the way her ribs had ached those first days, when Sefa said she'd cracked two of them falling from a horse. This was a smaller pain, an old one, the kind you get from pressing on a bruise to check whether it still hurts.

It still hurts, said her chest. It always still hurts.

"Who is that?" she asked Sefa, who had come to stand beside her, also watching, wearing the expression of a woman supervising a pot she was afraid would boil over.

"Quentin," said Sefa. "Friend of the house." A pause. "You've met him. Before."

"I don't remember him."

"No," said Sefa gently. "I know."

* * *

The trouble began at supper, because of course it did. The Wayward Compass held perhaps thirty souls on a good night, and tonight was a very good night, and the long tables were crowded shoulder to shoulder, and the only seat left was directly across from Paloma.

Quentin sat down in it, said "evening," and then went very still, the way a man goes still when he has stepped on ice and heard it speak.

"Evening," said Paloma.

"You're—" He stopped. Started again. "Sefa says you're recovering well."

"Sefa says a lot of things. She told me the stew was mutton. I have my doubts." She lifted her spoon, considered the grey depths of the bowl, and put it down again. "Do you eat here often?"

"When I can." His hands were wrapped around a cup of cider he wasn't drinking. "It's a good house. Good people."

"They've been very kind to me. Everyone keeps saying that. 'The house has been kind to you, Paloma.' As if the house itself did it. As if the beams tucked blankets around me." She smiled, and saw something flicker across his face at the sight of it — something quick and wounded and quickly buried. "Did I do something wrong? You look at me like I'm a debt you can't pay."

"No," he said, too fast. Then, more carefully: "No. Nothing like that."

Across the table, down the bench, the entire inn had developed a sudden fascination with its own supper. Old Doss was studying the ceiling beams with scholarly intensity. The stable girl was whispering to the stirrup-boy, who whispered back, and both of them kept glancing over like spectators at a play where everyone knew the ending except two of the actors.

"We're all staring, aren't we," said Paloma.

"A bit," Quentin admitted.

"Why?"

He opened his mouth. Closed it. Down the table, Sefa cleared her throat in a way that contained an entire paragraph of warning.

"Because you're new," Quentin said finally, "and people are curious."

"People are curious about new people the way cats are curious about new furniture," said Paloma. "This isn't that. This is the way people watch a fire they're trying to decide whether to put out." She leaned forward. "Tell me the truth. Did we quarrel?"

"No."

"Did I owe you money?"

A ghost of a smile. "No."

"Did you owe *me* money?"

"That," said Quentin, "would have been a much simpler life for both of us."

It was such an odd answer, and he gave it with such a strange tenderness, that the ache in her chest turned over in its sleep. She pressed her hand flat against her sternum, briefly, without thinking, and caught him watching the gesture with an expression she could not read at all.

"Sorry," she said. "Habit. There's a — I don't know how to describe it. An ache. Right here. The healers say it's nothing. Cracked ribs knitting wrong, probably."

"The healers say that," Quentin repeated, in the flat voice of a man reciting lines he did not believe.

"Well. What else would they say?" She picked up her spoon again, mostly to have something to do with her hands. "It's strange, you know. Everything's strange. I wake up and I know things I shouldn't know — I knew this morning that the third floorboard by the washroom is loose, and I've never been up there. I know how to read river water. I know seventeen ways to pick a lock, which Sefa says is *not normal*, and I said, well, what's normal for me, then? And she got that face again. Everyone gets that face."

"What face?"

"The face you're making right now."

He made a visible effort to make a different face. It wasn't entirely successful.

"I keep thinking," Paloma went on, quieter now, because the strangeness deserved quiet, "that whoever I was before, she must have been somebody. Somebody must remember her. And I want to ask — I want to grab people by the collar and shake them and say, *tell me about her, tell me anything* — but everyone looks so sad when I ask that I've stopped. So instead I learn the floorboards and the bread and the names of the goats, and I pretend that's enough." She looked up. "Is that enough? For a person?"

Quentin set down his untouched cider. When he spoke, his voice was steady, but she had spent nine days among smugglers and liars and had gotten quite good at hearing the cost underneath a steady voice.

"I think," he said, "that a person is not what happened to them. I think a person is what they do next."

"That's a very convenient philosophy for people who've done terrible things."

"Yes," he agreed. "It is. I use it constantly."

The laugh escaped her before she could stop it — a real laugh, surprising her as much as his had surprised him in the yard that morning — and around the table, three separate people exhaled at once, as though they'd been holding their breath since the stew was served.

* * *

After supper there was music, because there was always music at the Compass on a good night, and Paloma discovered that her body remembered things her mind had lost. Her hands found the rhythm of the fiddle tune before she'd decided to clap along. When the dancing started in the cleared space by the hearth, her feet itched with steps she couldn't name.

"Do you dance?" Quentin asked. He had appeared at her elbow holding two cups of cider, one of which he offered her, and he asked it casually, the way a man asks a question he has rehearsed in front of a mirror.

"I don't know," said Paloma honestly. "Let's find out."

What they found out was: yes. Catastrophically yes. Some smuggler's reel swept them up within minutes, and her body knew every turn of it — the cross-step, the swing, the moment to duck under his arm — while her mind stood baffled at the window of herself, watching a stranger's muscles perform with perfect confidence. She laughed the whole way through, breathless, and Quentin laughed too, and for the length of one tune neither of them was carrying anything at all.

Then the tune ended, and they stood facing each other in the crowd, close enough that she could see the lamplight in his eyes, and the ache in her chest woke fully and *howled*.

"Oh," she said.

"What?" Alarm crossed his face. "Are you dizzy? Sefa said not to let you—"

"Stop talking." She pressed her hand to her sternum again. "It's doing the thing. The ache. Only worse. Why is it worse?"

"I don't—"

"It does this," said a voice behind them. Sefa had materialized with the inevitability of weather. "When she laughs hard. Or dances. Or certain other triggers we haven't fully catalogued." She fixed Quentin with a look of profound, pointed meaning. "Triggers."

"Triggers," Quentin repeated faintly.

"Some people," Sefa continued, steering Paloma gently toward a chair by the fire, "shouldn't be allowed near the patient until they've learned to keep their face on. Which you are famously bad at, love, no offense."

"None taken," said Quentin, in the tone of a man taking enormous offense.

Paloma sat by the fire and held her cup and watched the room spin pleasantly, and thought about triggers. Thought about how the ache came when she laughed, and when she danced, and when a certain thin, tired man with rain in his hair looked at her like —

Like what?

Like she was a book he'd read so many times the spine had gone soft, and someone had burned it, and he kept reaching for the shelf anyway.

"Quentin," she said.

He crouched beside her chair immediately — immediately, no hesitation, as though he'd been waiting all evening for his name in her mouth. That frightened her a little, that readiness. That devotion with nowhere to go.

"How do we know each other?" she asked. "Really. Not the polite answer. Not whatever story everyone agreed on. How do we *know* each other?"

The fire popped. Somewhere behind them, the fiddle started a slower tune.

Quentin was quiet for a long time. Long enough that Sefa drifted closer without appearing to move. Long enough that Paloma understood the answer mattered enormously, and that whatever he chose to say would be a door he could never quite close again.

"You saved my life," he said at last. "More than once. And I failed to save yours, once, in the way that counted. And I've been—" His voice caught; he mastered it. "I've been coming here every few days to sit across from you and practice being a stranger. Because it's what you need. And I'm apparently very bad at it."

"Terrible," confirmed Sefa.

"World-class," said the stable girl, from somewhere.

Paloma looked at him — at the shadows under his eyes, at the careful hands, at the door-in-the-wall laugh — and the ache in her chest resolved itself, suddenly, into something she could name. It wasn't grief exactly. It was homesickness. The specific homesickness of standing inside a house you used to live in, with all the furniture rearranged, knowing the rooms by their bones.

"I don't remember you," she said softly. "But I think some part of me never got the message. I think she's still in there somewhere, waiting for you to say something only you would say."

Quentin's composure — the court-trained, exile-hardened composure that had survived palaces and wolves and burning gardens — cracked clean down the middle.

"Don't," he managed.

"Don't what?"

"Don't say things like that in that voice. Please." He laughed, wetly, hopelessly. "I practiced. I had a whole plan. I was going to be mysterious and undemanding and let you come to your own conclusions over the course of months, like a reasonable man, and instead you've dismantled me in a week with bread flour on your sleeves."

"Flour," said Paloma, looking down at her sleeves, where indeed the day's baking had left its evidence. "Well. Every siege needs a weapon."

That startled a real laugh out of him — the door-in-the-wall laugh, wide open now — and around the inn, the watchers gave up all pretense and simply grinned at the pair of them like a family watching a stubborn fire finally catch.

* * *

Later, when the fire had burned low and the inn had gone quiet, Paloma lay in her narrow bed above the kitchen and listened to the building settle. Below her, faintly, she heard Quentin and Sefa talking in the common room, voices pitched low, words indistinct except for one phrase that rose through the floorboards like smoke:

"—she asked about the ache, Sef. What am I supposed to do? Lie to her forever?"

"You're supposed to let her ask twice," said Sefa. "Then tell her true. That was the agreement."

"And if she asks tomorrow?"

Another pause. Then Sefa's voice, gentler than Paloma had ever heard it:

"Then you'll finally get to tell her about the ford, won't you. And the promise. And everything you've been chewing on like a man chewing stones." A creak of a chair. "Go to bed, your highness. You look dreadful."

Paloma stared at the ceiling in the dark.

*Your highness.*

She turned the word over, and over, and it fit nowhere in anything she knew, and yet her heart hammered against her ribs like a fist on a locked door, like something on the other side shouting *I know that word, I know that word, let me out.*

Tomorrow, she promised the darkness. Tomorrow I'll ask twice.

Downstairs, a chair scraped, footsteps climbed, paused outside her door for a long moment — she heard him breathing there, or imagined she did — and moved on.

In the hollow place beneath her ribs, something turned over in its sleep and, for the first time in nine days, dreamed.
