## 30. Remembering Forward

### Quentin

The inn at Hollowmere had a rule about evenings: no one ate alone unless they wanted to. It was not written anywhere. It was simply enforced by Marta the cook, who would appear beside any solitary table with a bowl of stew and an expression that made solitude feel like a bureaucratic error.

Quentin had eaten alone for most of his life. At court, dining was a performance — forty courses, a hundred eyes, every fork lifted in choreography. He had been lonelier at those tables than he had ever been on the road.

Here, he sat across from Paloma every night, and told her their story.

It had started by accident, three weeks after she woke hollow. She had asked him — politely, the way you ask a stranger the time — why his voice sounded familiar.

"You've heard it before," he'd said. "Many times."

"Where?"

And so he began. Not all at once. A story is a heavy thing to hand someone all in one piece; you give it to them the way smugglers move cargo, in small loads over bad roads, a little each night.

Tonight's load was the ledger.

"There was a book," he said, turning his cup slowly on the table. "Old paper, older ink. Written a hundred years before either of us drew breath. And it knew your name."

Paloma frowned. She did this when she was thinking — a small crease between her brows, like a door opening onto a room he couldn't see into anymore. "That's not how books work."

"This one did. Your grandmother wrote it. She wrote that a Thackeray thief would come to Thornfell on a certain night and find a certain vault. She wrote it before your mother was born. Before your mother's mother was born." He paused. "She wrote you, Paloma. Decades before you existed."

The crease deepened. "That sounds less like a prophecy and more like a very long con."

He laughed — really laughed, the kind that surprised him coming out. "You know what's strange? You said almost exactly that, the first time I told you. Word for word. 'A very long con.'"

"I did?" She looked pleased, then puzzled at being pleased. "Why does that make me happy? Knowing I'm consistent?"

"Because it means the person I knew is still in there," Quentin said, before he could stop himself. "Underneath everything they took. The shape of you is still the shape of you."

Marta chose that moment to arrive with bread, which was fortunate, because Paloma had gone quiet in a way that made him want to fill the silence with something, anything, and bread was better than babbling.

She tore a piece and held it without eating. "Tell me what happened next," she said. "In the story. After the ledger."

"After the ledger, there was a man waiting at a crossroads who looked like death had sent him back with complaints about the service."

"That would be you."

"That would be me," Quentin agreed. "I was hearing the Wolf in my skull by then. It had eaten half of me — my childhood first, then my mother's face, then whole years I can't name because, well." He gestured vaguely at his own head. "I saw you coming down the smuggler road with a stolen ledger under your arm and I thought: there's the thief from the prophecy. Either she saves us or kills us. Possibly both."

"And which was it?"

"Both," he said. "Definitely both."

* * *

### Paloma

She fell for him the second time on a Tuesday.

The first time, she didn't count, because she hadn't known it was happening — she only knew that somewhere around the third week of stories, she had begun waking early on purpose, just to be awake sooner, closer to evening, closer to the next installment. That wasn't falling. That was appetite. She understood appetite. Appetite she could manage.

But Tuesday — Tuesday was different.

He was telling her about the ford. The ambush. The memory-eater that had torn his childhood name out of him at the riverbank, and how she — the other her, the her with all her pieces still in place — had knelt in the shallows and said, *I'll remember for you.*

"She made me a promise," Quentin said. His voice did something careful here, the way a man walks carefully over ice. "She said she'd carry it alongside her own memories until she could give it back. And she did carry it. Through the capital, through the burning gardens, through everything. Right up until—" He stopped.

"Until she poured it all into the mountain," Paloma finished. She knew this part. She always knew how the stories ended, even the ones she'd never heard, because every story she'd heard ended the same way: with a woman giving away everything she had ever kept.

"Yes."

"So the promise failed."

"No," Quentin said, sharply enough that two smugglers at the next table glanced over. He lowered his voice. "No. The promise didn't fail. Promises aren't contracts. She *kept* it — she kept it perfectly, right up until keeping it cost more than she had. That's not failure. That's just arithmetic being cruel."

Paloma turned her cup the way he turned his, an unconscious theft, borrowing his gestures one at a time. "You loved her," she said. It wasn't quite a question.

"I love her," he corrected. "Present tense. She's sitting right across from me."

"But she's not *her*. I'm not—"

"You're the same person missing some pages," he said. "I've read the book twice now. I'd know it anywhere."

And that was when it happened — the second time. Not a lightning strike, nothing so dramatic. Just a quiet settling, like snow finding its place on a branch, like something inside her saying: *oh. There you are.* She looked at this gaunt, half-hollowed, stubbornly hopeful man who had spent months telling a stranger their own story, night after night, asking nothing, and she thought: *if I had a heart, this is what it would be doing.*

"I have a question," she said, because feelings were dangerous and questions were safe. "In the story. When she gave up her hoard. Did she hesitate?"

"No," Quentin said. "That's the thing. She didn't hesitate at all. I begged her to. I offered myself instead. We argued about it like merchants haggling over a horse — 'take my memory,' 'don't be stupid, yours is worth more' — honestly, it was the least romantic argument in the history of love. And then she just... smiled at me, and let go."

"What did she say? Right before?"

He was quiet a long moment. The fire popped. Somewhere upstairs, a smuggler snored like a landslide.

"She said, 'Keep the ones I gave you. Those were never stolen.'"

Paloma felt something tighten behind her ribs — the phantom ache, the chest-pain she got sometimes around him, around the inn, around Marta's stew, around all these people who treated her as though she were someone worth knowing despite having no reason to. "What did she give you? What did I give you?"

"A memory of my mother's face," Quentin said softly. "The one the Wolf took. She kept it safe for weeks and handed it back to me at the shrine, right before the end. It's the only reason I can still see her smile." His eyes were bright and wet in the firelight. "So no, Paloma. The promise didn't fail. Half of it came home. And the other half—" he reached across the table and touched her hand, briefly, gently, like touching something breakable that he refused to treat as broken, "—the other half is learning to fall in love with me all over again. Which, I'm told, takes about six weeks."

"It's been five," Paloma said, and then went scarlet, because she had not meant to say that out loud.

Quentin grinned like a man who had just won a war.

### Quentin

The trouble with telling someone your shared history every night is that you start living for the telling, and the days in between go thin as broth.

Quentin noticed it in himself first. He caught himself hoarding moments — saving up small things to tell her later. The way Sef the smuggler cheated at cards and lost anyway. The exact color of the frost on the chapel roof. A joke Marta told that wasn't actually funny but made everyone laugh because Marta had once fed the entire pass through a famine winter and earned the right to be unfunny forever.

He was building her a life out of anecdotes, brick by brick, and calling it memory.

One night, near midwinter, she stopped him mid-story.

"The other you," she said. "The one from before. Was she happy?"

He considered lying. He considered the truth. He settled somewhere honest between them. "She was lonely. Terribly, competently lonely. She was the best thief on the roads and she never kept a single thing she stole, because keeping things meant wanting them, and wanting things meant losing them." He watched her face do the thing it did — the crease, the door. "And then she met a half-eaten prince at a crossroads and accidentally acquired a family of smugglers, and I think the last year of her life was the happiest she'd ever had. Even the parts where we were running for our lives."

"Especially those parts, probably," Paloma said. "People are like that. Danger makes belonging easier to notice."

"When did you get wise?"

"I'm not wise. I'm empty. Empty rooms have excellent acoustics." She said it lightly, but he heard the true thing underneath, as he always did. "Quentin. Why do you tell me the sad parts? You could skip them. Give me the good version. Festivals and snowfalls and witty banter."

"Because you'd catch me skipping," he said. "You always catch me. Last week I softened the part where I cried at the ford and you called me a liar to my face."

"You did cry at the ford."

"I absolutely did. My childhood name had just been ripped out of my head. Crying was the reasonable response." He leaned forward. "I tell you the sad parts because they're yours too. I'm not going to curate you to yourself. You deserve the whole ugly, wonderful truth, delivered nightly, with bread."

Paloma was silent. Then, quietly: "My grandmother erased herself. Same way I did. Gave everything to the mountain. There's a page — I found it last month — she wrote, *what you give, the mountain keeps.*"

"I remember. You showed me."

"Do you think she's in there? In the stone? Do you think the mountain kept her?"

"I think," Quentin said carefully, "that the mountain returned a memory of you. A real one. Young Paloma, before the erasure. Stone doesn't invent. So if it kept her..." He shrugged. "Maybe the mountain is full of everyone who ever gave themselves away. Maybe it's not a mountain at all. Maybe it's a family that remembers."

Paloma looked at him for a long time. Then she laughed — a startled, rusty sound, like a door opening in a house long shut. "That's the worst theology I've ever heard."

"I'm an exiled prince raised by wolves-adjacent forces. My theology is mostly improvised."

"No, it's—" She shook her head, still laughing, wiping her eyes. "It's exactly the kind of thing she would have said. The other me. I can tell, because it makes me want to argue and kiss you at the same time, and apparently that's her signature feeling."

Quentin's heart performed a maneuver not approved by any physician. "Is it also yours?"

"Ask me again tomorrow," Paloma said. "I'll have heard another chapter by then. Ask me every day. Keep asking until the answer sticks."

### Quentin

On the first night of the thaw, the whole inn crowded into the common room — smugglers, courtiers-in-exile, Marta, Sef, old Brannic who claimed to have smuggled a dragon egg once and produced increasingly detailed evidence nobody wanted — and Quentin stood up on a bench and told the story to all of them, the whole arc, crossroads to coronation, while Paloma sat in the corner pretending not to be moved and fooling no one.

When he got to the part where she poured her hoard into the binding, the room went so quiet you could hear the fire thinking.

"And then," he said, "she collapsed, hollowed to the walls, and I caught her. And I have spent every night since handing her back the story, piece by piece, because a person is not their memories. A person is whoever keeps loving them after the memories are gone."

He stepped down. Walked to her corner. Held out his hand.

"Paloma Thackeray," he said, in front of everyone, because some things should be witnessed, "will you let me keep telling you our story? Every night. For as long as it takes. And when you're ready — not before, not after — will you write the next chapter with me? Not remembered. Made new."

The room held its breath.

Paloma took his hand. "Only if you stop skipping the crying parts."

"I would never."

"You skipped the crying part tonight. At the coronation. You definitely cried at your own coronation."

"...I was wearing a very heavy crown," Quentin said with dignity, as the inn roared with laughter, "and there was smoke from the festival torches—"

"He cried," Sef confirmed solemnly. "Big wet tears. Got some on my boot."

And Paloma laughed, and pulled him down into the seat beside her, and leaned her head against his shoulder — a gesture with no memory behind it at all, chosen fresh, authored rather than inherited, hers.

Later, much later, when the fire had burned to embers and the inn slept, she found him on the porch looking at the mountains. The shrine peak was visible from here on clear nights, a dark shape holding its secrets.

"The stone whispered to me today," she said quietly.

Everything in Quentin went still. "What did it say?"

"Not words. More like... a hunger. Something old, asking if I wanted anything back. Anything at all." She wrapped her arms around herself. "It sounded patient, Quentin. Like it could wait years."

He took her hand. Below them, the valley slept; above them, the mountain kept whatever the mountain kept.

"Then we'll teach it differently," he said. "Starting tomorrow."

"Tomorrow," Paloma agreed — and neither of them saw, far up the black slope, a faint light kindle in the shrine stone, like an eye remembering how to open.
