## 31. The Grandmother's Last Page

The inn at Millbrook smelled of woodsmoke and burnt honey cakes, which was what happened when you let Tam o' the Ford anywhere near an oven and told him it was for a celebration. Paloma sat at the corner table with the ledger open before her, a candle guttering at her elbow, and tried for the hundredth time to make sense of the last page.

It didn't make sense. That was the trouble.

The binding rite had worked. The Wolf was in the stone, or mostly in the stone, or as much in the stone as anything could be that had spent a century learning how to be everywhere else. Quentin was alive. The kingdom was wobbling toward something like sense. And Paloma Thackeray, who had once been able to hold four hundred stolen memories behind her eyes without dropping so much as a lullaby, now sometimes forgot which hand she wrote with.

The ledger lay open to its final written page, the one her grandmother had finished on a night a hundred years ago, in ink gone brown as old tea. Paloma had read it so many times the words had worn grooves in her:

*The thief must come willing. The thief must give all. So ends the prophecy of the Thackeray line.*

And then nothing. Blank vellum. A hundred years of nothing.

"It's not nothing," she muttered.

"What's not nothing?" Tam asked, appearing at her shoulder with a plate of honey cakes that were, generously speaking, charcoal with ambitions.

"The page." She tapped it. "Paper remembers, Tam. Everyone knows that. Paper holds the shape of what was pressed against it. If there'd ever been another page here, there'd be a ghost of it — an impression, a shadow of ink."

Tam squinted at the blank vellum the way he squinted at everything, which was with the deep suspicion of a man who had once been cheated by a fishmonger and never recovered. "Looks like paper to me."

"Because you're looking with your eyes." She said it gently. She liked Tam. He was one of the smugglers who had stood beside Quentin at the coronation, still wearing his good coat, which was his only coat, which made it both good and bad simultaneously. "Give me your knife."

He gave it over without question, which would have been unthinkable a year ago, when Paloma Thackeray trusted no one enough to hand them her back, let alone ask them for sharp objects. She slid the flat of the blade along the inner spine of the ledger's binding, where the stitching had gone loose from age and handling.

Something crackled.

She froze. Then, with the same care she'd once used lifting memories out of sleeping merchants — slow, patient, respectful of the architecture of things — she teased the stitching apart with the knife tip.

A second page slid out. Folded eight times, pressed flat against the spine, thin as a moth's wing. It had been there the whole time. Through the vault, through the ritual, through everything, her grandmother had been sitting in the dark of the binding like a held breath, waiting for someone to think to look *inside* the book instead of merely at it.

Paloma laughed. It came out wobbly.

"Found something?" Tam asked.

"I found my grandmother," Paloma said, "hiding in the furniture."

* * *

She took it upstairs. Not because she wanted privacy exactly, but because some pages deserve to be read sitting down, and this one, she already knew, was going to put her on the floor.

Her room at the inn was small and honest: a bed, a window, a chipped basin, a chair Quentin had repaired three times because he couldn't stop himself from fixing things he loved. She lit two candles, unfolded the page on the blanket, and read.

The handwriting was her grandmother's. Of course it was. She knew it the way you know your own reflection — the slight leftward slant, the letters that crowded each other like people huddled against cold. She had spent months with the ledger's script, deciphering the prophecy, learning its rhythms. But this was different. This wasn't formal. This wasn't a document composed for posterity.

This was a letter.

*My darling girl,* it began, and Paloma's chest went tight, because her grandmother had died when she was six, and six-year-olds don't remember much, and Paloma remembered even less than most, and yet —

*I have watched you sleep tonight. You are four years old and you fell out of the apple tree again and you cried and then you laughed about it before I even reached you, and I thought: this one. This is the one who will find this page. Not because of the prophecy. Because of the laughing.*

Paloma stopped reading. She pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes until colors bloomed.

She did not remember the apple tree. She did not remember falling out of it. She did not remember crying, or laughing, or being watched while she slept. Her childhood was a room with the furniture missing — she had known that since she was old enough to know it, since the first time someone said "do you remember when" and she had learned to smile and lie. She had assumed grief, or time, or the ordinary cruelty of memory had taken those years from her.

But she knew, now, with the terrible clarity of a woman holding a folded page in shaking hands, that nothing had been taken.

She had given it away. Or — she kept reading, and the truth arrived like cold water —

*Someone* had given it away. For her.

*If you are reading this, then the rite has been performed, and you are empty, and you are angry, and you have every right to be both.* Her grandmother's voice came off the page so clearly Paloma half-expected to hear the floorboards creak under her weight. *So let me tell you what I did, and why, and then you may be as furious with me as you like. I will wait. I am very patient. I'm dead.*

Despite herself — despite everything — Paloma snorted. Even from across a century, the old woman could land a joke.

*I was a thief too. Before I was a grandmother, before I was a prophetess, before I was any of the names they gave me, I was a girl on the Salt Stair road lifting memories out of travelers' heads for bread money. My mother taught me. Her mother taught her. We are a long line of women who take, and the taking hollows us, and we pretend it doesn't. You know this. You lived it.*

*I found the ledger in the Thornfell vault when I was younger than you are now. I read the prophecy. I saw my own name in it — not mine by name, but mine by blood, "the Thackeray thief," generation after generation listed like appointments. And I understood what the ledger truly was. Not a prediction. A machine. A trap built out of words, baited with fate, and every Thackeray woman who read it walked straight down its throat.*

*The rite requires a hoard equal to a kingdom. No thief can carry that honestly. So I made a choice, and it is the choice I am asking you to forgive.*

*I did not wait for you to gather a lifetime of theft. I gathered it for you. Every memory I had ever stolen — forty years of other people's sunsets and sorrows — I poured into the mountain's keeping, at the shrine, in the stone, the night before you were born. And then, because the stone does not accept payment on credit, I paid the balance with the only coin I had left that was truly mine.*

*I gave it my childhood. All of it. Every year of it. I do not remember my mother's face. I do not remember why I first stole, or what it felt like, or whether I was happy. I built you a life out of my own emptiness, my darling, and I would do it again before the ink dries on this sentence.*

Paloma set the page down. Her hands were not steady. She looked at the candles, at the wall, at the window where evening was going blue over the hills, and she understood — fully, finally, in the marrow-deep way that understanding arrives when it stops being information and becomes weight — what she was looking at.

Her emptiness had an inheritance. Her hollow had been dug by loving hands.

Quentin had figured it out weeks ago, back before the mountain, before the burning gardens — that her earliest theft had been her own childhood, that she had erased herself long ago. She had believed then that it was an accident of her trade, a wound she'd inflicted on herself out of cowardice or carelessness. Something broken in her. Something shameful.

It wasn't broken. It was *built*. Deliberately. By a woman who had looked at a prophecy naming her granddaughter as sacrifice and decided that if the mountain was going to demand a price, it could damn well be paid in advance, with interest, by someone who loved her.

"You absolute tyrant," Paloma whispered to the empty room, and her voice cracked in the middle of it, and she let it.

*You will want to hate me,* the letter went on. *Hate me, then. But hear the last of it first, because I nearly didn't write it, and it is the only thing I know that matters.*

*When I emptied myself at the shrine, I expected nothing. That is what giving means — you do not expect return; expectation is just theft with better manners. But the mountain surprised me. The stone took my memories the way soil takes rain — and some of it stayed. Not all. Not whole. But the shrine holds what it is given, and it gives back in fragments, in its own time, in its own ways. I heard my mother's voice in the rock a week later. I cannot explain it. I only know it is true.*

*So here is my last lesson, the one I paid more than a lifetime to learn:*

***What you give, the mountain keeps.***

*Not what you steal. Not what you hide. What you give freely comes back to you changed, but it comes back. The Wolf devours. The crown hoards. Thieves borrow. But the mountain — the mountain keeps. It is the only honest keeper in this world, and I have bound the worst thing in it inside the best thing in it, and I think — I hope — that was the whole point all along.*

*Go to the shrine, my darling. Go empty-handed. Go angry at me if you must. Put your hands on the stone and ask it for nothing, and see what it offers anyway.*

*And when you find something of yourself there — a laugh, an apple tree, a woman watching you sleep — do not grieve the years you lost. Grief is just love with nowhere to go. Send it somewhere. Send it forward. Be extravagant with what remains.*

*That is what I did. That is what I leave you. Not a prophecy. An inheritance.*

*All my love, past the end of everything,*

*Your grandmother,*

*Maren Thackeray,*

*who remembers you perfectly, wherever she is.*

* * *

Paloma read it three times. Then she did the sensible thing, which was to sit on the floor with her back against the bed and cry in the undignified, honking manner of a person who has been holding a door shut for a very long time and has finally been given permission to open it.

She cried for the apple tree she didn't remember. She cried for Maren Thackeray, who had walked up a mountain heavy with forty years of stolen lives and set them all down and asked for nothing. She cried for the long line of women — mothers teaching daughters, daughters teaching granddaughters — who had taken and taken and taken because the world left them nothing else to hold, and who had finally, at the end of the line, found the one thing worth giving everything away for.

And underneath the crying, quiet as a coal under ash, something else was kindling.

Anger, yes. Fury, even — the clean kind, the kind with a direction. Her grandmother had decided for her. Had shaped her life around a trap, raised her inside a design, let her walk into the vault blind. There would be words about that. There would be a long, one-sided argument conducted at a shrine, and Paloma intended to win it thoroughly despite the disadvantage of her opponent being dead.

But under the anger, warmer and stranger: relief. Because all her life she had felt the absence in herself and called it flaw. The hole where a childhood should be. The thief who kept nothing, not even herself. She had thought she was damaged goods.

She wasn't damaged. She was *paid*. Spent, deliberately, by someone who loved her enough to spend herself first — and who had left a receipt.

*What you give, the mountain keeps.*

Paloma wiped her face on her sleeve, which was what sleeves were for, and looked at the letter again in the candlelight. The phrase seemed to hum off the vellum, or maybe that was her. Maybe that was the small, stubborn part of her that had survived the rite — the part that wasn't memory at all but something deeper, something the mountain hadn't collected because it had never been hers to take.

Hope. Grandmother hadn't mentioned hope in the inventory. Sloppy bookkeeping.

Footsteps on the stairs — the particular uneven tread she'd know anywhere, one boot heel worn down because Quentin refused to replace boots that still had miles in them. The door opened and he stood there with two honey cakes on a plate, both visibly burnt, wearing the expression of a man delivering state secrets.

"Tam says you found something in the ledger," he said. "He also says you've been upstairs for an hour and the cakes are getting worse, which I didn't think was possible, but Tam is an innovator."

Paloma looked up at him — this gaunt, stubborn, half-remembered man who had carried her memories for her when she couldn't, who had promised to give them back one by one and had kept the promise, who was currently holding a plate of carbon like it was a peace offering to a foreign power.

"My grandmother erased herself," Paloma said. "Before I was born. To pay for the binding. She gave the mountain her whole childhood so I wouldn't have to give it mine." She held up the letter. "She hid this inside the spine. A hundred years, waiting."

Quentin set the plate down very carefully, the way he did when something mattered, and crossed the room, and read over her shoulder in silence. She watched his face do what his face did — the flicker at the hard parts, the softening at the end.

"*What you give, the mountain keeps,*" he read aloud, slowly. "Is that true? Do you believe it?"

"I don't know." Paloma looked at the window, at the dark hills beyond, somewhere out there the shrine with its patient stone. "She said she heard her mother's voice in the rock. Fragments. Given back in the mountain's own time." Her heart was doing something complicated. "Quentin. The rite was a month ago. I've been empty for a month."

He went very still. She could see him arriving where she had already arrived, the thought landing in him like a stone dropped down a well.

"And the mountain," he said quietly, "has had a month."

"A month," Paloma agreed. She stood up. Her legs were steadier than she expected. "To keep what I gave it."

She picked up the letter, folded it along its century-old creases, and tucked it inside her shirt, over her heart, where important documents belonged. Then she grabbed her coat off the peg, and the last of the unburnt honey cake for the road, because sentiment was no excuse for traveling hungry.

"Where are we going?" Quentin asked, already reaching for his own coat, because he was Quentin.

"To the shrine," Paloma said. "I have an argument to pick with my grandmother." She paused at the door, and the gentlest smile she'd owned in years tugged at her mouth. "And possibly an apple tree to reclaim."
