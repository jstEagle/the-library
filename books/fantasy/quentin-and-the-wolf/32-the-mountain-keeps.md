## 32. The Mountain Keeps

The shrine had not changed in the three days since the binding. Paloma had half expected it to — she had poured a lifetime of theft into that stone and watched the Wolf come apart like wet paper — but the mountain was indifferent to miracles. The snow still fell at its own pace. The wind still found every gap in Quentin's coat. And the stone still held her grandmother's handwriting, carved deep into the shrine's face, patient as a spider.

*What you give, the mountain keeps.*

She'd read it forty times now. She'd read it standing up, sitting down, lying flat on her back on the cold flagstones while Quentin hovered nearby pretending he wasn't hovering. She'd read it aloud, backward, and once very slowly through chattering teeth, as if the words might rearrange themselves out of shame.

They hadn't.

"Maybe it's a metaphor," she said.

Quentin, who had been whittling a stick into something that was either a spoon or an apology, looked up. "A metaphor."

"Grandmothers love metaphors. 'What you give, the mountain keeps' could mean, I don't know, wisdom. Character. A really excellent recipe for plum cordial."

"Paloma."

"It *could.*"

"You poured thirty years of stolen memories into a rock," Quentin said. "I watched you do it. I watched the mountain glow like a lantern and hum a note I felt in my teeth for two days afterward. That is not plum cordial."

She sighed and lay back down on the flagstones, because if she was going to be disappointed, she preferred to do it horizontally. Above her, the shrine's archway framed a sky the color of old pewter, and somewhere under the snow, under the stone, under everything — maybe nothing. Maybe her whole childhood, the parts she'd cut out of herself before she understood what cutting was, gone the way of the Wolf: dissolved, digested, kept by a mountain that had never promised to give anything back.

"Sorry," Quentin said, softer now. He sat beside her, close enough that his shoulder touched hers. "I shouldn't have said it like that."

"You said it exactly right. That's what's annoying about you."

"I'm told it's my most royal quality."

She laughed despite herself — a short, surprised sound, the kind that escaped rather than arrived. Three weeks ago she hadn't known this man. Two weeks ago he hadn't known his own mother's face until Paloma handed it back to him, one returned memory at a time, like coins counted out from a purse she'd sworn she'd never open again. Now here they were, camped in a dead woman's trap-turned-shrine, waiting for a rock to remember a girl neither of them was sure existed anymore.

Because that was the thing about young Paloma Thackeray. Paloma herself didn't remember her.

"Tell me again why we're doing this instead of literally anything else," she said. "You're going to be crowned in a month. There are smugglers in your kitchen arguing about table linens. There's an entire kingdom—"

"There is an entire kingdom full of people who got their memories back because of you," Quentin said. "Every freed vial from the memory-eaters' vaults, every restored mind. You've spent a month giving things away." He turned the stick over in his fingers. "Let the mountain try returning the favor. Once. For ten minutes. If it doesn't work, we go home, and I will personally ensure the plum cordial metaphor enters the official histories."

"That's a terrible use of royal authority."

"I'm an exiled heir. I'm told my judgment is questionable."

She looked at him — this gaunt, stubborn man who had lost his name to a memory-eater at a river ford and gotten it back in pieces, who had fought a Wolf inside his own skull and walked out of that burning house carrying someone else's grief in each hand — and felt the ache in her chest that had no story attached to it yet. The ache that said *him* before her mind supplied any reason.

"All right," she said. "Ten minutes."

*

Quentin did not believe the stone would answer.

He wanted to — gods, he wanted to, for her sake more than his own — but he had been raised in a court where hope was currency, and he knew how counterfeit it ran. His father had hoped the Wolf was only a sickness. His mother had hoped exile would be temporary. Quentin had learned early that hoping loudly just meant falling further when the floor gave way.

So he built the fire instead. Small, practical, the kind of fire a smuggler would make rather than a prince — low and tucked behind the shrine's eastern pillar, throwing heat without announcing itself. Paloma had taught him that trick on the pass, back when they were strangers trading desperation like rations. Some lessons stayed even after the memories around them burned away.

"Put your hands on the stone," she said.

"Is there a ritual?"

"The ritual is that I don't know any rituals. My grandmother wrote all the rituals, and she also wrote *you'll know when*, which is the least helpful sentence ever carved into granite."

He pressed his palms flat against the shrine wall. The stone was cold in the way deep things are cold — not winter-cold but cellar-cold, vault-cold, the cold of places that hold things carefully. Beneath his hands, faint as a heartbeat heard through a wall, something shifted.

Or he imagined it. He'd imagined the Wolf's voice for years; his imagination had a documented history of treason.

"Anything?" Paloma asked. She'd put her own hands against the stone a foot from his, and her face wore the careful blankness she used for card games and negotiations — the look of a woman refusing to want something in case wanting scared it off.

"Something," he admitted. "Or my hands are asleep."

"Romantic."

"We do our best."

Then the humming started.

It came up through the ground first, through their knees, through the soles of their boots — the same note Quentin had felt on the day of the binding, the day Paloma emptied herself into the mountain. It climbed the shrine walls and gathered in the carved letters until the old script seemed to swim, and the snow on the archway shivered loose and fell in a soft white curtain around them, and Quentin thought, absurdly, of the festival of forgetting in the capital — except this was the opposite, this was a festival of *remembering*, thrown by nobody, attended by two people and a stick he was probably ruining.

"Paloma," he said.

"I hear it."

The stone beneath his hands grew warm.

Not gradually — all at once, like a hand closing over his, like being *gripped*. He flinched, and Paloma's free hand shot across and clamped his wrist, holding his palm to the rock.

"Don't let go," she breathed. "Whatever happens, don't let go."

And then the mountain spoke.

Not in words, not at first. In weather. In pressure. In a voice like wind moving through a house at night, touching every room, and then — threading up through that vastness — something smaller. Older. A woman's voice, dry as autumn paper, with an accent from the southern coast that Quentin had heard only in old recordings and once, memorably, in a dream that had left him homesick for a place he'd never been.

*"Well,"* said the voice of Odile Thackeray, long dead, *"it took you long enough."*

Beside him, Paloma made a sound he'd never heard from her before — small and cracked, the sound of a lock turning.

"Grandmother?" she whispered.

*"Don't shout, girl. Stone carries. And stop making that face — I can't see you, but I know you're making it. Your mother made the same face, and her mother before her. It's hereditary, the face."*

"How—" Paloma's voice wobbled, steadied, wobbled again. "How are you *here?*"

*"Where do you think I went, when I finished the ledger? I was a thief too, child. I stole myself out of the world and paid it all into this rock, same as you did. Every year of me. The mountain keeps what it's given."* A pause, and then, drier still: *"Though I confess I expected the Wolf to be the one who came asking for change. You've muddled the accounting beautifully. Well done."*

"She's complimenting the apocalypse," Quentin murmured.

*"And who is that? The voice sounds hollowed-out. Are you the boy? The heir?"*

Quentin cleared his throat. "Quentin. Yes. Formerly."

*"Formerly,"* the voice repeated, with unmistakable approval. *"Good. Titles rot. Now listen, both of you, because stone is expensive to talk through and I'm spending my granddaughter's savings to do it."*

Paloma's grip on his wrist tightened. "My what?"

*"Savings, girl. What you gave, the mountain keeps — but keeping isn't spending. I designed the binding to hold, not to hoard. There's room in the ledger for withdrawals. Small ones. Careful ones."* The wind-voice softened, and for a moment it sounded less like a monument and more like a tired woman on the far side of a very long night. *"I couldn't tell you this in the prophecy. Prophecies leak. The Wolf read mine for a century, looking for the trick in it, and I couldn't risk it finding the true door. So I hid the last page where only a Thackeray would look — inside the giving itself. You already found the message, child. You just haven't asked it for anything yet."*

Paloma was crying. Quentin could see it without looking — the particular silence of her shoulders, the way her breathing went shallow and deliberate. She had told him once, on the road, that she couldn't cry properly anymore, that some of her tears had gone into the hoard with everything else. Apparently the stone disagreed.

"Ask it," she whispered. "Ask it for what?"

*"For whatever you gave that you miss most,"* said Odile Thackeray. *"That's the withdrawal terms. One memory, returned whole. Choose well. And Quentin-of-formerly — keep hold of her wrist. Withdrawals make people dizzy."*

*

Paloma had known the answer before the question finished forming.

It wasn't a hard choice. It was the only choice, the one that had been sitting in her chest since the night she opened the client's vial and understood what her emptiness cost other people: the memory of herself. Young Paloma. The girl she'd stolen away at eight years old because the truth of those years had been too heavy to carry and too dangerous to keep.

She had assumed the mountain had digested that memory along with everything else — that it had gone into the binding, spent against the Wolf, burned as fuel.

But her grandmother had built a bank, not a pyre.

"I want—" Her voice failed. She tried again, pressing her palm harder against the warm stone, feeling that vast patient attention underneath like an ocean regarding a candle. "I want the first thing. The earliest one. The one I took from myself."

The humming changed pitch.

*"Ah,"* said her grandmother softly. *"Yes. I wondered if you'd ask for that or for the sunset from the Salt Stair. The pretty one. Everyone asks for the pretty ones first."*

"I'm not everyone."

*"No,"* Odile said, and there was something in the old voice now that Paloma had never heard in life and barely believed in death: tenderness without armor. *"You never were. Hold on, child. This part stings."*

It did sting. It stung like frostbite reversing, like a limb waking up after too long asleep, pins and needles the size of knives. The warmth in the stone concentrated under her palm, gathered, coiled —

— and rose.

It came up her arm like water climbing a thread, and it carried a smell with it, which was impossible, memories were supposed to arrive as knowledge, but this one arrived as a *kitchen*. Woodsmoke and burnt sugar and rain on a slate roof. And a voice — not her grandmother's, younger, rougher, a woman's voice singing badly and happily, off-key in a way that suggested she knew it and didn't care—

And then Paloma was eight years old.

She was small and sharp-elbowed and furious about having to wash her hair, and there was a woman — her mother, her mother's face, her mother's laugh, a face Paloma had erased so completely she'd forgotten there was anything to erase — kneeling on a kitchen floor, toweling her daughter's head, saying, *"Honestly, child, you fight soap like it owes you money."*

And little Paloma, eight years old, gap-toothed and dripping, looked up at her mother and said the words that would eventually become a career:

*"If I stole it, would I have to wash it?"*

Her mother laughed. Not politely — helplessly, doubling over, banging the towel on the floor. *"Gods help us all. A Thackeray to the bone."*

Paloma came back to herself on her knees on the shrine floor, and Quentin was holding her the way you hold someone through a fever, one arm braced across her back, his chin resting on top of her head, and she realized distantly that she must have swayed and he must have caught her, and that her face was soaked, and that she could remember.

She could *remember.*

Not everything. Not the whole childhood — the stone had given her the one withdrawal, exactly as promised, one memory returned whole, and this was it: a kitchen, a towel, a joke, a laugh. A single evening out of seven lost years.

It should have felt like a crumb. Instead it felt like a sunrise.

"There was a woman," she said. Her voice sounded scraped and new. "In the memory. Quentin, there was a — she was singing. Badly. She was my—"

"Your mother," Quentin said quietly. He'd watched the memory surface in her face; she could see it reflected there, secondhand, his expression soft in the firelight. "I saw it hit you. You smiled like you'd never done it before."

"I might not have."

"You have. Twice since we met. Both times at my expense."

She laughed — wet, ragged, real — and gripped his sleeve, and turned back to the stone. "Grandmother. Grandmother, are you still—"

*"Still here,"* said Odile Thackeray. *"Barely. Stone is dear, and you've spent nearly all your coin on one kitchen."* A pause. When the voice came again, it had gone quiet in a way that made Paloma lean closer. *"One evening, child. That's what I can return today. But hear me: the mountain keeps. It keeps *everything* you gave. Every stolen sunset, every borrowed grief, every year of you. Nothing is spent that cannot, someday, be counted back out. Slowly. Carefully. A memory at a time, like medicine."*

"How long?" Paloma whispered.

*"As long as it takes. You were always good at long roads."*

"And the rest of it? The childhood? Is it all still—"

*"Kept. Safe. Waiting for a richer thief than me."* The wind-voice was fading now, thinning toward the edges of hearing. *"One more thing, and then I must sleep, because talking through granite is worse than childbirth and I did that twice. Listen. The binding holds, but bindings are doors, and doors swing both ways. Something scratched at the stone last night. Something small and hungry that the Wolf left behind, the way wolves leave fleas."*

The warmth under Paloma's palm flickered.

*"It whispers,"* her grandmother said. *"To whoever stands too close, too long, too sad. Do not listen to it. Do not answer it. And if it learns your names —"* the voice caught, thinned to a thread — *"then come back, both of you, and bring the ledger. There's one more page. There was always one more page."*

And then the stone was only stone — cold, silent, ancient — and the snow fell, and the fire crackled, and Paloma knelt on the shrine floor with one recovered evening held against her chest like a coal she was afraid to breathe on.

Quentin didn't say anything for a long moment. Then he reached over and turned the stick in his hands, examining his work.

"It's a spoon," he announced.

"What?"

"The carving. I've decided. It's a spoon. For the plum cordial." He set it down between them with tremendous dignity. "We'll need it when the kingdom hears the mountain talks back. There's going to be a queue, Paloma. People will bring offerings. Someone will absolutely try to pay you in goats."

She laughed again — easier this time — and then sobered, her thumb tracing the edge of the memory like a scar she was learning to be proud of. A kitchen. A bad song. A mother who laughed helplessly.

"One page left," she said.

"One page left," Quentin agreed. He looked up at the shrine, at the carved letters catching the firelight, and his jaw set in the way she'd learned meant trouble ahead, faced anyway. "And something scratching at the door in the dark."

Above them, far up the mountainside where no footsteps had disturbed the snow, something answered.

A single whisper, thin as wire, tasting the air:

*"…Thackeray…"*

Paloma's hand found Quentin's in the dark, and held on.
