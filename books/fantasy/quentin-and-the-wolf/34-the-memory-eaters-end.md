## 34. The Memory-Eaters' End

The Hall of Kept Things smelled of dust and lavender and something older underneath — the scent of a hundred years of other people's lives, bottled and shelved like preserves nobody intended to eat.

Paloma stood in the doorway with her hands in her coat pockets and let herself feel it: the hum. Every memory-thief knew the hum. A room full of vials didn't stay silent; it murmured, the way a full beehive murmured, all those little lives pressing against their glass wanting out. She'd felt it in her grandmother's ledger vault, in Quentin's cache beneath the chapel, in every smuggler's cellar where somebody had paid her to take what they couldn't bear to keep.

She had never stood inside the crown's own larder before.

"By the Mountain," breathed Sable behind her, one of the Thornfell smugglers who'd come south with them, a broad woman with a face like weathered oak and a crossbow she treated like a beloved pet. "There must be ten thousand of 'em."

"Twelve thousand, four hundred and six," said Paloma, reading the master ledger chained to the entrance desk — because of course the memory-eaters kept records; bureaucrats were bureaucrats whether they ate souls or merely taxed them. "Filed by province. Cross-indexed by name." Her finger stopped on an entry. *Thackeray, M.* — Province of Thornfell — confiscated under Edict of Forgetting, year of the Wolf's coronation. "They took my grandmother's memory too."

Quentin said nothing beside her. He'd gone very quiet when they'd entered, the way he always did in places that remembered his father too loudly. But his hand found the small of her back, brief and steadying, and then withdrew, because even after everything they were still shy about it in front of the smugglers, who found this endlessly funny.

"Right," Paloma said, and pulled off her coat. "Everyone get comfortable. This is going to take a while."

* * *

Here is what nobody understood about the memory-eater order, Paloma thought as she worked: they weren't monsters. That was the terrible part. Monsters you could fight. The memory-eaters had been clerks. Kindly ones, mostly, at least at first — the founding edict had been about mercy, taking memories of war and plague from people who begged to forget. And somewhere across a century, *begged to forget* had become *were made to forget*, and the mercy had become a pantry, and the pantry had become a treasury, because a king made of stolen memory needed resupply.

The Wolf was dead — or bound, or digested slowly by a patient mountain, depending on which scholar you asked, and Paloma had decided not to ask, because the answer gave her nightmares either way. But the order remained. Its halls remained, in every provincial capital. Its vaults remained, fat with confiscated lives. And its eaters — the trained ones, the ones who could reach into a skull and pull — those men and women still drew crown wages and still went out on Tuesdays to make troublesome people quieter.

The new king had issued a decree abolishing them. Decrees, Paloma had learned, traveled about as fast as a lame mule and stuck about as well as buttered rain.

So she'd come herself.

"You know," Sable said, watching Paloma lay vials out in rows on the long table, "most folk would just smash the shelves."

"And scramble twelve thousand minds into soup?" Paloma snorted. "A memory's got a shape. Break the vessel wrong and it pours into whatever's nearest — usually whoever's standing closest. You'd have a crossbow-wielding oak tree suddenly convinced she's a miller's widow from Osterhale with a fear of geese."

"That happened to someone?"

"To me. Twice." She held up a vial to the lamplight, checking the seal, the sediment, the color. Amber meant grief; you handled amber gently. Silver-blue meant joy, which was worse — joy fought hardest to stay lost, because some people couldn't bear getting it back. "You can't just give a person back a memory like handing over a parcel. You have to *fit* it. Find the hole it came out of. Smooth the edges. Some of these folk have built whole new selves around the absence. You rip the shelf away and the whole house falls down."

"So how do you do it?"

"Slowly," said Paloma. "And kindly. And one at a time."

She picked up the first vial — a miller's widow from Osterhale, as it happened, though the fear-of-geese entry turned out to belong to a different file entirely — and pressed her palms around the glass, and began the work she'd spent her whole life learning backwards.

* * *

It took three weeks.

Word spread the way word did along smuggler roads, which was faster than any royal courier and with better snacks. People began arriving at the Hall of Kept Things before dawn, forming a queue down the Salt Stair steps, holding little paper slips with their ledger numbers written in hopeful, trembling hands. An old soldier who'd forgotten why he flinched at thunder. A woman missing eleven years and her daughter's face. A boy of nineteen who'd been made to forget a girl of eighteen, and who sat across from Paloma with his hat wrung in his fists saying, "Is she married? Don't tell me if she's married. No — tell me. No. *Don't—*"

"She waited," Paloma told him, because she'd checked. "Two years. Then she moved to the coast. She's happy. And she kept a letter for you, and I've read it, and if you want it back I'll put it in your head right now along with everything else."

The boy wept so hard Sable had to fetch him water.

Some of them didn't want it back. That surprised Paloma at first, until she thought about her own hoard — the things she'd taken from herself, the childhood she'd poured into the mountain without hesitation. Grief was grief. She offered everyone the choice, and about one in ten chose the forgetting, and she honored it, and marked their files in red ink: *Released by will.* The eaters had never done that. The eaters had never asked.

That was the difference, she wrote in her own growing ledger each night. Not the skill. Any competent thief could lift a memory and set one down again; she'd been doing the second half badly and alone since she was fifteen. The difference was asking. The difference was *returning*.

Quentin helped where he could, though his gift ran the wrong direction — he could lose things beautifully, hold things, carry things, but setting memories home was thief's work. So he managed the queue instead, learning names, brewing tea, sitting with the frightened ones while Paloma worked. The former heir to the throne, handing out biscuits to a line of peasants. He was terrible at it for exactly four days and then unexpectedly wonderful, because it turned out that a man who'd grown up hollowed out by a wolf knew precisely how to talk to people carrying absences.

"You're staring," he said one evening, catching Paloma watching him explain tax stamps to a bewildered farmer.

"I'm supervising."

"You're making the face you make when you're pleased but embarrassed about it."

"I don't have that face."

"You absolutely do. It's your third-best face."

Sable, from across the hall, without looking up from oiling her crossbow: "He's right, love. It's third-best."

Paloma went back to her vials with as much dignity as she could gather, which was none, and which everyone present noted with satisfaction.

* * *

The trouble came in week four, and it wore a crown clerk's badge.

His name was Master Fenwick, deputy director of the Osterhale branch, and he arrived with two guards and a folder of edicts and the expression of a man who had rehearsed being reasonable all the way there. Paloma met him on the steps, wiping wax residue off her fingers.

"Madam Thackeray," he said. "I've read your file."

"All of it? There's a lot."

"Enough to know you're committing theft. These are crown property. Seized assets of the former administration revert to—" He consulted his folder. "To the current administration. Which means to His Majesty, who has, I'm sure, simply not yet gotten around to formally re-authorizing our operations, given the disruptions of the past year—"

"He abolished you," Paloma said. "In writing. With a seal. I watched him stamp it. It took him three tries because he kept pressing too hard."

"The decree lacks implementing legislation," said Fenwick, with the serenity of a man whose entire soul was made of folders.

Paloma looked at him for a long moment. Behind him, the queue shifted and murmured. The old soldier. The widow. The boy with the letter in his chest now, walking taller than any lad had a right to.

Then she smiled the smile that had gotten her through a hundred locked doors, and stepped close, and said, quietly, "Master Fenwick. May I show you something?"

And before he could decide whether to be offended, she touched his temple — light as a moth landing — and showed him.

Not a stolen memory. Just his own. One he'd filed himself, years ago, in the order's own archive: a young Fenwick, newly sworn, escorting a weeping farmer out of an intake chamber, and feeling, for one bright horrified second, that something was *wrong*, that the man's emptiness was wrong, that the whole lavender-smelling building was wrong — and then the training taking hold, smoothing it over, filing it away. *Doubt, personal. Confiscated per protocol.*

She hadn't taken anything. She'd just handed him back his own doubt, still warm, still sharp.

Fenwick stood very still on the steps. His guards looked at him. The queue watched.

"Oh," he said finally, in a small voice completely unlike his folder voice. "Oh, that was — I remember that day." He looked at the Hall of Kept Things behind her, at the murmur of ten thousand waiting lives. "How many are there?"

"Twelve thousand, four hundred and six here. Nine more halls across the provinces."

He closed his folder. It seemed to take him physical effort, like closing a door on a house he'd lived in all his life.

"I'll need a requisition team," he said. "Proper preservation protocols. Half these seals are degrading — amateur storage, frankly, it's been driving me mad for years." He paused, and added, stiffly, as if each word cost a coin: "You'll want administrative help. I am extremely good at ledgers."

Paloma grinned. "I know the type."

* * *

They finished the Osterhale hall at midwinter, and Paloma marked the last file closed and sat down on the floor amid the empty shelving and laughed until Quentin came running, because she couldn't decide if she was crying or not and had settled on both.

Empty shelves had never looked so beautiful.

They left for Thornfell two days later — nine halls yet to open, a kingdom of holes to fill, and Paloma traveling lighter than she'd ever traveled, keeping nothing, giving everything, a thief with empty pockets and a full heart. On the last night, at the Inn of Small Kindnesses, with the whole found family crammed around one table and Sable teaching the miller's widow to play knucklebones and Quentin laughing with his head tipped back, Paloma opened her ledger to write the day's account and found, tucked at the back, a page she didn't remember putting there.

Her own handwriting. Recent. She read it twice.

*Osterhale done. Eight halls remain. Note for future Thackerays: the eating was never the hard part. The hard part was believing you deserved to give things back.*

She stared at it. Then she looked up, and caught Quentin watching her with the expression of a man guarding a secret badly.

"When did you write in my ledger?"

"I didn't." He sipped his cider, entirely too innocent. "You did. Last night. You said, and I quote, 'hold my pen, I've had a thought.'"

Had she? She tried to remember, and found — nothing. A small, clean gap, like a tooth lost to a fair trade rather than a fist.

Somewhere north, a mountain was digesting a wolf very slowly, and keeping what it was given, and — apparently — lending things back on its own schedule now.

"Well," Paloma said, closing the ledger, warmth spreading through her chest like brandy. "Then I suppose I'd better write the next page before I forget what I figured out."

Outside, snow began to fall on the road that would soon bear her name — and far to the north, in stone old as the world, something whispered.
