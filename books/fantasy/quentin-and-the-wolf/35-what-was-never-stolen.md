## 35. What Was Never Stolen

Quentin had attended perhaps two hundred festivals in his life, and he could not remember a single one of them.

That was not entirely the Wolf's fault, though the Wolf got blamed for most things these days. The truth was uglier and smaller: as a prince, he had stood at the edge of every festival the way one stands at the edge of a warm room while wearing wet boots. Present. Observing. Waiting for it to end so he could return to whatever duty waited behind the next door. His father's court had taught him that joy was something you supervised, not something you fell into.

So when he told Paloma that he wanted to give her a first memory — *a real one, made on purpose* — and she had looked at him with those careful gray eyes and said, "You've never actually been to a festival, have you," it had stung precisely because she was right.

"I've been to hundreds," he'd protested.

"You've been *stationed* at hundreds. There's a difference."

And now here they were, in the market square of Millbrook, three weeks after her recovery began, standing at the edge of the Harvest Lights festival with the smell of frying apples and woodsmoke thick enough to chew, and Quentin realized with dawning horror that he had absolutely no idea what one did at a festival.

Paloma, for her part, looked like a sparrow that had wandered into a cathedral — fascinated, wary, and prepared to fly at any moment.

"Everyone keeps smiling at me," she murmured. "Why are they all smiling?"

"Because you're holding a candied apple like it might be armed."

"It might be." She turned it slowly in her fingers. The sugar shell caught the lantern light, red as a garnet. "I don't remember ever eating one. I don't remember deciding whether I like them. That's an odd feeling, Quentin. Standing in front of a thing and not knowing if you love it or hate it."

He thought about that. He thought about how many times she must have felt it since the binding emptied her — reaching for herself and finding only smooth stone where a person used to be.

"Then we find out now," he said. "Together. That's rather the point of tonight."

She raised an eyebrow — a gesture he had learned meant *I am allowing this to continue but reserving judgment* — and bit into the apple.

Her whole face changed.

It started at her eyebrows, which shot up in genuine surprise, and traveled down through her nose crinkling (the sugar was too sweet), to her mouth, which kept chewing with increasing commitment, to her eyes, which went wide and bright with something that was not memory at all but was its close cousin: discovery.

"Oh," she said, around a full mouth. "Oh no. I *like* these. This is a problem. How much do these cost?"

"A copper each."

"How many coppers do you have?"

"Paloma, we're trying to build a treasury for the coronation, not—"

"How. Many. Coppers."

He counted out six from his purse. She bought four apples, ate two immediately, and tucked the other two into her coat pockets with the solemnity of a woman provisioning for a siege. Then she grabbed his sleeve — she still initiated contact cautiously, always asking first with a look, always letting him meet her halfway — and pulled him toward the music.

* * *

The musicians were playing something fast and fiddly near the maypole, and the dancing around it was less choreography than cheerful collision. Quentin hung back. Some habits lived deeper than memory; even hollowed by the Wolf, his body had known how to stand at a wall and watch.

Paloma watched the dancers with her head tilted, doing what she always did — reading the pattern. He could nearly see the thief's mind working behind her eyes, mapping the steps the way she'd once mapped guard rotations and smuggler roads.

"It's simple," she decided. "Three beats, turn, change hands. Like carrying a vial past a drunk guard." She glanced at him, and there it was again — that flicker of ache under the curiosity, the ghost of skills she no longer understood having. "Was I good at that? At the... sneaking parts? Everyone says I was, but it's like hearing about a stranger."

"The best I ever saw," Quentin said honestly. "You once stole a memory off a man while apologizing to him. He thanked you afterward."

"That sounds exhausting."

"It sounded exhausting. You made it look like breathing."

She absorbed this quietly, then held out her hand, palm up. An offering, or a dare.

"Dance with me. If I'm going to have a first memory, let it be a good one. Not standing at a wall watching."

His heart did something complicated. He took her hand.

The first thirty seconds were a disaster. Quentin had been drilled in eleven formal dances of the royal court and none of them applied; his feet kept attempting a stately pavane while the fiddle demanded chaos. He stepped on her foot twice. She elbowed a large farmer in the ribs. A child darted between their legs and very nearly toppled them both.

Then Paloma laughed.

Not the polite, recovered-patient laugh she'd been offering people for weeks — a real one, sudden and unguarded, startled out of her by the sheer ridiculousness of two adults failing so completely at something children were managing nearby. And once she laughed, something loosened in Quentin too, some knot of prince-and-heir-and-survivor he hadn't known he was still tied in, and he stopped counting steps and simply moved, and she matched him, thief-quick, and somewhere in the fourth song they were actually dancing — badly, joyfully, spinning through lantern light while the fiddler grinned at them.

When the song ended they stood breathless in the crowd, her hand still in his, and she said, "There. Now it's mine. Nobody gave me that. Nobody stole it. It just — happened. To *me*."

"Yes," Quentin said, and found his voice slightly rough. "That's exactly the point."

She looked at their joined hands for a long moment. "Quentin. Why does this matter so much to you? The new memories. You've been — collecting moments for me like I collect—" She stopped. Smiled wryly. "Like I *used to* collect things."

He considered lying. He was, by upbringing, reasonably good at it.

"Because I know what it is to have your past taken," he said finally. "The Wolf ate mine bite by bite, and everyone kept telling me who I used to be — my tutors, the old servants, the histories. It never fit. It was like being dressed in a dead man's clothes." He squeezed her hand gently. "I won't dress you in your past, Paloma. It's gone, and grieving it forever would be its own kind of prison. But a past isn't only what happened *to* you. It's what you choose, remembered. So we choose. Every day. And someday you'll have a history that's entirely yours — authored, not inherited."

She was quiet for a moment. In the lantern light her eyes were wet, though her voice, when it came, was dry as ever.

"That was almost eloquent. Are you sure you're a smuggler's associate and not a speechmaker?"

"My father was a king. It's hereditary."

"There's the Quentin I keep falling for," she said lightly — and then froze, hearing herself, and he froze too, and for one suspended heartbeat the whole festival seemed to hold its breath around them.

She recovered first, magnificent and terrible. "For. Falling *for*. Your schemes. Obviously. You bribed a fiddler earlier, I saw it."

"I tipped him, Paloma. For playing well."

"Mm." She was already walking away, ears pink, two candied apples jutting from her pockets like trophies. "Come along, Your Majesty. Show me the rest of the festival. We're building you a history too, you know. You can't supervise it from a wall."

* * *

They stayed until the lanterns burned low. They threw rings at bottles (she won; she was insufferable about it). They listened to an old woman sing a ballad about Thornfell that got half the details wrong, and Paloma whispered corrections with proprietary delight, because the true version belonged to her now too, secondhand and beloved. They drank spiced cider that made Quentin cough and Paloma laugh until she wheezed.

And when they walked home under a sky salted with stars, she stopped abruptly in the middle of Thackeray Road — the sign had gone up that week, and she still flushed every time she saw it — and said:

"It's snowing."

It was. First snow of the season, fine as sifted flour, catching in her dark hair. She tilted her face up, and flakes landed on her lashes, and she blinked at them with the wonder of someone meeting weather for the first time — which, in every way that mattered, she was.

"I know winter," she said softly. "I know it the way you know a word in a language you can't speak. Cold. Snow. But this—" she caught a flake on her fingertip and watched it die there, "—this is mine. My first snowfall. Chosen. On purpose. With you."

"With me," Quentin agreed.

She looked at him then, really looked, the way she'd looked into sealed vaults and locked ledgers, patient and unflinching, and whatever she found in his face made her step closer — into the space between them she'd been circling for weeks with a thief's caution.

"Quentin Ishikawa," she said, "I don't remember loving you. They tell me I did. It feels like a coat that almost fits. But tonight I danced with you and it fit. Tonight I watched snow fall with you and it fit. So I'm not going to wait to remember it. I'm going to do it *again*, from the beginning, on purpose. If — if that's—"

He kissed her before she could finish the sentence, gently, under the falling snow, halfway between the festival and home, and it fit.

When they parted, she was laughing and crying at once, brushing flakes from his shoulders. "Well," she managed. "*That* memory I intend to keep. Nobody steal it."

"No one will," he promised. "I'm told the finest keeper on the roads lives around here."

* * *

Later, much later, wrapped in blankets in the inn's best room with the snow ticking against the shutters, Quentin lay awake listening to Paloma breathe, and allowed himself, for one rare hour, to feel happy without checking over his shoulder.

Which was, of course, when he noticed the light.

It came from down the road — faint, wrong, flickering in a color fire doesn't burn: a pale gold, like candlelight seen through ice. It pulsed once, twice, from the direction of the shrine mountain.

Beside him, without waking fully, Paloma frowned in her sleep and murmured something. A single word, slurred and soft, in a voice that was hers and yet somehow older:

*"Grandmother?"*

Quentin sat up very slowly, watching the gold light pulse against the clouds above Thornfell, and reached for his boots.
