## 36. The Wolf's Whisper

The trouble began, as most trouble does in my experience, with someone being kind to a stranger.

I had come up to the shrine on an errand so domestic it would have made my younger self sneer: a jar of plum preserves for the mountain that remembered, because Marta at the inn insisted the stone "liked sweets," and because I had learned, over these months of rebuilding myself out of other people's memories and Quentin's patient storytelling, that when Marta insists on something you simply do it. The woman once convinced an entire caravan of bandits to surrender by feeding them stew until they were too comfortable to fight.

So there I was, climbing the switchback path with a jar of plums bumping against my hip, when I saw the girl.

She was maybe sixteen, sitting cross-legged before the shrine stone with her forehead pressed against it, and she was crying — not the loud crying of someone who wants to be found, but the quiet, worn-out kind, the kind I knew from the inside. The kind where you've run out of tears and are just leaking anyway.

"Hello," I said, because I am, despite everything, a soft touch.

She startled back like I'd caught her doing something shameful. Maybe she had. "I wasn't— I'm not praying. It's not a god."

"No," I agreed, setting down my pack. "It's worse than a god. Gods only want your worship." I crouched beside her, keeping a respectful distance, the way you'd approach a spooked horse or a feral cat or a smuggler who owed you money. "What did it offer you?"

Her eyes went wide and wet. "How did you know—"

"Because I know this stone. And I know what lives in it." I held out my hand, palm up, empty. A thief's habit — show them there's nothing hidden. "My name is Paloma. What's yours?"

"Wren," she said, after a long moment. "Wren Ashvale. My brother went to the capital eight months ago for work and stopped writing, and everyone says he's dead but nobody will *say* it, and I came here because—" She swallowed. "Someone told me the old stone remembers things. That if you ask it right, it can show you where lost people are."

"And did it?"

"It showed me him." Her voice cracked down the middle. "He was alive. He was working in the tanneries and his hands were ruined and he was *alive*, and then—" Her face crumpled. "Then it said it could show me more. It said if I brought it something. A memory. Any memory, it didn't have to be a good one, just a true one. And I was going to. I walked all the way here from Lowmarsh with a memory of my mother singing, and I was going to give it away, and I don't even remember deciding to, I just—"

I sat down hard on the cold granite.

*It didn't have to be a good one. Just a true one.*

That was how the Wolf had always fed. Not on joy — joy was gristle, joy fought back. It fed on grief and longing and the small desperate hungers of people who wanted one thing too much. I knew this because Quentin had told me, over and over, in the weeks after the unbinding, whenever I woke at night reaching for a childhood that no longer existed anywhere in the world. He'd told me like a man reciting a prayer against the dark: *It eats what you can't stop wanting. So stop wanting, and it starves.*

We had thought we'd killed it. We had thought wrong.

"Wren," I said carefully. "Did you give it anything?"

"I put my hand on the stone. Just to— just to feel closer to him. And it felt like—" She shuddered. "Like being understood. Completely. Like something finally knew exactly how much it hurt. Is that mad? It felt *good*, and I think that's why I'm frightened."

No, sweet girl. That's not madness. That's the oldest trap in the world, and I fell into a version of it myself once, trading pieces of myself away because being seen — even by something hungry — felt like love.

"Give me your hand," I said.

She gave it. Her palm was warm and chapped and human, and underneath the skin, faint as a watermark, I could feel it: a smudge. A place where something had pressed against her and left a print, the way a thumb leaves grease on paper. Not a theft. Not yet. An *appetite*, marked.

I had spent thirty years reading memories the way other people read weather. And what I read off Wren Ashvale's hand made my stomach drop through the mountain.

* * *

Quentin took the news the way he takes most news now, which is to say he first made tea, because Marta has taught him that no crisis is discussed before the kettle sings, and then he sat very still and let the fear move through him like weather instead of fighting it.

"A fragment," he said. "In the stone."

"In the stone. Under the stone. In whatever the stone is *for*." I paced the inn's back room, three steps one way, three steps the other, a habit from a life of narrow spaces. "When we poured everything into the binding — my whole hoard, a kingdom's worth of stolen years — I thought we were drowning it. But you don't drown a thing like that, do you. You feed it. We fed it the largest meal in history and sealed it in a larder."

"That's a horrible way to put it."

"I'm a thief, Quentin. Horrible is my native language."

He almost smiled. Then he didn't. "The rite worked. The Wolf was torn from the crown. Your grandmother's design — teach the mountain to digest it, not destroy it. That's what we finished last month. The stone swallowed the last of it."

"The stone swallowed the last of it," I agreed. "And stones, it turns out, digest slowly. And while they're digesting—" I sat down across from him. "While they're digesting, they burp."

He stared at me. "Paloma."

"I'm serious! Wren put her hand on the stone and something answered. Something that knew exactly what she hungered for and offered it back to her, priced in memories. That's not digestion. That's a mouth."

Quentin set down his cup. I watched him do the arithmetic I'd already done, watched it land on his face the way bad news lands — heavy, then heavier. Because he of all people knew what a fragment of the Wolf could become. He had carried half of one in his skull for years. He knew how small a beginning it needed. A whisper. A willing ear. One grieving girl with a memory of her mother's singing and a hole in her heart shaped exactly like a missing brother.

"How many?" he asked quietly. "How many people have been up there?"

"That's the question, isn't it." I pulled out the scrap of paper where I'd been tallying since morning. "Marta says pilgrims started coming two months ago. Word spreads along the roads — word always spreads along the roads, it's the one cargo every smuggler carries free. They call it the Listening Stone now. Grieving mothers. Missing sons. People who lost farms, lovers, names. The war scattered so many, and the crown's memory-eaters scattered more, and there's this—" I gestured vaguely at the whole wounded country outside the window. "There's this enormous appetite for getting things back. And something in that stone has learned to say yes."

The fire popped. Somewhere in the common room, Tam was losing spectacularly at cards to a one-eyed smuggler named Brack, and the swearing was cheerful, and the smell of Marta's bread was doing its usual work of making the world seem survivable. Ordinary warmth. I held onto it like a rail in rough water.

"We go up tomorrow," Quentin said. "Together. And we listen to what it's saying."

"Quentin." I reached across the table and took his hand — his real hand, scarred and warm and present, nothing like the cold press of stone. "You don't have to. You of all people don't have to hear that voice again."

"Yes, I do." His eyes were steady. Half-hollowed once, half-remade now, and somehow more himself for the mending. "It wore my father's face and ate my childhood, Paloma. If it's whispering to girls like Wren, then it's using the same trick it used on me: it makes being known feel like being loved." His jaw tightened. "Nobody feeds it another piece of anyone I—"

He stopped. We both heard the sentence he hadn't finished. Neither of us named it. We were very good at that, the two of us — building whole rooms out of words we didn't say.

"*Nobody,*" he said instead, "feeds it another piece of anyone. Full stop."

* * *

We went up at dawn, and we brought witnesses, because I had learned that from my grandmother's ledger too: *never face a hunger alone; hungers multiply in solitude.* Marta packed enough food for a siege. Tam came because Tam comes everywhere. Brack came because Brack had decided, in the mysterious way of one-eyed smugglers, that I was worth following. And Wren came because it was her grief sitting on that stone like bait, and she had a right to see the trap sprung.

The shrine looked the same as ever — grey granite, older than kingdoms, wearing its moss like a shawl. Harmless. Stones are excellent liars; they've had eons of practice.

I put my palm flat against it and listened the way only a Thackeray can.

At first: nothing. The deep slow hum of the mountain remembering, the vast sedimentary patience of it, layer upon layer of kept things — my hoard somewhere in there, my grandmother's voice, the single bright shard of young Paloma that the stone had given back to me like a bone returned to a dog. All of it still, still, still.

And then, beneath the stillness, at the very bottom of the sound, so faint I might have invented it:

*...she misses you... your brother... I could show you where...*

Not a roar. Not a howl. A murmur. A kindly uncle's voice. The Wolf had learned, in its long slow churning, to be *small*. To be patient. To stop demanding kingdoms and start asking for pocket change — a memory here, a grief there, one desperate soul at a time, each transaction too little to notice, each one a thread, and threads, given time, weave cages.

"It's farming," I whispered, pulling my hand back like I'd touched a stove. "Quentin, it's not hunting anymore. It's *farming*. Little harvests. Sustainable ones." My voice shook. "That's what frightens me. Big evil, you can fight. This is evil doing bookkeeping."

Quentin knelt and laid both hands on the stone, and I braced myself to pull him back, but what came through him was different. He was listening as royalty listens — as the heir of a line bound to this mountain since before the ledger, as a man who had been eaten once and knew the taste from the inside.

"It's weak," he said at last, wonderingly. "Paloma, it's *starving*. The binding holds. Everything it whispers, it whispers on credit — it can show, it can promise, but it can't take unless someone gives freely. It's a beggar wearing a king's voice." He looked up at me, and there was fury in him, and under the fury, something worse: recognition. "It's doing what I did. In exile. Surviving on scraps and telling itself the scraps were a kingdom."

"So we starve it faster."

"So we starve it faster. But gently." He stood, brushing moss from his knees. "If we seal the road, the desperate will find another way up — desperation always does, you taught me that. If we post guards, we make it forbidden fruit. No." He turned to Wren, who had been hovering at the edge of our circle, clutching her jar of courage the way I'd clutched mine of plums. "Wren. Your brother. Do you want to find him?"

"With my own feet," she said, chin up, wet-eyed, magnificent. "Not with its eyes."

"Then here's what we'll do." And Quentin smiled — that rare, crooked smile that had survived a usurper, a devouring, and a coronation, and which I would walk barefoot over coals to keep. "We'll build something better than a listening stone. We'll build a *finding* one. Every name of every lost person, written in the ledger. Every smuggler road mapped. Every memory-eater vault opened and every stored memory sent home. The stone offers ghosts. We'll offer the real thing, and it'll take work, and it'll take years, and it'll be *true* — and truth, properly tended, is the one thing that thing cannot digest."

I looked at him — this exiled, half-eaten, stubbornly hopeful man — and I thought about my grandmother writing her prophecy a century ago, laying a trap out of patience and paper, and I thought: *ah. So that's where he gets it. Not blood. The same school.*

"There's just one problem," I said.

"Name it."

"Somebody has to stay close to the stone. Listen regularly. Learn its patterns, track what it's swallowing, make sure the whispers never grow louder than murmurs. Somebody who can hear it the way I can." I crossed my arms. "And I already know you're going to argue with me about who that somebody is, so let's save the time: it's me. I'm the thief. Reading hungers is my craft. And unlike you, your majesty, I'm expendable."

The look he gave me could have curdled milk at forty paces.

"You," he said, with terrible calm, "are not."

"Quentin—"

"We'll take turns," he said, in the tone of a king ending a border dispute. "Alternating days. With witnesses. And Marta's rules: no listening alone, no listening hungry, and absolutely no listening sad, because that's its favorite flavor." He paused, and some of the steel went out of him, replaced by something softer and far more dangerous. "Besides. If anyone's going to understand a small hungry thing surviving on scraps in the dark—" his voice dropped, wry and gentle, "—it ought to be the two of us. We're experts."

Behind us, Wren made a small sound that was suspiciously like a teenage girl trying not to giggle at adults, and Tam loudly demanded to know if anyone else smelled burning, and the moment broke the way moments do, scattering into laughter and logistics.

But that night, packing for the long work ahead — ledgers, maps, the names of the lost — I climbed up alone one last time. Just for a minute. Just to say goodbye to the silence.

I put my hand on the stone, and I let it feel me listening.

And from the deep of the mountain, faint as a coin dropped down a well, it whispered — not to Wren, not to the grieving, not to any easy hunger.

It whispered to *me*: *Thief. I remember what you gave. I remember everything you poured into me. And I am keeping it safe for you... until you want it back.*

I snatched my hand away and stood in the moonlight with my heart hammering, understanding at last, with perfect clarity, the shape of the trap still to come.

Because the one hunger I had never managed to cure — the one it had waited all this time to name —

was my own childhood.
