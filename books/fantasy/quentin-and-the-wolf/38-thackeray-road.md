## 38. Thackeray Road

The first sign went up in spring, nailed to the old waypost at the mouth of the Salt Stair road, and Paloma would have missed it entirely if the innkeeper's daughter hadn't come running down the hill with her boots unlaced and her voice carrying half a valley.

"Paloma! Paloma Thackeray! Come see what Da did!"

She had been mending a saddle strap on the inn's back step, because mending things turned out to be a thing she was good at, and because her hands got restless when they were empty — a thief's hands, Quentin called them, always looking for something to lift. She followed the girl up the track, past the goat pen, past the beehives that Maren swore were haunted and Paloma suspected were merely rude, to the waypost at the crossroads.

Someone had pried off the old marker — the one that read *SMUGGLER'S CUT, KEEP OFF, CROWN PROPERTY* in letters so official they'd been shot at twice — and nailed up a fresh board. The paint was still tacky. The letters were crooked as a drunk's walk.

**THACKERAY ROAD**, it said. **ALL TRAVELERS WELCOME. NOTHING HERE IS TAKEN.**

Below it, smaller, in different handwriting: *except maybe your patience at the ford.*

"Your father," Paloma said slowly, "painted this."

"He did! He says the roads were never anybody's, really, but if they're going to belong to somebody, they ought to belong to you." The girl beamed. "He says you gave them back."

"I didn't give them anything," Paloma said. "I just stopped taking."

The girl looked at her the way children look at adults who are being deliberately dense. "That's the same thing, silly."

* * *

It spread, is the thing. She hadn't asked for it and couldn't have stopped it with both hands and a crowbar.

By midsummer there were four signs. By autumn, eleven. Some of them spelled her name right. One of them, carved into a birch trunk by someone with more devotion than literacy, read **THACKERAY ROADD — SAFE PASSAGE FOR THE HONEST AND THE REPENTANT**, which Paloma found hilarious and Quentin found deeply concerning from a spelling standpoint.

"They've made you a saint," he said one evening, tracing the carved letters with a fingertip. "A semi-literate saint."

"Semi-literate saints are the only kind worth praying to," Paloma said. "The literate ones get ideas."

They were walking the long loop of the northern roads together, the route she'd begun calling the Kindness Circuit, though she'd deny it if asked. It wasn't mapping, exactly, what she did now. Or it was, but not the way she'd once mapped — memorizing smuggler cuts and crown patrols and the exact depth of every ford, hoarding it all in her head like coin in a locked box, spending nothing, trusting no one else to hold even a corner of the knowledge.

Now she walked the roads with charcoal and good paper, and she drew them, and she gave the drawings away.

That was the part that had taken the longest to learn. Not the drawing — she'd always been able to hold a road in her mind's eye, every switchback and deadfall, could render it blindfolded. No, the hard part was standing in some waystation kitchen, handing a copy of the high pass map to a wool merchant with bad knees, and watching him look at her like she'd handed him a live coal instead of a kindness.

"This is worth money," he'd said, the first time. "This is worth a lot of money."

"It's worth people getting home," she'd said. "Those aren't the same thing anymore. I checked."

And then, because old habits die face-down in a ditch: "Don't sell it. Copy it. Give copies away. If I find out you sold one, I'll know, and I'll be disappointed, and trust me, my disappointment is worse than any curse your grandmother ever laid."

He'd copied it. He'd given away nine. By winter there were forty copies of the high pass map moving along the roads like a rumor, each one annotated by its new owners — *landslide here, spring '04; widow Osgood leaves bread at the third cairn; wolves denning below the scree, leave them be, they keep better watch than we do.*

The maps grew. That was the miracle nobody had planned. Every hand that touched them added something, and every addition came back to her eventually, carried by travelers who'd heard the name and wanted the source. She kept a master copy at the inn, pinned to the wall by the hearth, and it had become a kind of living thing, thick with marginalia, soft at the edges from handling. Travelers consulted it the way other folk consulted scripture.

Quentin stood before it one night, reading the annotations with an expression she couldn't parse.

"What?" she said.

"My father had cartographers," he said. "Forty of them. The finest in the world. They mapped everything — borders, ore veins, troop routes. Everything the kingdom owned." He touched a note in a child's handwriting: *beware goose.* "Nobody ever mapped anything for anyone. They mapped *for the crown*. Taking, all of it. Even the geography." He turned to her. "You've done something my father never managed with an army and a treasury."

"I made a goose famous?"

"You made knowledge move downhill," he said. "Toward the people who need it. Do you understand how rare that is? How *unnatural*? Kingdoms run on the opposite principle."

"I'm told I'm unnatural," Paloma said. "It's on several signs."

* * *

The strangeness of it never quite wore off — being known. Being *wanted* known.

For twenty years she had built her life around the opposite proposition: that the less anyone knew of Paloma Thackeray, the longer Paloma Thackeray lived. She'd been a rumor, a shadow on the Salt Stair, a name whispered between smugglers and never spoken above a murmur. Her whole craft had been erasure — taking memories out of heads, leaving herself out of stories, keeping nothing, belonging nowhere.

And now there were songs. Bad ones. There was a ballad going around the lowlands in which she fought twelve memory-eaters barehanded on the frozen ford, which was flattering and almost entirely false, since there had been three memory-eaters, she'd had a boat hook, and the ford hadn't been frozen, it had been *muddy*, which was somehow worse to sing about.

There was also a verse about Quentin that made him go a specific shade of pink whenever he heard it, and Paloma had taken to humming it at breakfast purely for the pleasure of watching his ears redden.

"The ballad is historically inaccurate," he said, for perhaps the hundredth time.

"It's a *ballad*. Accuracy is for ledgers."

"It says I slew a memory-eater with a *harp*."

"A tuning fork, in the original account, which is somehow worse."

"I was buying time while you climbed the mill!"

"And history remembers you as the Harp-Slayer of Cold Ford. This is what happens when you let other people do the remembering." She leaned back against the doorframe, watching him glare at the ceiling. "You could correct the record. Stand up in taverns. *Actually, it was a tuning fork—*"

"I would rather be eaten by the Wolf again."

"There he is. My reasonable king."

He wasn't king yet. That was its own story, still unfolding in the capital, where the regency council dithered and the courtiers learned, slowly, painfully, to fill their hollow faces back in. But he came and went along the roads now without disguise, or with only the thin disguise of ordinary clothes and a traveler's pack, and the smugglers had decided among themselves — in that sovereign way smugglers decide things, by consensus and beer — that when he did take the throne, he'd be *their* king. The road's king. Half-remembered, fully claimed.

Maren put it best, one night at the inn, banging her mug down: "Crown's a heavy thing. Good to know somebody carried it up the mountain honest." And then, to Quentin, with terrifying tenderness: "You eat something, love. You're all elbows."

* * *

In the deep of winter, Paloma took the road alone.

Not from loneliness — that was the strange discovery of the year, that she could travel alone without it hollowing her. She took the road alone because the shrine needed visiting, and some visits you make by yourself.

The mountain path was iced over, and she climbed it slow, breath steaming, past the cairns, past the place where the stone held memory like wax. The shrine chamber was cold and dim and smelled of granite and old snow, and she sat down on the ledge where she'd once poured out everything she'd ever stolen, and she talked to the rock.

It was a habit now. She knew how it looked. She did it anyway.

"Road report," she said, unwrapping the bread and dried plums she'd brought. Two portions. One for her, one set on the stone ledge, because you don't visit family empty-handed. "The ford bridge is done. Proper timber, raised footings. Old Cobb cried at the opening, which he'll deny. The high pass maps are on their eleventh generation of copies — somebody's added a recipe to the margin now, which I've decided to allow. And the memory-eater archives are emptied. All of it. Every stored memory freed and delivered. Took most of two years, but it's done."

She broke bread. Ate. The silence of the mountain was the good kind, the listening kind.

"There's a boy in Thornfell village," she said, after a while. "Eight years old. His mother died last month, and the grief-eaters — the private ones, the ones that used to work for lords before we shut the trade down — they got to him first. Sold him a forgetting. He doesn't remember her face." She turned the crust in her fingers. "I can't give that back. What's eaten is eaten. But I've been sitting with him. Telling him stories about her — the neighbors remember her, the baker remembers her, everyone remembers her except him. So I write it down. Little book. *Your mother liked her tea bitter. Your mother sang off-key on purpose to make you laugh.*" She paused. "I'm not stealing for him. I'm just... keeping. For him. Until he's big enough to carry it himself."

The stone said nothing, in the way it had of saying nothing very attentively.

"Yeah," Paloma said. "I thought you'd approve. You started it, after all. *What you give, the mountain keeps.* Well. The lowlands keep too, now. Whole country full of grandmothers, if you know where to look."

She gathered her things, brushed the crumbs from her knees, and stood. At the chamber's edge she paused, the way she always did, and pressed her palm flat against the stone — the ritual of it, the small superstition.

Deep under the rock, faint as a heartbeat, something shifted. Not the Wolf — that fragment was quiet these days, bound and digesting, a hunger slowly becoming geology. This was older. Warmer. For half a breath she felt it: a voice like her own mother's might have sounded, saying a word she almost caught —

Then gone. Just stone, and cold, and the long echo of her own breathing.

"Tease," she told the mountain, and smiled, and went down the hill toward home.

* * *

Home, it turned out, had acquired a sign too.

She saw it from the ridge above the inn as the light went gold and blue: a fresh board over the stable door, painted in Maren's unmistakable hand, letters big enough to read from the next valley.

**THACKERAY & QUENTIN — MAPS COPIED FREE. MEMORIES RETURNED FREE. ROOMS COST MONEY, WE'RE NOT SAINTS.**

Underneath, smaller: *the goose is real. ask inside.*

Paloma stood on the ridge and laughed until she had to sit down in the snow.

Quentin found her there twenty minutes later, half-frozen and wheezing, and hauled her up by both arms with the exasperated tenderness of a man who has done this before. "You saw the sign."

"I saw the *goose*."

"Maren insists the goose is load-bearing to the establishment's reputation." He brushed snow off her shoulders, frowning at how cold she'd let herself get. "There's a traveler waiting inside for you. Been asking after the Thackeray woman by name. Nervous type. Keeps checking the windows."

Paloma's smile faded by degrees. Twenty years of instinct didn't die; it just learned manners. "Trouble?"

"He wouldn't say. Only that he'd come a long way, and that someone told him you were the one who *keeps* things now." Quentin hesitated. "He had a ledger with him, Paloma. Old one. Leather cover, brass clasps. And the handwriting on the spine—" He met her eyes. "It's hers. Your grandmother's."

The cold went through her deeper than the snow ever could. Another ledger. Another page of a life written before she'd lived it.

Inside, the fire was high and the stranger sat hunched in the corner booth, gaunt and road-worn, clutching a leather-bound book to his chest like a shield. When Paloma came through the door he stood up so fast he knocked his mug over, and his eyes — gray, frightened, familiar in a way she couldn't name — fixed on her face.

"Thackeray," he breathed. "You have to read page one. Before anyone else finds it. Because it isn't a prophecy this time." He pushed the ledger across the table toward her, hands shaking. "It's a *map*. Of somewhere even the mountain forgot. And it's addressed—" he swallowed— "it's addressed to *you*, by name, in her hand. Dated tomorrow."

Paloma looked down at the brass clasps, at the worn leather, at the looping script she knew better than her own heartbeat.

Then she opened the cover, and began to read.
