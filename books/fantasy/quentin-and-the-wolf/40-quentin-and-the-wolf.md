## 40. Quentin and the Wolf

The ledger was Paloma's own idea, which surprised no one more than it should have.

"It's a bad idea," said Marta, who kept the inn of small kindnesses and had kept it through two wars and one coronation, and who therefore felt entitled to opinions about paper. "Ledgers get found. Ledgers get read by people you don't want reading them. I've buried three ledgers in my life and every one of them came back up like a bad turnip."

"This one won't," Paloma said, spreading the fresh vellum across the kitchen table. It was good stock, bought honestly from a papermaker in Vell's Crossing who had wept when she paid full price without haggling, because apparently that had never once happened to him in forty years of trade. "This one isn't for hiding. It's for keeping."

Quentin looked up from the far end of the table, where he was losing gracefully at cards to Tam the ferryman's daughter, who cheated with such open delight that refusing to play would have been an insult. "A ledger of what?"

"Of us." Paloma smoothed the first page flat. "Everything. From the Salt Stair road to tonight. Every chapter."

Marta squinted at her over the rim of a teapot she was refilling for no one's benefit but her own. "You mean a history."

"I mean a ledger," Paloma said, dipping her pen. "History is what other people decide happened to you. A ledger is what you count yourself." She glanced up, and there — Quentin saw it even from across the room, saw it the way he had learned to see all her small tellings — there was the flicker of the old thief in her eyes, quick and bright as a coin turning. "And nobody," she added, "steals this one. Not prophecy, not crown, not Wolf. It's mine. Ours. Written down so plain that nothing in it can ever be turned against us again."

"That's not how writing works," said Tam, taking another card she shouldn't have.

"No," Paloma agreed pleasantly. "It's how *mine* works."

* * *

She began, as her grandmother had taught her without ever meaning to, at the beginning.

Her grandmother's ledger — the true one, the prophetic one, the trap dressed as a fortune — lived now behind glass in the royal archive, where scholars argued about its marginalia and two of them had already come to blows over the ink chemistry. But Paloma had kept copies of everything in her head, because she was Paloma Thackeray, or she had been, before she gave that name away to a mountain along with everything else, and what came back to her afterward was a Paloma-shaped person assembled from other people's remembering.

That was the thing no one warned you about, being remembered instead of remembering. You got the shape of yourself from the outside in. Quentin told her she had laughed at his terrible jokes on the smuggler road, so she supposed she had a generous sense of humor. The smugglers told her she'd once talked her way past a checkpoint by pretending to be a tax auditor named Grimsdottir, so she supposed she was brave and possibly a menace. Marta told her she ate her stew too fast and cried at weddings, so she supposed she was hungry for things and soft-hearted, both of which felt right in the chest, the way a coat feels right even when someone else tells you it's yours.

But some things only she could have known, because they'd happened inside her after the hollowing — and those were the things she wrote first, in her careful thief's hand, the hand that had forged a hundred seals and now wrote only truth, which was harder:

*I am sitting at Marta's kitchen table on a morning in late autumn. The bread is burning because Tam was asked to watch it and Tam has never watched anything in her life except for ways to win at cards. Quentin is laughing at her. Outside, the mountain keeps its secrets and gives them back slowly, like a grandmother handing out sweets one at a time.*

*I don't remember my childhood. I want to say that plainly, here on the first page, so that anyone who reads this understands what kind of book it is. This is a book written by a woman who bought herself back secondhand, and loved anyway, and is going to write down why.*

The pen paused. Ink pooled.

Because here was the secret under all the others, the one she'd only admitted to Quentin on a night in early spring when the thaw-water was loud on the inn roof: she wasn't writing this ledger to guard against prophecy. Prophecy was dead. They had killed it together on the shrine-mountain, unbound the Wolf, sprung the trap, done all the terrible necessary arithmetic of it, and the kingdom had come out the other side bruised but breathing.

No. She was writing it because memory chosen was stronger than memory stolen. Her whole life — both lives, the first one and the assembled one — had been spent taking what other people kept. And then giving it away. And then learning, in the long slow convalescence at the inn, the last lesson, the good one:

That a memory you make on purpose, with people you chose, written down in your own hand — that one no one can ever take, because it was never theirs to begin with.

* * *

She worked on the ledger through the autumn and into the winter, and the household learned to orbit her hours the way planets learn an odd little moon.

Tuesdays were for the road chapters. The smugglers came in off Thackeray Road — their road now, renamed by acclamation in a tavern argument that Marta had refereed with the authority of a woman holding a rolling pin — and they would crowd into the kitchen and argue over details while she wrote. Had it been three guards at the ford or four? (Four, said old Brannic; three, said everyone else; the fourth had been a very committed sheep.) What color had the memory-eater's cloak actually been? (Grey, obviously, said Paloma, I was there; *you were unconscious*, said Brannic, with the serenity of a man correcting scripture.)

"You're making me sound heroic," Quentin complained one Tuesday, reading over her shoulder. He did this often. He claimed he was quality-checking. She suspected he simply liked hearing himself described by someone who loved him, and she let him keep the fiction of editorial rigor because it cost her nothing and gave him much.

"You were heroic. I'm making you sound *tired*, which is more accurate and more flattering."

"You wrote 'he stood between her and the hunters with nothing but a broken sword and the stubbornness of his line.'"

"He did."

"He also fell down the embankment immediately afterward."

Paloma considered. "I'll add it in the margins."

"Add that I *tripped*."

"I'm writing 'the mountain moved beneath him.' History deserves dignity."

He groaned, and kissed the top of her head, and went to help Marta haul ale, and Paloma sat smiling at the page for a moment before she wrote the next line, because that was another thing about ledgers: you could afford to record the undignified parts when you knew, absolutely, how the story held you.

Wednesdays were for the hard chapters, and she wrote those alone.

She wrote about the vault, and the ledger that had known her name before she knew it was a name in anyone's book. She wrote about opening the vial and catching the king's last living moment like a fever — the taste of cold iron, the terrible gentleness of the Wolf as it leaned in close wearing his father's face. She wrote about the ford, where a memory-eater tore Quentin's childhood name out of him, and about the night after, when she had carried it for him in the hollow behind her ribs and promised: *I'll remember for you.* She had. She still did. Some mornings she woke with his mother's face in her mind like a photograph left in a window, sun-faded but present, and she would lie still and hold it carefully until Quentin woke, and give it back to him fresh, and watch him put it on like a coat.

Those chapters took the longest. Not because the words were hard — she had always been fluent — but because each one required her to walk back into a room she'd already survived, alone, with a lamp, and check that the walls were still standing.

They always were. That was the discovery of her middle life: survived rooms stay standing if you visit them kindly.

Thursdays were for the funny ones, and those she wrote with the door open, so the whole inn could heckle.

* * *

On the first snow of the year, she wrote the chapter about the Wolf.

Not the battle — that was Chapter 23, fought room by burning room inside Quentin's skull, and she'd needed three weeks and two pots of Marta's plum brandy to get it onto paper, rendering a war of the mind as faithfully as a woman could who'd only seen its weather from outside. No. This chapter was quieter. This was the one titled, in her neat hand, *What the Wolf Was For.*

Because she had thought about it, all these months, and she had reached a conclusion she intended to write down so firmly that no future reader — no frightened scholar, no anxious heir, no child of hers or Quentin's or anyone's — would ever have to rediscover it the hard way.

*Here is what I know about the Wolf,* she wrote, while the snow ticked against the window. *It was not a monster that happened to us. It was a hunger that we fed. A kingdom that forgets its own stories leaves a hole exactly the Wolf's size, and something will always come along and pour itself in. My grandmother understood this. That's why her trap wasn't a sword. It was a ledger. It was memory, organized, aimed, willing. The binding didn't defeat the Wolf with force. It defeated it with specificity. You cannot wear a face that everyone remembers truly.*

*So this book is not sentiment. Well. Not only sentiment. This book is fortification. Read it, whoever you are. Remember us specifically. Be a wall.*

She signed that chapter twice: once *Paloma Thackeray*, the name the mountain had given back to her, worn now like a well-mended coat; and once, underneath, in smaller letters, *keeper of the road that bears the name anyway.*

* * *

Winter deepened. The ledger grew fat.

Quentin's court duties pulled him to the capital more often now — the hollow faces filling in year by year, the palace learning to be a house again — and on the nights he was away, the inn felt too large in a specific old way, and Paloma dealt with it the way she dealt with everything now: she made something.

She made a chapter about the Inn of Small Kindnesses itself, interviewing Marta at length while Marta pretended to find the whole exercise ridiculous and answered every question in exhaustive detail. She made a chapter about Tam, whose card-cheating had become legend and whose ferry, since the roads were safe, had become prosperous, and who had recently begun teaching children to swim in the summer and fleece them at cards in the winter, "for balance." She made a chapter about Brannic and the sheep, which ran four pages and was, she felt, her finest comic work.

And on the longest night of the year, with the fire low and the snow deep and Quentin due home tomorrow, she sat down to write the last chapter.

She had been saving it. She'd known she was saving it, the way she used to know which purse in a market was worth the risk — a thief's instinct repurposed, pointing not at treasure but at timing.

Because the book had to end where it began. That was the whole point of it. That was the spell. Her grandmother had caught the kingdom in a circle of ink once, a trap; Paloma meant to catch her family in one too, a hearth. The last line of the ledger had to be the first line of everything — the sentence that had opened this whole story, back on the day a lonely thief lifted a merchant's memory on the Salt Stair road and thought she knew what her life was.

She dipped the pen. She thought about that morning. The frost on the stones. The merchant's easy, careless wealth of yesterdays. Her own rule, spoken to herself like a prayer: *never keep what you steal.* How long it had taken her to learn the corollary — *and never steal what you can make.* How a girl with empty hands and a full pack had walked down a road believing she was alone in the world, and how wrong she had been, and how the wrongness had turned out to be the best news anyone ever gave her.

Outside, the wind moved through the pass, and somewhere under the snow the mountain kept what it had been given — her childhood, her grandmother's voice, a fragment of the Wolf still being patiently digested stone-deep — and gave back, slowly, sweet by sweet, the pieces it had decided she'd earned.

Tomorrow Quentin would come home. Tomorrow there would be stew eaten too fast and cards cheated at and a fire argued over and a family at one table, the smugglers and the courtiers and the innkeeper and the ferryman's daughter, the half-remembered king and the keeper who'd emptied herself to save him. Tomorrow she would read the finished ledger aloud to all of them, every chapter, and Marta would call it a bad idea, and Quentin would complain about the embankment, and Tam would insist the sheep deserved co-authorship.

But tonight was hers. Tonight the pen was hers, and the page was blank where the ending went, and for once in her long strange double life, Paloma Thackeray knew exactly what happened next — because she was the one writing it.

She set the nib to paper and wrote, in a steady hand, the words that began it all, and closed the cover, and banked the fire, and went to bed:

*The thief came down the Salt Stair road at first light, with frost on her boots and other people's years in her pack, and she believed she was alone — which was the last mistake she ever made that turned out well.*
