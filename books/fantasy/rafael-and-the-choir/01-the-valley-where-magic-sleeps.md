## 1. The Valley Where Magic Sleeps

The wolf was dying in the alder thicket below the terraced fields, and Ansel Nakamura knelt beside it in the wet grass and did what he had done his whole life, which was listen.

"Where?" he asked softly.

*North ridge,* the wolf said. Her voice was a smell, a pressure behind his eyes, a taste like cold iron. Beast-speech had no words, not really — it came through as meaning wrapped in sensation, and after twenty-six years of it Ansel had learned to translate without thinking. *North ridge. The den is north ridge. My pups.*

"There are no pups," Ansel said gently. "I checked last winter, remember? Your den's been empty since the hard frost."

*Liar,* she said, but there was no heat in it. Old wolves lied about their litters the way old men lied about their wars. It kept them going.

He put his hand on her flank. She was gray going white at the muzzle, one hind leg at a wrong angle beneath her, and she let him touch her because she knew him, because he'd been talking to her kind since he was six years old and too young to know that what he could do would get him burned alive in any city from here to the coast. Here in Vael Hollow, magic couldn't follow anyone over the ridgeline. That was the valley's whole promise, carved into the waystone at the southern pass: **HERE THE GODS SLEEP. HERE THE CRAFT FAILS. COME AS A MORTAL OR NOT AT ALL.**

People came to Vael Hollow for that reason. People like Ansel's mother, who had carried him through the pass as an infant with a gift already stirring in him, and who had spent the rest of her life teaching him to bury it. *You speak, you die,* she used to say, not unkindly, kneading bread or mending nets or whatever her hands were doing while her eyes drilled the lesson into him. *Not might die. Will. So we don't speak.*

She was four years gone now, into the ground on the hillside where the valley buried its dead, and Ansel still lived by her rule. He spoke only to beasts, and only alone, and even that felt like holding his hand over a candle flame and calling the burn nothing.

"You should rest," he told the wolf now. "There's a dry hollow under the alders. I'll bring you meat."

*Cold coming,* she said. *Smell it in the wind. Something else in the wind too.*

"What something?"

She didn't answer right away. Her ears swiveled — both of them, toward the south pass, though her eyes were fixed on nothing.

*Iron,* she said finally. *Many men. Many horses. Coming up the pilgrim road.*

Ansel went very still.

"No one comes up the pilgrim road this time of year."

*I know what I smell.* A pause. The wolf's breathing was labored now, a wet rattle underneath. *Boy. You smell afraid. Why do you smell afraid?*

"Because I don't like soldiers," Ansel said, which was true, and also not the truth, and the wolf knew it, because wolves always knew it. But she was dying, and she didn't press.

He made her comfortable in the hollow under the alders, dragged brush around her for warmth, and promised meat by dark. Then he stood, brushed the wet grass off his knees, and walked back up through the terraces toward the village, and the whole way he kept his face smooth and empty the way his mother had taught him, the way every god-touched child learned before they learned their letters: *nothing here, nothing to see, just a man walking home.*

* * *

Vael Hollow sat in its cup of hills like a coin dropped in a purse, forty farmsteads and a mill and a stone meeting-hall and a shrine with no god in it. The shrine was the thing strangers always asked about. It had been built generations ago for whatever deity had once watched over the lowlands, and when the gods went silent — a hundred years ago, two hundred, nobody in the valley agreed — the people of Vael Hollow hadn't torn it down. They'd simply swept it out and left it empty, the way you leave a chair for someone who may never come back but might.

That evening the meeting-hall was full, and the talk was all iron.

"They came up the south pass at midday," said Marta Osei, who kept the toll ledger at the waystone and therefore knew everything first. "Six men on horseback, one officer. Gray cloaks, silver boar on the shoulder. They asked for lodging at the inn and paid honest coin, which I didn't like. Soldiers who pay are soldiers who want something."

"The usurper's colors," said old Tomas, spitting into the fire. "Silver boar. Cassian Vey's own livery, come all the way up into our valley. What in the name of nothing does Vey want with us? We've got barley and sheep and not one soul worth conquering."

"They weren't asking for souls," Marta said. "They were asking about people. God-touched folk, they said. Refugees from the lowlands who might have — " she glanced around the hall, lowered her voice as if the walls themselves might be listening, " — gifts. They're offering a reward. Ten crowns for information leading to any 'practitioner of forbidden prayer.' Ten crowns." She let that sit. In Vael Hollow, ten crowns was most of a year.

Silence, then, the particular heavy silence of people doing arithmetic they didn't want to do.

"We have no god-touched folk," said Elder Wren, who chaired the hall and had chaired it for thirty years. "That's why they come here, isn't it? We're the valley where magic sleeps. Nothing answers prayers here. Nothing burns in your palm if you get angry. Whatever the world outside is doing — and I hear it's doing something bad — it stops at our ridgeline." She looked around the room, and her gaze passed over Ansel without pausing, and Ansel kept his face smooth and empty and thanked his mother's ghost for the twenty years of practice. "So we tell them nothing, because we have nothing to tell. And tomorrow, everyone goes about their business, and the soldiers get bored, and they leave. Yes?"

Murmured agreement. But Ansel saw Tam Kessler's eyes flick sideways — Tam, who owed money to half the valley, whose brother had left for the lowlands three years ago and never written. Saw him look down at his hands. Said nothing.

*Nothing here, nothing to see.*

Afterward he walked home along the millstream in the blue dusk, and the fear caught up with him the way it always did when he was finally alone — rising out of the ground itself, out of twenty-six years of buried things. Because the soldiers weren't hunting rumors, were they. The usurper's men were asking about *gifts*, plural, *forbidden prayer*, and that meant somewhere outside the valley something had changed. Magic-dead Vael Hollow was the safest place in the world precisely because the world had been safe nowhere else. If the world outside had gotten dangerous enough that Vey's riders were climbing mountain passes looking for people like Ansel —

Then the valley wasn't a sanctuary anymore. It was a hiding place, and hiding places only worked until they were searched.

He reached his cottage at the edge of the village, last building before the alder thickets began, and stopped.

A crow sat on his fence rail. Not one of the valley crows, which knew him and begged crusts with the shameless entitlement of the well-fed. This one had mud on its feathers and a torn wing membrane and the flat, exhausted stare of something that had flown a very long way to die.

*Found you,* the crow said.

Ansel's blood went cold. "I didn't call anything. What do you mean, found me?"

*Not me,* the crow said. Its voice was thin, frayed at the edges. *Him. The singer. He fell at the tree line an hour ago. He's been saying your name to the dark, boy, and the dark sent me, because the dark is awake again, and it knows your name too.*

The wind moved through the alders, and far away — impossibly far, at the edge of hearing, at the edge of everything — Ansel thought he heard humming.

* * *

He found the stranger at the tree line where the valley's magic-dead soil met the wild country beyond, half in and half out of the shadows like a man who couldn't decide which side to die on. Younger than Ansel expected. Dark-haired, hollow-cheeked, dressed in traveling clothes that had once been fine and were now mostly blood. His lips were moving.

Ansel knelt and put two fingers to the man's throat. A pulse, thready but present.

"Hey," Ansel said. "Hey. You're in Vael Hollow. You crossed the waystone?"

The man's eyes opened. They were gray, and fever-bright, and they focused on Ansel with an intensity that made him want to step backward.

"Rafael," the man whispered. "My name is Rafael. I need — " a cough that shook his whole body — "I need to tell someone. Before it takes me. Tell someone the gods are answering again."

Ansel froze. Every instinct he owned screamed at him — *walk away, walk away, nothing here, nothing to see* — and then the man lifted one trembling hand, and hummed.

It was nothing. Three notes, maybe four, barely more than breath. It should have been nothing.

But for one impossible heartbeat, the air above the stranger's palm flickered — light where there had been no light, a shimmer like heat-haze in cold air, like a candle remembered rather than lit — here, in Vael Hollow, where magic had not followed anyone over the ridgeline in living memory. Where the waystone promised the gods slept.

Here, where Ansel had spent his entire life believing that his gift was the only secret the valley held.

The light died. The stranger's eyes rolled back, and he sagged against the roots, unconscious, still whispering: *"answering... they're answering..."*

Ansel sat back on his heels in the dark, heart slamming, the crow settling onto a branch above him like a witness come to stay.

Ten crowns for a god-touched refugee. Six gray-cloaked riders sleeping at the inn. And at his feet, a stranger who glowed.

*They're answering,* the man had said.

Ansel looked south, toward the pass, toward the world he'd spent his life pretending didn't exist — and for the first time in twenty-six years, he was afraid not of being found out, but of what finding out might actually mean.

Then he bent, took the stranger under the arms, and began to drag him home.
