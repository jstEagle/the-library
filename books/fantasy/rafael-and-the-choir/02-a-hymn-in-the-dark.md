## 2. A Hymn in the Dark

The frost came early that year, and with it the crows stopped talking to me.

Not all at once. First they grew cautious, then silent, then gone — a whole season of small gray voices folding themselves away like wings. I told myself it was weather. I told myself a lot of things in Vael Hollow. That was the valley's whole trade, if you thought about it: everyone there was telling themselves something.

I was mending a fence on the eastern slope when I heard the humming.

It came up through the ground before it came through the air, or so it seemed — a low thread of melody winding between the stones, under the roots of the black pines. I set down my mallet and stood very still, the way you do when a wolf crosses your path and you're not sure yet whether you're prey.

Nobody hummed in Vael Hollow. We were not a humming people. The valley had a way of pressing sound flat, of swallowing song before it could echo off the cliffs, and after enough years even the children learned to keep their voices low, as if the valley itself might be listening and disapprove.

But this humming did not flatten. It *rose*.

And then — I will spend the rest of my life trying to describe this, and I will fail every time — the air above the fence posts flickered. Not light, exactly. More like the memory of light, the way a dead man's face can flicker behind your eyes when someone says his name. For one heartbeat, maybe two, the space between the pines went strange and silver, as though the world had been a painting of itself and something behind it had moved.

Then it was gone. The cold rushed back in like water filling a footprint.

I stood there with my heart slamming against my ribs, and every instinct I owned said: *go home, Ansel. Bolt the door. You saw nothing.*

Instead I followed the humming downhill.

* * *

The stranger lay crumpled at the valley's edge, where the pilgrim road gave up and became a goat track and then nothing at all. He'd made it perhaps fifty paces past the boundary stone — the old carved marker that read, in letters worn nearly smooth, *VAEL HOLLOW: HERE THE WORLD RESTS* — before his legs had simply quit beneath him.

He was young-ish, or he would have been, under the ruin of him. Dark hair matted with blood. A coat that had once been fine, now burned along one sleeve in a way I recognized from the village stories: not fire from a hearth, but fire from a *branding*, the kind they pressed into flesh to mark what a man was. His boots were worn through. His lips were cracked and moving.

Humming. Even half-dead, even unconscious, he was humming.

I knelt beside him and did what I always did first, the thing that would get me burned in any other place in the world: I listened for what the animals around us knew.

The valley was quiet. It was always quiet — that was its curse and its shelter both. But a hare frozen in the bracken nearby offered me a flash of simple animal truth: *large predator passed here, days ago, following the road; the man smells of smoke and iron.* And a crow — one of the few who still tolerated me, an old scarred thing named Sorrel who had never explained why she stayed — settled on the boundary stone and sent me a single image, sharp as a dropped knife:

*A column of riders. Black banners. A cage on wheels.*

My mouth went dry. "Who's hunting you?" I asked the stranger, out loud, which was foolish, because he couldn't answer.

He answered anyway.

His eyes opened — pale gray, fever-bright — and fixed on me with an intensity that had nothing left in it but purpose. "You heard it," he whispered. "The hymn. You *heard* it."

"I don't know what you—"

"You stopped walking." A ghost of a smile. "Everyone else walks through it. The valley eats the song. But you stopped." His hand found my wrist with shocking strength. "What's your name?"

"Ansel." I said it before I could stop myself, and then hated myself for it. In Vael Hollow you didn't give your name to bleeding strangers at the edge of the world. "Ansel Nakamura."

"Rafael," he breathed. And then his eyes rolled back and he collapsed against me, still humming, that impossible thread of melody threading now through *me*, and I swear on every god whose name I didn't believe in — the air flickered again. Silver. One heartbeat. Two.

Sorrel the crow screamed once from the boundary stone and took flight east, fast, the way birds fly when they're carrying news.

* * *

I got him to my cabin by dark, dragging him the last mile on a sledge I used for firewood, praying to no one in particular that Elder Maren wouldn't see the trail. I fed him broth he was too far gone to notice. I bound the brand on his arm — and when I peeled back the bandage the villagers' healer had given me weeks ago in trade, I saw what it marked.

A circle of seven stars, seared into his skin. The sigil of the old gods. The ones the priests of the Golden Tongue called *devils*. The ones my grandmother had whispered about when she thought I was asleep — the ones who had gone silent a hundred years ago and taken all the world's magic with them, everywhere except, somehow, inside me.

Everywhere except inside the beasts I spoke to.

I sat by my own hearth with my hands shaking around a cup of tea I didn't drink, and I looked at this stranger wearing the mark of the silenced gods, hunted by men in cages on wheels, humming a song that made dead air flicker — and I understood, with terrible clarity, that my life in Vael Hollow had just ended. I simply hadn't worked out the details yet.

Near midnight he woke properly, and I gave him water, and he drank like a man who had crossed a desert, because he had.

"The usurper," he said, when he could speak above a whisper. "Lord Cassian Vey. You've heard the name?"

"Everyone's heard the name. He's a long way from here."

"He was." Rafael's laugh was a dry, broken thing. "His riders reached the lowland shrines last month. They burn them, Ansel. Not the buildings — the *prayers*. Anyone caught praying to the old gods is branded. Anyone caught *answering* prayers—" He touched the star-circle on his arm. "Anyone like me is caged. They put us in cages and march us through the towns so people learn what happens to those who listen."

"Listen to what?"

Rafael turned his head on my rough pillow and looked at me, and there was something in his face I'd only ever seen in the eyes of animals — that absolute, unguarded honesty that creatures have because they cannot afford lies.

"The gods are answering again," he said. "After a hundred years of silence. After a hundred years of the world going magic-dead and gray and hard. They are answering prayers, Ansel. Small ones. A candle relighting. A sick child waking clear-eyed. Rain where rain was promised." His voice cracked. "I'm a conductor. My choir sang those prayers straight up to heaven, and heaven *answered*, and Vey has spent three years hunting every one of us down because he cannot bear a world where power belongs to anyone but him."

The fire popped. Outside, the wind moved through the black pines, and I thought about the flickering air, the silver heartbeat, the melody that rose instead of dying.

"That song you were humming," I said. "At the valley's edge. What was it?"

"A hymn. The oldest one we have." Rafael closed his eyes. "It's how we call them. Or how we *used* to. This valley—" he gestured vaguely at the walls, the world beyond them, "—this place swallows everything. Magic can't live here. That's why your elders love it, isn't it? That's why people come here to hide."

My throat tightened. "Something like that."

"But you heard it." He opened his eyes again, and now the fever-light in them had a different quality — sharper, more certain, and aimed directly at me. "The valley ate the hymn, Ansel. It eats every hymn. But you *heard* it, and the air *moved*, and I felt it — I felt something answer, just for a moment, right before I fell. Something on *this side* of the silence." He sat up slowly, wincing. "There are only two kinds of people who can hear a hymn through dead air. Singers. And speakers."

"I don't know what you mean."

"Don't you?" Gentle. Terrible. "The hares go still when you pass. The crows sit on your fence like hired guards. I watched you talk to that crow at the boundary stone, Ansel — your lips moved, and she *answered*." He shook his head slowly. "That's not corruption. Whatever your valley taught you to be afraid of — that's not it. Beast-speech is older than the choir. Older than the temples. It's the first prayer, the one mortals made before we had words for anything else. The gods didn't fall silent because of people like you." His voice dropped. "They fell silent because people like Vey *caged* them. And now he wants to finish the job, and the only proof that they're awake again — the only proof that will make the free cities stand and fight instead of kneeling — is walking around on two legs in a valley where nobody believes in anything."

I should have denied it. I should have laughed, or lied, or done any of the ten thousand things I'd done for fifteen years to stay invisible in Vael Hollow. Every survival instinct I possessed stood in a row inside me, arms folded, shaking their heads.

But the firelight caught the star-circle on his arm, and I thought about the silver flicker in the air, and I thought about Sorrel flying east with her sharp knife of news, and I heard myself say:

"If your riders come here, they'll burn the whole valley looking for you. The elders won't protect you. They'll hand you over by morning and sleep fine afterward."

"I know."

"And if I help you, I'm not a farmer anymore. I'm whatever comes next."

"I know," Rafael said again, softer. "You can still choose otherwise. I'll leave at dawn either way. I won't bring the fire down on you."

We sat in silence then, the two of us, while the wind worried the pines and the fire burned down to embers. And I want to say I wrestled with it all night, that I weighed my life against his with wisdom and care. The truth is dumber and truer than that. The truth is that I looked at this half-dead man humming a hymn to gods I'd secretly talked around my whole life — gods whose absence I'd blamed for every gray and silent year of my exile — and I felt something crack open in my chest that wasn't fear.

It was wonder. Wonder, cracking fear open like ice on a spring river.

"Sorrel flew east," I said finally. "Crows carry news fast, but riders ride faster. If your hunters are within three days—"

"They're within two," Rafael said quietly. "I heard their dogs on the road yesterday. Dogs talk, Ansel. Even to people who can't listen."

Despite everything, despite the terror climbing my spine, I almost laughed. "Then we don't have until dawn."

"No," he agreed. "We have until the dogs arrive."

And far off across the frozen valley, faint but unmistakable, carried on the wind that had swallowed songs for a hundred years — a dog began to bark.
