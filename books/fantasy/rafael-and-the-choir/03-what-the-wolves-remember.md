## 3. What the Wolves Remember

They came for him before the frost had lifted off the windows.

Ansel heard them first — not their boots on the packed earth of the lane, but the dogs. Old Bram's herding bitch was whining at the gate in a voice he'd never heard from her, a thin thread of *wrong wrong wrong* that woke him better than any bell. The valley's dogs did not whine at neighbors.

He dressed in the dark and stood at the window of his mother's old house and watched four elders cross his yard with lanterns held high, as if they were afraid of what the light might show them and needed to see it anyway.

Maren Hale led them. Of course she did. She'd been leading Vael Hollow since before Ansel could walk, back straight as a fence post, her face carved into the particular patience of a woman who had spent forty years making sure nothing changed. Behind her came Tobin Marsh, who ran the mill and owed Ansel money; Sister Odile, who kept the shrine to no one in particular because keeping shrines was habit older than memory; and young Pell, Maren's nephew, whose job it was to look stern and mostly managed confusion.

They knocked the way people knock when they already know you're going to answer.

Ansel opened the door. "Maren."

"Ansel." Her eyes went past him, through the dim room, cataloguing. "We need to talk about your guest."

"He's sick. Fevered half out of his mind. I've been dosing him with willow bark."

"Mm." She stepped inside without being asked, which was how Maren entered every room in the valley, including ones she didn't own. "Tobin says he heard singing. Night before last. Out past the boundary stones, where nobody sings, because nobody goes there, because nothing out there works."

"The fever," Ansel said. "Fevered men sing."

"Fevered men babble." She turned to face him fully, and the lantern light caught the gray in her braid. "This man walked into our valley from outside, Ansel. Nothing walks in from outside. The valley doesn't let things in. And yet here he is, in your house, humming tunes that make my teeth ache and my dog hide under the porch. So you'll tell me plainly: who is he, and why did the valley forget its own rules for him?"

The truth sat in Ansel's chest like a swallowed stone. *He's a conductor. He sings prayers straight to gods we were told were dead or never existed, and he says they're answering again, and he says men like me — men who can hear beasts speak — are exactly what the usurper burns first.*

What he said was: "I found him collapsed at the stones. I couldn't leave him to die."

"No," Maren agreed softly. "You couldn't. That's always been your trouble." She let the silence stretch until it creaked. "The council meets tonight. You'll bring him, or you'll bring an account of him. One way or another, Ansel, this ends in the meeting hall."

* * *

He went to the wolves instead.

There was a den above the tree line, past the last farm, in rocks so old the valley's deadness hadn't quite soaked into them — or so Ansel liked to believe, though he knew better. Magic slept everywhere here. But the wolves still talked, because beast-speech wasn't magic, not really. It was older than magic. It was just listening, done properly.

The pack matriarch was gray-muzzled now, a female he'd known since she was a pup stealing his snares. She lay on warm stone with two yearlings drowsing against her flanks, and when Ansel settled cross-legged in the scree she opened one amber eye.

*Small-voice,* she said. Her name for him. *You smell of fear and lies.*

"Both mine?"

*The lies are yours. The fear belongs to the whole valley.* She raised her head. *Something crossed the boundary. Something that hums.*

"You felt it too?"

*Everything felt it. For one breath, the world remembered itself.* She said it without drama, the way she might report a wounded elk. *Then it went back to sleep. But the birds have been strange since. The crows came down from the pass yesterday, all of them, in one river of wings, and crows only move like that when they are fleeing something or following something.*

Ansel picked up a flat stone and turned it over in his hands. "Tell me about the lowlands."

The matriarch was quiet a moment. When she spoke again, her voice had changed — slower, heavier, the tone of a creature delivering a death sentence to a herd mate.

*Fire,* she said. *Three nights south, the grasslands burn. The deer came through the high passes in numbers I have not seen in my life, and they do not stop to graze. They run like animals running from the end of the world. And among them run dogs — farm dogs, shepherd dogs — wearing collars, small-voice. Dogs whose people are ash.*

His hands went still on the stone.

*And the two-legs,* she continued. *The ones in metal. They march up the pilgrim road in columns, and they take villages, and where they find anyone who hears voices — bird voices, wolf voices, the voices under the world — they make fires.* A pause. *The crows say the fires sing. They say the burning ones sing while they burn, hymns, and the soldiers cannot make them stop.*

Ansel set the stone down very carefully, as if it might go off.

"There's more," he said. "You've saved the worst for last. I can hear it in you."

The matriarch looked at him for a long moment, and the yearlings stirred uneasily, sensing the shift in her.

*My grandmother's grandmother told it to her,* she said. *Before the silence. When the world was loud. There were two-legs then who sang — not the way your kind sings, but the way the wind sings, the way the pack sings at the moon. Many throats made one throat. And through that one throat, the great voices answered. The sky-voice. The deep-earth voice. The voice in green growing things.* Her ears flattened. *A choir, the old ones called it. They sang the gods awake every spring. And then the two-legs grew afraid of their own song, or greedy for it, and there was a war, and after the war the valley was made — this place where everything sleeps — and the choir was scattered to the winds.*

"And now Rafael—"

*Is one throat looking for the rest,* she finished. *Or is bait in a trap. We cannot know, small-voice. We can only tell you what the roads smell like.* She rose, stiff in her old joints, and pressed her muzzle briefly against his hand — an intimacy she permitted perhaps twice a year. *The metal men will come here eventually. Valleys do not stay forgotten forever. When they come, they will ask who heard voices. And everyone in this valley has watched you talk to my pack across ten winters, and everyone has decided not to see it, because deciding not to see is how valleys survive.*

She pulled back and met his eyes.

*But deciding not to see stops working the day someone makes them look.*

* * *

The council met by lamplight in the meeting hall, and Ansel stood in the middle of it with Rafael's fate in his hands and thirty faces turned toward him like a jury of weathered, worried stone.

Rafael himself they'd left sleeping at Ansel's house — too weak to argue, which Ansel suspected was the only reason the elders hadn't dragged him along. Maren sat at the head of the long table, hands folded, and let the silence do most of her talking.

"Your account, then," she said finally.

"He's a musician. A singer. He was traveling alone and fell ill at the boundary stones."

"A musician," Tobin Marsh repeated, sour. "Walking the pilgrim road in wartime. Alone. Singing songs that curdle milk — I've heard it from three separate people, Ansel, don't look at me like that."

"My dog won't go near his house," someone called from the back.

"Your dog won't go near Tobin's mill either," Ansel said, and got a rough laugh, and hated himself a little for spending it.

Maren didn't laugh. "Sister Odile has a question for you."

The old priestess leaned forward. She was the oldest thing in the valley except the mountains, and her eyes were milky and missed nothing. "Child," she said, "when you found him, what did the valley feel like?"

The hall went quiet. Ansel's mouth was dry. "I don't understand."

"Yes, you do." Odile's voice was gentle and terrible. "For one heartbeat, the night he came, every soul in this valley dreamed the same dream. Green light. Voices under the ground, glad voices. My grandmother told me the valley used to feel that way all the time, before the Founders sealed it. Before we chose to be safe instead of awake." She folded her hands. "So I ask you again, and think hard before you lie to an old woman: what did the valley feel like?"

*Like coming home,* said a voice in his blood that was not his own. *Like the first day of spring after a long winter. Like being remembered.*

What he said was: "It felt like nothing. Like always."

Odile held his gaze for a long, long moment. Then she sat back, and something in her expression told him she would carry his lie gently, and use it later.

Maren stood. "Then here is what happens. The stranger stays under watch until he can walk. When he can walk, he leaves the way he came. If the council finds he's brought trouble behind him—" she looked around the hall, at the farms, the children, the whole small mortal architecture of Vael Hollow— "then we decide as a valley what's owed. Nobody acts alone. Not even you, Ansel."

"Not even me," he agreed.

But walking home under the cold stars, the wolves' words moved through him like water through gravel, rearranging everything. *Dogs in collars running from ash. Hymns sung on pyres. Columns of metal men on the pilgrim road, marching toward passes that led, eventually, here.*

They would come. Maybe a season, maybe a year. And when they came, Maren's council would sit in this same hall and decide, carefully, reasonably, to give up whoever they had to — and Ansel knew, with a certainty that felt like the wolves' teeth, that the person they'd give up first was him.

* * *

He packed by candlelight. Not much: his father's knife, dried meat, the wool cloak, a coil of good rope. His hands shook and he let them.

Rafael watched from the bed, propped on one elbow, fever-bright eyes tracking every item into the bag. "You're leaving."

"We're leaving. Can you ride?"

"I can sit on a horse if the horse agrees to it." A ghost of the man's strange smile. "Why? What happened tonight?"

"They're going to send you away the moment you can walk. Politely. With food for the road." Ansel cinched the bag shut. "And the moment you're gone, they'll start wondering about me. About the years I've spent going up the mountain and coming back knowing things I shouldn't know. Maren's not cruel, Rafael. She's careful. Careful people feed strangers to wolves to keep the village safe. I've watched her do it with disputes, with blights, with a tax collector once." He shouldered the bag. "I'm not going to be here when the feeding starts."

Rafael swung his legs off the bed, wincing. "You believe me, then. About the gods answering."

"I believe the wolves." Ansel almost smiled. "They remember a choir that sang the gods awake, before the war, before the silence. They remember fire coming for anyone who listens to the wrong voices. And they remember that valleys like this one are just places where hiding gets renamed as living." He looked at the window, at the gray line starting under the shutter. "I've been hiding since I was nine years old. Ten winters of talking to wolves in secret and lying to everyone I love about who I am. And the lowlands are burning, and the crows are fleeing over the pass, and I am done deciding not to see."

Rafael stood, unsteady, and gripped the bedframe. "It will cost you everything you know."

"It already has." Ansel opened the door onto the freezing dawn. Frost smoked off the yard. Somewhere upslope, a wolf began to sing — one long note, then another joining, then the whole pack, a farewell or a blessing, he couldn't tell and didn't need to.

They went out together through the sleeping lanes, past the shrine where Sister Odile stood waiting in the dark as if she'd known, and she pressed something into his hand without a word — a small wooden charm, ancient, worn smooth, shaped like a bird mid-flight — and stepped back and said only, "Awake suits you better than safe, child."

At the boundary stones, Ansel stopped once and looked back. Vael Hollow lay below in the blue morning shadow, chimneys just beginning to smoke, every roof he'd ever known. He grieved it there, quickly and completely, the way you pull a tooth.

Then Rafael touched his arm and pointed east, where the pilgrim road climbed out of the valley toward a horizon stained faintly, impossibly, with the orange glow of distant burning.

"That glow," Rafael said quietly. "That's three days' ride ahead of us, not behind. We're riding toward it."

"Why would we ride toward it?"

"Because," Rafael said, pulling his cloak tight against the wind, "the next voice the choir needs isn't a singer at all. It's someone the beasts already trust. And Ansel — the man who can gather that voice is standing in a burned village right now, praying to gods he's been told are dead, and Cassian Vey's riders are two hours behind him."

Ansel looked at the fire-stained sky, and felt the wolves' last words settle over him like a mantle: *when they come, they will ask who heard voices.*

"Then we'd better ride faster than they do," he said, and stepped past the boundary stones, out of the valley where magic slept, into a world that had just begun to wake.
