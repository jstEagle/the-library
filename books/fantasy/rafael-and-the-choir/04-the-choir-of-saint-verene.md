## 4. The Choir of Saint Verene

Rafael woke to the smell of woodsmoke and the sound of someone arguing with a mule.

Not shouting — arguing. Low, patient, the way you'd talk to a stubborn child. He opened one eye and found Ansel crouched beside the animal they'd bought two days ago in the last village before the pass, his head tilted at that particular angle Rafael had come to recognize. Listening.

The mule flicked an ear. Ansel nodded, as if the ear had said something worth agreeing with, and adjusted the saddle strap.

*He doesn't even know he does it,* Rafael thought. *Or he knows, and he's spent so long hiding it that hiding has become breathing.*

"You're staring," Ansel said, without turning around.

"I'm assessing," Rafael said. "You're my most valuable asset. I like to know where my assets are."

"Your asset wants to know why we're walking toward the war instead of away from it."

"Because away from it is where it will eventually arrive." Rafael sat up, wincing. The fever had broken in the night, but it had left him feeling like a wet page — every word written on him too clearly. "And because I have something to show you. Something I should have shown you three days ago, when I was too busy dying to be honest."

Ansel finished with the mule and came to sit across the dead fire from him. Up close, in the gray morning light, Rafael could see the toll the last few days had taken on him: the hollows under his eyes, the way his hands wouldn't quite stay still. A man who had spent years listening for boots on gravel.

"I'm listening," Ansel said.

"No, you're not. Not yet." Rafael reached into his coat and drew out the tuning fork. Plain steel, unremarkable, the kind any choir-master might carry. "This is going to seem strange. I need you to promise me you won't run."

"I don't make promises I can't keep."

"Then just — stay seated. That's all I ask."

Rafael struck the fork against his knee and held it up.

The note was small. That was the first thing. It wasn't power, not really — not the way the old stories told it, thunder and pillars of fire. It was a single clean tone, and it went out into the morning air of Vael Hollow, into the valley where magic had been dead for two hundred years, and it *waited.*

For one heartbeat, nothing.

Then the world answered.

It started at the edges of Rafael's vision — a shimmer, like heat off summer stone, except it was cold and silver and it moved *wrong*, flowing uphill, pooling around the fork's note the way water finds a drain. The dead grass at their campsite stirred without wind. The mule snorted and shied. And somewhere beneath them, deep under the valley floor, Rafael felt something vast turn over in its sleep and almost — *almost* — open one eye.

Then the note faded, and the valley sealed itself again, smooth and silent as a frozen lake.

Ansel hadn't moved. But his face had gone through terror and out the other side into something hungrier.

"What was that?"

"A prayer," Rafael said. "The oldest kind. Before temples, before priests, before anyone thought the gods needed to be addressed through intermediaries — there were people who discovered that certain sounds, sung together in certain ways, could carry a voice straight up. Past the silence. Into whatever place the gods went when they stopped answering." He turned the fork over in his fingers. "We called ourselves the Choir of Saint Verene. Verene was the first of us, four hundred years ago. She figured out that the gods hadn't died when they went quiet. They'd been *muffled.* And that a human voice, trained right, joined with others, could slip under the muffling like a needle under a door."

"And the others? Where are these others now?"

Rafael looked at the fire pit. At the mule. Anywhere but at Ansel's face.

"There were forty-one of us when I was born into it. Singers, and the families who kept them fed and hidden. We lived in the hill country south of the Serein, moving every season, singing once a month whether we felt heard or not — because faith, real faith, is doing the thing when there's no proof it works." His mouth twisted. "Eighteen months ago, Lord Cassian Vey began buying land near our camps. Then soldiers came instead of buyers. We scattered. Eleven of us made it to the sanctuary at Saint Verene's original chapel, in the mountains east of here. Two weeks ago, they found that too."

Ansel was very still. "How many are left?"

"Seven. Scattered along the pilgrim road, moving separately toward the free cities. Each carrying half of a hymn no single voice can sing." He met Ansel's eyes at last. "That's what I was doing at the edge of your valley, dying of a crossbow bolt. Carrying my half to the next singer. And being hunted by men who know exactly what a choir-master looks like."

* * *

They walked all day, and Rafael talked, because he could feel the questions building in Ansel like water behind a dam, and he would rather the dam break on his terms than in front of strangers.

"The usurper isn't hunting us because we're rebels," he said. "Rebels he could buy or kill. He's hunting us because of what happened eight months ago, at the siege of Marrow Ford."

"I've heard of it. The walls didn't fall. They *sang.*"

"They sang." Rafael laughed, and it came out bitter. "That was us. Thirty singers on the ramparts, singing the Hymn of Unbinding, and the old gods answered for the first time in living memory. Real answer, full strength. The river rose. The siege engines rotted overnight. And Vey's own soldiers — half of them raised on the old rites before their grandmothers' gods were banned — heard it and *wept.*" He shook his head. "Vey won the field anyway, eventually. Numbers. But he learned something that day that frightens him more than any army: the gods are waking up, and they aren't waking up on his side."

"So he kills singers."

"He *collects* them." Rafael let that word sit. "I've seen what happens to the ones he takes. There's a tower in his citadel at Corvane — the Golden Spire, they call it. Singers go in. They don't come out. And the prayers—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "The prayers we sing have to go somewhere, Ansel. When a god answers, that answer is power, loose in the world, looking for a door. Vey has built himself a door. A golden one. And everything the choir ever sang awake, he's been standing underneath it with his mouth open."

Ansel walked in silence for a long time. When he finally spoke, his voice was strange.

"When I talk to animals. What am I actually doing?"

And there it was. The question Rafael had been carrying since the wolf, since the first night, since he'd watched a fugitive boy murmur to a dying predator and seen the predator *understand.*

"Can I tell you what I believe?" Rafael said. "Not doctrine. Belief."

"Yes."

"Before the gods went silent, every living thing prayed. That's the oldest teaching — the one Verene found carved in the chapel stones. Humans sang it. But the beasts never stopped." He gestured at the treeline, at the hawks riding the thermals, at the mule plodding behind them. "A wolf howling at the moon is a prayer. A crow warning its fellows is a litany. Every creature that has ever gone hungry and cried out into the dark has been praying, continuously, for two hundred years, into a silence that never answered. Except—"

"Except it did," Ansel said hoarsely. "Sometimes. To me."

"Except sometimes it did. To you." Rafael watched him carefully. "You're not corrupted, Ansel. You're not cursed, or touched by anything that needs burning. You're what a human being looks like when the oldest form of prayer never got trained out of them. The beasts hear the gods the way fish hear water. And somehow — bloodline, accident, grace, I don't pretend to know — you hear *them.* You're not a broken version of what we do." He smiled, and it hurt. "We're a broken version of what you do."

Ansel stopped walking.

Rafael got several paces ahead before he realized, and turned. The other man stood in the middle of the road with his fists clenched and his eyes bright and wet, and Rafael understood that he had just handed a drowning man the one sentence he'd waited his whole life to hear.

"Say it again," Ansel said.

"You're not corruption. You're the original."

Ansel nodded once, sharply, and wiped his face with the back of his hand like a boy, and started walking again, and neither of them mentioned it after that. Some things are too large to survive being discussed twice.

* * *

They made camp in the ruins of a waystation, one wall still standing, and Rafael dug the last of the travel bread out of his pack and split it. Ansel ate mechanically, then set down his portion.

"The seven singers. Your scattered choir. If we find them — if we get all the halves of this hymn to the free cities — what happens?"

"Then we sing it in front of the council. All of it, complete, with witnesses. And the hymn does what hymns do." Rafael poked the fire. "It carries. Straight up, past the muffling, past whatever Vey has built to catch the answers. And the gods answer *publicly,* in front of ten thousand people who will see it with their own eyes. You can murder a choir in the mountains. You can't murder a miracle performed before the assembled free cities."

"And if Vey gets there first? If he offers them peace — his peace, worship-his-golden-boot-heel peace — before we ever open our mouths?"

"Then we lose," Rafael said simply. "Not a battle. Everything. Because a god who answers prayers is powerful, but a god who owns the only door to heaven owns the people praying. Forever."

The fire cracked. Somewhere down the slope, a fox screamed, high and lonely, and Rafael saw Ansel's head turn toward the sound — quick, involuntary, the motion of a man hearing his name called.

"It's saying there are men on the far ridge," Ansel said quietly. "Four of them. With dogs. They've been circling since dusk."

Rafael felt the warmth drain out of the evening. "Ours?"

"No. Too disciplined. They move like wolves that hate each other but work anyway." Ansel's jaw tightened. "Rafael. They're not following our trail. They're following *yours.* They knew which road we'd take."

"How could they possibly—"

"Because someone told them." Ansel looked at him across the fire, and his eyes were not unkind, but they were not soft either. "You said you were carrying your half of the hymn to the next singer. Who else knew that? Who knew the route?"

Rafael opened his mouth. Closed it. Thought of the sanctuary at Saint Verene's chapel, and the seven who escaped, and the ones who hadn't.

"One of us," he said slowly, "may have been bought. Or broken."

Above them, on the far ridge, a dog barked once — a sharp, carrying note, like a struck bell — and Ansel was already on his feet, dousing the fire with his boot heel, whispering something low and urgent to the darkness that Rafael could not hear but the darkness, evidently, could.
