## 5. Ashes on the Pilgrim Road

The war announced itself before we saw it.

First the crows — not the scattered handfuls that squabbled over valley roadkill, but rivers of them, hundreds deep, flowing east to west above the pilgrim road like smoke given feathers. Then the smell: burnt thatch and something sweeter underneath that I refused to name until I had to.

"Embermere," Rafael said, standing at the crest of the hill with his travel-worn coat snapping in the wind. He didn't need a map. He'd been singing his way across these lands for twenty years, and he knew every village the way a priest knows his psalms. "Or what's left of it."

I had never seen a burned village. Vael Hollow's wars were all stories — old men arguing about borders settled before my grandfather was born. The magic-dead valley kept even violence drowsy. So I wasn't prepared for the scale of it. Embermere wasn't a ruin; it was a carcass. Forty homes, a granary, a shrine with its bell torn down and dragged into the square — all of it blackened, collapsed, still breathing thin gray threads into the cold morning air.

And it wasn't old ash. This had happened within days.

"They were harboring someone," Rafael said quietly.

"How do you know?"

He pointed with his chin. Nailed to the shrine's doorframe, which alone had been left standing like a warning post, hung a sheet of hammered gold. Stamped into it, official as a tax notice: *This village gave shelter to a god-speaker. Let all who harbor the god-touched share its fate.* Below it, the seal of Lord Cassian Vey — a sunburst with a human face at its center, smiling.

"The usurper doesn't burn villages for wolves or bandits," Rafael said. "Only for us."

*Us.* The word landed strange in my chest. Three weeks ago I'd been a man who talked to dying animals in secret and hated himself for the relief when they died and couldn't tell anyone. Now I was *us.*

We picked our way down into the village because we had no choice — the pilgrim road ran straight through it, and skirting it would cost a day we didn't have. That was the story I told myself, anyway. The truth was that I heard something.

Not with my ears. With the other sense, the one I'd spent thirty years pretending was indigestion or nerves or an overactive conscience. A thread of pain, faint and fraying, coming from beneath the collapsed granary.

"There's something alive under there," I said.

Rafael looked at me. "Animal?"

"A dog. Maybe two." I swallowed. "And something else. Something that thinks in words." I hesitated over the next part, the part I'd never said aloud to another living soul. "It's praying."

Rafael went very still. "Praying," he repeated. "To whom?"

"I don't know. It's just — repeating. Over and over. *Please. Please. Please.*" My hands had started shaking. "Rafael, if there's a person alive under that granary, and soldiers did this—"

"Then we dig," Rafael said simply, and pulled the shovel from his pack.

* * *

The dog found us first.

We'd barely started shifting the first beam when a shape exploded out of a gap in the rubble — a huge, filthy, half-starved sheepdog, ribs like a harp, hackles up, teeth bared. Rafael froze. I didn't. I knelt, slow, and let the thing I usually strangled rise up through me instead.

*I'm not going to hurt you,* I said, in the way that isn't sound. *Neither is he. We're here for the one under the stones.*

The dog's snarl faltered. Animals always faltered at that first touch — I'd watched it happen a hundred times, that flicker of *how*, that animal equivalent of a man crossing himself. But this dog was past fear. She was running on duty alone.

*Three humans under the floor,* she pushed at me, images more than words: darkness, dust, a small hand gripping her fur. *Two grown. One child. They stopped calling yesterday. I stayed.*

*You stayed,* I echoed, and something in my chest cracked open. Thirty years of hiding this gift, treating it as a disease, and here was a starving dog who had done with it the bravest thing I'd ever heard of.

Her name, as much as she had one, was Bracken. Her people were the Thellers — miller's family, the images told me, flour-dust and warm bread and a girl named Pip who fed her crusts under the table. The girl was the small hand in the dark.

We dug for two hours. Rafael worked like a man possessed, hauling beams twice his size, and I realized somewhere in the second hour that he was humming — low, almost subvocal, that same melody from the night he'd collapsed at the valley's edge. And where he hummed, the rubble moved easier. Stones shifted a finger's width when they should have needed a lever. Beams groaned and rolled aside as if tired of holding their dead weight.

"You're doing that on purpose now," I panted.

"The gods are listening again," Rafael said, not stopping. "Might as well give them something worth hearing."

We got the family out through the granary's collapsed root cellar. The father, Hollis Theller, came up coughing and blind with dust, and the moment he could see he grabbed Rafael by the collar and said, hoarse: "They took Marta. The captain took my wife. Said god-speakers' kin make good bait."

"Which captain?" Rafael asked.

"Harrow. Captain Harrow." Hollis spat red-tinged phlegm. "He made us watch the shrine burn. Made Pip watch. Said it was mercy, letting us live." His eyes were hollowed out, a man whose world had been reduced to a hole in the ground. "There are others. From the outlying farms. Nineteen of us were in the cellar when the soldiers came. Some fled north to the fens. The rest—" he gestured weakly at the ruins "—scattered. Harrow's patrols are sweeping the hedgerows. He means to collect every last one. Proof of his work, he called them."

Pip, seven years old and gray-faced with dust, had wrapped both arms around Bracken's neck and not let go since.

I looked at Rafael. Rafael looked at me. Neither of us said the obvious thing, which was: *we have proof to carry, a mission, no time, no army, no plan.* Neither of us said the other obvious thing either, which was: *nineteen people.*

"We'll need horses," was all Rafael said. "And a route through the fens. And a miracle."

"I know where to find the first two," I said. "The third one's your department."

* * *

The horses were the easy part. Vey's cavalry had picketed twelve destriers in Embermere's orchard, using it as a forward camp, and horses will always listen to me before they'll listen to any man with a whip. By full dark, six of the best had quietly decided, all on their own, to wander out of the orchard and gather at the fen's edge where nineteen frightened farmers were materializing out of hedgerows like ghosts answering a dinner bell.

Bracken did the herding. I've never worked with a better partner and I've worked with wolves.

The hard part was the fens themselves — black water, sucking mud, paths known only to eel-catchers and smugglers. Old Wenna, a grandmother with a crossbow she clearly knew how to use, led us along the ridges. We moved in silence, nineteen souls and six horses and one dog, and above us the crows kept streaming west, and I finally understood why.

They weren't scavengers. They were messengers. Every murder of crows that passed overhead carried, faint as a whispered prayer, the same report: *riders, riders, riders, moving along the south road, torches, hounds.*

"Harrow's hunting parties," I murmured to Rafael. "Three of them. Converging."

"Can you turn them?"

"Crows can harass. They can't stop thirty men." I closed my eyes and reached wider, out into the fen's dark congregation — frogs gone silent, herons frozen in the shallows, wild ponies on the far ridge, and foxes, three foxes trotting the same ridgeline we were on, drawn by curiosity. "I can slow them. Confuse the hounds. Buy an hour, maybe."

"Buy the hour," Rafael said. "I'll spend it."

And he began, very softly, to sing.

I felt it go through the column behind us like warmth through cold hands. The crying baby quieted. The limping miller straightened. Even the horses stepped lighter. It wasn't the hymn he'd sung in the cathedral dark — this was smaller, working-man's music, a walking song with a blessing folded inside it, and I understood suddenly that this was what the choir actually *was*: not spectacle, but sustenance. Bread baked into melody.

Then the torches came over the southern ridge, and everything got worse.

They'd cut ahead of us — not following, anticipating. A dozen riders and twice that many foot, hounds straining at leads, and at their center a man on a black courser with a voice like a whetstone: "Lights on the fen! Company, form line!"

"Go," Rafael breathed. "Take them north along the eel-cutter's path. I'll hold his eyes."

"Rafael—"

"*Go.*"

I went. There wasn't time for heroics about it. I put two fingers in my mouth — a habit from another life — and then didn't whistle at all, just *pushed*: *ponies, run the west ridge, make noise; hounds, there are foxes here, better foxes, juicier foxes, forget the men.* The fen erupted. Wild ponies thundered along the ridgeline dragging their shadows, the hounds broke their leads in twos and threes to chase what smelled like the finest hunt of their lives, and Harrow's neat line dissolved into cursing chaos.

For ninety seconds it worked beautifully.

Then a lantern swung and caught me full in the face, ten yards from Captain Harrow himself, and I saw him see me — a stranger, standing apart from the fleeing villagers, mouth open, making no sound at all while every animal in the fen obeyed him.

Harrow's eyes went wide. Then narrow. Then hungry.

"YOU," he roared, wheeling his horse. "The god-speaker! Take him ALIVE — Vey wants the beast-talker breathing!"

Six riders peeled off after me. Behind me I heard Rafael's song change — rising, sharp, a blade of harmony — and heard men scream and horses balk, and I ran, lungs burning, Bracken streaking at my knee, the fen water grabbing my boots—

And then the ground lit gold ahead of me. Not firelight. Not lanterns.

A ring of standing stones I hadn't noticed in the dark, each one carved with worn spirals, each one now glowing like a banked coal waking. And standing between me and the riders, staff planted, was a woman in traveling grays with a singer's build and a soldier's stance, and behind her, melting out of the fen mist, a dozen more figures — and from their throats, in unison, came a single sustained note that made the riders' horses scream and refuse the ground.

The woman looked at me over her shoulder, and I saw the same melody in her face that lived in Rafael's.

"Well," she said. "You must be the reason he stopped writing."
