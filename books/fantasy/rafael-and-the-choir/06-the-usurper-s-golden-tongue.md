## 6. The Usurper's Golden Tongue

They took him at the ford of the River Selle, three days out from Saint Verene, and Rafael almost admired how clean it was. No shouting, no swords drawn. Just a toll of soldiers in black-and-gold who checked his papers, smiled at them, and said the Lord Protector would like a word.

The word turned out to be four days east, in a column of wagons with velvet seats and barred windows, guarded by men who never spoke to him and never stopped humming.

That was the part that frightened him. Every soldier in Vey's army hummed. Snatches of melody, half-remembered hymns, worn down over miles of marching into something tuneless and gray — but underneath it, unmistakable, was the skeleton of the Verene liturgies. The songs his choir had sung to carry prayers upward. Someone had taken the music apart and fed it back to these men as discipline, as cadence, as something to fill the silence between orders.

*He's using our songs as leash-lengths,* Rafael thought, watching the lowlands burn past the barred window. *And they don't even know what they're carrying.*

On the fifth day the wagons climbed into hills that smelled of iron and cut stone, and Rafael was delivered to Caer Vey — not a castle so much as a cathedral that had decided to become one. Spires everywhere. Bells in every tower, and every bell wrapped in chains.

They walked him through halls floored in marble the color of cream, past statues of saints whose faces had been recut into Vey's likeness, and brought him at last to a door of gilded cedar that opened onto warmth, light, and the smell of honeyed wine.

Lord Cassian Vey was smaller than his coins.

That was Rafael's first thought, and he hated himself for having it, because everything else about the man was built to make you forget it. He sat in a chair too large for him, dressed in gold thread on white, and he was beautiful in the way a blade is beautiful — all edges and polish, nothing wasted. His eyes were pale amber, almost the same color as the light pouring through the windows behind him, so that when he looked at you it seemed the room itself was looking.

"Rafael of the Choir of Saint Verene," Vey said. His voice was warm. It was a very good voice; Rafael recognized the training in it, the same breath-work, the same placement. A singer's voice. "Or should I say, *conductor*. Sit. Please. You've had an ugly week and I'd rather this conversation be a kind one."

There was only one other chair. Rafael did not sit.

Vey smiled like a man watching a horse decide whether to bite. "Standing, then. Suit yourself." He lifted a decanter, poured two glasses of wine the color of garnets, and set one on the table between them, within easy reach. An offering. A test. "Do you know why I let your people scatter instead of burning them where they stood? I could have. I had the names. I had the houses."

"You wanted the choir broken, not dead," Rafael said. "Dead singers don't sing."

"Exactly." Vey's smile widened by a degree. "You understand economics. I admire that in an enemy." He rose, crossed to the window, hands clasped behind his back. Below, the chained bells caught the afternoon light. "Tell me what you felt at the shrine at Marrow's Ford. Three weeks ago. Be precise."

Rafael kept his face still. Marrow's Ford was a ruin, an old roadside chapel where he'd stopped to pray — really pray, the old way, harmonics and breath and the ache in the chest that meant someone was listening. And someone *had* been listening. For the first time in twenty years, the prayer had gone somewhere, had come back changed, warm as a hand on the back of his neck.

"I don't know what you mean."

"Mm." Vey turned. "I'll tell you what *I* felt. Because I felt it too. All across the valley lands, all along the pilgrim roads — little flickers. Prayers going out and coming back *answered*, for the first time since the Silence. Do you know what that means, conductor? It means the gods are waking up. And it means that every answered prayer is—" he paused, savoring it, choosing the word the way a man chooses a knife, "—*unclaimed property*."

"They aren't property."

"Everything is property," Vey said pleasantly. "The only question is who holds the deed." He came back toward the table, and up close Rafael could see it now — the thing under the beauty. A faint golden shimmer beneath the skin of the man's throat, moving when he swallowed, like light through honey. Like something swallowed that hadn't finished being swallowed. "You've noticed. Good. Yes. This is what an answered prayer looks like, once it's been... collected."

Rafael's stomach went cold. "You've been eating them."

"*Harvesting*," Vey corrected, with the mild patience of a teacher. "A prayer is a request made in faith. An answer is power moving from the divine to the mortal. For twenty years the pipe was blocked, and then—" he snapped his fingers, and the sound rang oddly in the room, doubled, harmonic, wrong, "—it wasn't. And now every farmer praying for rain, every mother praying for her fevered child, every soldier praying before a charge — their answers flow, and I stand at the mouth of the river with a cup." He picked up the wine glass and turned it slowly. "Do you know how many prayers a kingdom says in a day? Millions. I have drunk millions. I am, at present, the most prayed-to entity in the world. They call me Lord Protector. They kneel. They ask. And I *answer*."

"That's why the bells are chained."

"The bells are chained because bells are honest," Vey said, and for one moment the pleasantness dropped and something older and hungrier looked out through the amber eyes. "A bell doesn't care whose hymn it carries. Ring one true note near a waking god and the whole city hears it. No. The age of open channels is over. One voice. One cup. Mine."

* * *

Rafael had planned, in the wagon, for torture. He had planned for the rack, for fire, for the slow questions. He had not planned for this: Vey pouring more wine, settling back into the oversized chair, and saying, gently:

"Now. Your choir."

"Don't."

"Forty-one names I have," Vey continued, unbothered. "Sera Voss, singing under a dead girl's name in the fishmarkets of Corvel. The Tam brothers, digging slate. Little Odile, who was twelve when you disbanded them — do you remember her voice? Of course you do. First soprano. A voice like a needle finding the vein." He laced his fingers. "My men are patient. They have been following them for months, waiting for my word. Waiting, in fact, for *you* — because singers are sentimental creatures, and they always, always drift back toward their conductor."

"What do you want?"

"Such relief in your voice. Good. Honesty is faster." Vey leaned forward. "The choir is the last instrument in the world that can carry a prayer to the old gods *without passing through me*. You know this. I know this. So I offer you a trade, conductor, simple and clean: the forty-one live. In exchange, you bring me the thing you were carrying east."

Rafael said nothing. But Vey watched him the way a hawk watches a field, and whatever he saw there made him nod slowly.

"There's a boy," Vey said softly. "A beast-speaker. Valley-born. My riders found wolf-tracks around a burned patrol in the Hollow passes, and wolves do not mourn soldiers." He smiled. "The gods are answering prayers again, and they chose to answer *him*. Do you understand what that means? Not through a choir. Not through a cathedral. Through a filthy valley boy talking to animals. That is the old covenant, Rafael. That is the first tongue. And if he stands in front of the free cities and opens his mouth—"

"He's just a boy."

"He's a *door*," Vey said, and the gold under his skin flared bright enough to see. "And doors can be closed. Bring him to me. Sing him to me, if you must — you're very good at making people trust you, I'm told. Do this, and your forty-one singers grow old in peace, unmolested, perhaps even comfortable. Refuse..." He spread his hands, apologetic, gracious, monstrous. "...and I will send you each of their voices. Preserved. I have a way, now, of keeping what I take. You could listen to them forever."

The room was very quiet. Somewhere below, a chained bell shifted in the wind and made no sound at all.

Rafael thought of Odile's needle-voice. Of Sera laughing with fish scales on her wrists. Of Ansel Nakamura standing at the valley's edge with a dying wolf's head cradled in his hands, whispering to it in a language older than any hymn Rafael had ever conducted — and the wolf, impossibly, *comforted*.

He thought about how easy it would be. How easy Vey was making it look, which meant it wasn't easy at all, which meant Vey was afraid.

"Yes," Rafael heard himself say. "I'll need time. And I'll need to believe you'll keep your word."

"Oh, you'll believe me." Vey rose, radiant, generous, terrible, and pressed the wine glass into Rafael's hand. "Drink. We leave for the council road at dawn — you'll travel with my own guard, as an honored guest. And conductor?" At the door, Vey paused, and the light through the windows made a halo of him, perfect and false. "When you find the boy — and you will — remember that I answered your prayer. Whatever you call that, it isn't betrayal. It's just the world working as it should. One voice. One cup."

The door closed. Rafael stood alone in the golden room, wine untouched in his hand, and beneath the floorboards, beneath the marble, beneath the chained and silent bells, he felt it — a vast, muffled thrumming, like a heart beating under a floor, like forty-one voices singing in a room with no doors.

Somewhere east, Ansel was walking toward him, trusting him.

Rafael drank the wine. It tasted of honey and iron, and he swore, quietly, in the old harmonic tongue that no chain could hold, that he would find a way to make this cup run both directions.

At dawn, they rode west — toward the free cities, toward the boy, toward the council of broken bells — and Lord Cassian Vey's golden army sang its gray, gutted hymns all the way to the horizon.
