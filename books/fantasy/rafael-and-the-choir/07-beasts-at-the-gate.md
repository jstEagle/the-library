## 7. Beasts at the Gate

They took Rafael east, toward the river fort at Karst's Crossing, and they took him in a cage wagon with iron bars and a hood over his head, because Lord Cassian Vey's soldiers had learned to be afraid of singers.

Ansel watched from the treeline as the column wound out of sight. Sixty men. Two outriders ahead, two behind. The wagon wheels cutting twin scars into the mud of the pilgrim road.

He should have run west. Every instinct he'd honed across ten years of hiding said *run* — put the valley behind you, put the war behind you, find a hole deep enough that even gods couldn't call your name through it. The wolves pressed close against his legs, reading the tremor in him the way they read weather.

"You can't," said Marta, one of the choir remnants who'd made it out of the ambush with them. She was cradling a broken wrist against her chest, her face gray with pain. "There are sixty of them, Ansel."

"I know."

"And you're one man and some animals."

"I know." He crouched, laid his palm flat against the frozen ground, and felt the valley's dead magic flicker — faint, stubborn, alive since Rafael's hymn had cracked something open here. "But Rafael sang for me when no one else would have. He walked into Vael Hollow half-dead to find a beast-speaker nobody believed existed anymore." He stood. "I'm not going to let them feed him to a man who eats prayers."

Marta stared at him. Then she laughed — a short, broken sound — and said, "The others are scattered along the road. Sera and the twins went north when the riders came. If you're doing this, we do it together."

Ansel looked at the wolves. At the crows already gathering in the bare branches above, black leaves that had learned his name. Somewhere beyond the ridge, he could feel the half-wild horses of the lowland herds, running from the armies like everything else ran from the armies.

"Then we'd better start talking," he said.

* * *

Beast-speech was not words. That was the first thing people never understood, the thing that got his kind burned — they imagined a man barking commands at dogs. But it was older than words. It was need, spoken in the language need was born in: hunger, fear, territory, grief, the fierce bright wanting of a thing alive.

Ansel had spent his whole life speaking it small. A dying wolf soothed. A sparrow warned off a snare. Never more than a handful of voices at once, because every voice cost him something, carved a little heat out of his chest.

What he did on the night before the rescue was not small.

He sat on the ridge above Karst's Crossing with the fire banked and Marta humming Rafael's hymn under her breath — broken, halting, but enough — and he opened himself the way Rafael had taught him to open, not gripping the gift but *offering* it, and he called.

The wolves came first. Not just the pack that knew him — packs from three valleys over, gray shapes flowing up the slopes, eyes catching the starlight. They circled at a respectful distance and waited, and their waiting was a question: *why*.

*The cage wagons,* Ansel answered. *The men who burn and take. The ones who make the land go silent.*

He felt the answer move through them like wind through grass. Wolves remembered the burning years. Wolves remembered, in the marrow-deep way of beasts, what it was like when men had worshipped something older than themselves — the old shrines in the deep woods had been built by hands that left offerings, and some animal memory of those days still lived in the blood.

The crows came next, hundreds of them, settling over the ridge like a second darkness. And past midnight, trembling on the edge of his awareness, the horses — wild herds driven mad by drums and marching, stallions screaming challenges at the army fires below.

By dawn Ansel was hollowed out, shaking, nose bleeding freely onto his collar. Marta held water to his lips and said nothing, which was its own kind of mercy.

"There are more coming," she said quietly. "I can hear them. Things I don't have names for."

"So can I." He wiped his face. "Good. We'll need all of them."

* * *

Karst's Crossing was less a fort than a choke point — an old stone bridge over the grey river, flanked by timber walls thrown up in the first months of the war. The cage wagon stood in the yard, and Rafael was still alive, because Vey wanted him alive; a choir-master's voice was a tool too valuable to break carelessly.

Ansel lay on the hillside above the fort with his cheek in the frost and listened through a hundred pairs of ears at once.

It nearly broke him.

Sixty men were sixty storms of sound — boots and breath and heartbeats, the sour churn of their stomachs, the mutter of their thoughts rendered as tone without language. He sorted through it desperately, hunting the patterns: the gate watch, bored and cold. The stable hands. The officer in the stone tower, awake, uneasy, chewing the inside of his cheek.

And beneath it all, faint as a thread of gold pulled through grey wool, a hummed melody. Rafael, in his cell, singing to keep his own soul stitched together.

*Hold on,* Ansel pushed toward it, knowing the man couldn't hear, sending it anyway. *Hold on. I'm coming.*

At midday the column's captain gave the order Ansel had been praying for: a supply detail, eight men, driving out to meet a provision wagon on the north road. The gate opened.

"Now," Ansel breathed.

The crows hit the fort like a black fist.

They didn't attack — they *swarmed*, a shrieking wheeling storm of wings that blotted out the sun over the yard, diving at faces, at eyes, at the torches, plunging the whole garrison into screaming chaos. Horses in the stable screamed and kicked their stalls to splinters. And through the gate, before anyone could think to bar it, came the wolves.

Not to kill. Ansel held them back from killing with everything he had left, a leash of pure will drawn taut across sixty throats. The wolves flowed through the panicked soldiers like floodwater through a broken dam — knocking men down, tearing weapons from hands, herding them into a corner of the yard where they huddled under shields and prayed to a god who wasn't listening.

Ansel came down the hill at a dead run with Marta limping behind him, and the half-wild horses thundered at his back, answering a call older than any bridle.

The cell door was oak and iron. It took the stallion's hooves three strikes to cave it in.

Rafael stood in the wreckage, hood gone, wrists raw from chains, blinking in the sudden light. He looked at the wolves sitting silent in the snow, at the crows roosting on the broken doorframe, at the horses steaming and stamping, all of them waiting on one exhausted man's word — and then at Ansel, bleeding from the nose, swaying on his feet.

"You absolute fool," Rafael said, and his voice cracked in the middle. "You beautiful, impossible fool."

"Later," Ansel gasped. "Ride. *Now.*"

* * *

They rode north through the afternoon with the sun low and red behind them, forty refugees from the burned villages trailing the horses on foot because there was no room and no one would be left behind — not after Ansel had stood in the fort's yard and shouted at the cowering garrison in a voice made of a thousand throats: *Run, and tell your lord his stolen prayers are being counted.*

The soldiers hadn't followed. Some had knelt.

By dusk they reached the ruins of an old wayshrine, and the fugitives collapsed among the fallen stones, laughing and weeping in the same breath. A woman pressed bread into Ansel's hands. A boy with a burned arm fell asleep against a wolf's flank, and the wolf let him, and licked his hair when he stirred.

Ansel sat apart, emptied out, watching them. All his life his gift had been a wall between him and other people. Now these strangers looked at him like he was a door.

"You're staring at them like you've never been looked at before," Rafael said, lowering himself onto the stone beside him. His wrists were bandaged with Marta's scarf. "How does it feel?"

"Like I'm standing somewhere I don't have a map for."

"Get used to it. That's what belonging feels like at first." Rafael was quiet a moment. "You know what you did today. Word travels faster than horses. By tomorrow morning, every spy Vey has will know the beast-speaker exists — know your face, if anyone drew it. You've stopped being a rumor."

Ansel looked down at his hands, still trembling. "Good," he said. "Let him come looking. Because while he's hunting us—" he nodded toward the north, where the free cities' council waited behind broken bells and older fears, "—we'll be carrying the gods' answer straight to his front door."

Far off in the dark, a crow cried once — and a hundred voices answered it, and began to move north.
