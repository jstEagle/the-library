## 8. Where the Gods Went Silent

**Ansel**

The cathedral of Saint Verene had been dead longer than Ansel had been alive, and it wore its death the way mountains wore snow — patiently, as if it might outlast everything beneath it.

Its roof had fallen in a century ago, some said during the Silence itself. Stars showed through the ribs of broken vaulting. Moss climbed the pillars where saints had once stood, their faces worn to suggestion, their hands raised in gestures no one remembered the meaning of. Wind moved through the nave and made a low, hollow note against the empty window frames — almost music, almost not.

Rafael stopped just inside the doors and did not move for a long moment. Ansel watched him take off his glove — the left one, the one he never removed — and press his bare palm flat against the cold stone.

"It remembers," Rafael said quietly.

"What does?"

"Every hymn ever sung here." He looked up at the shattered vaults. "Stone holds song longer than flesh does. That's why we built with stone. That's why they built us at all."

*Us.* Ansel was still turning that word over when the shadows inside the choir stalls began to move.

They came out one by one, and there were only seven of them. Ansel had imagined dozens — Rafael spoke of *the choir* the way kings spoke of armies. But seven people stepped into the starlight: an old woman with a conductor's straight back, a boy no older than fifteen whose throat bore a scar like a rope's kiss, twin sisters who moved in perfect mirror, a gaunt man who coughed blood into a cloth and smiled anyway, a broad-shouldered woman with a soldier's burn scars, and last, a small gray-haired man carrying a birdcage with no bird in it.

Seven. The remnant of something that had once filled this nave to the vaulting.

"Rafael," the old woman said. Her voice cracked on his name like ice on a pond.

"Maren." He crossed the nave and took her hands. "You got my crows."

"Barely." She studied him, then Ansel, with eyes gone pale as frost. "And this is the beast-speaker."

Ansel's spine went rigid. "I'm not—"

"He is," Rafael said gently. "And that word isn't an insult here. Not anymore."

The scarred boy laughed — a short, bitter sound. "Not anywhere, if Vey has his way."

Maren raised a hand and the laughter died. She looked at Ansel for a long time, and Ansel felt the look go through him like a lance through water, finding nothing to catch on, which somehow frightened him more than suspicion would have.

"You came through the Hollow," she said. It wasn't a question. "Magic-dead ground. How did you bear it? Most god-touched sicken within a day."

"I don't know," Ansel said. And then, because lying to her felt like lying to the wolves: "Maybe because what I carry isn't magic. Maybe it never was."

Something shifted in Maren's face. "That," she said, "is exactly what I hoped you'd say. Come. There are things you need to hear before dawn, and things you need to see. And then—" her jaw tightened— "then you'll understand why we sent for you, and why we may all die for it."

* * *

**Rafael**

They gathered in the crypt beneath the cathedral, where the air smelled of candle smoke and deep earth, and Rafael felt the old dread settle over him like a familiar coat.

He had been here before, eleven years ago, the night the choir scattered. He had sworn he would never come back. Places held memory; that was the whole point of stone.

Maren lit the candles one by one around a slab of altar stone, and on it she laid three objects: a bell no bigger than a fist, cast in dull bronze; a strip of vellum dense with old notation; and a shard of white stone that seemed, when Rafael let himself look at it directly, to be *listening*.

"The Silence didn't happen," Maren said. "It was done."

"We know this," the soldier-scarred woman said. Her name was Odile. "We've always known it."

"We knew pieces." Maren touched the little bell. "This is a cage-bell. One of nine hundred cast in the year the gods went quiet. Do you know what it does?"

No one answered. Rafael found he couldn't.

"It doesn't silence a god," Maren said. "Nothing can silence a god — not truly. What it does is narrow them. A god is a chorus, children. A thousand thousand voices of prayer, all braided into one vast harmony. Ring a cage-bell in the right place, tuned the right way, and you cut threads from the braid. You don't kill the song. You just make sure only *your* prayers reach the ear. Only your petitions. Only your shape of worship."

The gray-haired man set his empty birdcage on the altar with a click. "A god in a cage still sings," he said softly. "But only what the keeper wants to hear."

"And mortals did this?" Ansel asked. His voice was careful. "To the old gods? Why would anyone—"

"Fear," Maren said simply. "The same fear that burns beast-speakers in village squares. A god who answers everyone is a god you can't control. So the kings of the old age built the bells, and cut the gods down to size, and told themselves they had tamed heaven. And the gods—" her voice caught— "the gods went silent because a voice that is only permitted to say one thing eventually stops saying anything at all. They withdrew. They waited. They grieved."

Rafael had heard fragments of this before, in Verene's oldest archives, and had never believed all of it. Hearing it whole, in this room, with the shard of listening-stone humming faintly against the altar, was different. It was like hearing the diagnosis of a sickness you'd suspected for years.

"And now?" Ansel asked. "Now they're answering again. Why now?"

"Because the bells are failing. Age, neglect, the slow forgetting of how they're made." Maren's mouth twisted. "Prayers started slipping through the gaps. Small ones, at first. A shepherd's plea. A dying woman's last words. The gods heard them — and after four hundred years of silence, they *answered*. And Lord Cassian Vey—"

"—found the first answered prayer before anyone else did," Rafael finished. His stomach had gone cold. "In the war. He was a quartermaster. He had access to the battlefields, the plague camps, all the places where desperate people pray hardest."

"Yes." Maren looked at him steadily. "You understand faster than I did. Vey didn't just find the gods returning, Rafael. He found that an answered prayer has *weight*. Power. And he learned that if you ring a cage-bell while a prayer is being answered, you can steal the answer mid-flight. Take the power for yourself. Leave the praying soul with nothing but silence."

The coughing man spat blood and laughed wetly. "So the usurper isn't fighting the gods' return. He's *harvesting* it."

"He's building cages," Maren said. "Our people in the capital report foundries working day and night. Nine hundred bells broke over four centuries. He means to cast nine thousand. And he doesn't mean to narrow the gods this time." She paused, and the candle flames leaned as though the room itself had drawn breath. "He means to finish it. To cut every thread but one — the thread that runs through *him*. One god-shaped hole in the world, with Cassian Vey standing in it. Every prayer answered, every miracle performed, every worshipper kneeling — and none of it divine at all. Just one man, wearing heaven like a stolen coat."

Silence in the crypt. Even the wind above seemed to hold still.

Ansel spoke first, and his voice was very quiet. "The beasts told me something in the lowlands. They said the two-legs' god had gone hungry and started eating. I didn't understand it until now." He looked up, and Rafael saw something kindle in his face that hadn't been there in Vael Hollow — not wonder, not fear. *Purpose*, raw and unpolished. "Vey isn't just stealing power. He's starving the real gods. Every prayer he intercepts is a meal they don't get. Another thread cut. If he finishes the bells—"

"The gods don't die," Maren said. "But they go silent forever. True silence, this time. And the world gets one god, and his name is Vey, and there will be no beast-speakers, no choirs, no wild voices at all. Because a single kept god cannot abide rivals."

* * *

**Ansel**

They climbed back into the nave as the sky behind the broken vaulting went from black to iron-gray, and Maren called the choir to stand in their old places among the ruined stalls.

Seven singers. Rafael made eight. Ansel stood apart, at the edge of the transept, feeling like a man watching a fire from across a river.

"Sing me the answering verse," Maren said. "The one you carried through the Hollow. I want to hear what the gods actually said."

Rafael nodded to the others. And they sang.

Ansel had heard Rafael hum snatches of melody on the road — half-phrases, mostly, like a man remembering a dream. This was different. Eight voices wove together in the ruined nave, and the melody rose through the broken roof toward the fading stars, and Ansel felt it in his chest the way he felt the wolves' speech — not as sound, but as *meaning arriving*.

The hymn was a question. He understood that much without knowing how. *Are you there? Will you speak? What do you want of us?*

And then the cathedral answered.

It was not the singers. It was the stone — the worn saints, the moss-eaten pillars, the empty window frames where the wind had moaned all night. Every surface gave back the hymn transformed, deepened, layered with harmonies no human throat could make, and threaded through it all came a single phrase, repeated, insistent, rising like water:

*Speak for us. Speak for us. We have been silent so long we have forgotten our own voice.*

The song ended. The echo hung in the nave for ten heartbeats, twenty. Then it faded, and the seven remnants stood pale and shaking, and Rafael had tears running freely down his face and didn't seem to know it.

"They've forgotten how to speak to us directly," Maren whispered. "Four hundred years of bells. They can only answer through vessels now — through stone, through beasts, through..." she turned to Ansel. "Through you."

Ansel's mouth was dry. "I'm not a vessel. I'm a fugitive who talks to wolves."

"You are the only living soul who carries a voice the gods recognize as older than the bells," Maren said. "Beast-speech predates the cages, child. It is the first prayer, the prayer before words. When you speak with the beasts' voice, you speak in the gods' own mother tongue." She stepped closer. "In six days, the free cities convene their council at Meridian. Vey is coming under truce to offer them peace — peace bought with worship of him alone. If the cities kneel, the last free prayers in the world become his to harvest."

"So someone has to tell them the truth," Ansel said.

"Someone has to *show* them. Words can be dismissed. But if the council hears the gods' own answer — carried in a voice they cannot cage, cannot buy, cannot silence—" Maren's pale eyes burned. "Then maybe the covenant breaks open instead of closing forever."

The scarred boy laughed nervously. "And if Vey's spies identify him on the road? If the captain from the pilgrim village reported what he saw? They'll be hunting a beast-speaker with everything he has."

"Then we travel as pilgrims," Rafael said. He had wiped his face and his voice was steady now, conductor-steady. "All of us. A choir travels openly — it always has. And Ansel—" he met Ansel's eyes across the ruined nave— "you don't have to do this. I swore I'd stop asking you to risk things."

Ansel looked at the worn faces of the stone saints. He thought of the wolf dying in the snow in Vael Hollow, the first voice that had ever spoken to him kindly. He thought of the village on the pilgrim road, and the animals that had come to him out of the burning dark, trusting. He thought of the phrase still ringing faintly in the stone: *we have forgotten our own voice.*

"No," he said. "Keep asking. The answer's yes. It's been yes since the valley — I just wasn't brave enough to hear it myself."

Maren closed her eyes briefly, as if in prayer — or in grief. "Then rest today. We leave tomorrow. And Ansel—" she hesitated, and for the first time the old woman looked uncertain. "There's a crow arrived this morning. From Meridian. The council has announced the terms of the truce parley." Her jaw worked. "Vey has requested — by name — that any 'god-touched persons' presenting themselves to the council be seized and tried as heretics. He knows someone is coming. He's inviting you into a trap."

Outside, the dawn came up gray over the broken roof, and somewhere far to the south, faint as memory, Ansel thought he could hear the foundries beginning their work.
