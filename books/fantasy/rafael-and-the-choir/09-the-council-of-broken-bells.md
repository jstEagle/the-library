## 9. The Council of Broken Bells

The council hall of Mereth had been a temple once. You could still see where the bells had hung — nine iron yokes bolted into the rafters, empty now, their bells melted down generations ago to mint coins for men who wanted gods they could count.

Ansel stood beneath those empty yokes and tried not to shake.

The free cities had gathered in force, which meant they had gathered in argument. Delegates from Corvane and Sallowmere were already shouting across the floor about grain tariffs while the Ossuary's bone-masked envoys watched in silence, and the harbor lords of Tessaunt had brought their own chairs, as if the council's furniture might be a trap. Two hundred voices, none of them agreeing on anything except that the war at their borders was someone else's fault.

And above them all, on the dais where the high altar used to stand, sat Lord Cassian Vey.

He had come under a flag of truce — a white banner worked with a single golden bell — and he had come beautifully. His armor was polished to the color of dawn. His cloak was dyed the deep violet that only his household was permitted to wear. He sat with one ankle crossed over one knee, hands folded, wearing the patient smile of a man who has already read the last page.

Rafael stood beside Ansel at the back of the hall, among the choir remnants: old Mother Ilsa whose voice had gone gravelly but true; the twins Perrin and Sef, nineteen years old and white-knuckled; Brother Callum, who kept touching the frayed cord at his wrist like a rosary. Seven singers left out of forty. Ansel counted them again, uselessly. Counting didn't bring the rest back.

"They'll let Vey speak first," Rafael murmured. "Truce etiquette. Then he'll make his offer, and it will be generous, and half this room will kneel before sundown."

"And us?"

"Then we speak." Rafael's eyes stayed forward. Under the hall's smoky lamplight the conductor looked older than his years, the scar along his jaw pulled tight. "Ansel. Whatever happens — whatever you hear in there—" he touched his own chest, briefly, "—the beasts chose you. Remember that when your courage runs out. They don't choose carelessly."

* * *

Vey rose without hurry, and the hall quieted the way a forest quiets when a predator walks through.

"Councilors. Delegates. Friends." His voice carried effortlessly; Ansel felt it settle over the room like a warm coat. "I did not come here to threaten you. I came because I am tired."

A ripple of sympathy moved through the benches. Ansel felt sick watching it work.

"For ten years, the lowlands have burned. For ten years, your sons have died holding lines no one can name a reason for." Vey spread his hands. "I know why the gods fell silent. I found their graves. And I learned something in those grave-quiet centuries that none of your priests dared tell you: the old gods are *gone*. What remains is power — raw, orphaned power, waiting for a worthy steward. I have taken up that stewardship. I have ended the burning in three provinces already. Give me what I ask, and I will end it in all of them."

He let the silence stretch. When he spoke again, his voice dropped, almost tender.

"All I ask is worship. Not your freedom — your freedom I leave intact. Not your laws, your trade, your temples. Only this: acknowledge me. Sing to *me*. One voice, unified, and I will give you peace such as the world has not known since the bells rang."

One by one, delegates began to rise. The harbor lords first. Then half of Corvane. Ansel saw an Ossuary envoy incline her bone mask, slow, deliberate — a nod.

Rafael stepped forward. "Lord Vey."

The hall turned. Vey's smile widened fractionally, the smile of a host greeting an expected guest. "Maestro Quintana. Alive after all. Your choir sings beautifully, I'm told. In hiding."

"My choir sings," Rafael said, "to the actual gods. Who are not gone. Who are *answering*." He lifted his chin. "You've been drinking from their well and calling yourself the rain."

Murmurs. Somewhere a delegate laughed, then stopped laughing when Vey glanced at him.

"You carry proof?" Vey asked pleasantly.

"I carry a speaker." Rafael turned, and his eyes found Ansel's across thirty feet of marble. "Come forward, Ansel Nakamura."

Every step to the center of the hall took a year. Ansel had faced wolves and soldiers and fire, but nothing had ever felt like two hundred pairs of eyes and the usurper of heaven looking down at him with courteous amusement. His mouth was dry. His gift — the thing that had gotten villages burned, that had kept him friendless in a valley where magic went to sleep — pressed against his ribs like a second heartbeat.

"Beast-speaker," Vey said, tasting the word. "Yes. My spies described you. A party trick, surely. Wolves fetching sticks."

"Not wolves." Ansel's voice cracked. He cleared his throat. "Everything. Everything that lives outside your walls."

"Then show us." Vey gestured graciously toward the great doors. "Amuse the council."

Ansel closed his eyes.

He reached out the way Rafael had taught him to reach — not grasping, but opening. Past the walls of Mereth, past the fields stripped bare by marching armies, into the hedgerows and the woodlots and the hills. And they came. They had been coming for three days, gathering at Rafael's quiet instruction, answering a call that no human ear could hear: crows off the midden-heaps, foxes from the warrens, deer driven down from the high forest, dogs slipping their chains, and wolves — wolves beyond counting, the packs of the burned lowlands who remembered every village that had fed them and every soldier that had hunted them.

The doors of the council hall groaned open.

They poured in — not charging, not snarling, but walking, flowing, thousands of bodies filling the aisles and the galleries and the spaces between the delegates' frozen chairs until the hall was a single held breath. A stag stood shoulder to shoulder with a wolf. Crows layered the empty bell-yokes above until the rafters went black.

Ansel opened his eyes. His whole body trembled. He raised both hands.

And he spoke with every throat at once.

The sound that came out of him was not one voice but a chord — the growl beneath, the birdsong above, the low ocean-moan of hoofbeats and wingbeats and ten thousand hearts beating in time. It filled the hall the way Vey's voice never could, because it did not persuade. It *testified*.

*"We were here before your walls,"* said the beasts through him, and every delegate heard it in their own mother tongue. *"We were here before your bells. We heard the gods when they sang the rivers into motion, and we heard them when you built your cages and they went silent with grief. And we have heard them these ten years — faint, returning, waking — while THIS ONE—"*

The chord bent, all its harmonies turning toward the dais, and Vey rose from his seat for the first time.

*"—drank their answered prayers like wine. Every miracle he sold you was stolen. Every 'peace' he bought you was paid for with their stolen voice. Ask him what happened to the god of the Ashen Ford. Ask him what happened to the singing wells of Sallowmere. ASK HIM WHY THE LAND ITSELF GOES QUIET WHERE HE WALKS."*

The hall erupted. Delegates were on their feet, shouting, some in fury at Vey, some in terror at Ansel, some simply weeping at a sound like the world remembering its own name. The Ossuary envoys knelt — all four of them, bone masks pressed to marble.

Vey stood very still. Then he smiled, and it was the worst smile Ansel had ever seen, because there was no courtesy left in it at all.

"Clever beast-tongue," Vey said softly. "Lies wear any costume." He lifted one hand, and golden light kindled around his fingers — hoarded light, prayer-light, and Ansel felt the beasts flinch as one, felt the stolen divinity press down on the hall like deep water. "But you've made one mistake, little speaker. You've gathered every creature I need to break, in one room, before every throne that matters. Watch how easily—"

He never finished.

Because above them, in the black rafters, among the crows roosting on the empty iron yokes where the bells of Mereth used to hang — something began to hum.

It was Rafael. Standing at the foot of the dais, arms rising, drawing the seven surviving singers of Saint Verene into line behind him. And the melody he hummed was one Ansel knew from a dark night at the valley's edge, half-dead, impossible: the hymn that made dead magic flicker. But now it wasn't flickering. Now it caught, spread, climbed — the choir's voices braiding with the beasts' chord, mortal harmony locking into animal testimony, and the empty bell-yokes overhead began to ring with a sound that came from nowhere and everywhere at once.

The gods' answer, arriving at last at the council of broken bells.

Vey's golden light guttered. His face went white with something worse than rage.

"Sing," Rafael said to his choir, to the hall, to the sky. "*Now.*"
