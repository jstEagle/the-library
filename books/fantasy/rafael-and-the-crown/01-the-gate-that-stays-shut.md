## 1. The Gate That Stays Shut

The frost had come down off the high crags overnight, and by the time Theo Pemberton reached the Thornfell gate, it lay across the old stones like a sheet of grey glass, thin enough to crack under a boot heel but honest about what it meant: winter was coming, and the mountain would be closing its fist.

Theo walked the gate's length the way they did every morning, every evening, and twice more on days when the wind smelled wrong. Left pillar first — sound, though a seam of lichen blackened the mortar where water crept in summer and split it open again each freeze. Then the arch itself, forty feet of masonry that no living stonemason could have raised, its blocks fitted so close that Theo had never once found room to slide a knife between them, though they'd tried in their younger years, out of boredom, then out of something closer to fear.

Then the gate itself. Sealed. Always sealed.

It wasn't a door anyone could have opened. There was no bar to lift, no lock to pick, no hinges at all that Theo had ever found. The twin leaves of dark stone stood flush with the wall as if the mountain had simply decided, one day three hundred years ago, to stop letting people through, and the world had agreed. The carvings on the leaves had worn smooth in places — spirals, once, and figures that might have been kings or might have been prisoners; Theo had stopped being able to tell which was which long before they'd stopped caring.

They pressed a palm flat against the cold stone anyway. Ritual, not inspection. You checked a thing like this the way you checked your own pulse: not because you expected to find anything different, but because the morning you didn't check would be the morning something had changed without your knowing.

Cold. Still. Shut.

"Good," Theo said aloud, to nobody, and their breath hung in the air a moment before the wind took it.

* * *

The traveler came up the pilgrim road an hour past midmorning, and Theo saw him from two hundred paces, because seeing people from two hundred paces was half of what kept them alive up here.

He was young — twenty, perhaps twenty-five — dressed for weather he clearly hadn't understood when he'd set out, in a wool coat better suited to the lowlands and boots already dark with meltwater. He walked like a man who had been told the road was shorter than it was. And he kept glancing back over his shoulder, which on this road meant one of two things: either he was followed, or he thought he was, and both kinds were trouble.

Theo let him come to them. That was the way of it. You never hurried toward trouble on a mountain; you stood still and let it spend itself reaching you.

"You're past the turn," Theo said when he was near enough. Their voice came out flat and unhurried, the voice they used for strangers, sanded down until it carried nothing of anything. "The lowland road forks back at the shrine cairn. Half a day behind you."

"I'm not—" The young man stopped, caught his breath. Up close he looked worse than he had at distance: chapped lips, eyes red-rimmed, hands shoved deep in his pockets like he was afraid of what they'd do otherwise. "I'm looking for the pass. The Thornfell crossing."

"There isn't one."

"They said there was. In Verre, they said—"

"In Verre," Theo said, "they say a great many things about these mountains, and most of what they say is that the mountains are very far away." They nodded past him, back down the valley. "The crossing closed sixty years ago. Before my time. Before my mother's time. Rockfall, they wrote in the toll ledgers. You can read it yourself if you ride to the garrison at Marrow Ford, if you enjoy reading lies in a warm room."

Something flickered across the young man's face — not disappointment exactly. Recognition. The look of a man hearing a door described that he had been promised was open.

"It isn't closed," he said quietly. "Is it. I heard it from a drover at the ford. He said the gate's real. He said there's a keeper."

Theo studied him. Wind moved through the pines below the road, and somewhere above, a shelf of snow let go with a sound like a long exhale.

"A keeper," Theo repeated.

"He said the keeper knows every road through the mountain. Every path, every cave, every smuggler's cut. He said the king himself couldn't move an army through Cael's spine without the keeper's leave." The young man laughed, a short unhappy sound. "I thought it was a story. But then why would he lie?"

"People lie for all sorts of reasons. To sell you something. To send you somewhere. To see what you do when you believe them." Theo turned, deliberately, and looked back down the valley the way he'd come — the long empty thread of the pilgrim road unwinding through the trees, brown and silent, nothing moving on it but wind. When they looked back, the young man was watching them with an expression too naked to be comfortable. "Nobody's following you."

His shoulders dropped half an inch. It was answer enough.

"Whoever you're running from," Theo said, "they aren't on this road yet. Go back to the fork. Take the river trail south. In four days you'll reach Lowmark, and in Lowmark nobody asks names. From there you can be anywhere in the low country inside a month, and whatever it is will have forgotten your face."

"And the gate—"

"Is closed." Theo said it without heat, without emphasis, the way you'd say the sun is up or the river runs downhill. "Sixty years closed. It'll be closed tomorrow, and next year, and after we're both dead some other keeper will stand here saying the same words to some other fool. Go south."

For a long moment the young man just stood there, breathing the thin cold air, and Theo watched the war move behind his eyes — the war between the story he'd carried up the mountain and the plain fact standing in front of him wearing frost on its collar. It was a war Theo knew well. They fought it themselves most mornings.

Then his gaze went past Theo's shoulder, up to the gate itself, rising above the treeline where the road ended in a wall of tumbled rock. His face changed. Theo had seen that change before too — wonder curdling into something hungry, something that made people do stupid things in high places.

"Don't," Theo said.

"I only want to look at it."

"No, you don't. You want to touch it. And then you want to find the loose stone everyone thinks is there, and then you want to climb around the side where the footing goes, and then I spend my afternoon scraping what's left of you off the rocks and explaining it to whoever comes asking." Theo jerked their chin southward. "The gate doesn't open. That's the whole of what I know and more than you need. Four days to Lowmark. Don't stop on the road. Don't light a fire where the smoke shows against the ridgeline."

The young man hesitated one last time — and then, absurdly, bowed. A little formal bow, lowland court manners in the middle of nowhere, and Theo watched him go with something that wasn't quite pity and wasn't quite envy. He walked lighter going south than he had coming north. Whatever he'd been running from, he'd left a piece of it behind at the gate, the way they all did.

Theo stood in the road until he was gone from sight, then a while longer.

Useful to everyone, loyal to no one. That was the trade. That was the whole architecture of their life, load-bearing and invisible: the garrison at Marrow Ford believed Theo reported on the smugglers, and the smugglers believed Theo reported on the garrison, and neither belief was quite true, and both had to be fed regularly or the whole structure came down on their head. The villagers at Thornfell Foot believed the keeper kept the gate, and mostly that was even true, though what the gate needed keeping *from* was a question Theo had learned, very early, never to ask aloud.

Wary contentment. That was what they'd bought. It cost everything and it was worth it, and on mornings like this one, with the frost burning off and the woodsmoke smell drifting up from the village and no one dead on their doorstep, Theo could almost believe the price was settled.

Almost.

* * *

Brannic came at dusk, because Brannic always came at dusk, when the shadows were long enough to hide a man's approach and short enough that a man could prove he wasn't hiding.

He came up the goat track from the west with two of his crew hanging back at the treeline, and he carried a sack of salt over one shoulder like an offering, which is precisely what it was. Salt from the coast, good white salt, worth more in these mountains than silver and harder to trace. Theo had never once asked where it came from. That was part of the arrangement too.

"Pemberton." Brannic set the sack down on the flat stone by the cottage door, the stone that had served as counter, altar, and negotiation table for longer than either of them had been alive. He was a big man gone soft in the middle, with a beard shot through with grey and small quick eyes that never finished counting things. "Brought you the sea."

"How thoughtful. How did it get here?"

"Terrible roads," Brannic said solemnly. "Practically impassable. Lucky I know the way."

"Lucky." Theo came down the steps and broke the sack's seal with a thumbnail, checking the salt the way they always checked it — depth of the pour, color, whether it had been cut with ash. It hadn't. It never was. Brannic cheated everyone else in three counties, but he paid Theo exact weight and pure goods, and both of them understood this as the tribute it was. "You're late. Moon's nearly up."

"Moon's a gossip. Everything she sees, she tells." Brannic lowered himself onto the chopping block beside the flat stone with a groan that was half performance, half truth. His knees had been going bad for years, and he complained about them the way other men talked about weather — constantly, and as a way of not talking about anything else. "Roads are crawling, Pemberton. That's why I'm late. Gray riders on the lowland fords, asking questions at every ferry. King's men, inquisitor's writ. Not the usual tax-farmers either. These ones are *looking* for something."

Theo kept their hands moving through the salt. "Looking for what?"

"That's the interesting question, isn't it. Because it isn't contraband, and it isn't runaways, and it isn't the usual heretics." Brannic leaned forward, elbows on knees, and the firelight from the cottage window caught his face and made the small eyes smaller. "A courier. Young man, riding hard, carrying something. My people saw the gray cloaks turn a whole village upside down at Saint Verre crossing over it. Turned a man out of his own house in his nightshirt and made him kneel in the frost while they searched his rafters." He paused. "You wouldn't know anything about that."

"I keep a gate."

"You keep *every* gate. Officially the Thornfell gate is sealed and forgotten, and unofficially you're the only man alive who knows which of the mountain's thirty-odd ways are open, half-open, or waiting for a bribe." Brannic spread his hands, all injured innocence. "If a courier wanted to cross Cael's spine unseen, there's exactly one person he'd need to talk to. And I'm looking at him."

"The courier didn't talk to me."

"Didn't come through at all, then? Didn't ask his way? Didn't stand right where I'm sitting and ask you about passes?" Brannic's voice stayed pleasant, conversational, and that was the danger of him — Brannic at knife-point was a brawler, but Brannic being friendly was a man doing arithmetic. "Because my people say a rider matching the description passed through Thornfell Foot two nights ago. Bought bread. Asked about the old pilgrim road north."

Theo poured the last of the salt back into the sack and tied it off, unhurried, giving themselves the three seconds they needed. Two nights ago. Before the whiteout. Before—

Before whatever it was they'd find when the weather lifted, because Theo had been up on the high road yesterday, in the teeth of the storm, and had seen something dark in the snow near the old pilgrim track that they had told themselves was a rock, and had almost — *almost* — gone to check, and had told themselves the light was going and the slope was dangerous and it was none of their business.

None of their business. That was the rule. That was the whole architecture.

"There's traffic on the pilgrim road sometimes," Theo said. "Pilgrims, mostly. It's a pilgrim road."

"It's a dead road that ends at a sealed gate, and yet somehow people keep walking it." Brannic sighed, and for a moment the arithmetic left his face and something older and wearier looked out of it. "Look. I'm not your enemy. I've never been your enemy. Twenty years I've paid you fair and taken your silence fairer, and in all that time you've never asked me one question about my business and I've never asked you one about yours. That's the treaty. I like the treaty."

"But."

"But the gray cloaks make me nervous, and nervous men pay badly and talk freely, and I can't afford either." He stood, joints cracking, and shouldered the empty space where the salt had been, leaving the sack on the stone between them like a hostage. "So I'll ask you once, plainly, man to man. Is the courier on your mountain?"

And here was the moment, Theo thought later, though they didn't think it then. Here was the hinge the whole door swung on, and they didn't feel it turn.

Because the truthful answer was: *I don't know.* Because yesterday in the whiteout they had seen a darkness in the snow and walked away from it, and tonight the wind had shifted and brought them a smell they hadn't smelled since childhood — blood, faint and iron-old, riding down off the high road on the freezing air — and because Theo Pemberton, who knew every road through the mountain, every cave, every smuggler's cut, better than any living soul, had spent the entire conversation lying to a man they despised needing, and had done it badly, and Brannic knew it.

"No," Theo said.

Brannic held their gaze for a long moment. Then he smiled, wide and warm, and it was somehow worse than anger.

"Course not," he said. "Silly question. Get some sleep, keeper. Storm's breaking — clear skies tomorrow." He whistled low as he went, and his crew melted out of the treeline to fall in around him, and the three of them went down the goat track into the blue dusk like drops of water finding their way home.

Theo stood by the flat stone until the sound of them faded. Then they picked up the sack of salt — heavy, pure, exact weight — and carried it inside, and set it by the door, and did not sleep.

Because the wind had shifted again, and the smell was stronger now. Iron-old. Coming down off the high road.

Coming from the direction of the thing in the snow they had told themselves was a rock.

Theo banked the fire, pulled on their boots, and took down the storm-lantern. Outside, the clouds were tearing apart ahead of a fat rising moon, and the peaks stood up black against the stars, and somewhere up there, on the old pilgrim road, the mountain was keeping something for them that they would have to go and collect.

They started climbing.
