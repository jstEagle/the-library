## 2. A Stranger at the Treeline

The whiteout came down off the shoulder of Mount Cael like a held breath finally let go.

Theo Pemberton had watched the sky all morning, the way they watched everything—sideways, without seeming to. There was a particular greenish bruise to the clouds before a mountain storm, a color the peaks wore only when they meant business, and by midday the light through the cottage window had gone the color of old pewter. Theo had banked the fire, brought in the split wood, and stood in the doorway with a cup of barley tea going cold in their hands, reading the treeline the way other people read letters.

*Come on, then,* they thought at the mountain. *Do your worst. I've seen worse.*

The first flakes arrived fat and slow, almost lazy, as if the storm were still deciding. By the time Theo had walked the quarter-mile to check the gate's outer seal—habit, pure habit, though no one traveled in this—the snow came sideways, and the world beyond twenty paces had been erased.

Thornfell gate was not much to look at: two leaning posts of black oak, an iron bar older than any living memory, and the narrow defile between them where the mountain road pinched down to a single cart's width. Beyond it, the old pilgrim road climbed toward the high passes, and beyond that, nothing anyone admitted to wanting. Theo checked the bar out of ritual rather than necessity, pressed a palm flat against the oak the way they did every day of their life and would have denied under torture, and turned back for home.

That was when they heard the bell.

Not a real bell. The gate-bell, the little iron thing hung on the pilgrim road half a mile up, which travelers rang when they wanted passage and Theo pretended not to hear. It clanged once, wild and arrhythmic, then again—and then a sound that was not a bell at all but a man's voice, thin with distance and wind, screaming something that the storm shredded before it arrived.

Theo stood in the falling white and cursed, long and fluently, in the private language of people who have built their whole lives around not being needed.

They went anyway. That was the trouble with keeping a gate; you became the kind of person who answered bells.

* * *

The old pilgrim road was a scar of packed earth and broken flagstone winding up through black pines, and in a whiteout it became a country of its own—a place where sound died a foot from your ear and the world shrank to the circle of your own lantern. Theo moved with the ease of forty years on this mountain, shoulders hunched, boots finding the stones by feel, counting turns without thinking. Third bend, the fallen birch. Fifth bend, the shrine niche where someone long dead had carved a sunburst into the rock. Seventh bend—

The man was lying across the road like something the storm had coughed up.

He was on his back, one arm flung out, and even before Theo's lantern found him properly, even before the light touched the dark wet gleam spread beneath him, Theo knew. You learned to know it, living alone with death always a valley away. The stillness was wrong. Living things curled against cold; this man lay open to it, generous with himself, as if he'd already decided the argument was over.

Theo knelt. The lantern showed a face young-ish and ruined—hollow cheeks crusted with ice, lips gone the blue-gray of slate, eyes half-open and fixed on nothing. His cloak was fine wool once, now stiff with frozen blood. There was blood everywhere, more than a body could politely spare: soaked through his side, painted down his left arm in a long smear where he had apparently dragged himself along the stone, pooled and refrozen beneath him so that when Theo shifted, the ground cracked like thin ice over a pond.

"Ah," Theo said, uselessly, to the storm. "Ah, no."

And then the man's hand closed around Theo's wrist.

Theo did not scream, though it was a near thing. The grip was shocking—not strong, exactly, but *present*, a drowning man's anchor thrown with the last strength in him, and his eyes rolled over and found Theo's face and *fixed* there with an intensity that made the storm feel like a courtesy.

"You're—" His voice was a ruin. He swallowed, tried again. "You're the keeper. The gatekeeper. They said—down in the valley—they said there was a keeper at Thornfell who knew every road."

"There's no keeper," Theo said automatically. It was the answer they gave everyone. "There's just me, and I'm nobody, and you're dying, and I can't—"

"Yes." Something that might have been a smile moved through the frost on his face. "Yes, you are."

He tried to push himself up and failed, breath tearing wetly in his chest. Up close, in the lantern light, Theo could see the wound now: a crossbow bolt had gone through him below the ribs and been snapped off, leaving a ruin the size of a fist. He'd bled for miles. He must have run or crawled for miles. The pilgrim road hadn't seen a traveler in years, and yet this man had come up it knowing—*knowing*—that somewhere ahead there was a gate, and a keeper, and that the keeper was the last door left in the world.

"They're behind me," he said. "Riders. Gray cloaks. You have to—listen—you have to listen—"

"I'm listening."

"No one listens." His eyes were fever-bright and terribly clear, both at once, the clarity of a man spending his final coin all in one place. "Twenty years I've run. My mother ran. Her mother. We are the letter and we are the ones who carry it, do you understand? And now the letter is dying and the riders are close and you—" His grip tightened on Theo's wrist, hard enough to grind bone. "You have to take it."

"Take what?"

His free hand fumbled at his chest, at the inner fold of his ruined cloak, and Theo—God help them, some instinct older than caution—helped him. Their fingers found oilcloth wrapped tight and tied with cord, and beneath the oilcloth something flat and stiff, and beneath that, when the wax seal cracked open under his thumb, a folded sheet of heavy paper that the storm immediately tried to take.

Theo closed their hands around it. The man covered their hands with his own, and his were shaking, and his blood was warm through their gloves, and that warmth was the most obscene thing about the whole scene—everything else frozen, and this one small heat, leaving.

"The tomb," he whispered. "Beneath Mount Cael. Under the king's own mountain, under the crown itself. You swear it. You swear on whatever you keep holy, on your gate, on your miserable hidden life—swear you'll carry it there. Not read it. Not sell it. Not burn it. *Carry it.*"

"I don't even know your name."

"Rafael." He said it like a password, like a key turning, like the last word of a prayer. "Rafael Vael. Now swear."

Somewhere below them, far down the valley, muffled by the storm, there came a sound that did not belong to weather. A horn. One long note, patient as arithmetic.

Rafael heard it too. His whole body flinched, and then, horribly, he relaxed—as if the horn settled something for him, confirmed the arithmetic. He looked at Theo with those burning clear eyes and said, gently, the way you speak to someone you're sorry for:

"They'll ask you questions. Tell them nothing. Tell them you found a dead man. Grieve a little—it won't be hard, I'm very likable. And when they're gone, you'll open that map, and you'll understand why I chose a stranger, and you'll hate me for it, and you'll carry it anyway."

"I never agreed to—"

"You swore."

"I didn't—"

"You *swore.*" And here was the terrible thing, the thing Theo would turn over a thousand times in the years after: he smiled when he said it, and the smile was kind. "Everyone swears, keeper. That's how it starts. Someone swears, and someone carries, and someone dies, and the world goes on pretending it doesn't remember. Swear it. Let my family be worth one honest oath in a hundred years of lies."

The horn sounded again. Closer. Or maybe the same distance, and only the storm thinner.

Theo looked down at the blood-warm map in their hands, at this dying stranger who had climbed a mountain in winter to die at the feet of someone whose entire art was being forgotten, and felt something crack open in their chest that they would spend a long time learning the name of.

"I swear," Theo said. The words came out rough, scraped. "On the gate. On my life. I'll carry it."

Rafael exhaled. It seemed to take everything with it. He sagged back against the frozen road, and his grip on Theo's wrist loosened—not released, never quite released, but eased into something almost like trust, almost like rest.

"Good," he murmured. Snow was gathering on his eyelashes now, and he didn't blink it away. "Good. Knew you would. Saw it in your face when you knelt. You've got a keeper's hands." His eyes drifted, focused somewhere past Theo's shoulder, past the storm, at something twenty years away. "My mother used to say... the roads remember..."

"What? Rafael. *Rafael.*"

But the light in his eyes had gone somewhere the lantern couldn't follow, and the hand on Theo's wrist was only weight now, and the snow fell on him with the patience of something that had all the time in the world, covering him the way it covered the road, the shrine, the whole indifferent mountain.

Theo knelt there in the whiteout, holding a dead man's cooling wrist and a sealed map they already wanted to burn, and grieved—grieved hard and incomprehensibly, grief arriving years before understanding, tears freezing on their face for a stranger whose name they'd known for less time than it takes to boil water.

Then the horn sounded a third time, and grief stepped aside for fear.

* * *

There wasn't time to bury him. There was barely time to think.

Theo worked with the speed of desperation, dragging Rafael off the open road and into the shrine niche at the fifth bend, wedging the body behind the carved sunburst where the snow would drift deep and hold him till spring—or till the riders found him, whichever came first. They broke off pine boughs and piled them over him, crude and insufficient, a lie of a grave. Then they took the lantern, killed its light, and crouched in the black of the pines with the map pressed against their sternum like a second heartbeat, and waited.

The riders came up the pilgrim road perhaps twenty minutes later.

There were four of them, moving slow, and they were wrong in a way Theo couldn't immediately name. Not because of the livery—though the livery was wrong enough, gray traveling cloaks cut fine despite the weather, the king's colors worn by men who rode like butchers. It was their *pace*. Nobody hunted through a whiteout at a walk unless walking was enough. Unless they already knew where the quarry had gone, and were merely collecting him.

Their leader reined in at the seventh bend, where the blood was. Even from the shadows of the treeline, Theo saw him dismount, crouch, drag one gloved finger through the frozen smear, and study it with the unhurried attention of a man reading a ledger.

"He stopped here," the leader said. His voice carried flat and clear through the dead air, pitched for his men and no one else, and yet it reached Theo with horrible intimacy, as if the storm itself were conspiring to deliver it. "Bled a while. Went on."

"On foot?" one of the others asked.

"On something. Keeper's hut is down the road. Someone lives at the gate."

"Dead man tells no tales."

"This one told plenty." The leader rose, and turned his head slowly, and for one suspended moment his gaze swept the treeline where Theo crouched with their heart hammering like a trapped bird—and passed on. "We ride down. Questions first. If the answers are bad, the fire after. Mount up."

They rode past the shrine niche without a glance. Pine boughs and drifting snow held. Theo stayed crouched in the dark until the hoofbeats faded to nothing and then for a long while after, until their knees screamed and the cold had gotten into the marrow of them, and even then they didn't move so much as unfold, joint by joint, like something learning to be alive again.

Then they stood in the falling snow above a hidden corpse, holding a dead man's map, and understood—with the perfect, useless clarity that comes too late—that the life they had spent forty years building, the careful invisible life, the peace bought with silence and small usefulness and never, ever being interesting—

Was already over.

The storm was closing behind the riders like a door. Down the valley, faint through the white, a light burned in the window of Theo's own cottage, warm and gold and utterly betrayed, marking the spot where four gray-cloaked men would soon be asking their questions.

Theo touched the oilcloth bundle through their coat. Wax crumbs and Rafael's dried blood clung to their gloves.

"Well," they said aloud, to the mountain, to the dead man, to no one. "Someone had to answer the bell."

And they started down through the whiteout toward the light in their own window, carrying the beginning of the end of everything they'd ever kept safe.
