## 3. What the Map Shows

The dead man's blood had dried to a brown crust on the oilskin, and Theo sat looking at it for a long time before they could bring themselves to touch the map again.

Outside, the whiteout had spent itself against the mountain. Wind still worried the eaves of the cottage, a low animal sound, but the worst had passed, and with it any excuse not to look at what Rafael had pressed into their hands with his last strength. The lamp burned low on the table. Theo trimmed the wick, and their hands were steady, which surprised them. Everything else in them felt unsteady, like a slope after rain.

They had buried him at first light, up past the treeline where the pilgrim road bent out of sight of the village. No marker. A cairn of loose stone that would read as any traveler's waymark to anyone who passed, because that was the point of everything Theo had built in thirty years at Thornfell: nothing that pointed back at them. They had said no words over him. Words were for people who could afford witnesses.

Now the map lay on the table between them and the lamp, and the blood was on it, and there was no burying that.

Theo turned it over.

* * *

*They tell you the mountain is dead rock, and you believe them because believing is easier than digging.*

*I was nine when my mother first showed me the underside of a map. She held ours up to the window so the light came through the paper, and there—faint as breath on glass—were roads that weren't printed on the face of it. "Everything true is written twice," she said. "Once for the eyes they expect, and once for the eyes they fear."*

*Our family has kept the second writing for eleven generations. We are not heroes. Heroes get songs. We are keepers, which means we get graves, usually shallow ones, usually far from home.*

*If you are reading this, then I am one of those graves, and you are holding my inheritance, and I am sorry. Not for the weight of it. For the fact that no one will ever thank you.*

*Go to Cael. Find the door under the mountain. And do not trust the man on the throne, because the man on the throne is not a man.*

*—R.V., written at Saint Verre, in the year they burned the granaries*

* * *

Theo read it three times by lamplight, lips moving over the faded ink, and each time the last lines landed harder than the last. *Not a man.* The journal page was torn from a larger book, water-stained along one edge, the handwriting slanting leftward as though the writer had been moving even while he sat still. There were other pages folded inside the oilskin wrap—a handful, maybe a dozen—and Theo did not open them yet. Some part of them understood that once they read all of it, they would be in this past the point of climbing out. And some other part, a part they didn't much like, wanted to be.

They turned back to the map instead.

It was drawn on skin—not parchment; Theo knew parchment, had bought and sold enough contraband of every make to know the difference—and it was old, older than anything else in the cottage except perhaps the keeper themselves. The ink had gone the color of dried violets. But the linework was fine as frost on a windowpane, and the more Theo looked, the colder they felt.

Because it was Thornfell. It was Thornfell and the whole spine of the mountains south of it, drawn with a precision that made Theo's own knowledge feel like a child's chalk scrawl. There was the gate they kept—their gate, the only gate, marked with a small careful symbol like an eye with the lid half-closed. There was the smugglers' road Brannic's people ran, the one that hugged the cliff above the Greywater falls. There was the old pilgrim road where a dying man had bled into the snow not twelve hours ago, all of it exactly where it should be, all of it known.

And then there was the rest.

Roads that went *through*. Not around the massif, not along its flanks, but into it—passages threading the mountain's heart like veins through a fist. Theo traced one with a fingertip and felt their stomach drop, because they had walked every pass, every goat track, every collapsed mine adit within three days' walk of the gate, and none of these existed. None of these *could* exist. The deep rock under Cael was granite older than the kingdoms, and men did not tunnel through it; men tunneled around it or died trying.

Unless, said a voice in Theo's head that sounded unpleasantly like their mother, unless the tunnels were not dug by men.

The passages converged. That was the thing Theo couldn't stop seeing, the thing that made their tea go cold untouched on the table. However they wandered, however they branched through the dark, every line bent eventually toward a single point beneath Mount Cael's crown, and at that point the mapmaker had drawn a door.

Not a cave mouth. Not a mine entrance. A door—rectangular, framed, rendered in strokes so deliberate they might have been cut rather than drawn. And above it, small and unmistakable even after centuries of fading, the royal seal: the crowned stag of House Aldric, antlers spread like branches over the threshold.

Theo sat back. The chair creaked. Outside, the wind had died entirely, and the silence was worse.

They were, by trade and temperament, a person who dealt in certainty. Which passes froze first. Which ledges took a man's weight and which lied about it. What Brannic would pay for brandy and what Vey's kind would pay for silence. Thirty years of keeping the gate shut had taught Theo that the world was survivable if you knew exactly how big it was and never let it get bigger. This map made the world bigger. This map reached into the one place Theo had spent a lifetime pretending wasn't there—the deep heart of Thornfell, the reason the gate existed at all, the thing their predecessor had told them about with a shaking voice and then drunk himself quiet about until the shaking stopped.

"The thing wearing his crown," Theo said aloud, just to hear how it sounded in the empty room.

It sounded like madness. It sounded, also, like the truth sounded, which was a problem, because Theo had learned long ago that truth and madness made the same noise when they knocked.

They began, methodically, to fold the map away.

That was when the dog started barking.

* * *

*My father used to say the crown was a leash, and I thought he meant it the way priests mean things, as a story to keep children from stealing. He did not mean it that way. He meant: somewhere at the end of that leash is a thing that pulls.*

*Grandmother saw Aldric's grandfather at a feast, before she went into hiding. She said he laughed at all the right moments and ate nothing all night, and when the candles guttered his eyes stayed lit a half-second too long after the flame died. She said everyone noticed and no one said anything, because what would you say? "Your majesty, please stop being wrong"?*

*We are sworn to unbind it. I want to write that plainly so whoever comes after me understands it was never a choice dressed up as duty. It is a debt. Eleven generations of interest.*

*The gray riders are in the valley. If I am slow, remember: the map shows the way in, but the door opens both ways. Write that on your heart. The door opens both ways.*

* * *

Theo was at the window before conscious thought caught up, lamp snuffed, the map stuffed inside their coat against their chest like a stolen loaf. Down the switchback below the cottage, where the track met the pilgrim road, four horses were coming up through the melting snow at a pace that had nothing casual in it. Gray cloaks. Gray hoods. The livery of the king's inquisition, which in thirty years Theo had seen perhaps six times, always at a distance, always going somewhere else.

The dog—half-wild, more wolf than companion, a creature Theo fed without ever pretending to own—was bristling at the end of her chain by the woodshed, making a sound low in her throat like rocks grinding.

*Riders.* Rafael had been running from riders. Riders wearing this same gray, who had put at least two crossbow bolts into him and would very much like to know where he'd been headed, and who he'd given it to, and whether the snow had kept anyone warm overnight.

Theo's mind did what it always did under pressure: it got small and fast and practical. The back of the cottage faced the scree slope; there was a path down to the old quarry workings that a stranger wouldn't find in daylight, let alone in slush. They could run. They had run before, early years, before they'd learned that a keeper who runs stops being useful and starts being a question. Or—

Or they could answer the door.

Their hand was already on the latch. That frightened them more than the riders did, that some part of them had decided before the rest of them got a vote. Because the map was against their heart, and the journal was in their sleeve, and a dead man's oath was sitting in the room behind them like a third guest, and Theo Pemberton had spent thirty years being no one to everyone, and here came four someones in gray to ask questions, and the old arithmetic—the safe arithmetic, the *stay invisible, stay alive* arithmetic—said: *know nothing, show nothing, be boring, be stone.*

But the blood on the oilskin was drying against their ribs, and Rafael's voice was in their ears saying *swear it*, and Theo had sworn, God help them, they had sworn with a dying man's hand crushing theirs, and oaths were the one currency Theo had never counterfeited.

Three knocks. Measured. Official.

Theo opened the door.

Four riders stood in the slush, and the one in front—a woman with a face like a closed ledger and eyes that took inventory of the cottage, the woodpile, the dog, and Theo themselves in the space of a breath—held up a sheet of paper bearing the royal seal.

"Keeper." Her voice was pleasant the way ice on a pond is pleasant. "We're searching for a fugitive. A courier, wounded, traveling alone. Have you seen him?"

"No," Theo said, and it was even mostly true, because what they'd seen hadn't been a courier by the end. It had been a man becoming a grave.

"You keep this pass alone?" The woman's gaze drifted past Theo's shoulder, into the dim interior, unhurried, thorough. "Thirty years, the records say. Alone. That's a long time to be alone, Keeper."

"It's a long pass," Theo said.

Something flickered at the corner of the woman's mouth—not quite a smile, more the memory of where a smile would go on a different face. She held the sealed paper out. Theo took it. Their hands did not shake, and they hated how proud they were of that.

"Orders from the Crown," she said. "All traffic through Thornfell is to be reported. All travelers questioned. Anyone found aiding the fugitive will be treated as the fugitive." She let that sit in the cold air between them. "You'll want to remember which side of that sentence you'd rather stand on."

"I don't take sides," Theo said. "I keep a gate."

"Yes," the woman said softly. "So the records say."

She turned her horse. The other three followed, hooves punching holes in the crusted snow, and Theo stood in the doorway watching them descend the switchback toward the village, toward the inn, toward the ears and mouths of forty people who would now be asked about a stranger on the road during a whiteout.

Forty people. Any one of whom had seen Theo carry a body up from the treeline, if they'd been looking. Any one of whom talked, the way people talk, to stay warm.

Theo closed the door and leaned against it and let themselves shake now that shaking was affordable. The dog had gone quiet. On the table, the lamp waited to be relit, and beside it the folded oilskin, and inside the oilskin a dozen pages Theo had promised themselves they wouldn't read yet.

They crossed the room. They relit the lamp. They unfolded the journal, and read until the candle guttered, and outside the window the mountain stood black against the stars, full of roads that shouldn't exist, ending at a door that opened both ways.

At dawn, Theo packed a bag, banked the fire, filled the dog's trough to overflowing, and stood a long moment in the doorway looking down the pass—at the gate, their gate, sealed these thirty years, and beyond it the long gray road to Cael.

Then they went inside, and took down the key they swore they'd never use, and began, with dread hardening in their chest like frost in a wall, to plan.
