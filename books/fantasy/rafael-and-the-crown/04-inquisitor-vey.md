## 4. Inquisitor Vey

The snow had stopped falling by the time the column reached Thornfell, which Inquisitor Vey took as a small courtesy from a world that rarely offered her any.

She rode at the head of six soldiers in the king's gray, and she did not need to look behind her to know how they appeared to the village: a blade of winter come down the pilgrim road. She had spent twenty years learning what her own arrival looked like in the faces of others—the way doors closed softly rather than slammed, the way conversations died mid-word as though strangled, the way children were pulled indoors by hands that trembled only after the latch fell. She did not enjoy it. She noted it, the way a physician notes a fever. Fear was a symptom. It told her where the sickness lay.

Thornfell was a sick little village. She could see it before she dismounted.

Not in anything so crude as guilt written on faces—guilt was common as dirt and twice as useless—but in the *arrangement* of things. The innkeeper had swept his step that morning; she could see the broom marks under the fresh dusting of snow, and no one swept a step in this weather unless they expected it to be seen. The smith's forge was cold at midday, though smoke curled from his chimney, meaning he had banked it rather than let it die, meaning he had been watching the road. And the shrine at the crossroads—old, pre-royal, the kind of carved stone the crown tolerated in the mountains because tearing them down cost more than it gained—had fresh wax on its ledge. Someone had lit a candle for someone dead.

Three days ago, a courier had died on the old pilgrim road above this village. Vey knew this because she had made the ravens tell her: a rider out of Grayhollow with dispatches for the coast garrison had failed to arrive, and when the garrison sent men back along the route they found blood on the snow near the treeline, drag-marks leading off the road, and no body. The report had been filed, stamped, and forgotten by every clerk between here and the capital except one.

Vey remembered everything. It was the whole of her gift, and she had paid dearly for it.

"Master Aldous," she said, and the innkeeper flinched as though she'd struck him. "Your fire is warm. I'll take your common room for an hour."

"Inquisitor, we're honored, but—" He was a soft man, gray-bearded, with the particular sweat of a man whose conscience had begun working ahead of his fear. "We're simple folk up here. Nothing comes through Thornfell but weather."

"Nothing," Vey agreed. "That's what makes the exception interesting."

She took the bench nearest the hearth without waiting to be offered it, laid her document case on the table, and opened it with the unhurried care of a woman unwrapping something precious. Inside: paper, ink, a wax seal, and a thin ledger bound in gray leather that held, in her own cramped hand, twenty years of names.

"Bring me your headman. Bring me whoever found the blood on the road. Bring me anyone who saw a stranger pass in the last ten days." She looked up, and gave him the smile she had practiced until it no longer felt like anything at all. "And Master Aldous? Sit down. You look unwell. I'd hate for you to be unwell in front of your neighbors."

* * *

They came to her one at a time, as she preferred. Crowds lied collectively; individuals lied alone, and alone they always broke differently.

The headman was a farmer named Corwin Hale who smelled of turnips and dread. He confirmed that yes, a traveler had passed through nine days ago—young man, dark-haired, riding hard, said he was carrying salt contracts for a merchant house in Grayhollow. Had stayed one night. Paid in good silver. Left before dawn.

"Salt contracts," Vey said. She wrote nothing down. Writing made men relax; they thought the danger lived on the page. "In winter. Through the mountains."

"He seemed in a hurry, m'lady."

"They usually do, at the end." She let that sit. "Describe his horse."

"A bay. White star on the forehead. Lame in the near hind, a little."

Vey nodded slowly. A bay with a white star. The ledger in her case held a sketch of exactly such a horse, drawn eleven years ago by a customs officer outside Marrowgate who had seen a young man with dark hair ride past at night and thought nothing of it until the officer's superior showed him a wanted bill. The officer had described the horse. He had not survived the year—not from violence, merely from fever, but Vey had long ago stopped believing in coincidence at that scale.

"And while he stayed," she said, "did he speak with anyone? Anyone at all?"

Hale's eyes flicked—just once, just slightly—toward the window that faced the upper village, toward the crooked lane that climbed away from the square toward a cottage set apart beneath the black shoulder of the mountain.

There it was.

"There's a gate-warden," Hale said carefully. "Keeps the old pass sealed. Theo Pemberton. Strangers sometimes ask them about the roads, is all. It's nothing."

"Nothing," Vey repeated, and wrote *nothing* in her ledger, because sometimes the most useful thing a word could do was lie on a page in plain sight. "Tell me about Pemberton."

* * *

Theo Pemberton. The name went into the ledger beside a dozen others, but Vey turned the page afterward and looked, for a long moment, at what was already there.

Twenty years of it. Twenty years since a priest named Edouard Veraine had stood in the cathedral square in chains and refused, with a serenity that still haunted her, to recant his heresy: that the king was not the king, that something else wore the crown, that a hidden line of keepers existed sworn to undo it. Vey had been nineteen then, standing in the crowd with her sister's funeral still raw in her mouth, hating Veraine for the comfort his madness offered a plague-ridden city that needed someone to blame. The crown had burned him. His family had scattered like sparks.

And the sparks kept catching.

A smuggler executed in Marrowgate with a royal seal tattooed inside his wrist. A nun defrocked in Saint Verre for possessing a genealogy no nun should have owned. A courier network that moved letters in cipher through the mountain passes, generation after generation, like water finding cracks in stone. Each time the crown cut one down, another surfaced years later, somewhere new, and Vey had come to understand—with the patience of a woman who had nothing else—that you did not hunt a family. You hunted a *faith*, and faiths shed their names the way snakes shed skin.

Rafael. That was the name the last captured letter had carried, three years ago, before the cipher changed. Rafael Veraine, of the salt merchants' house in Grayhollow. Younger son. Trained in the roads. Sworn, if half the confiscated correspondence could be believed, to reach a place called the tomb beneath Mount Cael.

He had finally come to the mountains. And now there was blood on the snow above Thornfell and no body, and a gate-warden nobody could describe clearly because everyone described them differently—which meant either that Pemberton was forgettable by nature or unforgettable by practice, and Vey had met enough survivors of purges to know which kind interested her.

"The warden keeps to themselves," Hale was saying. "Been here fifteen years, maybe more. Keeps the gate shut. Nobody questions it—it's useful, having someone to turn travelers back. Landslides up there. Bad winters."

"Fifteen years." Vey smiled at him. "And in fifteen years, has anyone ever seen Pemberton pray?"

Hale blinked. "M'lady?"

"At the shrine. The crossroads shrine. Fresh candle-wax, I noticed. Who lit it?"

"I—I couldn't say. Mountain folk keep their own devotions."

"Yes," Vey said softly. "They do."

* * *

She walked the village alone that evening, as the light went blue and the lamps came up one by one, and she let herself be seen doing it. That was part of the machinery too: not the interrogation, not the ledger, but the slow orbit of the inquisitor through the lanes, pausing here, touching a fencepost there, looking too long at a window. By morning every household would have re-examined its own memory three times, and memory, repeatedly handled, always dropped something.

The cottage sat apart below the crag, smoke rising straight from its chimney in the windless cold. Neat woodpile. Snow shoveled in clean strokes. A garden plot turned and mulched even in deep winter—a warden who worked the earth through the frozen months, preparing against a spring they might not see, or simply unable to bear idleness. From the lane, Vey could see the gate itself, higher up: a slab of ancient fitted stone set into the mountain's flank, older than the kingdom, seamed with iron bands gone black with age. Sealed. The villagers said landslides. Vey had read the original survey reports from the reign of Aldric's grandfather, and the surveyors had recorded no landslide risk whatsoever. They had recorded, in a clerk's careful hand, an order: *let this way remain shut, by the king's pleasure, forever.*

Kings did not seal passes forever out of caution. Kings sealed passes forever out of fear.

She stood in the lane longer than was polite, and she knew the cottage knew it. Smoke behavior didn't change; the figure that crossed the lamplit window once, briefly, did not pause or hide—but it crossed *once*, where a person unaware of being watched crosses many times. So. Pemberton had seen her, measured her, and decided to be exactly as boring as possible.

Good. Boring people made mistakes on the third day, not the first.

"You're staring at my warden's house, inquisitor."

Vey turned. The smuggler—she had already catalogued him, Brannic, licensed for timber freight, suspected of everything else—leaned against the shrine wall with the ease of a man who had never once been arrested successfully. He was big through the shoulders, gray at the temples, with a face built for grinning and currently doing a masterful impression of friendly neutrality.

"Am I?"

"Everyone stares at Pemberton eventually. There's nowt else to look at up here." He pushed off the wall. "Bad business, though, what happened on the high road. Bandits, I heard."

"You heard bandits."

"Snow wolves, maybe. Terrible season for it." His grin widened a fraction. "Course, I'd know better than most if something else came through these parts. Being as I know every track and trail better than the crows do."

"Is that an offer, Master Brannic?"

"It's a courtesy, m'lady. Between professionals." He spread his hands. "I keep the crown's peace same as you. Different tools."

Vey studied him for a long moment. Smugglers were weather vanes; they pointed wherever the wind of profit blew, and right now Brannic smelled opportunity, not loyalty. Which meant he had already guessed the dead courier had been carrying something worth killing for, and he was pricing his silence accordingly.

"Here is my courtesy in return," she said. "When you learn something worth selling, bring it to me before you sell it to anyone else. I pay in coin. The other buyers in this economy pay in knives."

"Now that," Brannic said warmly, "is the most honest thing anyone's said in this village all winter."

He strolled off whistling. Vey watched him go and added a note to her mental file: *Brannic — useful, treacherous, predictable. Revisit.*

* * *

Night settled over Thornfell like a lid. In the inn's common room, Vey sat late over her ledger by candlelight, and around her the village slept badly, doors barred, dogs quiet, the whole settlement holding its breath around the wound she had come to probe.

She read back through the Veraine file, as she did every night on campaign. Edouard Veraine, burned. His wife, dead in flight. Two sons—one drowned crossing the Marle, one vanished. A daughter taken by the Temple, died of fever at fourteen, though Vey had personally inspected the grave registry and found the entry suspiciously convenient. Grandchildren: none confirmed. The line thinned and thinned, and yet it never quite snapped, and Vey had learned to distrust lines that refused to snap.

Her sister had died in the Gray Fever when Vey was seventeen, in a city where the king's physicians had been forbidden—by direct decree—to treat the poor quarters. She had held her sister's hand for six days while the fever burned her down to a whisper, and on the sixth day her sister had asked her why the king hated them, and Vey had had no answer, and she had joined the Inquisition within the year because it was the only institution in the kingdom that promised, however imperfectly, that someday the guilty would be *found out*. That the rot had a source. That someone, somewhere, would answer for it.

Twenty years later she served a rot she could not name, hunting a family that might be the cure, and some cold honest corner of her mind whispered, on nights like this, that she had gotten it backward. She silenced that corner. Everyone served something. Hers was justice, and justice required proof, and proof required patience, and patience she had in abundance.

One thing troubled her, and she turned it over now like a stone with something underneath.

No body. Blood on the road, drag-marks, no body. If Rafael Veraine was dead—and the quantity of blood argued strongly for it—who had moved him? Bandits stripped bodies and left them. Wolves scattered them. But *someone* had carried a dying man off the pilgrim road, and the only dwelling within two miles of that stretch was a cottage below a sealed gate, occupied by a warden who had lived fifteen years in the mountains without once lighting a candle at the public shrine.

Someone had taken Rafael Veraine in. Someone might have heard him die. And dying men with secrets spoke, if only to the snow.

Vey closed the ledger. Tomorrow she would interview the warden herself, gently, thoroughly, and watch the way they watched her back. She would leave a watcher of her own in Thornfell—a quiet man named Doss, who could sit in an inn for a month without being noticed, and who reported in cipher. And then she would ride the circuit to Grayhollow, because the salt merchant's house there had closed its shutters the day the news of blood on the road reached it, and closed shutters were confessions.

At the door of the common room she paused, candle in hand, and looked north through the frost-furred window toward the black mass of the mountain, where a sealed gate slept under iron and snow, and beneath it—her instinct insisted, though she had no proof, only twenty years of pattern—something the crown had been afraid of for three generations.

"Sleep well, Warden Pemberton," she murmured to the dark. "Tomorrow, we'll talk."

* * *

Doss arrived before dawn, as arranged, a gray man in a gray coat whom the village absorbed without noticing, the way a floor absorbs a nail driven flush. He took a corner table in the inn, ordered ale he barely drank, and began the patient work of becoming furniture.

By full light, Vey was mounted. She drew her column up in the square, checked her ledger one final time, and permitted herself the small ritual she allowed at the close of every inquiry: she read aloud, silently, the day's single true sentence.

*The heir came to the mountain. Someone sheltered him. The mountain keeps its mouths shut.*

It was not much. It was, however, a beginning, and beginnings were her trade.

She raised one gloved hand, and the column turned south toward Grayhollow, hooves striking sparks of frost from the cobbles, six riders in the king's gray shrinking down the valley road—while behind them, in the shadow of the inn's eaves, a gray man in a gray coat finished his ale, set down a copper coin, and settled in to wait.

Above the village, under the black shoulder of the mountain, a thin thread of smoke rose straight from a cottage chimney into the windless sky, patient as a pulse.

Vey did not look back. She never did. Looking back was for people who doubted, and doubt—for all that she was about to learn otherwise—was a luxury she still believed she could not afford.
