## 5. Brannic's Price

Theo buried Rafael at dawn, in the thin frost-hard ground behind the cottage where the heather grew stubborn against the scree.

There was no prayer to say. Theo had never learned the ones that mattered, and the ones the crown taught—*blessed be the king's long reign*, murmured over every grave from here to the coast—would have been a blasphemy worse than silence. So they dug with a spade older than they were, and they laid the courier down in his ruined gray coat because there was nothing else to dress him in, and they marked the mound with a flat stone because even a man who died a stranger deserved something a wolf couldn't scatter.

The map was in their shirt, against their chest, wrapped in oilcloth. It had not left their body since the night Rafael pressed it into their hands. Theo had slept with it, eaten with it, carried it to the privy and back like a madwoman guarding a child. Two nights now. The wax seal sat unbroken under their palm when they lay awake listening for hooves.

Riders in gray livery. That was what Rafael had gasped before the light went out of him—the only whole sentence the man had managed between the blood and the terror. *Riders. Gray. Don't let them—* And then the rest, the part that had lodged in Theo's ribs like a fishhook: *Swear it. Swear you'll carry it.*

Theo had sworn. They still didn't know entirely to what.

They tamped the last of the earth down with the flat of the spade and stood a while in the wind coming off the high passes, the wind that smelled of stone and old snow and, faintly, of woodsmoke from the village below. Three chimneys smoking in Thornfell. Nine if you counted the shepherds' bothies. Every one of them full of people who would sell Theo's name for a sack of barley flour if the asking price were right—not out of malice, but out of the same arithmetic that kept everyone alive up here: you gave what you had to give, and you hoped the mountain took someone else first.

The mountain had taken Rafael instead.

"You're getting soft," Theo told themselves aloud, and picked up the spade, and went down toward the village to do the stupidest thing they had done in twenty years of keeping the gate.

* * *

Brannic kept a room above the cooper's shed at the edge of Thornfell, which was the kind of arrangement that existed nowhere on any ledger and everywhere in practice. He called it an office. It contained a cot, a crate of unwatered brandy that had never paid duty, a wall of pegs hung with gear that shifted seasonally—fishing lines in spring, bird snares in summer, and come autumn, things with edges—and a table scarred with knife-points and ring-marks and, once, according to Brannic himself, the imprint of a customs man's teeth.

Theo knocked twice, paused, then once. The code was twelve years old and probably meaningless now, but habits were the only currency that held its value in the mountains.

"It's open," said Brannic's voice, "if you're buying, barred if you're begging."

Theo pushed the door open. Brannic sat at his table with his boots up on it, a big man gone soft in the middle the way a rope goes soft—not weak, just settled into its own shape. He was fifty-something and looked it honestly: beard going to iron, hands like root-balls, a face that had been broken at least twice and reset by amateurs. His eyes, small and quick as a stoat's, did not soften when they landed on Theo. They sharpened.

"Well," he said, and took his boots down slowly. "The keeper comes calling. Sit, sit. Or stand. You're the sort who stands." He poured two fingers of brandy into a tin cup and slid it across the table without being asked, which was itself a message: *I know why you're here, and I've decided to enjoy it.* "I heard you put a man in the ground this morning. Behind your cottage. No rites."

"Word travels."

"Word travels faster than anything in these hills except me running from word." Brannic drank, watching over the rim of the cup. "Stranger, was it? Nobody's cousin. Nobody's business. Just appears on the pilgrim road in the worst whiteout in three winters, and dies in the gatekeeper's cottage, and gets buried before sunup like a dead dog." He set the cup down with a click. "And now Vey's riders have been through twice asking after him, and half the village is saying the keeper wouldn't let anyone see the body, and the other half is saying there wasn't a body at all." He smiled. It was not a warm smile; Brannic's smiles were mostly architectural, load-bearing structures built to hold up whatever he was about to say next. "So. What are you selling, Pemberton?"

"I'm buying," Theo said.

That got a raised eyebrow. "Go on."

"Your silence. For a season. Maybe longer. You saw nothing, you heard nothing, and when the gray riders come back—and they will—you tell them the truth, which is that you know nothing. That part should be easy for you. You've practiced it your whole life."

Brannic laughed, a short bark with no humor in it, and leaned forward across the table. Up close he smelled of pitch and horse and brandy, and beneath all of it something like iron filings. "Here's my trouble with that offer," he said. "Silence from me costs me something, see? I make my living on knowing things. What came down the pass. What didn't. Who's carrying what and who's looking for it. My silence isn't empty—it's a warehouse full of goods, and you want me to board it up for free." He tapped one thick finger on the tabletop, once, twice, like a man counting coins. "So the question I keep circling back to, sitting up here nights, listening to you dig—is what's in the warehouse. What did the dying man give you that's worth a season of Brannic keeping his mouth shut?"

"Nothing."

"Mm." He reached under the table. Theo's hand moved before thought did—not to a weapon, Theo had never carried more than a skinning knife, but to the counter's edge, weight shifting to the balls of the feet—and Brannic noted the movement with the satisfaction of a man confirming a suspicion. He brought up not a blade but a scrap of cloth, ragged, scorched along one hem, and dropped it on the table between them.

Theo knew it at once and hated that they knew it at once. A scrap of gray wool, military weave, torn. From Rafael's coat. Theo had burned the bloodied blanket from the cot night before last, and the fire had been hot, and the wind had been coming off the crag—

"And here's the thing about fires," Brannic said conversationally, "is they throw sparks. And here's the thing about your chimney, Pemberton, is it draws like a bellows. Anyone standing downwind of your cottage two nights back got themselves a little blizzard of ash, and some of that ash was wool, and some of that wool was dyed the particular ugly gray that only one tailor in the capital is licensed to make." He spread his hands. "Now, a courier in king's livery dies on your floor and gets burned in your hearth within a day. Either you killed him—which would be newsworthy—or somebody else did, and he gave you something worth killing over, and you're sitting on it right now like a hen that won't leave the nest." His stoat eyes glittered. "Am I close?"

Theo stood very still. This was the discipline of twenty years: stillness. Panic was loud and loudness traveled. Somewhere below them a dog barked at the cart track, unhurried, ordinary, a world away from the cold center of the room.

"If I say yes," Theo said quietly, "what then?"

"Then we stop wasting each other's morning." Brannic leaned back, and the performance fell away from him by degrees, the way fog burns off a valley, and what was underneath was harder and older and, in its way, more honest. "You think I don't know what's moving, keeper? Gray riders in the Thornfell passes for the first time in a decade. Inquisitors knocking door to door in a village so poor even the tax men stopped coming. A stranger bleeding out on a road that hasn't seen a pilgrim since my grandfather's day." He ticked them off on his fingers. "Things like that don't happen alone. Things like that mean something's *loose*. Something worth finding. And I will find it—with you or without you. With you, I keep my mouth shut and my knives pointed the right way. Without you—" he shrugged, vast and amiable and terrible— "well. Inquisitor Vey strikes me as a woman who pays well for informants. Not coin. Amnesties. New arrangements. And I've had the same arrangement with the crown for fifteen years, and it's grown stale."

The room was very quiet. Theo became aware of the brandy sweating in its cup, untouched.

"You'd sell me," Theo said. Not accusing. Establishing terms.

"I'd trade you," Brannic corrected. "Selling's crude. There's no future in it." He turned the cup a slow quarter-turn on the table. "But understand me, Pemberton, because I'll only be generous once: I don't actually want Vey. Vey is a bad market. Vey takes and gives nothing back but permission. What I want is what the dead man was carrying, or a share of it. Whatever it opens, wherever it leads—a cut. One share out of however many shares there are, and mine's not negotiable, and neither is my company on the road, because I don't trust you not to vanish into the mountain the moment my back's turned, and you shouldn't trust me either, and isn't it nice that we're honest?" He extended one root-ball hand across the table. "Partners. Of the armed and mutually suspicious variety."

Theo looked at the hand and did not take it.

Everything in them that had survived this long screamed the same lesson: *no one*. No allies, no debts, no names shared over fires. The smuggler was a lever that Vey could pull, a loose thread Vey could find, and worse than a liability—he was a liability with a network, a man whose silence was rented rather than owned. Every instinct said walk out, take the map deeper, go alone, go dark, go the way Theo had always gone.

But the map showed roads through the mountain's heart. Roads even Theo didn't know, and Theo knew every goat-track and collapsed adit in these ranges better than the shepherds knew their own flocks. Roads that meant rockfall and drowned galleries and places where the dark had been undisturbed for centuries. Alone, with winter closing, with Vey's watcher somewhere on the paths already—that wasn't invisibility anymore. That was just dying further from help.

And Rafael had made them swear. The oath sat in Theo's chest like swallowed river-stone, smooth and heavy and impossible to cough up. An oath needed reaching the tomb. Reaching the tomb needed living. Living, apparently, needed Brannic.

"One share," Theo said at last. "Of whatever the tomb holds. If it holds anything. And you follow my lead on the road. Where I say camp, we camp. Where I say turn back, we turn back."

"Do I look like a man who turns back?"

"You look like a man who's never been underground," Theo said. "You will learn."

Something flickered across Brannic's face at that—curiosity, sharp and genuine, quickly filed away—and Theo understood they'd given away a piece of the puzzle for free and cursed themselves for it. But the hand was still extended, waiting, and refusal now would cost more than the slip had.

Theo shook it. Brannic's grip was dry and crushing and entirely without warmth, a handshake like signing a ledger in blood.

"Wonderful," Brannic said, and finally pushed the brandy across. "Drink. You look terrible. Then we talk crew—I can raise four, maybe five, good lads who ask no questions, which is fortunate, because I haven't got answers." He knocked back his own cup and refilled it, and for a moment his eyes went distant, calculating routes and rations and betrayals-to-come. "We move tomorrow. Before dark, anyway. Because I had a lad down at the ford this morning, and he says the inquisitor's column turned back toward the village an hour ago, and she's brought friends this time. Twice the horses. A cage wagon."

A cage wagon. Theo's stomach went cold and level, the way water goes just before it freezes. Not for smugglers. Smugglers got fined, flogged, occasionally hanged in the lowlands. Cage wagons came for witnesses. For people who'd *seen a stranger pass*.

"How long," Theo said, "do we have?"

"If her outriders push hard? She rides tonight. Knocking on doors by morning." Brannic grinned, and this time there was nothing load-bearing about it; it was the real thing, the grin of a man who had spent his whole life one step ahead of exactly this and found the taste of it sweeter than safety ever was. "So drink fast, keeper. We leave within the hour."

* * *

They left within forty minutes, which was thirty more than Brannic wanted and ten less than Theo needed.

It took no time at all to pack a life Theo had spent twenty years keeping weightless. Oilskin, flint, dried mutton, the good rope coiled from its peg by the door. The iron key to the Thornfell gate stayed on its nail—Theo looked at it a long moment, that blunt black length of metal that had defined them longer than any name had, and left it hanging. Whatever happened next, it would not involve locking anything.

The map went inside the shirt again, oilcloth against skin, Rafael's cooling wax seal pressing its faint circular promise over Theo's heart.

At the treeline behind the cottage, Theo stopped once and looked back at the flat stone on the fresh-turned earth. *I'm taking it,* they didn't say, because the dead heard plenty enough promises. *I don't know what it is. I don't know what it costs. But I'm taking it, and I've shaken hands with the devil's accountant to get it there, so if you've got opinions from wherever you are, now would be the time.*

The heather bent in the wind. The mountain said nothing, as mountains do.

Below, five figures were assembling in the blue shadow of the cooper's shed with the quiet economy of men who had loaded for flight many times: Brannic at their head checking girth-straps, and behind him a lean youth called Wren who never blinked enough, and the brothers Osk and Tam who were large in complementary ways, and a gray-templed woman named Maren with a crossbow slung across her back and the flat patient stare of someone who had buried two husbands and expected to bury more. None of them asked questions. All of them looked at Theo with open, unhurried appraisal—the look of hired weapons measuring the person they'd been hired around.

"We go up the drover's path," Theo said, "past the old cairn, and we don't light fires until we're over the shoulder of the fell. Questions later. Movement now."

"And after the shoulder?" Brannic swung up into the saddle, easy as breathing, and looked down with those quick stoat eyes. "Where does our road lead, partner? Since we're being honest with each other."

Theo looked north, to where Mount Cael stood above the cloud line with its summit sheathed in the season's first snow, beautiful and blind and hiding, beneath miles of stone and darkness and the sealed gate of Thornfell, a door marked with the royal seal.

"The tomb of a king nobody remembers," Theo said, and put their heels to the horse. "Try to keep up."

Behind them, far below, the first lanterns of Inquisitor Vey's column came around the bend of the valley road—small, orderly points of light, closing softly on an emptying village—and the mountain took the five riders and the keeper into its shadows, and shut its mouth.
