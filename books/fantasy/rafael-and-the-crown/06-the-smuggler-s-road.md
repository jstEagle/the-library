## 6. The Smuggler's Road

Theo Pemberton had crossed the Serpent's Spine forty times in twenty years, and every one of those crossings had begun with the same prayer: *let the mountain be indifferent today.* It was not a prayer to any god. Theo had stopped believing in gods around the time they'd stopped believing in kings, which was roughly the age of nine, when their father had first walked them out to the Thornfell gate and shown them the iron and said, *This is what we keep, and this is why no one can ever know us.* The prayer was older than theology. It was the prayer of people who lived by weather and stone.

The mountain was not indifferent today.

Wind came down off the high passes in long white fists, driving grit and old snow across the cliff path that Brannic's smugglers called the Widow's Ledge — a name Theo suspected Brannic himself had invented, because it sounded good over drinks and because fear kept his crew obedient. The ledge clung to the mountain's western face like a scar of mortar, wide enough for two mules abreast where it was generous, barely wide enough for one boot where it was honest. Below it, eight hundred feet of air ended in a river the color of hammered tin.

"Keep your eyes on the wall," Theo called back along the line. "Not the drop. The drop does nothing but make your knees stupid."

"You've a talent for comfort," Brannic said from behind them. He rode a shaggy gray mule with the easy slouch of a man who had never once feared falling, on the theory — Theo suspected — that death would have to catch him sober.

"Comfort costs extra."

"So does lying badly." Brannic urged the mule up alongside, close enough that Theo could smell the tar he slicked through his beard against the cold. "You told me last night you didn't know what the map showed. Then you spent half the night reading it by candlelight with your lips moving like a priest at mass. I don't read maps, Pemberton. I read people. You're the worst liar I've smuggled gunpowder past, and I once smuggled gunpowder past a customs man who was my own mother."

Theo kept their eyes on the path. "Your mother was a customs officer?"

"My mother was many things. That's not the point." Brannic's voice dropped into something less theatrical. "The point is that whatever's at the end of that map, you mean to reach it without me knowing why. And men who carry secrets up mountains tend to leave them scattered along the way. Usually along with their bodies."

Ahead, the line of the company snaked along the ledge: six of Brannic's crew leading pack-mules, and behind them Dagny Holt, the ferrywoman's daughter who had insisted on coming when she learned where Theo was bound — *because Rafael bled out on our stones,* she'd said, *and that makes him ours now.* She walked with her hood up and her chin down and the stubborn set of someone who intended to be a problem for everyone involved.

Theo said nothing. Silence had been their armor for twenty years; it did not shed easily, even now, even with the wax-sealed map riding against their ribs like a second heartbeat.

*He made me swear.* Rafael's hands had been so cold, pressing the cylinder of oilcloth and wax into theirs. *Swear, or all of this was nothing.*

They climbed.

* * *

Brannic watched Pemberton walk and thought: *there goes a person built entirely out of locked doors.*

He had known the gatekeeper of Thornfell for eleven years, in the way smugglers know border officials — as an obstacle to be priced, flattered, or waited out. In all those years, Pemberton had taken exactly three bribes, refused nine, reported none of either, and never once asked what was inside any crate that passed beneath their lantern. That kind of incorruptibility was rare, and Brannic distrusted it the way he distrusted unwatered wine: technically superior, probably poisoned.

But this was different. This wasn't a man refusing a bribe. This was a man carrying something worth more than every bribe Brannic had ever offered combined, and pretending it was nothing, and doing it *badly.*

He let the silence ride for a while. The wind filled it, screaming down the couloirs above them, and once a stone the size of a lamb came loose somewhere overhead and cracked against the path thirty yards back, and everyone froze except the mules, who were wiser about such things than men.

"Pemberton," Brannic said, when the line moved again. "Question."

"You'll ask it whether I say yes or no."

"Why does a gatekeeper keep a gate shut? Twenty years, same post. No promotion, no rotation. Vey's graycoats come through twice a year and you show them the seal and send them away. Smugglers pay you and hate you and nobody hates you enough to knife you, which means somebody bigger than all of us has an interest in you staying exactly where you are." He scratched under his hat brim. "I've run this road since before you grew your first beard. There are easier ways to hide. Deeper ones. So why the gate?"

"The gate pays."

"The gate pays *poorly.*"

"The gate pays in quiet," Theo said, and there was something in their voice then — not warmth, exactly, but the ghost of warmth, like sun on a stone that hadn't seen sun in years — "which is more than most wages buy."

Brannic laughed, short and sharp, and let it go. For now. He had a rule about pushing people on ledges, which was that you didn't, unless you were prepared to catch them or watch them fall. He wasn't sure yet which he preferred.

What he knew was this: three days ago, Pemberton had come to him asking after silence, offering coin, which meant the courier who'd died at the treeline had left something the crown wanted buried. And when a man pays for silence, Brannic had learned, what he's really buying is a witness. Better to own the witness outright.

And there was the other thing, the thing he hadn't told his crew. Last winter, hauling blackbrandi through the deep roads below Ravenmere, Brannic had found a passage that shouldn't exist — a cut stair, ancient, its walls worked with symbols worn nearly smooth, breathing warm air in the dead of winter. He'd turned back. Not from fear, he told himself. From sense.

Pemberton's map, glimpsed by firelight, had shown lines running through the mountain's heart.

*Same stone,* Brannic thought. *Same warm dark.* And he touched the little iron charm at his throat — a habit he mocked in others — and rode on.

* * *

The ledge failed them at midday.

It began as a sound — a deep, grinding exhalation from the mountain's shoulder, like some enormous sleeper turning over — and then the world tilted sideways into noise. Theo had heard avalanches before. They had a grammar: the crack, the rush, the long white silence after. This one broke the grammar. It came too fast and too low, spilling off the slope *above* the ledge rather than the peaks, as though something had been waiting there, coiled, and had chosen its moment.

"Off the path! Against the wall!" Theo's voice tore itself raw. They grabbed Dagny by the collar and shoved her flat against the rock face as the snow came down — not the soft powder of a natural slide but a churning flood mixed with scree, and within it, spinning, branches. Green branches. Snapped pine boughs with needles still fresh, carried down from slopes that stood bare above the treeline.

Then it was over. The line had been strung out along eighty yards of ledge; the slide had hit the middle of it. When the white haze cleared, Theo counted heads twice and got five crew both times, where there should have been six.

Merrick was gone. A young man, quick-handed, forever whittling; Brannic's best rope-worker. They found him forty feet down-slope, wedged in the rocks above the drop, and there was nothing to do but sit with him while he finished dying, which took longer than anyone wanted, and say the words over him that Brannic's people said — not a prayer, exactly, but a list. *Merrick of the Salt Road, who tied a bowline blindfolded, who owed me four crowns, who is forgiven.* Brannic said it flat and fast, like a man paying out rope, and his voice did not break until the very end of it, on *forgiven*, and then only slightly, and he pretended it hadn't.

"He was twenty-three," Brannic said afterward, standing at the edge where the snow had sheared the ledge away entirely. Ten yards of path, simply gone. Beyond the gap, the route continued, but between lay a fall no rope could safely cross loaded with mules. "Twenty-three, and he followed me because I promised him the Salt Road run next spring. Warm country. Girls with opinions." He spat over the edge. "That slide came off a slope with nothing on it. You saw the branches."

"I saw them."

"Snow doesn't pick up green wood, Pemberton. Somebody rolled that slope. Somebody who wanted us stopped here, or funneled." His eyes were red-rimmed and absolutely steady. "Graycloats don't climb. So who does?"

Theo looked at the broken path, and at the map burning against their ribs, and understood with a cold clarity that the choice had been made for them. The ledge was done. Behind them lay Vey's road, Vey's watchers, Vey's patient machinery. Ahead, below the gap, the mountain fell away into a fold of shadowed rock — and there, if Theo remembered the map rightly, and they always remembered rightly — a cleft in the cliffside. An entrance no honest road used.

An entrance the map drew in ink darker than all the rest.

"There's another way," Theo said.

Brannic turned slowly. "Through the mountain."

"Yes."

"And how is it," Brannic said, very quietly, in the tone of a man laying a card face-down, "that a keeper of gates knows what's inside my mountain?"

*My mountain.* Theo almost smiled at that, and the smile must have shown, because Brannic's eyes narrowed further.

"It's on the map," Theo said, which was true, and a lie, because the map showed the way but it could not explain why Theo's mouth had gone dry at the sight of it, why the ink-dark line had seemed to pulse faintly, like a vein.

"Show me."

Theo took out the map — carefully, watching every face among the survivors, watching Dagny's pale and resolute one — and unrolled just enough of it against the rock. The lines ran inward, down, threading passages that no surveyor had charted, converging on the mountain's heart like streams finding a sea.

Brannic studied it for a long moment. Then he laughed — one hard bark with nothing in it of humor.

"That's the deep road," he said. "The one I found last winter. The warm one." He looked at the gap in the ledge, then back at the map, and Theo watched a whole war happen behind his eyes — greed and grief and superstition and the particular arithmetic of a man who had already paid one life for this venture and could not bear to have paid it for nothing.

"Merrick's body comes with us," Brannic said at last. "We wrap him proper. And Pemberton — when we're underground, you tell me what this map is *for*. Not where it goes. What it's for. Or I turn us around and take my chances with the graycloaks."

"That may cost you more than Merrick did."

"Everything costs more than Merrick did." Brannic turned away and started shouting orders in the flat, carrying voice of a man who had buried crews before and would bury them again. "Rope! Oilskins! We're going under the world, gentlemen, so kiss whatever god you insult least!"

* * *

The cleft swallowed them at dusk.

It was narrower than it looked from the ledge — a vertical slot in the cliff, half-hidden by hanging birch, and the first hundred feet were the worst climbing Theo had done in a decade, mules screaming softly in protest as they were lowered by sling down through the dark. But then the slot opened, and widened, and became a gallery: a vaulted corridor of stone so old its toolmarks had dissolved into ripples, like water frozen mid-flow.

And it was warm.

Not the stale warmth of enclosed air. A living warmth, rising from the rock itself, gentle as breath. Theo held their palm flat against the wall and felt it — a slow pulse, faint, patient, like the heartbeat of something sleeping very deeply and very near. Fungus glowed along the seams of the stone in pale blue-green clusters, shedding light like drowned moonlight, enough to see by, enough to make every shadow look like it was considering moving.

Dagny pressed close to Theo's side. "Is it supposed to do that?" she whispered, meaning the light, meaning the warmth, meaning everything.

"No," Theo said honestly. "But it's going to."

Behind them, the crew had gone quiet in the way frightened men go quiet — loud jokes abandoned, boots placed carefully, weapons gripped. Brannic brought up the rear with a lantern he didn't need, and as Theo glanced back, they caught his expression: not fear. Recognition. He was looking at the worked stone, the ancient stair-cut in the gallery floor, the symbols worn smooth, and Theo realized he *had* found this place before, or its cousin, and had turned back, and had never told anyone.

Their eyes met in the fungus-light. Neither spoke. There would be a reckoning about that later, Theo knew — the debt of secrets was compounding between them hour by hour, and debts like that always came due at the worst possible moment.

They walked on into the mountain's warm dark, following a line of ink toward a door marked with a king's seal, and far above and behind them, faint as memory, the wind mourned along the broken ledge where Merrick of the Salt Road lay wrapped in oilskin, waiting to go home.

The pulse in the stone beat on. Once, Theo thought — just once — it quickened, as if the thing beneath had noticed their footsteps, and was glad.
