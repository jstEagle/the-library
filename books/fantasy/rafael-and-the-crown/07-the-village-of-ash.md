## 7. The Village of Ash

They smelled it before they saw it.

Brannic called a halt at the mouth of the pass, nostrils flaring like an old hound's, and Theo knew the smell too—knew it from childhood, from the year the eastern slopes burned and every farmer between Thornfell and the river stood in their fields watching the smoke rise and saying nothing to one another. Char and wet ash. Rendered fat. Underneath it all, something sweeter and worse that Theo refused to name.

"Waystation's over the ridge," Brannic said. "Half a day off the road. My crew watered there last autumn." He looked at Theo sidelong, and there was no greed in his face now, only the flat calculation of a man revising a ledger. "Nobody burns a waystation for warmth."

Theo said nothing. They had been saying very little for two days, since the avalanche took Dellis off the cliff path—since the mountain had shrugged and swallowed a man whole while Brannic's crew stood shouting into the white roar of it—and since Theo had led them, map folded against their chest, onto routes that appeared on no chart any cartographer would admit to owning. The company had stopped asking where they were going. That was its own kind of answer.

They went over the ridge single file, boots quiet on scree, and came down through a stand of blackened pines into the village of Hallowmere.

It was not a village anymore. It was a lesson.

* * *

Hallowmere had been, in Theo's memory of other people's memories—a waystation community, forty souls perhaps, living off pilgrim traffic on the old road that ran beneath Cael's southern shoulder. A longhouse for travelers. A well with a stone lip worn smooth by centuries of cupped hands. Gardens terraced up the hillside, and a shrine to some minor road-saint whose name Theo had never bothered to learn, because the shrine was small and harmless and asked nothing of anyone.

The longhouse was a skeleton of black beams. The gardens were trampled to mud by horses—many horses, shod, moving in formation, the hoofprints overlapping in patterns that spoke of riders wheeling and circling, herding. The well still stood. Someone had been thrown down it; Theo could see the shape of what lay at the bottom before their mind finished refusing the image.

And everywhere, the ash. Drifted against doorstones like gray snow. Coating the shrine so that the little saint wore a shroud. Rising in a fine, choking dust when the wind moved, so that the whole village seemed to breathe around them, a slow dead exhalation.

"Dellis," Brannic said quietly. Not the man they'd lost—their scout, a lean woman named Sefa, who had gone ahead at first light and now came back down the hill at a run, face gray above her scarf.

"Three survivors," she reported. "In the root cellar under the east garden. An old woman and two children. The rest—" She stopped. Swallowed. "The rest are in the longhouse. Or they're not anywhere."

"How many riders?" Theo asked. Their voice came out level, and they hated how easily levelness came, hated the lifetime of practice behind it.

Sefa looked at Brannic before answering. "She says gray cloaks. King's colors, but wrong—no crest, just gray. A dozen or more. They came three nights ago. Took everyone who could walk. Burned the rest of it and rode north." A pause. "The old woman says they asked questions first. About a stranger on the road."

The wind moved through the ashes. Somewhere in the ruin of the longhouse, a beam settled with a groan like a dying animal.

"They wanted to know if anyone had seen him," Theo said. "Rafael."

"You know this," Brannic said. Not a question. His hand rested near his belt knife in a way that might have been habit and might not have been. "You've known since the night you came to me with that map sewn into your coat. You know why a dozen men in king's gray burned forty people over a stranger passing through."

Theo looked at the well. At the trampled gardens. At the root cellar door, half-hidden under ash, where two children waited for someone to tell them what world they had woken into.

"I know he died in my arms," Theo said. "I know he gave me something worth killing villages for. And I know that if I'd turned that traveler away at my gate like I turn everyone away—" They stopped. The sentence wouldn't finish itself, and they couldn't force it. Everyone in the company heard the ending anyway.

Brannic held their gaze for a long moment. Then he spat into the ash. "Get the survivors. We move in twenty minutes."

"We're taking them?" Sefa sounded startled.

"We're not leaving children in a graveyard." He turned away, already shouting orders, and Theo understood suddenly that the smuggler had made his own arithmetic: that whatever was hunting Rafael's map had shown him exactly what happened to people who stood apart from it. There was no standing apart. There never had been. Theo had spent thirty years believing otherwise, and forty strangers in a burning longhouse were the interest come due on that belief.

* * *

The old woman's name was Maren. She walked out of the root cellar leaning on Sefa's arm, straight-backed despite her years, and she would not look at the longhouse. She looked at Theo instead—at their weathered gatekeeper's coat, their travel-worn gear—and her eyes were dry and terrible.

"They sat us in the square," she said, when Theo brought her water from a stream beyond the gardens. Her voice was steady the way ice is steady. "Twelve riders and an officer with a white glove. He asked each of us the same question: had we seen a wounded man pass, tall, dark-haired, carrying a courier's satchel. And one by one we said no, because most of us hadn't, and then they got to Tam Wicker's boy, who's eleven and honest as bread, and he said yes, a man like that stayed one night in autumn and left before dawn."

Maren drank. Her hands did not shake.

"The officer thanked him. Gave him a coin. Then they took every soul who'd answered yes to anything—Tam's boy, the miller who remembered hoofbeats, the widow who'd sold bread to a dark-haired traveler in spring, though gods know that was half the valley. Took them north, tied in a line. Burned the village so no one would come looking and ask why."

"Where's Tam's family?" Sefa asked softly.

"The miller's wife is in the well," Maren said. "Tam's wife is in the longhouse. The officer said the king protects those who protect the crown." She handed back the waterskin with terrible courtesy. "So. You'll be going after them, then. Whoever you are. Because someone has to."

It wasn't a question either, and Theo couldn't answer it honestly, because the honest answer was *I don't know what I'm doing, I'm a gatekeeper, I've spent my life keeping doors shut*, and that answer would get them all killed faster than any inquisitor. Instead Theo asked about the riders' direction, their pace, whether they'd left anyone behind, and Maren answered everything with that same awful steadiness, because grief past a certain depth stops being loud.

* * *

Theo found the letter at dusk, alone in the ruins.

They told themselves they were searching the longhouse for supplies—iron nails, a kettle, anything the company could use—and that was even partly true. But their feet carried them to the far corner, to the spot where the ash lay deepest, where something small and flat had survived the fire beneath a collapsed roof beam, sheltered like a seed under a fallen tree.

A letter. Water-stained at one edge, scorched at another, but legible. Theo recognized the handwriting before they read a word—the same careful, slanting script as the marginal notes on the map, as the journal pages they'd studied by lamplight until the candles drowned in their own wax.

*To my cousin Aldous, greetings, and may the Road-Saint keep your feet—*

*I write in haste and trust you to burn this. Mother says the summons came again from the capital; the third this year. They ask Father to attend the court as "advisor to the archive," which we all understand means hostage and question-answerer both. Grandfather's papers grow dangerous to keep. Vey's people searched the ferry-house at Marlow twice this season.*

*You asked once why our family keeps faith with a dead king when the living one sits right there in Cael's own shadow. I will try once more to answer. The man on the throne is not the king. The king is underneath, in the tomb, and the crown walks his body around like a puppeteer walks a marionette, and every year the marionette's strings grow shorter. Our oath is to the man, not the mask. If the strings are ever cut, it will be by our hands or never. That is the whole of it, Aldous. We serve the true king because someone must remember him, and remembering is the beginning of every rescue.*

*Burn this. Give my love to your girls. —R.*

Theo knelt in the ash a long time, the paper trembling in their grip—not from wind.

*Our oath is to the man, not the mask.* Generations of it. A family that had carried the truth the way other families carried heirlooms, feeding it down the years father to child, knowing—knowing—that the crown would kill them for it eventually, and keeping faith anyway. And now the crown had kept its part. Gray cloaks and white gloves and villages burned to sift for one courier's trail, one family's bloodline, hunted across twenty years until it came down to a dying man on a snowy road pressing a map into the hands of a stranger because there was no one else. Because everyone else was ash.

Because of Theo. Thirty years of locked gates and turned-away travelers and *not my business, not my war, let the mountains keep their secrets*—and the rot had spread the whole time, patient as fungus, eating village after village whenever a dark-haired stranger passed through. Invisibility hadn't been peace. It had been surrender, sold at a price others paid.

Theo folded the letter along its creases and put it inside their coat, against the map, where it pressed like a second heartbeat.

When they rose, Brannic was standing at the doorway of the ruined longhouse, watching them. He didn't ask what they'd found. He'd gone very quiet since Hallowmere, quieter than Theo had ever seen him, and there was a new thing in his face that Theo couldn't read and didn't trust—but didn't entirely fear, either.

"Root cellar's stocked," he said. "We've pulled everything useful. Sefa's rigged a litter for the old woman." A pause. "You look like a man deciding something."

"I'm not a man," Theo said absently, "and yes. I am."

* * *

The watcher made his mistake at moonrise.

He was good—Theo would grant him that. He'd tracked them across two days of broken country without showing himself, and he'd chosen his approach well: downslope from the shrine, through the black pines, close enough in the last hour that Theo felt him the way you feel weather changing, a prickle between the shoulder blades that thirty years of solitary gatekeeping had honed finer than any soldier's instinct. When Theo turned from the fire, ostensibly to check the litter straps, they saw him.

A shadow among shadows at the treeline. A lens-glint where eyes should be. He was writing—Theo could see the faint motion of his arm, scratching notes by feel, recording numbers and headings and direction of travel. By morning that report would be in Inquisitor Vey's hands, and Vey would have their route, their number, their faces.

Theo weighed it in the old cold arithmetic: slip away tonight, scatter the company, go to ground. It might work. It might have worked, once, when the enemy was only hunting a map instead of erasing whole villages to find it.

But Maren was asleep by the fire. Two children were curled against her sides. And the well was full.

"No going back," Theo murmured, and moved.

They didn't call out—they owed the watcher no warning, and warning would cost seconds—but the crunch of Theo's boot was enough. The man spun, hand to his belt, and got halfway to a whistle before Brannic came out of the darkness on his blind side like a landslide given purpose. There was no duel in it, no fair exchange. Brannic's forearm caught the watcher's throat, his knee took the man's legs, and they went down together into the ash, and Brannic's knife did its brief, efficient work before the whistle ever found air.

It was fast. That was the only mercy in it, and Theo wasn't sure it was a mercy at all.

Brannic crouched over the body a moment, breathing hard, then went through the dead man's pockets with a professional's economy. He came up with a notebook—small, oilcloth-wrapped, dense with a clerk's tiny handwriting. He flipped through it by the light of the coals and his face went strange.

"He's been following us since the pass," Brannic said. "Since before Hallowmere. Days of entries." He read a line aloud, flat and toneless: "*Subject consults map at intervals. Subject displays knowledge of undocumented passages. Recommend continued observation pending confirmation of route terminus.*" He looked up. "He wasn't sent to kill us, Pemberton. He was sent to *follow* us. Which means somebody wants to know where that map ends."

"The tomb," Theo said. "Vey wants us to lead her to it."

"Then we've been carrying bait this whole road." Brannic closed the notebook and tucked it into his coat, and Theo watched the smuggler do the arithmetic one more time—bait, bounty, buried treasure, dead villages—and arrive somewhere none of them had planned.

"So what now?" Sefa asked from the fire. "Every road south is watched. We can't take the old woman and the children through a cordon."

Theo looked at the fire. At Maren's face in its light, awake now, listening to everything. At the letter against their chest, and the map beneath it, and the memory of Rafael's blood going cold on their hands while he made them swear.

"Now we stop running the roads they know," Theo said. "The map charts ways under the mountain that no king's rider has ever ridden. Warm caves, underground rivers—Rafael's journal calls them the deep roads. It's slower. It's darker. Half of it probably kills us." They met Brannic's eyes across the fire. "But nobody's waiting for us down there. And nobody's burning villages over it."

"And the survivors?"

"Ondine's chapel is six days west, at the foot of Cael. The hermit who tends it owes my family a debt older than either of us knows. Refugees gather there—I've heard it from the traders for years. Maren and the children will be safe among refugees." Theo stood, brushing ash from their coat, and felt the decision settle over them like armor that had been fitted for someone larger. "After that—we go where the map goes. All the way."

Brannic studied them for a long moment. "You understand what you're saying. You're talking about walking into the king's own mountain, after the thing that burned this village, with four smugglers, an old woman's blessing, and a dead man's letters."

"Yes," Theo said.

Something that was almost a grin touched Brannic's mouth, there in the firelight, grim as it was. "Good," he said. "I was worried you'd suggest something sensible."

Above them, Cael stood black against the stars, vast and silent, breathing its strange warm breath from some depth no honest map had ever charted. Theo touched the letter through their coat, once, like a talisman—or a debt.

At dawn they struck camp, set Maren and the children on the litter, and turned west, toward the hermit and the deep roads and whatever waited beneath the crown.

Behind them, Hallowmere's ash drifted on the wind, going nowhere, settling over everything.
