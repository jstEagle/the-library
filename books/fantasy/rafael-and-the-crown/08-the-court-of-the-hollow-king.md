## 8. The Court of the Hollow King

Aldric had learned, over nineteen years, to live the way a drowning man lives: in fragments, in stolen breaths between waves.

The mornings were worst. The thing woke before he did—it was always waking before him—and by the time Aldric surfaced from whatever dark it kept him in, the king's body had already been bathed, dressed, fed, and seated at the council table with its hands folded in an attitude of patient majesty. Aldric would come back to himself in small increments. First sensation: the weight of the crown, that cold circlet resting on his skull like a hand of iron. Then sound: the murmur of councilors, the scratch of a quill, the tick of the great clock in the corner that no one ever wound and yet never stopped. Then sight—and this was always the cruelest part—sight came last, so that for a few moments each day Aldric existed as pure consciousness trapped inside his own eyes, watching hands that were not his move across parchment, signing decrees in a handwriting that had once been his and had slowly, imperceptibly, become something else. Tighter. Older. The script of something that had been practicing being human for a very long time.

*Good morning, little king,* it said, in the place where his own thoughts should have been.

Aldric did not answer. He had stopped answering years ago, when he learned that the thing fed on attention the way fire fed on oil. But silence was its own kind of speech, and he maintained it the way other men maintained prayer.

* * *

The council session began at the ninth bell, as it had every morning for two decades, and Aldric sat through it the way a man sits through his own funeral: present, powerless, attentive to every detail because details were all he had left.

The chamber itself was beautiful, and he hated it. Vaulted ceiling painted with the ascension of Saint Verre, gold leaf catching the morning light from windows of colored glass. Below the saints, below the gilded cornices, eleven men sat around a crescent table discussing grain tariffs while their king listened with an expression of grave benevolence that Aldric could not feel and could not stop.

He had learned to find the cracks. That was the whole of his existence now—finding cracks. A blink held half a second too long. A finger twitching against the armrest. Once, years ago, during a particularly tedious dispute over fishing rights on the Vell, he had managed to move his own left hand—a single centimeter, a tremor, nothing more—and Lord Cassel had paused mid-sentence, frowned at the king as if noticing a draft, and moved on.

It had cost him three days of darkness for that centimeter. Three days of the thing pressing him down into the cold, down past memory, down to the place where he forgot his own name and almost, almost let go. He did not regret it. That was important. Whatever else happened, he refused to regret the small rebellions, because regret was the first thing the parasite ate.

*You are quiet today,* it observed, as Chancellor Ossory read aloud from a report on southern harvests. *Quiet even for you.*

Aldric watched the light move through the stained glass. A saint's face, rendered in ruby and cobalt, looked down on the council with painted compassion. He wondered if the saints knew what went on beneath them. He wondered if they cared.

*I know what you are doing,* the thing said. Its voice had no sound, exactly. It was more like pressure, like water finding the low places inside him. *You are counting the bells again. You think I don't notice.*

He was counting the bells. Nine strikes for the session's start; he marked each one somewhere deep, in a ledger only he could see. Nineteen years of ledgers. If he ever got out—if there was ever an out—he would be able to tell them exactly how long. Someone ought to know how long.

*There is no one to tell,* the parasite said gently, almost kindly, which was worse than cruelty. Cruelty implied he was worth the effort. Kindness meant it didn't care whether he struggled at all.

Ossory finished the harvest report. The Lord Marshal rose to speak of border levies. And then, at the tenth bell, the doors opened and Inquisitor Vey entered without announcement—which she alone was permitted to do—and the temperature of the room changed the way a room changes when a blade is drawn, slowly, under a cloak.

Aldric felt the thing in the crown stir with interest. It was subtle—the faintest quickening, like a predator lifting its head—but he felt it through his whole body, through the bones the crown touched. Interest. Appetite. After nineteen years he knew its hungers better than his own.

Vey crossed the marble floor and bowed. She was gray from collar to boot, the king's livery worn like armor, her face composed into the particular blankness that Aldric had come to recognize as her version of courtesy. Her eyes swept the council table once—cataloguing, always cataloguing—and settled on the throne.

"Inquisitor," the king's mouth said, in the king's voice. "Your report."

"Highness." She produced a leather folio but did not open it. "The courier is dead. Rafael of the line of Sarel."

The name struck the chamber like a dropped goblet. Aldric saw it land on each face around the table—confusion on most, blank incomprehension, the name meaning nothing to men who had never heard it spoken aloud in twenty years. But Aldric felt the crown flare hot against his scalp, a ring of fire, and behind it the thing's attention sharpened to a needle's point.

*Rafael.* It tasted the name. *Rafael, Rafael. Twenty years I have hunted that bloodline, and now one crawls into my mountains to die. How convenient. How suspicious.*

"He died on the Thornfell road," Vey continued. "Pursued by riders wearing our livery—though not ours. Smugglers found the body. The map he carried was not recovered." A pause, precise as a scalpel. "Nor was it burned. My watcher reports the gatekeeper took possession of something before the courier expired. Something sealed in wax."

"The gatekeeper," the king's mouth repeated. The thing made the voice flat, unreadable. "Theo Pemberton."

"You know the name."

"I know every soul in those mountains worth knowing." A pause that the council read as royal deliberation and Aldric recognized as the thing consulting some vast, slow patience. "And I know that the Pembertons have kept the Thornfell gate for six generations, asking no questions and answering none. Such discretion is either loyalty or camouflage."

"Which do you believe?"

"I believe," said the thing wearing his crown, "that dead men cannot carry maps. Send riders to Thornfell. Quietly. And, Inquisitor—" the king's head turned, and Aldric felt his own neck muscles obey a will not his own, felt his eyes fix on Vey with an intensity that made even her composure flicker, "—the mountains are to be scoured. Every road, every waystation, every village that has ever traded with the smuggler paths. If the map exists, I want it in my hand before the Binding Feast. If it does not exist, I want the ashes of whoever imagined it."

Aldric screamed, somewhere very far down, where screaming made no sound at all. Villages. He thought of the villages he had ridden through as a young prince—Thornmere with its bell tower, Ashcombe where they made the good cider—and understood, with the terrible clarity available only to the helpless, precisely what *scoured* meant.

*Yes,* the thing said, and there was satisfaction in it, rich and unhurried. *You understand me perfectly. You always have.*

Vey bowed again. "It will be done, Highness. There is... one matter more." She hesitated, and Aldric, who had spent nineteen years studying faces because faces were the only landscape left to him, saw something move beneath her stillness. Not doubt. Grief, perhaps, or its fossil. "The courier's effects. Among them was a letter. Sealed with the old royal signet—not yours, Highness. The true seal. The one your grandfather used before the Reforms."

The chamber had gone very quiet. Old Lord Cassel, who remembered the Reforms, had gone the color of tallow.

"What did the letter say?" the thing asked, and now the voice had changed—lower, hungrier, the mask of kingship thinning enough that Ossory glanced up nervously from his papers.

"It was addressed to the tomb," Vey said. "To the king sleeping beneath Mount Cael. It spoke of debts unpaid. Of a binding that must be unbound." Her eyes came up. "Of *the thing wearing his crown*."

For one suspended moment, Aldric felt the parasite forget him entirely. Its attention wrenched away, inward, downward, toward some depth in the mountain's roots where it had slept before men forged it a cage of gold and ceremony. In that moment—brief as a blink, precious as water—the pressure holding Aldric down eased.

He spent it the way he had learned to spend such moments, instantly and without hesitation. His right hand moved. Two inches, maybe three, dragging across the armrest, and one finger tapped—once, twice—against the gilded wood. A rhythm any court musician would know. Three beats and a rest. The old signal, the one his father had taught him, the one that meant *I am alive, I am here, hear me.*

No one saw. Or rather—no one showed that they saw. But as Vey withdrew and the council resumed its murmuring, Aldric noticed that Lord Cassel would not look at the throne. The old man's quill hovered above his parchment without writing, and his lips moved once, silently, shaping a word that might have been a prayer or might have been a name.

Then the wave came back. The thing returned from wherever it had gone, settled into his bones like cold water filling a boot, and pressed him down, down, down.

*Clever little king,* it murmured, almost fond. *Tap tap tap. Did you truly think I would leave you your fingers and not count them?*

The darkness rose to meet him. Aldric sank into it, clutching the only things he had: nine bells counted, two inches traveled, one word possibly seen. Somewhere far above him, in a body he could no longer call his own, the King of Cael smiled at his councilors and asked, graciously, whether there was any more business that morning.

* * *

They kept him, sometimes, in the high gallery above the throne room—a habit of the thing's that Aldric had never understood until late in his captivity, when he realized it liked to look at itself. At night, when the palace slept, the parasite would bring his body here and stand at the rail, gazing down at the empty throne lit by banked coals, and Aldric would surface just enough to watch himself watch himself. It was the closest thing to conversation they had.

"Why the feast?" Aldric asked it once, years ago, in that hour. He hadn't spoken aloud in so long that his voice came out ruined, a whisper full of gravel. "Why renew yourself in front of the whole court? What is it for?"

The thing considered the question with the patience of a mountain considering weather. When it answered, it used his mouth, and hearing his own voice reply was a violation never dulled by repetition.

"Ceremony is a chain, little king. It binds me as much as it binds you. The crown was forged to hold me, and hold me it does—every rite, every feast, every oath sworn in my presence tightens the links. The Binding Feast renews the oldest link of all." A pause. His lips curved, and it was not his smile. "But chains grow old. Metal fatigues. And lately, little king, I have felt the links... thinning. So I will renew them in front of everyone who matters, with everyone watching, so that no one ever wonders again what sits upon the throne."

"And after?" Aldric whispered. "After you've consumed what's left of me?"

"After?" The thing laughed, and the laugh was the worst sound in the world, because it was his laugh. "There is no after for you. You are the candle, and I am the burning. But take comfort—you will not be alone in the dark forever. One day another vessel will sit beneath this crown. Perhaps a child of your body, if you'd ever managed to produce one. Perhaps a brother, a cousin. The binders were thorough. They built me a dynasty to feed on."

"They built you a prison," Aldric said.

"They built me a *larder*."

That had been years ago. Tonight, standing at the gallery rail with the coals dying below, Aldric felt the thing distracted again—that same downward pull, that same restless attention turning toward the mountain's roots. It had been like this since the council. Since the name *Rafael*. Since the words *unbinding*. Something had frightened it, and Aldric held onto that knowledge the way a starving man holds bread.

*You felt it too,* he said, into the dark of his own mind. *The letter. You're afraid.*

The thing did not answer. But it did not press him down, either, and in the strange truce of that silence Aldric stood at the rail and did the mathematics of hope. A courier had died carrying a map. A gatekeeper had taken it. Riders would ride, villages would burn, and somewhere in the gray peaks a stranger named Theo Pemberton was holding the only key to a door the thing in the crown had spent twenty years pretending wasn't there.

*Hold it well,* Aldric thought, toward a person he would never meet, in mountains he would never see again. *Whatever it costs you—hold it well.*

Below him the coals shifted, flared, and settled. The crown caught their light and threw it back in a thin gold line, like a seam of fire around a prisoner's brow.

Far to the north, past the capital's walls, past the pilgrim roads and the burned waystations, Mount Cael waited under the stars with its buried king and its locked door—and Aldric, surfacing one last time before the dark took him, felt through the crown's cold circle a tremor he had not felt in nineteen years.

The thing was afraid.
