## 9. Underground Rivers

The map had lied about the cold.

Theo had expected the mountain's heart to be a place of ice and stone silence, the kind of deep that pressed on a person like a thumb on an eye. Instead, as they descended past the last of the smuggler tunnels Brannic knew—the ones he'd sworn up and down he'd mapped in his own head, until the map proved him wrong for the fourth time that day—a warmth rose to meet them. It came up through their boot soles, faint at first, then insistent, like standing too long on a sleeping animal's flank.

"Feel that?" Brannic asked, for the fifth time.

"I feel it."

"In forty years under these peaks I've never felt a mountain warm." He said it lightly, but Theo had learned his tells: the way his hand drifted to the axe at his belt when something troubled him, the way his jokes came faster and thinner. "Caves are cold. That's the whole point of caves. Cold and dark and honest about it."

The fungus began where the warmth did.

At first Theo took the pale glow for moonstone, some vein of luminescent ore catching light from their lanterns. Then they rounded a shoulder of rock wide enough to drive a cart through and stopped walking entirely.

The cavern walls were growing it. Pale, finger-thin fronds of it, rising from every crack and ledge, glowing with a soft blue-white light like frost given breath. It carpeted the floor in places, yielding slightly underfoot, and where the ground sloped down toward the cavern's center it grew taller—waist-high, then higher, pale spires that swayed very slightly in a current of air Theo could not feel on their own face.

Nobody spoke. Even Wren, the youngest of Brannic's crew, who had not stopped talking since the avalanche two days ago except when she was eating or swearing, went quiet.

"It's beautiful," she said finally, hushed, the way people speak in temples.

"It's wrong," said Hesk, the scarred one who never smiled. He crouched and held his knife-blade near one of the fronds without touching it. "Nothing grows this far down. Nothing grows *this color*."

"The map says follow the river," Theo said.

"There is no river," Hesk said. "I hear no river."

But Theo heard it. Had been hearing it for an hour, maybe longer—a sound beneath the sound of their own footsteps, low and rhythmic, like breathing heard through a wall. Like the sea in a shell, if the sea were vast and slow and patient.

They followed the glow downward.

* * *

The river was there after all, though no water ran in it.

The passage opened into a gallery so large their lanterns could not find its ceiling or its far wall. The fungal light grew denser here, tall as trees, great branching antlers of pale growth rising from terraced stone, and between them lay what had once been a riverbed—wide, worn smooth, its stones rounded by centuries of current. But the bed was dry. Had been dry, Theo thought, for a very long time. The stone had the bleached look of bone.

And yet the sound continued. Louder here. Rhythmic.

In. Out. In. Out.

Wren put her ear against the riverbed stone and pulled back as if scalded. "It's coming from below," she whispered. "It's coming from *under the mountain.*"

Brannic stood very still, looking down the dry channel into the blue-lit distance. "My grandfather worked these hills," he said slowly. "Told me stories I never believed. Said there were rivers under Thornfell that didn't run with water. Said the old folk before the old folk built their doors facing down instead of out." He turned to Theo, and his eyes in the fungal light were strange, stripped of their usual merchant's gleam. "You knew about this."

It wasn't a question. It wasn't quite an accusation either. It was the voice of a man doing arithmetic and not liking any of the numbers.

"You keep a gate," Brannic said. "Twenty years you've sat on that gate, turning everyone away, knowing every road better than roads know themselves. Everyone in the valley thinks you're mad or paid or both. I've thought worse. I've thought you were hiding gold. I've thought you were hiding from a hanging." His jaw worked. "What are you hiding, Pemberton?"

Hesk and Wren had drifted closer. Even Sorrel, silent Sorrel who hadn't spoken since the canyon, had stopped pretending to watch the dark end of the gallery.

Theo looked down the dry riverbed, into the breathing dark, and understood that there was no path forward that didn't pass through this conversation. The map would take them deeper. Deeper meant warmer. And whatever warmed the mountain's bones was going to make itself known to all of them eventually, whether Theo confessed or not.

Better, then, to spend the truth while they still chose the moment.

"Build a fire," they said. "We're not walking further tonight. And we're not talking about this standing up."

* * *

The camp settled into a hollow above the riverbed, ringed by low stands of the pale fungus that gave more light than any fire needed. They built one anyway; Brannic insisted, saying a man should be able to see flame even when the world glowed wrong. Hesk cooked. Wren talked until Sorrel told her, in his first words in three days, to stop. The rhythm of the mountain breathed beneath them all, slow and enormous, and Theo found it easier now to name it than it had been at first. Easier, and worse.

When the others had eaten and wrapped themselves in blankets, Theo sat across the small fire from Brannic, and told him the truth—or the shape of it, which is sometimes all a truth will give.

"My family kept the gate for nine generations," Theo said. "Before my family, other families. Before the kingdom had this name, someone was keeping that gate. Do you want to know what's behind it?"

"A road," Brannic said. "A sealed road through to the high pass. Every smuggler from here to the coast has dreamed of it."

"A door," Theo corrected. "And yes, behind it is a road. But the door isn't there to keep travelers off the road." They fed a stick to the fire and watched it catch. "It's there because of what's underneath the road. Underneath everything. Underneath Cael."

The word hung in the fungal light. Mount Cael. The tomb beneath it. The map rolled tight in Theo's coat, warm as a living thing.

"Go on," Brannic said quietly.

"When I was a child, my father opened the inner chamber for me. Once. Only once. There's a room behind the door, and the room isn't empty. There are carvings on every wall, older than any script anyone living can read, and in the middle of the room—" Theo paused, because they had not spoken this aloud in thirty years, and the memory came up whole and cold as spring water. "In the middle of the room is a shaft. It goes down farther than torchlight. Farther than rope. And when you stand at the edge of it, you can feel air moving. Warm air. Coming *up*. In and out. In and out." They looked at Brannic. "Like this. Exactly like this. The whole mountain breathes, Brannic. It has been breathing since before there were kings. And my family's gate was never a toll station. It was a seal on the lid of a thing that sleeps under Cael, and the crown's own founders helped build the lid, and then—" Theo laughed, and hated the sound of it. "And then the crown forgot. Or chose to forget. Or decided a story about smugglers was cheaper than a story about what's down there."

Silence. The fire cracked. Somewhere in the blue dark, a frond of fungus swayed against air none of them could feel.

"And the courier," Brannic said at last. "The dying man on the pilgrim road. The map you won't let me look at twice." His voice was careful, flat, the voice of a man crossing ice and testing each step. "That's what he was carrying you toward. Not treasure."

"He made me swear an oath over his blood," Theo said. "To carry it to the tomb under Cael. He died before he could tell me why. Everything I've learned since, I've learned from a journal I haven't finished reading and from things that have been trying to kill us since the day I buried him." They met Brannic's eyes. "So no. I don't know what's waiting down there. I know it's old, I know it's bound, and I know the binding is failing, because the king on the throne hasn't been a man for years—he's a glove, and something wears him. And I think—" They stopped. Started again, quieter. "I think the thing under Cael and the thing in the crown are the same thing. Or halves of the same thing. Or the crown was forged to chain it and the chain has been rusting for a thousand years. I don't know. I'm a gatekeeper, Brannic. I know weather and roads and how long a lie needs to be before people believe it. I don't know this."

Brannic stared into the fire for a long time.

Theo let him stare. This was the moment, they knew, where a sensible man counted his men, weighed his coin, and walked away into the dark he understood rather than the dark he'd just been told about. Theo had spent twenty years teaching themselves to expect abandonment—it was, after all, the trade they practiced. You were useful to everyone and loyal to no one, and no one stayed, and the staying-ness of no one kept you alive.

Wren had stopped pretending to sleep. So, probably, had Hesk. Let them hear it. If they ran, they'd run toward Vey's gray riders anyway, and the truth might buy them faster feet.

"You're telling me," Brannic said finally, "that the gate I've been skirting my whole life—the gate every man I've ever fenced with has cursed—you're telling me it's holding shut a lid on a god."

"Not a god."

"What would you call it?"

Theo considered. "A hunger," they said. "With a shape. Maybe a shape left over from when it was something else. My father called it the Sleeper, but that was just a word to make children behave. The carvings call it nothing. They only show it being put down. Over and over. Different hands, different ages, putting it down again and again, and the hands get fewer each time." Theo looked down at their own palms, scarred from rope and frost and twenty years of latch-work. "The hands are nearly gone, Brannic. I'm the last keeper there is. And Rafael—" Their throat tightened unexpectedly. "Rafael was the last of whatever his line was. And he's dead on a slab of stone under a cairn I piled myself, and I'm sitting here with his oath in my coat, and I don't know what I'm carrying except that everyone who ever carried anything like it ended up burned or hunted or dead."

The fire settled. Above them, unseen, the pale forest of fungus stirred.

"So walk," Theo said. "Take your crew and go back the way we came. The map burns fine, I've checked the paper against flame in my head a hundred times. Vey wants me, not you. A dead courier, a heretic's bloodline, a stubborn gatekeeper—none of that needs to touch a smuggler who was just passing through."

Brannic laughed. It was a short, ugly, honest laugh, the first true one Theo had heard from him since the canyon.

"Passing through," he repeated. "Pemberton, I've been passing through things my whole life. Passed through a marriage, passed through two sieges, passed through the good graces of every customs officer on the coast. You know what I've learned?" He leaned forward, and the firelight and the fungus-light fought across his seamed face. "There's no through. There's only what you're standing in when you stop lying to yourself about which way you're walking." He jerked his chin toward the dry riverbed, the breathing dark beyond it. "I heard that thing breathing before you said a word. We all did. Wren heard it first, if she'll admit it. You think I can walk out of this mountain now and go back to running brandy past bored soldiers? Knowing what I know? Knowing the king is a glove and the ground under my boots has lungs?"

He spat into the fire, decisively, the way he did when a bargain closed.

"No. You don't get rid of me that easy. You want to confess sins, confess them to somebody charging by the hour." He pointed a thick finger at Theo. "But understand the terms, gatekeeper. Whatever's down there—if there's gold, I get my cut. If there's a weapon, my crew eats well the rest of their lives. And if there's a *hunger with a shape*—" his finger swung to point down, through stone, through the breathing dark, "—then you tell me exactly how we put it back to sleep, or kill it, or whatever the rite requires, and you tell me before we're standing in front of it. No more truths spent like copper at the last moment. Full price, upfront. That's my rate."

"That's fair," Theo said, and found, to their surprise, that they meant it—that some knot in their chest, wound tight since the treeline, since Rafael's blood cooling on their hands, had loosened half a turn. Confession, it turned out, was a currency after all, and it bought exactly what Theo had been refusing to purchase for twenty years: witnesses.

Behind Brannic, Hesk opened one eye and closed it again. Wren exhaled, long and shaky, and whispered something to Sorrel that made him snort.

Sleep came hard for Theo after that, and strangely deep, in the blue glow with the mountain breathing beneath them. They dreamed of a door standing open, which had never once happened in twenty years of dreams, and woke with their hand pressed flat against the stone floor, feeling the rhythm come up through their palm.

In. Out. In. Out.

Faster than yesterday. They were almost sure it was faster than yesterday.

* * *

They broke camp at what Theo guessed was dawn, though dawn meant nothing down here, and followed the dry riverbed into the deepening blue. The fungus grew taller, stranger; in places it had fused into arches overhead like the ribs of some drowned cathedral, and the light pooled and shifted so that shadows moved independently of anything casting them. Twice Theo caught Wren staring at her own shadow, counting its edges.

Near midday—or the mountain's version of it—they found the first carving.

It was cut into the riverbed's stone bank at knee height, so worn that Theo knelt and traced it with fingers before understanding it: a procession of figures, walking downward. Human figures, robed, bearing something between them on poles—a shape Theo's eyes kept sliding off, the way eyes slide off a star when you look straight at it. Behind the procession, smaller figures carried jars. Behind those, figures with raised tools, breaking something.

Breaking a door.

"They came this way," Theo murmured. "Down the river. When the river ran." They looked up the channel, back the way they'd come, then down, toward the breathing dark. "This wasn't wilderness to them. This was a road. A pilgrimage."

"Going where?" Wren asked softly.

Theo stood, dusted the stone grit from their knees, and unrolled the map across a flat boulder, weighting the corners with rocks. By lantern and fungus-light together, the inked passages showed clear: the route they'd traveled matched the ancient riverbed line for line, and ahead the inked way descended in long patient switchbacks toward a symbol at the page's edge, drawn in faded red-brown ink that Theo had told themselves, day after day, was merely old pigment.

A circle. A seal. And beneath it, in cramped antique lettering:

*Where the sleeper's heart keeps time.*

The mountain breathed beneath their knees. In. Out. Faster.

"We're close," Theo said, and heard their own voice come out steady, which surprised them, because inside they were already running. "Another day. Maybe less. And Brannic—about the full price, upfront?"

Brannic looked up from checking the heads on his axes.

"When we meet the hermit Ondine sent word of—the priestess at Cael's foot—I'll tell you everything. The binding, the rite, all of it. Every copper." Theo rolled the map closed and gripped it tight, and only then noticed that their hand, pressed all night against the breathing stone, had gone pink and warm as sunburn, and would not cool. "Because I think we're not the only ones following this road. And I think the thing in the crown already knows exactly where it leads."

Far down the gallery, in the direction they were walking, the pale forest of fungus bowed—all of it at once, thousands of fronds bending like grass under wind—toward something ahead of them that had just, unmistakably, begun to move.
