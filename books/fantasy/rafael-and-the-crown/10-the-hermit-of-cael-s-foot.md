## 10. The Hermit of Cael's Foot

Sister Ondine had been dead for eleven years, and she had never once regretted it.

The crown's registers said so. *Died of fever, winter of the Gray Frost, interred in the cloister yard at Saint Verre.* She had written the entry herself, in a fair imitation of the abbess's hand, and then she had walked out of the convent before dawn with nothing but her mother's ring, a knife, and the clothes she stood in. The woman who had served the crown's rites for nineteen years—the woman who had anointed two princes and sung at the old king's deathbed—had been buried under another name in a grave that held only stones.

The woman who climbed down from Cael's foot each morning to draw water was nobody at all. That was the whole point of her.

She lived in a hollow beneath a shelf of granite where the mountain's shoulder met the treeline, a cave she had lined with moss and hung with drying herbs until it smelled less like stone and more like a kitchen. She kept goats. She kept bees. She kept, in an iron box buried beneath the hearthstone, the one thing in the world that could still frighten her: a copy of the Rite of Unbinding, transcribed in her own hand on the last night of her old life, when she had understood what the prayers she'd been singing all those years were actually *for*.

She was drawing water at the spring when the strangers came down out of the high passes.

Ondine heard them before she saw them—boots on scree, too many boots, moving without any attempt at quiet, which meant they were either soldiers or fools or smugglers, and the smugglers at least knew better than to come this way. She set down her yoke, unslung the knife from her belt, and stepped behind the split boulder that screened her spring, because being dead did not mean being careless.

Six of them came around the bend of the trail, and Ondine's breath went out of her slowly, like air from a punctured lung.

Five were rough-dressed, road-worn, armed like men who expected to be robbed by their own companions. Smugglers, or something near enough. But the sixth—

The sixth was tall and gray-eyed and moved like someone carrying a weight strapped inside their chest, and across their shoulders they wore a courier's oilskin satchel, and hanging from the satchel's strap, swinging gently with each step, was a wax-sealed map case stamped with a sigil Ondine had seen exactly twice in her life: once on a document she should never have read, and once, burning, on the night everything ended.

The blood-oil seal of the House of Vaelin.

*Oh,* she thought, with a strange, floating calm. *So it has begun after all.*

---

Theo had not wanted to come.

Three days underground, following the map through passages that breathed warm air and dripped water that tasted faintly of iron, had stripped away whatever remained of Theo's talent for disbelief. The mountain hummed. Theo had felt it through their boot soles, a vibration too slow to be water and too deep to be anything living, and when they had finally confessed as much to Brannic—confessed the gate, the purpose of Thornfell, the thing the mountain was built to sit upon—Brannic had gone pale in the fungus-light and said only, "Keep walking," which was more than Theo had expected.

But the map's final route surfaced here, at Cael's foot, and the map's final route ended at a door, and the only person within a hundred miles whose name appeared anywhere in Rafael's journal was a hermit marked on no chart, described in a marginal hand so cramped it might have been apology: *If all else fails, ask for the beekeeper. She will be angry. Let her be angry.*

They found the spring first, and then the boulder, and then the knife-point resting against the underside of Theo's jaw with the unhurried precision of someone who had done this many times and enjoyed none of it.

"Say nothing," said a woman's voice, low and dry as paper. "You have a seal on your satchel. Where did you get it."

"A dying man gave it to me," Theo said, very carefully still. "His name was Rafael."

The knife did not move. But behind Theo, Brannic's crew had gone for their weapons, and Brannic himself said, in the voice he used for talking men off ledges, "Easy. All of us. Nobody's dying today."

"He died three days ago," Theo went on, ignoring him, eyes fixed on nothing. "Riders in gray livery ran him down on the pilgrim road above Thornfell. He made me swear an oath on his blood to carry his map to the tomb beneath Mount Cael. I don't know what's in the tomb. I know the king is not the king. I know the mountain is not empty. And I know—" their voice caught, and they hated it, and pushed through anyway, "—I know that whatever he was, he trusted a stranger with the only thing he had left. So either you cut my throat, or you tell me what I've sworn to."

The silence stretched long enough that Theo could hear the goats shifting somewhere upslope, and the bees working the late flowers, and their own heart, loud and stupid.

Then the knife withdrew.

"You'd better come inside," said Sister Ondine, stepping out from behind the boulder—a small woman, sixty-odd, iron-gray hair cropped short, eyes the color of slate weathered smooth. "All of you. And touch nothing."

---

Her cave was larger than it looked from outside, and cleaner than any soldier's billet. Bundles of herbs hung from the ceiling in ordered rows. A fire burned low in a hearth of fitted stone. On a shelf above it sat a single object that made Brannic's youngest crewman, Pell, go still: a small bronze bell, green with age, its handle worked into the shape of a crowned figure whose face had been deliberately, carefully effaced.

Ondine saw him looking. "Yes," she said. "That's real. Sit down."

They sat. She poured watered wine into clay cups with the economy of a woman who had fed travelers before and resented every one of them, and then she looked at Theo for a long moment across the fire.

"Show me the journal," she said.

Theo passed it over. Ondine read the way she did everything—without wasted motion—and when she reached a page near the middle, her hands stopped moving entirely.

"'My grandmother told me the priestess still lives,'" she read aloud, flatly. "'The one who ran. If the map fails, find her. She knows the words.'" Ondine closed the book. For a moment her face did something complicated and old. "Ilse Vaelin wrote that. I taught her letters when she was nine years old. They killed her in the second purge, along with her husband and both daughters." She set the journal down with exaggerated care, the way one sets down something that might otherwise be thrown into the fire. "Twenty years I have waited for someone to walk up this mountain holding that seal. Twenty years, and I confess I hoped it would never happen. Now. Ask me your questions. You will not like the answers."

"The king," Theo said. "What is he?"

Ondine took a sip of wine, and when she spoke, her voice changed—took on the cadence of liturgy, the rhythm of a woman who had once recited these truths in incense-dark chambers to robed listeners, and hated herself a little for how naturally it returned.

"Before there was a kingdom, there was the mountain, and in the mountain there was a hunger. Not a beast. Not a god, though it was worshipped as one. The oldest texts call it the Wardenless Fire—a spirit that devours. Not flesh. *Selves.* It eats what a person is and wears the shape of them afterward, the way a beetle wears the shell of the prey it has emptied. The first peoples of this land fed it their kings deliberately, the way you'd feed a dog to keep it from the sheep. One vessel, chosen and willing, consumed generation by generation, and the rest of the world left alone."

"And then?" Brannic asked. He had not touched his wine.

"And then a warlord came out of the west and conquered everyone, as warlords do, and he was not content to feed the mountain one king at a time. He wanted the hunger *bound*. Chained. Made into a tool." Her mouth twisted. "So his smiths forged a crown—not ornament, understand, but a lock. Iron quenched in the blood of the last willing vessel, inscribed with bindings in a language older than the conquest. The crown would hold the spirit fast. It would need a vessel to anchor it. And the vessel would be whoever wore the crown." She looked up. "Every king since has been the lock's keyhole. The spirit sits in the crown; the crown sits on the king; and year by year, feast by feast, the spirit drinks the king. First his dreams. Then his judgments. Then his loves. Then his name. What sits on the throne now is a shell with a hunger inside it, wearing Aldric's face the way you'd wear a coat."

"How long does a king last?" Pell asked quietly.

"The strong ones, thirty years. Aldric was crowned young. He has perhaps a winter left—perhaps less. And when a vessel empties completely, the spirit must renew itself, publicly, at the Binding Feast, drinking deep of the court, the city, the *country*, to tide it over until the next crown finds the next head." Ondine set down her cup. "There is a reason the purges have grown worse. There is a reason villages burn. It is getting hungry, and it is getting impatient, and it does not care anymore what the shell pretends."

Silence. Somewhere outside, a goat complained.

"So we kill it," Brannic said. "We go to the tomb, we take the crown off the corpse, we break it. Simple."

"No," Ondine said. "It is not simple, and if you attempt it simply, you will not die—you will do something considerably worse than die. Listen to me, all of you, because I will say this once." She leaned forward, firelight carving her face into planes and shadow. "The binding can be undone. That rite exists; I have carried it in my head for twenty years and under my floor for nineteen. But unbinding is not destruction. To unbind the spirit is to release it from the crown—and a released spirit does not evaporate, child. It must *go somewhere*. The rite requires a vessel. A living vessel, of the binder's own line, standing at the tomb, who takes the hunger into themselves and holds it while the crown is broken around them. The spirit dies in the vessel's blood, or the vessel dies in the spirit's hunger, and the old texts are notably silent on which happens more often."

"Rafael," Theo said. The word came out cracked. "That's why his family. That's why they were exterminated."

"They were the binder's line. The warlord's own blood, sworn across twenty generations to stand ready if the lock ever failed. The crown has known about them for centuries and tolerated them—until recently, until the hunger grew strong enough to be afraid. The purges of the last twenty years have not been politics. They have been a hunting." Ondine's slate eyes fixed on Theo, and Theo felt the ground tilt beneath them, some vast understanding approaching like weather. "And now you arrive wearing his seal, having sworn an oath on his blood, carrying his map. Blood-oil is not ink, keeper-of-gates. It is a covenant. When Rafael pressed that seal into your hands and you swore, the line did not end with him."

The fire popped. No one moved.

"It passed to you," Ondine said softly. "You are the last of the binder's line now, whether you wish it or no. The rite will accept you—or it will reject you, and then we will all learn something interesting at very close range."

Theo's mouth was dry. "When. You said the Binding Feast—"

"Nine days hence. The Feast of Chains, the court calls it, when the crown renews its grip and the king renews his face before the assembled realm." Ondine rose, went to the hearthstone, and pried it up with a practiced motion, lifting from the cavity an iron box older than any of them. "Which means you have nine days to cross forty leagues of hunted mountain, enter a capital crawling with inquisitors, reach a tomb beneath the palace that opens only to the binder's blood, and perform a rite that may kill you in front of the entire court." She opened the box. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth, lay a bundle of vellum covered edge to edge in dense, archaic script. "I am coming with you. Do not argue; I have been dead eleven years and I find I miss the work."

Brannic laughed—one sharp, disbelieving bark. "Forty leagues in nine days, past the king's own riders, into the palace itself. You're all mad. This isn't a quest, it's a funeral procession that hasn't noticed yet."

"Yes," Ondine agreed placidly, wrapping the vellum into her pack. "It usually is."

Theo stared into the fire and felt the shape of the thing they had inherited settle onto their shoulders like a yoke—Rafael's oath, Ilse Vaelin's letters, twenty generations of the dead queuing up behind them. *Someone had to pay the toll.* They had said it to themselves for years about the gate. They had not understood, until now, how large the toll could grow.

"Then we leave tonight," Theo said, "while the light's bad and the riders aren't watching."

They were still dividing the packs when Pell, who had taken the watch at the cave mouth, came scrambling back down the slope with his face drained white.

"Smoke," he said. "Down-valley. A column of riders on the south road, flying gray—and they're not passing through. They've stopped at the shrine below the ridge." He swallowed. "There's a woman leading them. Gray cloak, gray eyes. She's asking the shrine-keepers about a beekeeper."

* * *

Inquisitor Vey knelt briefly at the wayside shrine, not out of piety but out of habit, and studied the trampled mud of the path leading up toward Cael's foot. Six sets of prints. Fresh. One set lighter than the rest, favoring the left foot.

"Beekeeper," she said to the trembling shrine-keeper. "On the mountain. Show me where."

Behind her, her column waited in the failing light, and far to the north, in a palace she had served faithfully for twenty years, something hungry turned over in its sleep and dreamed of feasting.
