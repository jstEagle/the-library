## 11. Vey Closes In

The report arrived at dusk, folded inside a courier's dispatch case that had traveled faster than any living rider should have been able to manage—three relays, two changes of horse, and a final stretch carried by falcon to the garrison tower at Marrow's Rest, where Inquisitor Vey kept her field headquarters in a room that smelled of tallow and cold iron.

She read it twice before she allowed herself to feel anything at all.

*Watcher Corvan, last report, undelivered until now.* The hand was his—she knew it the way she knew her own, having read four years of his cramped, dutiful script—but the letters wavered near the end, as though written by someone whose fingers no longer fully belonged to him. *Subject Pemberton departed Thornfell with armed company, six or seven, smuggler-badged. Route taken: the old cliff path above the Serpent's Stair. One man lost to rockfall. They have turned onto roads not on any survey I know. The mountain is warm where it should not be. I will follow at distance. If this reaches you, I am—*

It ended there. No signature flourish, no closing formula. Just the sentence breaking off like a snapped thread.

Vey set the paper down on the scarred oak table and pressed both palms flat against its surface, feeling the grain bite into her skin, grounding herself the way she had learned to do twenty years ago, when she first understood that an inquisitor who felt too much would soon be an inquisitor no longer—or worse, an inquisitor who lied.

Corvan was dead. She had known it since the second day of silence, the way one knows a tooth has gone bad before the ache arrives. But knowing and reading were different things. Reading made it real, gave it weight and ink and a broken sentence that would never be finished.

"They turned off the survey roads," she said aloud, to the empty room, because saying things aloud helped her think, and because there was no one in the room worth lying to.

She crossed to the great map of the Thornfell massif that dominated the eastern wall—a thing of vellum and candle-smoke, annotated in three generations of hands, hers the most recent and the most heavily inked. Her finger traced the route Corvan described. The Serpent's Stair, yes. She had walked it herself, eleven years ago, hunting a counterfeiter who had thought the cliffs would hide him. The cliffs had not hidden him. But past the Stair, the map went quiet—the cartographers' convention of blankness that meant *here be nothing worth your time*, which in the mountains always meant *here be something we could not measure*.

And into that blankness, Theo Pemberton had led six armed men.

Vey stood before the map for a long time, the candle guttering beside her, and let the shape of it assemble itself in her mind the way she assembled all her cases: not from what people said, but from what they did, and from the spaces between the two.

Pemberton had spent fifteen years being nobody. That was the maddening thing about them—that was the thing that had kept Vey awake more nights than she cared to admit. Everyone in Thornfell knew the gatekeeper, and no one knew anything true about the gatekeeper. Useful to the smugglers, obedient to the crown's tolls, courteous to travelers, forgettable within an hour of meeting them. Vey had interviewed forty-one residents of the valley over nine days, and she had come away with forty-one portraits of a person who could not possibly all be the same person. A drunk. A teetotaler. Kind. Cruel. Chatty. Silent. Forty-one mirrors, each reflecting back the face of whoever stood before it.

That was not innocence. Innocence was clumsy. Innocence left edges showing. This was craft—long, patient, expensive craft—and people only paid that price to hide something that mattered.

And then Rafael Serrano had died in their keeping, with a royal seal on his effects and gray riders' arrows in his back, and the invisible man had done the one thing an invisible man must never do: he had run.

"You're taking me somewhere," Vey murmured to the map, to Pemberton, to the blank space past the Stair. "Or you're taking something. And either way, you finally made a mistake."

She heard footsteps on the tower stair—heavy, deliberate, the tread of a soldier who wanted to be heard coming—and did not turn. "Enter, Captain."

Captain Dree Halloway filled the doorway the way she filled every doorway, which was completely. Twenty-two years in the king's service, a face like a closed fist, and the particular loyalty of a woman who had decided long ago that Vey's certainty was the next best thing to a religion. "The column's fed and shod," she said. "Sixty lances ready to ride on your word. The quartermaster wants to know if we're provisioning for a week or a month."

"A month." Vey turned from the map. "No. Provision for six weeks and plan for eight. We're going into the high country, Captain, and the high country does not forgive poor arithmetic."

Halloway's eyes flicked to the dispatch case, to the report still lying open on the table. "Corvan?"

"Dead." Vey said it flatly, the way she reported all losses, but something behind the flatness shifted, and Halloway—who had served beside her long enough to hear the undertow—said nothing, only waited. "His last report puts our quarry on unsurveyed roads above the Serpent's Stair, moving east toward Cael's foot. He followed them alone instead of sending word ahead." She paused. "That was my instruction. Follow at distance. Report weekly. He obeyed it exactly, and it killed him."

"Or the mountain killed him," Halloway offered. "Cliffs do that."

"The mountain didn't put an arrow pattern in him that matches crown fletching, Captain. Someone found him first. Someone else is hunting our quarry." Vey gathered the report, folded it along its original creases, and slid it into the leather folio she carried everywhere—the folio Halloway called, behind her back, the coffin, because everything that went into it seemed to end up dead or ruined. "Which means we are racing, and I despise races."

"When do we ride?"

"First light. Earlier, if the fog lifts."

Halloway nodded and turned to go, then stopped, one hand on the doorframe. "Inquisitor. May I ask a question that isn't about provisions?"

"You may ask. I may not answer."

"Why this one?" Halloway's voice was carefully neutral, the neutrality of a soldier approaching ground she suspected was mined. "We've hunted traitors before. You've never moved a full column for one dead courier and a gatekeeper. You've never ridden yourself half-starved for a family that's been ash for twenty years." She met Vey's eyes. "What makes the Serranos worth sixty lances?"

Vey looked at her for a long moment. Outside the narrow window, the last light was dying off the western peaks, and somewhere below, the garrison was striking the evening bell, and the sound of it went through her like a needle finding an old wound.

"Sit down, Captain," she said.

Halloway sat.

* * *

Her sister's name had been Liss. Vey almost never said it aloud anymore—not because the grief had faded, but because the grief had calcified into something structural, load-bearing, and she had learned that naming load-bearing things carelessly could bring whole buildings down.

"Liss was four years younger than me," Vey said. "She wanted to be a herbwise. Not a physician—she wasn't patient enough for the physicians' college—but a herbwise, the kind who rode village to village with satchels of willowbark and feverfew and knew every midwife's name between here and the coast." She found she was turning her signet ring, around and around, and made herself stop. "She was good at it. People liked her. Children liked her, which I have never managed."

Halloway said nothing. She was good at that—good at the silence that lets a story come out whole instead of in pieces.

"The year Liss was nineteen, the sweating sickness came out of the river valleys. You'll remember it. They called it the Gray Summer." Vey watched Halloway's face change slightly; everyone old enough remembered the Gray Summer. "It started in the fishing towns. By midsummer it was in the market towns. By harvest it was at the capital's gates." She stood, walked to the window, because it was easier to say the rest looking at mountains than looking at another person. "Here is what the histories will tell you, if anyone ever writes honest histories: the sickness was containable. The herbwises' guild proved it—quarantine the river towns, burn the bedding, close the roads for one month, one month, and it dies out. They sent the proposal to the palace. Twice. The second time they sent it with the seals of nine guildmasters on it."

"And?"

"And the king's council returned it unopened." Vey's reflection in the dark glass was very still. "Unopened, Captain. I've seen the return mark. I obtained a copy of the document myself, years later, through channels I won't describe. The council's clerk wrote on the outer fold: *His Majesty declines to burden the realm's commerce with precaution.* Declines to burden the realm's commerce. Thirty thousand people died of a burden on commerce."

The bell below finished striking. Neither woman moved.

"Liss was in the river towns when the roads closed—closed too late, after the sickness had already walked out of them wearing travelers' boots. She wrote to me. Eleven letters. I have all eleven. In the fourth one she says she's tired but well. In the seventh she says the feverfew has run out and they're boiling pine needles. In the ninth—" Vey's voice did something she hated, a small fracture, and she mastered it the way she mastered everything, with pressure and practice. "In the ninth letter, the handwriting is someone else's. A stranger writing to say that the herbwise Liss Vey had died on a Tuesday, that she had spent her last strength nursing other people's children, and that her effects were being burned per quarantine law, and would the family like the ashes sent."

"Did you get the ashes?"

"No. The quarantine office lost them. Or sold them. Or never existed to begin with." Vey turned from the window. "I joined the Inquisition eight months later. My father thought it was grief making a fool of me. Perhaps it was. But grief pointed me at a question, Captain, and I have spent twenty years pulling on that thread, and everything I have found says the same thing."

"Which is?"

"That the king let them die." She said it simply, without theater, because the plainness was the horror. "Not passively. Not by negligence alone. I have spent twenty years in the archives, in the confessional booths of guilty chancellors, in the private papers of dead ministers, and the pattern is unmistakable. Every crisis that might have exposed the crown's weakness—plague, famine, the border revolts—was permitted. Managed. Allowed to burn until it burned out on its own, because suppression required spending power, and spending power required explanations, and explanations were dangerous." She let that sit. "You have served the crown your whole life, Captain. Tell me it doesn't chill you. Tell me you haven't noticed that the king's mercy always arrives exactly late enough to cost him nothing."

Halloway was silent for a while. "I've noticed," she said at last, slowly, "that the king hasn't aged. Not properly. Fifteen years I've stood guard at court functions, and he's grayer, but he doesn't stoop, doesn't slow. Men age unevenly, I told myself. But—" She shook her head, as if dislodging the thought. "That's not why we're riding."

"No. That's not why we're riding." Vey returned to the table and laid her palm flat on the map, over the blank space beyond the Serpent's Stair. "This is why we're riding. The Serrano family, Captain. Do you know what they were?"

"Traitors. Old ones. The line was attainted a generation ago."

"They were binders." Vey opened the coffin-folio and drew out a page she had copied so many times she knew its flaws by heart—a fragment of pre-attainment liturgy, rescued from a burning chapel when she was young and still believed burning things solved problems. "Before the dynasty, before Aldric's grandfather's grandfather, there were rites in this country that no priest will speak of now. Rites of binding. The Serranos were the hereditary keepers of one of them. When the family was attainted, the official charge was treason. The unofficial truth—which I have pieced together across two decades and three exhumations—is that they refused to surrender something. An office. A duty. Something the crown wanted under its own control, and when it couldn't have it, it exterminated them and called the extermination justice."

"You believe the courier was the last of them."

"I believe Rafael Serrano was the last of them, and I believe he was carrying something to the tomb beneath Mount Cael, and I believe whatever it was explains why the crown's riders shot him rather than took him." She met Halloway's eyes. "A dead man can't be interrogated, Captain. Whoever killed him wasn't afraid of what he'd say. They were afraid of what he'd *do*. There is a difference, and the difference is the size of a kingdom."

Halloway frowned. "Then the crown itself—"

"Is afraid of its own shadow. Yes." Vey closed the folio. "I have served the Inquisition for twenty years, and in twenty years I have learned that the surest way to find the truth is to follow what power fears. Power fears the Serrano inheritance. Therefore the Serrano inheritance matters. Therefore Theo Pemberton—whoever and whatever they truly are—is carrying it toward Mount Cael, and I intend to be standing at the foot of that mountain when they arrive."

"And when you catch them?" Halloway asked quietly. "What then? The writ says execution. All of them. The gatekeeper included."

Vey was silent for a moment. Through the window, the first stars were pricking out over the peaks, cold and indifferent, and she thought of Liss's ninth letter, of the stranger's careful handwriting, of ashes that had vanished into some clerk's ledger.

"The writ says execution," she agreed. "And I have executed the writ before, faithfully, and slept afterward. But I am not seventeen anymore, Captain, and I have learned that the crown's justice and actual justice are sometimes different animals wearing the same skin. If Pemberton is a traitor, they die, and I will not lose sleep. But if they're carrying the answer—if they're carrying the reason my sister boiled pine needles while a council clerk worried about commerce—then I want the answer more than I want the death." She straightened. "Get your column ready, Captain. We ride at first light, hard, through the Stair and past it, into the blank part of the map. And Captain—if anyone asks what we're chasing, tell them rebels. It's simpler, and mostly it isn't even a lie."

Halloway rose, saluted, and went. Her boots receded down the stair, measured and steady, and Vey stood alone again with the map and the candle and the folded report of a dead man's unfinished sentence.

* * *

She did not sleep. Sleep was a country she visited rarely and trusted less, and tonight she spent the hours instead at her writing desk, composing orders, reviewing supply manifests, and—when those were done—reading, for perhaps the hundredth time, the interrogation transcripts from the Serrano attainments.

They made grim reading. She had read worse—her trade was worse—but these had a quality she could never quite inoculate herself against: the quality of ordinary people refusing, with their last breaths, to say the thing their torturers most wanted to hear. Not names. Not locations. Over and over, in different mouths, the same refusal. *Ask me anything else.* One grandmother, ninety if she was a day, had answered eleven hours of questioning with a single repeated sentence, recorded faithfully by a scribe whose hand grew shakier with each entry: *The binding holds while the blood remembers.*

The binding holds while the blood remembers.

Vey had spent years trying to parse it. Liturgical scholars had shrugged. Archivists had suggested it was a corruption of some older phrase, meaningless outside its original context. But Vey had interrogated enough fanatics and martyrs to know that people did not die slowly and terribly for corrupted phrases. The words meant something. The blood meant something. And Rafael Serrano had died with a royal seal in his pack, running toward a mountain, pursued by men who wanted him dead rather than talking.

*The binding holds while the blood remembers.*

And now the blood was down to one man, and the man was dead, and the only person who might know what he'd been carrying was a gatekeeper who had spent fifteen years learning how to be nobody.

Unless—

Vey set down the transcript. A thought had been circling her all evening, patient as a hawk, and now it stooped.

Pemberton had run. That was the anomaly she kept returning to. Fifteen years of perfect invisibility, and the moment the courier died, the gatekeeper broke pattern and fled east with armed men. Flight was fear. But fear of what? Vey's riders had not yet reached Thornfell in force; the village interview had been conducted by a single under-inquisitor with four troopers. Pemberton could have stayed. Could have hidden, as they had hidden for fifteen years, and been untouchable.

Unless they weren't fleeing Vey at all.

Unless they were fleeing the same thing that had killed Corvan. Unless the gatekeeper had looked at the arrows in Rafael Serrano's back, recognized the fletching, and understood—as Vey herself was only now beginning to understand—that the danger was not the crown's justice but the crown's *fear*, and that a crown which kills couriers rather than questioning them will kill gatekeepers rather than questioning them too.

"They know something," she whispered to the candle. "They've known it for years. That's what the invisibility was. Not hiding from us. Hiding from *it*."

The candle flame leaned in a draft that hadn't existed a moment before, righted itself, and Vey sat very still, listening to her own heartbeat, aware that she had just crossed a line she could not uncross. Inquisitors did not use the word *it* about the crown. Inquisitors did not lie awake wondering whether the king was a king. And yet the transcripts lay before her, and the dead courier's seal lay in her strongbox, and Corvan's unfinished sentence lay in her folio, and the sum of all of it whispered a single wordless truth she had been refusing to hear for twenty years:

Something was wrong at the center of the realm, and it had been wrong for a very long time, and everyone who had ever gotten close enough to see it clearly was dead.

Except, perhaps, Theo Pemberton.

Vey snuffed the candle. In the darkness, the map on the wall became a pale ghost-shape, the surveyed roads fading first, the blank space beyond the Serpent's Stair seeming almost to glow.

At first light, sixty lances would ride into that blankness after a gatekeeper who had spent a lifetime becoming nobody, carrying something worth killing for, toward a tomb that the crown feared and the archives denied and the blood remembered.

And Vey intended, whatever it cost, to be the one who finally asked the right questions.

She was already saddling her horse when the watch-sergeant came running across the frozen yard with news from the relay post: a smuggler's bird, intercepted, bearing a message in cipher that the cryptographer swore referred to a canyon fork on the eastern road—and to payment owed for a position sold.

Someone in Pemberton's company, it seemed, had begun negotiating with the wrong buyer.

Vey smiled, and it was not a kind expression, and she gave the order to mount.
