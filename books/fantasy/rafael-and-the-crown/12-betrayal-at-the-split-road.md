## 12. Betrayal at the Split Road

The canyon fork came at dawn, the way bad news always does—suddenly, and with a light that made everything look worse than it was.

Theo had known this place since childhood, back when they'd still been allowed to be a child. The Wren's Throat, the old shepherds called it: two gray walls of stone pinching together where the smuggler's road split, one branch climbing toward the high passes and the honest cold, the other dropping into the lowlands and the king's law. Meltwater ran somewhere beneath the scree, and you could hear it if you held your breath—a sound like whispering, like a hundred small voices agreeing about something.

Theo did not hold their breath anymore. They'd stopped trusting silences years ago.

"Left or right, keeper?" Brannic stood at the fork with his thumbs hooked in his belt, studying both roads with the flat professional interest of a man pricing meat. Three days underground had not softened him. His knuckles were scabbed from the crawl through the flooded gallery, his beard crusted with pale fungus dust that glimmered faintly when he turned his head, and he hadn't once stopped looking at Theo the way a man looks at a strongbox he's decided to open.

"Right," Theo said. "Down."

Brannic's eyebrows went up. "Down is toward Vey. Down is toward every garrison between here and the capital. I've been following you on the promise that you knew something I didn't, keeper, but even my faith has limits."

"That's exactly why we go down." Theo crouched and drew in the gravel with one finger: the fork, the two branches, then a third line—a thread so fine the map had barely acknowledged it, a pilgrim stair cut into the canyon wall itself, hidden behind a curtain of frozen waterfall that thawed only in high summer. "Vey will expect us to run for the passes. She'll have her riders strung across the high road like beads on a wire. But if we drop into the lowlands for two days and cut east along the river—" they scratched the line eastward, "—we come out behind her column. Behind her *supply*. Nobody guards their own tail."

"And after that?"

"After that we stop running." Theo stood and wiped their hand on their coat. They did not say *toward the tomb*. They had learned by now that Brannic fought better when the prize stayed vague.

Behind them, the company was breaking its fast in the gray half-light: Ferro chewing hardtack with the grim patience of a man who hated every meal he'd ever eaten; the twins, Sela and Maren, arguing over a dice game played on a flat stone; young Petch dozing against his pack with his crossbow cradled like a child. And Doss.

Theo's eyes found Doss without meaning to. They always did lately. He was the newest of Brannic's crew, a lean, soft-spoken man who'd joined them at the last waystation before the mountain, and there was nothing wrong with him—nothing you could point to. He laughed at the right times. He pulled his weight on the rope. He asked careful questions about the map, about the route, about how much farther, and each question landed as lightly as a moth on skin.

Three days ago, Theo had caught him standing at the mouth of the fungus caverns, staring down into the warm dark with an expression no curious man should wear. Not wonder. *Measurement.*

Now he sat apart from the others, mending a strap, and he wasn't looking at the fork or the map or the mountains. He was looking south. Down-canyon. The way a man looks when he's waiting for something he's already paid for.

*You're imagining it,* Theo told themselves. *Paranoia is just fear wearing armor.* But Rafael had bled out in their arms with gray riders' arrows in his back, and paranoia, Theo had come to believe, was simply grief that had learned to count.

"We move in ten minutes," Brannic announced to the company. "Keeper says down. Anyone objects, object now, because after this morning I'm done listening to objections."

Ferro grunted. The twins gathered their dice. Petch woke swearing softly. And Doss set down his strap-mending, very carefully, the way a man sets down a tool when his hands are no longer steady enough to hold it.

Theo saw that. And said nothing. And would hate themselves for the silence for a long time afterward.

* * *

They were a hundred yards into the descending branch when the first bolt came.

It took Petch through the throat. No warning cry, no challenge from the rocks—just the flat *thwack* of a crossbow loosed at long range and the boy folding sideways off the path, his crossbow clattering away down the scree, his boots scraping stone as he slid. One moment he was alive and complaining about his blisters. The next he was a red smear on the rocks below, and the canyon was full of the sound of men shouting themselves awake.

"Ambush!" Brannic roared, and went flat behind a boulder with a speed that belied his size. "Ambush! Off the path! OFF THE PATH!"

Theo threw themselves into the shadow of the canyon wall as a second bolt sparked off stone where their head had been. From above—from *above*, from the rim of the western wall, three hundred feet up—gray figures moved among the rocks. Inquisitors' men. Not the road-worn riders they'd outrun twice already; these had climbed in the dark and waited in the cold for hours, which meant someone had told them exactly where to wait.

*Doss.*

The name arrived in Theo's mind with a physical sensation, like a key turning in a lock. They looked back up the trail. Doss was thirty yards behind them, crouched, unarmed—and untouched. Every bolt had gone wide of him. Every single one.

"Doss!" Theo shouted it across the chaos, and the man flinched. That was all. A flinch, a widening of the eyes, and then he turned and ran—not toward cover but back up the trail, toward the fork, toward the open ground where a rider in gray sat waiting on a horse that had been kept quiet all night in some fold of the hills.

Sela put a throwing axe past his ear and missed by inches. He didn't look back.

"Let him go," Theo heard themselves say, and their voice came out strange and cold, a stranger's voice. "He's already spent."

Then the world narrowed to survival. Bolts fell like iron rain. Ferro dragged Maren behind a fallen slab; Brannic bellowed orders that dissolved into the echo of the canyon; and Theo pressed their cheek against freezing stone and thought, with terrible clarity: *she knew. She knew the fork. She knew the day. She knew us.*

Which meant Vey had not been chasing them at all. She had been herding them, patient as a shepherd, letting the map lead them wherever it led while her traitor counted their fires and marked their camps. And now the only thing between her and the end of twenty years of hunting was a scrap of waxed leather riding against Theo's ribs.

The thought arrived whole, fully formed, ice-cold: *the map must never be taken from my body. Because my body can be taken.*

A scream cut through the arithmetic. Theo twisted around in time to see Brannic lunge—not away from danger but *into* it, out from behind his boulder, arms wide, moving with a speed no man his age had any right to. Moving toward *Theo*. Toward the space where Theo stood exposed against the canyon wall, framed against the lighter stone like a mark on a practice target, while on the rim above a crossbowman calmly reloaded and leveled.

Theo never saw the bolt leave the string. They saw Brannic hit it—or rather, hit the space where it traveled. It struck him high in the chest, left of center, punching through the shoulder where it met bone, and spun him around, and he went down onto the trail with a sound Theo felt in their own chest, a wet exhalation, a door closing partway.

Everything after that happened in pieces.

Theo crawling to him through the hail of stone-splinters. The twins laying down covering fire with a ferocity that seemed to surprise even them. Ferro appearing at Theo's shoulder, hauling Brannic's arm across his neck, growling "I've got him, *move*." The five of them—their number reduced by two in the space of a morning—staggering off the path into a side ravine that Theo's memory supplied from twenty years of keeping secrets, a goat-track narrow enough that a mounted man couldn't follow and steep enough that the crossbowmen lost their angles within minutes.

The bolts stopped. Somewhere above, horns sounded—two notes, then three, coordinating a search. Vey's voice, distant and clear as a bell, calling orders Theo couldn't make out and didn't need to. She had the fork. She had the high ground. She had time.

She didn't have the map. Not yet.

* * *

They made camp at dusk in a hollow beneath an overhang, a fire the size of two cupped hands, because Brannic was shivering and the shivering had to stop.

The bolt had gone through the muscle above his collarbone and out the back, missing the lung by what Ondine's teachings—half-remembered, secondhand, from a life Theo had buried—said was the width of two fingers. Ferro had pulled it through clean on the trail, wrapped the wound with boiled cloth, and now sat watching his captain breathe with an expression Theo had never seen on the hard-faced smuggler: naked, unguarded fear.

"He'll live," Maren said quietly. She'd taken over the tending, her hands surer than her voice. "If fever doesn't take him. If the wound doesn't rot. If we can keep him warm and still for three days." She looked up. "We don't have three days."

"No," Theo agreed. "We don't."

Brannic's eyes opened. They were too bright, fever kindling already behind them, but the voice that came out was still his—gravel and irony, worn thin. "Still here, keeper. You'll have to find another way to be rid of me." A ghost of his grin. "Took a bolt for you. That's going on your account. I keep accounts."

"You idiot," Theo said, and their voice cracked in the middle of it, and they let it. "You stupid, greedy, brave idiot. Why?"

Brannic's grin faded into something older and stranger. He was quiet long enough that Theo thought he'd drifted off. Then: "Twenty years I've run these roads. Never saved anything. Never *kept* anything. You spend your whole life being useful to everyone and loyal to no one—" his eyes fixed on Theo's face, and Theo understood that he was quoting them, quoting the philosophy they'd both lived by, "—and one morning you see some fool standing in the open with death falling out of the sky, and your legs decide for you." He coughed, winced. "Hate that. Legs deciding things."

Theo sat with that. Around them the hollow breathed its strange warmth, the deep heat of the mountain rising through the stone floor, and far off, water whispered in the dark the way it had whispered at the Wren's Throat, like a hundred small voices agreeing about something.

"Sela," Theo said. "Maren. Ferro. Listen to me."

They gathered close. Even Petch's absence had a shape now, a cold spot in the circle where a boy with blistered heels used to sit.

"What happened today happens again tomorrow, and the day after, until Vey gets what she wants. Doss sold us once. There will be another Doss, or another ambush, or another night when one of us is taken alive and talks. As long as the map exists—as long as it's a *thing*, a piece of leather that can be cut out of a dead man's coat—we are all of us just a delay." Theo reached inside their coat and drew it out. The wax seal, broken now, glinted dully in the firelight. Rafael's blood had soaked into one corner weeks ago and turned it black. "So it stops existing."

Sela frowned. "Burn it? Then we've walked all this way for nothing—"

"Not burn it. Burn it *last*." Theo unrolled the leather across their knees. "Tonight, every one of us learns it. Every line, every mark, every distance and depth. The twins have the best memories—I've watched you count cards. Ferro, you've hauled cargo through half these ranges; the routes will stick to you like pitch. By dawn, the map lives in six heads instead of one hide. And then—" they held one corner to the fire, watched the wax begin to soften, and did not yet let it catch, "—then it burns, and Vey can torture every one of us to ash and get nothing but six different stories that don't agree."

Silence. The fire popped. Brannic, propped against the stone, began—very quietly—to laugh, which turned into a coughing fit, which Maren smothered with a glare.

"You're going to make *smugglers* memorize a treasure map," he rasped, "and then destroy it. On purpose. Keeper, that is the single most insane thing I've ever heard, and I once watched a man try to pay me in eels." His too-bright eyes gleamed. "Do it."

They worked through the night. Theo traced each line with a fingertip and spoke it aloud: the third line at the Wren's Throat, the drowned gallery, the breathing caverns, Sister Ondine's hermitage, the descent beneath Cael, the door with the royal seal. The twins repeated it back in whispers, trading halves like verses of a song. Ferro closed his eyes and recited it with a freight-man's precision, correcting himself twice. Even Brannic, gray-faced and sweating, made Maren repeat the lowland route to him three times until he could murmur it along with her.

And Theo, teaching it, felt something shift in their chest that had nothing to do with wounds. For twenty years they had been the sole keeper of the Thornfell gate, the sole holder of the mountain's secrets, and the loneliness of that had calcified into identity: *no one else needs to know; no one else can be trusted; knowledge shared is knowledge lost.* Now they sat in a hole in the ground giving it all away to four criminals and a dying man, and the mountain did not fall, and the secret did not curdle, and the burden—gods, the burden—got *lighter*.

Rafael, Theo thought, you clever, desperate ghost. This is what you were carrying. Not a map. A *company*.

Dawn came gray through the ravine mouth. When the others slept—truly slept, for the first time in days—Theo fed the map to the fire corner by corner and watched Rafael's blood-black ink curl and vanish, watched the royal seal blister and go to smoke, watched the last route out of the mountain rise as sparks and scatter up through the crack in the stone toward a sky that was already brightening.

It smelled like burning hair. Theo sat with it until the smell faded.

"There," they said aloud, to no one, to a dead courier, to a boy named Petch, to whatever was listening in the warm dark under the world. "Now it's ours. All of ours. Come and take it."

Behind them, Brannic stirred against the stone, fever-bright and grinning faintly in his sleep. And far to the south, down the canyon and down the river and down every mile of gray road between here and the capital, Theo imagined—in the way you imagine a storm you can feel in your knees—Inquisitor Vey kneeling over Doss in the ruins of the Wren's Throat, asking her gentle questions, and learning, at last, that she had chased a piece of leather for twenty years while the thing she feared had learned to live in people.

The Binding Feast was eleven days away. Theo got up, shouldered their pack, and started walking east along the river, toward the king.
