## 13. The King Wakes

The king of Caelmor woke, as he did every morning, in a body that was not entirely his own.

He knew the moment of waking better than he knew his own name now—knew it as a drowning man knows the surface, a brief silver shimmer above him before the water closed. For a handful of heartbeats, sometimes as many as twenty, Aldric Vane was Aldric Vane again. He could feel the weight of his own arms, the ache in his lower back from thirty years of sitting a throne badly, the taste of last night's wine gone sour at the back of his teeth. He could remember. That was the mercy and the cruelty of it: remembering.

Then the crown would settle its cold fingers deeper into his skull, and the man would fold away like a letter into a drawer, and the *thing* would go about the business of ruling.

This morning, though, something was different. This morning the thing was afraid.

Aldric felt it before he understood it—a tremor running through the shared flesh, a hollowness opening behind his sternum like a door swinging on rusted hinges. The parasite did not fear. He had learned that across nineteen years of captivity inside his own skin. It hungered, it schemed, it wore his face to council and his voice to war, but fear was a thing with roots, and the thing in the crown had no roots in this world. It only had him.

And yet it feared now. He could taste it, metallic and thin, at the border where his mind met its will.

*The map,* he thought. *Something has happened to the map.*

He had never seen the map himself. He knew it the way a prisoner knows the key hanging on the jailer's belt—by rumor, by the shape of other men's terror. Rafael's family had carried it for generations, sworn keepers of a road they were forbidden to walk until the hour came. Aldric had met Rafael's grandfather once, before—before the coronation, before the binding took root and began to eat him from the inside out. The old man had gripped the young prince's hand hard enough to bruise and said, *When the time comes, boy, remember: the door opens both ways.*

Aldric had not understood then. He had been twenty-two and flattered that a servant of the old rites would trouble himself with royal ears.

Nineteen years later, caged behind his own eyes, he understood everything too late.

* * *

The council chamber smelled of tallow and lilies—the flowers the thing demanded, always lilies, funeral flowers, though no one at court dared say so aloud. Aldric sat beneath his own face and watched through his own eyes as the ministers filed in, bowing to the hollow man on the throne. Somewhere beneath the surface, he screamed. He had stopped screaming aloud years ago; there was no one left who could hear it, and the effort only fed the thing, somehow, the way fear fed it, the way despair fed it. It drank what he gave it. So he had learned to give it nothing. To lie flat and quiet in the flooded cellar of himself and wait.

Chancellor Merev was speaking. "—the courier's body has been recovered, Majesty. A gatekeeper in Thornfell, one Pemberton, reported finding him dead on the pilgrim road. No map was found on the corpse."

*Pemberton.* The name snagged somewhere in Aldric's fading memory, a fishhook in deep water. He held onto it. Holding onto things was the whole of his existence now—a name here, a face there, hoarded like a miser's coins against total darkness.

"The gatekeeper," the thing said, using his mouth, his voice, the low measured tone it had perfected over two decades of wearing him. "Describe this person."

"Reclusive. Keeps the old gate sealed. Has done for years. Useful to the smugglers, we're told, which is why no one has ever looked too closely."

"Look closely now." A pause, and Aldric felt the thing reach outward through him, feel the faint pressure of its attention turning toward the mountains like a searchlight. "Send word to Inquisitor Vey. She has hunted this line for twenty years. Let her finish it."

*Vey.* Another coin for the hoard. Aldric turned the names over in the dark. Pemberton. Vey. Rafael. Somewhere in the mountains, a dead courier's blood was cooling on an old road, and a stranger who kept gates shut was holding a map that could end all of this—or begin something far worse.

Because that was the truth the binders had never written down plainly, the truth Rafael's family had guarded in fragments and oaths: the unbinding was not a rescue. It was a choice, made at a door that did not care who walked through it or in which direction.

*The tomb lies,* his grandfather's friend had told him, or someone had told someone, the words worn smooth by generations of passing. *The door opens both ways.*

* * *

That night the thing was restless, and restlessness meant looseness, and looseness meant minutes—precious, stolen minutes when the grip slackened and Aldric rose through the black water toward air.

It happened in the privy chamber, of all places. The thing disdained such bodily necessities, retreating during them into its cold interior watchfulness, and so Aldric had learned to treasure the small indignities of kingship. He knelt at the basin, splashed water on his face, and saw in the mirror a fifty-one-year-old man with gray coming into his beard and shadows carved under his eyes—and behind those eyes, if you knew how to look, a flicker. A man looking out.

He tested the borders first, the way he always did. *I am Aldric Vane,* he thought, firmly, the way you press a tongue against a loose tooth. *Son of Maric. Father of none. Keeper of nothing but my own guilt.* The crown's cold shifted, probing, found nothing worth seizing, and settled.

His hands were shaking. Good. Shaking hands still obeyed.

On the writing desk lay the instruments of a hollow king's hollow day: seals, correspondence, a jeweled knife for cutting wax. His eye caught on the knife and slid past it—he had considered the blade a hundred times, a thousand times, and always arrived at the same arithmetic. Kill the vessel and the thing simply waited, patient as frost, for the next head to crown. Worse: it might already have begun seeding itself into the succession, into the rituals, into the very stones of the palace. Dying was easy. Dying usefully—that was the work of a lifetime, and his lifetime was nearly spent.

But there were other ways to spend a dying.

He pulled back the sleeve of his nightshirt and bared his left forearm to the candlelight. The skin there was pale as a grub's belly, untouched by sun for nineteen years. He picked up the jeweled knife.

The first cut made him gasp. Not from the pain—he had made his peace with pain, had made it his companion and his clock—but from the sound, the small wet whisper of steel parting a king's skin in a locked room where no one would ever be permitted to hear it. Blood welled dark and slow. He set the point against his own flesh and began to scratch.

Letters, not words at first. He had planned this for months, rehearsed the strokes in his mind during the long gray hours when the thing paraded him through audiences and feasts. Letters small enough to hide beneath a sleeve, shallow enough to pass as scarring to a physician's glance, deep enough to survive. He worked quickly, the candle guttering, sweat sliding down his ribs.

THE TOMB LIES.

Four strokes of agony per letter. He breathed through them the way he had once breathed through his father's funeral, through his coronation, through the first night he understood what sat upon his head. Then, because there was time—there was always either no time at all or a strange, windless abundance of it—he added the second line:

THE DOOR OPENS BOTH WAYS.

He sat back and looked at what he had written, this testament inked in a king's own blood on a king's own arm, addressed to no one, delivered to everyone, legible only to whoever finally cut the sleeve away. It was the most honest document of his reign. Nineteen years of decrees and treaties and proclamations issued in his name, and the only true sentence he had ever authored was written in his own skin with a fruit knife.

*The tomb lies.* He needed them to know that. Whoever came—Pemberton, Vey, some child not yet born carrying Rafael's blood—they would descend into Mount Cael believing they approached a weapon, a cure, a clean solution. They would believe the binders had built a vault to imprison a devil, and that the vault could be opened and the devil destroyed. But Aldric had spent nineteen years inside the binding. He knew how it felt from within. And it did not feel like a cage.

It felt like a throat.

The binding did not hold the spirit in the crown. The crown held the spirit in *him*. The tomb below Cael was not a prison—it was the mechanism's heart, the place where the rite could be reversed, completed, or *transferred*. The original binders had built themselves a door precisely because a door swings both directions: something could be put in, and something could be taken out, and nothing about the architecture cared which. If the parasite reached the tomb with a willing vessel, it would shed the crown like a snake shedding skin and walk out wearing new flesh, and no army in the world would dig it out again.

Unless someone stood in the doorway. Unless someone of the binder's line stood in the doorway and paid the toll.

*Rafael,* Aldric thought, and grief rose in him then, sudden and enormous, grief for a man he had met only once across a courtyard twenty years ago, a lean dark-eyed courier who had bowed exactly correctly and looked at his king with something that was not quite pity and not quite hope. *Your family died keeping a secret I should have shouted from every tower. Your blood bought me nineteen years of being eaten alive, and I never even learned your middle name.*

The candle had burned down to a stub. Somewhere beyond the door, a guard shifted his weight; the thing stirred in its cold interior, gathering itself, preparing to resume the shape of a king. Aldric pressed his sleeve down over the wound. The blood had already begun to stick the linen to the cuts, which was good—it would seal, it would scar, it would last. He had made it last. That was the one craft he had mastered in this long captivity: making things last. A warning. A memory. Himself.

*I am Aldric Vane,* he thought once more, pressing the words down into the flood like stakes driven into riverbed mud. *I am still here. I am still—*

The crown closed over him like water over a stone.

* * *

But the thing was frightened, and frightened things make mistakes.

Three days later, at the evening meal, it ordered the kitchens cleared and ate alone—which it never did, for solitude served no purpose in its theater of governance. It summoned no companions, reviewed no documents. It sat with its hands flat on the table, and Aldric, surfacing briefly through the loosened seams, felt the thing reaching outward along channels he had never sensed before, threads of attention strung south and west, toward the mountains, toward Thornfell, toward—

Toward a burning sensation. Toward absence. Something had been destroyed. Something it had hunted for twenty years had ceased to exist, burned to ash in a mountain cottage or a canyon fork, and the loss had struck the parasite like a blow to the body it wore.

*You're afraid of a piece of paper,* Aldric marveled, from his cellar in the dark. *Nineteen years I have begged the gods for a weapon against you, and the gods sent a map.*

And then, worse—better—the thing did something it had never done. It spoke to him.

Not in words. The parasite had never wasted language on its meat. But a pressure formed against the inside of his skull, vast and cold and deliberate, and meaning bloomed in it like frost on glass:

*IT DOES NOT MATTER. THE FEAST COMES. ON THE FEAST NIGHT I RENEW, AND THE RENEWAL WILL BE PUBLIC, AND YOU WILL WATCH YOURSELF DIE FROM INSIDE.*

Aldric, alone at his own table in his own body's silence, smiled.

It was a terrible smile, a ruined thing, the smile of a man who has been underwater so long that even drowning feels like movement. But it was his. He let the thing see it, let it feel the defiance humming in the meat it wore, because he had calculated the cost and decided it was worth paying. Let it tighten its grip. Let it drown him a little faster. Fear made it careless, and carelessness made hours like this one possible, and hours like this one were the only currency he had ever had to spend.

*The Binding Feast,* he thought. *So that is the deadline. That is when the door must open—or never.*

He thought of the gatekeeper in Thornfell, whose name he had hoarded like a coin. Pemberton. A keeper of a sealed gate, loyal to no one, useful to everyone. The kind of person, the only kind of person, who might carry a dead man's oath through fire and flood without ever once believing in it—and do the right thing anyway, simply because they had promised.

*If you're out there,* Aldric thought, addressing a stranger across three hundred miles of frozen mountain, *if you're reading his map and cursing his name and wondering why you of all people—know this. The tomb will offer you a way out. It will offer everyone a way out. That is how it was built. That is the trap.*

*The door opens both ways. Someone has to stand in it.*

Deep beneath the palace, in the old smuggler roads the builders had bricked over and forgotten, something ancient shifted in its sleep and dreamed of an open gate. Above it, the hollow king raised his wine cup to no one, and drank, and behind his eyes a dying man began, very quietly, to plan.

The feast was eleven days away.
