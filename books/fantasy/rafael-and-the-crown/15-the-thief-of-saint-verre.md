## 15. The Thief of Saint Verre

Brannic Vael had robbed eleven churches in his life, and every one of them had deserved it.

Not this one, though. That was the trouble with Saint Verre. He stood in the shadow of a dyer's awning across the Candlemarket, arms crossed against the evening cold, and studied the cathedral the way he'd once studied a merchant's strongroom before putting his name to the job—and everything about it told him this was going to hurt.

It wasn't the size of the place, though the thing was monstrous enough: a mountain of pale granite and slate that seemed to have grown out of the city rather than been built upon it, its spire lost in the low clouds, its buttresses arcing out like ribs. It wasn't the guards, either. There were only four at the doors, and Brannic had seen worse watching smuggler caches worth a tenth of what lay inside. No—what made his teeth ache was the light. Every window of Saint Verre glowed like a lit lamp from within, warm gold bleeding through glass worked into scenes of saints and kings, and the whole city seemed to orbit that glow like moths. You couldn't creep up on a building that wanted to be seen. The cathedral didn't hide its treasures behind locks and iron. It hid them in front of everyone, in plain sight, under ten thousand candles, trusting to the sheer audacity of holiness to keep honest people away.

Honest people. Brannic smiled without humor. He hadn't been honest since he was nine years old, and the priest who caught him stealing tallow had beaten him with a candle-snuffer for the good of his soul. He still had the scar on his left ear. He intended, tonight, to collect a little interest on it.

"You're scowling," said Sefa beside him, one of the three crew who'd come down out of the mountains with them—the three who were left, after the split road and the bolt and all the rest. She was small and quick and could walk a ridgepole in her sleep. "You always scowl when the take's too easy."

"The take's never easy." Brannic nodded at the cathedral. "That's the reliquary house. Third floor of the cloister gallery, behind the sacristan's stair. I counted six locks on the model and two of them are puzzle-work."

"Six locks for a key."

"For *the* key." He kept his voice flat. "The Tomb-Key. Old as the crown, Ondine says. Iron and bone, and it opens a door that hasn't opened in three hundred years. If they keep it behind one lock, I'll eat my boots."

Below them, the Candlemarket seethed with festival traffic. The Binding Feast was five days off, and the city had gone strange with it—garlands strung from balcony to balcony, wax-sellers crying their wares, pilgrims kneeling in the gutters because the inns were full. Somewhere beyond the river, smoke rose from another quarter that had stopped trading garlands and started trading accusations. Harboring the courier's kin, they said. Harboring traitors. The king's gray riders went through those streets at night and came out at dawn carrying people who were never seen again.

Brannic had spent thirty years staying out of other people's wars. He was beginning to suspect those days were over.

* * *

They'd planned it in the cellar of a tannery that smelled so strongly of lime and rotting hide that no watchman would linger there willingly. Theo had spread the map's ghost across the table—not the map itself; that was ash now, scattered somewhere along the canyon fork—but the memory of it, redrawn from their own head in careful charcoal strokes. Brannic had watched them do it and thought, not for the first time, that Pemberton was the strangest person he'd ever thrown in with. A gatekeeper who knew roads that weren't on any map. A liar so bad it was almost a kind of honesty. Someone who had spent a lifetime learning how to be invisible and then, piece by piece, was learning how to stop.

"The key is in the reliquary," Ondine had said, tapping the drawing with a knuckle scarred by old rites. "Saint Verre keeps it because the founders gave it to the Church for safekeeping when the tomb was sealed. The binding oaths say the key must rest 'in holy hands until the feast requires it.'"

"Then we wait until the feast requires it," Brannic had said. "They'll carry it out themselves. We take it on the road."

"No." Ondine's voice had gone quiet in that way that made even Brannic sit straighter. "On the feast night the key goes to the palace by royal escort, and the parasite-king will have it in hand when the rite begins. Whatever the unbinding needs, it needs to happen *before* that thing renews itself. We steal the key while it sleeps in the reliquary, or we lose."

So here they were. Two ways in. Brannic would go over the top, through the roof of the cloister gallery where the slate was old and the gargoyles made convenient ladders, with Sefa and young Corrin behind him. And Theo—

Theo would walk in the front door.

He'd argued with them about it for an hour. *Why you?* Because, Theo had said, in that flat, tired voice of theirs, everyone in the capital was praying right now, terrified of purges and plagues and the end of the world, and nobody looked twice at a penitent. Because Theo had a face that forgot itself. Because Brannic couldn't kneel for two hours without his knees filing a formal complaint.

And because, though Theo hadn't said it, if anything went wrong below, the penitent was the one who'd be caught first and questioned longest. They'd volunteered for the dangerous half and dressed it up as the simple one. Brannic had let them have it. He'd learned that much about Pemberton: you couldn't protect them from sacrifice. You could only make sure it bought something.

* * *

The climb began at moonrise, such as the moon was—half-swallowed by cloud, giving just enough silver to kill a man on wet slate.

Brannic went up the back of the chandlers' row like water finding a crack, Sefa at his heel and Corrin puffing behind, and from there the rooftops of the cathedral close unrolled before him: the tanners' sheds, the bellfounder's yard with its great buried molds, the pilgrim hospice whose dormer windows breathed out snores and prayer in equal measure. He moved the way he'd taught a dozen thieves to move, weight low, hands reading the stone ahead of his feet, never committing until the rock had promised to hold. Thirty years of smuggling roads and mountain paths had given him a thief's balance without ever requiring the theft; tonight the old skills came home like dogs hearing a whistle.

The cloister gallery ran along the cathedral's north flank, two stories of covered walkway around the monks' garden, and above its inner roofline the gargoyles crouched in their centuries-old agony—winged hounds and weeping faces, mouths open to spit rainwater at sinners. One of them, a gaunt figure with folded wings, leaned out from the gallery wall at exactly the height a climbing man needed. Brannic had watched the sacristans from the market for three days. He knew which gargoyles had been repointed recently and which were original, and the winged one was original, its wings worn smooth as river stones by five hundred years of weather.

He went up it like a lover up a trellis.

Sefa followed, silent as spilled sand. Corrin took longer, breath sawing, but he made it, and then the three of them were flattened against the gallery roof in the lee of a parapet while the bells above them weighed the silence. From here Brannic could see straight down through the arcade into the cloister walk, where two novices hurried past with lanterns, arguing softly about some doctrine, utterly unaware that death and robbery hung twenty feet above their heads.

"Sacristan's stair," Sefa breathed, pointing. Through a grilled window at the gallery's end, a spiral stair climbed toward the third-floor reliquary house. Light showed at its top. "Someone's awake."

"There's always someone awake in a church. That's the point of churches." Brannic eased forward along the parapet, counting windows. "We go in through the ventilator shaft above the reliquary itself. Slate comes off, we drop lines, in and out before the sacristan finishes his prayers. Ten minutes."

"And if it takes twenty?"

"Then we're very devout burglars."

* * *

Below, in the body of the cathedral, Theo Pemberton knelt among strangers and prayed to nothing.

They had chosen their disguise well—a widow's gray, a penitent's hood, ashes rubbed into the hollows of their cheeks, and the bowed shoulders of someone carrying grief too heavy for words. Half the city wore that face these days; the purges had made mourners cheap. Theo had entered with the dusk crowd, bought a stub of candle with coins Brannic grudgingly provided, and knelt at the rail of Saint Verre-of-the-Lamp herself, the cathedral's patron, whose gilded effigy held a lantern aloft above the altar.

They had been kneeling long enough for their knees to pass through pain into numbness. They had confessed, sotto voce, a series of sins both true and invented. And all the while, beneath the hood, their eyes moved.

The reliquary of Saint Verre sat behind iron grillwork to the right of the transept, visible even from here: a case of black oak and gilt, tall as a coffin standing on end, its door locked behind a lattice of silver. And within it, resting on a cushion of faded crimson velvet, something long and dark caught the candlelight and held it—a bar of ancient metal crossed with paler material, shaped like no key used on any living door.

Even at forty paces, even through glass and lattice and five hundred years of piety, the Tomb-Key had presence. Theo felt it the way one feels a draft from a hidden door. Ondine had warned them: *iron and bone, forged in the same fires as the crown. The two objects know each other.* Which meant, Theo thought grimly, that somewhere in this city, the thing wearing King Aldric's face might feel it too—that somewhere in the palace across the river, behind walls and guards and ceremony, a hollow man might turn his head tonight like a beast scenting weather.

*Hurry, Brannic.*

* * *

The ventilator shaft fought him.

The slate came off clean—Brannic's knife did quiet, respectful work—but beneath it the builders of Saint Verre had laid an iron grille, and behind the grille the shaft dropped into darkness that smelled of dust and candle-tallow and something older, something mineral and cold, like the air off deep caves. Brannic hooked his fingers through the lattice and paused.

"Problem?" Sefa whispered.

"It's warm." He frowned. Down the shaft, faint as memory, came a breath of heat. Not hearth-warmth. Mountain-warmth. The same strange exhalation they'd felt in the caverns under Thornfell, the breathing of whatever slept beneath Cael. Here? Under a cathedral in the middle of the capital?

Ondine had said the founders built Saint Verre directly over the old processional road—the smuggler roads, the deep ways, the veins of the mountain that ran farther than any map admitted. Perhaps the warmth leaked up through them like heat through a floor. Perhaps the cathedral itself had been raised as a cork in a bottle.

"Means we're in the right place," he muttered, and wrenched the grille free.

The drop into the reliquary house was twelve feet onto a floor of worn flags. Brannic went down the line first, landing soft, blade already drawn, and found himself in a room that made even him stop a moment. Shelves rose to the vaulted ceiling, and every shelf held saints: fingerbones in filigree, skulls crowned in silver, a whole arm encased in crystal, robes, sandals, a vial of tears, a comb said to belong to a queen whom history remembered only for her hair. Hundreds of candles burned in niches, tended day and night, their light pooling gold on the flagstones. The dead of a thousand years watched him with polished, patient eyes.

"Blasphemy has a smell," Sefa whispered, dropping beside him. "Like copper and fear."

"Save the poetry. Find me the case."

It stood against the far wall, black oak and gilt, exactly as advertised. Six locks. Brannic knelt before it and felt his heart sink with professional admiration. Two were ordinary ward-locks, child's play. Two were combination dials of the sort the guilds used for debt-boxes, each ring inscribed with tiny letters of old liturgical script. And two were puzzle-locks, interlocking brass plates that had to be slid in sequence—one wrong slide and, he'd wager his other boot, a bell rang somewhere pleasant and monastic.

"Corrin. Watch the stair."

He set to work. This was the part of thieving he actually loved, though he'd have died before admitting it: the quiet conversation between a maker's cleverness and a breaker's patience. The ward-locks surrendered in a minute. The dials took longer—he read the wear on the rings, the letters touched most often by the sacristan's thumb, and let the machine tell him its habits the way he'd read a mountain path by which rocks were loose. *Ver-re-lux*, the rings finally agreed. The saint's own name. Devout men made terrible locksmiths; they trusted God to guard what their fingers grew careless about.

The puzzle plates were the true work. Sweat ran down Brannic's spine. Below them, muffled by floors, the service bells chimed the hour, and he thought of Theo alone in that sea of worshippers, hood up, heart hammering out a rhythm no one could hear. He thought of Rafael, dying on a frozen road, pressing a stranger's duty into a stranger's hands. He thought of the crossbow bolt that had nearly taken him apart at the split road, and the scar it had left, and the fact that he was still here, still breathing, doing the one thing in the world he was genuinely good at.

The last plate clicked home.

The case door swung open on oiled hinges with a sigh, and there it was, cradled on its crimson cushion: the Tomb-Key. Longer than a forearm, wrought of dark iron shot through with veins of something white—bone, actual bone, fused into the metal—and cold to the touch with a cold that bit through leather gloves. When Brannic lifted it, the candle flames in the room all bent sideways at once, as if the room had inhaled.

"Right," he whispered. "Everyone hates that. Good. Wrap it."

* * *

Theo felt the moment the key moved.

There was no way to describe it except as a sound heard through the floor of the world—a single deep note, struck somewhere beneath consciousness, felt in the sternum rather than the ears. Around them, hundreds of worshippers murmured on, oblivious. But near the transept, a woman in fine wool went still mid-prayer, her lips freezing around the words. An old monk lifted his head like a deer. And at the pillar nearest the west door, a figure in traveling leathers turned—not toward the reliquary, not toward the choir, but slowly, deliberately, scanning the congregation with eyes trained by twenty years of hunting.

Inquisitor Vey.

Theo's blood went to ice water. She stood barely fifteen paces away, plain-cloaked, gray at the temples, her face carrying that terrible patient conviction he'd glimpsed across an interrogation table—except that now she was released into the city's chaos by her own choice, treason she could not undo, and doubt she carried like a stone. She had let them go. She had given them until the Binding Feast. And if she saw them here, in a cathedral, in a penitent's hood, on the eve of the crime they'd sworn to commit—

She was looking at faces. Methodically. Row by row.

Theo should have lowered their head. Should have become the widow again, the ash-cheeked mourner, invisible as they had been their entire life. Every instinct bred into them by decades at the sealed gate screamed *down, still, unseen.*

Instead, Theo raised their head and let the hood fall back.

Vey's gaze swept past them—stopped—came back.

For one heartbeat neither of them moved. Candlelight trembled between them. Fifteen paces of marble floor, and on it, wordless, passed the whole argument of the last months: the table, the truth, the plague, the sister, the doubt. Vey's hand did not go to a weapon. Her eyes widened a fraction—recognition, and beneath it, fury, and beneath *that*, something rawer. She had staked everything on letting them prove it. And here they were, proving it, and she could not ask how, could not shout, could not arrest them without destroying the very chance she'd granted.

Theo held her gaze. Then, slowly, deliberately, they stood, turned, and walked—not ran, walked—with the unhurried tread of a penitent leaving early, toward the north aisle, away from the sacristan's stair, away from Brannic's route, making themselves a target in full view of God and the crown's chief hunter.

Behind them, soft as falling ash, they heard Vey begin to move.

* * *

Brannic had the key wrapped in oilcloth and slung across his back when Sefa's hand closed on his arm like a trap.

"Movement below. Gray leathers. Two, maybe three, coming up the cloister—fast."

"Vey's watchers." Of course. The inquisitor never hunted alone; even released into doubt, she'd have eyes she trusted seeded through the city. "Stair?"

"Blocked. Window?"

"Window."

They went out through the reliquary house's narrow lancet onto the gallery roof, Corrin first, then Sefa, then Brannic with the Tomb-Key dragging at his spine like a sleeping animal. Below, torchlight bloomed in the cloister walk. A voice called out—*the sacristan! Intruders in the reliquary!*—and the whole cathedral below them seemed to wake at once, bells beginning to stir high overhead, doors booming, the beautiful machine of the church converting itself into a cage.

They ran the rooftops south, gargoyles blurring past, slate skidding underfoot. Behind them whistles shrilled. Ahead lay the chandlers' row and freedom, forty feet of open gap between the gallery and the first warehouse roof, with a drop and a running jump that Brannic had judged, from the ground, as merely terrifying.

From up here, carrying a relic that hummed against his vertebrae, it looked impossible.

"Go," he gasped. "I'm heavier than I look."

"You've *always* looked heavy," Sefa said, and jumped.

She cleared it. Corrin cleared it, badly, rolling. Brannic backed up three steps, prayed to no saint in particular—*sorry, Verre, nothing personal*—and launched himself into the dark.

For one endless moment he flew. Then the opposite roof hit his feet, buckled his knees, and sent him sliding down wet slate toward the gutter's edge with the whole candlelit city tilting around him. His free hand slapped down on a chimney stack and held—held—held. He lay there gasping, cheek against cold brick, the Tomb-Key pressing into his back with its impossible chill, and listened to the hunt swirl and eddy below.

But it was eddying the wrong way. He realized it gradually, lying broken-backed on a stranger's roof: the shouts, the torches, the gray-leathered runners—all of it streaming *north*, away from him, toward the cathedral's west side, toward the market lanes. Toward the route Theo had taken.

Toward the penitent who had pulled back their hood in a crowded nave and let the most dangerous woman in the kingdom see their face.

"Oh, you absolute—" Brannic couldn't finish it. Grief and admiration arrived together, twins, indistinguishable. He knew exactly what Theo was doing. Making themselves the loudest object in the hunt so the quiet ones could slip away. Spending their invisibility—the thing they'd guarded longer than Brannic had guarded anything—as if it were coin, flinging it into the street for strangers to scramble after.

He got up. He ran, softer now, south and east, Sefa and Corrin ghosts at his heels, the key humming its cold note against his spine.

* * *

They reached the tannery cellar an hour past midnight, and Ondine took one look at Brannic's face and knew.

"Theo?"

"Drew them off." He set the Tomb-Key on the table between the lime-stained barrels, and the candle flames all bent at once, and nobody spoke for a moment. "Walked out the front of the holiest building in the capital with Vey's eyes on their back. Bought us the whole city's worth of confusion." He laughed, and it came out wrong, ragged. "Thirty years I've kept myself alive by never mattering to anyone. And that mad, stubborn gatekeeper just handed themselves to the crown to save a smuggler's errand."

Ondine's weathered hands hovered over the key without touching it. "Will they escape?"

"They escaped the cathedral. But they *let* her see them, Sister. On purpose. You don't undo a thing like that. By morning, every gray cloak in the city will know Pemberton's face." He sank onto a barrel, suddenly feeling every one of his years, and stared at the dark iron coiled in its cloth. "Five days to the Binding Feast. We have the key. We have the road under the cathedral. And we have until the crown realizes what's missing."

Above them, muffled by floors and distance, the cathedral bells began to ring the alarm proper—deep bronze voices tumbling over one another, waking the city, calling the faithful to witness a sacrilege. Brannic listened to them and understood, with terrible clarity, that somewhere out in those ringing streets, Theo Pemberton was running through the dark alone, spending the last of their invisibility one coin at a time.

"Hold the circle till the feast," he said quietly. "I'm going to get our gatekeeper back."
