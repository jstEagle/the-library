## 16. Interrogation by Candlelight

The cell smelled of wet stone and old prayers.

Inquisitor Sera Vey had stood in a hundred of these rooms, in a dozen keeps across the king's domains, and she had learned to read them the way other officers read battlefields. This one had been a chapel once—she could see where the alcove for an icon had been chiseled flat, where the drain in the floor's center had been cut to carry away something holier than blood. The crown repurposed everything. That was its genius, and its rot. It took the vessels people built for their faiths and poured its own purposes into them, and the walls remembered nothing, because walls were never asked.

She had asked for no chains on the prisoner. Chains were for those who might run, and Theo Pemberton would not run. Vey had watched them walk out of Saint Verre's cathedral nave at her signal, hands loose at their sides, chin level, as though being taken by the crown's inquisition were a thing they had scheduled between supper and sleep. She had interrogated smugglers who wept before she spoke. She had broken fanatics with nothing but silence and a pitcher of water. She had never once met a prisoner who looked *pitying*.

Pemberton looked pitying. Sitting across the scarred table now, wrists bare, shoulders settled against the wall behind them like a traveler resting at an inn, they watched Vey arrange her papers with an expression she could only describe as patience. As if she were the one about to be examined.

"Candle," Vey said to the guard at the door, and the man lit the second candle and withdrew, closing the door with the soft click of men who knew better than to linger. Two flames now, one at each end of the table, and between them the prisoner's face moving in and out of shadow like something surfacing in dark water.

"You've been busy," Vey said. "Saint Verre. The reliquary. The Tomb-Key." She laid each item on the table like a card: the watch report, the sacristan's testimony, the sketch a frightened novice had made of a penitent whose hood slipped at precisely the wrong moment. "Twenty years I have followed your friend's family through this kingdom. Graves, mostly. Ashes, sometimes. Never once a face. And then Rafael Corvane dies on a mountain road, and within a season his map is ash in a canyon, his allies are ghosts in my net, and his last heir is robbing God's house in the capital."

"His last heir," Pemberton repeated quietly.

"Don't." Vey kept her voice level, almost gentle; gentleness was a blade too, and she had honed it longer than any other instrument she owned. "You are not his heir. You are a gatekeeper from Thornfell who got handed a dying man's errand and mistook it for a destiny. I have read what that family left behind, Pemberton. I burned most of it myself. Whatever he pressed into your hands, whatever oath you think you swallowed—it is a rope, and someone above you is holding the other end, and they have been pulling for two hundred years."

Something flickered in the prisoner's eyes—not fear. Something worse. Recognition, maybe. Or grief for *her*, which was intolerable.

"You're going to tell me everything," Vey said. "Not because I'll hurt you. Because you've spent the whole journey wanting to say it aloud, and I am the first person with the rank to hear it and the authority to do anything about it. Am I wrong?"

For a long moment the only sound was the small wet breathing of the candles.

"No," said Theo Pemberton. "You're not wrong. But you won't believe me. So let me ask you something first, Inquisitor. One question. Answer it honestly, and then decide whether to listen."

Vey folded her hands. "Ask."

"Your sister." The name landed in the room like a dropped stone. "What did she die of?"

The candles hissed. Vey sat very still. Twenty years in the crown's service had taught her face to be a locked door, but the lock had rusted somewhere in the middle, and she felt it give—one heartbeat, two—and hated herself for every one of them.

"The gray fever," she said finally. "In Kellering. Eleven years ago."

"And how many died in Kellering?"

"Four hundred. Perhaps more. The records were—" She stopped.

"Were sealed," Pemberton finished. "Yes. We found the village of Ash on our way down the mountain. Same story there. Fever comes, fever spreads, the king's physicians arrive three weeks late with wagons and burn everything worth burning, and the survivors are moved to places where they can be counted. You investigated Kellering yourself, didn't you? After she died. You filed a request to examine the quarantine ledgers."

"I did."

"And it was denied. By order of the crown. Twice."

"*Twice*," Vey said, and heard her voice crack on the second word, and despised the room for hearing it.

Pemberton leaned forward into the candlelight, and Vey saw them clearly for the first time: tired past tiredness, a bruise going yellow along the jaw from the arrest, hands that had done rough work all their life and recently rougher work still. Not a fanatic. Fanatics glowed. This one had been *burned down to coals* and was running on them anyway.

"Then here is the truth you denied twice," Pemberton said. "And I will tell all of it, and you will verify every piece, because verification is the only sacrament you have left. But you will not interrupt, and when I am finished you will not reach for your sword, because if you do, everything your sister died in ignorance of dies with her. Agreed?"

It was blackmail. It was insolence. It was, Vey realized with a cold turning in her chest, exactly what she herself would have demanded in the prisoner's place.

"Agreed."

* * *

They talked until the first candle drowned in its own wax.

Theo Pemberton told it plainly, without ornament, which was the only reason Vey believed any of it—the liars she'd broken always decorated, always reached for the dramatic detail, and this woman gave her none. A courier bleeding out on a pilgrim road. A wax-sealed map charting passages under Mount Cael that even the Thornfell keeper hadn't known. A journal in a dead man's hand speaking of *the thing wearing his crown*. A gatekeeper's confession: the Thornfell gate had never been built against smugglers at all, but to seal the old roads leading down toward something buried beneath Cael itself—a thing old enough that the first binders had needed a crown to chain it, and kings to wear the chain.

"A vessel," Vey said. Her voice came out strange to her. "Each king becomes a vessel."

"Until it consumes him. Ondine—the hermit at Cael's foot—she served the crown's rites before they stripped her. She told us the binding renews at the Binding Feast. Every generation, the parasite feeds publicly, deepens its hold, and the man wearing the crown gets a little less real. Ask yourself, Inquisitor—you've seen him. Have you ever once, in all your years of audiences and councils, seen King Aldric change? Age? Grieve? *Blink* at the wrong moment?"

She had seen him. She had stood in the throne room four times in her career, and each time she had come away with the same wordless unease she'd never been able to file in a report: the sense of a portrait being maintained rather than a man being alive. She had assumed it was majesty. Majesty was supposed to be unreadable.

But—

"The Binding Feast," she said slowly. "It's held in nine days."

"Yes."

"And you intend to kill the king. At his own feast. With a smuggler, a defrocked priestess, and whatever soldiers of mine you imagine you've recruited."

"I intend to free him," Pemberton said. "Killing may be incidental. There's a difference. You'd know it if you'd watched a man fight for one hour inside his own skull for the right to scratch six words into his own arm."

Vey went still. "What words?"

"'The tomb lies. The door opens both ways.' We found the message. Scratched into his forearm, hidden under a sleeve, written in a hand that shook so badly the letters barely held their shapes. That was Aldric, Inquisitor. What's left of him. Reaching out through the thing that wears him, spending the last of himself to warn strangers he'll never meet." Pemberton's voice didn't waver, but something behind it did, some load-bearing grief. "That is your king. That has always been your king. And your sister—"

"Don't." Now it was Vey's turn to say it.

"—your sister died of a fever the crown allowed to spread," Pemberton went on, merciless and quiet, "because the parasite does not care about plague villages except as fuel. Fear, Inquisitor. Grief. Four hundred deaths in Kellering, and the thing in the crown fed on the mourning of a whole province, and when you requested the quarantine ledgers, the order to deny you came from a council chamber where the king sat listening with eyes that don't blink. You built your whole life into a spear to avenge her. You aimed it at the family that was trying to stop the thing that killed her. They aimed you, Vey. For twenty years, the crown has used your grief as a hound uses a nose."

The second candle guttered. Neither of them moved to trim it.

Vey sat with her hands flat on the table, and beneath them, she noticed distantly, they were trembling—not with rage. Rage she knew how to hold. This was the tremor of a foundation being tested, of a building deciding whether it was going to stand or come down with everyone inside it.

She thought of Maren. Not the way she usually allowed herself to—in fragments, rationed, the way one drinks in a desert—but whole. Maren at nineteen, laughing in the kitchen with flour on her hands. Maren coughing blood into a rag and insisting it was nothing. Maren's last letter, which Vey had carried against her heart for eleven years and never reread, because she knew what it said: *ask someone why the physicians didn't come, Sera. Someone has to ask.*

She had asked. She had been denied. And instead of following the denial up the chain to its source, she had turned around and hunted downward, easier prey, a family already marked, already ruined—because hunting the guilty was bearable and doubting the crown was not.

"You want verification," she said at last. Her voice was steady now. Steadier than she felt. "Fine. Here is mine."

She drew a key from her belt—the plain iron key she was permitted to carry and almost never permitted to use—and unlocked the drawer of the table. Inside lay the folder she had fought eleven years to see and been refused: not the Kellering ledgers themselves, but her own copies, her own notes, twenty years of an inquisitor's obsessive documentation. Quarantine timelines. Physician dispatch orders. The pattern, once laid side by side, of fevers blooming across the kingdom like crop circles of death—each one within a week's ride of a Binding Feast, each one extinguished with suspicious efficiency, each one followed by reports from court chroniclers of *the king in excellent spirits*.

She spread the papers between the candles.

Pemberton looked down at them for a long time. When they raised their head again, their eyes were wet, and they did not wipe them.

"How long," Vey asked, "have you known about the plagues?"

"Since the village of Ash. Ondine confirmed it after." A pause. "I'm sorry. About your sister. I've carried dead friends' errands long enough to know it doesn't help, anyone saying that."

"It doesn't," Vey agreed. And then, because honesty was a fire that, once lit, consumed protocol along with everything else: "It helps a little."

* * *

They worked through what remained of the night, and it was the strangest night of Sera Vey's life: prisoner and inquisitor bent over papers together like scholars, the cell door unlocked, the guards outside dismissed on pretext of a prolonged interrogation. She verified. It was the only prayer she knew anymore. Every claim Pemberton made, she tested against the archive in her head—twenty years of crown intelligence, dates and routes and faces—and the terrible thing was how *well* it fit. The Corvane family's movements tracking the binding sites. The purges that always preceded a Feast. The watcher she'd planted in Thornfell, dead in a burned village, and Brannic's smuggler crew slipping through mountain passes that existed on no survey she'd ever commissioned—because the surveys had been forbidden, for two hundred years, by men who did not want anyone asking what was under Cael.

Near dawn, she stopped writing.

"If I release you," she said, "I am committing treason. Not suspicion of treason. Treason, full-bodied, in the sight of God and the crown. There is no version of the coming days in which I survive it. If you fail at this Feast, I hang beside you. If you succeed—" she laughed, short and bitter, "—there is no precedent for succeeding. No law covers freeing a king from his own crown. I would be a traitor who happened to be right, and history is written by whoever's left standing."

"I know," Pemberton said.

"And yet you sat across from me all night and gambled everything on my grief." Vey rose, gathered her papers, and stood a moment with her hand flat on the table, looking down at the woman who had dismantled her life's certainty in the space between two candles. "That was crueler than torture, Keeper. I hope you know that."

"I know," said Theo Pemberton, gently.

Vey unlocked the cell door herself. Beyond it, the corridor was grey with early light, and beyond that, the city was waking into chaos—she could hear it even here, bells and shouting and the rumble of carts, refugees pouring in from the purged provinces, the capital groaning under the weight of a kingdom tearing at its seams. In eight days, the Binding Feast. In eight days, either the parasite fed again before all the court, or it died.

"Nine days ago I would have executed you where you sat," Vey said. "So understand what I'm telling you when I say this: you have until the Feast to prove it. All of it. Publicly, undeniably, before witnesses I choose. If you fail—if this is some elaborate Corvane vengeance dressed in scripture—I will spend what's left of my life making sure the world knows exactly what you are." She stepped aside, clearing the doorway, an inquisitor opening a door for treason. "Go. Find your smuggler before my colleagues find him. And Pemberton—"

The keeper paused in the threshold, silhouetted against the light.

"When it's done," Vey said, "someone has to ask him. The king. Why the physicians never came. He owes the kingdom that answer. He owes *me* that answer."

"He knows," Pemberton said softly. "Whatever's left of him—he's been asking himself the same question for thirty years. That's the trap of that crown, Inquisitor. You watch everything and can stop nothing."

Then they were gone, footsteps fading down the corridor into the noise of the waking city, and Sera Vey stood alone in a chapel-turned-cell littered with the ashes of her certainty, and began, methodically, to plan how a loyal servant of the crown might betray it in nine days.

Outside, the feast-bells began their rehearsal.
