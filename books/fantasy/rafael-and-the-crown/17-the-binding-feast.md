## 17. The Binding Feast

The palace of Saint Verre had been built to be seen from everywhere and to see everything, and so it was built blind at its own feet. No architect, in three hundred years of adding towers and terraces and gardens that cascaded down the hill like frozen waterfalls, had thought to guard what lay beneath the foundations—because nothing was supposed to be there. The old smuggler roads ran under the city like veins under skin, and one of them, the deepest and oldest, ended in a stair that rose into the palace's bones.

Theo knew it now the way they knew their own hands. They had spent eleven days with the burned map's memory, walking it over and over in the dark behind their eyes until every junction, every collapse, every fork where Brannic's crew had once turned back was as familiar as the road from Thornfell gate to the cottage. The map's final secret was not a treasure. It was this: that the mountain's heart and the capital's heart were joined by a passage older than either, dug by hands that had wanted a way in that no king could close.

*The door opens both ways,* Aldric had scratched into his own arm, and Theo carried those words like a stone behind the breastbone. The tomb lied. The tomb opened both ways. And tonight, on the eve—or rather on the very night—of the Binding Feast, they would walk through it.

They gathered in the last dry chamber before the stair: Theo, Brannic leaning on a spear he refused to call a crutch, Sister Ondine with her defrocked vestments hidden under a smuggler's oilskin, and twelve of Vey's soldiers in plain leathers, their gray livery folded away in their packs like shed skins. The fungus-light of the deep roads made everyone look drowned, pale and slow, and the mountain's warmth pressed against them from all sides, patient as a held breath.

Brannic broke the silence first, because Brannic always did.

"Twelve years I've run these roads," he said, "and I never once wondered what was above me when I passed under the palace. You know why? Because wondering is how you die in my trade." He looked around the circle, his scarred face unreadable in the pale light. "Tonight I'm wondering. Feels terrible."

"You can still turn back," Theo said.

"I can't, though, can I. That's the joke." He shifted his weight off the bad leg—the bolt wound at the split road had healed crooked, and it ached in the deep warm air. "You held the doors open for me at Thornfell when the inquisitors came asking. You could have let them take me and been rid of the only man in the mountains who knows your face. Instead you lied to Vey's watcher with a straight face while I stood behind your woodpile listening to every word." He shrugged. "Debts are debts. I pay mine ugly, but I pay them."

Ondine spoke without opening her eyes. She had been praying, or something like praying; her lips moved through formulas Theo suspected predated the crown itself. "The rite must begin before the parasite completes its renewal. Once it drinks the king fully—in front of the whole court, witnessed, sanctified—it will have taken root in the law itself. Not just the man. The *office*. Every oath sworn to the crown thereafter will feed it. There will be no unbinding after tonight that any living hand can perform."

"And if we're late?" one of the soldiers asked—a young woman named Kesst, who had followed Vey out of loyalty and doubt in equal measure.

"Then we're late forever," Ondine said simply. "The Binding Feast falls once a generation. The binding weakens, the vessel thins, and the thing renews its grip. Rafael's family died keeping the knowledge of the alternative alive. We do not arrive late."

Theo looked at the stair. It rose into darkness that was not the darkness of caves but the darkness of cut stone, dressed and mortared, ancient work going up into the palace's roots. Somewhere above, faint as memory, music was already beginning—strings tuning, the vast machinery of a court assembling for the most important ceremony of a generation.

"Then let's not be late," Theo said, and started up.

* * *

Inquisitor Vey stood in the gallery above the throne room and watched the court assemble, and hated how beautiful it was.

She had served the crown for nineteen years. She had tortured men whose only crime was knowing too much, and she had slept afterward, which she had once believed proved her righteousness and now understood proved only her exhaustion. She had come to this night believing it would be the end of a twenty-year hunt: Rafael's line extinguished, the courier dead in the snow, the last keeper of the Thornfell gate soon to be in chains or cooperating. Clean. Finished.

Instead she had released her prisoner into the city with a forged pass and a lie on her own tongue, and every hour since had been a wager placed against her career, her soul, and possibly the world.

The hall filled below her. Nobles in feast colors, gold and wine-red, flowing to their places like water finding level. The royal choir in white. The high priests of the Binding Rite swinging censers whose smoke smelled, to Vey's trained senses, of something underneath the frankincense—something mineral and old, like hot iron and buried stone. She had attended four Binding Feasts in her service. She had never before noticed the smell. Now she could not stop noticing it, and it made her want to climb down into that glittering crowd and start screaming.

*If you are wrong,* she told herself, *you have committed treason on the word of a gatekeeper and a thief.*

*And if you are right,* answered the other voice, the one that sounded like her sister coughing blood in a plague ward while the king's physicians rode past the quarantine without stopping, *then everything you ever did in the crown's name was done for a parasite, and the only clean act left to you is the one you do next.*

Her soldiers were in place. Twelve of them, scattered through the galleries and service corridors in plain clothes, armed with blades short enough to hide. Kesst had reported an hour ago: the underground party had reached the foot of the old stair. On time. Which meant that somewhere beneath the marble floor where nobles were now arranging themselves by precedence, Theo Pemberton was climbing toward the throne of the realm, and Vey had personally unlocked the door.

Below, trumpets. The great doors at the far end of the hall swung wide, and the procession began: the high priest first, then the bearers of the regalia, then—

Vey's breath stopped, as it always did, at the sight of him.

King Aldric came down the aisle slowly, too slowly, each step placed with the exaggerated care of a man walking on ice. He was fifty-four years old and looked eighty. His face had gone hollow over the years the way fruit goes hollow, the skin still whole but the substance gone, and his eyes—his eyes were the worst part, because sometimes, for half a heartbeat, they were a tired man's eyes, frightened and human, before the shine came over them again like frost forming on a window.

Tonight, as the crown caught the light of ten thousand candles, Vey saw something she had never seen at a Binding Feast.

The king stumbled.

It was nothing. A half-step, a catch of his own foot, quickly corrected. The court murmured and smoothed over it, the way courts do. But Vey saw the king's hand rise—just slightly, just for a moment—and press flat against his own chest, over the heart, as if holding something in. Or holding something *out*.

*He's fighting it,* she thought. *Right now. In front of all of them. He's been fighting it for thirty years and none of us ever knew.*

She found Kesst at the gallery rail beside her. "Signal the others," Vey said quietly. "When the doors below open, no one moves until Pemberton gives the word. Whatever happens—we hold the exits, not the hall. If this fails, someone has to survive to tell it."

Kesst hesitated. "And if it succeeds?"

Vey watched the hollow king take his seat upon the throne, the crown blazing, the parasite within him settling deeper for its long-awaited meal.

"Then history won't need us to tell it," she said. "It'll have been there."

* * *

The stair ended behind a wall of the palace's oldest cellar, and the last lock between them and the throne room was a door that had not been opened in living memory—which, Brannic observed, was the best kind of door, because nobody remembered to guard it.

Picking it took him four minutes and cost him two broken picks and a stream of profanity delivered in the reverent whisper of a man who respects both locks and gods. Then the door swung inward onto a service corridor that smelled of tallow and roasting meat, and the company moved into the beating heart of the palace like smoke moving into a house.

They went in ones and twos, smuggler-fashion, hugging walls, wearing the livery of minor household servants that Vey had procured through channels she would deny to her grave. Ondine went first, guided by Theo's whispered recitation of the map's final pages—the route from cellar to gallery to the antechamber behind the throne room's eastern wall, where, according to markings on the map three centuries old, a second door waited. A private door. A binder's door, cut into the throne room itself by the same hands that had dug the roads beneath the mountain.

Because the binders had known. Theo understood it now, walking the corridors with their heart hammering against the scar that wasn't there yet. The original binders had built themselves a back entrance to their own working. In case the binding failed. In case the vessel needed replacing. In case the door needed to open both ways.

The palace roared softly around them—music, laughter, the clink of a thousand cups—as they threaded through its arteries. Twice they froze in alcoves while processions of servants hurried past bearing platters. Once a guardsman looked directly at Brannic, took in the servant's tabard stretched over a body built for other work, and Brannic smiled at him with such perfect, brainless servility that the man nodded and moved on.

At the antechamber door, they gathered in the dark, all thirteen of them pressed together among dust sheets and broken furniture, and through the wall they could hear the ceremony beginning.

A voice like poured honey, resonant and wrong, speaking words that Ondine's lips silently tracked, her face going grayer with each syllable.

"That's not the liturgy," she breathed. "That's the *renewal*. He's not blessing the binding—he's inviting it deeper. Listen—" Her hand closed on Theo's wrist hard enough to bruise. "The vessel is nearly empty. That's why the public rite, why the witnesses. It needs the court's belief. It needs hundreds of minds consenting, even unknowingly, to be ruled. Belief is the mortar of the binding, child. Tonight it means to rebuild itself with the whole kingdom as its foundation."

Theo pressed their ear to the ancient door. Through the stone, muffled and distant, the honeyed voice rose and fell, and beneath it—so faint they might have imagined it—another sound. A thinner voice. Cracked. Human.

Saying, over and over, words that Theo had read scratched into dying flesh:

*The tomb lies. The door opens both ways.*

"He's still in there," Theo whispered. "Aldric. He's still fighting."

"Then we go now," said Brannic, "while the thing's busy drinking." He drew his blade, and around him the twelve soldiers did the same, steel whispering out of leather in the dark. "Doors don't open themselves, gatekeeper. But you've been opening doors your whole life, haven't you? Just usually the other direction."

Theo set their palm against the binder's door. The wood was cold—colder than anything in this warm-boned palace, cold as Thornfell stone, cold as the tomb waiting far below the mountain. Old iron hinges groaned once, softly, as they pushed.

Light spilled through the widening gap: candlelight, ten thousand flames, and the blaze of the crown across the length of the hall. The honeyed voice faltered mid-syllable.

On the throne, the hollow king's head began, slowly, terribly, to turn toward the small dark door in the eastern wall that had not opened in three hundred years.

The doors were open. Every debt had come due.

Theo stepped through.
