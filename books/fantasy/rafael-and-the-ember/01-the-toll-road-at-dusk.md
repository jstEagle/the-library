## 1. The Toll Road at Dusk

The first wrong thing was the birds.

Xanthe Fairweather had crossed the Thornfells enough times to know their moods the way other people knew the faces of their neighbors. The pass sang when it wanted you through and went silent when it didn't, and the small brown finches that lived in the switchback scrub always rose in a ragged gray cloud an hour before dusk, wheeling down toward the valley like a purse of spilled coins. Xanthe counted on them the way they counted on their own heartbeat.

Tonight the scrub stood empty. No birds. No wind. Just the road unspooling ahead of them, pale as an old scar in the last of the light, and the mountains standing around it like witnesses who'd been told not to speak.

"Come on, then," Xanthe muttered to no one, and clicked their tongue at the mule.

Bramble—stubborn, swaybacked, smarter than half the guild's membership—plodded on without comment. The cargo rode easy: forty pounds of Thornfell blueleaf tea bound for the lowland markets at Corrow's Ford, six vials of mountain honey-wine for a tavern keeper who paid double because he never asked where it came from, and one sealed letter from a merchant's wife to her sister that Xanthe hadn't opened and wouldn't. That was the whole religion of the trade, the thing Elder Maren had hammered into them before they were tall enough to see over a packing crate: *you carry what you carry. You don't ask what it is. You don't lose it, and you don't look inside.*

Simple rules. They'd kept the Fairweather guild alive for four generations, through two border wars, a famine, and the Crown's long slow squeeze of tariffs and tolls and honest-looking men with dishonest ledgers.

They had not, so far, kept the guild alive against whatever this was.

Xanthe smelled the smoke before they saw the firelight—a thin woodsmoke tang riding the cold air down the pass, wrong somehow, too clean, too disciplined. Bandit fires stank of wet green branches and burnt fat. This smelled like army cookfires, the kind fed on split cordwood stacked by soldiers who'd been taught to build them square.

Xanthe stopped Bramble in the shadow of a leaning scarp, crouched, and pressed two fingers to the cold stone. Old trick. The road ran through a shallow cut here, walled on both sides by rock, and sound carried along stone the way water carried along a channel. If there were men ahead, the rock would tell them.

The rock told them plenty. Boots. Dozens of boots. The rhythmic clatter of a pick being driven into something. And underneath all of it, faint but unmistakable, the creak of heavy timber being dragged across stone.

Xanthe closed their eyes and did the arithmetic they'd been avoiding since the empty sky.

Every pass sealed overnight. It wasn't possible. The Thornfells weren't a wall; they were a maze—seven named passes, eleven unnamed ones, more goat tracks than a shepherd could count in a summer. Sealing one took a garrison. Sealing all of them took an army and a reason, and nobody had given the high country any reason. The tax wagons had come through in autumn same as always. The toll collectors at Graywater had been their usual flavor of bored and bribeable. Nothing had changed.

Except everything had changed, quietly, while Xanthe slept in a waystation bunk three valleys east, and now the birds were gone and the rock was full of boots.

They led Bramble off the road and up through the scrub to a shelf of granite they'd used since childhood—a smuggler's perch, flat and hidden, overlooking the cut. Below, exactly where the road narrowed between the walls, the Crown's soldiers were building a gate.

Not a checkpoint. A *gate*. Black iron bars thick as a man's wrist, set in a timber frame bolted straight into the living rock, with a chain-hoist mechanism and a guardhouse going up beside it. Lanterns hung from poles driven into the roadside every twenty paces, burning already though the dark wasn't fully down. Soldiers moved among them with the unhurried competence of people doing a job they expected to do for a long time.

Xanthe lay flat on the granite and watched, and felt something shift under them that had nothing to do with the stone.

It was the boots that did it. Not the gate—the gate was bad, the gate was a wound—but the boots. These soldiers weren't garrison troops. Garrison men slouched. These walked like the guild's own best couriers walked, like people whose bodies had been shaped by years of hard roads, and their gear was new-mended, their lanterns uniform, their picks ringing in the same rhythm. This was an operation. Planned, funded, ordered from somewhere far above the level of any border captain Xanthe had ever bribed.

Somebody had decided the Thornfells should close. All of them. At once. And nobody had told the mountains.

*You're spiraling,* Maren would say, if Maren were here, which she never was, because Elder Maren Fairweather hadn't climbed a pass in nine years. *Spiraling is just fear wearing your thinking face. Get under it. What do you know?*

What Xanthe knew: forty pounds of blueleaf tea that rotted if it sat more than a fortnight. A client list that had thinned from thirty names to eleven in three years, because the Crown's new toll roads were slow but they were safe, and safe was winning. A guild hall in Hollowmere with a leaking roof, four members left counting Maren, and a strongbox that held less every season. And now a wall of iron and soldiers growing across the only trade artery they had left, built overnight, by order of someone who could afford to build it overnight.

The guild couldn't afford a closed road. The guild couldn't afford anything anymore. That was the ugly truth Xanthe carried alongside the tea and the wine and the sealed letter: the Fairweathers were dying, slowly, respectably, and everyone pretended otherwise the way families pretend about a sick elder.

Xanthe stayed on the shelf until full dark, memorizing. Guard rotations—one officer, eight men on the gate, pairs walking the lantern line. Relieving sentries at the top of each hour, marked by a whistle. A supply wagon parked behind the guardhouse with a canvas cover, and—this was the detail that made their stomach drop—the wagon bore the seal of the Office of Roads and Covenant, stamped in wax so fresh it caught the lantern light like a wound.

Office of Roads and Covenant. Xanthe knew the name the way you know a word you've heard a thousand times without ever asking what it means. It appeared on toll receipts. It appeared on the little brass plaques nailed to mile-stones. It was the most boring branch of the most boring ministry in the whole boring apparatus of the Crown, the office that existed to make roads legible, taxable, tame.

Covenant. Xanthe turned the word over like a coin found in the dirt. Why would the Office of Roads put *covenant* in its name? Roads and covenant. As if the two things belonged together. As if there was some old bargain the roads were part of, and the Crown had decided to administer it.

Their grandmother had used the word once. Xanthe had been very small, sitting on the hearthstone while the old woman mended netting, and a traveler's song about the mountain gods had come up from the valley, and Gran had gone still for a moment and said, *Hush, child. The covenant holds.* Then she'd laughed at herself and called it an old woman's nonsense and never said it again.

Xanthe hadn't thought of it in fifteen years. Now it sat in their chest like a swallowed coal.

Below, the work went on. By midnight the gate frame stood finished, and a sergeant with a voice like gravel called the watch to order, and Xanthe heard him read out something to his men—formal, rehearsed, the kind of thing read from orders.

"—by decree of His Majesty, the high passes are closed until further notice, by authority vested in the Office of Roads and Covenant. No traffic shall pass, save by writ. Any soul found upon the high roads after this hour is declared in breach of the King's Peace and subject to seizure." A pause. "Questions?"

A young soldier asked something Xanthe couldn't catch. The sergeant's answer carried clearly up the rock, flat and final:

"Because the Crown says so, boy. You want more than that, write a petition."

Xanthe lay back on the cold granite and stared at the stars, which burned hard and clear the way they only did at altitude, and thought: *I have been smuggling past the Crown my whole life, and I have never once felt watched by it. Tonight it feels like something looked at these mountains and decided.*

That was the unease, they realized finally. Not the gate. Gates could be bribed, tunneled under, blown, waited out. The unease was the shape of the thing—the suddenness, the completeness, the sense of a vast hand moving without warning over ground Xanthe had believed was theirs. For twenty-three years the world had run on rules they understood: the Crown taxed, the guild slipped, the mountains kept their silence, and everyone got by. Tonight the rules had been rewritten in the dark, and no one had even bothered to tell the story of why.

Stories mattered in the high country. Every pass had its story, every shrine its offering, every old family its oath. When a place changed without a story, the change didn't sit right. It slid around like ice underfoot.

Bramble shifted and blew warm air against Xanthe's neck. "I know," Xanthe murmured. "I know. We'll go around."

Going around meant the Deep Cut, the old mining road above Graywater—not really a road anymore, more a rumor of one, choked with scree and deadfall, passable only to someone who'd been shown it by someone who'd been shown it, back and back, until the knowledge was older than anyone alive. Three extra days. The tea would survive it. Barely. Their margins wouldn't.

But that was tomorrow's problem. Tonight's problem was getting off this shelf unseen, and tonight's problem at least was one Xanthe knew how to solve. They waited for the whistle, mapped the lantern line in their head, and began the slow backward crawl off the granite, hand over hand, breath shallow, every motion practiced down to the rhythm of it.

They were ten feet from the shelf when the ember flared below.

There was no warning to it. One moment the guardhouse lanterns burned their steady orange, and the next a light bloomed in the eastern sky—above the peaks, beyond them, a glow like a forge door thrown open, red-gold and pulsing, bright enough to throw the soldiers' shadows long across the road. The gate-builders froze. Someone dropped a hammer. The sergeant started shouting questions no one answered, because no one had answers, and the light pulsed again, twice, slow, deliberate, like a heartbeat.

Then it faded. Took maybe ten heartbeats, start to finish. The dark rushed back in and seemed thicker for it.

Xanthe knelt frozen on the slope, Bramble's lead tight in their fist, and listened to the soldiers below come apart into fear. Not discipline-fear, the fear of orders. Real fear. A young voice saying, "That's the third one this week, I saw one over Corrow—" and the sergeant snarling at him to shut his mouth, and another voice, older, quieter, saying words that drifted up the stone to Xanthe like a message addressed to them alone:

"The old man was right. It's waking."

*Waking.*

Xanthe stood in the dark with a dying guild's cargo at their back and the Crown sealing the world below, and understood—with the particular clarity that comes when the body understands before the mind consents to—that the rules hadn't just been rewritten. They'd been broken. Something had been asleep in these mountains for longer than the guild had existed, and the guild had been living, all this time, in the quiet it left behind. And the quiet was ending.

"Come on, Bramble," Xanthe whispered, and turned uphill, away from the gate, away from the road, toward the Deep Cut and the long way home.

Three days later, hollow-eyed and frost-burned, they came down into Hollowmere with the tea saved and the question unsaved, and found Elder Maren Fairweather waiting in the guild hall doorway with a letter in her hand and a look on her face Xanthe had never seen before.

Maren Fairweather had buried two husbands, survived a massacre, and once stared down a Crown assessor until he apologized for existing. She did not have looks. She had armor.

Now the armor was gone, and behind it she looked, for the first time in Xanthe's life, afraid.

"You saw it," she said. Not a question. "The light."

"I saw a gate," Xanthe said, dumping the pack inside the door. "I saw every pass in the Thornfells sealed in one night by an office I've never heard of, with *covenant* written on its seals. I saw soldiers scared of the sky." They pulled off their gloves finger by finger. "Start talking, Elder. Because the world moved while I was on the mountain, and I want to know who moved it."

Maren looked at the letter in her hand for a long moment. Then she stepped back from the doorway, and held it out, and said the sentence that would undo everything:

"There's a client coming. High priority. Hidden road. And Xanthe—before you take this job, there's something about the guild you need to know. Something about the Ember."
