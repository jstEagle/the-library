## 2. What the Guild Remembers

The guildhall smelled of tallow and old smoke, the way it always had, but this morning it smelled wrong underneath—like a fire that had been put out badly and was still thinking about coming back.

Xanthe stood in the doorway of Maren's study with their boots still crusted in pass-mud and waited to be acknowledged. That was the ritual. You didn't speak first with Elder Fairweather. You stood, and you let her finish whatever she was pretending to read, and you understood that every second she made you wait was a second she owned.

She made them wait long enough to count the cracks in the ceiling plaster. There were more than there had been last winter. The whole hall was coming apart by inches, and nobody had coin to fix it, because nobody had cargo worth carrying since the Crown started taxing the Thornfell routes into ruin.

"You found the seals," Maren said at last. She didn't look up from the ledger.

"Every pass. Greymouth, Hollowmere, the low ford at Ashen Bridge. Chains and Crown wardens, like they'd been expecting trouble." Xanthe crossed the room and dropped the confiscated travel writ on her desk—the one they'd lifted off a warden's tent stake before slipping out through the drainage cut at Hollowmere. "This is the part I don't like. It's not a tax seal. It's not even a customs order. It's stamped *by order of the Office of God-Hunters*."

That got her attention. Maren looked up, and for half a heartbeat Xanthe saw something move behind her eyes that they'd never seen there before. Not surprise. Recognition.

"God-hunters don't close roads," Xanthe said. "They hunt people. So either the Crown thinks someone's hiding in my mountains, or—"

"Or nothing." Maren took the writ, read it once, and fed it to the candle on her desk. Xanthe watched three months of smuggling fees go up in paper and smoke. "You'll take no more runs through the low passes. Too many eyes."

"The low passes are the only ones that pay."

"The high pass pays better." She said it flatly, the way she said everything, as if the words had been weighed and stamped before she spoke them. "You have a client. One traveler, one crate, sealed instructions. High road over the Widow's Shoulder, down past the old shrine, delivered to no one—you leave it where the marker stone stands and walk away."

"Past the old shrine." Xanthe leaned against the doorframe and folded their arms. "Nobody ships anything past the shrine. The shrine's been dead for sixty years. Even the crows stopped nesting there."

"Then it will be quiet work." Maren returned to her ledger, which meant the conversation was over, which meant it hadn't been a conversation at all.

Xanthe didn't move. "Gran used to say the guild never carried what we couldn't name. First rule she taught me. Know your cargo or refuse the run."

"Your gran also taught you to pick locks before you could write your own name, and look how much good her rules did her." Maren's pen scratched. Stopped. "It's one crate, Xanthe. One traveler. You want the guild to eat through winter? Take the run."

There it was. The guild's hunger, laid on the table like a knife—not a threat, never a threat, just a fact, sharp and patient. The Fairweathers had run these mountains for nine generations, and nine generations of cleverness had come down to this: an elder counting coins in a rotting hall, an apprentice who hadn't been paid in two months, and a client willing to pay triple rates for a road nobody else would walk.

Xanthe should have said yes and walked out. That was the obedient thing. That was the thing the guild raised them to do.

Instead they heard themselves ask: "What's the Ember?"

Maren's pen went very still.

Xanthe had been turning the word over all night, ever since Hollowmere, ever since they'd crouched in the drainage cut listening to two wardens talk in low voices about *the ember shipment* and *the priest* and *if he reaches the mountains we're all damned*. They'd assumed it was contraband code. Coal, maybe, or dragonglass, or some alchemist's powder. But then they'd gotten home and gone digging in the guild's oldest records—the ones Maren kept locked, the ones Xanthe had been picking open since they were twelve—and found something stranger.

"I read the founding oath," Xanthe said. "The real one, not the pretty version we recite at initiations. The one from before the Fairweathers were elders. It says: *We keep the roads between the hearth-fires. We carry what the Ember requires. We do not ask what burns.*" They watched Maren's face the way they watched a lock—looking for the give. "That's not smuggler talk, Gran-Maren. Smugglers ask exactly what burns. That's *priest* talk. Or it used to be."

For a long moment the only sound was the candle eating the air.

Then Maren closed the ledger, rose, and crossed to the far wall—the one with the tapestry of the first crossing, faded to ghosts of red and gold. She pulled the tapestry aside with one hand, revealing the old iron strongbox set into the stone, and Xanthe felt their stomach drop, because in twenty-two years they had never once seen that box opened.

"You were going to tell me eventually," Maren said. It wasn't a question. "I've known since you were fourteen and I caught you reading the deep ledgers. I told myself I'd tell you when you were elder. Then I told myself when the roads closed. Then I told myself there was time." She turned the key she wore on the chain around her neck—a key Xanthe had assumed was sentimental, a keepsake, a grandmother's trinket. "There isn't time anymore. Sit down."

Xanthe sat.

Maren opened the box and lifted out a bundle of oilcloth, unwrapping it with the care of someone handling a wound. Inside lay papers—old ones, browned and brittle, tied with cord gone soft as string—and beneath them, a scrap of metal no bigger than a thumbnail. It should have been rusted black after all these years. Instead it glowed. Faintly, sullenly, like a coal buried under ash that refused to die.

Xanthe's skin prickled. Every hair on their arms stood up, and the room seemed suddenly smaller, closer, as if the walls had leaned in to listen.

"That's not possible," Xanthe heard themselves say. "Metal doesn't stay hot for—"

"It isn't hot. Touch it."

"I'd rather not."

"*Touch it.*"

Xanthe touched it. And it wasn't warm at all—that was the mad thing. It was cool as river stone, and yet the glow didn't fade, and somewhere behind their sternum, in the place where fear lives, something answered it. A hum. Not a sound. More like remembering a sound. Like hearing your name called from another room in a house you thought was empty.

They snatched their hand back. "What is it?"

"A piece of the Emberforge," Maren said. "Or so my grandmother claimed, and her mother before her, back to the one who broke it off and ran." She sat down across from Xanthe, and for the first time in Xanthe's life, Elder Fairweather looked old—not weathered, not hard, but *old*, worn through like a boot sole. "You know the stories we tell the new ones. Gods walked the mountains once. Vaelis of the ember-heart, and his kin, and the covenant that bound them to us and us to them. You know we tell those stories as bedtime nonsense."

"They are bedtime nonsense."

"They were terms of a contract." Maren tapped the brittle papers. "The covenant wasn't a myth, child. It was a bargain. The gods kept the mountain roads safe, kept the winters gentle, kept the ember of the world burning so the land stayed green. In return, mortals kept faith with them—tended their shrines, spoke their names, chose them, freely, generation after generation. The covenant ran on choice. That was its whole engine. Nobody commanded it. Nobody enforced it. People simply had to keep *wanting* it."

"And people stopped."

"People stopped." Maren's mouth twisted. "Slowly. Comfortably. The Crown built temples to its own order, and the shrines went untended, and the names went unspoken, and each generation cared a little less. The gods grew quiet. Everyone called it peace." Her eyes went to the glowing shard. "But a covenant doesn't die quietly. It frays. And when it frays all the way through—" She stopped herself, jaw tightening, as if she'd already said too much.

"When it frays all the way through, what?"

"Winter solstice," Maren said. "That's what the old papers say. The realms were joined at the forge and they can be severed at the forge. If no one renews the covenant before the solstice, the gods are cut loose from this world entirely. The mountains go cold. The roads go wild. Everything the guild has ever survived on—" she gestured at the rotting hall around them, the cracked plaster, the empty coffers, "—everything, gone. Permanently. Not a bad season. An ending."

The hum behind Xanthe's sternum hadn't stopped. It had settled in, patient as debt. "And the god-hunters?"

"My grandmother's doing. Hers and the guild's." Maren said it without flinching, and somehow that was worse than if she'd wept. "Sixty years ago the Crown came asking questions about the old faith. Who still tended the shrines. Where the faithful hid. My grandmother sold them the answers. Names, routes, the locations of every working shrine in the Thornfells. In exchange, the guild got its charters, its exemptions, its protected roads. We became the Crown's smugglers instead of the gods' keepers, and we ate well for sixty years while the covenant starved." She met Xanthe's eyes. "That's what the Fairweather fortune is built on. Now you know."

Xanthe sat with it. The room was very quiet. Somewhere below, the hall's ancient timbers ticked and groaned, settling, settling.

"Why tell me now?"

"Because the client I'm giving you is a priest." Maren wrapped the shard back in its oilcloth with steady hands. "One of Vaelis's. The last one, near enough—the rest of his order burned in the spring, south of here, and the Crown has been hunting survivors since. He's paying us to get him over the Widow's Shoulder to the high country. And whether you believe the old papers or not, the god-hunters believe them, because they've sealed every pass in the range looking for him." She pushed the bundle across the desk toward Xanthe. "Which means the story's true, Xanthe. The Crown doesn't spend armies chasing bedtime nonsense."

Xanthe stared at the oilcloth bundle. At the faint glow bleeding through the cloth like a lamp behind a curtain. Their hands wanted to reach for it again, and they hated that, and they reached anyway—

"No," Maren said sharply. "The shard stays with me. You carry the man. Nothing else."

"Why does the shard hum when I touch it?" The question came out before Xanthe could weigh it, raw and too honest, and they saw Maren register that honesty with something like alarm.

"I don't know," Maren said. Which was the first true lie Xanthe had ever caught her telling, because Maren Fairweather knew everything that happened inside these walls, and her eyes had flicked—just barely, just for a breath—to the founding oath still lying open on the desk, to the line Xanthe had quoted: *we carry what the Ember requires.*

*We do not ask what burns.*

"Take the run," Maren said, rising, done. "Client arrives at the Drover's Rest waystation tomorrow at dusk. Travel dark, travel alone, trust no one on the road—not wardens, not shepherds, not other guild runners. Deliver him past the shrine and come home." She paused at the window, silhouetted against the grey morning light, and for a moment she looked like she might say something else. Something that mattered.

She said: "Wear your hood up. The hunters have descriptions."

* * *

Xanthe spent the day preparing the way they always did—checking rope, waxing boots, counting rations twice—and tried not to think about the humming. They failed.

By late afternoon they'd given up on not-thinking and gone down into the guild's record cellar instead, ostensibly to pull route maps for the Widow's Shoulder. Really to think where no one could see their face.

The cellar was a tomb of the guild's whole history: ledgers stacked to the ceiling, route scrolls, confiscation logs, nine generations of bargains written down in ink that had outlived everyone who'd signed them. Xanthe lit a lamp, found the maps they needed, and then—because their hands apparently had opinions of their own—pulled the founding ledger again. The real oath. The ugly, beautiful, impossible one.

*We keep the roads between the hearth-fires. We carry what the Ember requires. We do not ask what burns.*

Below the oath, in the same ancient hand, someone had written a line Xanthe had missed the night before, small and cramped, squeezed against the margin like it had been added in secret:

*When the Ember calls a keeper, the roads will know them, and the roads will choose.*

Xanthe read it four times. The lamp flame leaned in the draft from the stairs, and the shadows swung, and the hum behind their sternum stirred like something rolling over in its sleep.

"Roads choosing people," they muttered. "Nonsense." But their voice echoed strangely in the cellar, off stone that seemed almost to listen, and they gathered the maps and climbed back into the daylight faster than dignity allowed.

* * *

They left at dusk, as ordered, alone, as ordered, hood up, as ordered. The Drover's Rest waystation sat two hours' hard walk north, a squat stone inn hunched under the first shoulders of the high country, its windows the only light in a valley going blue with evening.

Xanthe arrived early, as they always did, and watched the waystation from the treeline, as they always did. Habit was the only inheritance that had never lied to them.

The inn looked ordinary. Smoke from the chimney. A carter's wagon in the yard. A dog asleep by the door. Ordinary, ordinary, ordinary—except that Xanthe counted four horses too many in the stable yard, and none of them wore guild brands, and one of them was shod in the matched pattern the Crown's post riders used.

Their gut tightened. They were still deciding whether to walk in anyway when the waystation's side door opened and a figure stepped out into the dusk—tall, robed in travel-stained grey, moving carefully, like a man whose hands hurt him. Even at fifty paces, even in the failing light, Xanthe could see the bandages wrapped around both of them, from palm to forearm, clumsy and blood-spotted.

The man paused in the yard. Lifted his head. And looked directly at the treeline—at the exact shadow where Xanthe stood—as though he'd known precisely where to look.

Behind him, inside the inn, a chair scraped. Voices. The dog began to bark.

The man in grey started walking toward the trees, unhurried, bandaged hands held slightly away from his body, and Xanthe understood with sick, total clarity that the horses in the yard weren't the client's escort.

They were the trap. And the client was walking straight out of it toward them, trusting a smuggler he'd never met to do the one thing the Fairweather guild had sworn, nine generations ago, it would always do.

*We keep the roads between the hearth-fires.*

"Oh, hell," Xanthe breathed, and stepped out of the shadows.
