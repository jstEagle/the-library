## 3. A Priest in the Cargo Hold

The client arrived an hour before dawn, and he arrived wrong.

Xanthe knew every kind of traveler the Thornfells produced. Wool merchants with soft hands and softer spines. Pilgrims who'd paid their last coin for a blessing and smelled of incense and desperation. Soldiers traveling light because they traveled to kill. Xanthe had smuggled all of them at one time or another, folded into false bottoms and mislabeled crates, and none of them—not one—had ever made the hair on their arms stand up before they'd said a word.

This man did.

He came through the waystation's back door the way water comes through a cracked hull: quietly, completely, filling the room before you noticed. He was tall, but stooped, like a man who'd learned that height was a target. His cloak was road-gray and rain-stiff. And his hands—

His hands were ruined.

Xanthe saw them when he set his pack down on the table, careful as a man laying down a sleeping child. The left was a map of old burns, shiny skin pulled tight over knuckles that no longer bent quite right. The right was worse: fresh burns, blistered and weeping, wrapped in linen gone brown at the edges.

"Fairweather," he said. Not a question. Maren's message had been specific, then. *One client. High pass. No names.*

"No names," Xanthe agreed. "That's the arrangement."

"I'm paying for the arrangement." He drew out a purse and set it on the table, and the sound it made was heavier than coin alone should make. Xanthe didn't touch it. Old rule: never count money in front of a stranger, because counting tells them what you value.

"You're hurt," Xanthe said instead.

"It looks worse than it is."

"That's what everyone says right before it gets worse."

Something moved behind his eyes—not quite a smile, not quite a flinch. "You're younger than I expected."

"You're more burned than I expected. We're both disappointed. Sit. Eat. We leave when the sky goes gray, not before. The high pass eats people who travel by starlight this time of year."

He sat. He ate little. And through the whole meal, in the waystation's single smoky room, he kept his burned right hand curled around something inside his coat like a man guarding a wound—or a secret.

Xanthe had been raised on secrets the way other children were raised on porridge. They knew the taste of one being kept close. This man was keeping his against his heart.

* * *

The cargo hold of the mule train was really just three wagons' worth of false bottoms, tarped bales, and a lifetime of Xanthe's grandmother's engineering, but it had held everything from Crown-taxed brandy to a fugitive tax assessor, so Xanthe figured it could hold whatever this man was.

They loaded him at first light, between a shipment of salted pork and a crate marked *loom parts* that contained no loom parts whatsoever. He went into the space beneath the middle wagon's floor without complaint, folding himself into a gap built for contraband, not a grown man, and if it hurt—and it must have, with those hands—he made no sound.

"Rules," Xanthe said, crouched at the hatch. "You don't knock, you don't cough, you don't pray. If someone opens that panel, you're a crate of candlewax and you'll be treated accordingly. Understood?"

"Understood."

"One more thing." Xanthe hesitated. It wasn't in the contract. It wasn't smart. They asked anyway. "What am I carrying?"

Silence from the dark. Then, quietly: "A dead man."

"Dead men don't pay in advance."

"This one's in a hurry to settle his accounts." A pause. "And he's carrying something that isn't his. Something he has to get home."

Xanthe closed the hatch and latched it, and stood there a moment with their palm flat against the wagon's side, feeling the grain of the wood, telling themselves the unease crawling up their spine was just the weather turning.

It wasn't the weather. The weather had been turning for weeks. Ever since the passes sealed. Ever since Maren started drinking her tea cold because she forgot it steeping, staring at nothing, saying *the Ember, the Ember* under her breath like a prayer she'd forgotten how to finish.

Xanthe shook it off, whistled the mules forward, and took the high road north.

* * *

Trouble found them at midday, the way trouble does: wearing a uniform and asking polite questions.

There were two of them at the toll bridge over the Kestrel Water, where the high road narrowed to a stone span barely wide enough for one wagon. Crown soldiers—but not garrison troops. These wore charcoal-and-ash livery, and on their shoulders rode badges Xanthe had only seen once before, years ago, on a man whose body had later been fished out of the Thornfell river with his tongue cut out.

God-hunters.

Xanthe had heard the stories like everyone else. The Order of the Ashen Seal. Hunters of heresy, of unlicensed miracles, of the old gods waking where old gods had no business waking. The Crown had spent two generations teaching the mountains that Vaelis and his kin were stories, and now, apparently, the Crown was spending real soldiers making sure the stories stayed buried.

"Halt," said the taller one. Young. Nervous. The nervous ones were dangerous. "Cargo inspection. By order of the Lord High Hunter."

Xanthe leaned back against the wagon rail and did the arithmetic they'd been doing since childhood: what's in the open, what's hidden, what can be bought, what can't. The pork was legal. The salt was legal. The loom parts were technically legal until opened. And beneath the floorboards of the middle wagon lay a burned man who called himself a dead one.

"Inspection costs time," Xanthe said. "Time costs money. Who pays for my delay?"

"The Crown compensates loyal subjects."

"Loyal subjects don't run cargo through sealed passes." Xanthe smiled the Fairweather smile, the one that had gotten three generations of smugglers out of trouble and one of them into a marriage. "Which makes me either very stupid or very well connected. You want to guess which?"

The young hunter's hand drifted toward his sword. The older one—a woman, gray at the temples, with a crossbow slung across her back and eyes like a winter river—put a hand on his arm.

"Papers," she said.

Xanthe passed them over. Forged, of course, but forged by Brannoc's cousin in Deephollow, who charged triple and earned it. The woman read them twice, slowly, and Xanthe watched her eyes and thought about the weight beneath the floorboards and whether a burned man could hold still long enough to be candlewax.

Then the woman looked up—not at Xanthe, past Xanthe—at the middle wagon. And she went very still, in a way that Xanthe recognized, because it was the same stillness Maren got when she spoke of the Ember.

"You're carrying livestock?" she asked.

"Not this trip."

"Something's breathing in there."

"Mules breathe. Wagons creak. Mountains groan." Xanthe shrugged. "The Thornfells are full of noises, huntress. That's why the Crown pays people like me to know them and people like you to chase them."

She held Xanthe's gaze for a long moment. Her hand rose—toward the wagon, toward the tarp, toward the truth—and then, somewhere down the valley, a bell rang.

Not a church bell. Not a warning bell. Something older, thinner, a sound like a struck crystal heard through three feet of stone, and Xanthe felt it in their teeth more than their ears. The gray-haired hunter felt it too; Xanthe saw her flinch, saw her go pale, saw her hand stop moving.

"What was that?" the young one whispered.

"Rockfall," said the woman, but she didn't believe it, and neither did Xanthe, and neither, Xanthe would have bet anything, did the man pretending to be candlewax six feet away.

The woman stepped back. "Move along. Keep your papers visible at the next checkpoint."

Xanthe clicked the mules forward and did not breathe again until the bridge was a mile behind them.

* * *

They made camp that night in a hollow below the treeline, and Xanthe waited until the fire was low and the stars were out before opening the hatch.

The man climbed out stiffly, hands cradled, and sat by the fire without being asked. Up close, in honest light, he looked worse than he had at the waystation. There were shadows under his eyes like bruises, and burn scars crawling up past his collar, disappearing somewhere Xanthe decided not to imagine.

"You breathed," Xanthe said. "At the bridge. She heard you."

"I was praying."

"I told you not to pray."

"I know." He looked into the fire. "I'm not very good at obeying. It's why I'm a fugitive."

"Are you going to tell me what you are? Because I've carried heretics, deserters, and one very apologetic bigamist, and you don't move like any of them. You move like a man walking to his own funeral and trying to enjoy the scenery."

That almost-laugh again, there and gone. "My name is Rafael. I was a priest of Vaelis—the Ember, the hearth-god, the one your Crown says never existed. Two weeks ago, my order was slaughtered. All of them. Forty-one souls in the sanctuary at Cinderhold, and I lived because I was in the crypt fetching this." He drew his burned hand from his coat, and opened it.

Xanthe had seen a lot of things glint in firelight. Gold. Steel. Poison vials. What sat in Rafael's palm was none of those things. It was a shard of something like glass and something like coal, the size of a fist, and it was *warm*. Xanthe knew it was warm without touching it, the way you know a hearth is lit in a house you're passing on a cold night. Light moved inside it, slow, like breathing.

"They say it's a piece of the god," Rafael said. "His heart, or a piece of it. The hunters want it destroyed. My order died keeping it safe. And I—" his voice caught, steadied, "—I have to carry it to the Emberforge before the solstice, or everything my order believed dies with it."

Xanthe stared at the shard. And the shard, Xanthe would swear until their dying day, stared back.

Every instinct the guild had ever bred into them said one thing: *put it down, walk away, take the coin, forget the face.* Cargo was cargo. Dead men were dead men. Gods were stories the Crown taxed out of existence a generation ago.

But the shard pulsed, once, like a heartbeat heard through a wall. And Xanthe thought of Maren whispering *the Ember, the Ember*, and of the bell that rang through solid rock, and of the gray-haired hunter who'd reached for the wagon and stopped as if something had told her to.

"How much of the payment is left?" Xanthe asked.

Rafael blinked. "Half. Due at delivery."

"Keep it." Xanthe stood, kicked dirt over the fire, and started re-harnessing the mules in the dark. "New terms. I don't deliver cargo to the Emberforge. I deliver *you*. Alive, intact, and praying somewhere I can't hear it. And when it's done, you tell me everything—what the covenant is, why the passes sealed, and why a priest's corpse smells like my family's oldest oath. Deal?"

Rafael rose slowly, the shard clutched to his chest, looking at Xanthe like they'd spoken a language he'd only read in dead books. "Why?" he asked. "You don't know me. You don't know what this costs."

"No," Xanthe said, tightening the last strap. "But I know a sealed road when I'm standing on one, and I know the difference between cargo that's worth coin and cargo that's worth something else. Get in the wagon, priest. Dawn's in four hours and the Emberforge is a long way off."

Behind them, far down the valley, faint as memory, the bell rang again.

And this time, Xanthe could have sworn it rang *closer*.
