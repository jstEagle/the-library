## 4. Hunters on the Scree

The scree field began where the trees gave up, a gray ocean of broken stone sloping down from the pass like something the mountain had vomited and then regretted. Rafael had crossed worse ground in his life—pilgrims always did—but never at a dead run, and never with a smuggler shouting at him to move faster as if prayer were a thing you could sprint.

"Left!" Xanthe Fairweather barked, twenty feet ahead, moving through the loose rock like they'd been born on it. "Stay in my tracks. The stones remember weight."

*The stones remember weight.* He'd have laughed, once, at smuggler superstition. Three days ago he'd have laughed at a lot of things.

He ran. His boots found Xanthe's prints and he trusted them the way he'd been taught to trust scripture—completely, and without evidence. Behind them, maybe half a mile back and closing, came the sound he'd been hearing since the waystation: the horns. Two notes, flat and deliberate, repeated every few minutes. The Crown's god-hunters didn't shout or whoop. They let the horns do the talking. *We know where you are. We will always know where you are.*

His burned hand throbbed inside its wrappings. Against his chest, wrapped in oilcloth and bound to his ribs with a strip of altar cloth, the ember shard pulsed—slow, patient, warm as a sleeping bird. It had been warm for six days now. It had been cold for eleven years before that.

*They're answering prayers again,* he thought, and the thought still hit him like cold water, even now, even running for his life because of it. *They're answering, and my order is dead because we were the first to hear it.*

A stone shifted under his heel and he went down hard, palms slapping rock, the shard jolting against his sternum. Xanthe was back at his side before he'd finished gasping—not touching him, just crouched, eyes flicking between his face and the slope behind them.

"Breathe," they said. "Thirty seconds. Then we move."

"I'm sorry," Rafael said, because it seemed important to say it while he could. "You should understand what you're carrying me toward. What I've already cost you."

Xanthe's expression did something complicated. "You're costing me time. Save the confessions for flat ground."

But there was nowhere flat left in the world, Rafael thought. Not anymore. The world had tilted six days ago in the chapter house at Vaelmoor, when Brother Aldous had come stumbling into the scriptorium with blood on his vestments and joy on his face, of all things—joy—and said, *Rafael, come quickly, it answered. The candle-prayer. It answered.*

He'd gone. Of course he'd gone. And he'd stood in the reliquary and watched a votive flame bend against the draft, bending *toward* the kneeling novice who'd prayed over it, and the ember shard in its case—the relic they'd all been taught was dead, a fossil of a dead faith, kept out of habit rather than hope—that shard had glowed. Faintly. Like an eye opening underwater.

And Aldous had wept, and old Sister Imre had begun reciting the Litany of Return with her voice shaking, and Rafael had felt something in his chest crack open that he hadn't known was closed, and none of them had known—none of them could have known—that the Crown's hunters were already two days' ride away, that someone in the capital had read an old report, an old ledger, an old secret, and understood exactly what a glowing relic meant: *the covenant is failing, and the gods are waking, and both must be stopped.*

The horns sounded again. Closer.

"Time," Xanthe said, and pulled him up by the good hand.

* * *

They ran the scree for an hour, maybe more, dropping from stone shelf to stone shelf with the whole gray slope sliding gently beneath them, a slow avalanche that never quite committed. Twice Rafael heard the hunters' dogs—no, not dogs; he corrected himself grimly; hounds bred on ash and consecrated iron, animals that could smell a relic the way other hounds smelled fear. Twice Xanthe changed their line sharply, cutting across the fall-line, muttering about scent and wind and how the mountain owed them one favor today.

The third time, Xanthe stopped behind a spine of granite the size of a chapel and said, "All right. Now you confess."

Rafael leaned against the stone and tried to get his breath back. Below them the scree ran down into a white riverbed, dry this season, and beyond it the next ridge rose dark with pine. Somewhere on the slope above, the horns had gone quiet, which was somehow worse than the noise.

"You want the whole of it?"

"I want the part that explains why the Crown sealed every pass in the Thornfells overnight to stop one priest." Xanthe's eyes were sharp and unimpressed, but they'd also, Rafael noticed, put themselves downhill of him, between him and the pursuit. They'd done it without seeming to think about it. "Start there."

So he told it. The candle that bent. The shard that woke. Aldous's joy. Sister Imre's litany. And then the riders, and the fire, and the way the chapter house had burned so fast it must have been set in three places at once, and how he'd survived only because Aldous had shoved the shard-case into his hands and pushed him down the well-shaft with words he would carry until he died:

*"It answers because it was never allowed to refuse us. That's why they're afraid. Go north. Find the Emberforge. Tell the mountain its priest is coming home."*

"The mountain," Xanthe repeated flatly.

"That's what he said."

"And the Crown slaughtered your entire order—how many?—over a candle trick."

"Forty-one." The number sat in his mouth like a stone. "Forty-two, counting me. And it wasn't a candle trick." He worked the oilcloth loose just enough to show her—a glimpse, no more—the shard nested in its wrappings, glowing now even in daylight, a coal-colored light that moved like breath. "Eleven years ago the gods went silent. Every prayer unanswered, every oracle dark, every miracle dried up across the nine realms. The Church called it the Quiet and pretended it was doctrine. But my order kept the old records. We knew the covenant required *exchange*—gods give, mortals give back—and we knew the exchange had stopped. We thought the gods had withdrawn in anger." He swallowed. "We were wrong. They withdrew because they were *dying*. And now they're waking again, and the Crown knows that when the old gods wake fully, the covenant either renews or severs, and if it severs—" he gestured at the sky, the stone, the thin cold air, everything— "every blessing, every ward, every binding the mortal realms run on comes undone. The Crown doesn't want to renew it. The Crown wants the severance. Gods gone. Crown supreme. No power left in the world that doesn't answer to a throne."

Xanthe stared at the shard for a long moment. When they spoke again, their voice had lost its edge and gained something quieter and more dangerous.

"My guild has carried cargo through these mountains for four generations. We have a saying: *know what you carry, because the mountain does.*" They looked up. "I asked Elder Maren what the client was. She said, 'a pilgrim.' She said, 'don't ask twice.'" A muscle worked in their jaw. "She knew. She sent me up this road knowing."

"I'm sorry."

"Don't apologize yet." Xanthe rose, scanning the upper slope. "Apologize if we live. Move."

* * *

The hunters caught sight of them midafternoon, on the long open shoulder below the ridge crest, and after that there was no more hiding—only distance, and the spending of it.

Rafael saw them clearly for the first time as they flitted between boulders above: six figures in slate-gray, moving with the unhurried confidence of people who had never once needed to hurry. Crossbows. Ash-hounds straining at silver chains. And at their center a tall figure with a white sash who walked the scree as though it were a ballroom floor.

"Sera Vane," Rafael breathed, before he could stop himself.

"You know her?"

"She trained me." The admission came out of him raw. "Before I took orders. We were novices together at Vaelmoor—she left for the Hunt two years ago. Said she wanted to serve the gods by protecting people *from* them." He watched the white-sashed figure pause atop a boulder, raise a crossbow, track them with a leisure that felt almost personal. "She's the best shot in the nine realms. If she fires, one of us falls."

"Then let's not be interesting," Xanthe said, and cut hard left.

The bolt came anyway. Rafael heard it pass—a sound like a large bird deciding something—and then a second bolt, and neither touched them, and some animal part of his brain registered what his conscious mind refused to: both shots had missed by the same precise margin. A hand's width left. Both times. The best shot in the nine realms did not miss twice by accident.

He had no time to think about it. The slope steepened, the scree turned to true climbing, and Xanthe hauled him up a chimney of rock with hands that were smaller than his but far more certain, and then they were over the shoulder and descending into a fold of the mountain where the wind died and the light went strange and gold, and the horns, for the moment, fell silent.

Xanthe called a halt in the lee of a fallen slab. Rafael slid down against it and discovered his legs had opinions. The shard against his chest pulsed faster now—he could feel it quickening like a heart asked to keep pace—and he pressed his palm over it and whispered the old steadying words without thinking, the ones novices used on frightened pilgrims, and felt absurd doing it, and did it anyway.

"You prayed," Xanthe said. They'd been watching him. "Just now. To it."

"To *him*. Through it." Rafael shrugged helplessly. "Habit."

"Did it answer?"

And here was the thing he had not told anyone, the thing sitting in him like a swallowed coal: *yes.* Not in words. Never in words. But when he prayed over the shard, warmth spread down his burned hand and the burning eased, and last night, freezing in the high pass, the warmth had reached his chest, his throat, and he had slept without shivering while Xanthe took both watches. He was being carried. He was being *kept*, the way a lamp is kept, and he did not know whether that made him chosen or fuel.

"It answered," he said quietly. "That's why I'm alive. That's why any of this is happening. Six days ago I was a copyist. I have ink stains older than my vocation." He laughed, and it came out wrong. "Now I'm the last priest of Vaelis, hauling a god's heart through the mountains with a stranger the elder sold to me, hunted by my oldest friend, and I don't—I don't even know if I'm a prophet or a coward. Prophets are supposed to *lead*. Cowards at least admit they're running. I'm doing both and calling it faith."

Xanthe was quiet for a moment. The gold light moved on the stone around them.

"For what it's worth," they said at last, "in my trade we don't ask if the courier is holy. We ask if the courier delivers." They stood, rolled their shoulders, and offered him their hand—the same brisk, unsentimental gesture as before, and somehow, this time, it landed differently. "You haven't dropped the package yet. Come on. There's a tunnel mouth past the ridge Brannoc uses—stone-singer roads, under the mountain. Hunters don't go underground. They hate what they can't burn."

"A tunnel," Rafael repeated, letting himself be pulled up. "Into the mountain."

"You have a problem with mountains, priest?"

"No," Rafael said, and touched the shard through the oilcloth, and felt it pulse—once, twice, unmistakably, like something answering a question no one had asked aloud. "I think the mountain has been waiting for me."

Above the ridge, faint but clear, the horns began again—and this time there were more of them.
