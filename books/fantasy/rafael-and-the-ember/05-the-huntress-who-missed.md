## 5. The Huntress Who Missed

Sera Vane had killed forty-one people in eleven years of service to the Crown, and she could have named every one of them. Not their names—she'd never been given those, only descriptions and crimes—but the small details that made each kill its own. The way a smuggler in Duncarrow had paused to retie his bootlace before running. The old woman in the fen country who'd held her rosary of river-glass so tightly the beads had cut her palm bloody. The boy at the Ashford chapel who couldn't have been more than sixteen and who had died reaching for something on the floor that Sera still thought about at night.

She remembered them because High Hunter Corvane said remembering was weakness, and Sera Vane had spent her whole life being told what she was and deciding to be the opposite.

Now she lay on her belly at the crest of the scree field above Thornfell's eastern pass, cheek against cold stone, the wind worrying at the fringe of her hood, and watched a burned-handed priest climb for his life through the rocks below with a stranger at his side.

The stranger was good. She noted it professionally, the way she noted everything: low center of gravity, sure-footed on the loose shale, choosing the line up the slope like someone who'd read mountains since before they could read words. A smuggler, then, or close enough. The guilds had thinned over the years but they hadn't vanished, and this one moved like the last ember of a fire that used to warm whole valleys.

The priest was less good. He climbed like a man whose body had been built for kneeling and had never quite forgiven him for standing. His bandaged hands slipped twice, and both times the smuggler caught his wrist without breaking stride, hauling him upward with the casual efficiency of someone doing a job.

*The cargo,* Sera thought. *That's all you are to them. Cargo.*

Something about that should have comforted her. It didn't.

She settled her cheek against the stock of her crossbow and sighted down the rail. Two hundred feet of falling air between her bolt and the priest's back. The wind was gusting left to right, quarter strength, nothing she hadn't compensated for a hundred times. The angle was clean. The light was clean. In eleven years she had never missed from a position this generous, and everyone in the Crown's god-hunting service knew it. *Vane doesn't miss.* It was on her commendation. It would be on her gravestone, if the Crown bothered with gravestones for people like her.

Her finger rested on the trigger lever, feather-light, the way Master Hesk had taught her when she was nine years old and still believed the Crown's work was holy.

And beneath her breastbone, where her heartbeat lived, the memory stirred again—the one that had been stirring for six days now, waking her in the watch-tents, walking beside her on the road, refusing to be buried no matter how much stone she piled on top of it.

*Ashford.*

Six days ago there had been a monastery in the hills above Ashford, and thirty-one brothers and sisters of the Order of the Rekindled Flame, and one night of fire and screaming that Sera had been ordered to witness and not prevent.

Witness. That was the word Corvane had used. *You will attend as an observer, Vane. This is policy, not slaughter. The covenant is dead. What these people practice is superstition at best and sedition at worst. You will observe and you will learn what your oath actually costs.*

She had stood in the courtyard while the Hearthguard—Crown men in grey lacquer, faceless behind their visors—had gone cell to cell with fire and steel. She had counted the bodies afterward because counting was the only thing they let her do, and because she'd thought, in some naive corner of herself, that if she made herself look at every single one, she would understand why.

Body nineteen had changed everything.

A brother named Aldous—that was written in the margin of the psalter he'd died holding—a man so old his spine had curled like a question mark, found kneeling in the chapel ruins with his arms raised toward the smoke-blackened rafters. And in the rafters, impossibly, where the fire should have consumed everything and consumed it thoroughly, one beam still burned. Not smoldering. *Burning.* With flame the color of banked coals shot through with gold, burning without fuel, without smoke, without end, in a building that had been drenched in accelerant and given three hours to die.

Sera had climbed up to it. She still didn't know why. Her hand had passed within a foot of the flame and felt warmth—not the greedy tearing heat of ordinary fire but something patient, something *attentive*, the way a hearth is attentive, the way a mother watching a sleeping child is attentive—and the flame had leaned toward her fingers. Leaned. Like a cat. Like recognition.

Then Corvane had called her name, and when she turned back the beam was only char, and she had spent six days telling herself she'd imagined it, and six nights failing to believe it.

*The gods are dead.* That was the first truth of her service, inked into her induction oath before she was old enough to question it. *The covenant failed a generation ago. The Ember went dark. What remains is cleanup.*

But dead things did not lean toward you. Dead things did not burn without fuel in a ruined chapel while grey-lacquered men sang hymns to the Crown below. And dead gods certainly did not send fugitive priests stumbling through the Thornfells carrying whatever it was the Hearthguard wanted badly enough to seal four mountain passes overnight—an act of logistics so enormous it had taken the Crown twenty years to stop pretending it couldn't be done.

Below her, the priest reached a shelf of rock and stopped, doubling over, and the smuggler crouched beside him, and even at two hundred feet Sera saw the moment: the priest saying something urgent, the smuggler shaking their head, the priest pressing on anyway. Arguing. About what? About turning back? About whether the stranger should abandon him?

*They'll leave you,* she thought at the smuggler, with something that wasn't quite contempt and wasn't quite envy. *That's how it goes. Everyone leaves. The Crown pays better than loyalty does, and the Crown always collects.*

It was what she'd told herself at seventeen, when her father had drunk away the farm and the recruiters had come through offering bread, purpose, a uniform that meant something. *Everyone leaves. Take the wage. Be the one who does the leaving.* She'd been so proud of that arithmetic once. Lately it refused to balance.

The priest started climbing again. The smuggler followed.

Sera exhaled slowly and put the priest's back in the notch of her sight.

*One bolt,* she thought. *One clean bolt, and it's done. The passes reopen. Corvane stops looking at me like I'm a problem to be managed. The order stands restored, the ledger balances, and the world goes back to being the simple place I agreed to keep it.*

Her finger tightened on the lever.

The wind gusted. She adjusted without thinking, ten years of craft moving her body for her, and in the space of that adjustment her eyes crossed the smuggler—still crouched, still waiting, one hand raised behind the priest like a shield that wouldn't stop anything but was offered anyway—

—and the image slid sideways into another image, unbidden: her own hand raised behind a recruit named Tamsin during the border action at Coldmere, seven years ago, when the raiders came over the wall in the dark and Sera had stood over a terrified girl with a borrowed blade and shouted *get up, get UP* until she did, and Tamsin had lived, and Sera had gotten a commendation for it, and somewhere between then and now the woman who pulled strangers off the ground had become the woman who put bolts in their backs.

When?

Show her the moment. Show her the exact moment, and she'd take it back. She'd give back the commendation, the pension, the grey cloak, all of it, just to stand again in that courtyard at Coldmere knowing which direction she was going.

Her finger eased off the lever.

*No.*

The word arrived quietly, without drama, the way real decisions usually do. Not *I can't.* Not *I won't.* Just *no*, plain as a closed door, and behind the door a voice that sounded like her own at nine years old asking Master Hesk why the gods needed hunting if they were already dead, and Hesk's smile, and his answer: *Because dead things make poor masters, child, and living ones make worse.*

She had accepted that answer for twenty-two years.

The priest and the smuggler crested a ridge and dropped out of sight beyond it. Thirty seconds, maybe forty, before they came into view again on the far slope—if she fired now she'd lose them; if she waited she'd have a harder angle; if she signaled the second team in the pines to the south she'd have three crossbows converging and none of them hers to miss.

Sera Vane rolled onto her back and looked at the sky. Grey and vast and utterly indifferent, the kind of sky that had watched empires come and go and cared about none of them. Somewhere beyond it—or inside it, or under the bones of the world—something burned without fuel and leaned toward human hands.

*You're dead,* she told it silently. *Officially. On paper. Signed and sealed. So either you don't exist, or everything I am is a lie, and I have to pick.*

She sat up.

Then she did the thing that eleven years of service had trained her to do flawlessly and her conscience had never once authorized: she aimed her crossbow at the empty sky above the far ridge and fired, loud and clean, a bolt arcing up into nothing.

The signal shot. *Target sighted, engaging.* Every hunter in the pines to the south would hear it and read it and begin their approach—and begin it *slowly*, because Vane doesn't miss, Vane doesn't need help, Vane has it handled. Three minutes, maybe five, before anyone got close enough to see there was nothing to handle.

Three minutes was nothing. Three minutes was everything.

Sera slung the crossbow across her back, stood, and ran—not after the fugitives, but along the ridgeline, taking the high traverse at a dead sprint, vaulting the gaps, her boots finding the stones the way they always did, because her body belonged to her even if her oaths didn't. She cut the angle, dropped down a goat track she'd mapped on the approach, and came out on the trail ahead of them, breathing hard, crossbow leveled—at the ground, deliberately, muzzle down, hands visible.

The smuggler saw her first. They moved fast, putting themselves between her and the priest, and up close she could see them properly now: sharp-eyed, road-worn, younger than she'd expected, with the particular wariness of someone who'd learned young that every stranger was a bill waiting to come due.

"God-hunter," the smuggler said. Flat. Already calculating escape lines. She recognized the look; she wore it herself most mornings.

"Yes."

"You're supposed to shoot us."

"I'm aware."

The priest stepped forward, and this close she could see the burns on his hands disappearing under filthy bandages, and the shape under his coat where something was wrapped and bound and guarded like a newborn. He looked at her crossbow, then at her face, and whatever he saw there made him go very still. "You're the one," he said quietly. "From the courtyard. You counted us."

The bottom fell out of Sera's stomach.

"Nineteen," she said, before she could stop herself. "You were nineteenth. You were kneeling in the chapel." She shook her head. "No. No, you were—" But of course. Of course the old man with the question-mark spine had been praying *for* something, not just *to* it. Of course the beam had leaned. Of course the one survivor of Ashford had walked out of the fire and into the Thornfells with a god's heart under his coat, because that was what priests were *for*, and she had spent six days aiming at him without knowing it.

"I carried Brother Aldous out," Rafael said. "He was already gone. But the fire—" He stopped. Looked at her hands on the crossbow. "You saw it too."

"The beam." Her voice came out rough. "Burning without fuel. It leaned toward me."

"It leans toward everyone." He said it simply, without triumph, and somehow that was worse than triumph would have been. "That's what the Crown can't forgive. Not that the gods survived. That they *want* us. Still. After everything."

Down the slope, faint but unmistakable, came the sound of hunters moving through the pines—boots on stone, the jingle of gear, voices calling positions. Four minutes, maybe less. Sera had planned on three and the mountains had disagreed.

"Listen to me." She stepped closer, and the smuggler tensed, but she kept her hands where they were. "Second team is in the treeline below the ridge, six hunters, standard sweep pattern. I fired my signal shot; they think I have you handled. When they reach the clearing and find nothing, Corvane will know. And Corvane doesn't chase. Corvane *encircles*. There's a gorge east of here—the Widow's Tooth. If you cross at the old bridge and take the miner's stair on the far side, you're out of the net before it closes. Anywhere else, you're dead by tomorrow's dusk."

"And you?" Rafael asked.

Sera laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "I'm the one who missed. There's a procedure for that." She finally let the crossbow slide off her shoulder, held it out to the smuggler, stock first—an absurd gesture, a knight surrendering a sword to a highwayman, and she did it anyway. "Corvane will want to know why Vane doesn't miss. Let him wonder. Wonder buys you hours."

The smuggler took the weapon slowly, turning it over, reading her the way they'd read the mountain. "You're defecting."

"I'm *missing*," Sera said. "What I do after that is my own business." But her voice shook on *my own business*, because she had never once in her adult life had any such thing, and the size of the empty territory ahead of her was only now arriving, vast and cold and entirely unmapped.

Rafael touched her arm—gently, with a burned hand, careful as a man handling something already broken. "Hunter Vane. Whatever you believe you're leaving behind—the door isn't locked behind you. That's what I came to tell the world. The doors aren't locked."

She wanted to say something to that. She had a speech half-built, about duty and treason and how he shouldn't thank her, and it collapsed in her throat when the voices in the pines called out again, closer, and the smuggler snapped "*Move*" in a voice that had clearly commanded mules, mercenaries, and worse.

They ran. All three of them, down the switchbacks, the priest stumbling, the smuggler towing him, Sera taking point because she knew these slopes and they didn't—she led them off-trail through the scree, down the hidden ramp of the goat track, the gorge walls rising around them, the Widow's Tooth roaring somewhere below.

Behind them, from the ridgeline, a horn sounded. Long note. Short note. Long note.

*Contact lost. Encircle. Close the net.*

Corvane, wasting no time. Corvane, who had watched her count the bodies at Ashford and seen something in her face she'd thought she'd hidden. Corvane, who had assigned her to this hunt personally, and whom she had told herself it was coincidence.

Ahead, the old bridge came into view—rope and plank and rot, swaying over the white water, older than the Crown, older probably than the guilds. The smuggler skidded to a halt at the near anchor and looked back at her, and in their eyes was the question that Sera had been dodging since she fired her bolt into the sky:

*Are you coming, or are you buying us time?*

Both, she realized. Both, and she'd known it when she surrendered the crossbow. Because the net was closing, and someone had to hold the gap open, and she was the only one on this mountain who knew exactly how the Crown closed nets.

"Go," she said. "Cross. Cut the far anchor when you're over—I'll hold this side."

"You'll die," the smuggler said.

"Probably." She drew her belt knife and turned to face back up the trail, where the first grey figures were already ghosting between the trees. "But not today, and not for free. Go, *smuggler*. Every second you argue, I start charging you."

A pause. Then, grudging, almost admiring: "Fairweather. Xanthe Fairweather. Remember the name—you might want to collect on it someday."

And then they were on the bridge, priest and smuggler, out over the roaring water, and Sera Vane stood alone on the near bank with a knife in her hand and her whole life burning down behind her like the chapel at Ashford, and for the first time in twenty-two years she was not afraid of what was coming.

She was afraid of what came *after*—of the unmapped country of a self without an oath—but that fear could wait its turn.

The grey shapes broke from the treeline.

Sera rolled her shoulders, set her feet in the scree, and smiled at them with all her teeth.

"Well," she said. "Come and miss me."
