## 6. Stone That Sings

The mountain had a back door, and my grandmother's generation had bricked it shut.

That was the story, anyway—the one guild children learned with their letters. *When the roads close, the stone remembers.* A smuggler's proverb, meant to comfort. I'd always taken it for the kind of pretty lie old people tell to make the trade sound holy. Smuggling wasn't holy. It was arithmetic: weight against distance, distance against risk, risk against the price of silence at the toll gates.

Three days into the deep tunnels, I stopped believing the proverb was a lie.

* * *

We'd come down through the Hollow Stair behind the burned waystation—Rafael limping on a wrapped ankle, Sera moving like a woman who'd forgotten how to be a hunter and hadn't yet learned what to be instead, and me in front because someone had to be, and apparently that someone was always me.

The tunnels under Thornfell weren't mine. I knew the passes the way a shepherd knows his hills, but the deep ways belonged to another era entirely—vaulted galleries cut by hands that predated any map I'd ever stolen or sold. The Fairweathers had kept one route through them, marked in my grandmother's hand in a ledger I'd never been allowed to read, and I'd followed those marks down into the dark because every road above us now crawled with Crown hunters.

"Your family built this?" Rafael asked, running his bandaged fingers along a wall carved with spirals so old they'd worn shallow.

"My family *used* this. There's a difference." I held the lantern high. "Builders were somebody else. Somebody older."

"Older than the guild?"

"Older than crowns." I said it lightly. It didn't come out lightly.

Sera brought up the rear with her crossbow strung and loaded, which she'd done two hours ago and hadn't unstrung since. "You two want to save the theology for somewhere without an echo?" she said. "Sound carries. If anything is living down here, it already knows we're coming."

"Nothing lives down here," I said. "It's been dead for a hundred years."

I would remember saying that. I would remember being wrong.

* * *

It started as a sound I mistook for water.

Deep under the mountain there's a frequency you feel in your teeth before your ears find it—a hum below hearing, the kind that makes lantern flames shiver and dust lift off ledges no finger had touched in generations. Rafael heard it first. He stopped dead in the middle of the gallery, head cocked like a deer, and the look on his face made my stomach drop before my mind caught up.

"You hear that," he said. Not a question.

"Water," I said. "Underground river. Happens in karst."

"It's not water." His voice had gone strange and soft. "Xanthe, that's a chord."

"A what?"

"A chord. Three notes, held together. It's—" He pressed his palm flat against the tunnel wall, and I watched the color leave his face by lantern-light. "It's singing."

"Stone doesn't sing."

"I know what I felt at the massacre," he said quietly. "I know what answered me when I prayed over forty dead brothers. And I'm telling you the mountain is doing it again. Right now. Under our feet."

Sera had her crossbow up, aimed at nothing, aimed at everything. "Then we go around it."

"There's no around," I said. My grandmother's marks ran straight through the next gallery. Every alternate way I could half-remember from stories ended in collapse or worse. "There's only through."

"Of course there is," Sera muttered. "With you people there's always only through."

* * *

The singing got louder the deeper we went, which was backwards from every law of echo I knew. Sound should scatter. This gathered. By the time we reached the gallery my grandmother's ledger called the Long Choir, the hum had resolved into something unmistakably deliberate—three low notes braided together, rising and falling like breath.

And the walls were answering it.

Not metaphorically. As we walked, seams of pale light woke in the stone ahead of us and dimmed behind, tracing lines that might have been veins or roots or script in a language older than script. Rafael walked with both palms out, touching everything, weeping openly and not seeming to know he was doing it.

"This is a temple," he breathed. "This whole mountain—it's not a tunnel system. It's a throat. We're walking down the throat of something that's been asleep."

"Comforting," said Sera.

"No. No, you don't understand—" He turned, and the ember shard at his chest, the thing he'd carried out of his burning abbey wrapped in lead and prayer-cloth, was glowing through its wrappings like a coal remembering fire. "It's waking up *because of this.* Because I carried it here. The gods aren't gone. They went quiet because we stopped listening, and the moment someone carries a piece of their heart into the dark—"

"Rafael." I grabbed his sleeve. "Breathe. Preach later."

He laughed then, high and cracked, the laugh of a man standing at the edge of something enormous. "You think I'm preaching. Xanthe, I've spent my whole life praying into silence and calling it faith. For the first time, the silence is *praying back*, and you want me to breathe."

"Yes," I said. "Because if the mountain wants us dead, panicking just saves it the trouble."

But my hands were shaking too, and not only from fear. Because the shard's light had found me. Wherever I stood, the wall-seams brightened nearest to me first, waking at my approach like lamps lit by a servant who knew my habits. I told myself it was coincidence. I told myself it was Rafael; the glow followed the relic, and I happened to walk beside it.

The mountain disagreed. The mountain, I was starting to understand, did not care what I told myself.

* * *

We found him at the heart of the Long Choir, sitting cross-legged on a floor of polished black stone with his bare hands flat against it, humming the three notes back.

He was a dwarf, broad as a doorway and built like one, gray-bearded, wearing robes so patched they'd achieved a kind of architecture. His eyes were closed. Around him, the walls blazed with light, every seam and spiral burning steady as a hearth.

He spoke without opening his eyes. "Took you long enough."

Sera's bolt was leveled at him in half a heartbeat. "Who are you?"

"Brannoc." The dwarf opened his eyes—pale gray, like granite under frost—and looked at each of us in turn, unhurried, as though we were latecomers to a meeting he'd scheduled decades ago. "Stone-singer. Last one, near enough. And you're the priest, the huntress, and—" his gaze settled on me and stayed there, and something in my chest went tight, "—and the keeper."

"I'm a smuggler," I said.

"That's what I said."

"We're not staying," Sera said. "We're passing through. Nobody touch anything, nobody answer anything it asks, and nobody—"

"The mountain isn't asking you anything, huntress." Brannoc rose, joints cracking like settling stone, and looked at her with something close to pity. "You're the one who missed, aren't you? At the abbey."

Sera went white. "You don't know anything about me."

"I know what the stone knows. Stone hears everything. Footsteps, arrows loosed wide on purpose, prayers said over graves." He shrugged, vast shoulders rolling. "The stone forgives faster than people do. You'll get used to it, or you won't."

"How long have you been down here?" Rafael asked. He'd drifted forward like a man in a dream, and I couldn't blame him—Brannoc radiated the same gravity as the shard, the pull of something true after a lifetime of thin substitutes.

"Eleven years. I came to sing the mountain back to sleep." He said it flatly, a confession. "My order's task, since the severing began. Sing the old ways shut, keep the gods dreaming, keep the peace the Crown bought." His mouth twisted. "Forty years I sang lullabies to a god, priest. And eleven years ago, the god started humming along instead. Waking up under me, note by note, whether I sang or not. So I stayed. To listen. To learn the song it wanted sung when it rose."

"And what song is that?" I asked.

Brannoc turned to me again, and this time he smiled, and it was not entirely comforting.

"Not a dirge, keeper. That much I can tell you. Beyond that—" he spread his hands, "—I've got the melody and none of the words. The words are coming. On feet. Down my mountain. That's you lot."

* * *

It was Sera who broke first, because Sera breaks outward, all edges and noise. "No. Absolutely not. I have heard enough destiny-talk in the last week to fill a cathedral. We are three exhausted people hiding in a hole, and you—" she jabbed a finger at Brannoc, "—are either a madman or a trap, and I have killed men for less than that smile."

"Likely," Brannoc agreed pleasantly.

"And *you*—" she rounded on me, and her voice dropped, went rough. "Tell me you're not buying this. Tell me you didn't drag us into the dark to chase a fairy story."

And I wanted to tell her exactly that. Wanted it badly. Because the alternative was admitting what had been building in me since the waystation burned: that the world under my boots had shifted, that the maps I trusted were drawn over truths no one had survived long enough to correct, that the guild's proverbs about listening stone were not proverbs at all but instructions we'd forgotten how to follow.

Instead I heard myself say, "Show me."

Brannoc's eyebrows climbed. "Show you what?"

"The mountain waking. You've sat here eleven years learning its song. Prove it's real. Prove it's not fever-dream and echo tricks." I stepped closer, chin up, heart hammering. "Prove it, or we walk past you and take our chances in the dark."

Rafael made a small alarmed sound. Sera said, "Xanthe—" but Brannoc raised a hand and silenced them both.

"Clever," he said softly. "Demand proof. Most folk either fall on their knees or run. You haggle." Something warmed in his granite eyes. "Aye, keeper. I'll show you. But the showing isn't mine to give. It's yours." He nodded at Rafael's chest. "The shard. Take it out. Hold it."

"He shouldn't—" Rafael started.

"It won't hurt him," Brannoc said. "It's been waiting for him since before he was born. That's rather the point."

* * *

Rafael unwrapped it with hands that trembled—not from fear, I thought, but from reverence, the way a man handles the last letter from a dead friend. The lead casing fell open. The prayer-cloth peeled back.

The shard was the size of a fist and wrong in every way a stone can be wrong: it threw heat without weight, light without flicker, and looking directly at it felt like looking at someone mid-name. Rafael held it out to me across the black floor, and the whole gallery leaned in—the light-seams brightening, the three-note chord swelling, the air going thick as incense.

"Take it," Rafael whispered. "Please."

"Why me?"

"Because it's already reaching for you. Look."

I looked. The shard's glow bent toward me, faintly, the way a flame bends toward an open window. Toward me. Not the priest who had carried it through fire and slaughter. Me—the smuggler, the liar, the girl-no-boy-no-both who'd spent a lifetime making sure nothing in this world could pick her out of a crowd.

My hands remembered how to shake. I took the shard anyway.

It hummed.

Not the distant mountain-chord—something smaller, closer, intimate as a cat's purr against a palm. The vibration ran up my wrists, my arms, my breastbone, and settled somewhere beneath my ribs where I keep the things I don't say out loud. And the mountain *answered*. Every seam of light in the Long Choir flared at once, and the chord broke open into a fourth note—a new note, one that had not sounded in eleven years of Brannoc's listening—and the new note was pitched to my pulse. I could feel it. The stone was keeping time with my heart.

"Oh," I said. Very quietly. Very small.

"What is it?" Sera had her hand on my shoulder—I hadn't seen her move. "What do you feel?"

"Warm," I managed. "It feels warm. Like—" I searched for it and found something I'd buried so deep even I'd forgotten the address. "Like the guild hall, the night my mother came home from the pass. Everyone singing. Before." I swallowed. "Like being chosen for something. Like being *known*."

"Careful," Brannoc murmured, but he was smiling, wide and unguarded, a man watching a prophecy arrive on schedule. "That's the oldest trick the gods have. Being known."

I pulled my hands back. The shard passed to Rafael, and the fourth note faded—but slower than it should have, reluctant, the way a conversation ends when neither speaker wants to hang up first.

The gallery dimmed to embers. The chord settled back to its long patient breathing. And I stood in the middle of it all with my heart still keeping time to a rhythm the mountain had taught it, knowing—knowing with the certainty I usually reserved for bad deals and worse weather—that whatever Rafael was carrying, it wasn't only his.

Something in the dark under Thornfell had put my name in its mouth.

* * *

We camped in the Choir because there was nowhere else, and because Brannoc fed us barley stew from a pot older than the Crown, and because none of us had the strength left to argue with a dwarf whose kitchen was a temple.

Sera took first watch, naturally, crossbow across her knees, pointed at the ceiling as if the rock itself might attack. Rafael slept like the dead for the first time since I'd met him, the shard cradled against his chest, his burned hands curled around it like a child around a lamp.

I lay awake staring at the faintly glowing seams overhead and doing the one thing I was best at: arithmetic.

Weight against distance. Distance against risk. Risk against price.

Except the numbers had changed. Somewhere between the waystation fire and this impossible room, the ledger of my life had gained a column I couldn't read. The shard had hummed for me. The stone kept time with my heart. Brannoc called me *keeper* like it was my mother's maiden name, and the worst part—the part that scared me more than hunters, more than the severance, more than all the gods waking at once—

The worst part was that some traitorous cellar-deep piece of me had *liked* it. Had wanted the warmth. Had wanted to be known.

I'd spent twenty-three years making myself unreadable. Unpickable. The best courier in Thornfell because no one could ever learn what mattered to me, because nothing did, because that was safety.

And now a god's heart had purred at my touch like it had been waiting.

"Can't sleep either?" Brannoc lowered himself onto the stone beside me with the grace of a landslide. He didn't look at me. Old people and cats had that in common—they watched sideways or not at all.

"What happens to me?" I asked. Too honest. Exhaustion makes honest men of smugglers.

Brannoc was quiet a long moment. The chord breathed around us.

"I'll tell you what the stone says, and you'll hate it," he said finally. "The stone says the covenant isn't breaking, keeper. It's *coming due*. Everything your family's guild carried through these mountains for six generations—every sworn road, every kept promise—was a payment on something. And the debt's called in soon. At the forge, at solstice, mark me." He tapped the floor twice with one thick knuckle. "The stone also says the payment was never meant to be blood. But I've sung at this mountain for eleven years, and it hasn't told me what was meant instead. Only that it needs someone who keeps roads."

"Someone who keeps roads," I repeated.

"That's you."

"I keep roads for money. I keep promises when they're cheap. I lie professionally." I laughed, and it came out wrong. "You've got the wrong Fairweather."

"Mm." Brannoc lay back, hands folded on his belly, utterly content. "The stone says otherwise. And the stone, little thief, has never once been wrong about a name."

I didn't sleep after that. I lay in the breathing dark doing sums that wouldn't balance, until finally, to quiet my own skull, I pulled out the one possession I'd carried without examining for years—my grandmother's ledger, the one with the tunnel-marks, the one Maren had pressed into my hands at the gate with a look I'd been too rushed to read.

Now, by ember-light, with a god's chord humming patience underneath me, I opened it past the route-marks for the first time.

Past them, in cramped fading ink, were other pages. Accounts. Dates going back sixty years. And payments—in Crown coin, regular as tides—to a name I knew the way you know your own heartbeat.

Fairweather.

The last entry in my grandmother's hand was a single line, and I read it four times before the words would hold still:

*For services rendered regarding the terms of the Covenant — paid in full.*

The mountain hummed on, warm and waiting, and for the first time since the passes sealed, I was cold all the way through.
