## 7. Maren's Ledger

The safehouse was older than the guild itself.

That was the first thing Brannoc noticed, and Brannoc noticed stone the way other people noticed faces. He stood in the cellar of the Hollowmere waystation with his palm flat against the foundation wall and said, "This rock remembers hands that weren't Fairweather."

"Everything under this mountain is Fairweather," I told him. "My family bought it, stole it, or buried someone under it. Sometimes all three for the same plot."

He grunted, unconvinced, and kept his hand where it was. The ember shard sat wrapped in oilcloth in Rafael's pack by the hearth upstairs, and even down here through two floors of timber and slate I could feel it—the faint wrongness of warmth where there shouldn't be any, like standing near an oven door in summer. It had been humming more since the tunnels. Since it had hummed *for me*, specifically, which was a fact I was doing my best to not think about at volume.

We'd been in Hollowmere three days. Long enough for Sera's wound to stop weeping and start scabbing. Long enough for Rafael to stop flinching at every knock of settling wood. Not long enough for me to stop calculating how long we could stay before the Crown's hunters traced us east from the Widow's Bridge, which was a debt Sera had paid with four inches of steel through her shoulder, and which I intended to repay by never letting her name reach a Crown ledger.

Which was, as it turned out, exactly the kind of irony the gods enjoyed.

* * *

It was the storm that found the ledger, not me.

The third night the rain came sideways off the peaks and found every weakness in Hollowmere's roof, because Hollowmere was ninety years past caring. Water came down the chimney breast in the back room, down inside the wall, and pooled behind the paneling until the whole sheet bowed out like a belly about to laugh.

I went up with a lamp to move the stores off the floor and found the wall breathing.

"Brannoc," I called down the stairs. "Your singing stone might want to see this."

He came up muttering, pressed his ear to the bulging panel, then did something with his knuckles that sounded less like knocking and more like asking a question. The wall answered. Somewhere inside it, plaster gave up.

Behind the paneling was a cavity running the height of the wall, dry as a crypt despite the storm, lined with lead flashing—someone had built this space to keep water *out*, which meant someone had built it to keep something *in*. On a shelf of oiled cedar sat a strongbox no bigger than a bread loaf. No lock. Just the Fairweather mark stamped on the lid: the wheel with the broken spoke.

Our mark. The old one, from before the Crown made us register our routes and our grudges.

"You knew this was here?" Rafael asked from the doorway. He had that look he got when the ember shard woke—half reverence, half nausea.

"I knew my grandmother kept the back room locked," I said. "I thought she was hiding brandy."

Brannoc lifted the box out and set it on the table between us, and none of us reached for it for a moment, which should have told me everything. Three people who'd spent weeks outrunning death, all suddenly shy of a bread loaf.

Sera opened it. Of course it was Sera; her hands were the steadiest, and her conscience was already ruined, so what was one more secret.

Inside: papers. A bundle of letters tied in waxed cord gone brittle as onion skin. A folded map so old the ink had gone the brown of dried blood. And beneath them, wrapped separately, a page that wasn't old at all—fresh ink, Maren's handwriting, unmistakable, the sharp downstrokes I'd been copying since I was six years old.

A letter. Addressed to whoever found it.

Addressed, in fact, to *the last apprentice*.

* * *

Maren Fairweather wrote the way she gave orders: no warmth wasted, every word load-bearing.

*Xanthe.*

*If you are reading this, you are in the back room at Hollowmere, and you have already gone further than I ever wanted you to go. That is my doing and my failure, both. Read the rest. Then decide whether you hate me. You will. Do it thoroughly, and then get back on the road.*

*I will tell you plainly what the guild oath never let me say: the Ember is real. The god Vaelis is real. The covenant between the mortal realms and the old gods is real, and it has been failing for sixty-one years because of my grandmother, Odile Fairweather, and I have known since I was twenty-two years old.*

I read it twice because the first time the words refused to mean anything.

Rafael made a sound low in his chest, like a man taking a blade he'd been bracing for anyway.

*You were raised on the smuggler's oath,* the letter went on. *Every road kept. Every promise honored. Every cargo delivered. You think those words are ours. They are not. They are a copy—a faded tracing of the covenant itself, the oath the first road-keepers swore to the gods when the realms were joined. Our family were not smugglers originally, child. We were priests of the passes. Wardens. The toll we collected was never coin. It was witness. Every traveler who crossed the Thornfells swore a small piece of the great oath, and the gods heard all of it, and the mountains held because the mountains were loved.*

*Odile sold it.*

*In the winter of the famine, the Crown came to her with gold and with guns and with an offer: tell us the terms of the covenant—the true terms, the sacrifice it demands—and the guild keeps its roads, its immunity, its name. She took it. She told them everything. And the Crown, understanding that a covenant freely sworn cannot be enforced but only chosen, set out to make sure no one would ever choose it again. They burned the warden line. They rewrote the maps so the old shrines fell off them. They taught three generations that the gods were stories, and stories are so easy to starve.*

*That silence you've felt your whole life—that hush in the high places? That is not the gods sleeping. That is a wound, kept open on purpose.*

I had to put the letter down. My hands were doing something embarrassing.

Sixty-one years. My whole life plus twenty-two. Every story I'd been raised on—every tale of Fairweather cunning, Fairweather luck, the Fairweather knack for finding the pass that wasn't there yesterday—I'd thought we survived because we were good. Because the mountain liked us.

We survived because we were the ones who killed it. We'd been living fat and quiet in the house of the thing we murdered, sweeping the floor every morning, calling ourselves its family.

"You didn't know," Rafael said quietly. Not a question. A kindness, and I hated how much I needed it.

"I suspected Maren was hiding something." My voice came out flat and strange. "I thought it was money. Debt. Maybe a second family in some valley town. Something *small*. Something a person could—" I stopped. "She watched me swear the guild oath when I was twelve. She put her hand on my head and gave me the words. *Every road kept.* She knew what they really meant and she gave them to me anyway like it was an inheritance and not a—"

"Not a confession?" Brannoc rumbled. He hadn't touched the letters. Dwarves, I was learning, understood better than anyone what it does to a people when their own stone is used against them.

I picked the letter back up because Maren had written *read the rest* and Maren did not make requests twice.

*You will want to know why I didn't undo it,* she wrote. *Here is the truth that will make you hate me properly: I tried. At thirty, I went looking for what Odile destroyed. I found one of the last ember-priests, a dying man in Vaelmark, and I asked him how to restore the covenant. He told me it could not be restored—it could only be reforged, at the Emberforge, before the severance completes. And he told me the price. The covenant demands a soul bound by oath, given willingly, at the forge's heart. A life, Xanthe. Freely offered.*

*I had a guild full of orphans depending on me. I had you, though you were only a baby then, left on my doorstep like all the best Fairweathers are. So I did what Odile did. I chose the living over the truth. I kept the guild alive another generation on the strength of a lie, and I told myself the gods were sleeping anyway, and sleeping things don't grieve.*

*I was wrong. They have begun to wake, which means the severance is close, which means everything I protected is about to be worthless unless someone pays the price I wouldn't pay. I could not choose which of my children to spend. So I am spending you instead—not your life, I pray to whatever is listening, but your loyalty. Take the client through the high pass. Protect him with everything the oath actually means. And when you learn what I did, remember that I am telling you myself, in my own hand, because you deserve at least that much truth from a Fairweather.*

*Odile's bargain papers are in the box. The Crown's copy of the covenant terms. Read them and know that the enemy you're running from is running from us too—they cannot afford for the covenant to be reforged any more than we can afford for it to fail. The High Hunter knows what's at stake. Why do you think they hunt priests and not smugglers?*

*Every road kept, Xanthe. I am sorry. It was always going to come back to that.*

*M.F.*

* * *

Nobody spoke for a long time. The storm filled the silence, hammering the roof like it wanted in, like it had opinions.

Sera read the Crown documents last, holding each page at arm's length the way she held anything official—with visible disgust. "This is it," she said finally. "This is the original warrant. Sealed by the Lord Protector himself." Her finger traced a line of clerk's script. "'Terms of the covenant between the mortal realms and the elder powers, as confessed by O. Fairweather, warden of the Thornfell roads, being as follows: that the realms are bound by witness-oath; that the binding renews at each solstice at the forge called Emberheart; that the renewal requires the willing offering of an oath-bound soul—'"

"'And that the severance,'" Rafael finished, "will complete within six generations of the terms' betrayal." He was standing very straight, the way he stood when he was using posture to hold himself together. "Six generations. Odile's bargain was sixty-one years ago." His eyes came up and met mine. "Xanthe. This is the last winter. This solstice. It isn't soon. It's *now*."

"I know." My voice sounded like someone else's. "I did the arithmetic the same time you did."

"And Maren knew. For forty years, she's known the deadline." Rafael laughed, once, without any humor in it at all. "And she still almost didn't send anyone. If I hadn't come to her—"

"If you hadn't come, she'd have let the worlds come apart rather than risk the guild's name." I said it and immediately wished I hadn't, because saying it made it load-bearing, made it real, made it part of the architecture of my life going forward.

But Rafael didn't flinch. "Yes," he said simply. "That's what I think too. I'm sorry."

"Don't be sorry. Be angry. One of us should get to be angry and I'm using it all up on her."

Brannoc finally reached into the box and lifted out the oldest thing in it—not paper but a strip of leather, cracked and dark, marked with runes in a script older than the Crown's whole alphabet. He held it near the lamp and went very still.

"This is dwarven work," he said slowly. "First-age. This is a *witness-strip*. Road-keepers wore these. It records oath-sworn passage—the stone listens to it." He turned it over. His thumb stopped on a maker's mark worn nearly smooth, and I saw the exact moment he recognized it, because his face did what dwarf faces do, which is nothing, which is everything.

"What?"

"The Fairweather wheel." He looked at me with something that wasn't pity, which I appreciated, and wasn't surprise, which I didn't. "Your family didn't just keep the roads, girl. Your family helped *cut* them. Before the dwarves came to these mountains, someone was already walking them and swearing them." He set the strip down gently, like laying a body to rest. "You aren't descended from smugglers. You're descended from priests who forgot their own god's name and called the forgetting a business."

* * *

I took the lamp and went out onto the loading porch alone, because there are conversations you have to lose in private.

The storm had broken open to the west, and through the tear in the clouds the peaks showed white under a hard clean moon—the high pass, our route, the road I'd run since I was small enough to ride in a cargo crate. It looked exactly the same as it always had. That was the cruelest part. The mountain hadn't changed. Only the story I'd been telling about whose it was.

Every road kept. I'd sworn it at twelve with Maren's hand heavy on my head, and I'd thought I was joining a family business. I'd been renewing a covenant I didn't know existed, a drop of witness in a bucket sixty years empty, and the woman who gave me the words had known. Had known and smiled and poured the tea after.

I should have hated her cleanly. That was the deal she'd offered in her letter—*decide whether you hate me, do it thoroughly.* But hate needs a clean shape and Maren wouldn't hold one. She'd raised eleven orphans on the proceeds of treason. She'd fed me, trained me, trusted me with the last run worth trusting anyone with. She'd also, knowingly, sat on the death of the world for forty years because the guild's name felt heavier than the sky.

Both of those women were real. I just had to figure out which one I owed.

"You're doing the thing," Sera said from the doorway, "where you stand very still and pretend you're fine."

"It's a Fairweather specialty. We're famous for it."

"Mm." She came out, moving carefully around her bandaged shoulder, and leaned on the rail beside me. Below us the valley ran black to the tree line. "For what it's worth—in the Order, when a hunter finds out their mentor fed them a lie, the protocol is report, denounce, execute if resistance is offered. Very tidy. Very loyal." She turned her head, and moonlight caught the scar along her jaw. "I executed the protocol for nine years. Then I watched a man walk out of a burning chapel carrying a piece of a god, and everyone he was supposed to trust was dead, and the only person who stood between him and my crossbow was a smuggler who owed him nothing." A pause. "Loyalty's not the problem, Xanthe. Loyalty's just a road. The question is always what you're keeping it *for*."

"That's suspiciously wise for someone who shot at us for a week."

"I missed on purpose for most of it."

Behind us, inside, I could hear Rafael reading the rest of Odile's correspondence aloud to Brannoc, his voice low and steady, doing the work of a priest even now—especially now, maybe, when the faith he'd been slaughtered for had turned out to have a traitor's signature at its founding. He'd take this harder than me in the end. I could tell. He'd built his whole martyrdom on the idea that mortals had failed the gods through weakness. Learning instead that they'd failed them through *bargaining*—through ordinary, greedy, survivable human choice—would either break him or remake him.

From the doorway he called my name, soft. "There's more. Odile kept writing to her handler for years. There's a whole file." He hesitated. "And there's a route map. The Crown's original survey. Xanthe—it marks every shrine they erased. Every warden station. Every place the oath was sworn." His face had changed. Hope is a dangerous thing to watch arrive on someone who'd planned to die. "If we're going to the Emberforge, we won't be finding it by guesswork. The road to it is on this map. The *real* road."

Of course it was. The Crown had needed to find the forge in order to strangle it, and the only guide they'd had was a Fairweather. Odile had sold the heavens and kept the receipt.

I looked at the white pass a moment longer. Then I went inside, took the map from Rafael's hands, and spread it on the table across the scattered bones of my family's confession.

"We leave at first light," I said. "South through the valley villages—we'll need supplies, fresh horses, and nobody hunting our faces yet. Brannoc, you know stone; check this route against what the mountain will actually allow. Sera, you know how the Order thinks; tell us where the net will be tightest." I traced the inked line south with one finger, feeling the strangest sensation, like the map was warm under my touch—or maybe that was the shard, or maybe it was neither, maybe it was just sixty years of waiting pressing up through old paper.

"There's a village here," I said, tapping a mark two days south, where the map showed a shrine erased and a well-road bending toward it. "Half a day off our line, but it sits on one of Odile's erased roads. If any of the old ways survive, they start there."

Rafael leaned over my shoulder to look, and frowned. "I know that village," he said slowly. "Vaelis's order had a mission there, years ago. They wrote us letters. There was talk of a child born after the massacre who—" He stopped. Shook his head. "No. It can't be relevant."

The ember shard, packed in its oilcloth by the hearth, hummed.

All of us heard it this time. All of us pretended, briefly, that we hadn't.

"Pip," Rafael whispered, like a name he'd promised never to say aloud. "The girl who hears bells. She lives in that valley."
