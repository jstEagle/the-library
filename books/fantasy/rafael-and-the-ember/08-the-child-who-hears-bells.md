## 8. The Child Who Hears Bells

The village was called Lowbell, and it had earned the name honestly. It sat in a fold of the valley like a coin dropped in grass, one crooked street, a shrine with no god in it, and a bell tower that hadn't rung in Rafael's lifetime. He knew it hadn't rung because he'd asked. The innkeeper's wife had looked at him as if he'd asked whether the sun still rose.

"Bells went quiet when I was a girl," she said, setting down his stew without meeting his eyes. "All of them. Everywhere. Nobody talks about it."

Nobody ever did. That was the covenant's great trick, Rafael thought—not erasure, just silence, laid down so gently and so long ago that silence felt like the natural state of things. Mortals had stopped listening, and then they'd stopped remembering there had been anything to hear.

He sat by the window with his burned hands wrapped around the bowl, feeling the heat through the scarring the way he felt everything now—distantly, through a layer of something else. The ember shard rode against his chest in its oilcloth wrapping, and it had been humming since dawn. Not loudly. Not the urgent thrum it gave off near Xanthe, or the deep answering note Brannoc had coaxed from the tunnel stone. Just a low, patient vibration, like a cat settling in for a long wait.

*We are close to something,* it seemed to say. *Be ready.*

Rafael was tired of being ready. He was tired of running, tired of Sera checking the window every quarter hour out of habit she couldn't break, tired of watching Xanthe sleep sitting up with a knife in their belt because rest, for them, was a luxury they'd never been taught to take. Five days since the tunnels. Five days since the shard had hummed under Xanthe's fingers and Rafael had watched something choose a smuggler and understood, with a prophet's terrible clarity, that his own role in this story might be smaller than he'd believed.

Across the common room, Xanthe was haggling with the innkeeper over stabling for the mule, all sharp gestures and easy charm, and Rafael caught himself smiling at the performance before he could stop. Sera noticed. She always noticed.

"You're staring," she said quietly, not looking up from the knife she was sharpening.

"I'm resting my eyes."

"On him."

"On *them,*" Rafael said, and Sera's mouth twitched—the closest thing to a laugh he'd gotten from her since the bridge. Since she'd stood on the Widow's Bridge with an arrow through her shoulder and held her own comrades back while the rest of them ran. The wound was healing wrong; he could see it in how she held her arm. She wouldn't let him look at it. Priests made people nervous, even defected ones' companions. Even, sometimes, especially.

The door opened and the cold came in with it, and with the cold came the child.

She was small—seven, maybe eight—and she walked into the common room the way water finds a channel, unhesitating, as if she'd been walking toward this room her whole life. Her hair was dark and uncombed. Her boots were too big. And she was carrying a slate under one arm and a piece of chalk in her fist like a weapon.

She crossed the room without a word, without a glance at anyone, straight to their table, and stood in front of Rafael.

Sera's hand went to her bow. Xanthe appeared from nowhere, the way they did, sliding between the child and the door with the particular body language of a smuggler assessing exits. But the girl wasn't looking at either of them. She was looking at Rafael—or not at him, precisely, but at his chest, at the place where the shard slept against his heart.

Then she held up her slate, and drew.

Rafael would remember the sound of that chalk for the rest of his life. The small dry scratching of it, in a silent room, drawing a line—a road, winding up through mountains drawn in quick confident strokes. A pass. A high pass, with a notch in the ridge that he recognized, that *he* recognized, though he'd never seen it except in dreams and in the oldest maps of his order: the Emberforge road, the last approach, the one that existed on no living chart.

She drew five figures on the road. One tall and thin. One broad. One bent like a bow. One small, with a scribble of dark hair. And one whose chalk outline she pressed so hard the slate nearly cracked, standing at the head of the line, holding something above its head that glowed—or would have glowed, if chalk could glow. She colored it in until her fingertip went white.

Then she turned the slate around and showed it to them, and her eyes were enormous and calm and absolutely certain, and Rafael felt the shard against his chest go hot as a coal.

"That's us," Xanthe said softly. Not a question.

The girl nodded once.

"She can't speak," said a voice from the doorway, ragged with something between fear and shame. A woman stood there, flour on her hands, a baker's apron over a dress gone thin at the seams. The girl's mother. "Pip. Her name is Pip. She hasn't spoken since the bells—" She stopped herself, as if the sentence were a cliff edge. "Please. Whatever you are, whatever you're carrying—we heard the forge-hum in the mountain two nights past. The whole valley heard it. And Pip woke up *drawing.*"

* * *

They took the family into the inn's back room because the mother, Wren, refused to have the conversation where neighbors could see, and because Sera insisted on a wall at her back and only one door. Brannoc filled most of the room by himself, seated on the floor because the chairs groaned under him, and the girl—Pip—sat cross-legged beside him with her slate on her knees, watching everyone with the patience of someone who already knew how the story went.

Her father, Aldous, was a miller with sawdust in his beard and terror in his eyes. He talked the way drowning men swim.

"She hears them," he said. "That's what we call it. The bells. Since she was four—she'd stop in the middle of anything, mid-meal, mid-game, and tilt her head like a bird, and after, she'd draw things. Little things, at first. Rain coming a day early. The Hendersons' lost cow, and where to find it. We told ourselves it was cleverness. A gift for patterns." His hands worked at each other. "But last winter she drew the river flooding three days before the thaw did it, and she drew our neighbor's boy falling through the ice at the exact bend, and we kept her home from the river and he went anyway and—" He swallowed. "And after that she stopped trying to tell us anything at all. Stopped everything. A year of silence. Until your mountain sang, and now she won't put the chalk down."

"They're getting louder," Wren said. Her voice was flat with exhaustion, the voice of someone who has cried everything there is to cry. "Whatever they are. Every year louder. And the Crown's men came through in autumn asking about children who 'heard voices.' Asking about *bells.*" She looked at Rafael, at his priest's ruined hands, and her chin came up. "So you'll understand why we're begging strangers in a smuggling inn to take our daughter into the mountains in winter."

The room went very quiet.

"We're not going somewhere safe," Xanthe said carefully. "Wherever we're going, it's the opposite of safe. There are hunters behind us and worse ahead, and I won't dress it up for you—"

"We know," Wren said. "Gods forgive us, we know. We've talked about nothing else for two years, Aldous and I. What happens when the Crown comes for her. Because they will come. They came for the Hendersons' boy's family over a *drawing*, and that was before the mountain woke." Her composure cracked, just once, a single fault-line. "You're taking her somewhere the bells come from. Aren't you? That's what the drawing means. You're going to them."

Rafael looked at the slate. At the five figures on the impossible road. At the small figure with the scribbled hair, walking willingly among them.

"The gods," he said, and his voice came out rougher than he intended, "have been silent for three hundred years. My order spent those centuries keeping the old rites alive in secret, believing the silence was grief—that the gods had withdrawn and left us a promise instead of a presence. Six weeks ago, prayers started being answered again. Real answers. And my order was slaughtered for knowing why." He turned his scarred palms upward. "I don't fully know what your daughter is. But I know the gods are waking, and I know the Crown kills what it cannot control, and I know that a child who hears them is in more danger in this valley than on any mountain I can name."

"So you'll take her," Aldous said. Not a question either. A man setting down a weight he could no longer carry alone.

"It's not that simple," Sera said sharply. "You don't hand your child to fugitives because of a chalk drawing. We could be anyone. We could be exactly what the Crown says we are."

"You're not," Pip wrote.

Everyone turned. She had flipped her slate over and chalked three words in a careful, laborious hand, and she held it up with the absolute authority of a judge delivering a verdict:

**YOU'RE NOT ANYONE**

Xanthe laughed—a short, startled bark, quickly strangled—and Rafael saw something shift in Sera's face, some armor loosening a plate. The girl watched them all with those enormous calm eyes, and then she erased her words with her sleeve and drew again, fast now, chalk flying: the bell tower of Lowbell, and above it, faint lines radiating outward like ripples in a pond. Sound, rendered by a child who had never once heard a bell and yet knew exactly what one looked like when it rang.

Beneath the tower she wrote: **THEY'RE WAITING FOR YOU TO RING THEM.**

Brannoc made a sound low in his chest, half hum, half groan, and every head turned to him. The dwarf was staring at the slate with an expression Rafael had never seen on him—something close to reverence, edged with fear.

"Stone-singer's truth," Brannoc said slowly. "I've sung to granite my whole life, and granite tells ye what it's seen, no more. But there's a deeper singing under the world, lass and lad, a ringing, and my people sealed our deepest mines rather than listen to it. I thought it was fear-madness. Old stories." He tapped the slate with one thick finger. "It's no madness. She's hearing what the mountain's been holding its breath to say. And if the bells are waiting to be rung—" he looked up at Rafael, and his eyes were grim, "—then someone's meant to ring them. And I'd wager my beard it's no' the Crown."

* * *

They left at first light, because staying longer meant questions, and questions meant hunters, and because Wren had packed Pip's bag with the fierce efficiency of a woman who had rehearsed this goodbye for two years and refused to let it become a scene.

But it became a scene anyway, briefly, at the edge of the village, where the frost stood in the fields and the dead bell tower rose black against the grey sky. Wren knelt and took her daughter's face in both flour-dusted hands and kissed her forehead, her cheeks, her eyelids, saying nothing, saying everything. Aldous hugged her so hard Rafael winced to watch it. Then they stepped back, and Wren pressed something into Xanthe's palm—a small copper bell, green with age, no bigger than a thumb.

"It was Pip's grandmother's," she said. "It used to ring. It doesn't anymore—try it, nothing, dead as the tower. But she loved it, and—" Her voice failed and rebuilt itself. "Bring her back to us. After. However this ends. Bring her back."

Xanthe closed their fist around the little bell, and Rafael saw their throat work once before the smuggler's mask came down again. "Every road kept," Xanthe said quietly. "That's the oath. She'll come home on one of them."

Pip didn't cry. That was the thing that broke Rafael's heart about it—she didn't cry, she didn't cling, she simply took her father's hand and her mother's hand and pressed them together, palm to palm, and then picked up her slate and her chalk and her too-big boots carried her up the road without looking back, because she had never once looked back, because she had known they were coming since before they knew it themselves.

She fell into step beside Rafael. Of course she did.

After a mile, she tugged his sleeve. He crouched. She held up the slate.

On it, in her careful letters: **THEY SAY YOUR NAME NOW. WHEN YOU SLEEP. THEY'VE BEEN SAYING IT FOR YEARS AND YOU COULDN'T HEAR.**

Rafael stared at the words until they blurred. The shard against his chest pulsed, warm and insistent, and far away—or not far away at all, closer than his own heartbeat—he thought he heard it: a bell, ringing once, in a tone he had known all his life without knowing it, the way you know your own name in a stranger's mouth.

Behind them, Sera shifted her bow. Ahead, the pass waited, white and silent. Beside him, a child who heard gods swung her feet and waited, patient as stone, for him to start walking.

And in the valley below, so faint that only the five of them could have caught it, the dead bell of Lowbell's tower shivered—and rang.
