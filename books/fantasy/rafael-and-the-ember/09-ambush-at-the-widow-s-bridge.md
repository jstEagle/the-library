## 9. Ambush at the Widow's Bridge

Sera Vane had taken the Widow's Bridge twice in her life, both times as a hunter with a writ and a warrant, and both times she had thought it the ugliest span in the Thornfells. It was a rope-and-timber crossing slung over a gorge so deep the river at its bottom was only a rumor of sound, named for some smuggler's wife who had supposedly hanged herself from the western pylon when her husband never came home. The boards were gray with frost-rot. The handrails swayed. The whole structure groaned like an old woman climbing stairs.

Now she stood on the eastern side of it with a bow that wasn't hers, watching five figures pick their way down the switchbacks toward her, and she understood the widow perfectly. Some things you waited for knowing they would break your heart.

"They're coming," Brannoc said behind her. He had his palm flat against the cliff face, reading the stone the way other men read weather. "Four on foot. One mounted, hanging back at the tree line." A pause. His voice went flat. "The one on horseback wears silver."

High Hunter Corvane himself. Of course. Sera had known it the moment Maren Fairweather's letter reached the guild hall — or rather, the moment Xanthe read it aloud in a voice that got quieter with every line, until by the end they were barely whispering, as if the words might shatter if spoken at full volume. *For services rendered and services to come, the location of the fugitive priest and his companions will be conveyed to the Crown's hunters at the Widow's Crossing, where the road narrows.* Signed with Maren's own seal, the fairweather vane that pointed wherever the wind blew hardest.

"She sold us," Xanthe had said, not looking at anyone. "She sold *me*."

"No," Rafael had said gently. "She sold me. She tried to keep you out of the ledger. There's a difference, to someone like her."

Sera had watched Xanthe absorb that — watched them understand that being spared was its own kind of betrayal — and something in her chest had cracked along a seam she'd been pretending wasn't there since the night of the massacre at Candlemere.

Because Sera knew what came next. She knew how Corvane worked. She'd served under him for six years; she'd been the arrow he loosed when he wanted deniability. He wouldn't take prisoners at the bridge. He couldn't afford to — not with Rafael carrying proof that the gods were waking, not with a mute child who heard bells, not with a defected hunter whose testimony could unravel the entire Office of the God-Hunt like wet rope. Corvane hadn't come for an arrest. He'd come for silence.

And silence was something Sera Vane could fight.

"You don't have to do this," Xanthe said now, coming to stand beside her at the bridgehead. They had that way of speaking that made every sentence sound like a question they already knew the answer to. Below them, the others were striking camp with the grim efficiency of people who'd done too much of it lately. Pip sat on a rock, drawing in the dirt with a stick — she'd drawn the bridge three days ago, before any of them knew where they'd be, five stick figures on one side and one lone figure on the other, arms spread wide.

Sera hadn't told anyone about the drawing. She suspected Pip hadn't needed to tell anyone either.

"Four against one is bad odds," Xanthe pressed. "Even for you."

"Three," Sera corrected. "Brannoc stays with the tunnel mouth. If I fall, he collapses the passage behind you and you go through the deep ways without me."

"That's not what I meant."

"I know what you meant." Serra checked the bowstring, ran her thumb down the fletching of an arrow. Goose-fletched, gray-banded — her own make, from her own kit, the one thing she'd kept from her old life besides the scar on her left forearm. "Xanthe. Look at me."

They did. Up close, Xanthe Fairweather had the kind of face that lied professionally — easy grin, easy shrug, nothing behind the eyes but weather. But they were tired enough, scared enough, that the mask had slipped, and underneath was just a young person carrying more than any person should: a god's ember in a lead-lined box, a traitor elder's name like a stone in the gut, and a growing, terrible suspicion that the prophecy about the keeper of roads was pointing at them.

"When I missed that shot on the scree," Sera said quietly, "I told myself it was the wind. Then I told myself it was the miracle at Candlemere — that I'd seen a dead man stand up wreathed in fire and my hands stopped trusting my eyes. Both true. Neither the truth." She looked out at the switchbacks, where four dark shapes moved steadily closer through the pines. "The truth is I've been aiming wrong my whole career. At the wrong things. For the wrong reasons. This—" she tapped the bow, "—is the first shot I've ever taken that I actually believe in. You don't get to take that from me. Nobody does. Not even you, especially not you, because you've got that look like you're going to try to carry everyone across this bridge yourself."

"That's ridiculous," Xanthe said. "I can't even carry the ember box and my pack."

"You carried Rafael for two miles through the snowmelt last week."

"He twisted his ankle."

"You carried him, Xanthe." She let that sit. Then, softer: "Let me carry this."

Below them, Rafael had stopped packing. He was watching her with those burned hands folded together, and there was something in his face she recognized from mirrors — the specific anguish of a man who had decided long ago that his life belonged to other people and hated how much company he had in that decision.

"The Widow's Bridge," Brannoc rumbled, still touching the stone. "It's older than the guild. Older than the Crown. My grandda sang a song about it — said the mountain holds the pylons up out of respect for the woman who died honest." He turned his head, and his stone-gray eyes found Sera. "You die honest today, huntress, and this gorge will remember you."

"Don't jinx me, dwarf."

"I'm not jinxing you. I'm making arrangements."

That startled a laugh out of her, short and sharp, which she supposed was the point.

* * *

They crossed first — all of them, quick and light, Rafael with Pip on his back because the girl refused to be carried by anyone else and Rafael had long since stopped questioning the small sovereignties of children who heard gods. The bridge bucked and complained under them. Halfway across, Pip stopped, looked down into the gorge, and made a gesture — flat hand, cutting motion, then fingers spread like branches.

"What did she say?" Sera called.

Rafael's jaw tightened. "She says the river remembers names. And that mine will be on it soon if we don't move."

Cheerful child. Sera loved her fiercely and had no idea when that had happened.

On the far side, the trail climbed immediately into a wall of rock broken only by the tunnel mouth — Brannoc's deep way, half-hidden behind a curtain of frozen waterfall. They could hold there, or they could run, but they could not do either while four hunters held the eastern bridgehead. Someone had to keep the far side of the gorge clean.

Someone had to be the widow.

Sera kissed her own arrowheads out of habit — an old superstition from her first commander, dead now, probably better off — and slung her quiver. "Brannoc. When I give the signal, drop the scree chute onto the lower trail. That cuts their numbers at the crossing to two, maybe three if they're stupid."

"And if they're clever?"

"Then I'm dead and none of this matters, so let's assume they're stupid."

Xanthe hugged her. That was the thing she hadn't planned for. She'd braced for argument, for guilt, for Rafael's priestly blessings and Pip's solemn gestures, but Xanthe simply stepped in and wrapped their arms around her, brief and fierce, and said into her shoulder: "Every road kept. Every promise honored. That's the oath. You're on our road now, Sera. So we'll keep you. That's how it works."

"That's not how oaths work."

"It's how ours works. Ask literally any god."

Then they were gone, all four of them swallowed by the tunnel, and Sera Vane stood alone on the eastern side of the Widow's Bridge with the wind pulling at her cloak and the pines below giving up their hunters one shadow at a time.

* * *

They came up the last switchback in good order, which annoyed her. She'd trained two of them herself.

Dace and Merrin took the flanks — Dace with his crossbow, Merrin with the hooked net-pole the Office used for taking priests alive, though Sera noted neither man carried the suppression irons anymore. No irons meant no intention of taking anyone alive. Corvane's orders had evolved. The fourth was a new face, young, nervous, a recruit Sera didn't know, which somehow made it worse; she was going to have to put arrows near a kid who still polished his badge.

And then Corvane himself rode up through the trees and dismounted, and the temperature of the morning dropped.

He was not a large man. He had never needed to be. High Hunter Aldous Corvane wore authority the way other men wore armor — fitted, polished, load-bearing. His silver badge caught the weak sun. His eyes found Sera immediately, and he smiled the way a ledger smiles.

"Sera Vane." Her name in his mouth sounded like an entry being crossed out. "Six years of exemplary service. Three commendations. The Candlemere citation, posthumously pending, I'm afraid, given current circumstances." He walked toward the bridgehead, unhurried, boots crunching frost. "Do you know what I admire about you, Vane? You were always honest. Even your treason is honest — standing in the open, weapon drawn, no tricks. Most defectors skulk. You've made a stand. It's almost professional."

"Corvane." She kept her voice level. Fifty feet of rotten bridge between them, and she'd already counted the boards she trusted. Eleven. Maybe twelve. "Give me one reason not to put an arrow through your warrant."

"Certainly. Because you'll miss." He said it without malice, which was the terrible part. "You missed the priest on the scree. I reviewed the report. Wind conditions negligible, range well within your capability, target moving laterally at an easy pace. You missed, Vane. You, who once put a shaft through a cultist's eye at ninety yards in a thunderstorm. Something broke in you at Candlemere, and whatever it was, it hasn't healed." He tilted his head. "So here is my offer, and I'll only make it once. Step aside. Help me take the priest. Full pardon, reinstatement, and I'll bury the defection so deep even the historians won't find it. You were the best shot I ever fielded. I'd rather have you back than dead."

Behind him, Dace shifted his crossbow toward the tunnel mouth. Toward where Pip had gone.

Something in Sera went very quiet and very clear, the way it did in the half-second before a release.

"You saw it too," she said. "At Candlemere. Don't lie to me, Aldous, you were standing right beside me when the pyre lit itself and the dead priest stood up in the flames and *answered* the boy's prayer. You saw a god reach into the world with your own eyes. Six years we hunted people for believing less than what we saw that night." Her voice cracked and she let it. "What did you tell yourself? What story makes it possible for you to keep doing this?"

Corvane's smile didn't waver, but something behind it did — a flicker, a hairline fracture, gone as fast as it came. "I tell myself the same thing I've always told myself. That the realm survives because the gods sleep, and it dies the day they wake. Whatever I saw at Candlemere, the answer is the same: put it back to sleep, or cut it out. Order isn't a belief, Vane. It's a discipline." He raised one hand. "Last chance. Stand down."

"I missed that shot on purpose," Sera said.

The words came out steady, and she watched them land — watched Dace frown, watched the young recruit's crossbow dip an inch, watched Corvane go very still.

"Every shot I ever took for the Office, I took straight. That day, I chose. And I've chosen every day since. So no, Aldous, my hands aren't broken. They're finally aimed right." She drew, anchored, and settled her sight picture on the center of his silver badge. "Here's my counteroffer. Turn around. Ride back to the capital and tell the Crown the pass collapsed. Live with it. It's easier than what happens next."

"Take her," Corvane said.

* * *

Later — much later, in the fevered arithmetic of memory — Sera would count the fight in releases, one breath each.

First release: Dace, mid-step onto the bridge, arrow through the rope guard on the western pylon's near side. Not to kill — to *warn*. The bridge lurched, boards tilting, and Dace grabbed rail and swore, and the young recruit froze at the bridgehead exactly as green recruits do.

Second release: Merrin's net-pole spinning up toward her position, and she was already moving, dropping off the rock shelf to the lower ledge where Brannoc's scree chute met the trail. The chute came down on cue — a roar of stone and snow that swallowed the lower path and cut the recruit off entirely, leaving Dace and Merrin on the bridge and Corvane shouting orders at the treeline.

Third release: Dace, through the shoulder, dropping his crossbow into the gorge. He screamed. She felt the scream in her teeth and kept shooting, because mercy now would cost four lives on the far side of the mountain.

Merrin was the problem. He was fast and angry and he knew her habits, and he made the bridge while she repositioned, boards hammering under his boots, net-pole leveled. She took him at forty feet, moving, bad angle — and her arrow hit the rail instead of him, and he laughed, and she understood in one cold flash that Corvane had been right about one thing: something in her *had* changed, and it cost her half a heartbeat at every draw.

Merrin's net caught her across the thighs and hauled her off her feet. They went down together on the frost-rotted planks, and the bridge swung out over the gorge like a bell being rung, and below them the river-that-was-only-a-rumor roared up suddenly loud, as if the water had heard its name.

She got her knife into the net. He got his boot onto her wrist. Somewhere above, Corvane was walking onto the bridge — she could hear the deliberate weight of his steps, unhurried even now, a man crossing his own property.

"Sera Vane," he said, from very close. "Look at me."

She looked. His sword was drawn, and it was not pointed at her heart. It was pointed at her drawing arm, at the tendons that made her the best shot the Office had ever fielded, and she understood the shape of his cruelty completely: he would not kill her. He would unmake her, precisely, surgically, and send her back to the capital as a lesson.

"Hold her arm," Corvane said to Merrin.

And that was when the bridge sang.

Not groaned — *sang*. A low, resonant note out of the pylons, out of the ancient rope, out of the frost-gray timber, a note like a struck bell heard through stone, and every hair on Sera's body stood up. The mountain holding the pylons out of respect for the woman who died honest. Brannoc's grandda's song. The gorge remembering names.

The ropes snapped taut. The bridge steadied under her like a hand catching a falling cup.

And Corvane — disciplined, orderly, unbelieving Corvane — hesitated. One step from her, sword raised, he *hesitated*, because six years of hunting faith had never prepared him for faith reaching back.

Sera didn't waste his hesitation. She wrenched her knife arm free, rolled, and put her last full-strength draw into the space between his stride — not flesh, never flesh, she was done spending lives like coin — but the board directly beneath his forward boot, splitting it clean along the grain. The plank gave. Corvane dropped to one knee, caught the rail, and for one suspended moment the High Hunter of the Crown hung over the gorge with the old song humming through his fists.

Merrin lunged for her.

She gave him the bridge's opinion of him instead — threw her weight against the rail on the far side, and the whole span heeled like a ship, and Merrin, net-pole and all, went over the edge with a shout that lasted longer than she expected and ended softer.

Then there was only Corvane, hauling himself up, and Sera on her knees ten feet away with an empty quiver and a knife, and the bridge singing its low patient note between them like a judge waiting for testimony.

"Yield," she rasped.

"To a bridge?" Blood ran from his palm where the rope had burned it. He laughed — genuinely, horribly delighted. "There it is. There's the madness. You think the mountain chose you. You think the gods are *watching*." He rose to his feet. "They're not watching, Vane. They're waking. And everything they touch comes apart. Kingdoms. Oaths. People like you." He leveled his sword. "I am the last sane officer in the Thornfells, and I am going to—"

The arrow took him through the sword-arm from behind.

Sera knew that fletching. Gray-banded goose, her own make, from her own abandoned kit — the kit she'd left at the tunnel mouth that morning, the kit someone had fetched.

Xanthe Fairweather stood at the eastern bridgehead with her bow, badly, elbows wrong, stance a crime, having clearly fired the single luckiest shot of their life. Behind them, Rafael's burned hands glowed faintly like banked coals, and Pip peered out from between them, and the child raised one small hand and made a gesture Sera was beginning to understand meant *enough*.

"Every road kept," Xanthe called across the gorge, breathless, terrified, grinning anyway. "Every promise honored. Including yours, Sera. Now stop bleeding and get over here, because Brannoc says the mountain's about to close the front door, and I don't want to find out what that means from the wrong side of it."

Corvane knelt on the singing bridge, cradling his ruined arm, and looked up at her with an expression she would carry for the rest of her days — not hatred. Recalculation. The look of a man discovering the ledger doesn't balance and deciding, coldly, to change the arithmetic instead.

"This isn't over," he said. "Solstice is in nine days. And I don't need to catch you anymore, Vane. I only need to follow you."

Sera got to her feet, thigh screaming where the net had torn it open, and limped west across the Widow's Bridge — past him, close enough to touch, close enough to see the blood and the fury and the terrible patience — and did not look back.

The mountain closed the door behind her with a sound like a hymn ending.
