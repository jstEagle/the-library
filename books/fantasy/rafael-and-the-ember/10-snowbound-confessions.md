## 10. Snowbound Confessions

**Xanthe**

The storm came down the pass like something with intent.

One hour they'd been climbing the switchbacks above the Widow's Bridge, Sera's blood still on the snow behind them, and the next the sky simply closed—white roaring white, wind that didn't howl so much as *shout*, and Brannoc bellowing that if they didn't find shelter in ten minutes they'd be three corpses and a god's heart frozen in a drift.

The cave was barely a cave. A hollow under an overhang of granite, deep enough for four bodies pressed close, shallow enough that Xanthe could see the snow swirling past the entrance like a curtain that never stopped moving. Brannoc wedged himself against the stone at the back and began, quietly, to sing to it—a low thrumming note that made the rock warm by degrees, the way a hearthstone holds heat after the fire dies.

Pip was asleep within minutes, curled against Rafael's side, her small hands still moving in her sleep like she was signing to someone in her dreams. Maybe she was. Xanthe had stopped doubting it days ago.

That left Xanthe sitting at the mouth of the hollow, watching the storm erase the world, and watching Rafael watch Pip sleep with an expression Xanthe had learned to read the way you learn a dangerous stretch of trail: carefully, and with growing dread.

Because Rafael looked at the girl the way a man looks at something he's already said goodbye to.

"You should sleep," Rafael said, without looking up.

"So should you."

"I will." He adjusted the blanket around Pip's shoulders. His burned hands moved gently, the scarring catching the faint ember-light from the shard wrapped in oilcloth at his chest. "After."

"After what?"

He didn't answer. The wind filled the silence for him, pressing at the cave mouth like a hand testing a door.

Xanthe had been a smuggler long enough to know what an unanswered question meant. It meant the answer was worse than the silence. It meant the person holding it was deciding whether you could afford to carry it too.

They'd crossed half the Thornfells together. They'd run from hunters, buried Sera's old badge in the snow, watched a mountain sing. And through all of it, Rafael had carried one thing close and never once shown it—and Xanthe had carried their own matching secret just as tight, because that was what Fairweathers did. You held the cargo. You didn't open the box.

But the storm wasn't going anywhere, and neither were they, and tomorrow the climb got steeper.

"Rafael." Xanthe turned from the cave mouth. "Tell me what happens at the forge."

His head came up slowly. In the dim warmth of Brannoc's singing stone, his face was all shadow and ember-glow, and he looked, for a moment, exactly like what he was: the last priest of a dying order, carrying a god's heart toward an altar.

"You knew," he said quietly.

"I asked first."

* * *

**Rafael**

He had rehearsed this confession a hundred times across a hundred miles. On the scree fields, with hunters' arrows singing past his ears. In Brannoc's tunnels, with the stone humming around them like a struck bell. Walking behind Xanthe on the switchbacks, watching the set of their shoulders and thinking: *I should tell them. I will tell them. Tomorrow.*

Tomorrow had always been easier than now.

Now there was no tomorrow left to borrow. The solstice was nine days out. The forge was five hard climbs away. And the storm had pinned them in a hole in the rock with nothing but time, and time was the one thing he'd sworn he wouldn't waste.

"The covenant isn't a wall," he said. "It's not a treaty written on paper. It's a knot. Mortal thread and divine thread, tied together at the Emberforge, renewed every generation since the mountains were young." He pulled the shard from its wrappings, and its light breathed between his scarred palms. "And every renewal has required a binding. A soul, freely offered. A death."

Behind him, Brannoc's song faltered—one missed beat, then resumed, lower. He'd known too, then. Or guessed.

"Vaelis is the ember," Rafael went on, before his courage could fail him. "Not a man who carries fire. The fire itself. When the last priest bound themselves into the covenant, three hundred years ago, they became part of the flame—and the flame has been thinning ever since, because a god cannot renew a mortal bargain alone. It takes both hands. It always took both hands." He looked up. "It takes mine."

The words hung in the warm dark. Pip stirred but didn't wake.

"And you've known this," Xanthe said, "since when?"

"Since before I met you. Since the night my order died." His voice caught on it, the way it always did—the memory of the sanctuary, the arrows, the High Hunter's calm voice reading charges while the vestments burned. "We weren't slaughtered for heresy. We were slaughtered because we remembered the terms. Because we were preparing to go north and renew what the Crown wanted lapsed. I am not fleeing, Xanthe. I'm delivering myself."

"I know."

The word came out flat and certain, and it stopped him cold.

* * *

**Xanthe**

"I know," Xanthe said again, because once hadn't been enough to make it real even to themself.

Rafael stared. "How?"

"The shard hummed." Xanthe held out their hand, palm up—an old smuggler's gesture, *show me your goods*. "Back in Brannoc's tunnels. When I touched it. Don't look at me like that—you saw me touch it. You went white as the snow outside and you never said why."

"It hums for everyone. It's a piece of a god; it sings to—"

"It doesn't sing to Sera. She told me. It doesn't sing to Brannoc, and he talks to rock for a living. It sang to me like a tuning fork finding its note." Xanthe kept their voice level, the way they did over ledgers and loaded crossbows. "I've spent every mile since then working out why a smuggler's hand makes a god's heart ring. There's only one answer I keep landing on."

"Don't." Rafael's voice cracked on it. "Whatever you've decided it means—don't."

"The forge opens for a keeper of roads. That's what the bells told Pip. That's what she drew." Xanthe nodded at the sleeping girl, at the charcoal sketches folded in her coat pocket, the route none of them had chosen yet rendered in a child's careful lines. "I've been telling myself it could mean anyone. A road-keeper. A guide. But Rafael—I've kept roads my whole life. Every pass, every toll, every promise the guild ever swore on a crossing. If the covenant needs someone who knows what it is to hold a road open—" They spread their hands. "Here I am."

"No." He was on his feet now, and Pip woke at the motion, blinking, and Rafael softened instantly, lowering himself back down, gathering her close until she sighed and slept again. When he spoke it was a whisper, fierce and low. "You don't get to take this from me. It's the only thing I have left. My whole order died so I could carry that shard north. If it isn't me—if it was never going to be me—then I'm not a priest. I'm not a martyr. I'm just a man who got people killed for nothing."

"That's not—"

"That's *exactly* what it is." His eyes were wet and furious in the ember-light. "Sera took a blade on that bridge. Brannoc left his halls for this. Pip's parents handed their daughter to strangers because I said I could keep her safe. All of it, balanced on the idea that I'm the one who's supposed to die at the top of that mountain. Take that away and what am I?"

Xanthe sat with that. Outside, the storm screamed and screamed.

"A survivor," they said finally. "Like the rest of us. It's not as noble. It's harder."

* * *

**Rafael**

He wanted to hate the answer. He reached for hatred the way a freezing man reaches for anything, and found he couldn't hold it.

Because Xanthe was right, and being right was the cruelest thing they'd done yet.

He settled back against the warm stone, and Pip's weight against his ribs was steady and alive, and the shard pulsed between them like a second heartbeat, and Rafael understood suddenly that he had built his entire remaining self out of the architecture of his own death. Every plan, every step, every dawn—he'd measured them all against an altar waiting at the end of the road. It had made the dying bearable. It had made the living feel temporary, which was its own strange mercy.

And now Xanthe sat across from him in the dark and said, plainly, without drama: *it might be me instead.* And the mercy curdled.

"If it's you," he said, "I won't allow it."

"You don't get to allow or disallow. That's the whole point of a free offering. Nobody can give it for you and nobody can refuse it for you." Xanthe's mouth twisted. "Believe me, I've thought about the theology. I had a lot of miles to fill."

"You're a smuggler."

"I'm a smuggler whose family has been keeping a covenant-shaped hole in their ledgers for three generations. Did you really think Maren sent me with you out of piety?" Xanthe laughed, short and bitter. "She sent me because she's known longer than either of us. Why do you think she gave me the high pass? Why do you think she sold you to the Crown and couldn't look at me when she did it? She's trying to spend me the way her grandmother spent the gods. And I am done being spent."

The fury in it startled them both. Rafael watched Xanthe breathe through it, watched them press their palm flat against the stone floor as if checking whether the mountain agreed.

"Why does it not frighten you?" he asked. "Dying. You speak of it like a toll."

Xanthe was quiet a long moment.

"Because I've been dead already," they said. "Years of it. Running cargo for a guild that was rotting, serving a woman who loved me the way you love a tool, keeping roads for people who'd sell me the moment the price was right. That was dying. Slow, comfortable, respectable dying." They looked up, and in the ember-light their eyes were very clear. "Then a burned priest fell out of a cargo crate and everything started hurting again, and I thought—oh. This is what being alive feels like. I'd forgotten."

* * *

**Xanthe**

They hadn't meant to say that last part aloud. It had been sitting behind their teeth for two hundred miles and the storm shook it loose.

Rafael went very still.

"Xanthe—"

"Don't." They held up a hand. "Don't make it a speech. Don't make it a vow. We're both standing on ice and I know exactly how much weight it'll hold."

"What if it holds?"

The question was soft. Softer than the storm, softer than the stone-song, and it landed harder than any of it.

Xanthe looked at him—at the burned hands that had healed Pip's fever with prayers nobody had answered in centuries until suddenly they did; at the shard-light caught in the hollows of his face; at the man who had walked out of a massacre carrying a god's heart and a death sentence and had still stopped, on the worst night of his life, to pull a stranger out of a burning waystation.

Love, Xanthe thought, was just smuggling with better cargo. You carried what mattered through country designed to stop you, and you expected to pay for it, and you paid anyway.

"We're nine days from the solstice," they said. "Five from the forge. One of us walks up that mountain expecting not to walk back down. Maybe both of us." Their voice stayed steady. Their hands didn't quite. "If I let myself want something, I want it to be after. Do you understand? Not now. Not in a cave where the wind's the only witness and the odds are a knife at both our throats. After. When we know who's still standing."

Rafael was silent. Then, slowly, he smiled—not the gentle priest-smile he wore like vestments, but something rawer underneath it, something that looked almost like hope and almost like grief.

"All right," he said. "After."

"After," Xanthe agreed.

And because the storm was loud and the night was long and neither of them would sleep, Xanthe slid across the warm stone until their shoulder rested against his, and Rafael did not move away, and Pip murmured in her sleep and turned toward them both like a compass finding north.

* * *

**Rafael**

He woke to silence.

Not the silence of exhaustion—that blessed blankness he'd chased for weeks and never caught—but true silence. The storm had stopped. Through the cave mouth, the sky was swept clean and violent with stars, and the snow lay blue-white and endless, and somewhere far below, a sound was rising up through the rock.

Bells.

Brannoc was already awake, standing at the entrance, his head cocked. Pip stood beside him, wide-eyed, her hands moving fast in the starlight—signing, signing, her face lit with terror and joy in equal measure.

Rafael touched Xanthe's shoulder. They came awake instantly, the way smugglers do, one hand already finding a weapon.

"Bells," Brannoc rumbled. "Under the mountain. Coming up from the deep ways." He looked back at them, and in his beard the frost had melted, and his eyes were shining. "The stone's never sung like this. Not in my grandfather's grandfather's day. Something's waking, and it's *moving*."

Pip grabbed Rafael's sleeve and tugged, urgent, and held up her hands:

*It knows we're coming.*

*It's counting the days.*

Xanthe looked at Rafael. Nine days. Five climbs. One altar.

"Then we'd better not be late," Xanthe said, and shouldered their pack, and stepped out into the blue snow.
