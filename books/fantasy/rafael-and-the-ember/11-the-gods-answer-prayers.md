## 11. The Gods Answer Prayers

Pip had three ways of saying things, and only one of them was silence.

The first was her hands, which could say *bread* and *cold* and *Mama* and *don't*. The second was her charcoal, which could say everything else — the shape of a hawk's shadow, the exact blue of the hour before snowfall, the way Rafael's face looked when he thought no one was watching him grieve.

The third was the bells.

She never told anyone about the bells. She couldn't have, anyway. They weren't sounds exactly, not the way bread was bread. They were more like warmth through a wall — you knew the fire was there even if you couldn't see it. The bells had been with her since before she could remember, ringing somewhere behind her ribs, and for most of her life they'd been quiet. Distant. A lullaby played in another house.

Three days ago, on the road out of Thornfell, they had started ringing like a house on fire.

* * *

The valley village was called Lowmark, though Pip didn't learn its name until later. What she learned first was that it smelled of woodsmoke and wet wool and fear, and that its people looked at Rafael's burned hands and Sera's hunter's coat and decided, all at once, to be brave about it.

They were given the loft above the shrine-house. It was a small room with a crooked floor and one window, and Pip loved it immediately, because from the window she could see the whole valley laid out like a drawing someone had abandoned halfway: fields stitched grey with frost, the river a pencil line, the mountains beyond pressing close like something leaning in to listen.

Xanthe carried her up the ladder because her legs were tired, which was embarrassing, but Xanthe carried her the way you carry something you've decided matters — carefully, without making it strange. Pip had decided Xanthe mattered around the second day, when she'd seen them count their coin twice and then give half of it away to a farmer whose barn had burned. Smugglers weren't supposed to do that. Pip knew what smugglers were supposed to be, because she'd drawn them: shadows with knives, faces like closed doors. Xanthe was more like a door left open a crack, light coming through whether they meant it or not.

That night she couldn't sleep. The bells wouldn't let her.

They had changed. That was the thing she couldn't explain to anyone, because she didn't have the words for it even inside her own head. For her whole life the bells had been many — dozens of voices, far apart, each ringing its own tune. Now they were fewer. And the ones that remained rang closer together, tighter, like people gathering in a square before something happened.

Like people deciding who would speak for them.

Pip sat up in the dark. Rafael was asleep by the window, his burned hands curled against his chest like two dying birds. Brannoc snored in the corner, low and rhythmic as grinding stone. Sera wasn't there — she was downstairs, sitting where she always sat, between the family and the door.

Pip climbed down the ladder alone.

* * *

The shrine-house below was empty at this hour except for the god.

Not Vaelis. Not yet. The little shrine held a carved figure of some harvest mother, her paint flaking, her offering bowl full of melted snow. But when Pip stood in front of it, the bells in her chest went still — all of them at once, the way birds go quiet before a storm — and then one bell began to ring that she had never heard before.

It was deep. Older than the others. It didn't ring so much as *turn*, slow and vast, like a wheel under the world.

And then she wasn't standing in the shrine anymore.

She was standing in a place made of ember-light and stone, a cavern so large the ceiling was only a rumor, and every surface breathed a dull red glow like coals under ash. In the center of it burned a fire that did not flicker. It simply *was*, upright and patient, and Pip understood without being told that this was not a place she had gone to. It was a place that had come to meet her, because she was the only door left open.

*You hear us,* said the fire, and its voice was the deep turning bell, and it was sad. *Little bell-hearer. We are sorry for that. It is a heavy thing, hearing.*

Pip did not have words, even here. But she found she didn't need them. She thought her question — the one that had been sitting in her chest since the night Rafael arrived with his burned hands and his terrible cargo — and the fire heard it.

*Why are they quiet?* she asked, thinking it as hard as she could. *The other bells. Where did they go?*

The fire dimmed, which she understood was grief.

*They are being severed,* it said. *One by one. The rope between our realm and yours is fraying, and where it frays, we go silent. By the winter solstice it will part entirely, and then no prayer will reach us, and no answer will return. Your world will forget us — not by choice. By amputation.*

Pip felt tears, though she hadn't known she could cry in a dream. She thought: *Can it be mended?*

*Yes.* The fire brightened. *There is a forge older than your kingdoms, older than our sorrow. The Emberforge. There the covenant was first bound, and there it can be bound again — but the severance comes at solstice, and solstice is nine days away, and the forge will open for no army, no priest, no king.*

Pip waited. The bells in her chest leaned forward.

*It will open for a keeper of roads,* the fire said. *One who has kept every promise the roads required of them. One whose oath is not written in blood or law, but in the walking. Find the keeper of roads, little bell-hearer, and carry them to the forge before the longest night. Do this, and the realms will hold.*

Then the fire did something that undid her completely. It bent — the whole upright column of it, bending down toward her, the way an adult bends to a child — and its voice went soft.

*We know what we ask. You are small, and the road is high, and the men who hunt your friends are many. If you are afraid, that is not a failure. Fear is just love with nowhere to put itself yet. Put it into your hands, bell-hearer. You have good hands. We have watched you draw the truth when your mouth could not. Draw them the way.*

And the cavern filled with light, and Pip woke on the shrine-house floor with charcoal already in her fist.

* * *

She drew until her fingers ached.

She drew the forge: a mountain opened like a mouth, fire standing upright in its throat. She drew the fraying rope, the bells going dark one by one, a calendar of moons with the last moon crossed out. She drew nine marks and scratched out one — eight days now, after tonight. She drew herself, small, holding a lantern, and behind her five figures on a road: the tall one with burned hands, the broad one like a boulder, the sharp one with the bow, the door-left-open one, and herself.

Last of all, because the fire had told her to, she drew the keeper of roads.

She didn't decide what to draw. Her hand moved and she let it. A figure on a road at dusk, pack on their back, counting coins — no, giving them away. A figure whose face she knew better than any face in the world now, because she'd been studying it for days the way she studied everything she meant to keep. She drew the door left open, and light coming through it, and she drew the figure's feet, because the fire had said *in the walking*, and she understood that the feet mattered most.

Then she sat back on her heels in the cold shrine-house, surrounded by paper, and heard footsteps on the loft ladder, and did not turn around, because she already knew.

* * *

Xanthe came down quietly, the way they did everything — like someone who'd spent a lifetime practicing not being noticed and had recently, reluctantly, given it up. Pip heard them stop when they saw the drawings spread across the floor. Heard the breath catch. Heard them kneel.

"Pip." Their voice was rough with sleep and something else. "What — where did these come from?"

She turned. Held up the drawing of the forge. Pointed at the fire. Then pressed both hands flat over her heart, where the bells lived, and tapped twice.

"You saw it," Xanthe said slowly. "Or — heard it. The gods talked to you."

She nodded. Then she pushed the important drawing across the floorboards to them: the figure on the road. The keeper of roads.

Xanthe picked it up. Pip watched their face do several things very quickly — confusion, then recognition, then a kind of horror, like a man reading his own name on a grave marker.

"This is—" They stopped. Started again. "This is me."

Pip nodded again, firmly, the way you nod when a smaller person is trying to tell a bigger person something obvious.

"No." Xanthe set the drawing down carefully, the way you set down something hot. "No, you've got it wrong. The priest — Rafael — he's the one. He's carried the ember across half the country, he's the one they're hunting, he's the one who—"

Pip took the drawing back. Tore it in half — the head from the body — and laid the body-side in front of Xanthe. Feet on a road. Pack. Open door.

*In the walking,* the fire had said. She pointed at the feet. Then at Xanthe's boots. Then she took Xanthe's hand — their callused, coin-counting, coin-giving-away hand — and pressed it flat against the paper.

"The forge opens for a keeper of roads," Xanthe whispered. Pip read their lips more than heard them; the room had gone very quiet. "Every promise honored. Every road kept. That's not a priest's vow, that's—"

They stopped. Pip watched them arrive at it, watch the understanding land like a stone dropped down a well — a long fall, and then the deep, far-off sound of it hitting bottom.

"That's an oath," Xanthe said. "A smuggler's oath. Mine."

Upstairs, faintly, a floorboard creaked. Rafael was awake. Of course he was awake; he slept like a man keeping watch over his own funeral. Pip heard him pause at the top of the ladder, heard the silence stretch, heard him begin to climb down — and she saw Xanthe look up at the sound with an expression she had never seen on them before, and never wanted to see again.

It was the expression of someone who has just learned they are going to be asked to die, and has realized, worse, that they might say yes.

Rafael reached the bottom of the ladder. His eyes went to the drawings. To the torn page. To Xanthe's hand still pressed flat against the keeper of roads.

"What's this?" he said softly.

Pip looked at Xanthe. Xanthe looked at the fire-drawing, at the upright flame that did not flicker, and Pip saw them choose — in the space of one breath — not to say it yet. Not to take this from him. Not here, not half-asleep, not with eight days left and a mountain between them and everything.

"Nothing," Xanthe said, folding the pages closed. "Go back to sleep, Rafael. We've got a long climb tomorrow."

But Pip had drawn the truth, because her mouth could not, and the truth does not fold shut as easily as paper. She caught Xanthe's eye as they gathered the pages, and she signed to them, small and clear, the sign her mother had taught her first:

*I know. And I'm coming with you.*

Xanthe's jaw tightened. Outside the window, far off in the high passes, the wind changed direction — and beneath it, so faint that only Pip could hear it, the bells began to count down.
